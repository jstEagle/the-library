## 13. Flight Through the Underside

The oldest ledger weighed more than a sleeping cat and had roughly the same temperament—dense, uncooperative, and inclined to bite if handled carelessly. Its corners were iron-clamped, its spine wrapped in leather gone the color of strong tea, and it did not want to be carried.

Lior carried it anyway.

They were four levels below the Guildhall's counting room, in a corridor where the lamps burned green because no one had ever gotten around to fixing them, and behind them—above them, everywhere in the way that bad news is everywhere—the Guildhall was being sealed.

"Left here," Sefa hissed from the mouth of a doorway Lior would have sworn was a wall.

"It's a wall."

"It's a door pretending to be a wall. There's a difference, and the difference is that we're about to be through it." She shouldered past, pressed her palm flat against the mortar at shoulder height, and pushed. A seam appeared, thin as a hairline crack, and then the whole slab swung inward on hinges that groaned like an old man asked to run.

Lior stared. "How long have you known about this?"

"Since I was nine." Sefa glanced back down the corridor, listening. Somewhere above, boots—many boots, in the disciplined rhythm of people who had been told to hurry but not to panic. Vael's wardens. "Courier apprentices find everything. We're the ones who carry things, so we're the ones who learn where things hide."

"And you never told anyone?"

"I told Corvane once. He gave me a silver mark and a look, and I understood the look meant I'd tell no one else ever." She grabbed Lior's sleeve. "In. Now. Arguing is for people who aren't being hunted."

Lior went in, ledger clutched to their chest like a child, and the wall closed behind them with a sound like a held breath released.

Darkness. Then Sefa struck a match off her thumbnail—a courier's trick, one of forty she'd never bothered to teach Lior—and touched flame to a stub of tallow wedged in a niche by the door, as though the tunnel had been expecting company and had left a light on. Which, Lior realized, looking at the niche, the shelf beside it, and the neat row of three more candles, it had.

"Corvane stocked it," Sefa said quietly, reading the same thing. "Recently. The wax isn't dusty."

*Carry the debt.* That was what he'd said, pressing the ledger into Lior's arms while smoke poured up the stairwell behind him and Vael's wardens hammered at the front doors. He'd been calm the way deep water is calm, and he'd smiled at Lior with something that wasn't quite apology and wasn't quite hope, and then he'd turned back toward the fire and the seals and whatever he intended to say to Inquisitor Vael when she got through the doors.

"He knew we'd come this way," Lior said. Their voice came out strange in the narrow dark. "He stocked the tunnel for us."

"For *you*, probably. I'm incidental." But Sefa didn't sound wounded by it. She sounded, if anything, pleased—like a person discovering they'd been included in a plan without having to do any of the worrying. She took a candle, tested the air with a wetted finger the way sailors test wind, and started walking. "Come on. It's two miles to the river gate, and half of it's uphill, which makes no sense until it does."

"What does that mean?"

"You'll see. Keep your hand on the left wall. If the wall goes warm, stop and tell me immediately."

"Why?"

"Because warm means someone lit a stove on the other side of it, and stoves mean houses, and houses mean we've wandered under the city instead of out of it." She glanced back, candlelight catching the crooked grin she wore the way other people wore badges. "Also, occasionally the undersiders tap the walls to hear who's walking. You'll feel it before you hear it. Like a knock coming through your bones."

"The what?"

"Undersiders." Sefa said it the way you'd say *neighbors*, casual and fond. "Tunnel folk. Smugglers, mostly, and smugglers' children, and one very old woman who claims she built half these passages during the last war and charges tolls in gossip. They're not dangerous as long as you don't lie to them."

"Liar's tunnels," Lior said. "Of course. Of course the Guild of Impossible Things has liar-detecting smuggler tunnels."

"The guild doesn't have tunnels, Lior. The guild has *accounts*. The tunnels just happen underneath, the way bedrock happens underneath accounts." She walked on, and Lior followed, and the candlelight swayed their shadows along walls slick with old mortar, past niches where long-dead smugglers had scratched tally marks, initials, and—at least twice—what appeared to be small, heartfelt complaints about cheese.

The ledger thumped against Lior's ribs with every step. *Carry the debt.* They'd had maybe ten seconds to understand what that meant before understanding became irrelevant and running became the whole of the law. Ten seconds of watching Corvane stand in his burning counting room, straightening his cuffs, waiting for the doors to give.

"He let her take him," Lior said. Not meaning to say it aloud. Saying it anyway.

Sefa slowed. "Vael?"

"He could have fought. He could have run—he knows every exit better than I know my own handwriting. He stood there and straightened his *cuffs*, Sefa. That's a man who decided something." Lior's hand found the ledger's iron corner and held it, the cold biting their fingers. "I keep thinking: everything in this book, everything he spent a century keeping quiet—it was all so the city could go on not knowing. And now the city's going to know anyway, because she'll read the current ledgers, or she'll interrogate him, or—" Their throat tightened. "She's polite, Sefa. Did you notice? She apologized to the doorman. She's going to be polite all the way through him."

"I noticed." Sefa's grin had faded somewhere in the last hundred paces. She held her candle higher, and the light reached far enough to show the tunnel bending ahead, downhill now, the air cooler and tasting faintly of river. "My brother's in the refugee camps on the east fields. Tam. He's fifteen and he thinks wardens are exciting."

"They're not exciting."

"No," Sefa agreed. "But he thinks they are, which is worse. So when I get out of this tunnel, I'm going to figure out how to keep an inquisitor's attention pointed anywhere except at a fifteen-year-old who thinks she's exciting." She resumed walking, briskly, as if she'd simply been pausing to admire the masonry. "One problem at a time. Right now the problem is a ladder."

"A what?"

The tunnel ended—or rather, stopped being a floor and became a suggestion of one, opening downward into a vertical shaft where rusted iron rungs descended into black. Cool air rose up around them, smelling of water and stone and something older, mineral and patient.

"That's the uphill part explained," Lior said weakly. "We go *down* to get out of the city."

"The river gate sits lower than the Guildhall foundations. Geography doesn't care about our feelings." Sefa wedged her candle into another niche—there was always another niche; the tunnel was practically furnished—and swung herself onto the ladder with the ease of someone who had done this at an age when most children were still afraid of cellars. "Ledger first. Strap it across your back if you can, or hold it one-armed and climb with the other. Do not drop it. If you drop it, I will go down after it, and I will be extremely unpleasant about it the entire time."

"You'd fetch it?"

"Sefa of the courier lines does not lose packages." She began descending, her voice floating up through the shaft. "It's the one professional principle I have left, and I intend to die owning exactly one."

* * *

The ladder was ninety-one rungs. Lior counted, because counting was what they did when fear needed a job. By rung sixty their arms had filed formal complaints. By rung eighty-five, the ledger had developed opinions about balance that it communicated through sharp iron corners in the spine. At rung ninety-one their boot found solid ground, and they stood swaying in a passage wide enough for two people abreast, floored in rounded cobbles that had plainly once been a street—before the city grew upward and buried its own childhood.

Sefa relit her candle and held it low.

"Oh," Lior said.

The buried street ran straight as an arrow into darkness, and it had not been abandoned empty. Doors still stood in doorframes. A sign hung over one entrance, its paint flaked but legible: *MERRIWEATHER & DAUGHTERS, KEYS CUT WHILE YOU WAIT.* Someone had chalked a hopscotch grid on the cobbles outside it, and the chalk lines were crisp, protected from rain and sun and time by thirty feet of city overhead.

"People lived here," Lior whispered.

"People lived everywhere. Then they lived higher up." Sefa's voice had gone soft too, matching the place. "Tam and I used to sneak down through a grate near the tanneries when we were kids. There's a whole square back that way with a fountain. Dry now, but it's got fish carved on it, and Tam swore the fish moved." She snorted gently at the memory. "Everything moves, if you look long enough. That's basically the guild's founding philosophy."

They walked the dead street, boots loud on cobbles that hadn't heard boots in generations. Lior kept touching the walls—old habit, Maren's habit, *stone remembers warmth longer than flesh does, little bird, put your hand on things*—and the stone was cool and silent and kept its memories to itself.

Halfway along the street, a lantern bloomed.

Not slowly, either—one moment darkness, the next a hooded figure standing in a doorway that had definitely been empty, holding a shuttered lamp angled to show nothing of their face and everything of Lior's and Sefa's.

"Halt, name yourselves, and mind the third question," said the figure, in the tone of someone reciting a rule they found tiresome but binding.

Sefa stopped so smoothly that Lior, still moving, walked into her back. "Evening, Wren."

"Wren's my mother. I'm Sparrow." The figure tilted the lantern. Under the hood was a girl of perhaps sixteen, unimpressed in a highly practiced manner. "Evening, Sefa. Who's the stranger with the book?"

"Lior. Ledger-keeper. Friend of mine, currently in trouble, currently in a hurry."

"Everyone down here is in trouble and in a hurry. It's practically the neighborhood motto." Sparrow's eyes flicked to the ledger, and something in her posture changed—not alarm, exactly. Recognition. "Iron corners. Tea-colored spine." She looked up sharply. "That's the Old Book."

"You know it?" Lior asked.

"Everyone down here knows it. Grandmother says it came through these tunnels three times before you were born, and every time, the city nearly ended within a year." Sparrow shrugged. "Grandmother also says she built the fountain square, and Mother says she absolutely did not, so her reliability is a matter of some debate. But the book part everyone agrees on. Third question, then, since you're carrying it: *who do you owe?*"

The question dropped into the tunnel like a stone into a well. Lior felt it fall all the way down.

"We don't know," they said honestly. "That's rather the point."

Sparrow studied them for a long moment. Then she stepped aside from the doorway and swept the lantern in a small, oddly formal bow. "Honest answer. Pass, then, and take the left fork at the fish fountain—the right fork floods at high water and the middle fork belongs to the Kettle Street families, and they charge." She paused. "Ledger-keeper. If you're going where I think you're going—"

"North," Sefa said. "To the Spire."

"—then Grandmother will want to talk to you first. Everyone who carries that book talks to her first. It's tradition, and the traditions down here are enforced by people who are very old and have nothing else to do." Sparrow pulled her hood back, revealing a face suddenly younger and more worried than her voice. "She's at the fountain square. Follow the chalk arrows. And—" hesitated, then finished quickly, "—wardens came to the Tanner's Gate an hour ago. Six of them. Asking after couriers. Asking after a book."

Sefa went very still. "Six?"

"With dogs." Sparrow grimaced apologetically. "Sorry. I'd have warned you sooner, but you only just fell down my ladder."

* * *

They ran the buried streets after that, chalk arrows flashing pale in the candlelight—arrows fresh enough that Lior understood, with a lurch, that someone had drawn them minutes ago, that the undersiders had watched them descend and made a decision about them, fast and collectively, the way such people decide things.

The fish fountain square opened around a bend: a round chamber with a domed ceiling lost in darkness, ringed with the dry mouths of shop fronts, and at its center a broad stone basin carved with leaping fish whose scales had been polished bright by centuries of hands. Lanterns hung on hooks around the rim, unlit until now—and as Lior and Sefa pounded into the square, hands reached from the shadows and lit them, one by one, until the whole ring blazed.

A dozen people stood among the shops. More watched from doorways. All of them looked at the ledger.

And from the fountain's rim, a tiny woman rose to her feet—rose being generous; she unfolded herself upright with the creaking dignity of a ship coming about—wearing three shawls and an expression of vast, unhurried interest.

"So," said the old woman, in a voice like gravel poured slowly. "The book walks again. And it walks with a keeper who owes and doesn't know whom." She beckoned with one knotted finger. "Come here, child, where I can see whether you're a fool or merely unlucky. It matters for the advice."

Lior approached, ledger heavy, heart heavier. Behind them, Sefa hovered, glancing repeatedly back the way they'd come, where—far off, faint through stone—came a sound that made Lior's stomach drop.

Barking. Dogs, and beneath them, voices. Wardens in the tunnels, and closing.

"Grandmother," Sefa said urgently, "they're behind us, we need the north passage—"

"You need sense first, passage second, or the passage will kill you and save the wardens the trouble." The old woman caught Lior's chin in a grip surprisingly like iron and turned their face to the light, peering. Then her eyes dropped to the ledger, and she went quiet in a way that silenced the whole square. When she spoke again, it was almost gently. "You've got Maren's look about you. Hedge-witch eyes. Always looking at people like they're books worth reading." She released Lior's chin. "You know her?"

"She raised me," Lior managed. "Is she—"

"Down here? No. Up there, in trouble, like everyone." The old woman waved dismissively at the world in general. "Listen fast, then. North passage runs under the river in an old aqueduct. It comes up in the charcoal-burner's hollow beyond the walls—your wardens know the aqueduct, so you won't take it straight. You'll take the side branch at the third arch, up through the well house, and walk out the top of the city like honest ghosts. My granddaughter will show you the branch." She jerked her head; Sparrow detached from the crowd, already gathering a satchel and a hooded cloak. "And you—" the finger rose again, aimed at Lior's chest, at the ledger, "—when you reach the tower, remember what the book is. It isn't a weapon. It isn't a key. It's a *voice*. Things that have been robbed a long time stop wanting gold. They want somebody to read the account aloud and admit what was done. Can you do that, keeper? Can you say it out loud, even when saying it costs?"

Far away, closer now, the dogs bayed, and the sound came down through thirty feet of city like judgment arriving early.

"Yes," Lior said. And discovered, saying it, that it was true.

"Then run, and live long enough to keep your word." The old woman folded herself back down onto the fountain rim, done with them, already ancient again. "Go. Go. And Sefa—"

"Ma'am?"

"Your brother. East camps. When the stars come back—and they'll come back, or nothing I built these tunnels for was worth building—you bring him down to see the fish. Promise me."

Sefa's face did something complicated and brave. "I promise."

They ran—Lior, Sefa, and Sparrow with her satchel and her mother's sure feet—out of the blazing square and into the dark of the north passage, the barking swelling behind them like a tide, the aqueduct's cold breath rising to meet them. At the third arch Sparrow hauled open a hatch and shoved them through it with no ceremony whatsoever, pressing a stub of chalk into Lior's hand as a parting gift.

"For marking your way," she said. "Everyone should leave some record they existed. That's the whole religion down here."

Then the hatch banged shut, and Lior and Sefa were alone in the dark under the river, climbing toward the well house and the night beyond the walls—with six wardens, a sealed Guildhall, a captured master, a promised reunion, and one impossible book between them and the road north.

Above them, faint through stone, the first stars should have been coming out.

There were none.

But far off, at the edge of hearing, in a direction Lior refused yet to name, something vast and hungry had begun, very softly, to hum.
