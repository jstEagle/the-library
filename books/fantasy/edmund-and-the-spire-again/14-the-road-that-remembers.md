## 14. The Road That Remembers

The old ledger rode against Lior's chest in a sling made from a courier's sash, and by the second day on the north road they had developed a theory about it: the book was heavier than it had been in the Guildhall.

Not much heavier. An ounce, perhaps two. But Lior had carried ledgers for six years and knew their weight the way a mother knows her children's coughs, and this one was gaining, page by page, as though the accounts inside were accruing interest even now.

"You're talking to it again," Sefa said, without turning around. She walked point, as she insisted on doing, her courier's staff clicking against the cobbles in a rhythm that said *I am unbothered* very loudly for someone who had fled a sealed guildhouse through smuggler tunnels two nights ago.

"I am not talking to it."

"You mutter when you think. You've been muttering since the ford at Millbrook."

"That's not talking to it, that's thinking with commentary."

"Same thing," Sefa said, "if you do it out loud." She glanced back, and her grin was there — crooked, quick, the grin that had gotten them both extra rations during the flood year — but it sat oddly on her face lately, like a coat borrowed from someone broader in the shoulders. It kept slipping. "How far to the next village?"

Lior consulted the map Corvane had pressed into their hands along with the ledger, back when the Guildhall doors were being nailed shut behind them. The map was older than the city walls and annotated in three hands, one of which had been Corvane's own, young, judging by the confident loops. A village called Harrowmere sat a day's walk north. Then the grey country. Then the Spire.

"A day," Lior said. "If the roads are kind."

"The roads haven't been kind since the stars went out," Maren said from the cart.

Maren rode in the cart because her eyes could no longer be trusted with edges and ditches, and because she had declared, in the tone that had once sent apprentice-Lior scurrying to chop herbs, that walking was for people who hadn't spent seventy years on their feet. She sat among the bundles with her hands folded and her face turned toward the sky, which was the only direction she could still see anything, and what she saw there was nothing at all: a black where ten thousand stars should have been wheeling.

Behind the cart, the mule — named Turnip, because Sefa had won naming rights in a card game and had no shame — plodded with the resigned air of an animal who had understood, before any of them, that this journey would be long and badly provisioned.

They came over the rise above Harrowmere at midday, and Lior stopped so suddenly that Turnip nearly sat down in protest.

The fields below were burning.

Not burning — that was the thing, the wrongness of it, the way a face is wrong when it smiles too long. The wheat stood gold and whole in the afternoon light, heavy-headed, ready for a harvest that would never come because no one had lived in Harrowmere for a hundred years. But across the gold ran lines of fire, silent and sourceless, licking up stalks that did not burn, curling through furrows without smoke or heat. And in the fire, if you looked — Lior looked, because looking was both their gift and their affliction — there were shapes. People. Small at this distance, running, or standing very still with their arms raised, or bent over something on the ground as though trying to lift it.

"It's doing it again," Sefa whispered. She had seen it too. Everyone on the road north had seen it; the refugees coming south spoke of the burning fields in the flat voices people used for things they had decided not to feel yet.

"No," Maren said quietly, from the cart. "It isn't doing anything. It's remembering. There's a difference, child, and it matters more than you'd think."

Lior descended the rise alone, because someone had to, and because the ledger had grown warm against their chest in a way that felt less like warning than like recognition. At the field's edge stood a boundary stone, moss-eaten, its carving worn to suggestion. Lior brushed the moss away with a thumb.

*HARROWMERE PARISH. MAY THE GROUND KEEP WHAT IT LOVES.*

Below that, newer, shallower, cut in haste: a date. A date a century old, from the last month of the last war.

The fires played on. Lior watched a woman — a shape of fire shaped like a woman — kneel in the wheat and gather armfuls of it, pressing the burning stalks against her chest as though she could carry the harvest somewhere safe. She did this over and over, gathering, rising, finding her arms empty, kneeling again. She would do it forever. She had probably been doing it for a hundred years, in the place where time went when nobody was watching.

"She can't see us," Lior called back, voice rougher than intended. "Can she?"

"No," Maren said. "And we can't help her. Both of those facts are going to sit in your chest like stones for a while. Let them. Stones are honest."

Sefa came down and stood beside Lior at the stone. For once she didn't joke. She took off her cap — a small courtesy, absurd and enormous — and put it back on. "Which battle was this?" she asked.

"Not a battle." Lior had been reading the old ledger by candlestub every night, decoding Corvane's cramped marginalia, and some of it had begun to make a terrible kind of sense. "The ledger calls it the Scouring. When the wards started failing the first time, before the theft, the enemy came over the border and burned everything within thirty miles of the Spire. Not for strategy. For hunger. They ate—" Lior stopped. Said it plainer. "They ate what the fire didn't. Memories, mostly. Whole villages forgot themselves while the wheat still stood. The fire was just tidying up after."

"And then Corvane stole the tower's heart and ended it."

"Borrowed," Lior said. "He insists it was borrowing."

"Mm." Sefa looked at the burning woman in the wheat. "And a hundred years later the debt comes due, and here we are, walking straight toward the creditor with a book under your shirt." She clapped Lior on the shoulder. "You know, most apprentices just steal stationery."

That was the thing about Sefa, Lior thought, watching her walk back to the cart. She made the terror portable. She broke it into pieces small enough to carry. Later — much later — Lior would learn what it cost her to do that, and what she had already traded away to keep them all moving, but that knowledge was still waiting for them down the road, patient as debt.

* * *

They camped that night in the shadow of the burning fields, because there was nowhere else, and because Maren said the echoes wouldn't cross open ground after dark — "the land dreams at night, and dreaming things don't like company" — which was either wisdom or nonsense, and with Maren it had always been impossible to tell the difference, and lately the difference mattered less than the sound of her voice.

Lior took first watch, wrapped in a blanket, the ledger open on their knees, working by the light of a lamp filled with oil the guild had sold as *light that remembers daylight*. It burned with a small golden steadiness, and if you stared into it you could almost believe the sun was merely behind a hill instead of gone from the sky entirely. Half the refugees on the southern roads carried one. The guild had made a fortune. The guild had always made fortunes out of other people's emergencies; Lior had catalogued enough impossible goods to know that the impossible was mostly just the necessary wearing a costume.

The entry they were stuck on was dated ninety-nine years ago, in Corvane's young hand:

*Item: one (1) Heart, removed from the anchor-stone, condition excellent. Payment rendered: cessation of hostilities, term of one century. Interest schedule attached. Note to successor: the interest is not paid in coin. Do not let them find out what it is paid in until they have read this far. By then they will understand why I waited.*

Lior had read this entry eleven times. Each time, the note to successor felt less like instructions and more like an apology written by a man who knew he would be dead or disgraced before anyone read it, and who wanted, desperately, to be understood by exactly one person.

"You're frowning at it again," Maren said. She hadn't seemed asleep. With Maren it was hard to tell that too.

"It says he wanted mercy," Lior said. "'Forgetting will be kinder than knowing.' His words, in the margin. He stole the heart and wiped the border clean of memory so the war couldn't be rekindled by grudge. He thought he was saving everyone twice. Once from the enemy, once from the past."

"And do you understand him?"

Lior turned a page. The interest schedule ran down the column in figures that made their stomach fold: villages, dated forward, decade by decade. Names Lior now recognized. Harrowmere was on the list. So was the blank-faced village they'd passed two days ago, its people smiling in the lanes, offering bread to strangers whose names they couldn't hold onto for an hour.

"He wasn't wrong," Lior said slowly. "That's the horrible part. If he'd done nothing, the war finishes and everyone in the borderlands dies or worse. He bought a century. He just—"

"Just spent other people's yesterdays to do it," Maren finished. "Yes. That is how it goes, child. Every kindness in the world has a column somewhere. Most people never see the accounting." Her blind face tilted toward the burning fields. "I saw mine. Once. It's why I taught you the way I taught you, and it's why you ran, and we'll get to all of that when my mouth is braver than it is tonight."

Lior's chest did something complicated. "Maren—"

"Hush. Watch the field."

Lior watched. The burning woman was still gathering her harvest, still finding her arms empty. But near the boundary stone, something new: a second figure had appeared in the fire, a soldier-shape, tall, and it was walking toward the woman, and the woman had stopped kneeling. She was facing it. The fire of her flickered in a way that fire shouldn't, quick and birdlike, like breath before words.

"What's happening?" Sefa asked softly. She'd woken. Of course she had; Sefa slept like a cat in a strange house.

"I don't know," Lior breathed. "They never interact. The echoes don't—"

The soldier-shape knelt. In the firelight it was impossible to say whether it offered something or took something, whether the woman struck it or embraced it. The flames rose together, tangled, and for one moment the whole field blazed white — white like starlight, white like the sky used to be — and then it guttered down to the ordinary gold-and-fire, and the woman was kneeling again, gathering, empty-armed, forever.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Turnip shifted and sighed.

"There were people," Lior said finally, "who loved each other on both sides of that war. Weren't there. And the Scouring didn't care, and the wards didn't care, and Corvane's arithmetic didn't care, and now the ground replays it every day and none of it cares either."

"Yes," Maren said. "Now you understand what the ledger costs to read. Not the eyes. The rest of you." She lay back against the bundles. "Sleep, keeper. Tomorrow we cross into the grey country, and you'll want your whole self about you. Whatever's left of it."

* * *

The grey country began the next morning not with a line but with a fading, the way sound fades underwater. First the color went out of the grass — not grey exactly, but uncommitted, a green that had forgotten to insist on itself. Then the birdsong thinned. Then, strangest, the smells: the road smelled of nothing, and Lior hadn't realized until that moment how much of memory was smell, how much of *themself* was stored in manure and woodsmoke and wet wool, until all of it lifted away like a cloth drawn off a table.

The ledger grew heavier. Lior was sure of it now. Two ounces, maybe three.

"Stop weighing it," Sefa said.

"I'm not—"

"You keep shifting your shoulders like a packhorse with a rub. What happens when you reach the Spire? Does the book weigh as much as you do?"

Lior didn't answer, because they had done the arithmetic, in the night, by remembered daylight. The ledger recorded a debt. Lior was the keeper of the debt. And debts, in the guild's oldest grammar, were not abstractions — they were weights, they were bindings, they were the difference between a name written in ink and a name written in water. Somewhere ahead a tower was starving, and every mile Lior walked toward it, the account between them grew more literal.

At noon they passed a farmhouse that was replaying its own evening. This was worse than Harrowmere, because it was smaller. Through the unshuttered windows — the house itself stood solid, roofed, untouched by a century of weather, which was its own quiet horror — firelight moved, and voices, and the ghost-smell of stew. A family. Four shapes around a table, passing dishes, laughing at something one of the children had said. The laughter came faintly through the glass, on a loop, the same laugh, the same beat of silence, the same laugh. Outside the door, half-visible in the failing light, stood a fifth shape, drenched in rain that wasn't falling anymore, hand raised to knock, hesitating, hesitating, hesitating.

"They never let it in," Sefa said. She had stopped the cart. She was staring at the door with an expression Lior couldn't read. "Or it never knocks. Either way, a hundred years of almost."

"Sefa—"

"Drive on," she said, to Turnip, and climbed back up beside the cart, and her face when she turned away was the borrowed-coat face again, slipping badly, and Lior filed it away the way they filed everything: carefully, in a place they'd find later.

In the late afternoon the road crested a long ridge, and there it was.

The Spire.

It stood on the horizon like a needle through the world's fabric, blacker than the starless sky behind it, and its shadow lay across the grey country for miles, pointing south, pointing at them, pointing at the city and everyone in it. Where the shadow fell, the replaying fields went silent. The burning woman of Harrowmere, Lior knew, was somewhere under that darkness now, kneeling in wheat that had finally, mercifully or terribly, stopped burning.

Maren stood up in the cart. Steadied herself on Sefa's shoulder. Looked at it with whatever was left of her sight.

"Well," she said. "There you are, old horror. Still hungry."

"You've seen it before?" Lior asked.

"I've seen its work before. Same thing, with that one." She sat back down. Her hands, Lior noticed, were trembling, and she noticed Lior noticing, and she folded them, and dared them with a raised eyebrow to say one word about it.

They made camp early, in the lee of the ridge, out of the shadow's reach — Maren was firm on that, out of the shadow's reach, *nothing sleeps in that dark, nothing wakes from it well* — and while Sefa saw to Turnip and the fire, Lior unwrapped the ledger and found, tucked into the binding where no tuck could have hidden it yesterday, a loose page.

Not a loose page. A new page. Fresh paper, guild-watermarked, in ink that was not quite dry.

Corvane's hand. But shakier than the margins, shakier than a man writing history, the hand of a man writing fast:

*To the keeper, if you live to read this. Vael has warrants and names, and names travel faster than carts. One of yours gave her a thread to pull — I don't know which thread, or which of yours, and I am sorry to hand you a knife you can't see. Trust the one who tells you the truth ugly rather than the one who tells it smooth. And keeper: when you stand before the Spire, remember that it does not want your guilt. Guilt is just another currency, and it has been paid in that coin for a century, and look what it bought. Bring it something else.*

Lior read it three times. Then looked up at Sefa, who was feeding the fire and not looking at them, whose face was doing the slipping thing, whose brother was somewhere in the refugee camps south of the city, whose bargain — if there was a bargain — Lior could now see the shape of the way you see a shape through fog: wrongly, but unmistakably.

"Sefa," Lior said. "When Vael interviewed you. Before the Guildhall was sealed. What did you tell her?"
