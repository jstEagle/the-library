## 15. Campfire Confessions

The fire was Sefa's idea. Not the fire itself—Maren had built that, with the brisk competence of a woman who had been lighting fires since before the road north had a name—but the *sitting around it*. The stopping.

"We can't walk all night," Sefa had said, when Lior had tried to push past the third milestone marker without so much as a glance at the darkening sky. "Even couriers sleep. Even couriers in a hurry. Especially couriers in a hurry, because tired people miss things, and right now missing things gets us killed."

Lior had opened their mouth to argue and then closed it again, which was becoming a habit Sefa didn't love. For three days now Lior had been doing this thing where they looked at her like she was a ledger entry they couldn't quite reconcile—a number in the wrong column—and then said nothing about it. Sefa knew what that look meant. She'd seen it on customs officers' faces a hundred times, right before they asked to see her papers.

So: fire. Stopping. Sitting.

Maren had produced tea from somewhere in her endless coat pockets, which was impossible, because they had packed light and Maren's pack contained, by Sefa's count, one blanket, one knife, and a jar of pickled something that nobody wanted to discuss. And yet there was tea. There was also bread, slightly stale, toasted on sticks like children at a festival, and for a little while the four of them—the courier, the keeper of accounts, the half-remembered hedge-witch, and the silent ex-inquisitor who sat apart from the fire with her sword across her knees—were just travelers on a cold road, and nothing worse.

It was during this little while that Sefa made her decision.

She watched Lior across the flames. They were turning their bread stick too slowly, letting one side char while the other stayed pale, and frowning at it as though the bread had personally failed them. Three days of that frown. Three days of Lior checking the road behind them, then checking *her*, then pretending they hadn't checked either. She was a courier. She knew what it was to carry something heavy and pretend your shoulders didn't ache.

"Right," she said, before she could talk herself out of it. "I have to tell you all something, and I'd rather do it before the bread runs out, because if you're going to hate me I want it to happen while I still have toast."

Lior looked up. Maren went very still, in the way of old women whose stillness is more alarming than other people's movement. Down the slope, Vael's head turned a few degrees—not enough to seem interested, exactly enough to be listening.

Sefa set down her stick. Her hands wanted to do something, so she made them stay flat on her knees.

"In the city," she said, "after Vael sealed the Guildhall—before we ran—I talked to her."

"You told me that," Lior said carefully. "You said you got past her. That you bought us time."

"I did buy us time." Sefa took a breath. It tasted like smoke and honesty, neither of which went down easy. "But not the way I said. I didn't slip past her. I traded with her. A guild secret, for my brother. Tam is in the refugee camps by the river gate—he came in with the second column from Thornhollow, he's sixteen, he's got a bad leg and no papers, and when the wardens started listing everyone in those camps for questioning, I—" Her voice did something embarrassing. She pushed through it. "I gave Vael the name of the person carrying the oldest ledger. I told her the guild's records hadn't burned. I pointed her straight at you, Lior. That's how I got Tam moved to the safe list. That's the bargain."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl asked its question and got no answer.

Lior had gone very quiet. Not the good kind of quiet, the thinking kind. The other kind.

"You pointed her at me," Lior repeated.

"Yes."

"And then you ran *with* me."

"Yes."

"So when the wardens nearly caught us in the tunnels—that was you. That was your trade catching up."

"That was my trade catching up," Sefa agreed. "Yes."

Lior stood up. The bread stick fell into the fire and they didn't even notice, which for Lior was a sign of true catastrophe; they had strong opinions about wasted food, born of apprentice years, and had once delivered a ten-minute lecture on the moral failures of crust-discarding.

"You let her *find* me," Lior said. "You sat across from me at that fire in the smuggler's rest and shared my blanket roll and the whole time you'd already sold me."

"I didn't sell you. I bought my brother."

"With *me* as the coin!"

"Yes!" Sefa was on her feet too now, though she couldn't remember standing. "With you as the coin, yes, that's exactly what it was, and I've been choking on it for three days, and I'm telling you now because I can't keep carrying it, but don't you dare stand there and act like you wouldn't have done the same!"

"I would not have—"

"You would have! You *did*!" Sefa jabbed a finger at the oldest ledger, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Lior's pack. "You stole a page out of Corvane's own book before any of this started. Before the stars went out. You broke guild law because you thought you had a right to know something, and you never once asked whether knowing it might burn down someone else's life. So don't lecture me about trading people for what we need. We've all been doing it. The whole city's been doing it for a hundred years. That's the entire *point* of the ledger you're hauling around!"

"That's different—"

"It is *exactly* the same and you know it!"

Lior's face had gone blotchy, the way it did when they were furious and losing. Sefa had seen it a dozen times over cards, over route assignments, over the great custard incident of last spring. Usually it ended with Lior storming off to sulk and returning two hours later with a peace offering and no apology, both parties pretending the fight had been about something smaller than it was.

But they weren't in the Guildhall kitchen now. There was nowhere to storm off to except the dark, and the dark had wardens in it, and the dark had the Spire's shadow creeping up the road ahead of them, and so Lior just stood there shaking, and Sefa stood there shaking, and between them the fire cracked and spat like a nervous audience.

"Sit down," Maren said.

Neither of them sat.

"*Sit down*," Maren said again, in the voice she must have used on apprentices for fifty years, the one that bypassed the ears entirely and gripped something older in the spine. They sat. Sefa found herself back on her log with no memory of the journey, which was frankly unsettling.

Maren leaned forward and fed another branch to the fire. Up close, in the flame-light, her eyes were terrible and kind—the clouded left one and the sharp right one, each looking at them from a slightly different distance.

"Now," she said. "Sefa has confessed. Lior has accused. Both of you are correct, which is the worst arrangement a quarrel can have. So we're going to do this properly." She pointed the stick at Lior. "You first. Say the true thing. Not the loud thing. The true thing underneath it."

Lior stared into the fire. Their jaw worked. When they finally spoke, the anger had drained out of their voice and left something much harder to hear.

"She's my friend," Lior said, to the flames. "She's the only person from the Guildhall I have left. Corvane's gone, the hall's sealed, everything I built my life on is ash or evidence. And I found out that the one piece of it I get to keep was already sold. I wasn't a friend to her. I was a debt she was paying." Their voice cracked on the last word. "I keep finding out I'm a debt. Everyone keeps *paying* me. It's the whole wretched book, isn't it? Everything costs somebody something, and I'm always the somebody holding the receipt."

The silence after that was long. Sefa discovered she was crying, quietly, in the undramatic way she'd learned as a child so that adults wouldn't notice and ask questions. She wiped her face with her sleeve and made herself say her part.

"The true thing under mine," she said, "is that I'd do it again." Lior flinched; she held up a hand. "No—let me finish, because it's worse than that. I'd do it again, and I'd feel exactly this bad, and I think that's what love actually costs. Not the feeling. The arithmetic. Tam is my brother. You're my friend. And when it came down to it I chose him, and I will never be sorry, and I will always be sorry, both at once, forever. That's the entry. That's what I owe *you* now—not an apology, because an apology would be a lie, it would mean I regret the choice. What I owe you is the rest of the road. Every mile of it. If Vael comes for you again, she goes through me first, and I don't mean that bravely, I mean it as a line item."

Lior looked at her for a long moment. The firelight moved on their face.

"A line item," they said at last, and something in their mouth twitched, despite everything, despite all of it. "Only you would apologize in accounting language."

"Only you would understand it."

"I hate that I understand it."

"I know. It's very annoying. I've suffered for years."

And then, horribly, Lior laughed—a wet, cracked, furious laugh, the kind that's mostly grief wearing a disguise—and Sefa laughed too, and for a moment the two of them sat there laughing and crying at each other across a dying fire like a pair of idiots, which is what they were, and Maren watched them with an expression of profound satisfaction, like a gardener watching rain finally arrive.

"I'm still angry," Lior said eventually. "I want that on the record. This doesn't fix it. I'm angry and I'm going to stay angry for a while and I reserve the right to bring this up at every possible opportunity for the rest of our lives."

"Noted," Sefa said. "I'll enter it in the book."

"Don't you dare enter it in the book."

"Too late. Mental ledger. Dated today. 'Lior, one (1), grudge, held in trust.'"

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Lior opened their mouth to argue, and then, visibly, decided against it, and instead reached over and took the last piece of toast off Sefa's stick, which was the closest thing to forgiveness either of them was going to manage tonight, and both of them knew it, and both of them accepted it.

Maren nodded slowly, as if a difficult patient had taken their medicine.

"Good," she said. "That's how it's done. Forgiveness isn't a feeling, children—it's a door you hold open while the feeling catches up. Sometimes the feeling takes years. Sometimes it never arrives at all, and you hold the door anyway, because the alternative is living in a house with no exits." She settled back against her pack. "I held a door open for twenty years once. Ask me sometime how that went."

"Did it go badly?" Sefa asked.

"It went honestly," Maren said, which was somehow worse, and she closed her eyes in a way that clearly announced the conversation was over.

They banked the fire and arranged the watches. Vael took first, as she always did—she slept least, and Sefa had stopped wondering why and started simply being grateful. As Sefa rolled into her blanket, she heard footsteps on the frost-brittle grass, soft and deliberate, and looked up to see Vael standing at the edge of the firelight, looking down at her.

"You told them," Vael said. It wasn't a question.

"You were listening."

"I'm always listening. It's my single most annoying quality." The ex-inquisitor crouched, forearms on her knees, her face half-lit and unreadable. "For what it's worth, courier: the information you sold me was accurate, timely, and delivered in good faith. By the standards of bargains I've witnessed, yours was almost honorable." She paused. "By the standards of the court I served, it was treasonous sentimentality. I find, lately, that I prefer the second standard."

Before Sefa could decide how to answer that, Vael straightened and turned toward the north, and her whole body changed—the stillness of a pointer dog, the stillness of someone reading a page written in a language only hunters know.

"But none of that matters tonight," she said quietly. "Look at the ridge line. North-northwest. Tell me what you see."

Sefa looked. The stars were still gone—had been gone for weeks, a black ceiling that pressed down on the world like a lid—but the darkness itself had texture tonight, and along the distant ridge, where the road bent toward the plains below the Spire, there was a glow. Faint. Wrong-colored. Not firelight. Firelight flickered. This light *breathed*, slow and vast, like something sleeping and dreaming of burning.

"What is that?" Sefa whispered.

Vael's hand had already found her sword hilt, an old habit, useless against whatever that was.

"That," she said, "is the shadow of the Spire moving faster than it should. And it's moving *toward* something." She glanced back down the dark road behind them, south, toward the city, and Sefa saw her do the arithmetic—the same grim, instant calculation Sefa had seen her make over maps and prisoner lists—and finish it with a face like stone.

"It's not moving toward the city," Vael said. "There's a refugee column on the north road. Two thousand souls, camped at the ford by morning." She was already reaching for her boots. "We have until dawn. Someone has to warn them—and I think I know exactly who the Spire's shadow will listen to."
