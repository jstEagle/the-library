## 16. The Warden's Dilemma

Inquisitor Vael had a theory about lies, and it was this: a lie was a door built in a hurry. It looked like every other door until you leaned on it, and then you felt the cheapness of the wood, the haste of the hinges, and you knew that whoever built it had expected you to knock and move along.

She had been leaning on doors all week.

The Guildhall sat sealed under her writ, its couriers dispersed to holding rooms, its ledgers stacked in her temporary office at the customs house in forty-one crates that smelled of ink and tallow and, faintly, of cinnamon — which Vael chose not to investigate, because a woman could only open so many doors in one day.

"Crates twelve through nineteen are account books," said Warden Pell, who was young, earnest, and built like a door himself — broad, dependable, slightly squeaky in damp weather. He set down his inventory slate. "Warden Osk says twenty through thirty are correspondence. And crate thirty-one is just... spoons."

"Spoons?"

"Guild spoons. There's a ledger *for* the spoons. It's very thorough. Each spoon has a name."

Vael pinched the bridge of her nose. "Of course it does. An organization that keeps accounts for its cutlery is an organization that keeps accounts for everything else. Put the spoons somewhere defensible. People get sentimental about named objects."

"Yes, Inquisitor." Pell hesitated at the door — a well-built door, she noted absently, no cheapness there. "The courier girl's been asking for you again. Sefa. She wants to know if her brother can have his blanket back."

"The blanket is evidence."

"It's a blanket, Inquisitor. It has holes in it."

"Evidence can have holes in it. That's often what makes it evidence." But she heard herself, and sighed. "Fine. The blanket goes back. Log it. If anyone asks, we confiscated it and returned it, which is the sort of thoroughness the court expects of us."

Pell went out looking pleased, as though he'd won something, and Vael returned to the crates.

* * *

Her method was simple and she was proud of it, in the way one is proud of a good knife. Every guild document passed through three questions. What does it say? What does it not say? And what would someone pay to keep me from reading it?

Most of the ledgers answered honestly, in their fussy way. Grain futures. A contract for bottled thunderstorms, six dozen, delivered quarterly. An invoice from a candlemaker whose wares burned only when lit by someone who missed the person they were lit for — Vael had read that one twice and decided it was either fraud or grief, and both were outside her jurisdiction.

It was in the correspondence — crate twenty-six, a bundle tied with court-green ribbon, which should itself have been the first alarm — that she found the letters.

There were four of them. All bearing the seal of the Chancellery of Borders and Wards. All addressed to Master Corvane. And all dated within the last eleven years.

She read the first one standing up. She read the second one sitting down. By the third, she had sent Pell for tea, because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

*Corvane — the surveyors' figures confirm your own. The anchor-wards lose coherence at a rate consistent with total failure within two decades, perhaps less if the Spire continues to draw as it does. The Crown is grateful for your discretion. Do not put any of this in writing again.*

That was nine years ago.

The second letter, five years old: *Your proposal for managed transition is received. The Treasury declines funding. Reiterate: nothing in writing.*

The third, two years old, and this was the one she read three times, because her eyes kept sliding off it the way eyes slide off a wound:

*The Crown acknowledges your warning regarding imminent collapse. The Crown further notes that the border provinces are, per the last census, a net drain on the treasury. Contingency planning proceeds on the assumption that the wards fail and that the resulting displacement will resolve certain long-standing demographic burdens. You will be compensated for silence. This letter will not exist.*

*This letter will not exist.*

Vael set it down very carefully, the way one sets down a sleeping animal that might bite.

She had served the court for nineteen years. She had taken the oaths — all three of them, including the third one, the quiet one, administered in a room without windows, where a woman with kind eyes had explained that an inquisitor's first loyalty was to truth, her second to the Crown, and that these would never conflict, because the Crown loved truth the way a father loves a child.

She had believed it. That was the humiliating part. Not that she'd been deceived — deception was the trade — but that she had walked into it with her eyes open and called it sight.

"They knew," she said aloud, to the empty office. "They knew for nine years and they did the arithmetic."

And the arithmetic came out the way arithmetic always comes out in chancelleries: the border folk were expensive, the dark would be inconvenient, and if a few thousand inconvenient people happened to be standing under the wards when the wards fell — well. Demographic burdens, resolved.

Pell came back with the tea and found her still sitting exactly where he'd left her.

"Inquisitor? Is it — should I fetch Osk?"

"No." She took the cup. Her hand was steady now; steadiness was a skill like any other, and she had practiced it longer than she'd practiced anything except lying politely. "Pell, a hypothetical. Suppose you discovered that the person who hired you had known about a danger for years — had *budgeted* for it — and had simply decided the people in its path were cheaper lost than saved. What would you do?"

Pell considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, including spoons. "I suppose I'd want to know why they decided that. Properly know. Not just the numbers version." He frowned. "Numbers lie easy. My mum always said you can make sums say anything if you're rude enough to them."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She's terrifying, Inquisitor. Wise and terrifying. Same thing mostly." He shifted. "Is this a real hypothetical?"

"It's becoming one."

* * *

She worked through the night, and by lamplight the letters stopped being a shock and became a pattern, which was worse. Patterns meant intention. Intention meant orders. Somewhere above her — above the whole apparatus of warrants and wardens and polite terrible methods she had once been proud of — someone had drawn a circle around the border provinces and written *acceptable losses* inside it, and then had gone to dinner.

Toward dawn she pulled the fourth letter, the oldest, and found something different in it. Not a dismissal. A question.

*Corvane — you claim the Spire can be fed. You claim memory will serve. The Crown requires assurance that whatever remedy you attempt will not reach the capital. Confirm.*

Fed. Memory. Remedy.

She thought of the courier girl, Sefa, who had traded a guild secret for her brother's safety and then cried about it in a holding room, thinking no one heard. She thought of the blank-faced villagers coming down from the fallen border in the refugees' accounts — smiling people who had forgotten their own names overnight. She thought of Lior Dravenmoor, the runaway ledger-keeper at the center of all of it, whom half her informants described as a thief and the other half described, confusingly, as *the only one doing the sums right.*

And she thought of the phrase in the third letter — *demographic burdens* — and understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like relief, what a chancellery meant when it spoke of remedies that must not reach the capital.

The Crown hadn't merely known the wards would fall.

The Crown had a plan for after. And the plan had a shape she recognized, because she had seen its prototype already, walking and smiling and nameless in the villages near the border.

"Pell," she said, when the light came up grey through the shutters. "Wake Osk. Wake everyone. Quietly."

He blinked at her. "Are we moving on something, Inquisitor?"

"We're moving on everything." She stood, and gathered the four letters, and held them a moment longer than she needed to — four doors, all of them cheap, all of them built in a hurry by people who had expected no one to lean. "Fetch me the courier girl too. The one with the brother. I think it's time someone in this city told me the truth, and I'm done waiting for it to come from above."

Pell hesitated. "Above being... the court?"

"Above being the sky, Pell. Everything above us has stopped answering." She tucked the letters inside her coat, against her heart, which was either sentiment or strategy and she declined to examine which. "We ride north at dusk. And Pell —"

"Yes, Inquisitor?"

"If anyone from the Chancellery arrives before we leave, they were never here. None of us were ever here." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "I've read enough guild ledgers to know how that particular entry is written."
