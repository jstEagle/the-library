## 17. The Village That Forgot Itself

The village of Harrow's Bend announced itself by smell before it announced itself at all: woodsmoke, bread, and something underneath both that I couldn't name—a sweetness like fruit left too long in the sun.

We had been walking north for six days along the old border road, following a map that Maren insisted on calling "a suggestion" and Sefa insisted on calling "a dare." The land had been strange the whole way—fields that flickered at the edges of vision, a river that ran backward for a hundred paces before thinking better of it—but nothing had prepared us for the bend in the road where Harrow's Bend sat in its little cup of hills, smoking its chimneys, baking its bread, and being entirely, catastrophically empty of anyone home behind its eyes.

"They're waving," Sefa said.

They were. A woman stood in her doorway with flour to her elbows, waving at us with the wholehearted enthusiasm of someone greeting family she couldn't quite place. Beside her, a man repaired a fence post with great diligence and no particular idea why. Children played a game in the lane that seemed to consist entirely of laughing, falling down, and starting over, as though each fall erased the last one too.

"Hello!" called the flour woman. "You've come! How lovely! Have you come?"

"We've come," Sefa said carefully.

"Wonderful," said the woman, beaming. She looked at us expectantly, then at the sky, then back at us, waiting for whatever came next in a conversation she clearly believed we were all having together.

I felt the hair rise on my arms. Not because anything was threatening. Because everything was *fine*. That was the horror of it—the village wasn't suffering. It was *content*. It was the happiest place I had ever seen, and it was happy the way an empty room is quiet.

"Maren," I said quietly. "What is this?"

Maren had gone very still. My teacher—half a teacher now, since the ford, since she'd spent decades of herself in one blinding spell to save us from Vael's wardens—stood in the road with her clouded eyes turned toward the village, and her mouth was a thin line.

She didn't answer me. She walked forward instead, past the fence-mender, past the children, straight up to the flour woman, and took her hands.

"Goodwife," Maren said. "Your name."

"Oh," said the woman, delighted, as though this were a charming game. "Yes. Isn't it funny? I keep reaching for it and it's just—*there*, isn't it?" She tapped her own chest. "Right there. Just out of reach. Like a cat under a bed."

"What did you have for breakfast?"

"Bread!" The woman laughed. "Or—I was going to make bread. Am making bread. There's bread somewhere in all this." She gestured at herself, at the flour, at the general concept of bread.

"And yesterday? What happened yesterday?"

The woman's smile faltered—not into fear, but into a kind of mild, sunny puzzlement, like someone asked to remember a dream. "Yesterday," she repeated fondly. "Wasn't there a yesterday? There must have been. There's always a yesterday." She patted Maren's hand. "You worry too much, dear. Whoever you are."

Maren let go of her hands and stepped back. Her face, in the grey light of the starless noon, was something I had never seen on it before: not grief exactly, and not fear, but the specific expression of a craftswoman recognizing another's work.

"I know this signature," she said.

* * *

We gathered them in the square—or rather, they gathered themselves around us, because the people of Harrow's Bend found us fascinating the way you find a traveling puppet show fascinating, and within an hour we had an audience of perhaps forty villagers who greeted us every few minutes as though for the first time.

Sefa handled it better than I did. She had spent years as a guild courier, which meant she had spent years talking her way past gate wardens who had already decided she was trouble, and she fell into it now with a patience that shamed me.

"So," she said to a boy of maybe ten, perched on the well's edge. "What's your name?"

"Dunno!" he said cheerfully. "Do you know it?"

"Not yet. But I will. What do you call yourself when your mum calls you in for supper?"

He considered this with enormous seriousness. "Supper!"

Sefa laughed, and the boy laughed, and I watched her file his face away in that courier's memory of hers—the one that never wrote anything down because it never needed to—and I understood she was doing arithmetic of her own: how many of these people were there, how long could this last, what happens to a child with no name when winter comes.

Because that was the thing none of the villagers could see, and all three of us could: this was not sustainable. Memory isn't just the past. It's the machinery of the future. A woman who can't remember she put bread in the oven will eventually stop putting bread in ovens. A fence-mender mends fences because he remembers the hole in the fence; take the remembering away and the fence stays broken forever, and so does everything else. The village was smiling its way off a cliff, one forgotten hour at a time.

"It's the Spire," Maren said.

We had retreated to the edge of the square, near the well, where an old dog lay sleeping. The dog, I noticed, still knew its own name—it came when Sefa whistled, tail thumping, answering to "Bramble" without hesitation. Animals, apparently, kept their accounts elsewhere.

"You're sure?" I asked. Stupid question. Maren was always sure. But I wanted her to say more than sure. I wanted her to say something that made it survivable.

"The signature's unmistakable." Maren lowered herself onto the well's rim, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet. "Memory-work leaves a hand behind it, the way penmanship does. Every hedge-witch has hers. Mine runs slanted, a little hurried. Yours—" she pointed a knuckle at me without looking, "—yours is careful to the point of apology. And the Spire's..." She searched for the word. "The Spire's is *vast*. No flourish at all. No personality. It doesn't take memory the way a thief takes a purse. It takes memory the way a field takes rain. Evenly. Everywhere. Without malice and without mercy, because it has never once occurred to it that there might be a difference."

I looked out at the square. The flour woman had started a second batch of bread, or possibly a ninth. Two men were shaking hands warmly, having just met, having evidently been neighbors for thirty years. A girl was trying to teach the game of falling down to Bramble the dog, who was too polite to explain he already knew it.

"How much has it taken?" I asked.

"All of it. Overnight, by the look. One clean sweep." Maren's voice went flat, which I had learned meant she was angrier than any raised voice could show. "This is what hunger looks like when it gets large enough. You don't gnaw anymore. You inhale."

"But the city—" I began. "Corvane said—"

"Corvane bought the city a century," Maren said. "Centuries end. The Spire has been starving since the night they took its heart, and starving things expand their territory. First the border villages. Then the nearer ones. Then—" she gestured north, toward the black line on the horizon where the Spire's shadow lived even in daylight, "—then everything inside its reach. And its reach grows every day it goes unfed."

The word *unfed* landed in my stomach like a stone. Because I knew what fed it. We all did, by then. Chapter by chapter, ledger line by ledger line, the oldest account book had taught us the shape of the debt: the Spire ate memory. That was its nature, its sustenance, the tax it had collected for a thousand years before anyone thought to steal from it. The wards had run on it. The stars had been kindled by it. And the guild, a century ago, had taken the heart that regulated the appetite, and the appetite had been wandering loose ever since, growing, reaching, sweeping through a village in the night while everyone slept—

"While everyone slept," I said aloud. "It didn't hurt them."

"No," Maren said.

"It could have. It could have taken the memories roughly. Torn them. Left them mad or empty or dead. Instead it—" I struggled with it. "It tucked them in. It took everything and left them *happy*."

"Yes," Maren said again, softer now. "That's the worst part. That's the part that should keep you up at night, apprentice. Cruelty you can fight. You can raise a hand against cruelty. But this—" she nodded at the square, at the laughter, at the bread, "—this is a famine wearing a festival mask. How do you raise a hand against a thing that loves you while it eats you?"

* * *

We stayed the night, because you cannot walk past forty nameless people and sleep soundly two fields away, however much wiser it would be.

Sefa organized them. That was the marvel of watching her work—she couldn't give them back their memories, but she could build scaffolding where memory used to stand. She went door to door with chalk and wrote names on lintels: *You live here. Your name is Aldous. Your wife is Petra. You love her; you'll notice it again tomorrow.* She set the bread-baking woman—Petra—to work with written instructions in her own hand, copied out fresh each morning: *Flour, water, salt, yeast. You have done this ten thousand times. Trust your hands.* By dusk the village smelled like competence again, if not quite like continuity.

I helped where I could, which was less than I wanted. My particular talents—ledger-keeping, cataloguing, the patient tending of impossible accounts—ran toward paper, and paper was useless here. These people couldn't hold a list overnight. So mostly I sat with them, and listened, and discovered something that unsettled me more than anything else that day.

They still had *skills*. Petra's hands knew bread the way Bramble knew his name—in the fingers, below the flood line. The fence-mender, whose name was Aldous, could splice a rail blindfolded. An old man by the church wall whittled perfect little birds without once looking at what he was doing, and when I asked him about it he said, "Aren't they funny? I don't know where they come from," and held one out to me with pure delight, a gift from a stranger to himself.

"The deep strata survive," Maren murmured beside me. She had found a bench and a portion of stew, and was eating with the grim efficiency of someone refueling a machine. "Habit outlasts recollection. Craft lives lower than memory does. Which tells you something, if you're the sort who thinks about such things."

"Which is?"

"That what the Spire takes sits high. Near the surface. Names, faces, yesterdays, the story you tell yourself about yourself." She scraped her bowl. "And what it leaves sits low. The hands. The heart's older habits." Her clouded eyes turned toward me, and even half-blind, even hollowed by the ford, she could still pin me like a specimen. "Which means, apprentice, that the Spire doesn't eat *people*. It eats *accounts*. It eats the record. The person underneath is still there, holding a whittled bird, wondering where it came from."

I thought about my own erased memories—the years Maren's magic had taken from me before I ever ran from her, the gaps I hadn't known were gaps until the ledger showed me their shapes. I thought about the day I'd learn, in some chapter yet unwritten, what those years contained.

"Maren," I said. "When the Spire took this village. Did it ask first?"

The silence stretched long enough that the whittling man finished a bird.

"No," she said finally. "It doesn't know how. That's not a defense, Lior. Don't go building one for it. Hunger that doesn't ask is still theft—it's just theft with no thief inside it to blame." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "But mark this, because it matters: a thing that steals without a thief can be *taught*. If there were ever a thief in it, long ago, before the heart was taken—if there was wanting, and choosing, and regret buried down in the deep strata where the habits live—then it might learn differently. Given time. Given a teacher patient enough to sit with a monster and read to it."

"That sounds like a terrible plan."

"It's the only kind I've ever had," said Maren, and went to check on Petra's evening loaves.

* * *

Near midnight, I couldn't sleep, and went out into the square.

The starless sky hung overhead like a held breath. I had almost stopped noticing the absence, which frightened me in its own small way—how quickly a person adjusts to a world with no stars, how the dark becomes furniture. In Harrow's Bend, with no city glow to compete with, the loss was total. The sky was a page with nothing written on it. I stood there feeling like a clerk staring at a blank ledger, knowing the entries existed once, knowing they'd been struck through by something vast and unhurried, and not knowing how to un-strike them.

"Lior."

Sefa, wrapped in her courier's cloak, came to stand beside me. She smelled of woodsmoke and chalk dust. For a while neither of us spoke. Below us, in the houses, forty people slept without dreaming, or dreamed without keeping, and the village breathed around us like something anesthetized.

"They smiled all day," Sefa said at last. "All day, Lior. I wrote 'your name is Marta' on a doorframe today and the woman inside thanked me and asked if I'd stay for supper, and she was *glad*, genuinely glad, and tomorrow morning she'll read that lintel like a stranger reading someone else's house." Her jaw tightened. "Vael hunts us with warrants and wardens, and honestly? Some days the wardens feel more honest than this. At least cruelty knows what it's doing."

"Maren says it's not cruelty. It's famine."

"Famine killed my grandmother's whole village, so forgive me if I don't find that comforting." Sefa pulled her cloak tighter. Then, quieter: "There were children playing that falling-down game. Did you see? They'd laugh, and fall, and get up, and it was like each one was the first time. Every single time was the first time." She shook her head slowly. "I keep thinking—that's the most joyful thing I've seen since the stars went out. And it's the most horrible. Both at once. In the same image. How am I supposed to carry both of those?"

"You don't carry one," I said. "You carry both. That's the job now, I think. Corvane carried only the horror for a century and it made him do monstrous things gently. Somebody out there is carrying only the joy right now, and it's letting the Spire eat a village a night without anyone raising an alarm." I looked up at the blank sky. "The ledger taught me that. Every entry has two columns. You have to read both or you haven't read anything."

Sefa snorted. "Listen to you. 'Every entry has two columns.' You've been spending too much time with that book."

"There's no such thing," I said, and she laughed, and the laugh rose into the starless dark and vanished there, unrecorded, and I felt the loss of it like a draft under a door.

That was when Maren came out of the inn, moving fast for a half-blind woman in the dark, and I knew before she spoke that something had changed.

"It moved," she said. "An hour ago. The hunger moved on."

"On? Where?"

"North-and-east. Toward the next village—Thistleford, if the maps still mean what they say." She gripped my arm with surprising strength. "Lior, listen to me. It swept this place clean in a single night, and now it's moving on *unsatisfied*. Do you understand what that means? A fed appetite rests. This one fed on an entire village and is already hunting again. It's accelerating. Whatever's driving it—starvation, panic, the wound where its heart used to be—it's getting worse, and it's getting worse *fast*, and at the rate it's spreading—"

She stopped. I finished it for her, because I was the keeper of accounts, and arithmetic is what I'm for.

"At the rate it's spreading," I said, "it reaches the city in weeks."

Nobody said anything. Above us the blank sky went on being blank, patient as an unpaid bill. Somewhere north-and-east, in the dark, something vast and wounded was turning toward Thistleford, and after Thistleford a dozen villages after that, and after them the walls of the city where Corvane sat with his century of borrowed time running out beneath him.

"We leave at first light," I said. "Ahead of it. And Maren—when we get wherever we're going, I need the oldest ledger. All of it. Every column. Because if the Spire eats memory, and the debt is denominated in memory, then somebody wrote those terms down once. And I want to know exactly what we owe, and to whom, and—" I heard my own voice steady itself, clerk-calm over the terror, "—and whether there's a clause anywhere in that contract for mercy."

Maren squeezed my arm once and let go.

"Then you'd best sleep," she said. "Ledgers wait for no one. Especially the impossible ones."
