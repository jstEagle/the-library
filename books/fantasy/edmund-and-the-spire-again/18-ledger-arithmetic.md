## 18. Ledger Arithmetic

The inn at Twoford had one good table, and Lior claimed it before the fire had even been lit, spreading the oldest ledger across its scarred oak like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"Should we be worried," Sefa said, dropping into the chair opposite, "that you look like that when you open a book? My brother used to make that face at his dinner, right before he ate the whole plate and mine too."

"Hush. I'm thinking."

"You're squinting. There's a difference."

There was, actually, and Lior resented that Sefa had learned to tell them apart in under a week. Squinting meant the light was bad. Thinking meant the numbers were bad, and the numbers had been bad for three days now.

The ledger lay open across the spread of pages they'd been copying since the village of Hollowmere — *the village that had forgotten itself*, though nobody said that aloud anymore, the way nobody said a dead man's name in a house where the widow was still sewing. Maren sat by the window with her hands folded in her lap, doing the thing she did now, which was being present in the way furniture is present: warm, solid, and not entirely sure who had put her there.

Lior dipped the pen. Began again.

The arithmetic was simple. That was the horror of it. Anyone could do it. A child with a slate could do it. It was only the conclusion that required a grown-up's stomach.

Here was what the old ledger recorded, in its crabbed century-old hand:

The Spire had a heart. The guild had borrowed it. The debt carried interest.

The interest was not paid in coin. The ledger was quite clear on this, in the way that old account books are clear about the things their writers least wanted to write. *Interest, per annum, remitted in kind.* And then, in a column Lior had at first taken for a scribe's error — a column of entries that looked like weather reports, or crop tallies, or nothing at all:

*Remitted: the summer of 843, as held by the weavers of Duncastle.*
*Remitted: the name of the river at Ferrow's Crossing.*
*Remitted: forty years, more or less, belonging to the miller of Harrow Fen.*

More or less. The ledger said *more or less*. As if memory were grain that could be heaped carelessly on a scale, as if a man's forty years were a sack with a hole in it.

And the entries had not stopped a century ago. They ran the whole length of the book, page after page, year after year, all the way up to the last entry before the wards fell — and then they stopped, because the heart was gone, and a creditor who has been robbed does not accept payment. A creditor who has been robbed comes to collect.

Lior did the sums the way you pull a tooth: quickly, and looking slightly away.

The Spire had been fed on a trickle — a summer here, a river's name there, the slow sipping of a thing kept politely hungry. The wards had stood because the debt was being serviced. Then Corvane had taken the heart, and the payments had stopped, and the Spire had begun to starve in earnest, and a starving thing does not sip.

Hollowmere had been its first mouthful. A village of two hundred souls, emptied overnight, smiling and blank. Lior had stood in the village square and watched a woman pat her own door as if greeting it for the first time, and had felt the arithmetic turn over in their gut like a stone.

Two hundred lives' worth of memory. One meal.

The Spire was a hundred miles north. It had no legs. It had no need of legs. It was a tower, and towers do not walk — but its *hunger* moved. Lior had charted it, the way you chart a tide: the blank places spreading down from the border, village by village, each one a little closer to the city. Hollowmere. Then the hamlet past it, which they'd found empty of everything but bread still warm in ovens. Then the shepherd camps on the high pasture, where the dogs still herded sheep on instinct and the shepherds stood in fields wondering, pleasantly, whose sheep they were.

The hunger was moving south at — Lior checked the figures, checked them again, hoped briefly for a mistake, found none — at a rate of perhaps four miles a day, and accelerating. Starvation accelerates. Any ledger-keeper knows that debts compound fastest at the end.

Four miles a day. The city was ninety miles south.

Lior set down the pen.

"Sefa."

"Mm." Sefa was cleaning her boots with a intensity that suggested she was not thinking about her brother, who was somewhere behind them in the refugee columns, and was therefore thinking about nothing else.

"When did we pass the burned-out mill?"

"Two days back. Why?"

"And the village after it — the one with the well and the terrible carved goose — when was that?"

"Yesterday morning. Lior, why?"

"Because the mill is thirty miles from the city," Lior said, "and the goose village is twenty-six, and the blank places are following the road."

Sefa stopped polishing. "Following the road."

"Roads are where the memories are thickest. Where people have walked the most, named the most things, argued about the most turnips." Lior turned the ledger around so she could see the map they'd drawn in the margins, the little inked dots of the villages, the spreading stain of the blank places creeping southward like ink through wet paper. "It's not wandering. It's *foraging*. It's following the richest food."

Sefa looked at the map for a long moment. "How long," she said, "do we have?"

Lior had the number already. Had had it since midnight, when they'd worked it out by candlelight and then sat very still for a long time listening to the inn's other guests laughing over their supper.

"Twenty-three days," Lior said. "Nineteen if the acceleration holds. Which it will. Debts compound, Sefa. That's the whole nature of a debt. It doesn't wait politely. It grows."

* * *

Maren spoke from the window without turning around. "You've worked it out, then."

It was not a question. It was also, Lior had noticed, the first full sentence she'd offered in a day and a half, and both of them went quiet to let it stand.

"Worked what out?" Sefa asked.

"What the payments were." Maren's hands unfolded, refolded. Her eyes, clouded as they were, seemed to be looking at something a long way past the glass. "I told you memory-magic has a price. Every spell paid for with the caster's own past. I told you that, and you were angry about it, and you were right to be." A pause. "But I only ever spent what was mine. I was a hedge-witch. Small debts, small interest. What do you imagine a *tower* pays with, when it owes?"

Lior's mouth was dry. "It doesn't pay. It's owed."

"No." Maren turned at last, and there was something in her ruined face that was almost the old sharpness, almost the woman who had once told a runaway apprentice *you'll remember why* and meant it as a kindness. "That's what I thought too, when I was young and clever. But look at the ledger again. Look at the wording. Not *taken*. *Remitted*. Not *stolen*. *Held in trust*." She tapped the air, as though tapping the page across the room. "A debt runs both directions, child. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes. The guild owed the Spire. And the Spire—" her voice caught on something, "—the Spire owed *us*. The wards. The anchored stars. The border. It held all of that in trust, and it paid its interest in the only coin it had, which was the memory of everything it protected. And when we took its heart—"

She stopped. The sentence sat there unfinished, and everyone in the room could finish it themselves.

When we took its heart, we took the coin it paid its debts with. We took its means of keeping its side of the bargain. And a thing that cannot pay its debts does not simply fade, the way a merchant does. A thing that cannot pay its debts gets *hungry*.

"It's not attacking the city," Lior said slowly. The idea was assembling itself in their chest with the awful click of a ledger balancing. "It's *collecting*. It's coming to collect."

"And the only currency it accepts," Maren said, "is memory."

The fire crackled. Somewhere downstairs, the innkeeper's wife was singing to her children, a song about a fox and a moon, and Lior thought: *she has sung that song every night for years, and the Spire would take it, and her children's faces with it, and the smell of the bread and the name of the fox and the reason she sings at all — and it would call that interest remitted, and it would be correct, and it would be monstrous, and both of those things would be true.*

"How much does it need?" Sefa asked. She was the practical one. She was always the practical one, and Lior loved her for it the way you love a rope when you're falling. "To stop. To be full. Whatever the word is. How much?"

Lior turned back to the ledger. This was the part they'd been circling since midnight, the column they'd measured three times, because the first two times they'd hoped they'd misread it.

The old entries recorded what the Spire had been paid in the years before the theft. A summer here. A name there. A miller's forty years. Small sips, politely taken — or politely *given*, Lior now noticed, because the entries were signed. Each one. Signed by the person whose memory it was, in a shaky hand, with a formula beside it that Lior had taken for a blessing and now understood was a *receipt*: *Given freely, of my own account, for the warding of the border and the keeping of the stars.*

Given freely.

The Spire had never taken. Not once, in a hundred years of entries. It had been *given to*, the way you give to a hearth, the way you give to a god — and then the guild had stolen its heart, and it had lost the ability to receive gifts, and all it could do now was the thing starving things do.

And the amount. The amount was the terrible part.

Lior worked it aloud, because saying it made it worse and worse was honest. "The old payments averaged — call it one village's worth of memory every few years. Small. A tithe. But that was maintenance, that was interest on a healthy debt. It's had a century of arrears now, compounded, plus the principal — the heart itself, which it counts as stolen, which means the whole debt has come due at once." The pen moved. The number came out. Lior looked at it and felt the floor tilt. "To stop the hunger — not to be satisfied, just to *stop* — it needs the memory of a city. Not a village. A city. Everyone in it. Everything they are. Or the equivalent, spread over time, but we don't have time, so—"

"So it reaches the walls in nineteen days," Sefa said, "and if we haven't fed it, it eats Niniva down to the cobbles."

"And if we feed it," Lior said, "we feed it *everything*."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that settles on the shoulders.

Sefa broke it, because Sefa always broke it. "There's a third option. There's always a third option. We run. We take the ledger and we run south past the city, past everything, and—"

"And it follows the roads," Lior said gently. "And roads are made of people. There is no *away*, Sefa. Not from a creditor. I've spent three years learning that in the guild's own books. A debt doesn't stop at a border. It just changes whose problem it is."

"Then what?" Sefa's voice cracked, and underneath the crack was her brother, somewhere in the refugee columns, whose face she was not thinking about. "What's the answer, Lior? Because I've been carrying this book for you through two storms and one ambush, and I'd like to know what it's *for*."

And Lior honestly did not know. That was the truth of it. They had the arithmetic and the arithmetic had no mercy in it. Nineteen days. A city's worth of memory. A creditor that could not be fought, because you cannot fight a debt; a creditor that could not be paid, because the coin was people.

What Lior knew — the only thing they knew, the small hard seed at the bottom of all the dread — was what Maren had just shown them. The signatures. *Given freely.* Every old entry, signed. The Spire had once lived on gifts.

Something had broken that. Something had turned giving into hunger. And whatever it was, it was in this ledger, in the century-old hand, in the entries Lior hadn't reached yet — the ones from the year of the theft, the ones Corvane had said *not yet* about, a lifetime ago, in a guildhall that felt like it belonged to someone else's story.

"I don't know," Lior said. "But the answer's in here. The year of the theft. The entries Corvane wouldn't let me read." They looked down at the ledger, at the crabbed hand waiting for them like a door. "And I think — I think we need to know who did this. Not the guild. A *person*. Someone signed the theft, same as the gifts were signed. Someone's name is in this book."

Maren made a small sound. Lior looked up. The old woman's clouded eyes had gone wide, and her hands were pressed flat to the table as though the room had tilted for her too.

"I knew him," she said. "The one who did it. I knew him when he was young." Her voice was very quiet. "He was kind. That's the thing nobody ever believes. He was *kind*, and he thought he was being merciful, and—" She stopped, and her face did something complicated, grief and recognition folding into each other. "You'll meet him, child. Sooner than you think. He's been walking toward this his whole life."

Lior looked down at the ledger. Turned the page. And there, at the top of the entries from the year of the theft, in a young man's careful, hopeful, terrible hand, was the signature — and the name beneath it was one Lior had known for three years, had dodged moods for, had hidden stolen pages from, had trusted.

The name was Corvane.
