## 19. Corvane's Century

The ink was still wet on the first entry when the world was young enough to be argued with.

That was how Aldous Corvane liked to remember it afterward — not as theft, not as treason, but as an argument. A very long argument, conducted over a hundred years, in which he had said his piece and the Spire had never once had the courtesy to reply.

He was twenty-three years old, and his hands shook so badly that the first line of the ledger came out looking like a spider had drowned in it. *Entry the first: one heart, received in trust.* He crossed it out. Wrote it again. Crossed it out again.

"You're going to ruin the page," said Wren, who was leaning against the doorframe of the counting room with her arms folded and her sword — a borrowed sword, she kept insisting, though no one had ever asked for it back — hanging at her hip like an accusation.

"The page is the least of what I'm ruining," Corvane said.

"Good. Then keep writing."

* * *

They had been four, that night on the grey field below the Spire. Four young people with a plan that should not have worked and did, which is the most dangerous kind of plan there is.

Corvane had been the youngest, and the only one who could read Old Ledger Script, which was why he was there at all. The others had talents. Wren could walk through wards without waking them — she called it "being forgettable," and she was right about herself in a way she would spend the rest of her life being wrong about everything else. Tam Ossler knew the Spire's old servant tunnels from his grandfather's stories, back when the tower had still let people in to sweep its floors. And Maren —

Maren had been nineteen, and already the finest hedge-witch in three counties, and she had looked at the black tower on the horizon and said, quietly, the way she said all her most frightening things: "It's starving. Can't you feel it? It's been starving since the war started, and nobody's fed it, and now it's going to start feeding itself."

They were standing in the ruins of Harrow's Ford at the time. Or rather, they were standing where Harrow's Ford had been, because three days earlier the enemy host had come through it, and when they passed, they did not burn the village. They simply walked through it, and the village forgot itself. Doors stood open. Bread sat half-eaten. A hundred and forty people wandered the lanes smiling at strangers, unable to say their own names, and the enemy had moved on without a single sword drawn, because you cannot fight someone who cannot remember you are their enemy.

"They've found a way to feed it," Tam had whispered. "The other side. They're feeding the Spire memories to make it eat *us*."

"No," Maren said. "They're starving it toward us. There's a difference, and it matters."

It mattered. Corvane would spend a century learning exactly how much it mattered, and by then the lesson would be written in other people's blood, which was always the most expensive way to take notes.

* * *

The plan, as plans went, was almost embarrassingly simple. The Spire anchored the border wards. The border wards held back the transformed host — the enemy who had once been men and women of the northern kingdoms before the first war taught them what hunger could do to a soul. And the Spire was hungry, and hunger made it cruel, and cruelty made it a weapon anyone could aim.

So they would steal its heart. Not destroy it — Maren had been very firm on this point, firm enough that Wren had joked about gagging her — but hold it. Keep it safe. Feed it slowly, carefully, on small and willing things, until the war ended and they could give it back.

"That's not stealing," Maren had said. "That's borrowing. There's a difference, and it matters."

"There's that word again," Wren said.

"It's the only word worth saying twice."

And Corvane, twenty-three years old and certain that ledgers could hold anything if you wrote the entries honestly enough, had opened his book and written: *One heart, received in trust.*

He believed it. That was the thing no one understood later, when they called him monster and miser and the man who sold the sky. He had *believed* it. He had believed that a debt honestly recorded was a promise, and that promises were stronger than wars, and that four young fools with a plan could hold a tower's heart in trust the way you'd hold a neighbor's ox while their barn burned down.

The Spire had let them in.

That was the part that haunted him longest, in the end. Not the wards parting for Wren, not the tunnels opening under Tam's grandfather's keys, not even the heart itself — a thing like a lantern full of slow golden weather, beating once every nine seconds, each beat a star being thought into place. No. What haunted him was that the great black tower, older than kingdoms, hungry past all sanity, had looked at four children climbing its ribs with knives and good intentions —

— and had *let them*.

He had written that down too, that night, in the margin, in a hand steadier than he'd ever managed before or since: *It wanted us to take it. Why?*

He would find out in ninety-nine years, from a runaway apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a stolen page. But that night he only felt the question settle into him like a splinter, and he told himself it was nothing, and he was twenty-three, and he believed himself.

* * *

The forgetting was Maren's idea.

Not the theft — the *forgetting*. Because here was the problem none of them had foreseen: a heart held in trust still beat, and a beating heart still needed feeding, and the only food it took was memory. They tried everything else first. Corvane had fed it gold — actual gold, coins melted down, a fortune — and the heart had swallowed it and beaten on, unimpressed, like a cat presented with a turnip. Tam had tried songs. Wren had tried, in a moment of desperation Corvane never stopped finding funny, a very good pie.

"It wants remembering," Maren said. "That's all it's ever wanted. It holds up the stars, do you understand? Every night it has to think ten thousand of them into place, and it's been doing it alone since before there were names for things, and we climbed up its insides and took the only part of it that gets to rest." She looked at the heart, turning slowly in its cradle of iron and oak in the guild cellar, and her face was terrible with pity. "We didn't steal a treasure. We stole its *nap*."

"So we give it back," Tam said.

"And the war starts again tomorrow," Wren said. "The moment it's whole, it's a weapon again, and the north will come for it, and this time they'll know exactly where it sleeps."

So Maren proposed the mercy. Feed it forgetting. Give it, not memories, but the *absence* of them — the soft grey wool of things unremembered, which she swore was a kind of food, the way sleep is a kind of food, the way rest is. She built the working herself. She tested it on herself first, as she tested everything, giving up the name of her first pony, then her mother's lullaby, then the taste of plums, small bright coins paid into the dark.

And it worked. Oh, it worked beautifully. The heart slowed. The hunger quieted. The wards steadied. And the war —

The war ended. Not with a battle. With a *yawn*. The transformed host reached the border one grey morning and found the wards singing calm instead of fear, and something in them — some scrap of the men and women they'd been — heard it, and faltered, and turned aside. Not home. Nowhere was home anymore. But away. Away was enough.

"We did it," Wren said, and laughed, and cried, and got drunk on Tam's terrible plum brandy, and Corvane sat in the corner writing the entry — *War, ended; cost, see attached* — and feeling the splinter in his chest turn over.

Because he had done the arithmetic. Someone always had to. The heart ate forgetting, and forgetting had to come from somewhere, and Maren could not pay forever. So they had widened the working. Made it general. A gentle fog over the borderlands, a century-long anesthesia, everyone sleeping soundly while the guild kept the accounts.

Everyone forgot the war. Everyone forgot the Spire. Everyone forgot what the enemy had been and why the towers stood empty along the marches, and it was peaceful, it was so peaceful, the peace of a sickroom after the fever breaks and the patient stops asking questions.

"Mercy," Maren had called it, the day she cast it, and her voice had cracked in the middle of the word like a plate.

Corvane had written: *Mercy, administered. One dose, ongoing.* And in the margin, smaller: *Why does it feel like lying?*

* * *

They died the way good people die — badly timed and inconveniently.

Tam went first, sixty years in, falling down his own stairs with a ledger under his arm, and Corvane buried him and recorded it and found he could not make the entry balance, because there was no column for what Tam Ossler had been worth. He invented a column. He used it only twice more.

Wren lasted eighty years, forgettable to the last, and died in her sleep in a house no tax collector had ever managed to find. At her funeral, Corvane realized with a cold horror that he could not remember her face — only that she had leaned on doorframes, and that her laugh had sounded like a gate swinging. The fog had taken her from him years before it took her body, and he had let it, because letting it was easier, and ease was the whole rotten architecture of the mercy.

Maren lasted longest, because she had always known the price and paid it with her eyes open. She came to the guildhall less and less, a small bent woman with a walking stick and eyes like wells, and the last time she came she stood in front of the ledger for a long time, reading the century of entries, and then she said:

"You know it's going to run out."

"I know."

"A hundred years is a hundred years, Aldous. Interest comes due. It always comes due." She tapped the oldest page, the one with his drowned-spider handwriting. "And when it does, whatever's left of that tower is going to be *hungry*, and it isn't going to remember why it ever agreed to wait."

"I'll leave instructions," Corvane said. "I'll leave the accounts in perfect order. Whoever keeps the ledger after me will —"

"Will what?" Maren asked, gently, terribly. "Understand? Child, I barely understand, and I was *there*." She looked at him a long moment, and then she did something she had not done in forty years: she touched his face, with a hand light as paper. "You were the best of us, Aldous. That's the tragedy. The best of us always thinks a debt is the same thing as forgiveness."

She left. He watched her go down the street, getting smaller, and he understood that he would not see her again, and he was right.

He wrote the entry anyway. He always wrote the entry. *Maren, departed. Accounts left in trust. Trust, definition of: see attached, see attached, see attached.*

* * *

Now.

Now he was an old man in a sealed guildhall with smoke rising from a brazier full of burning ledgers, and the current year's accounts curling into ash with a smell like burnt sugar, and in his hands, wrapped in oilcloth, the oldest book in the world.

Outside, somewhere beyond the walls, Inquisitor Vael's wardens were hammering on doors. Somewhere far to the north, a girl named Lior Dravenmoor — thief of pages, keeper of debts, the best apprentice he had ever ruined — was running toward or away from something, and Corvane had placed his entire final wager on *toward*.

He set the oilcloth bundle on the table. He opened the book to the first page, to the drowned-spider handwriting, and he read aloud, to the empty room, to the hundred years, to whoever came next:

"*Entry the first: one heart, received in trust.*"

His voice caught. He let it. At his age, dignity was a luxury item and he had never been able to afford it.

"I'm sorry," he told the book. "I told you it was trust. It was theft with good manners. It was theft with *arithmetic*. And the interest —" He laughed, or something near it. "You've kept better accounts than I ever did. Villages. Names. A hundred and forty people at Harrow's Ford who can't say who they are. You've been invoicing us all along, haven't you? Quietly. Patiently. Waiting for someone to read the whole column."

He dipped his pen. His hand, a hundred years steadier than it had been at twenty-three, did not shake at all.

On the flyleaf, beneath the title, he wrote:

*Lior —*

*Carry the debt. Not the guilt — the debt. Guilt is looking backward; a debt is a promise facing forward. Read every entry. Aloud, if you can manage it. Some things only hear you when you say them out loud.*

*And when it asks you what it's owed — and it will ask, child, it has been asking for a century — don't pay it. Don't refuse it either. Do what no one in this book ever had the nerve to do.*

*Rename the account.*

*— A.C., keeper, retiring*

He closed the book. Wrapped it. And because he was an old man who had spent a lifetime believing that honest entries were promises, he slipped out the postern gate into the smoke, and started walking north along the river road, toward the ford, toward the grey field, toward the advancing dark — carrying nothing but his pen, his empty ledger, and the last unpaid entry of his life.

Behind him, the city bells began to ring alarm. Ahead of him, on the road to Harrow's Ford, he could just make out, in the failing light, a small party of travelers hurrying south — a courier, a blind old witch, and a figure with ink-stained fingers carrying a book like a shield.

Corvane smiled, adjusted his collar against the wind, and prepared, at last, to introduce himself properly to his own legacy.

He did not yet see the wardens' torches kindling in the trees behind them.
