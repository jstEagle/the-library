## 20. Ambush at the Ford

Sefa had crossed the Greywater Ford a dozen times in her career as a guild courier, and every one of those crossings had been boring, which was the highest compliment a courier could pay a river. Boring meant alive. Boring meant the wards held and the roads were quiet and the only thing you had to fear was a wet boot.

This crossing was not going to be boring. She could feel it in the way the horses kept stopping to stare at the water.

"It's just a river," she told her mare, Bristle, who gave her the flat, unconvinced look that Bristle reserved for lies, and for Sefa's singing, which were apparently the same category.

The ford itself was beautiful in the wrong way. The Greywater ran shallow and wide here, silver-grey over pale stones, and on the far bank the reeds stood perfectly still in a morning that had no wind in it. No stars last night, of course. No stars for weeks. The sky was just a great grey lid, and everything underneath it had the flat, held-breath quality of a room where someone has just said something unforgivable.

Behind her, the party was doing what the party always did, which was argue.

"All I'm saying," Lior called from the second horse, "is that if we're going to be ambushed, we could at least be ambushed somewhere with cover."

"You can't schedule an ambush," Sefa said. "That's the entire point of them."

"You could if you were the guild. The guild has a ledger for everything. There's probably a column for *scheduled ambushes, quarterly*."

"Master Corvane did once bill a man for a betrayal," Maren said mildly, from the back, where she rode wrapped in three blankets despite the fact that it was, by the standards of a world without stars, not cold. "Charged him for the knife and the apology both."

Sefa smiled, and then stopped smiling, because Maren's voice had that papery quality it had carried ever since the village that forgot itself. Ever since Maren had stood in the middle of that blank, smiling square and said, in a whisper, *oh, it's starving, poor thing, poor hungry thing* — and then gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with age.

Three days now. Three days of Maren being *mildly* everything. Mildly tired, mildly hungry, mildly amused. Sefa had met people who were mildly everything before. They were usually people who had stopped having strongly anything.

Lior rode up beside her and lowered their voice. "She's worse today. She called me 'the girl' this morning. I'm not—" Lior's jaw worked. "I'm not the girl. I'm her apprentice. Was."

"I know."

"She looked right at me and asked if I was the daughter of someone called Tam."

"Tam was her sister's husband," Maren said, from behind them, perfectly clearly.

They both jumped. Horses are bad at secrets; Bristle sidestepped and nearly put Sefa in the reeds.

"I remember Tam," Maren went on, and there was something almost gentle in it, almost apologetic. "I remember him fine. It's the middle I lose. The middle of things. I remember the beginning of a thought and not the end. I remember your face, girl—*Lior*—and not your name, and then your name and not your face, and it swaps about. Memory's not a wall with bricks falling out. It's more like—" she searched for it, and Sefa watched her search, and hated it— "like a ledger with damp in it. Some pages blot. Some run."

"You'd know," Lior said, and it came out sharper than they meant, and Maren took it the way she took everything now, mildly, like rain on stone.

The ford was fifty feet ahead when Bristle refused.

Not balked — refused. Stopped dead, ears flat, and would not be persuaded by heel, rein, or the soothing nonsense voice Sefa had perfected over nine years of courier work. Ahead of them, Lior's horse did the same. Maren's old grey just stood there trembling, which was somehow worse.

Sefa's mouth went dry. Animals were never wrong about this particular thing. That was the first lesson of the courier lines: trust the horse, distrust the road, and never, ever take a contract where both disagree.

"Off," she said quietly. "Everyone off. Now."

Lior was already dismounting, hand on the ledger-bag slung across their chest — the old ledger, the one Corvane had pressed into their arms through smoke, *carry the debt*. Maren slid down with a creak and stood looking at the reeds with her head tilted, the way she did when she was listening to something.

"They're in the reeds," Maren said.

"How many?" Sefa's hand was on her knife.

"All of them."

* * *

The wardens came up out of the reedbeds on both banks at once, and Sefa had to give them this: it was a beautiful ambush. Twelve of them, maybe fourteen, grey-cloaked, moving with the drilled silence of people who had done this on purpose before. Vael's wardens. Vael's *polite and terrible* people, except that today there was nothing polite in the set of their shoulders, because Vael wasn't with them, and Sefa had learned on the road that a warrant without Vael behind it was just a knife with extra paperwork.

"Warden-Commander Ossory!" one of them called, a broad woman with a voice like a door slamming. "By authority of the court, stand and yield the ledger and the witch!"

"The witch," Maren repeated, to no one, sounding faintly delighted. "It's been thirty years since anyone called me a witch to my face. They usually go for *hedge-crone*."

"Maren." Lior's voice was thin. "Maren, please."

"Yield the ledger," Ossory called again, "and no one is hurt. The court only wants the accounts."

"The court," Lior called back, and Sefa heard them find their spine somewhere around the second word, "burned the current accounts. The court *seized* the Guildhall. The court wants the old ledger because it has the crown's own name in the debt column, and I will read it aloud in every market square before I hand it back."

Sefa closed her eyes. *Lior. Sweet, stubborn, catastrophically honest Lior.* There were perhaps forty better things to say to fourteen drawn crossbows and *you have got to learn to lie*, and Lior had said the one true thing, because of course they had.

Ossory sighed, and Sefa heard the sigh for what it was: the sound of a professional who had been hoping to avoid paperwork. "Loose on my mark, then."

Fourteen crossbows came up.

Sefa did the arithmetic she'd been trained to do, the courier's arithmetic, the *how many steps to the water, how heavy is the current, how fast can a bolt fly* arithmetic, and the answer to all of it was *not fast enough, not far enough, not enough* — and then Maren stepped forward, out from behind the horses, into the open mud of the ford's edge, and said:

"Children. You're standing in my spell."

There was nothing in her hands. There was no circle drawn, no chalk, no salt, none of the hedge-witch furniture Sefa had half expected. Maren just stood there, a small old woman in three blankets, and the air around the ford *changed*.

Sefa had seen Maren work before. Everyone had, on the road — little things, kind things, a charm to keep the damp out of the bedrolls, a word that made the horses calm. Little things, paid for in little coins. Lior had explained it to her once, at a fire, in the flat voice they used for the worst facts: *every spell is bought. Maren pays with her own past. That's why she taught me the way she taught me. That's why I ran.*

This was not a little thing.

The light went strange. Not bright — *deep*, as if the grey morning had been only a coat of paint and something older and truer was showing through underneath. The reeds bent all at once, away from Maren, like a crowd leaning back. And Sefa felt it happen inside her own head: a gentle, enormous pressure, like a hand closing around every memory she had of this morning — the ford, the horses, the argument, the *boring* — and holding them up to the light, weighing them.

Maren was gathering. Sefa understood that with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely. Maren was gathering every memory she had of this place, this kind of place — fords, and rivers, and crossings, and the people she'd ever watched cross them, and the wars she'd ever seen fought at them, decades and decades of it, whole years of her life pulled up by the root and held in her two bare hands like a bale of wool.

"Maren, *no*—" Lior's voice cracked across the water. "There are other ways, we can run, we can—"

"Running spends the same," Maren said. Her voice was still mild. That was the worst part. It was still *mild*. "This just spends it all at once, and buys something worth the price. Hold still, both of you. This is the last lesson and it's a short one."

She turned to the wardens, and her eyes were already going somewhere else, somewhere far behind her own face.

"You lot," she said to them, almost kindly. "You're going to remember this. I'm sorry. I'd make it gentler, but gentler costs more."

Ossory shouted something — *loose, loose, loose* —

And Maren *spent*.

* * *

Later, Sefa would try to describe it, and would fail, and would keep trying anyway, because some things you owed the telling even when the telling came up short.

The ford remembered a war.

Not showed — *remembered*. The Greywater rose up in a single silver sheet, and in it, in it and around it and through it, every battle this river had ever witnessed stood up and put itself back on. Sefa saw soldiers in armor three hundred years out of fashion wade out of the water on both banks. She saw a burning supply train that had gone into the river a century before she was born, still burning, still going in, over and over. She heard horns that had last been blown before the Spire ever stood. The past of that ford, all of it, every drowned man and broken spear and panicked horse, came up out of the mud at Maren's word and *charged*.

The wardens broke.

There was no shame in it. Sefa, flat on her stomach in the mud with Bristle's trembling body over her, watched fourteen of the court's best soldiers scream and run from men who had been dead since her grandmother's grandmother, and she thought: *anyone would. Anyone.* The dead did not touch them — that was the terrible elegance of it, Maren had built it so nothing could actually be hurt, the phantoms passed through the wardens like cold water, and the wardens' minds did all the wounding themselves. One man fell to his knees in the shallows and began weeping at a soldier who wasn't there. Ossory stood longest, sword up, shouting orders to people who could no longer hear her, and then a horse that had died at this ford two hundred years ago galloped through her, and she sat down in the water and did not get up quickly.

It lasted perhaps a minute. It felt like a year.

Then it was gone — all of it, the whole risen past folding back down into the river like a sheet pulled off furniture — and the ford was just a ford again, grey and shallow and quiet, and Maren was standing where she had stood.

Except she was smaller.

That was the only word for it. She hadn't shrunk; she'd *hollowed*. The three blankets hung on her like the frame of a tent with the pole knocked out. She swayed. Lior reached her first, catching her under the arms, and Sefa got there a half-second later, and together they lowered her to the mud, and Maren looked up at them with eyes that were trying very hard to focus.

"There," she said. "Tidy."

"Maren." Lior's hands were shaking. Sefa had never seen Lior's hands shake. "Maren, what did you— how much—"

Maren frowned, the frown of someone reaching for a thing on a high shelf. "I had a kitchen," she said. "I'm nearly sure I had a kitchen. There was bread in it. Someone burned the bread." Her eyes found Lior's face and held it, hungry and puzzled. "You burned my bread. First week. You were crying about it. I said—" the frown deepened— "I said something. I said something good. I can't—"

"You said the bread was a lesson," Lior whispered. "You said *the fire teaches what the recipe won't*."

"Did I?" Maren smiled, and the smile was real but it was a stranger's smile, warm and unanchored, like a boat come loose from its mooring. "That does sound like me. Everyone always said I sounded like me."

Sefa heard a sound and turned. The wardens were regrouping on the far bank — what was left of them, nine or ten, hauling their weeping comrades upright, and Ossory on her feet now with water streaming off her and an expression Sefa had never expected to see on a warden's face.

She wasn't angry. She was *shaken*. She looked across the ford at the small hollowed woman in the mud, and then at the water, and Sefa watched her do the arithmetic that Sefa had just done: *this is what one old witch buys with one life's worth of memory. And the court wants to feed the thing that's hungrier than her to a city full of people.*

Ossory raised a hand — a *hold* gesture, not a *loose* one — and called across the water, and her voice had lost its door-slam.

"The court's orders are to take the ledger." A pause. "The court isn't here. Go. Take the north road and don't take the north road fast. I was never at this ford. None of us were. We'll report the witch dead and the ledger burned."

"You'll hang for that," Sefa said.

"I'll hang for following the orders too, the way things are going." Ossory's mouth twisted. "The Spire's shadow is eating villages, woman. I've seen the maps. Somebody in that party is actually doing something about it." She turned away, then turned back, one last thing, thrown like a stone: "The inquisitor Vael went north ahead of you. Alone. She took nothing but her warrant and a horse. Draw your own conclusions."

Then they were gone, into the reeds, and the ford was theirs, and the silence came down like snow.

* * *

They got Maren across the water on Bristle's back, wrapped around Sefa like a child, which she let them do with a mildness that broke Sefa's heart in a new place every time. On the far bank they made a fire — Lior insisted, *she had a kitchen, we'll give her a kitchen* — and Maren sat close to it and watched the flames with an attention that Sefa slowly realized wasn't comfort-seeking.

She was reading it.

"Fire's simple," Maren said, to the fire, to them, to nobody. "Fire only knows now. That's why I always liked it." She held her hands out. "I've lost the middle of me. Do you understand? I'm all beginnings and endings. I know I had a sister. I know she's dead. I can't find the years between. I know I taught a child everything I had." Her eyes came up, and found Lior, and this time they held, and something in them *recognized*, even if the ledger-pages were blotched and running. "I know it was you. I can't always find your face, girl. But I know I spent a life on you, and I know I'd spend it again, and that's the whole of the lesson, so write it down: *the spending is the spell.* Everything else is furniture."

Lior made a sound that was not quite a word, and put their head down on their knees beside her, and Maren — not quite knowing why, and doing it anyway, because some things live deeper than memory — put her hand on their hair.

Sefa stood watch at the edge of the firelight and let them have the moment, and did her own arithmetic, the courier's kind, the honest kind.

North road. Vael ahead of them, alone, unaccounted for, her certainty — whatever Ossory had seen crack in it — a closed book. The Spire three weeks' walk if the weather held, and the weather was a grey lid that had never once held anything. Behind them, a city preparing for a war, and somewhere in it, an old man walking out alone to meet an army with nothing but a ledger entry and a hundred years of guilt.

And here, a fire, and a hollowed woman, and a keeper of accounts with their head down and their shoulders shaking, holding the only copy of the truth in the world.

Sefa looked at Maren — at the spaces where Maren used to be — and understood, with a courier's clarity, exactly what the road north was going to cost, and exactly what currency it was going to be paid in.

"Lior," she said quietly, when the shaking had stopped. "We should move. While the wardens' courage is still broken."

Lior lifted their head. Their eyes were red, and steady, and older than they had been that morning.

"Yes," they said. And then, looking down at Maren, who had fallen into a light doze against Bristle's flank, murmuring something about bread: "And Sefa — when we get to the Spire. Whatever it asks. I need you to remember what she just showed us. The spending is the spell. I don't want to face it and *forget the terms*."

Sefa looked north, where the grey sky met the grey road, and somewhere on it, a black tower's shadow was already reaching toward them like a hand.

"I'll remember," she said. "Somebody has to."
