## 21. Half a Teacher

The cottage Sefa found them was less a cottage than a suggestion of one—a shepherd's hut with a sagging roof and a door that had to be lifted on its hinges to close—but it had walls, a hearth, and most importantly, a floor that was not mud. After the ford, after the wardens, after what Maren had done, Lior would have accepted a ditch.

They carried her inside themselves. She weighed almost nothing now. That was the part that frightened Lior most—not the white streak that had spread through her hair overnight like frost across a window, not the way her hands trembled when she wasn't holding something, but the lightness. As if the spell at the ford had burned her down to kindling.

Sefa built the fire while Lior laid Maren on the cot near it, tucking the one blanket they had left around her.

"Is she—" Sefa started, then stopped, because she didn't know how to finish the question, and neither did Lior.

"She's alive," Lior said. "That's all I know for certain."

Maren's eyes were open. They had been open since the ford, mostly, watching things that weren't there—or worse, watching things that *were* there without any recognition at all. Now they fixed on the rafters with an expression of mild scholarly interest, as though the beams were a text she'd been meaning to reread.

"Maren." Lior knelt by the cot. "It's me."

Nothing. Not even a blink.

"It's Lior. Your apprentice. The one who ran away." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they hated it, and they kept going anyway. "You told me once I'd remember why. Well. I remembered. You could at least have the decency to be smug about it."

A fragment surfaced—Maren's voice, dry as autumn leaves: *Decency is for people who can afford it.* Lior laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and pressed their forehead briefly against the edge of the cot.

Behind them, Sefa said quietly, "I'll see if there's anything in the village worth trading for. Food. A second blanket. Herbs, if anyone remembers what herbs are for these days." The border villages had been thinning out as they traveled north, memory bleeding off them like heat from stones, and the people who remained were friendly in the vague, untroubled way of children. "I won't be long."

Lior nodded without turning around. The door groaned open, complained, and shut.

And then it was just the two of them, and the fire, and everything Lior didn't know how to say.

* * *

Tending a hedge-witch turned out to be mostly tending a person, which Lior discovered was harder.

They got water from the well—Maren drank when the cup touched her lips, mechanically, like a plant taking rain. They found half a loaf in the hut's crock, stale but sound, and soaked it in broth from Sefa's travel stores until it was soft enough for someone who had forgotten, apparently, how chewing worked. Maren ate three spoonfuls and then turned her face away with the gentle finality of a cat declining attention.

"You used to say," Lior told her, wiping her chin with a rag, "that food was the first magic. That every cook was a witch who'd chosen a smaller audience." Another fragment, surfacing unbidden. They were coming more often now, little bubbles rising through still water. "You said it while burning soup. I don't think you ever successfully cooked anything in your life. I used to do all of it and pretend you had."

Maren's eyes slid from the rafters to Lior's face.

Lior went very still.

There was something in her gaze—not recognition, exactly. Recognition would have been too much to hope for. But there was *attention*, the particular quality of attention Maren had always given to things she found interesting, which historically included storm fronts, cursed objects, and Lior's worst decisions.

"Lior," she said.

"Yes." Lior's heart slammed against their ribs. "Yes, that's me."

She frowned, working at something behind her eyes like a knot in wet rope. "Lior," she said again, tasting it. Then, with sudden irritation: "Where did I put my spectacles?"

"I—you don't wear spectacles. You never wore spectacles. You said lenses were a crutch for people who hadn't learned to squint properly."

"I did not say that."

"You absolutely did. You also said the same thing about maps, chairs, and gratitude."

Maren considered this. The frown deepened, then dissolved into something softer and more frightening: confusion, naked and unguarded, on a face that had spent seventy years being certain of itself. "I don't remember saying that," she whispered. "I don't remember much of anything. There was a river. There was light going out of me like water out of a cracked jug, and I let it, because the boy was going to die otherwise, and—" She stopped. Her hand crept out from under the blanket and found Lior's wrist, gripping with surprising strength. "Did the boy live?"

"He lived. Tam. He's asleep in the cart outside. Sefa's brother is watching him."

"Good," Maren said. "Good." And then her grip loosened, and her eyes drifted back to the rafters, and she was gone again—sailed off to wherever the decades of her had gone, leaving only the hull behind.

Lior sat on the floor beside the cot for a long time, holding her hand, watching the fire.

* * *

In the afternoon, the fragments came faster, and stranger.

They came, Lior realized, when Maren was doing something with her hands. It was Lior's idea originally—desperation dressed up as theory. If memory lived in the body as much as the mind—and Maren herself had taught them that, hadn't she? *The hands remember what the head forgets; why do you think we stir widdershins?*—then maybe the body could lead the mind home.

So Lior put things in Maren's hands. First the wooden stirring spoon they'd carried from her old kitchen, salvaged the night Lior ran away, wrapped in a spare shirt at the bottom of their pack for eleven years like a relic. Maren held it and looked puzzled. Lior set a pot of water on the hook over the fire and guided her hands through the motion—one circle, two, counterclockwise.

"Widdershins," Maren murmured.

"That's right."

"For luck?"

"For keeping the memories stirred in. Yours, I mean. Ours."

Maren stirred. The water went round. And slowly, like sediment settling in reverse, things began to rise.

"The Henderson girl," she said abruptly. "The one with the goat problem. Did she ever pay me?"

"You made me take payment in goat cheese. We ate goat cheese for a month. I still can't smell it without flinching."

"Hmph." A pause. The spoon went round. "Your handwriting has improved."

"You haven't seen my handwriting."

"I don't need to see it. I can tell. It used to look like a spider had died in an inkwell." The ghost of her old tartness flickered across her face and was gone, and Lior found they would have given anything—anything at all—to have it stay. "You were a terrible scribe. You're better now. Someone taught you patience."

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Lior said. "Ledger-keeping. I copy accounts for a living. Copied, I mean. Before."

"A ledger-keeper." Maren said the word the way she'd once said *tax collector*, which is to say with the precise intonation of someone filing it under *disappointments*. Then, unexpectedly: "Good. Someone should keep accounts. Most people think magic is about power. It isn't. It's about *bookkeeping*. Every spell is an entry. Something in, something out. Balance or ruin." Her hand stilled on the spoon. "I never balanced mine."

Lior's throat tightened. "You paid for my lessons," they said quietly. "Didn't you. Every spell you taught me. You said memory-magic draws on the caster's own past, but it wasn't yours you were spending. It was mine. That's why I ran. That's why I couldn't remember why I ran. You took the reason with you when you paid for the last lesson."

The spoon went round. Round. Round.

"I owed you a debt," Maren said at last, to the pot rather than to Lior. "Before you were ever born. Before your sister—" She stopped, frowning, reaching for something that slipped away from her like a fish in dark water. "There was a debt. I've been carrying it so long I forgot whose shoulders it sat on. Mine or yours."

"Yours," Lior said. "It was always yours. And you passed it to me without telling me, and I have spent eleven years thinking I was the one who abandoned *you*."

"Did you?" Maren asked. "Abandon me?"

"Yes."

"Then we're square," she said, with such matter-of-factness that Lior laughed despite everything, a wet, broken laugh. "An abandonment against an abandonment. That's balance, ledger-keeper. Write it down."

* * *

Evening came early, as evenings did now, under a sky with no stars in it. Lior lit the lamp and fed Maren more broth, and this time she ate six spoonfuls, which felt like a victory worthy of ballads.

Sefa returned after dark with bread, a jar of honey, a bundle of dried sage from a woman in the village who "couldn't recall what it was for but felt it ought to be kept," and an expression Lior had learned to read as *trouble, later*.

"How is she?" Sefa whispered, setting the provisions down.

"She called me a terrible scribe and forgave me for running away. So. Better than yesterday."

"And worse than before the ford."

Lior didn't answer, which was an answer. Sefa sat down heavily on the hearthstone and broke the bread into three pieces out of habit, then looked at the third piece and set it aside for morning.

Outside, Tam's voice rose and fell as he talked to Sefa's brother Bren about nothing—the blessed, ordinary nothing of children—and the wind moved through the hills with a sound like pages turning.

"Maren," Lior said, kneeling by the cot again. "I'm going to ask you something, and you can refuse to answer. You're allowed to refuse. I should have told you that years ago."

The old woman's eyes found them. Waiting.

"When you gave yourself to that spell at the ford—decades of yourself, Sefa says, she saw it leave you—you knew what it would cost. You'd already spent my past once, paying for my lessons. Why spend your own this time? Why not mine? I was right there. I'd have offered. I *would* have."

For a long moment Maren said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse conducted its small affairs.

Then she reached up, slow as sap, and touched Lior's cheek with fingers that smelled of sage and woodsmoke and eleven lost years.

"Because a teacher spends herself," she said. "That's the whole of the trade. Everything else is commentary." Her thumb traced a line down Lior's jaw, clumsy and deliberate. "I spent your past because I was a coward and a thief, and I told myself it was mercy—that you'd be lighter without it. I was wrong. I have known I was wrong for eleven years, which is precisely as long as you've been gone, which is not a coincidence, child. Guilt keeps excellent time."

"Maren—"

"But spending *yourself*," she went on, as though Lior hadn't spoken, "spending yourself for someone—that's not theft. That's the only coin that was ever truly mine to give." Her hand dropped. Her eyes were drifting already, the tide going out. "Do you see? Do you see the difference? One is an entry in the debt column. The other is a gift. The whole art, Lior—the whole *art*—is knowing which column you're writing in."

Her eyes closed. Her breathing slowed toward sleep.

Lior knelt there in the lamplight, and understood, finally and completely, what they owed her. Not repayment—she'd never accept it, would bite at the very idea. What they owed was the lesson itself, learned and carried forward: to know which column. To write carefully. To give, when giving came, in the currency that was truly theirs.

"I see it," they whispered to the sleeping woman. "I'm writing it down."

* * *

Later—much later, when Sefa and the boys were asleep and the fire had burned to a red contemplation of itself—Lior took out the oldest ledger, Corvane's ledger, the one that weighed like a sin, and opened it by lamplight to the page they'd marked.

The Spire's account. The debt column. The hungry arithmetic of a tower that ate memory to survive, inching north week by week toward the city, toward everyone Lior had ever failed or loved, categories that had recently begun to overlap.

But tonight, for the first time, Lior read it differently. Tonight they read it as Maren would: not as a list of what was owed, but as a record of *how* it had been taken. Hunger. Theft. Entries in the wrong column, a century of them.

And beneath the last entry, in Corvane's cramped antique hand, a note Lior had read a dozen times without hearing:

*It takes. It has only ever known how to take. God forgive us—we taught it that.*

Lior looked across the room at Maren, sleeping the sleep of the hollowed, her hands curled loosely around the wooden spoon she wouldn't let go of even in dreams. A woman who had spent her whole life learning, too late, the difference between taking and giving.

"We'll teach it differently," Lior said softly—to the ledger, to the tower far to the north, to nobody. "We'll teach it the other column."

The lamp guttered. And in the silence that followed, from the direction of the road—far off, faint, but unmistakable—came the sound of many horses moving at a trot through the night, and the cold particular jingle of court harness.

Wardens. Coming north along the same road they'd traveled, following the same trail.

Lior closed the ledger and reached for the pack.
