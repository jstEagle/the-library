## 22. Vael Defects

The Wren's Rest was the kind of tavern that pretended to be honest.

Vael had been in a thousand such places across her career—taverns that kept their ledgers clean and their floors swept and their secrets in the cellar behind the pickled eggs. She had learned long ago that dishonesty was never in the wine or the company; it was always in the architecture. The rooms too quiet near the back. The stairs that creaked on every step except two. The landlord who remembered your face a little too well and your coin a little too little.

She took the table by the window anyway, because it was the one her handler had named, because she was an inquisitor of the Crown of Vellmark and she did not skulk at the back of taverns like a smuggler's mistress. She ordered tea she did not intend to drink and set her warrant-case on the table where anyone could see it, which was its own kind of message: *I am armed with paper, and my paper is worse than knives.*

Master Handler Ossen arrived eleven minutes late, which was eight minutes later than he liked people to think was rude and four minutes earlier than he wanted them to notice he'd been waiting outside to watch her come in. He was a soft, grey man shaped like a folded letter, with the small wet eyes of someone who had spent thirty years reading other people's handwriting and none at all writing his own.

"Inquisitor." He sat. "You look tired."

"You look like a man about to tell me something I'll hate," Vael said. "Let's not waste the tea."

Ossen smiled the way a ledger balances: precisely, and with nothing left over. He signaled for his own cup, waited until the girl brought it, waited until the girl had gone back to the bar, waited until a cart rattled past the window loud enough to cover ordinary words—and only then leaned forward, which told Vael more than anything he would say next.

"Your report reached the chancery," he said. "The one concerning the Guild of Impossible Things. And your… reservations."

"My reservations," Vael repeated. She kept her voice level. She was very good at keeping her voice level; it was practically the whole job. "You mean my finding that the wards did not fail. That they were *spent*. That someone drew down a century of stored ward-power in a single night, and that the guild's own ledgers record the transaction."

"I mean your reservations," Ossen agreed pleasantly.

"And?"

"And the Crown thanks you for your diligence." He sipped his tea. "The matter is resolved."

Vael looked at him for a moment. Outside, the market noise went on—the vendors shouting prices that changed twice a day now, the refugees selling whatever they'd carried out of the border country, someone's child laughing at something, gods help them all, as though the sky were still a sky.

"Resolved," she said.

"The Crown has taken the matter under direct management. Your commission regarding the guild is concluded. You will return to the capital by week's end and file your final summary there."

"I'm mid-investigation."

"You *were* mid-investigation."

"Three hundred thousand people live under those fallen wards, Master Handler. The border villages are going dark—not lamp-dark, *memory*-dark. I have interviewed a woman who could not remember her own wedding and smiled about it. Something is eating the country from the north end inward, and you're telling me the matter is *resolved*."

"Yes," said Ossen.

There it was again, that pause before leaning in. This time he didn't wait for the cart. He didn't need to. What he said next wasn't secret, Vael would understand later—it simply hadn't occurred to him that anyone inside the machine might object to it, any more than one objects to arithmetic.

"So here is where matters stand," he said, "so that you don't embarrass yourself at court by asking after it. The Spire is awake and hungry. It eats memory—you've seen the villages. It is moving south along the old ward-lines, and it will reach the city within weeks. The Crown's engineers and the Chantry's divines are in agreement that it cannot be fought, bought, or prayed away. But it can be *fed*."

Vael felt the temperature of the room change, or perhaps just her own blood. "Fed what."

"What it eats, Inquisitor. Memory. On scale." Ossen turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a full rotation, like a man unlocking something. "The refugee camps hold some forty thousand souls from the border provinces. They are displaced, unrecorded in many cases, and—forgive the phrasing, it is the chancery's phrasing, not mine—*demographically redundant*. Their lands are already lost. Their trades are broken. The camps cost the treasury nine hundred crowns a day to keep in gruel and canvas."

He looked up at her then, and his wet eyes were almost kind.

"They are going to be fed to the tower, Vael. All of them. In batches, through the winter. And the city—the real city, the recorded city, the city with ledgers and lineage and votes—goes on. The stars come back. The wards rekindle. Everyone who matters survives. That is the resolution. That is the Crown's answer, sealed with the Privy Signet, and it is a *good* answer, and I am telling you this because you are one of ours and I would rather you hear it from me than from a rumor."

The tea had gone cold in front of Vael. She noticed this with strange clarity, the way one notices details in dreams: the film of oil on the surface, the leaf sediment, the chip in the cup's rim shaped like a tiny crescent moon.

Forty thousand people.

She thought of the camp at Marrowgate, which she had walked through three days ago with her notebook and her polite terrible questions. She thought of Sefa's brother—no. Not yet. First the other thing.

She thought of a boy in the camp, maybe seven, who had shown her a knotwork bracelet his grandmother made and explained, with enormous patience, as if to someone slow, that his grandmother was the woman over there by the cookfire, except he wasn't sure anymore why he knew that was true. The memory-eaters had passed through his family's village a month before the wards fell. His grandmother still cooked. Still hummed. Just no longer knew whose child he was, and so was kind to him the way one is kind to a stray.

"That's not an answer," Vael said. "That's an expense report."

"It's *governance*," said Ossen, without heat. "You of all people should appreciate it. You've read the old accounts. You know what the last war cost. The Crown is being asked to choose between forty thousand unrecorded lives and every recorded life in the capital, and it has chosen, and it has chosen correctly, and history will call it wisdom because history is written by whoever survives to hold the pen." He finished his tea. "Your warrants are rescinded. Your access to the archives ends tonight. Go home, Vael. Take the pension. You've earned a garden."

*You've earned a garden.* As if she were a horse being put out to pasture rather than a woman being asked to help shovel forty thousand people into a tower's mouth.

Vael found, to her own surprise, that she was not angry.

Anger would come later, she suspected—a great deal of it, probably, at inconvenient moments. What she felt now was something colder and cleaner and far more dangerous to the Crown: the specific, surgical clarity of an auditor finding the fraud. Ten years she had served the inquisition. Ten years of polite interviews and terrible consequences, and she had believed—had needed to believe—that the terror served something. That the machine was brutal but aimed. That the paperwork of power, however cruel, was at least *honest* paperwork, entries matching exits, debts owed to purposes.

And here was the bottom line, written in the Privy Signet itself: the Crown had known. Had known the wards were dying—her own archive dig had proven the court's engineers reported the decay twenty years ago—and had done nothing, because the Spire's hunger was an asset waiting for inventory. The border provinces hadn't been abandoned. They'd been *stockpiled*.

Every conviction she had was built on the belief that the ledger balanced against justice. She had just watched someone tear out the justice column entirely and call the remaining numbers sound.

"Thank you for the candor, Master Handler," she said.

Ossen blinked. He had expected tears, or fury, or the grey-faced obedience of the truly broken. Candor-thanks was off his script, and she watched him fumble for the page. "You… accept, then."

"I accept that you've told me the truth," Vael said, standing, taking up her warrant-case. "It's the first honest entry I've seen from the chancery in years. I find I want to preserve it." She smiled at him—she knew exactly what her smile did to soft men shaped like folded letters—and added, gently, "Enjoy your tea."

She walked out into the market noise and did not go home.

* * *

The problem, Vael reflected that evening from a rented room above a cooper's yard, was that defecting required somewhere to defect *to*.

Treason, done properly, was an administrative act. You needed documents. You needed names. You needed a recipient for your treason who could actually use it, and her options, enumerated on the back of a laundry list, were as follows:

One. Corvane and the Guild of Impossible Things. Currently sealed inside their own Guildhall under royal interdiction, master presumed dead or fled—reports conflicted, which meant he was alive, because dead men generated consistent reports. Assets: knowledge of the Spire beyond anyone's. Liabilities: the guild had sold the city its borrowed century in the first place, and half the capital blamed them for the dark sky. Also, they had, as an institution, committed the original theft. Hardly a moral upgrade.

Two. The runaway apprentice, Lior Dravenmoor, currently fleeing north with a courier and a hedge-witch, carrying the oldest ledger in the kingdom. Assets: apparently the only person alive who understood the debt. Liabilities: currently the subject of Vael's own arrest warrant, which was awkward, and possibly mad, and definitely a runaway apprentice.

Three. Nobody. Take the pension. Plant the garden. Become the kind of person who could watch the camp-fires at Marrowgate go out one by one and call it weather.

She burned the laundry list and started a fresh page.

Here was the thing about Vael that even she had only understood in fragments until tonight: she had never actually served the Crown. She had served *the account*. The idea that power should be legible, that every act of force should have a corresponding entry somewhere, that cruelty without record was merely appetite wearing authority's coat. The inquisition had been, in her heart, the kingdom's way of making itself confess. Every interrogation she conducted was an audit of a soul. She had sent people to the dungeons with genuine sorrow and genuine certainty, because the alternative—power that answered to nothing—was the thing she existed to prevent.

The Crown had just answered to nothing. Forty thousand people, entered as fuel.

So the question was not *whom do I serve now*. The question was: *which side is keeping books?*

She thought about the guild. Whatever else could be said of Master Corvane—and worse had been said, most of it earned—he had kept a ledger for a hundred years recording a crime he helped commit, with interest calculated honestly, marked *PAYABLE*, in his own hand. A guilty man who refused to let the guilt be forgotten. That was not innocence. But it was the shape of something she recognized: accountability as discipline, as devotion, as the only penance worth performing.

And the apprentice. Lior Dravenmoor, whom Vael had interviewed for less than an hour in a Guildhall sitting room, and who had lied to her badly about a missing page while visibly vibrating with the effort. She had noted the lie at the time with professional satisfaction. What she had failed to note—what she was noting now, in the lamplight, with the humiliation of hindsight—was that the boy had lied the way honest people lie: catastrophically, transparently, in defense of something they believed mattered more than their own safety. Guilty people lie smoothly. Innocent people lie terribly. It was practically a diagnostic criterion, and she had missed it because she'd been looking for a saboteur instead of a bookkeeper.

She had also, during that interview, noticed the gap in the old ledger. A page razored out at the binding, edges still sharp, done recently, done by someone left-handed working hurriedly in poor light—she could reconstruct all of it from the fiber damage, and she had, and she had filed it, and the file was now locked in the chancery archive where it would be used to hang whichever scapegoat the Privy Council found convenient.

Fine. She'd reconstruct it again from memory. Her memory, unlike the Crown's conscience, remained intact.

Vael dipped her pen. Beneath the heading *RECIPIENTS OF TREASON*, she struck through *nobody*, wrote *Dravenmoor, L. — via any courier line still running north*, and underlined it twice.

Then she got to work, because sentiment was cheap and paper was everything.

She worked through the night. By the grey hour before dawn she had assembled, from ten years of meticulous habit, the following: certified copies of the Privy Council's feeding schedule for the Marrowgate and Thornfield camps, including batch sizes and the chilling bureaucratic euphemism *provisioning transfers*; the engineers' twenty-year-old reports on ward decay, stamped RECEIVED and FILED UNACTED, which was the sentence the whole court deserved to hang by; the name of every councilor who had signed the order, with dates; and—this was the piece she sat over longest, pen hovering—her own confession.

Not of treason. Treason needed no confession; it announced itself.

Her confession was this: that six days ago, acting on chancery instruction, she had detained a guild courier named Perrin Sefa-who-was-called-Sefa on suspicion of smuggling, and released her when the girl traded information about the apprentice's route north. Vael had logged the exchange, filed it, and slept soundly. She had used a frightened young woman's love for her brother as a pry-bar and called it procedure.

And the information Sefa had sold—the route north, the party's composition, the ford at Alderwater—had gone into the chancery's machinery, and the machinery had spat out orders, and there were now wardens on the north road with a warrant in Vael's own hand. If the wardens caught Lior's party at the ford—and she had heard nothing since, which meant either they hadn't, or they had, and silence was the worse reading—then the only person who could settle the Spire's account would die in a ditch, and the Crown would proceed with its provisioning transfers, and the stars would come back over a city built on graves.

Vael looked at her own warrant, her own seal, her own signature authorizing the pursuit, and felt something crack along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing.

*Conviction,* she thought, *apparently rebuilds the way anything else does. Demolition first. Then the ugly question of what foundation you're willing to stand on.*

She had stood on law. Law had been poured into a mold labeled *expediency* while she wasn't watching. So: new ground. She would stand on this—that the account must balance, that no one gets fed to a tower by arithmetic, that a debt honestly reckoned can be settled and a crime honestly recorded can be judged, but a people erased from the ledger leaves no number left to argue with. Erasure was the one crime the inquisition had never been built to punish, because the inquisition worked for the erasers.

Time to change employers.

* * *

Dawn found her at the cooper's yard gate, dressed plainly, hair bound back, carrying one leather case and wearing the expression of a woman going to buy fish.

Getting word north was the practical problem. The courier lines were guild-run and the guild was sealed; the post-road stations were crown-run and compromised. But Vael had spent a decade learning how the city actually moved beneath its official skeleton, and the official skeleton had never once moved a single thing that mattered.

She walked to the Tannery Quarter, to a shuttered dye-house with a blue door, and knocked the pattern every inquisitor memorized and was supposed to pretend not to know: the smugglers' knock, three-and-two, the knock that said *I know what this door is and I'm not telling.*

The grate opened. One eye, brown and suspicious, above a beard that seemed to be attempting escape in several directions at once.

"We're closed," said the eye's owner.

"For everyone," Vael agreed. "But you're open for the guild, and the guild needs a rider, and I am carrying"—she let the case shift in her grip, heavy with paper—"the single most valuable cargo to travel this road since the wards fell. And I'm asking you to carry it for free, which should tell you how much I hate asking."

The eye narrowed. "Inquisitor Vael. The Grey Question herself. Do you know what we do to your sort in this quarter?"

"Feed them to informants, usually. I've read the reports. They're exaggerated; you mostly feed us bad stew and wrong directions." She held his gaze. "I'm not your sort anymore. As of last night I'm a traitor to the Crown of Vellmark, and I have the documents to prove it, and if you turn me away I'll go to the second option on my list, which is shouting the Privy Council's feeding schedule from the cathedral steps and getting executed messily, which serves no one. Whereas if you take my letters north—to the Dravenmoor party, care of any guild safehouse on the river road—then in a month's time you get to tell the story of how the smugglers saved the world, and I promise you, friend, that story will be *true*."

Silence. Somewhere behind the blue door, water dripped into a dye-vat, patient as accounting.

"The Grey Question," the man said at last, musingly, "is bribing *us*." He opened the door. "Times being what they are, I suppose the world's gone strange enough for it. Come in before someone sees you being respectable."

Inside, among the vats and the reeking hides, over a table scarred by a decade of illicit commerce, Vael laid out her papers one by one like a card sharp showing a winning hand: the copies, the names, the schedule, the confession. The smuggler read for a long time. When he reached the page about Sefa and the pry-bar, he glanced up at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once and say nothing, which from a smuggler was absolution, apology, and contract all at once.

"I'll ride myself," he said. "My boys are good but this needs someone who understands what he's carrying." He began folding the papers into an oilskin wrap with the tenderness other men showed to infants. "The ford at Alderwater, you said in your note. There was warden movement toward it yesterday. If your people crossed before—"

"Then my letters arrive late and useless," Vael said. "If they haven't crossed, these letters may arrive exactly in time to be late and useless in a more interesting way."

He laughed despite himself. "You're going to be terrible at being a criminal. Too honest."

"I'm counting on the world being desperate enough to prefer it."

He paused at the door, wrap slung across his chest, and gave her a look that was almost gentle. "For what it's worth, Grey Question—the city's been whispering about you since the Guildhall fell. That you went in polite and came out different. Folk were betting on when you'd crack."

"And the odds?"

"Heavily against you cracking *toward* us." He grinned, shouldered the door open onto the waking street. "Congratulations on beating the house."

She watched him go, and then she stood a moment in the dye-house doorway, breathing the chemical air, feeling the strange lightness of a woman who had burned her own warrant-book in the night and discovered the fire gave light.

North, then. After the courier, after the letters, after her own confession traveling the river road faster than shame ever had. And if Lior Dravenmoor lived—if the ford had been crossed, if the wardens had fumbled, if the ledger still moved north in a bookkeeper's hands—then Vael would find them, and kneel to no one, but offer something rarer:

An inquisitor's testimony. Names, dates, signatures, proof—the whole terrible weight of the Crown's own paperwork, turned around and pointed home like a spear taken from the enemy's hand.

Because the Spire was starving, the Crown was feeding it, and somewhere on the north road a runaway apprentice was doing sums that would decide whether anyone got to remember any of this.

Vael settled her case on her shoulder and started walking.

The account, she thought, was very much not closed.
