## 23. The City Prepares

The Guildhall had never been quiet, but it had never been *loud* like this.

Master Corvane stood on the counting-house steps with the burned stub of the current ledgers still smudging his fingers, and looked out at what remained of the Guild of Impossible Things. Which was, as it turned out, nearly all of it. The wardens had taken the building. They had not taken the people.

"Forty-one years I have run this guild," he said, and his voice carried the way a ledger line carries—straight, unornamented, impossible to argue with. "In that time we have sold light that remembers daylight. We have sold maps to places that stopped existing on Tuesday. We have sold, on one memorable occasion, a Tuesday." A ripple of tired laughter moved through the crowd of clerks, couriers, appraisers, and one very old woman who catalogued curses and was widely believed to be one herself. "We have done it all without once asking whether a thing should be sold. Only whether it could be."

He let that sit. Somewhere in the back, an apprentice dropped a crate of bottled echoes and everyone pretended not to hear the muffled sound of someone else's argument spilling across the floorboards.

"The court has sealed our doors. Inquisitor Vael has our names. And beyond the border," Corvane said, "something is coming that remembers being human, and hates us for what we did about it. So I am going to ask you to do something the guild has never done in four hundred years."

He held up the oldest ledger—the great black book, its spine cracked like riverbed clay, the one he had pressed into Lior Dravenmoor's hands in the smoke while the world's accounts went up around them.

"I'm going to ask you to give something away."

* * *

Rumor, as every courier knows, is just freight that carries itself.

Sefa had been running messages since before dawn, and by midmorning she had stopped needing to invent them. The city told her everything. She stood in the courier-yard with her boots muddy to the knee, sorting routes, and listened to the rumor move through the lines like weather:

*Corvane gave the ledger away. He gave it to the apprentice who ran.*

*Not ran. Escaped. There's a difference; ask any courier.*

*They say the apprentice can read the old accounts. Say the debt has a name.*

*They say the name is the Spire.*

*They say if you bring a memory to the Spire freely, it doesn't take—it asks.*

*They say the stars come back one at a time, like lamps being lit by someone walking down a very long street.*

Sefa pinned the last route-slip to the board and stepped back. It was a mad map. Every road north marked in red ink: *carry word, carry food, carry word again.* Every courier she had left—and she had eleven, which was nine fewer than she'd had a month ago—was assigned to a stretch of country where the fields replayed their own burning and the villages smiled with empty faces.

"You're sending Doss up the coast road?" said Tam, her second-best runner, squinting at the board. "Doss cries at sad *songs*."

"Doss is fast and Doss is kind," Sefa said, "and right now both of those are job requirements." She pulled her collar up against the wind coming off the dark sky. Four weeks now without stars. People had stopped looking up, mostly, the way you stop prodding a bruise. But they noticed anyway. Everyone noticed anyway. "Tell him if he meets anyone coming south from the Spire road—a keeper, a hedge-witch, a warden gone strange—he's to bring them to me first and the guild second."

"And if it's the apprentice? Dravenmoor?"

Sefa looked at the board a moment too long. "Then he brings them to me *first*."

Tam grinned. "That's favoritism."

"That's logistics," said Sefa, and threw him a route-slip.

* * *

Inquisitor Vael arrived at the Guildhall at the fourth bell, alone, which frightened everyone considerably more than an escort would have.

She found Corvane in the courtyard, supervising the loading of wagons. Not fleeing wagons—she noted this immediately, professionally, with a small cold shock—*provisioning* wagons. Grain. Lamp-oil. Blankets. Chalk, of all things, forty crates of chalk.

"Inquisitor," Corvane said, without turning. "You'll want to arrest me. I'd rather you help load first. My back is seventy years old and the chalk is heavy."

Vael looked at the crates. "Why chalk?"

"To write on walls. On doors. On roads." Corvane set down his end of a crate and straightened, wincing. "There's a story going through my guild, Vael. You've heard it—you've been listening to my couriers' gossip for a week, don't look surprised, it's half your method. The story says there is a third option. Not repayment. Not default. Something a runaway ledger-keeper thought of, standing in front of a hungry tower, that no one in four hundred years thought to write down." He brushed dust from his hands. "If the story is true, then when the Spire comes to the city—and it will come, it is arithmetic, it cannot not come—every soul in this city may need to make an offer. Freely. You can't make an offer if you can't write it down."

Vael studied him for a long moment. Around the courtyard, the guild's people had gone very still, watching the inquisitor the way mice watch a hawk that has unexpectedly landed and folded its wings.

"You believe it," she said. "The rumor."

"I believe in keeping good records," said Corvane. "It's the only faith I've ever practiced. And the record says—" he tapped his own temple— "that I helped steal a heart a hundred years ago because I believed forgetting would be mercy. I was wrong. I've had a century to check my work." His voice didn't break, but it came close enough to be honest. "I would like, before the audit closes, to enter one correct figure."

Vael reached into her coat. Half the courtyard flinched. What she drew out was not a warrant but a folded paper, worn soft at the creases—the court's own contingency orders, the ones she had read three times in a border inn until the candle guttered, the ones that used the word *provisions* in a way that made her sick.

She handed it to him.

"Read it," she said. "Then tell me where you want the chalk."

* * *

By evening the rumor had grown hands and feet.

It went through the refugee camps at the city's edge, where forty thousand border folk lived in tents under a starless sky, and it changed shape as it went, the way rumors do, becoming more useful and less accurate with each telling:

*The Spire eats memories—but if you give one, it gives one back.*

*My cousin's neighbor's sister-in-law saw it. A whole village remembered their own names. All at once. Like rain.*

*The apprentice is going to teach it. That's what the ledger's for. It's not a debt-book anymore, it's a lesson-book.*

An old woman in the fourth camp, who had lost her village's memory and gotten it back only in pieces, sat outside her tent teaching children a counting rhyme from a town that no longer appeared on maps. "So they'll have it somewhere," she explained to no one in particular. "In case the tower wants it back. Things given aren't things taken. Any fool can see the difference. Apparently it took a ledger-keeper to see it, but fools see it too, eventually."

A boy asked her what she'd give the tower, if it asked her.

She considered. "The day my daughter learned to walk," she said. "It's mine to give. That's the whole point of it." Then she went back to the rhyme, and thirty small voices carried it out past the tent-lines, into the dark, toward the north.

* * *

In the Guildhall courtyard, the work continued by lamplight.

They were no longer quite the Guild of Impossible Things, Corvane reflected, watching appraisers stack chalk beside grain sacks and curse-cataloguers teach wardens-turned-deserters how to sharpen pens instead of swords. They were becoming something older and stranger—an institution organized around consent instead of debt, though none of them had voted on it yet. Institutions rarely vote on what they become. They simply notice, one morning, that they already have.

Hessa the curse-cataloguer approached him with her list. "Master. We've inventoried everything usable. Light-that-remembers-daylight: two hundred units. Maps of places that no longer exist: those are suddenly in demand again, oddly, since half the border doesn't exist either. Bottled courage: six. Bottled patience: one, and it's leaking."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is the hard inventory." Hessa hesitated. "Memories, Master. People keep asking. They come to the door and hold out their hands and say, *what do I have that it will take?* And I don't—I'm a cataloguer. I know how to price a Tuesday. I don't know how to price a wedding day."

Corvane looked out over the courtyard—at the lamplight, at the chalk, at the young clerk showing a former warden how to letter a placard reading *GIVE FREELY / TAKE NOTHING / ASK FIRST*, the letters wobbling with the effort of being hopeful in public.

"Tell them this," he said. "Tell them the guild's oldest rule, the one from before my time, the one nobody ever needed. *Nothing entered in the new book may be written unwillingly.* Tell them whatever they choose to offer is theirs to choose, and whatever they keep is theirs to keep, and both are honorable. Tell them—" and here the old man smiled, tired and crooked and real— "tell them the guild is open. First time in four hundred years we're giving things away. Frankly, the accounting is going to be a nightmare."

Hessa laughed despite herself, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and went to spread the word.

* * *

Far to the north, on a road that remembered burning, a small party walked toward a black tower: a keeper with a ledger, a courier with a promise, a half-emptied teacher wrapped in blankets, and a defected inquisitor carrying warrants and names like a bundle of lit matches.

But that is another chapter's account.

Here, in the city, at midnight, Corvane climbed to the roof of the provisioner's warehouse—the highest point left to him, since the Guildhall was sealed—and did the thing he had not done in a hundred years.

He looked up.

No stars. No moon worth the name. Just the vast blank page of the sky, waiting.

"All right," he said quietly, to the dark, to the tower far north whose hunger he could almost feel like weather, to the runaway apprentice he had failed and trusted and sent everything he had left after. "The books are balanced on my end. The city is ready to give. Do your worst, or do your best—or better yet, do what the child suggested."

He turned back toward the lamplit courtyard below, where the guild of impossible things was busy doing the most impossible thing of all: hoping on purpose, in organized shifts.

"Do what the child suggested," he repeated. "Ask first."

And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the sky, at the very limit of what an old man's eyes could swear to—

a single point of light flickered, steadied,

and stayed.
