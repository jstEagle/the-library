## 25. Audience with the Hungry Tower

The Spire had no door. Of course it didn't. Doors were for buildings, and the Spire was not a building; it was a wound in the world that had learned to stand up.

Lior stood at its base anyway, ledger strapped to their back like a penitent's pack, and looked up until looking up stopped being a thing necks were meant to do. The tower rose out of its own shadow, black stone drinking the grey light of the starless sky, and where its shadow lay across the ground there was no color at all — grass gone to ash-grey, a fallen bird gone to ash-grey, even Lior's own hands, when they strayed into it, gone briefly to the color of unwritten paper.

"Right," Lior said aloud, because talking to oneself was better than listening to oneself, and one's self currently had opinions like *turn around* and *run* and also, traitorously, *you're a ledger-keeper, this is technically an audit.* "I've come about the account."

The shadow did not answer. But something in it shifted, the way a sleeping animal shifts when it hears its name, and a seam opened in the black stone — tall as a cathedral door, thin as a knife cut — and breathed out cold air that smelled of ink and rain and, faintly, unbearably, of Maren's kitchen: bread and bitter herbs and woodsmoke.

Lior's eyes stung. "That's cheating," they told the tower. "You don't get to smell like home. I haven't decided anything yet."

The seam widened.

Going in felt less like walking through a doorway and more like being read. That was the only word Lior could find for it, and Lior was very good at words for things. A pressure passed over them from crown to heel, slow and thorough, and Lior had the distinct impression of pages turning somewhere behind their own ribs, of a vast dry voice murmuring *entry, entry, entry,* cataloguing them the way Lior catalogued impossible goods: provenance, condition, outstanding obligations.

Then the pressure lifted, and the inside of the Spire was around them, and Lior forgot how to breathe for a moment that lasted several actual moments.

The interior was a hollow going up forever and down forever, walled in black stone veined with lines of faint silver light — no, not light. *Writing.* The walls were covered in writing, floor to unseeable ceiling, column after column after column, and every line of it was in a hand Lior knew, because it was Lior's own hand, or near enough that the difference would only matter to Lior.

"That's mine," Lior said stupidly. "That's my handwriting."

*It is your handwriting,* said the Spire, in Lior's own forgotten voice.

Lior had braced for many voices. A rumble like grinding continents. A whisper like ash. Something ancient and terrible and hungry, all teeth and echo. They had not braced for this: a voice with their own pitch, their own habit of rising slightly at the end of sentences, their own way of saying *your* like it was two words. It came from everywhere and nowhere and from directly behind Lior's left ear, which was the worst possible place.

"Where did you get my voice?" Lior asked, and hated how small it came out.

*I have been eating your memories for some time now,* the Spire said, with what sounded horribly like gentle humor. *Since you crossed into my shadow on the road. You gave me a joke you once made about Corvane's hat. You will not miss it. It was not a good joke.*

"It was an excellent joke," Lior said, and then tried to remember the joke, and found a smooth blank where it had been, and understood, with a lurch, exactly what kind of creature they were negotiating with. "You're doing it already. Just — taking things."

*Taking?* The voice turned the word over, examining it. *Your ledger says borrowed. Your ledger says held in trust. Your ledger says debt. I have read your ledger, keeper. I have read everything your guild ever wrote about me. Did you think I would not keep accounts of my own?*

"I hoped," Lior admitted, "you'd be too starving to be petty."

*I am always petty. Hunger does not improve anyone's character. Ask any siege.*

And there it was again, that flicker of something almost warm under the vastness, and Lior felt the trap of it closing gently around them: it was easy, so easy, to talk to this thing. It spoke like them. It joked like them. Somewhere in the last century of feeding, it had apparently digested enough of the guild's record-keepers to become, horrifyingly, *good company.*

"Don't do that," Lior said. "Don't be likable. I'm here to read you the riot act, and it's much harder if you're likable."

*Then read it,* said the Spire. *But first, since we are discussing accounts — let me show you the original entry. The one your guild's oldest book omits. The theft has a first line, keeper, and you have never seen it.*

The walls moved.

Not physically — or not only physically. The silver writing flowed like water finding a new channel, columns dissolving and reforming, and the whole hollow became something else: a memory with the volume turned up, played on the inside of the tower's own skull.

Lior saw a century ago.

They saw the Spire young — or rather, they saw it as it had been, and understood that *young* was wrong, because the tower had been old then too, but *whole*. Light ran through it like blood through a body, and the light went up, out of its crown, into the night sky, and where it touched, stars burned. Not near it. *Everywhere.* The Spire was an anchor, and the sky was a sail, and the wards along the border sang off it in visible threads of gold, and beyond those threads, in the dark country, shapes moved that Lior's hindbrain refused to name but their training did not: the transformed, the remade, the enemy the last war had been fought against.

And then Lior saw the guild come up the mountain.

They saw a younger Corvane — a boy, really, ink-stained, terrified, carrying a lantern he didn't need because the whole mountain glowed — climbing beside older figures whose faces the memory wore smooth, the way a river wears a coin. They saw the argument at the summit, conducted in voices worn down to whispers because shouting might wake the war. They saw the heart of the Spire: a thing like a seed of white fire cradled in a hollow of black stone, beating slowly, each beat sending a pulse up into the stars.

They heard the plan. And the plan was not robbery. That was the thing that made Lior's knees go strange. The plan was *surgery.* The war could not be won; the transformed host was endless and the border wards were failing and the court's armies were burning through soldiers like a spendthrift burns through inheritance. But the transformed were drawn by the Spire's light — they remembered what they had been before the first theft, before the towers, before the war, and the light tormented them across the border like a name you can't stop saying. Take away the light, the plotters reasoned, and the enemy would forget why they marched. Take away the stars, and the whole sky would go quiet. Take away the heart, and everyone alive today — everyone in the city, everyone in the villages, everyone in the whole soft doomed world — would live.

*Everyone who is alive right now,* the memory-Corvane whispered, and his voice cracked on *right now*, and Lior realized he meant it literally: the people alive in this moment, this generation, this handful of decades. Everyone else could go hang. It was the arithmetic of a drowning man, and it was monstrous, and it was completely, heartbreakingly reasonable.

They took the heart. The Spire screamed — Lior felt the scream in their teeth, in the fillings of history — and the stars went out one by one like candles pinched by a passing hand, and the wards fell, and the dark country went silent, and the boy Corvane wept into his lantern and told himself it was mercy.

*And then,* said the Spire, and the memory shifted, and Lior saw what the guild's ledgers had never recorded, *they discovered what I eat.*

The years blurred past. The Spire, heartless, began to starve. And a starving anchor does not simply dim — it reaches. It drank the memory out of the land around it, out of the villages, out of travelers who wandered too close, and the guild, horrified, had done the only thing its nature allowed: it had opened an account. Payments. Scheduled. A tithe of forgetting, delivered by courier, entered in the oldest book, compounding for a hundred years. The guild had not stolen the Spire's heart and run. The guild had stolen the Spire's heart and then spent a century paying interest on it, in other people's pasts, to keep the tower too fed to reach for the city.

"The village," Lior said hoarsely. "The blank, smiling people. That wasn't hunger getting loose. That was a scheduled payment."

*A missed one,* said the Spire. *Your master stopped paying three years ago. He thought he had found another way. He had not. Debts do not forgive themselves, keeper. That is the one law older than I am.*

The memory-walls went dark. Lior stood alone in the hollow again, shaking slightly, holding their own elbows, doing the arithmetic they had been avoiding since the ford: the village, the camps, the refugee columns camped in the Spire's widening shadow, the city itself with its million small remembered lives. Weeks. Maren's calculation had been weeks, and Maren was half a person now lying in a cart outside the shadow's edge, and Corvane was dead or worse on the grey field buying hours with his throat, and here Lior stood, in the belly of the creditor, holding the only ledger that mattered.

"So," Lior said. "The trade. You brought me here to make me an offer. Let's have it. I find long preambles insulting."

*Directly to business. I remember liking that about you.* A pause. *I am offering to forgive the debt. All of it. A century of entries, closed. The payments stop. No more villages. No more couriers carrying jars of other people's childhoods. The city keeps its memories, the camps keep theirs, and I learn to live on what falls from the sky instead of what I take from the living.*

"And the price?"

*You,* said the Spire, in Lior's own voice, kindly. *Everything you are. Every memory, every skill, every grudge and joke and grief. The whole ledger of you, surrendered. You are the keeper of accounts, Lior Dravenmoor. You are the one person alive who has read the entire debt and understands it. If I take you, I take the understanding — the full shape of what was done, the theft and the interest and the mercy and the crime, all of it in one meal. It would feed me for a generation. Perhaps two. Long enough for the world to find another way, if it ever does. Long enough that no one else need be eaten while they look.*

The hollow was very quiet. Somewhere far above, or perhaps far below, the silver writing ticked softly, entries updating themselves.

"And I'd be?" Lior asked. "After. What's left?"

*Alive,* said the Spire. *Breathing, walking, warm. You simply would not know why any of it mattered. You would be a stranger in your own story. Someone would have to tell you who you were, day by day, and you would have to decide whether to believe them.* Another pause, gentler. *I am told this is not entirely unlike how you arrived in this world the first time. Your mistress paid for your magic out of your own past. You have been a stranger in your own story before, keeper. I am offering you the chance to be one on purpose, and for a reason, and so that no one else pays.*

And that was the unbearable part. Not the threat — there was no threat in it at all. The offer sat in the dark of the tower like a well-kept account: balanced, honest, even generous. Nobody else would be hurt. The city would keep itself. The war would stay asleep. All it cost was one runaway apprentice who had already lost most of their childhood to memory-magic, who had already spent years keeping one foot out the door, who had already, in a sense, been practicing for erasure their whole life.

It was reasonable. That was the horror of it. Every other choice in this whole wretched affair had been made by desperate people doing terrible arithmetic, and here at last was an entry that actually balanced.

"You've been reading my thoughts," Lior said quietly.

*Your face,* said the Spire. *You have your mother's face when you do arithmetic. She bit her lip. You chew the inside of your cheek. I have her memory of teaching you to hold a pen. Would you like it back? I could give it to you now, as a gesture of good faith—*

"No." Lior's voice came out harder than they expected. "No trades inside the trade. You don't get to buy my yes with scraps. That's not good faith, that's seasoning."

Silence. Then, reluctantly, with something that in anyone else Lior would have called respect:

*Fair. I withdraw the gesture.*

Lior walked to the center of the hollow and put their hand flat against the black stone, and the stone was warm, which broke something in them a little, because they had expected it to be cold. Hungry things should be cold. This one was warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a kitchen wall, and it leaned very slightly into their palm like it had been a hundred years since anything touched it without wanting something.

"Here's what I know," Lior said. "You're starving. You've been starving for a century, and starvation makes monsters of everyone — I've seen it make a monster of my teacher, spending her own mind to save us at the ford. I've seen it make a monster of my master, selling a century of strangers' memories to save his city. And it's making a monster of me right now, because some cowardly practical corner of my brain is whispering that your offer is *fine*, that I'm one person, that the math works."

*Is it fine?*

"I don't know. That's the problem." Lior pressed their palm harder against the warm stone. "But I'll tell you what I've learned keeping accounts for impossible things. A balanced ledger isn't the same as a just one. You can make the numbers come out beautiful and still have committed a crime in the margins. Your offer balances. It also assumes the only two options are full payment or default. And I have read enough of this guild's books to know that whenever someone tells you there are only two entries, there is always a third page stuck to the back of the second one that nobody wants you to turn."

*There is no third page,* said the Spire, and for the first time its voice — Lior's voice — sounded tired. *I have been alive for longer than your language. I have read every covenant, every contract, every clever clause your species ever drafted. There is pay, or there is ruin. Everything else is a story debtors tell themselves.*

"Maybe," Lior said. "Or maybe you've only ever been read to by people who owed you something. Has anyone ever *taught* you anything? On purpose? Not paid you, not bargained with you — taught you?"

The silence that followed had weight. The silver writing on the walls slowed, column by column, like a heart considering.

*What could you teach me?*

"How to remember differently," Lior said. "How to take a gift instead of a tithe. How to be given to. It's a skill. Everything is a skill. Even hunger can be trained — ask any dog that's learned to sit before supper." Their voice shook and they let it. "I don't know if it works. I don't know if a tower can learn. But I know I'm not signing anything tonight, and I know that if you eat me now, you'll never find out whether there was a third page. So here's my counter-entry, pending negotiation: nobody gets eaten, nobody gets defaulted, and I start teaching. First lesson free."

*And if the lesson fails?*

"Then we renegotiate," Lior said. "With witnesses. I insist on witnesses. Any contract this size needs a second signature, and I happen to know at least two stubborn people are coming up that mountain after me whether I like it or not."

Far above, faint through the stone, through miles of dark air, the Spire's attention turned outward — Lior felt it go, a vast lens swinging — toward the road below the shadow's edge, where, though neither of them could yet see it, a courier with a courier's terrible sense of timing was already climbing, moving faster than any sensible person would, carrying something she intended to trade.

*Someone is coming,* the Spire said slowly. *Small. Determined. She is offering me her brother.*

Lior's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"Sefa," they said. "Sefa, no—"
