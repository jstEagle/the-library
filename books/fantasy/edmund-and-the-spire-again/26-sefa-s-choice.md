## 26. Sefa's Choice

Sefa had outrun blizzards, tax collectors, and once—memorably—a goose the size of a pony with opinions about her route planning. She had never outrun grief before, and it turned out grief was faster than all of them.

She'd left the camp at Grey Ford two days ahead of the others, on a pretext so thin Maren must have seen through it and let her go anyway. "I'll scout the road," she'd said, and Maren had looked at her with those half-erased eyes that still somehow saw everything, and said, "Take the eastern track. It's kinder." Which was Maren-speak for *I know what you're going to do, and I won't stop you, but I'll pretend you're doing something sensible.*

The eastern track was not kind. It climbed through hills where the color had gone out of everything—not grey exactly, but *unremembered*, like a room after the furniture's been sold. Grass grew there, but Sefa couldn't have told you what color grass was supposed to be. She kept her hand pressed flat against her satchel the whole way, feeling the shape of the ledger-page inside it, the one she'd copied from Lior's notes by candlelight while they slept.

*The Spire accepts memory. The Spire is starving. The Spire will reach the city in weeks.*

Lior had worked that arithmetic at the fire, voice flat, hands shaking. Sefa had watched them do it and thought: *there has to be something a person can trade.* That was the whole guild creed, wasn't it? Everything had a price; nothing was beyond negotiation. Sefa Dravenmoor—no relation, she'd checked, twice, because the name coincidence had embarrassed her for her first month at the Guildhall—had spent six years as a courier learning that any wall had a door if you knew who to bribe.

So she'd ridden east, alone, toward the black tower on the horizon that ate the sky around it like ink dropped in water.

Her brother Tam was down in the refugee camps outside the city. He was eleven. He had a laugh like a door hinge that had been oiled just to spite you, and he could name every courier horse in the guild stable, and he'd held her hand through the whole first night after the stars went out and said, very seriously, "It's probably just clouds, Sef." He'd been wrong. But he'd held her hand anyway.

If the Spire wanted memory, then fine. Sefa had memories to spare. She'd give it Tam's—no. Not Tam's. Her memories *of* Tam. There was a difference, she reasoned, pedaling the argument uphill along with the mule. If the Spire took her remembering of him, Tam would still exist. He'd still be down there in the camps being eleven and insufferable and alive. She would just... not know him anymore. A stranger with her brother's face.

That was acceptable. That was a bargain. That was—

That was the thing she couldn't make herself say out loud, even alone, even to a mule, so she stopped saying anything and rode.

* * *

The Spire's shadow reached her a full day before the Spire did.

She noticed it first as a wrongness in the light—the afternoon sun still up, but the road dimming as though a cloud had decided to be permanent. Then the cold came, not winter-cold but *forgotten*-cold, the chill of walking into your childhood home and finding strangers living in it. By the time she crested the last ridge, the shadow lay across the valley floor like spilled oil, and the Spire stood in the middle of it, black and impossibly tall, its top lost somewhere above the darkened sky.

There were no stars behind it. Of course there weren't. But standing there, Sefa understood for the first time that the Spire wasn't under the starless night. The starless night was under the Spire. The tower came first. Everything else was weather.

"Right," she told the mule, whose name was Duchess and whose opinion of the whole expedition was visible from space. "You wait here."

Duchess did not wait here. Duchess followed her at a respectful distance of thirty feet, which was as close as Duchess intended to get to that tower before the heat-death of the universe, thank you very much.

The shadow deepened as Sefa walked into it. Ten steps in, she forgot the name of the inn where she'd stayed on her first courier run. Twenty steps in, she forgot the words to the song couriers sang at river crossings, though she could feel the tune sitting in her chest with no lyrics attached, humming patiently. Thirty steps in, she stopped and turned around and made herself stand there until she remembered her mother's face—and when it came back, it came back slightly wrong, slightly smoothed over, like a coin worn by too many pockets.

*It's already eating me,* she thought. *Just from walking.*

She kept going. Somewhere behind her, at thirty feet and holding, Duchess made a low sound of protest that Sefa chose to interpret as encouragement.

* * *

The base of the Spire was not a door so much as an absence of wall. Sefa walked toward it expecting resistance and found herself simply *inside*, the way you find yourself asleep—no threshold, just a change of state.

Inside was not what she expected. She'd imagined something vast and hollow, a throat. Instead the interior was small. Intimate. A round room the size of Maren's old kitchen, with walls that weren't stone but something like stacked moments—you looked at them and they almost resolved into scenes, faces, weather, and then slid away when you tried to focus. Warmth radiated from somewhere. It smelled faintly of woodsmoke and, absurdly, of bread.

In the center of the room stood a figure.

It was roughly person-shaped. Roughly. Its edges kept renegotiating themselves, and its face was a blur that Sefa's eyes kept trying to finish and failing, the way you can't quite picture a word that's on the tip of your tongue. When it spoke, it spoke in a voice Sefa recognized, and the recognition made her stomach drop.

It spoke in Lior's voice. Or rather—in the voice Lior used when Lior was tired and honest, the low unguarded register Sefa had only heard a handful of times on the road, usually near dawn.

"You are not the keeper of accounts," the Spire said.

"No," Sefa agreed. Her mouth was dry. "I'm the courier."

"Courier." The word seemed to interest it. The blurred face tilted. "One who carries things between. You carry many things. Names. News. A letter you have never delivered." The blur shifted, and Sefa felt something brush past inside her skull, gentle as a hand through hair, and suddenly she was twelve years old again, standing in a doorway, holding an unsent letter to a father who'd left, and the Spire was looking at it, at *her*, with something that might have been sympathy or might have been appetite.

"Don't," Sefa said. Her voice cracked on it. "Don't take that one. Take—" She stopped. Breathed. This was why she'd come. Get to the point, Dravenmoor. You're a professional. "I've come to make a trade."

"The keeper of accounts makes trades."

"I'm making this one on their behalf. Consider me—an advance party. A down payment." She shrugged off her satchel and set it on the ground between them, though she suspected the Spire didn't care about physical objects, that the whole gesture was theater for her own benefit. Maybe that was fine. Maybe humans needed theater. "You're hungry. Everyone says you're hungry. And I have something you want."

"You have memories," the Spire said. Not a question. The warmth in the room seemed to lean toward her, the way a fire leans when someone opens a door.

"I have memories of my brother." Saying it aloud was harder than she'd prepared for. Her eyes stung and she blinked furiously, furious at them. "His name is Tam. He's eleven. He laughs like a bad door hinge. He knows every horse in the guild stable by name and he holds my hand when he's scared and pretends he's the one comforting me. I have six years of those. Every market day. Every letter he sent me on the courier routes—I can recite them. I can recite all of them." Her voice was doing something embarrassing now, so she pushed through it, louder. "You can have them. All of them. Every single one. In exchange, you leave Lior Dravenmoor alone. You don't touch them. You don't take their past, their name, any of it. You take mine instead. That's the trade."

The room went quiet. Even the almost-scenes on the walls stilled, like a crowd holding its breath.

Then the Spire said, gently, in Lior's tired-honest voice: "No."

Sefa stared. "No?"

"No."

"You're *starving*. I'm offering you six years of—of good ones, they're really good ones, I'll throw in some bad ones too if you want variety, there's a whole incident involving a wedding and a goat—"

"It is not enough," the Spire said. "And it is not yours to give."

"Not mine—" Sefa laughed, high and disbelieving. "Whose are they, then? They're in my head!"

"They are shaped by him. Your memories of your brother are half his. To take them would be to reach into him without asking." The blurred face did something that might have been sorrow. "I have eaten without asking for a hundred years. I know precisely what it costs. I will not be bribed into doing it politely."

Sefa stood there with her offer hanging in the warm bread-smelling air, rejected, and felt the strangest thing happen underneath the hurt: relief. Enormous, guilty relief. Some part of her had ridden two days east hoping to be refused, hoping someone would tell her that giving up Tam was not, in fact, a price she could pay, and now that someone had, she wanted to sit down on the floor of a living tower and cry about it.

She compromised by gripping the strap of her satchel very hard.

"Then what do you want?" she asked. "Everyone keeps saying you want the keeper of accounts. Why? What's Lior got that I haven't?"

"The keeper read the ledger," the Spire said. "Aloud. All of it. The theft. The interest. The villages." Something moved through the tower then, a shudder that Sefa felt in her teeth, in the floor, in the worn-smooth memory of her mother's face. "No one has ever read my accounts to me. Do you understand? For a century I have been owed, and owing, and no one has ever once sat down and shown me the arithmetic of what was done. The keeper did. The keeper stood in my heart and read me my own history in a human voice, and did not run." The blur that served it for a face steadied, just for a moment, into something almost like expression. "I am very old and very hungry and I do not know how to want anything except to eat. The keeper of accounts is teaching me otherwise. Or failing. I have not decided. But it must be the keeper. A courier carries things between. The keeper decides what the entries mean."

"That's not fair," Sefa said, and heard how young she sounded, and hated it. "They didn't steal anything. Corvane stole it. The guild stole it. Lior just does the bookkeeping."

"Yes," the Spire said. "And yet they came back. After I showed them what I am. After I offered them the easy trade—they could have paid with themselves and been done, clean, simple, one entry, closed account. They are still coming here. Walking toward me. Knowing what I cost." The warmth pulsed. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone walked toward me knowing what I cost?"

Sefa thought of Lior at the fire, working the terrible arithmetic with shaking hands, and not stopping. Lior reading the ledger aloud in the dark of the tower's heart. Lior, who ran away from one teacher and walked straight toward another.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "I think I do."

* * *

She stayed a while longer. She couldn't have said why. The Spire didn't ask her to—it seemed, if anything, puzzled by her continued presence—but she sat down on the warm floor with her back against the satchel and asked questions, because that was what couriers did: they gathered information and carried it home.

"Are you going to eat the city?"

"I do not wish to," the Spire said. "Wishing is new. It is uncomfortable. Like a tooth coming in."

"There's an army coming. From beyond the border. The transformed ones. Have you felt them?"

The tower shuddered again, longer this time. "They were made by my theft. My hunger calls to what my hunger made. Yes. I feel them. They are three days from the city." A pause, and then, in Lior's voice, almost shy: "Will the keeper arrive in time?"

"They're two days behind me. Moving fast. Maren's slowing them down, she insists on resting the mule, and honestly the mule has earned it—" Sefa stopped. Did the math. Did it again. "Three days. Two days. That's—that's one day. One day of margin, if nothing goes wrong."

Something always went wrong. Sefa had built a career on the certainty that something always went wrong.

She got up. She slung the satchel back across her shoulder. At the edge of the room, where the wall-absence waited to turn her back out into the shadow, she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, "they're not coming back because they owe you. Ledger-brain thinks that's what this is, debt and repayment, entries balancing. But I've watched them for months. They're coming back because it's *right*. There's a difference. You should learn it. It's the whole trick."

"I am trying," the Spire said, and the honesty in it, in Lior's voice, in that tired-dawn register, nearly undid her.

* * *

Outside, the shadow swallowed her again, and the forgetting began again at ten steps, twenty, thirty—she fought her way back up the ridge clutching memories like a woman gathering scattered papers in wind. Her mother's face. The crossing song. The inn's name: the Drowned Owl, it was the Drowned Owl, she had it, she had it.

Duchess met her at the top, ears flat, deeply judgmental.

"We need to ride," Sefa told her, swinging up. "Tonight. No rest stops. I know, I know, you're a professional athlete, you'll manage."

She spurred south, then west, toward the road where four figures and one heroic mule were pushing north through the unremembered hills. One day of margin. Maybe less. And somewhere beyond the border, three days out, an army of the transformed was marching on everything any of them loved, drawn like moths to a hunger that was only just learning how to want instead of eat.

Sefa rode through the colorless hills with her brother's letters safe in her head, reciting them under her breath like a prayer, like ballast, like proof.

And far behind her, at her back, so faint she might have imagined it, the horizon flickered—once, twice—like the first stuttering attempt of a star remembering how to burn.
