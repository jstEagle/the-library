## 29. The Reading of Accounts

The heart of the Spire was not a room, exactly. It was more like the inside of a held breath.

Lior stood on a floor that might have been glass or might have been night, and above them—where a ceiling should have been—the dark went up and up until it stopped pretending to be architecture and simply became absence. The walls breathed. Not metaphorically; Lior could feel the slow expansion and contraction of them against their skin, like standing inside something's chest, which, they supposed, they were.

And in the center of it all, suspended in nothing, hung the empty place where a heart had been.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the worst part. Lior had expected a wound, a black star, some visible horror. Instead there was only a hollow shaped like an absence of light—a socket in the world, patient as an unpaid column in a ledger. The Spire had been living around its own theft for a hundred years, the way a poor family lives around the missing chair, the way you stop noticing the gap until someone new visits and asks, *what happened there?*

Lior knew what happened there. They had read every entry twice.

They set the oldest ledger down on a surface that rippled to meet it, and their hands were shaking, and they let them shake, because Master Corvane had once told them—back when Corvane told them things—that a keeper whose hands never shook was either lying or dead.

"You're listening," Lior said to the dark. It wasn't quite a question. The walls contracted slightly, the way a person leans forward. "Good. Because I've done the arithmetic, and I have a great deal to say, and I warn you now: I am going to be thorough."

Somewhere far below, muffled by a hundred fathoms of hungry stone, the war was arriving. Lior could feel it as a vibration in the floor, a drumbeat of transformed feet on the Grey Field. Hours, maybe less. Sefa was down there. Vael was down there. Corvane had walked out alone two days ago and had not come back, and Lior had written *Corvane, Master, status: outstanding* in the margin of their own notebook because writing it any other way would have meant admitting it.

Maren was behind them, at the mouth of the stair, wrapped in blankets, half a teacher and all of herself in the ways that mattered. She had insisted on coming this far. "You'll want a witness," she'd said. "Truth that isn't witnessed is just noise."

So: one witness, half-blind, dozing upright against the wall. One debtor-creditor, vast and starving. One keeper of accounts, twenty-three years old, carrying a book older than the city.

Lior opened the ledger.

* * *

"Entry the first," they read, and their voice came out steadier than they'd expected. "Dated one hundred years and eleven days past. In the hand of Aldous Corvane, apprentice, aged nineteen."

The words glowed faintly as they spoke them—not magic Lior was doing, but magic recognizing itself being said aloud. The Spire's dark leaned closer.

"*Received of the Spire of the Northern March: the Heart, one (1), held in trust.*"

The walls went very still.

"I know," Lior said. "*Held in trust.* I've been chewing on that phrase for three hundred miles. Here's what I think it means. I think a frightened boy stood where I'm standing—or near enough—and he didn't call it a theft, because calling it a theft would have made him a thief, and he couldn't live with that. So he wrote it as a loan. A trust. Something that could be given back someday, when the war was over, when everyone was safe." Lior turned a page. "Except wars don't end. They just get entered into other books under other names."

They kept reading. The early entries were almost tender, in their way. Corvane the apprentice recording the wards' first flare of strength after the Heart was set into the border stones; the stars holding steady through a winter that should have scattered them; the enemy host—the people beyond the border, already changing, already forgetting their names—turning back from the walls. And beneath each triumph, small and neat, the same recurring line:

*Interest accrued.*

"The interest," Lior said, "was memory. Yours. You'd been feeding on it for centuries before the theft—gently, you fed gently then, asking in your way, taking only what dreams could spare. But cut off from your heart, you began to starve. And starvation doesn't ask."

The Spire shuddered. Dust of darkness sifted down like snow.

"No," Lior said firmly, and here the ledger-keeper in them rose up, the part that had spent years telling merchants *no, that invoice is wrong, we agreed on terms* to faces far angrier than any tower's. "Don't you dare apologize yet. You haven't heard the whole account. Sit there and be read to. That's how this works. I keep the books; you listen to them. Those are the terms."

And the Spire—vast, ancient, terrible—listened.

* * *

Lior read the middle century, and it was worse than the beginning, because the middle century was full of ordinary handwriting.

Corvane, grown now, master now, making payments. Not to the Spire—from it, in a sense. Buying time. Every entry balanced a withdrawal of years against a deposit of forgetting. A drought bought off with the memory of a harvest song. A plague turned aside at the cost of a village's recollection of its own founding. Each entry itemized. Each entry signed. Each entry followed, eventually, by the same small annotation in Corvane's aging hand:

*Forgive me. It seemed like mercy.*

"There it is again," Lior said softly. "'Mercy.' Do you know, I used to believe forgetting was mercy too? My teacher taught me memory-magic, and I paid for every spell out of my own past without knowing it, and when I finally ran, I ran without knowing why. For years I thought I was the kind of person who just leaves. That's what forgetting does—it doesn't erase the pain, it erases the *reason*, and then it hands you a blank space and calls it peace." They looked up into the hollow where the heart should be. "You've been handed a hundred years of blank spaces. And you've been handing them out. To villages. To the border folk. To that settlement we passed—the one where everyone smiled at us and none of them knew their children's birthdays."

The walls contracted. Grief has a shape, it turns out, even in stone.

"Yes," Lior said. "That one hurt to write down too."

They read on. Entry after entry. The debt compounding. The interest growing teeth. Somewhere in the sixties, Corvane's handwriting began to tremble. In the eighties, entries started appearing that weren't in his hand at all—apprentices, successors, each one inheriting the arithmetic and hating it a little more. Lior found their own name for the first time eleven years back, in a marginal note: *the Dravenmoor child shows aptitude. Erasure holding. Do not tell her why she flinches at ledgers.*

"That's me," Lior said, and their voice cracked, and they let it. "That's me you're reading about. 'Do not tell her why.' I spent my whole life flinching at things I couldn't name. Doors I wouldn't walk through. Songs I couldn't finish. There was a lullaby—" They stopped. Breathed. "There was a lullaby I loved and couldn't remember loving. It took me twenty years and a stolen page to find out that I had paid for a fire-spell with the sound of my mother's voice."

Maren stirred against the wall. Said nothing. Witnessed.

"And here is the last entry before tonight," Lior continued, turning to the final written page. "Dated four days ago. In the hand of Aldous Corvane, master, aged one hundred and nineteen. '*Bequeathal entire: the Guild of Impossible Things, its ledgers, debts, and obligations, to Lior Dravenmoor, keeper. Carry the debt. End it if you can. I go to buy her the hours.*'" Lior closed their eyes briefly. "He walked out to meet an army with nothing but an old man's stubbornness and a hundred years of guilt, so that I could stand here and do this. So whatever else is true about Aldous Corvane—and most of it is damning—he paid the last installment himself."

The Spire was silent in the way that mountains are silent.

"Now," Lior said, "the reckoning."

* * *

They had worked the numbers on the road north, by firelight, with Sefa holding the lantern and arguing about rounding errors, because Sefa argued about everything and Lior had come to love that about her the way you love weather. The totals were simple and monstrous.

A hundred years of interest, compounded. Eleven villages emptied of themselves. The border wards running on borrowed memory until the borrowing collapsed. An army of transformed people marching on the city because the first theft had unmade them and hunger had finished the job. And at the center of it, a tower starving, its heart in a vault beneath the Guildhall, beating out the seconds of a century it had never agreed to lend.

"The principal," Lior read aloud, "is one heart, held in trust. The accrued interest is—" and here they read the figure, and the figure was a number no one should ever have to say in a voice, and the Spire's walls bowed inward around it like a mourner.

"But," Lior said, and held up one finger, because they had learned this trick from watching Vael interrogate people: pause at the crucial moment and everyone leans in, even towers. "*But.* A debt is only real if both parties agree on the terms. And I have been through this book cover to cover, and do you know what I never found? Your signature."

The dark went taut with attention.

"Not once. A hundred years of entries, and every single one is signed by us. Corvane. His successors. Me, now. We wrote the loan. We set the interest. We decided what you owed and what you'd pay and we never once asked you to initial the page." Lior's voice was rising now, and they let it rise, because truth as weapon was half the reason they'd come. "You've been called a debtor in a book you never opened! You've been charged interest on a theft recorded as a gift! And yes—you took memories, you emptied villages, you ate a town's whole self while it slept, and I am not excusing that, don't you *dare* think I'm excusing that—but nobody ever sat down across from you and said, *here are the terms, do you consent?* Nobody treated you as a party. Only a resource. A thing with a heart in it, like a well with water in it."

The silence stretched. Then, slowly, the hollow where the heart had been began to pulse—a slow, arrhythmic flicker, like a lamp deciding whether to exist.

"You're angry," Lior said. "I can feel it. Good. Be angry. Anger means you're still in there, still somebody, still a *who* and not just a *what*. I'm angry too. I'm furious. I've been robbed by everyone who was supposed to teach me—my mistress, my master, my guild, my crown. We have that in common, you and I. We're both the injured party in a ledger full of other people's handwriting."

The pulsing steadied. Almost curious now.

"So here is where a lesser keeper would close the book," Lior said. "Declare the debt void. Hand you your heart and run, and let the war and the wards and the city sort themselves out. Or demand repayment in full—all the memories back, every village restored, every blank refilled—and watch a century of borrowed time collapse on a hundred thousand heads at once. Full forgiveness or full collection. Those are the only two entries anyone's ever imagined in this account." They put their palm flat on the open page. "I'm a guild-trained ledger-keeper. I've spent my whole career finding the third column nobody wrote in. And I think—I *think*—there's a third entry here. But before I offer it, you deserve the one thing this book has never given you."

They lifted the ledger, turned it around, and held it out into the dark.

"Your side of the story. Say it. However you say things. I'll enter it myself, word for word, and I won't soften a syllable. Even the parts about me."

* * *

What came was not words. It was remembering, aimed.

Lior staggered under it—the weight of a hundred years of hunger compressed into a single pouring-out. They saw through the Spire's long sight: the first theft, the young apprentices climbing with cold iron and good intentions, the heart coming loose with a sound like a name being forgotten. They felt the first winter of starvation, the awful discovery that memory could be eaten, the first village, the shame, the rationalization, the shame again, wearing grooves deeper each decade until only the hunger moved smoothly. They felt the transformed ones beyond the border—not as monsters but as kin, the people the wards had been built to hold out, who had been changed by the theft itself, who remembered nothing except that something had been taken from all of them at once, and who had marched toward that taking for a hundred years like blood toward a wound.

And underneath it all, thread after thread, the same small unbearable feeling: loneliness. A tower, standing alone at the edge of everything, holding up the sky, eating to survive, hated by everyone it fed, waiting a century for someone to open the book and ask.

When it ended, Lior was on their knees on the glass-dark floor, and their face was wet, and Maren's hand was on their shoulder—when had she crossed the room?—light and warm and certain.

"Well?" the old woman said. "Enter it."

Lior rose. Took up the pen. And in the oldest ledger in the world, on the first page ever offered to the debtor's own hand, they wrote:

*Testimony of the Spire, given freely, entered verbatim: I was robbed. I starved. I stole to live and hated the living for making me steal. I did not consent to the terms. I never stopped waiting for someone to ask.*

Then Lior looked up into the hollow, and took a breath, and prepared to say the maddest thing they had ever said in a life that had recently contained a great many candidates.

But before they could speak, the floor beneath them chimed—a bell-note from far below, urgent, familiar. The Spire's attention swung downward, toward the Grey Field, toward the war, and Lior felt it too through their boots: the rhythm of the advancing host had changed. Slowed. Stopped.

And threading up through a hundred fathoms of stone, thin as a hair, came a sound that made Lior's heart seize in their chest.

Singing. Thousands of voices, ragged and transformed and searching, singing a lullaby none of them should have known.

The lullaby Lior had paid away.

Behind them, Maren went very still, and said, in the voice of a woman meeting an old debt of her own: "Oh, child. I know that song. I taught it to your mother. And I think it's time you learned what I've been carrying for you all these years."
