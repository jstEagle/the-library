## 30. Maren's Final Lesson

Maren had spent her whole life teaching people that memory was a currency, and now, at the end of it, she finally understood why she had never liked the lesson herself.

She lay in the loft above the shepherd's cottage—three days north of the ford, two days south of the Spire's shadow—and listened to the sounds of the house below. Lior was arguing with the kettle again. That was a thing about Lior: they treated every object as if it might be persuaded, given enough patience and the right tone of voice. The kettle never yielded. Neither had Maren, once. It comforted her to know some things were constant.

Her hands would not stay warm. They hadn't since the ford, since she had poured sixty years of herself into one word of power and felt the decades go out of her like water through open fingers. What remained was thin. She could feel the edges of herself now, the way you can feel the edges of a coat when it has worn through.

But there was one thing left in her keeping. One thing she had carried longer than any other, sealed so deep and so carefully that even the spell at the ford hadn't touched it, because she had built its hiding place out of the same material as her love, and magic does not spend what it cannot tell apart from the keeper.

*Time to pay out the last entry,* she thought, and almost laughed. A lifetime of ledgers around that girl and she'd caught the habit off them in the end.

Footsteps on the ladder. Lior came up with two cups and the particular set of shoulders they wore when they had lost an argument with an appliance and did not wish to discuss it.

"You've made it too strong," Maren said.

"I've made it *correctly*. You just like it weak."

"I like it *kindly*. There's a difference." She took the cup anyway, cradling it for the warmth rather than the taste. "Sit. I want to talk before my talking goes."

"Don't say things like that."

"Boy." She used the old word, the one she'd called them across a kitchen table a hundred years of mornings ago, back when they were small and furious and full of stolen years they didn't know were stolen. "I have said nothing but true things for three days. Let me finish the set."

Lior sat. The candle between them guttered and steadied. Below, faintly, Sefa was singing to the horses—one of those courier road-songs with more verses than sense—and Maren found she was grateful for it. Dying went easier with a soundtrack that didn't know it was one.

"I'm going to give you something," she said. "And I need you to listen first, because if you interrupt me I'll lose the thread, and the thread is the whole point."

Lior's jaw tightened—their listening face, the stubborn one—but they nodded.

"When I took you in," Maren said, "you were six years old, and you arrived at my door knowing how to read but not your own surname. Knowing songs but not where you'd learned them. Knowing that you were sad without knowing what had been taken out of you to make room for the sadness. Do you remember wondering about that? The shape of the hole?"

"I remember the hole," Lior said quietly. "I stopped asking about it when you told me some doors are better shut."

"And I told you that because it was half true, and because the other half would have broken us both." Maren looked into her tea. "Here is the other half, then. Every spell I ever taught you was paid for out of your own past. You knew that. You learned it, and you hated it, and you ran. But you never knew the largest payment. The first one. The one I took before you could walk."

The candle flame leaned. Maren waited until it righted itself.

"The day you were born," she said, "I was there."

* * *

It had been autumn. She remembered—or rather, she had kept, which by then amounted to the same thing—a morning with frost on the window glass of a farmhouse outside Vell, and her sister Wren laughing at her for pacing, and the midwife's hands, and then a cry so fierce and immediate that Maren had laughed too, wet-eyed, because the child sounded less like a newborn than like someone arriving late to an appointment and determined to make up for it.

Wren had held her up—a scrap of fury with black hair plastered flat—and said, "Well? Aunt or godmother?"

"Both," Maren had said. "I don't do things by halves."

They had named the child Edmund. A family name, Wren said, going back generations, a name that meant *wealth-guardian* in the old border tongue, though nobody living remembered the wealth or the guarding. Edmund. Maren had held the child in the blue hour before dawn while Wren slept, and the baby had gripped her finger with the whole strength of a hand no bigger than a plum, and Maren had thought: *this one. This one will matter.*

That was the memory. That was all of it—frost, laughter, a name, a grip. And it should have been the beginning of a life's worth of memories, birthdays and scraped knees and lessons at the hearth, except that three days later the war came over the hills, and with it the men from the court, and with them the conscription of hedge-witches that Maren had spent her own youth dodging.

They wanted memory-workers for the wards. They wanted children who could be shaped into keepers. And they had lists.

Maren had known what would happen to a child marked as kin to an unregistered witch. She had seen it happen to others—taken to the capital, trained, hollowed, spent. So she had done the only thing her art offered. She had gone into her sister's sleeping mind and her nephew's brand-new one, and she had cut.

Not everything. Never everything—that way lies the village near the border, the smiling blanks. Just the thread that connected them. Wren woke not remembering she'd had a second child; the records showed a stillbirth, grief that faded fast because there was little underneath it to fade from. And the baby—Edmund, six days old, who would never remember this anyway—was wrapped in a blanket and carried through the frost to a village two counties over, and left on the step of a childless couple who owed Maren a favor and asked no questions, and whose names Maren then removed from her own memory so that even torture couldn't find them.

She kept only one piece. The birth. The frost and the laugh and the name and the grip. She hid it in the deepest vault she had, behind walls built of her love for the child, and she walked away, and she did not look back, because looking back was how wardens tracked you.

"And then," Maren said aloud, in the loft, "the couple died of fever when you were four, and the parish passed you along, and somehow—by then I had let myself start watching—you ended up apprenticed to a memory-witch in the hill country who was very careful never to ask where you'd come from, because she suspected, and hope is a coward."

Lior had gone very still. "Edmund," they said. "That's—" Their hand went to their collarbone, an unconscious gesture Maren had seen a thousand times and never understood until now. "Why does that name feel like a key that fits a door I've never opened?"

"Because it's yours. It was always yours. I stole it along with the rest." Maren set down her cup. Her hands shook, and she let them. "You asked me once—I think you were nine—why you never dreamed of a mother's face. I told you dreams were weather. I lied. You dreamed of her constantly. Wren had your chin, you know. And her temper, gods help everyone."

"Wren." Lior tried the name like a word in a foreign language. "My mother. Is she—"

"Gone twenty years now, and at peace, and she never knew what she'd lost, which was the mercy I told myself I'd bought you both." Maren's voice cracked on the last of it, and she let that too. "I have told myself a great many mercies, boy. Most of them were thefts wearing nice coats. That's the final lesson, if you want it. Not the magic. The arithmetic. Everything I did, I did believing the sum would come out kind. And the sum never comes out kind. It only comes out *paid*, or *owed*. I owed you a name and a morning and a mother's laugh for fifty years. Here."

She reached across and took Lior's hand—the strong one, the ink-stained one, the one that had held ledgers and swords and her own weight at the ford—and pressed her other palm against the back of it, the old way, the teaching way.

"This will hurt," she warned. "Truth usually arrives dressed as pain first. Let it in anyway."

* * *

The gift went between them like light through water.

Maren felt it leave her—the frost, the laughter, the name, the grip—and felt, in the space it left, not emptiness but *completion*, the way a ledger feels complete when the last line is written and the columns balance. Sixty years she had carried it. Fifty of those she had carried it alone, telling herself she was protecting the child, then protecting the apprentice, then protecting the young runaway from the weight of it, and every year the protection had looked more like cowardice and she had known it and kept carrying it anyway, because putting it down meant admitting what it was.

Now it was down. Now it was his. Hers. *Theirs.* Lior's.

She watched it land. Watched the boy—the keeper, the accountant, her sister's son—go rigid, then soft, then wet-faced, the tears coming without sound the way they always had, even at six, even furious, even running.

"Maren," Lior whispered. Her name. Not *teacher*, not *mistress*. Just her, the way you say the name of someone standing in the same room. "You were *there*. You held me. You—"

"I named you twice," Maren said. "Once Edmund, once the lie I gave the parish clerk. I've been waiting fifty years to say the true one out loud where you could hear it." She smiled, and felt the smile cost her something, and paid gladly. "Happy birthday, Edmund Dravenmoor. Late by half a century. Don't blame the post."

A sound came out of Lior that was half laugh and half sob, and then they were holding her, careful as if she were made of the frost on that faraway window, and Maren held them back with arms that had almost nothing left and found they had exactly enough.

Below, Sefa's song had stopped. Footsteps paused at the foot of the ladder—hesitated—then withdrew, deliberately loud, the courtesy of someone who understood that some rooms must be allowed their hour. Good girl, Maren thought. The courier had a keeper's instincts. The guild had wasted her on parcels.

"There's one more thing," Maren said, when the worst of it had passed through him. "And then I'll sleep, and you'll let me sleep, and we'll quarrel about tea again tomorrow if tomorrow wants to come."

"Anything."

"No. Not anything. That's the trap I raised you in—*anything*, *whatever it costs*, *take from yourself, it's only you*." She gripped his wrist, surprised by the strength still living in her fingers. "Listen to me, Edmund. The Spire will make you an offer. It makes everyone an offer, and its offers are always *reasonable*, that's the horror of it. It will ask for your past—all of it, the whole recorded run of you—and it will be fair, and the arithmetic will balance, and you'll want to sign because signing is what you do."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been starving for three days, and I know what hunger sounds like when it learns to talk." She pulled him close, forehead to forehead, the way she had when he was small and feverish. "Debt is not the only shape a gift can take. Remember that. Whatever you give that tower—give it *freely*, or not at all. A forced payment feeds it. A chosen one might—might—teach it something new. That's not in any book. That's just a hedge-witch's guess, from a woman who spent a life taking and is spending her last hour giving."

She kissed his brow, where the hairline met the skin, where mothers kiss.

"Now put out the candle," she said, "and fetch the girl. If I'm to die in a house, I'll die in a noisy one."

* * *

They came, of course. All of them. Sefa sat on the bed's edge and held her hand and told road-stories with the endings worn off, and Maren corrected the details out of pure spite, and Sefa laughed and cried in the same breath, which Maren considered the correct way to do both. Lior—Edmund, she practiced silently, getting the weight of it right—sat on her other side and read to her from the old ledger, not the accounts but the margins, the jokes and complaints of dead clerks, and she drifted in and out on the tide of it.

Near dawn, in the grey quiet, she surfaced one last time and found Edmund watching her.

"Question," she murmured. "When you get there. When it shows you the theft—it will, it keeps everything—it will show you the day I cut you from your mother. It will show it cruelly, because hunger tells truth sideways. When you see it—" She gathered breath. "See it whole. See the frost too. Both ends of the same morning. Promise me you'll do the accounting properly."

"I promise," Edmund said. "Both ends."

"Good lad." Her eyes were closing. The edges of her had gone soft at last, and she found she didn't mind. Somewhere beyond the walls, far to the north, a tower stood hungry in the dark, and somewhere south a city waited under starless sky, and none of it—not one bit of it—was hers to carry anymore.

"Last entry," she whispered, mostly to herself, entirely content. "*Paid in full.*"

And Maren Thackeray, hedge-witch, thief, teacher, aunt, closed her eyes on a world she had helped break, trusting the boy with her sister's chin to find a kinder way to mend it.

Edmund sat unmoving for a long time. Then he rose, took up the oldest ledger, and turned to the blank page at the end of everything written—and began, in a voice that shook only a little, to draft a counter-offer.
