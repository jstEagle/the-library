## 33. What Lior Gave

The Spire's heart was not a heart at all. It was a room.

That was the first thing Lior understood, standing at the center of everything: the thing Corvane had stolen a century ago, the thing wars had been fought over and villages had been emptied to feed, lived in a round stone chamber no bigger than Maren's old kitchen. The walls were black glass. The floor was warm under Lior's boots, warm like a sleeping animal, and it breathed—slow, vast breaths that moved the dust in spirals on the air.

In the middle of the room stood a pedestal, and on the pedestal lay an absence shaped like a heart. A hollow in the world, edged in faint grey light, humming with hunger so old it had forgotten it was hunger and thought itself simply *the way things were*.

"You came back," said the voice from everywhere—the voice made of Lior's own forgotten words, pitched low and patient as a debt collector who has all the time in the world. "I did not think you would. They rarely come back."

"I'm a keeper of accounts," Lior said. Their voice sounded small in the breathing dark. "We finish what we open."

Behind them, far above and far away, the Battle of the Grey Field was happening. Lior could not see it, but they could feel it through the floor—a tremor like distant thunder, rhythmic, terrible. Sefa was out there. Vael was out there. Corvane had walked out alone hours ago with his ledger closed under his arm, and Lior had not let themselves think about what that meant, because there would be time for grief later or there would be nothing at all, and either way the work came first.

The work came first. That was the first thing the guild had ever taught them, back when they'd been a runaway with ink-stained fingers and nowhere else to go. *The work comes first, apprentice, because the work is how we love people.* Corvane had said that, once, over a cataloguing error worth three years' wages. Lior had laughed then. They didn't laugh now.

"The ledger," the Spire said. "You brought it."

Lior lifted the oldest account book in the guild's keeping. It was heavier than any book had a right to be—not with paper-weight but with weight, the way a promise is heavy, the way a name is heavy. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as cloth. A hundred years of entries. A theft recorded in careful merchant's hand. Interest compounded in villages that woke up smiling and empty. And at the bottom of every page, in red ink faded almost to brown, the same line:

*Debtor: the Spire. Balance outstanding.*

"Before I do anything," Lior said, "I'm going to read it to you. All of it."

"I know what is written in it."

"I know you know." Lior set the book on the pedestal, beside the hollow where a heart should have been, and felt the floor shudder beneath them—not anger, they realized. Something closer to flinching. "But knowing a thing and having someone say it out loud are different debts. Ask anyone who's owed an apology. You've been waiting a hundred years for somebody to read your side of the column. So I'm going to read it. And then I'm going to make you an offer that isn't in the book."

The silence that followed was long enough that Lior counted their own heartbeat against the Spire's slow breathing. Eleven beats. Twelve.

"Read," said the tower.

* * *

So Lior read.

They read the entry dated one hundred years and two months past: *Received into trust, the Heart, one (1), condition: living. Purpose: cessation of hostilities. Signed, T. Corvane, journeyman, on behalf of the Guild of Impossible Things.* They read the note scrawled beneath it in younger handwriting, hurried, guilty: *It agreed. I think it agreed. I have to believe it agreed.*

"It did not agree," the Spire said quietly. "I was drowning. When a drowning creature stops struggling, men call it consent."

"I know," Lior said. "That's why I'm reading all of it."

They read the interest entries. The first one, only three years after the theft: *Payment collected, north road villages, memories of the war, est. four thousand souls. Purpose: sustenance of trust asset.* Then another. Then another, each decade, each hungrier, the handwriting growing steadier and more ashamed in equal measure until the last entries were Corvane's alone, written in the precise hand of a man who had decided that if he must be a thief he would at least be an honest one about the arithmetic.

They read the ward payments. The star payments. The line, dated ninety-one years past, that had started everything: *Borrowed against the Heart: one century of light, one century of warded borders. Due date: upon expiry of term. Penalty clause: see attached.*

"There is no attachment," the Spire said.

"No," Lior agreed. "There never was. That's the oldest trick in lending—you write the penalty somewhere the borrower can't check." They turned the final page. It was blank except for the debtor line, waiting. "And here's where the book ends. Everything after this—" they gestured upward, toward the battle, toward the dark sky, toward the city holding its breath behind its failing walls, "—that's all penalty. Every bit of it. The wards, the stars, the village that forgot itself, Maren—" their voice caught, and they let it catch; the Spire deserved honesty even when it cost something, *especially* then, "—Maren spending herself to nothing on the road. All of it is interest on a loan nobody agreed to."

The floor breathed. The grey light around the hollow pulsed, slow, like something listening so hard it had stopped being able to do anything else.

"What is your offer, keeper?" the Spire asked. "You will offer me the book. They always offer me the book. Burn the ledger, forgive the debt, go home whole. Or you will offer yourself—I showed you that trade already. Your recorded past, all of it, and I release the city. Both offers are lies. The first forgives me nothing. The second feeds me once and leaves me starving again by spring, and you know it, because you did the arithmetic. Chapter eighteen of your own journey, keeper. Ledger arithmetic. I watched you do it."

"I know," Lior said. "Both offers are lies. That's why I'm not making either one."

They took out the second book.

It was much smaller. Cheap paper, guild-stamped, the kind used for courier manifests and kitchen inventories. Lior had filled it over the past three days—on the road, in the ambush's aftermath, sitting beside Maren while she slept her hollowed sleep—by lantern light and by the memory-light Sefa carried, the little lamp full of remembered daylight that the guild had sold to frightened people in chapter four of this disaster. Their handwriting was terrible. There were ink blots. One page had a soup stain.

"This," Lior said, "is a counter-offer. Entry one of a new covenant. But before I read it, you need to understand why it can work, and the only way you'll understand is if I show you. Words won't do it. You've heard words for a thousand years. Promises, contracts, prayers. You need to see it done."

"Show me, then."

"All right." Lior closed their eyes. Opened them again, because closing them felt too much like hiding. "Here's the problem, as I understand it. You take memory because you're starving, and taking is the only way you know. Every memory you eat makes you stronger and makes the world smaller, and eventually there'll be nothing left but you, full and alone in the dark forever. That's not survival. That's just a slower way of dying. So the question isn't how we pay the debt. The question is whether you can learn to *receive*. Whether a gift given freely feeds differently than memory torn away."

"And if it does not?"

"Then we're all doomed anyway and at least I'll have tried something original." Lior smiled, and was surprised to find the smile mostly steady. "But I don't think it doesn't. I think you've never once been offered anything freely, so you've never had the chance to find out. A thousand years of theft, and not one gift. That's not proof gifts don't work. That's proof nobody's ever tried."

The grey light flickered. If a tower could lean forward, Lior thought, this one was leaning.

"So here is the demonstration," Lior said. "Not a payment. Not a trade. A gift. The first free gift in your entire hungry life. And I'm giving you the biggest thing I own."

They put both hands flat on the warm floor.

"The whole of it," Lior said. "My recorded past. Everything the ledgers hold of me. My name—which, incidentally, is a good one, you'll like it. My mother's face, which I got back from Maren two days ago and which I have been hoarding like a dragon ever since. The smell of Maren's kitchen. The argument I ran away over, and the reason underneath the reason, and the shame, and the coming home. Corvane saying 'not yet' in the archive. Sefa laughing so hard at my catalogue of impossible umbrellas that she snorted tea through her nose. The ford. The tunnels. The campfire. All of it. Every entry in the book of me."

The floor went very still. Even the breathing stopped.

"You showed me this trade already," the Spire said, and for the first time its voice wavered, lost its collector's patience, became something older and more frightened. "You refused it."

"I refused it as a *trade*, yes. Because a trade means I get something back, and anything I got back would poison it. A gift has to be free or it isn't a gift—that's the whole mechanism, that's the pattern. So I'm not getting anything back. Not forgiveness, not safety, not the debt cancelled. I'm giving this because it's true and it's mine and giving it is the only proof I have that receiving can work." Lior swallowed. Their hands were shaking on the warm stone. "You'll take everything. Who I am, where I've been, everyone I love. I'll wake up a stranger holding a book I can't read. And you—" their voice broke and they pushed through it, "—you'll have a pattern. One complete example of a memory given instead of taken. You're a creature of accounts. Learn the new entry. Copy it. Every star we relight, every mind you mend, every village you give back to itself—start from this. Start from me."

"If I learn wrongly," the Spire said, "you will have been eaten for nothing."

"Yeah," Lior said. "Welcome to giving. It's terrifying. Nobody tells you that part. Maren never mentioned it either, and she's been doing memory-magic her whole life." They laughed, wetly. "Actually, she probably did mention it and I wasn't listening. Story of our relationship."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of black stone, the battle thundered. Somewhere out there Sefa was fighting—or running, or bargaining, or doing that thing she did where all three looked identical—and Lior would never remember her. Would never remember the tea-snort, or the way she said 'Dravenmoor' when she was annoyed, or the promise she'd made to tell them their own story day by day, never lying about who they'd been. They were about to hand over the person who loved her and leave a stranger in his place, and the stranger wouldn't even know what he'd lost, and somehow that was the cruelest line item of all.

"Do it quickly," Lior said, before courage could reconsider. "I've seen what hesitation costs. Read the entry."

* * *

The Spire read it.

It read the small cheap book aloud in Lior's own voice—of course in Lior's own voice, it had no other—and the words went through Lior like cold water: *Entry the first, new covenant. Item: one keeper of accounts, whole, given freely, without condition, without expectation of return. Purpose: to serve as pattern. Note: the giver requests—does not require, requests—that the recipient learn kindness from the shape of this gift. Note appended in different ink: the giver's friends will come looking for him, and he will not know them, and someone should probably make sure he eats regularly, he forgets.*

"That last note is accurate," Lior managed. "I do forget. Forgot. Will forget. Ugh, tense is going to be such a problem for me."

Then the Spire began, gently, terribly, to take.

It did not hurt. That was the worst discovery. Lior had braced for pain, for tearing, for the white agony Maren had described—but it was warm, like sinking into bathwater, like being *read*. Each memory lifted out of them with the care of a librarian handling a rare volume, and each one glowed briefly in the air between Lior and the hollow before flowing home into the absence that had waited a century to be filled.

Maren's kitchen went first. The smell of bread and burnt rosemary, the chipped blue cup, the woman half-blind squinting at her herbs and calling Lior by the wrong name and then, sometimes, in good moments, by the right one. *Gone,* Lior thought, watching it float away, and found they couldn't feel sad about it, and that inability was its own kind of grief.

Then the guildhall. Ink and dust and Corvane's terrible moods. The stolen page trembling in their hands. *Not yet,* said the memory, in the master's voice, and then the master's voice was gone too, filed away in a tower's hunger, and Lior grasped for it and found smooth blankness where a hundred small mornings had been.

Then Sefa. Oh, Sefa—her laugh went in a burst of gold, the tea-snort, the night on the wall of the courier loft when she'd said *you're the first place I've ever wanted to stay*. Lior felt the memory lift free and screamed, actually screamed, reaching for it, and the Spire paused.

"I can stop," it said. "You may keep some. Keepers always imagine they want to keep some."

"No." Lior was crying and furious about crying. "No partial payments. No exceptions. That's how the old covenant worked—one exception, then another, then a village. Take all of it. *All of it.* If I'm the pattern, I'm the whole pattern." They wiped their face with their sleeve, leaving an ink smear they would never know the origin of. "Besides. She promised to tell me. She keeps her promises. I apparently know that about her even though I won't know her, which is—fine, that's fine, that's completely fine—"

"Liar," said the Spire, almost gently, in Lior's own voice.

"Obviously," Lior said. "Take it anyway."

And it did. The ford and the firelight. The fear and the fury at Sefa's bargain, and the harder thing after, the choosing-to-forgive. Vael's polite terrible questions. The village of smiling blanks. The road that replayed its burning. The birth-day Maren had kept for thirty years and returned as a gift—a mother's face, a sister's arms, a name spoken over a newborn: *Lior. We'll call her—call them—Lior.* It went last, held longest, glowing brightest, and Lior watched their own beginning rise out of them like a lamp being carried into another room, and thought, with the last coherent corner of themselves: *there. That's the one to learn from. That's what a gift looks like. Start there.*

The hollow drank it in.

And the room changed.

There was no dramatics—no flash, no quake. Just warmth spreading through the black glass walls like dawn through frost, and the breathing deepening, steadying, becoming something other than hunger's rhythm. The grey light around the heart-hollow turned, degree by degree, toward gold. And the Spire spoke one more time in Lior's voice—but the voice was different now, lighter, wondering, a voice trying on gratitude for the first time in a thousand years.

"Entry the first," it said. "Received: everything. Given: nothing demanded. Balance—" a pause, vast and astonished, "—the balance is *gift*. I do not have a column for gift."

"You do now," said someone who had been Lior Dravenmoor, smiling at the walls of a tower he no longer knew why he trusted. "Write it in."

Far above, through stone and shadow and the smoke of the Grey Field, the first star kindled.

Then a second. Then a third—and each one burned with a color that meant something, though the young man blinking up at the ceiling of the breathing room could not have said what. He only knew that he was holding a very old ledger he couldn't read, and that his cheeks were wet, and that somewhere beyond the walls people were shouting—not in fear, he somehow knew, but in hope—and that he ought to go to them.

He picked up the book. He had no idea whose handwriting filled it, but it felt, absurdly, like coming home.

The doors of the Spire opened for him, outward, toward the returning light.
