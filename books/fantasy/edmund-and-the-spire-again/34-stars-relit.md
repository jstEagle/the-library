## 34. Stars, Relit

Sefa had run messages through sieges, through floods, through the year the river froze so hard that she'd delivered a marriage contract across it on foot with her boots wrapped in sacking. She knew what waiting felt like in every variety the world offered.

This was worse than all of them.

She stood on the wall of Grey Field with Vael's warrant-men to her left and Corvane's guildsmen to her right—two groups of people who had spent the last week discovering they were on the same side and were still visibly annoyed about it—and she watched the Spire's shadow crawl toward them across the plain like spilled ink deciding where to stop.

Beyond the shadow, the transformed host came on.

They did not look like an army anymore. That was the thing nobody had warned her about. She'd expected monsters, or at least soldiers gone strange. Instead they looked like people who had been *edited*—faces smoothed into something almost right, movements too even, eyes the grey of paper left out in weather. They carried no banners because banners required remembering whose they were. They simply walked, thousands upon thousands, toward the city their old selves had once loved, and Sefa understood with a lurch that most of them had probably been born inside these walls, generations back, before the first theft sent them over the border as payment no one had asked them to make.

"They're slowing," said a guildsman beside her—a bindery boy named Tam, sixteen, who had insisted on coming to the wall because someone had to hold the signal lanterns and he'd lost the argument about who.

"They're not stopping," Sefa said.

"No," Tam agreed. "But slowing."

She checked the lantern oil for the fourth time. It was a courier's habit: when there is nothing you can do, check your equipment. The lanterns were Vael's idea. *If the stars come back,* the inquisitor had said, in that flat court voice that made everything sound like a legal clause, *the host will need to see them. Light travels faster than hope, but not by much.*

Sefa had liked her immediately, which was inconvenient, given that Sefa had sold her out to her three weeks ago and then been forgiven for it in a way that somehow made it worse.

*You pointed her at me,* Lior had said, on the road north, voice cracking like green wood.

*I pointed her at the truth,* Sefa had answered, and then Maren—half a teacher, half a stranger by then—had put a hand on each of their heads like they were children squabbling over bread, and said, *Both things can be true. Sit down and eat.*

That was the last full sentence Maren had spoken before the ford. Sefa kept replaying it. It seemed important not to lose it.

* * *

The battle, if you could call it that, began at dusk—or at what used to be dusk, back when the sky had a schedule.

The transformed host reached the outer ditch and stopped, all together, like a single body reconsidering. Then the front ranks pressed forward again, and the wardens' line bent, and Sefa stopped being able to think of it as anything but what it was: people pushing against people, with the end of the world standing behind one side and a hungry tower standing behind the other.

She ran messages. That was her whole war. Up the wall stairs, along the parapet, down again, carrying scraps of orders between Vael's lieutenants and the guild's hedge-mages, who had spent the morning rigging every lamp, lantern, and memory-glass in the city to burn with light that remembered daylight. The whole eastern wall glowed warm as a kitchen. It was beautiful and it was useless and everyone knew both things, and they lit it anyway, because a city that stops lighting lamps has already lost.

Somewhere past midnight—the hour was a guess; the sky gave nothing away—the shadow from the Spire reached the field.

Sefa felt it arrive before she saw it. A hush went through her head like a hand passing over a shelf of books. She grabbed the parapet. Names went first, she'd learned that much from Lior's notes: names, then faces, then the small facts that hold a self together—your mother's laugh, the smell of your street, why you're fighting. She gripped the stone and recited her own name over and over under her breath like a prayer with poor theology. *Sefa. Sefa Anwar Dray. Courier, third circle. Sister to Pellam. Friend to Lior Dravenmoor, who is up in that tower doing something either brilliant or unforgivable.*

The third name held. She clung to it.

And then the first star came back.

* * *

It happened low in the north, just above the black line of the Spire itself—a single point of light kindling out of nothing, small and stubborn, like the first coal in a banked fire.

The whole wall saw it. Sefa heard the sound the crowd made and would remember it the rest of her life: not a cheer. Something quieter and more enormous than a cheer. Ten thousand people inhaling at once.

"Is that—" Tam started.

"That's one," Sefa said. Her voice shook. "That's only one."

Because she knew how it worked now. Lior had explained it at the last campfire, in that careful ledger-keeper way, drawing columns in the dirt: the Spire could not be forced to give back what it took, and it could not be starved into mercy. But it could be *taught*. And teaching began with a gift freely given—one memory, surrendered without debt, without interest, without invoice. Lior had already paid the first. Everything they were, poured into the tower like water into dry ground, and the ground had learned to hold it instead of swallow it.

One star per gift. That was the arithmetic. The oldest, cruelest ledger in the world, rewritten one line at a time.

The second star came an hour later, in the east. Then a third, closer to the horizon, so near the ground it might have been a lamp on a distant hill.

And here was the thing—the thing Sefa could never quite explain afterward, when people asked: you could feel each one land. Not see it. *Feel* it. Every time a star kindled, somewhere on the wall or in the streets behind her, someone made a small sound—a gasp, a sob, a laugh bitten short—and Sefa understood that those were the givers. Somewhere below her, a grandmother was giving away the memory of her wedding day so that a star could burn. Somewhere a man was surrendering his father's face. They lined up to do it. Vael had organized the queues herself, with warrants and clipboards, because grace apparently still required good administration.

"It's working," Tam whispered. "It's actually—" He stopped.

Down on the field, the host had halted.

Not slowly this time. All at once, mid-stride, thousands of grey faces tilting upward toward the new points of light, and the evenness going out of their movements the way frost goes out of a window when the sun finds it.

* * *

The fourth star rose over the western hills, and a woman in the front rank of the host sat down in the mud.

Sefa saw it through the memory-glass they'd rigged on the wall—Corvane's old spyglass refitted by the bindery mages to show not what was far but what was *lost*. Through its lens the transformed ranks came into focus as themselves: farmers, weavers, two children holding hands, a soldier whose armor bore the crest of a house that hadn't existed for ninety years. People. Just people, worn smooth by a century of being forgotten.

The woman who sat down was crying. Through the glass, Sefa watched her mouth move, shaping a word over and over. The glass couldn't carry sound. But Sefa read her lips well enough, because couriers learn every language of distance.

*Ama.* Mother.

"She's remembering," Tam breathed. "Look—they're all—"

The fifth star. The sixth. They came faster now, the queue of givers in the city moving without pause, and each new light pulled another thread loose from the century-long knot. A man in the host dropped his weapon—not threw it, just let his hands forget why they were holding it—and knelt, pressing his palms flat to the frozen ground as if checking whether the world was real. A cluster of them near the ditch turned to face each other, touching faces, reading each other like letters long delayed. One found another wearing the same family crest and made a sound Sefa heard even from the wall, high and cracked, and she had to stop running messages for a moment and lean on the parapet and breathe.

"Pellam," she said quietly, to no one. "I hope you're watching. I hope you can see this."

Her brother was somewhere in the refugee camps south of the walls, safe since Vael's defection had torn up the crown's uglier plans. He didn't know she was on the wall. He thought she was still angry at him for signing on with the wardens' supply train. There was a whole apology she owed him, and she was suddenly, fiercely determined to live long enough to deliver it in person, with interest.

More stars. Seven, eight, nine. The sky was filling in like a page being written.

And the host was coming apart—not into violence, but into *grief*, which is what an army becomes when it remembers it was ever a hundred thousand separate homes. They wandered the grey field calling names. Some called names they'd only just gotten back. Some stood perfectly still, faces tipped to the light, letting a century pour back in, and Sefa did not envy a single one of them that flood.

A warden near her began to sing. Low, tuneless, half-swallowed—an old border song about coming home late for harvest. Within a verse, thirty people on the wall were humming it, and within three verses, voices from the field below had caught it, ragged and off-key and unmistakably human, and the song crossed the space between the city and the host like a bridge being thrown plank by plank.

Sefa sang too. She was terrible at it. She sang anyway.

* * *

Near dawn—or the hour that would have been dawn—the twelfth star rose directly above the Spire, larger than the rest, burning white-gold, and the shadow on the field stopped spreading.

Stopped. Drew back. Not retreating, exactly. More like a tide remembering the moon.

Through the memory-glass, Sefa saw the tower's black face change. Veins of light ran up it, faint as ink showing through paper, climbing from the base toward the crown where—she knew, everyone knew by now—Lior Dravenmoor stood in the heart of the thing, reading aloud from a ledger a century deep, refusing to close the account until every entry was honored.

"You'd better survive this," Sefa muttered. "You absolute disaster of a person. You'd better survive it, because I have promised myself I'm going to tell you your own story, all of it, every stupid detail, starting with the day you set Corvane's accounting ink on fire trying to make tea, and if you die I will have wasted a truly excellent story."

Tam glanced at her. "Who are you talking to?"

"A friend who can't hear me."

"Ah." He nodded solemnly, the way sixteen-year-olds do when they understand completely. "I do that with my mum."

By full light, the field was full of people kneeling, weeping, embracing, wandering toward the walls with their hands open and empty. The wardens opened the gates. Nobody had ordered it; Vael simply looked at the crowd beyond the ditch, looked at her warrants, and said, "Those are citizens," in a voice that permitted no further entries on the matter, and the gates swung wide.

The thirteenth star came at midmorning. The fourteenth before noon.

They did not stop.

* * *

Sefa found Vael on the wall at sundown, watching the sky fill in. The inquisitor had removed her court badge sometime during the night—it was gone from her collar, leaving a paler patch of cloth—and she stood with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman auditing the heavens.

"Fourteen yesterday," Vael said, without turning. "Twenty-two today. At this rate, the sky will be fully restored in—what does your guild arithmetic say?"

"Months," Sefa said. "Maybe a season. Each gift buys one star, and there are a lot of gifts owed." She hesitated. "There are a lot of people willing to give them, though. The queue outside the Guildhall goes around three blocks. Corvane says it's the longest line the guild has ever had for anything, including the year they sold luck."

Something moved at the corner of Vael's mouth. On her, it counted as laughter.

"And the keeper?" Vael asked. "Any word from the tower?"

Sefa looked north. The Spire stood against the darkening sky, threaded with light now, less a monument than a wick. "Nothing direct. But the stars keep coming, and Lior always did keep excellent accounts." She swallowed. "We'll know when they're done. The ledger will say so."

"How?"

"Because Lior writes everything down," Sefa said. "Even goodbyes. *Especially* goodbyes."

They stood together while the twenty-third star kindled, low over the eastern hills, and somewhere beneath it Sefa heard—faint, impossible, carried on wind that had no business being kind—a sound like a great many voices saying their own names, one after another, patient as rain, as if the whole world were taking attendance and finding, entry by entry, that everyone was present.

Then a guild runner arrived at Vael's elbow, breathless, holding out a strip of paper sealed with the Guildhall's mark.

"For the courier Sefa," he panted. "From the Spire. From—" He blinked. "From inside it, ma'am. A bird brought it. Nobody knows how."

Sefa broke the seal with hands that would not entirely hold still.

The handwriting was Lior's. Of course it was Lior's. But it was shakier than she'd ever seen it, and some of the words had been written twice, as if the writer kept forgetting the sentence halfway through and refused to give up on it.

*Dear Sefa. I don't know how much of me is left to write this, so I'll be quick. The covenant holds. The Spire is learning—it takes only what's offered now, and it gives echoes back, changed but true. Tell the city the accounts are open and the debt is forgiven. Tell Vael she was right about the light traveling faster than hope, but only barely.*

*Tell my teacher I finally understand the lesson.*

*And Sefa—I gave everything away, like I said I would. So I need you to come tell me who I am. Start at the beginning. Don't leave anything out, even the parts that make me look ridiculous.*

*Especially those.*

*Come soon. The stars are almost done, and I find I don't want to meet whoever I am now without you here to introduce us.*

*—L.*

Sefa read it twice. Then she folded it with a courier's precision, tucked it inside her coat over her heart, and turned to Vael.

"I need a horse," she said, "and the fastest road north."

Vael was already unpinning her own travel cloak. "You'll need two," she said. "I'm coming to see this ledger for myself."
