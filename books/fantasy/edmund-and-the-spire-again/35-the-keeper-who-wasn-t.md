## 35. The Keeper Who Wasn't

Inquisitor Second-Class Amara Vael had spent eleven years learning to read people the way her mother read tea leaves: by the dregs left at the bottom. She could tell a liar by the way he swallowed before answering. She could tell a guilty man by how still he made himself. She could tell a grieving woman by the hands—grief always lived in the hands first.

She had never learned to read someone who was not there.

That was the problem with Lior Dravenmoor, sitting on the steps of the Spire like a dropped parcel.

Vael had come up the grey field at dawn, past the halted host of the transformed—the ones who remembered their names now, who stood in loose, bewildered clusters like guests at a wedding where they'd forgotten the couple—and she had warrants in her coat and three days without sleep in her eyes, and she had rehearsed the arrest seventeen times on the road. *Lior Dravenmoor, you are taken into custody for crimes against the crown's wards, for theft of guild property, for—* except that the charges had dissolved somewhere around the ford, along with her certainty, and what remained in her mouth now was nothing she'd rehearsed at all.

Because Lior looked up at her approach and did not know her.

Not the way a criminal doesn't know her, playing dumb. Not the way a stranger doesn't know her, indifferent. Lior looked at her the way you look at weather. Polite. Slightly curious. A face waiting to be told what it was looking at.

"Inquisitor," Lior said. The voice was right. The voice was exactly right, and that was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "You look like you've been walking a long time. Would you like to sit? The steps are cold but they hold you up."

Vael stood there with her hand on her warrant and felt, absurdly, like a bailiff arriving to arrest a house.

"I'm here to take you into custody," she said, because procedure is a kind of prayer, and she needed one.

"Oh," said Lior. "Did I do something?"

"You—" Vael stopped. She looked down at the ledger in Lior's lap. It was the oldest book she had ever seen outside a museum; its cover was the color of storm-water, and its pages moved faintly when no wind touched them, as though breathing. Lior's hands rested on it the way a child rests hands on a sleeping dog. Fondly. Without claim.

"Do you know what that is?" Vael asked.

"No." Lior looked down at it too, and smiled—a small, rueful smile that Vael recognized from interrogation records she'd memorized and then, later, from a smuggler tunnel lit by lamps that remembered daylight. "It came with me. I think I was carrying it when I—when whatever happened, happened. I can't read it. I've tried. The letters move." A pause. "I don't remember much of anything, actually. There's a woman—Sefa—who keeps telling me things. She says I'm funny. I'm choosing to believe her."

Something in Vael's chest performed a maneuver she had no name for.

She sat down on the step beside them. It seemed suddenly important not to loom.

* * *

Here is what Inquisitor Vael knew about herself, in the honest ledger she kept behind her ribs: she had joined the court's service at nineteen because certainty was the only thing that had ever made her feel safe. Her father had been a customs officer who hanged a boy over a mislabeled crate of figs, and the shame of it had taught her early that being wrong was the most expensive thing a person could be. So she built herself out of procedure. Warrants. Chains of evidence. Questions asked in the correct order. If every step was correct, she could never be wrong, and if she could never be wrong, she could never be her father.

For eleven years it had worked beautifully.

Then a courier named Sefa had traded her a secret for her brother's safety, and Vael had followed the thread of it expecting a smuggler and found an account book instead. Then she had found the court's own fingerprints on the wards' collapse—orders dated years back, memos about "managed decline," a whole bureaucracy that had known the sky was going out and decided the knowledge was more dangerous than the dark. Then she had watched a hedge-witch spend decades of her own mind in a single spell at a ford, and a guildmaster walk alone toward an army with his ledger under his arm like a man going to post a letter, and a runaway apprentice stand inside a hungry tower and offer it, of all things, a different way to eat.

And now here she sat on cold stone next to the person she'd crossed half a country to arrest, and the person offered her a boiled sweet from a pocket.

"Peppermint," Lior said. "Sefa gave me a whole bag. She says I liked them. I don't remember liking them, but my mouth got happy when I tried one, so apparently some of me remembers."

Vael took the sweet. Her hand, she noticed distantly, was shaking.

"Lior," she said carefully. "Do you know who I am?"

"Sefa said your name. Vael. She said you were hunting us and then you weren't, and she said it in a way that meant I should be careful and also maybe grateful, which sounds complicated."

"It is," Vael said. "I arrested your master. Or—I took the Guildhall. Corvane surrendered it before I could. He burned the current ledgers and gave the old one to—" She stopped. "To you. You had it."

"Did I?" Lior looked at the book again, this time with something almost like suspicion, the way you regard a cat that has chosen you for reasons unknown. "I suppose that explains why everyone keeps asking me if I have it. Sefa checks about four times a day. She tries to be casual about it. She's very bad at casual."

"The court wants that book destroyed," Vael said. "Or locked in the deep archive, which is the same thing with better furniture. The entries in it are—" treasonous, she had been ordered to say; damning, she would have said a month ago; "—true," she said now. "Every line of it. And truth like that doesn't stay in archives. It gets out. It changes things."

"Is changing things bad?"

Vael laughed. It startled her; she couldn't remember the last time the sound had come out of her unbidden. "The court would say yes. The court has an entire department for saying yes."

"And you?"

She turned the peppermint over in her mouth and considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, which was more than she'd given any question in eleven years.

"I used to have a very good answer," she said. "I wrote it down once and memorized it. Certainty, procedure, the safety of the many. But I found out the people above me knew the stars were dying and filed it under 'pending review,' and I found out the Spire wasn't a monster but a creditor, and I found out—" she gestured vaguely at the sky, where the first relit stars hung like stitches in a wound, "—that the fix for everything was a girl with a ledger teaching a tower manners. None of that was in my training."

"So you don't know either," Lior said, with evident relief. "Oh, good. I thought it was just me."

* * *

They sat while the morning came up properly. Below them, the grey field was turning, slowly, back into a field—color returning to it in patches, like ink soaking into cloth in reverse. Here and there among the transformed, small scenes were occurring that Vael suspected historians would argue about for a century: a woman with bark for skin weeping over a soldier's outstretched hand; two men who had been enemies at dawn standing shoulder to shoulder staring at the same star, neither willing to be the first to speak.

"They're going to need somewhere to go," Vael said. "The transformed. The border villages. Half the refugee camps will want to go home and the other half will burn their old homes rather than return, and both halves will be right." She heard herself and winced. "Sorry. That's inquisitor thinking. I catalog outcomes for a living. It's a terrible habit."

"No, keep going," Lior said, leaning forward with genuine interest. "I like watching you do it. It's like watching someone knit very fast. What else?"

"What else." Vael rubbed her eyes. "What else is that the court will send someone to replace me within the month, and they won't be me, and they'll want the ledger, and they'll want you—not punished, necessarily. Filed. There are worse things than punishment. Punishment at least assumes you're a person."

Lior nodded slowly, absorbing this the way they seemed to absorb everything: completely, freshly, without the calluses memory usually leaves.

"And what will you tell them?" Lior asked. "When they ask where I am?"

And there it was. The actual question. The one she'd carried up the grey field in her coat alongside the warrants, feeling it weigh more every mile.

Vael had imagined this moment on the road, in all its versions. In one, she arrested Lior anyway—procedure as prayer, even faithless. In another, she burned her warrant and walked away, romantic and useless. In a third, she lied fluently, and hated herself efficiently, and got away with it.

None of those were what came out.

"I'll tell them the truth," she said. "A new policy. I'm told it's catching." She drew the warrant from her coat—the crisp vellum, the seal, the name *Dravenmoor* in a clerk's tidy hand—and held it up between two fingers. "This says you're to be taken into protective custody pending investigation. Protective custody. Do you know what that means, legally?"

"That I'm protected?"

"That you're a *thing* being protected. Like a document. Like a relic. Custody of persons is 'detention'; custody of objects is 'protective holding.' Someone in the chancery chose the word deliberately." She tore the warrant in half. Then, because she was thorough, in half again. "But here's what I choose it to mean: that there is a person in the world whom the court considers dangerous enough to watch and valuable enough to keep alive, and that watching is best done closely, and that I am volunteering."

Lior blinked. "You're offering to guard me."

"I'm offering to guard the ledger," Vael corrected, with the ghost of her old precision. "And its keeper. Whom I am classifying, for administrative purposes, as part of the ledger. It's the only legal framework that fits. Believe me, I've checked."

"That's ridiculous," Lior said, delighted. "That's completely ridiculous. Sefa will love it."

"There's one condition." Vael held up a finger, and eleven years of interrogations put iron in her voice, briefly, before she caught it and set it down. "No—there isn't, actually. I was going to say you'd answer my questions, but you can't. You don't have the answers anymore. That's the whole—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "My condition is that I get to learn it with you. Whatever this covenant is, whatever the Spire is becoming, whatever you gave up—I was on the wrong side of it for most of the story, and I'd like to be on the right side of the rest. That's not a condition. That's a request."

Lior regarded her for a long moment. And Vael had the strange, vertiginous sense of being read by someone with no technique at all—no reading of hands or swallows, just open attention, the kind children and dogs and, apparently, amnesiacs have access to.

"All right," Lior said. "But I should warn you, I ask a lot of questions. About everything. Sefa says it's exhausting."

"She would know," Vael said, and then, hearing the shape of what she'd said, added quickly, "I mean—from the tunnel. She talked about you. On the road north."

"Mm," said Lior, in a tone that suggested the amnesia did not extend to noticing when people blushed. "She does that."

* * *

Sefa found them an hour later, coming up from the camp with two tin cups and the expression of a woman who had run courier lines through three wars and still found mornings the hardest shift. She saw Vael sitting on the Spire's steps, saw the torn warrant scattered like confetti, and stopped dead.

"You didn't," she said.

"I did," Vael said.

"You *arrested* them?" Sefa's voice climbed. "After everything—you absolute—"

"Protective custody," said Lior helpfully. "I'm classified as part of the ledger. Isn't that wonderful? I've never been a document before."

Sefa looked from Vael to Lior to the confetti and back, and Vael watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes—the same arithmetic Vael herself had done on the road, the slow conversion of an enemy into something else, each term refusing to resolve until suddenly it did.

"You're staying," Sefa said. Not a question. A courier confirming a delivery route.

"I'm staying," Vael confirmed. "Someone has to keep the court off them, and I'm unfortunately the only person alive who knows exactly how the court thinks, because I was the court's best thinking for eleven years." She paused. "Also, someone should probably write down what happens next. Properly. With dates. I find I trust ledgers now, against all my prior professional judgment."

Sefa sat down on Lior's other side and handed out the cups—some kind of barley tea, bitter and hot. For a while none of them spoke. The Spire rose above them, black and quiet, and if it was listening, it gave no sign, which Vael supposed was itself a kind of progress. A tower that used to take. A tower learning to wait.

"Can I ask you something?" Lior said eventually, to Vael. "Everyone keeps telling me pieces of who I was. Sefa tells me stories. Maren—when she could—she told me things. But you knew me least, and shortest. So maybe you can answer honestly." They turned the cup in their hands. "Was I good? The person I was. Before. Because nobody will quite say it straight, and I'd like one straight answer, even if it's bad."

Vael thought about interrogation transcripts. About a frightened apprentice who, questioned politely and terribly, had answered every question true and none of them usefully, protecting a stolen page with nothing but stubbornness. About a fugitive in a tunnel who had paused, mid-flight, to free a warden's boot from a collapsing grate. About a ledger-keeper who had stood in front of a starving god and refused both the easy options, and paid for the refusal with everything they were.

"Yes," Vael said. "Straight answer. They were good. Annoyingly so. It made my job very difficult."

Lior smiled—that small rueful smile again, but warmer now, like a room someone had lit from inside. "Good," they said. "Then I have a standard to live up to, and I don't even have to remember failing it. That's convenient."

Below them, Sefa's brother appeared at the edge of the camp, waving; and beyond him the field went on greening; and above them the new stars held their places like patient clerks.

Vael drank her terrible tea and began, silently, to draft her first report to a court that would hate it: *Subject located. Subject cooperative. Subject poses no threat except to established lies. Recommend long-term observation.*

She signed it, in her head, *Amara Vael, formerly Inquisitor Second-Class.*

*Guardian,* she amended. *Currently.*
