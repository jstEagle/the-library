## 36. Relearning a Face

The first thing Sefa learned about telling someone their own story was that you had to start smaller than you thought.

Not the wards falling. Not the ledger, or the Spire, or the night the stars went out. Lior had heard all of that already—from Vael, from Corvane's surviving clerks, from half the city that had decided Lior Dravenmoor was either a saint or a swindler depending on which tavern door you'd come in through. The big story everyone could give them. It was the small ones Sefa kept for herself.

So on the first morning of the rest of it, she sat across the breakfast table in the little house behind the Guildhall—Corvane's old house, though nobody called it that anymore—and said:

"You hate porridge."

Lior looked down at their bowl. They'd been eating it with the grim diligence of someone paying off a debt. "I'm eating it now."

"You're *enduring* it." Sefa reached over and slid the honey pot across the table. "You used to smuggle honey into everything. Corvane caught you sweetening the ink once because you said ledgers tasted bitter."

"Did I?" Lior stirred the honey in, and something crossed their face—not memory exactly, but the shape where one might go. Like pressing a thumb into dough and finding it still warm. "That sounds like me."

"It is you. That's the whole point." She tried to keep her voice light. She'd made a promise about that too—never to lie about who they were, but also never to make the weight of it heavier than it had to be. "Day by day, remember? Today's lesson: you are a person who puts honey in things."

"I can learn that," Lior said, and smiled, and Sefa's chest did the painful folding thing it had been doing for weeks now, every time Lior smiled at her like she was a kind stranger instead of—

Instead of whatever they had been to each other. That part she hadn't told them yet. She wasn't sure it counted as lying if she just... waited. She'd decided it didn't. She'd decided this at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling, and then decided again at dawn, and then decided a third time when Lior came down the stairs rubbing sleep from their eyes and said good morning like the morning was genuinely good, like seeing her was a gift rather than an ache.

She kept the promise. Mostly. Some days the promise felt like carrying water in her hands.

* * *

She built a routine out of it, because routines were what couriers had, and Sefa trusted nothing she couldn't put in a satchel.

Mornings were small facts. The honey. The way Lior walked with their chin up when they felt watched, which Sefa recognized as armor because she'd seen them do it in the Guildhall corridors a hundred times. Their habit of reading menus left to right, bottom first, "like the account balances at the end," Lior had explained once, and Sefa had laughed so hard she'd had to sit down on a market crate.

Afternoons were bigger. She took Lior walking through the city with a purpose, pointing out landmarks of their own life. The alley behind the chandler's where they'd hidden from a guild warden during their first week. The fountain where they'd sat all night after the stars went out, doing arithmetic on a scrap of bark because numbers were the only thing that made sense. The spot outside the sealed Guildhall doors where they'd stood with the oldest ledger under their coat while wardens hammered at the gates.

"Do I feel anything?" Lior asked there, standing very still, eyes closed. "At these places. Should I?"

"I don't know," Sefa admitted. "You're the first person I've ever done this for."

They opened their eyes. "Then we'll find out together. That's fair. That's—" A pause. "That's more than fair, actually. Most people don't get to meet themselves with someone else holding the map."

Sefa looked away, because her eyes were doing something inconvenient. "You always said things like that," she managed. "Annoyingly generous things. Made it very hard to stay annoyed at you."

"What did you get annoyed at me about?"

"Oh, *everything.*" Gratitude flooded her—here was safe ground, here was a story with texture. "You alphabetized my courier satchels without asking. You argued with Corvane about rounding errors in front of clients. You once spent an entire evening explaining why the guild's exchange rates were morally indefensible to a man who just wanted to buy a luck charm."

"That last one sounds justified."

"It was. That's what made it insufferable." She bumped their shoulder. "You were right about the rates. You were wrong about the satchels."

"I stand by the satchels," Lior said, with such immediate certainty that Sefa stopped walking.

"*There* you are," she said softly.

Lior blinked. "There who is?"

"You. That's not a memory, that's just—you. It survived." She grinned, sudden and fierce, and turned the moment into motion before it could become something that made them both cry in the street. "Come on. Next lesson. You owe a woman named Petra three coppers and have for two years, and today we're going to pay it, because debt is basically your religion."

* * *

Evenings were the hardest, and Sefa saved the hardest for last, because she'd learned from watching Maren die that the things you save for last are the things that keep you going until you get there.

She told those stories by lamplight, sitting across from Lior, sometimes holding their hand and sometimes not, depending on how brave she was feeling.

"The night before you left for the Spire," she said one evening, "you came to my room and asked me if I thought you were a good person."

Lior's hand stilled around the cup. "What did you say?"

"I said I thought you were a *complicated* person, and that good was going to depend on the next month." She laughed a little. "Very helpful. Very reassuring. You thanked me anyway. You said, 'That's the most honest answer anyone's given me all year.'"

"And then I walked into a tower and gave away everything I was."

"And then you walked into a tower and gave away everything you were," Sefa agreed quietly. "And I've been angry at you about it every single day since, and I will probably continue to be angry at you forever, and I need you to know that the anger and the—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Kept the promise. "The anger doesn't cancel anything else out. That's the lesson for tonight. Complicated entries. Both columns."

Lior studied her for a long moment. Lamplight moved on their face, and Sefa realized with a jolt that she was learning them too—learning this version, this stranger with her friend's hands and her friend's stubborn chin, and that grief for the old Lior and love for this one were going to have to share a house inside her whether they liked it or not.

"Sefa," Lior said carefully. "Can I ask you something? And you can refuse to answer. You've been very clear about the rules, and I honor the rules."

Her stomach dropped. "Ask."

"When you tell me these stories—you hold back sometimes. I can see it. There's a shape in your face like a word you're not saying." Lior's voice was gentle, unbearably gentle. "Is it something bad? Did I do something to you?"

"No." It came out fast, too fast. "No, you never—you were my best friend. Are. Were. The tense is complicated."

"But there's a word you're not saying."

"There's a whole *sentence,*" Sefa said, before her brain could stop her mouth, and then pressed both hands over her face.

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the relit stars were doing their patient work over the rooftops, and the city murmured its evening murmur, and Sefa sat in the wreckage of two months of careful waiting.

When she finally lowered her hands, Lior was watching her with an expression she recognized—had *always* recognized—even in a face that had forgotten her. It was the look they'd worn decoding the ledger. Patient. Steady. Braced for arithmetic that might hurt.

"Tell me the sentence," Lior said. "Or don't. But either way, I want you to know that whoever I was—I trust their choice of you. Out of everyone in the world who could have held my story, they gave it to you. So whatever the sentence is, it can't be wrong."

Sefa breathed. In. Out. Courier's discipline: deliver the message, however heavy.

"We were going to be more than friends," she said. "After. When you came back. We'd never said it out loud, but we both knew, and I was going to tell you at the gate when you returned, except you returned without—" She gestured, helpless, at all of them. "So I decided I wouldn't tell you. Because it didn't seem fair. Making you responsible for a feeling you couldn't remember agreeing to. That would be like forging your signature."

Lior was quiet for a long time.

Then: "It's a good metaphor. Very you. You always thought in ledgers around me even though you claimed to despise them."

Sefa laughed wetly. "Did I?"

"Constantly." Lior turned their cup slowly on the table. "Here's my problem. I can't return a feeling I don't have yet. But I can tell you what's true right now, which is that you are my favorite person I know. All five weeks of you that I know. And I would like—" They stopped, started again, ears going pink. "I would like permission to fall in love with you properly. On purpose. As myself, the current edition. Not as a debt repaid to someone I used to be. Is that—is that an entry you'd accept?"

Sefa stared at them.

"You absolute *ledger-keeper,*" she said, and leaned across the table and kissed them, which was not in any account book anywhere, and was therefore, by the guild's oldest definitions, impossible—and the Guild of Impossible Things had never once refused new inventory.

* * *

After that, the stories changed flavor.

She still told them daily—the promise stood, and Lior held her to it, insisting that the record be complete—but now there was room in the telling for the future too. They invented traditions deliberately, so that Lior would have new memories growing alongside the grafted ones. Thursday nights became honey-in-everything nights. They adopted a half-starved cat from the courier stable whom Lior immediately named Receipt, "because he follows me around demanding attention I never agreed to give, which makes him a liability."

Petra got her three coppers. Lior cried a little about it, which confused them both until Sefa guessed: "You paid debts you remembered. This is the first one you didn't. I think it counts double for you."

"You should write that down," Lior said, sniffling. "In the new ledger. 'Dravenmoor, Lior: one (1) debt discharged, sentimental value incalculable.'"

"I'm not putting that in the official record."

"Then I will. I'm the keeper now. I can enter whatever I want."

"You absolutely cannot, that's how Corvane started."

But the truest change was slower than any of it, and Sefa only noticed it one grey morning in the market, weeks on, when Lior paused at the fountain—the fountain, the arithmetic-on-bark fountain—and said, absently, "I sat here the night the stars went out," and then kept walking, unremarked, unremarkable, a fact resting in them like a stone worn smooth.

Not remembered. Not quite. But *inhabited.* A story told so many times it had grown roots.

She caught their sleeve. "Do you realize what just happened?"

"I quoted your own lessons back at you?" Lior said, but they were smiling the particular smile, the one she was fairly sure—fairly *hopeful*—was becoming theirs again, new-grown.

"You *knew* that. Without me telling you this time."

"I know lots of things now." Lior laced their fingers through hers, casual as breathing, a gesture they had apparently decided was theirs regardless of provenance. "Mostly I know you. Turns out you're the part of the story that stuck."

Sefa opened her mouth to say something devastating and witty and was spared the effort, because at that moment a courier boy came pelting up the lane with a satchel and a face full of urgent news, skidding to a halt in front of them.

"Beg pardon—Keeper Dravenmoor—" He thrust out a folded paper, sealed with the black wax of the reformed guild. "From the Spire road. Marked urgent. Says it's for the keeper's eyes only, and—" he swallowed, "—and the rider said to tell you it's about a name. A name appearing in the margin."

Sefa felt Lior go still beside her.

Lior broke the seal with steady hands, read the single line inside, and then read it again. When they looked up, their expression was one Sefa had never seen on the current edition and somehow knew belonged to every edition there had ever been: wonder with its sleeves rolled up, ready to work.

"Well?" she demanded. "What name?"

Lior handed her the paper. At the top, in a clerk's careful transcription of a margin note found in the oldest ledger—the ledger itself, traveling north with the spring convoy—was written:

*Edmund.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink, as if the page itself had only just decided: *Edmund is asking to come home.*
