## 37. The New Ledger

The Guildhall smelled different in spring.

Lior noticed it the way they noticed most things now—slowly, with a small pause of wonder, like meeting an old friend who had changed their hair. Before, the great hall had smelled of ink and tallow and Corvane's pipe-smoke, and underneath all of it, faintly, of dust that had settled on ledgers no one dared open. Now there was bread. Someone had installed an oven in the east kitchen—Sefa's doing, though she'd claimed credit for "the entire renovation" until Vael pointed out she'd only carried the flour—and the smell of morning loaves drifted through the corridors and out the open doors into the courtyard, where refugees-turned-apprentices were learning to bind books instead of steal them.

Lior sat at the long table beneath the window, a fresh ledger open before them.

It was not the oldest ledger. That one rested now in a case of glass and iron at the far end of the hall, where anyone could come and read it, because secrets had been what broke everything in the first place. Lior still couldn't read most of it. The pages swam when they looked too long—the century of entries, the debt column, the name *Spire* written in a hand that might have been their own, once. But they could read enough. They could read the shape of it. And they had spent the winter learning to read the rest aloud, entry by entry, to anyone who wanted to listen, which turned out to be nearly everyone, because people are hungry for the truth about themselves even when the truth is terrible.

Today, though, was not about the old book.

Today was about this one: new, unblemished, its spine still stiff, its first page blank as a winter field waiting for seed. The guild's bookbinder—a round, cheerful woman named Odile who had lost her village to the Spire's hunger and remembered nothing of it except that she liked making things whole—had presented it to Lior three days ago with tremendous ceremony and a smudge of glue on her nose.

"The first page is yours," she'd said. "I made the paper myself. From the reeds by the Grey Field." She'd said it lightly, but her hands had trembled, and Lior had understood: paper grown from the ground where the war ended, where the transformed host had halted mid-march and remembered its names. Paper with memory in it, or the ghost of memory, or whatever it was that given things left behind.

Lior had been staring at that blank page for three days.

Not from fear, exactly. Their hands were steady—that was the strange part. A year ago they would have chewed the quill to splinters over an entry this important. Now the blanks inside them were simply... there, like furniture in a dark room. You learned where they were. You walked around them. Sometimes you bumped into one—a laugh they didn't recognize surfacing in their own throat, the smell of Maren's kitchen arriving without context, ink, always ink—and sometimes the blanks bumped back, and Lior would find themselves standing in the middle of a corridor unable to say why they'd come there. But the hands were steady. Whatever they had given away, it had left the hands behind.

*You gave them your past,* Sefa said once, when Lior complained about this. *Past doesn't hold pens. Present does.*

*That's not how ledgers work,* Lior had said.

*Neither is anything else anymore,* she'd said, and stolen half their breakfast, which was how she said affection.

Lior dipped the quill. Outside, the courtyard noises rose and fell: Odile teaching a boy of perhaps ten to fold a signature properly; two former wardens arguing amiably about whether a courier route through the Ashlands counted as impossible or merely stupid (the answer, historically, was both); somewhere, Vael's voice, crisp and dry, drilling new inquisitors on the difference between investigation and interrogation. *"If you already know the answer,"* she was saying, *"you are not investigating. You are rehearsing. Go back and ask better questions."*

Lior smiled. Then they bent to the page and wrote:

---

**Entry the First. Of the New Covenant.**

**In the year following the relighting of the stars, being the first year of honest accounts.**

**Item: the Guild of Impossible Things hereby dissolves itself as it was constituted, namely as a house of debts, holdings, and trusts held without consent, and reconstitutes itself as follows.**

**Item: nothing shall be taken that is not freely given. This applies to goods, to memories, to labor, to names, and to hearts, whether of persons or of towers.**

**Item: every account in this book shall be readable by any member who wishes to read it. No page shall be sealed, hidden, or stolen, save by vote of the full membership, and then only against those who would harm the covenant.**

**Item: the Spire is recognized as a person, being the eldest person known to us, and its account shall be kept with the same courtesy we extend to any elder: it may ask, and we may refuse, and both asking and refusing shall be recorded without shame.**

**Item: the office of Keeper of Accounts is hereby renamed. It shall be called the Office of Listening, and its holder shall write down what is offered, refuse what is not, and keep the arithmetic kind.**

Lior paused there, quill hovering.

*Keep the arithmetic kind.* They hadn't planned to write that. It had arrived the way Maren's kitchen smell arrived—from somewhere below thought, from some room in them they could no longer enter but whose light still came under the door. They suspected it was hers. They suspected a great many surviving fragments were hers, because Maren had spent her last stored memory on the day Lior was born, pressing it into their hands like a loaf still warm, and gifts echo back changed, and what comes back from a gift is usually the giver.

They wrote the last item slowly:

**Item: the undersigned, having given their own recorded past as the first free gift, records here only what remains, which is this: I do not know who I was. I know what I chose. The second, it turns out, is enough to build on.**

**Signed, Lior Dravenmoor, Listener.**

The ink dried in the window light. Lior read it over twice, found nothing they wished to change—which was rare enough to be remarkable—and blotted the page.

Then, because a covenant is only words until someone lives it, they got up and went to work.

* * *

The morning's business, these days, looked almost nothing like the guild's old business.

Under Corvane, the day had begun with the reading of demands: who owed what, whose impossible goods were forfeit, which trusts had matured. Under Lior—or rather, under nobody, which was the point—the day began with the Offerings Book, a slim volume kept beside the door where anyone could write what they wished to give, trade, or ask.

This morning's entries, in various hands:

*Marla the dyer offers three vats of color-that-remembers-sunlight, freely given, to whoever is rebuilding the border chapels.*

*Aldous asks: does anyone remember the tune my mother used to sing? I have the words but the melody went with the dark year. Will trade firewood or stories.*

*Sefa has posted: COURIER RUNS TO THE BORDER VILLAGES NOW LEAVING EVERY THIRD DAY. IF YOU HAVE LETTERS FOR PEOPLE THE SPIRE HAS ASKED ABOUT, BRING THEM BY NOON. YES THIS INCLUDES YOU, ALDOUS, STOP WRITING YOUR LETTERS ON SCRAP, THE SORTING OFFICE IS NOT A KINDLING PILE.*

And at the bottom, in careful, childish letters: *Tansy (age 10) gives one (1) tooth, fallen out this morning, to the Spire, if it wants it. It can grow something with it maybe. Please do not be sad, tower.*

Lior read that last one four times. Then they copied it into the new ledger exactly as written, because the covenant's first rule was that all offerings were honored equally, and because they suspected—correctly, as it would turn out—that the Spire would keep the tooth forever.

The Spire asked, now. That was the miracle no one quite stopped marveling at. Every third day, a courier—usually Sefa, who claimed the routes out of pure self-interest ("the roads are boring, the villages have good bread")—carried questions north: *May we take the memory of the harvest song from Hollowmere? May we have the story of the flood, told by someone who chooses to tell it? Is there anyone in Wend who would give the memory of their grandmother's garden, freely, so that we may learn what growing things mean?*

And the villages answered. Not always yes. Sometimes the answer was *not that one,* and the Spire recorded the refusal—it had learned refusals, Lior thought, the way a man learns a language late in life, clumsily and with enormous gratitude—and asked again elsewhere, or asked for something smaller. Sometimes the answer was yes, and a villager would sit by the tower's shadow with a courier taking notes, telling a story they chose to tell, and afterward they said the strangest thing: that it felt less like losing something than like hearing your own voice come back to you from a high place, slightly changed, more beautiful.

Given memories echoed back changed. Lior knew this from the inside. Last month, walking the Spire's grounds, they had caught a fragment surfacing—Maren's kitchen, the smell of onions and burnt honey, a woman laughing at something Lior had said—and the laugh had not been Lior's memory exactly. It had been warmer than memory. It had been the memory as the Spire now kept it, polished by gratitude, returned like a borrowed coat with the buttons sewn on tighter.

Vael called this "theological drift" and disapproved of it on principle, while spending every spare hour in the archive trying to determine whether it applied to court oaths. Sefa called it "the tower saying thank you." Both were probably right.

At midmorning, the doors banged open and Sefa came through at her usual velocity, courier satchel bouncing, followed by a nervous young man Lior recognized as one of Odile's binding students.

"He's here," Sefa announced, dropping onto the bench across from Lior and stealing an apple from the bowl there with the ease of long practice. "The envoy from Wend. The one who wrote the letter."

"I know. I'm expecting him."

"You're *calm.*"

"I'm a listener. Calm is the job."

"Calm is the job," Sefa repeated, with deep skepticism, and pointed the apple at Lior. "He's going to ask about the tooth."

"The tooth?"

"Tansy's tooth. Word travels. There are people in Wend—" Sefa lowered her voice, which for Sefa meant speaking at normal volume, "—who think the Spire takes children's teeth now, and there are other people in Wend who think that's beautiful, and they've sent an envoy to determine which it is, and either way he's going to want to see the entry, and Lior, the entry says *please do not be sad, tower.*"

"Yes."

"In the official covenant ledger."

"Yes."

"Good," Sefa said, with satisfaction, and bit the apple. "That's the sort of thing that ends wars. Nobody can march against a document that says please don't be sad."

The envoy, when he arrived, was a thin, serious man with ink-stained fingers and the air of someone who had expected to be disappointed and was bracing for the relief. Lior received him at the long table, opened the new ledger to the morning's entries, and let him read Tansy's tooth in silence.

He read it twice. His shoulders came down an inch.

"We were told," he said carefully, "that the tower feeds on memory."

"It did," Lior said. "When it was starving, and when taking was the only way it knew. It eats differently now. Or rather—it accepts. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole covenant."

"And if a village refuses?"

"Then it refuses, and the refusal is recorded, and the tower asks somewhere else." Lior turned to a marked page. "Here. Hollowmere refused to give the flood story—they're keeping it for a memorial next autumn. So the tower asked Ferrow instead, and Ferrow gave the story of their bell, and everyone involved reports being pleased. Here's the refusal. Here's the acceptance. Same courtesy, both directions."

The envoy ran his finger down the columns. Lior watched him do the thing Lior had done a hundred times in another life, in another ledger: looking for the trick, the hidden interest, the compounding clause. There wasn't one. That was the entire innovation. He seemed to need several minutes to absorb the absence.

"There are those at court," he said at last, "who will call this naive."

"There are those at court," Lior agreed, "who called the wards permanent, right up until they weren't. Naive is a word people use for arithmetic they haven't checked yet." They said it mildly, and the envoy blinked, and then—unexpectedly—laughed, a short startled sound, as if laughter had been confiscated at the border and only now returned.

"I'll need copies," he said. "Of the covenant. Of the rules of offering. Of—" he hesitated, "—of the tooth."

"Take the original," Lior said. "We'll copy it. Some things should live where they're needed."

After he left, Sefa looked at Lior for a long moment.

"That was good," she said. "That was actually—" She stopped, frowned, and started over, which she only did when being sincere. "A year ago I watched you get hunted across half the country for holding a page of a ledger. Today you handed a piece of it to a stranger because it might help. You know that's the whole story, right? That's the arc. Page hunted, page given."

"I don't remember being hunted."

"No," Sefa said. "But your hands do. Watch." She took Lior's hand and pressed it flat against the table, and indeed the fingers had gone tense without Lior noticing, curled slightly around nothing, around the ghost-shape of a stolen page. "See? Body keeps the minutes even when the mind loses the ledger." She released it. "Anyway. You'll want to hear the rest, because it concerns you. The envoy says the court wants to send a delegation to study the covenant. Officially. Unofficially, Inquisitor-General Vael's former handler has been asking questions about you specifically. About what you gave up. About whether a person with no past can legally hold office."

Lior felt the old dread stir—and was interested, genuinely interested, to note how small it was now. A fish in a pond where it used to be a leviathan.

"What did Vael say?" they asked.

"She said—" Sefa grinned. "She said, and I quote, because I memorized it for exactly this moment: 'Tell them the law requires a keeper to understand the accounts. Tell them Lior understands the accounts better than anyone alive, because Lior is the only person in history who has read the price column from the inside. If the court disagrees, the court may file its objection in writing, and I will file my response in person.'"

Lior laughed. It surfaced from nowhere, from some blank-adjacent room, and it sounded like someone they used to be, and it was warm.

* * *

Evening came gold through the west windows. The apprentices had gone; Odile had gone, after stopping to look at the new ledger's first page and press her glue-smudged thumb briefly, silently, to the corner of it—a binder's blessing, Lior guessed, though they didn't remember ever being taught such a thing, and suspected they never had been, and suspected it predated them anyway, the way good customs do.

Lior sat alone with the book a while before closing it.

There was a ritual now, small and private: each night, before shelving the ledger, they read back through the day's entries and listened for the ones that mattered—not the important ones, necessarily, but the ones with weight, the ones where a person had reached past their own fear to offer something. Tonight there were three. Marla's dyes. Aldous's request for a melody. Tansy's tooth.

And underneath them, in the margin, where Lior sometimes wrote small notes to no one—to the person they had been, maybe, or to the person they were becoming—they wrote tonight:

*Dear whoever I was: the books balance. Not the way you feared and not the way you hoped. They balance the way gardens do—messily, seasonally, with a lot of dying back and more coming up than anyone planted. You'd have liked it. I think you'd have liked it. I think some of the liking is yours.*

They blotted it, closed the cover, and were reaching for the shelf when they saw it.

A slip of paper, folded once, tucked beneath the ledger's resting place. Not there that morning; Lior was sure of it. The handwriting was cramped and hurried, and the message was short:

*Listener — the court delegation arrives within the fortnight, but that is not why I write. A courier came to my office at dusk, would not give a name, left this and went. Read it before the others. It concerns the first gift. Yours. Whether it was entirely yours to give. — V.*

Beneath the note, folded smaller still, was a single page in a hand Lior did not know—old-fashioned, precise, dated ninety-nine years ago.

Lior stood very still in the gold light, listening to the evening sounds of a guild reborn, and unfolded it.
