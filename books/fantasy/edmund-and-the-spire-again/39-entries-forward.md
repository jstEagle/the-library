## 39. Entries Forward

The inquisition had a new motto, painted over the door of its headquarters in letters so large that Vael suspected the painter had been paid by the word: *We Ask First.*

She had chosen it herself. It had taken three drafts. The first version—"We Ask"—had been vetoed by the council as too vague. The second—"We Ask Before We Burn"—had been vetoed by Vael herself, on the grounds that it sounded like an apology for all the times they hadn't asked before burning. The third draft was a compromise, as most things were now, and she had come to believe compromises were not failures but a kind of architecture: you built them carefully, and people lived inside them.

Inside, the reformed inquisition was quieter than it used to be. There were fewer cells—two, both with windows—and more desks. The wardens still wore grey, because grey was practical and Vael saw no reason to waste good reform on bad tailoring, but they no longer carried writs of seizure. They carried questionnaires.

The questionnaires had been Lior's idea, back when Lior could still remember having ideas. Vael kept the first one framed on her wall: a single sheet in a careful ledger-keeper's hand, headed *INQUIRY INTO ALLEGED IMPOSSIBILITY*, followed by questions like *Who was harmed? Who was helped? Was anyone asked?* and, at the bottom, in smaller script, *If the answer to the last question is no, please sit down and have some tea before proceeding.*

It was astonishing how many crises dissolved under tea.

Vael's morning began, as it did every morning, with the reports from the border. She read them standing up, a habit from her old life that she'd decided to keep, on the theory that a person should remain slightly uncomfortable about their work or they would stop doing it well.

The reports were good. That was the strange part. A year ago she had gone to sleep every night cataloguing catastrophes; now the worst item in the stack was a dispute in Millbrook over whether a fence post stood on one farmer's land or another's, which had escalated when the Spire's shadow passed over it and briefly erased the color from the argument. Both farmers had forgotten what they were angry about. One of them now baked bread for the other out of sheer embarrassment.

*The Spire asks,* the report noted, in the border warden's tidy hand. *It stood over the disputed field for two days. It did not take anything. When we sent a courier to ask what it wanted, it showed him a memory of the field being plowed, a hundred years ago, by hands neither farmer recognized. Both farmers have since begun calling the field "the old man's." Nobody knows who the old man was. The Spire has not said. I suspect it is enjoying the mystery.*

Vael read that twice and allowed herself a small smile. Enjoying the mystery. A year ago the thing had been a starving god eating villages whole, and now it was apparently developing a sense of humor, the way a houseplant turns toward light.

There was a knock at her door. Her aide, a young woman named Pell who had joined the inquisition specifically because it frightened her mother, put her head in.

"Inquisitor. The delegation from the Guild of Impossible Things is here."

"Send them through. And Pell—the guild is not 'the former regime' anymore. Try 'our colleagues.'"

"My mother says they're all criminals," Pell said, with the serene confidence of the young.

"Your mother also says you joined the inquisition to spite her, so perhaps weigh her sources."

Corvane came in leaning on a cane he absolutely did not need, because Corvane had discovered that a cane made people wait for you, and at his age—he claimed ninety-four, though the ledgers suggested otherwise, since ledgers always did—he considered waiting a form of tribute. Behind him came two junior guild-keepers carrying a crate between them, walking with the exaggerated care of people transporting something either priceless or explosive.

"Inquisitor Vael." He lowered himself into the chair across from her desk without being offered it, which was also tribute, of a kind. "I've brought the year-end accounts."

"I don't audit the guild anymore. We agreed."

"You don't. But the covenant does." He nodded at the crate. "Every gift given to the Spire, recorded. Every memory returned, recorded. Every time it asked and someone said no—which happens, Inquisitor, more often than you'd think, and it always withdraws politely, like a guest who's been told the kitchen's closed." He tapped the crate lid. "Four hundred and eleven entries this year. Not one taken by force."

Vael looked at the crate for a long moment. "You're telling me a tower has better manners than my predecessor."

"Your predecessor had never been hungry," Corvane said. "That's the whole of it, if you want my professional opinion. Hunger makes liars of everyone. Feed something honestly, even strangely, and watch how quickly it learns grace." He rose, cane first. "The council will want these copied into the civic record. I'd do it myself, but my handwriting has become a scandal."

At the door he paused, and for a moment the performance dropped, and he was only an old man looking at a younger one who had once hunted him.

"How is the keeper?" he asked quietly.

"Lior is well. Learning. Sefa reads to them every evening."

"And does Lior ever write things they don't remember learning?"

Vael thought of the margins of the new ledger, the small annotations appearing in a hand that was Lior's and somehow older than Lior. "Sometimes," she said.

Corvane smiled like a man watching interest compound. "Good," he said. "Keep those pages safe. Some debts pay themselves forward."

* * *

Sefa had run couriers before the dark year, back when the routes were simple loops and the cargo was mostly gossip with wax seals on it. Now the courier lines ran north to the border villages, and the cargo was stranger and better: seeds that remembered being wild, letters dictated by people who had forgotten their own handwriting, and—her personal favorite—a monthly shipment of honey from the city to the village of Ashen Ford, where the bees had died in the dark and the villagers missed the taste of sweetness more than they'd ever admitted aloud.

She stood on the platform at the courier yard at dawn, checking manifests, while around her the yard woke up in its usual chaos.

"Tamsin! Your satchel's leaking!"

"It's not leaking, it's *perfuming*—"

"It's leaking. Swap the jar for the tin one or the whole northern route smells like lavender by noon and the Spire gets confused about what century it is."

Tamsin swapped the jar.

The strangest part of the job, Sefa had decided, was the letters to the Spire itself. After the relighting, when the stars came back one by one, each kindled by a freely given memory, people had started writing to it. Not prayers, exactly. Letters. Thank-you notes, complaints, recipes. The couriers delivered them to the edge of the shadow and left them in a stone cairn, and by morning they were always gone, and sometimes—this was the part Sefa loved—an answer came back. Not words. The Spire answered in memories: a courier would touch the cairn and suddenly remember something that wasn't theirs, vividly, kindly, like a story told by a grandmother. A taste of bread from a bakery that burned down forty years ago. The feeling of a dog's head under a hand. Once, memorably, the sensation of being a star, which had made the courier lie down in a field for six hours and afterward quit drinking, so on balance a success.

Today's pouch held eleven letters. One was from a child in the city asking whether the Spire had ever been small. One was from Maren—or rather, it was addressed to Maren, care of the Spire, which was its own kind of heartbreak and its own kind of hope: Maren's mind had never fully returned after the ford, but she lived now in the border village of Hollowmere, tended by the people there, and sometimes she wrote letters full of sense and sometimes full of stars, and the Spire, they believed, read them either way.

And one letter had no name on it at all. Just a single line, in handwriting Sefa knew better than her own these days:

*Ask it what Edmund liked to eat.*

Sefa turned the paper over. Nothing on the back. No signature. The hand was Lior's—but Liar hadn't written letters in months, couldn't reliably write their own name, and besides, Lior was due at the yard at midmorning for the supply run, and Sefa had kissed them goodbye not two hours ago in a kitchen that smelled of ink and toast.

She looked north, toward the line of hills where the Spire's shadow stood against the brightening sky like a door left open in a lit room.

"Well," she said to Tamsin, tucking the letter into her breast pocket, closest to the skin. "Looks like I'm taking the north road myself today."

* * *

The village of Hollowmere had been called something else before the dark year. Nobody remembered what. This was not the tragedy it might have been; the villagers had held a festival about it, the Festival of the New Name, at which everyone agreed that whatever they'd been called before, Hollowmere suited them better anyway, since the town sat in a hollow and had a mere, and accuracy mattered.

Lior walked its lanes in the late afternoon, doing the rounds.

This was the work now. Once a month, Lior traveled the border villages—not as keeper of the old debt, that ledger was closed, but as keeper of the new one: recording gifts given, gifts received, gifts declined. Consent, written down. It was slow work. It was the best work Lior had ever done, though Lior couldn't actually remember any previous work, and had decided to trust Sefa's report on the matter.

They stopped at the house where Maren lived.

Maren sat in the garden in a chair dragged out into the sun, wrapped in blankets though it was nearly summer, her white eyes turned toward the sky. The woman who tended her—a broad-faced farmer named Odile who had lost two sons to the old hunger and gained, she said, a mother instead—waved Lior in.

"She's been chatty today," Odile said. "Well. Chatty for her."

"Maren." Lior knelt by the chair. Half a teacher. Less than half, most days. But the hands still knew things. When Lior took them, Maren's fingers found the calluses on Lior's palm—the pen calluses, the rope calluses—and mapped them like a reader going through a familiar book.

"Apprentice," Maren said. Sometimes she said it. It wasn't recognition, exactly. It was more like a tide coming in partway.

"Yes."

"You've been writing." Her clouded eyes crinkled. "I can smell the ink. You always smelled of ink. Your sister hated it—said you'd ruin every shirt you owned." She patted Lior's hand, twice, the way she had when Lior was small and skinned-kneed, though Lior didn't remember being small and skinned-kneed and had to take it on faith. "Did you bring the accounts?"

"Always."

"Read me the good ones."

So Lior read. The farmer who gave the Spire the memory of his wedding day and received back, unasked, the memory of his father teaching him to whistle, which he'd lost to fever as a boy. The widow who declined to give anything, and the Spire's withdrawal, courteous as a bow. The child's letter asking whether the Spire had ever been small, and the answer the courier carried home: a memory of being a seed of stone in a mountain's side, patient beyond human counting, waiting to be asked to grow.

Maren listened with her head tipped back, smiling at the sky.

"There," she said at the end. "That's the lesson, you see. That last one. Patience isn't waiting. Patience is trusting you'll be asked." Her grip tightened, sudden and strong. "I taught you everything wrong, apprentice. Memory-magic, taking, spending—it was all spending. But you—" her voice wandered, caught, steadied, "—you learned the other arithmetic somewhere. I don't know where. Maybe I did teach you one thing right."

"You taught me to keep accounts," Lior said gently. "Sefa told me."

"Sefa." Maren's face went soft and far away. "Tell Sefa the bread recipe is in the third drawer. Tell her the trick is the rind. She'll know."

"I'll tell her."

Maren drifted then, murmuring, her hands loose in her lap. Lior stayed until the sun moved, then rose, and Odile walked them to the gate.

"She has days like this more often now," Odile said. "Since the stars came back. The Spire stands over the mere some nights, just standing, and in the morning she remembers something small. A kitchen. A song. Whatever it gives her, it gives her *changed*—warmer, somehow. Like it's returning things it borrowed and can't help improving."

Lior looked north. The shadow stood against the evening sky, and the first relit stars were pricking through above it, one, then three, then a scatter of them, each one a memory somebody chose to give.

* * *

Sefa reached the cairn at the edge of the shadow at dusk, alone, the nameless letter in her pocket. The Spire loomed above her, black and vast and quiet, and she had long ago stopped being afraid of it, the way you stop being afraid of the sea: respectfully, never entirely.

She set the letter on the stones. Then, because it felt rude not to, she said aloud: "It's from Lior. Or near enough. Ask it what Edmund liked to eat."

The shadow stirred. Not wind—shadow didn't move with wind, it moved with attention. It gathered around the cairn, and Sefa felt the familiar brush of it, gentle as a hand hovering over a shoulder, asking.

She gave it permission. She always did. It was the whole point.

What came back was not a memory of hers. It was Lior's—she knew it instantly, the way you know a coat by its weight—a memory of a kitchen, of Maren younger and sharp-eyed, of a boy sitting on the table swinging his legs, a boy with Lior's laugh and a name Sefa had seen only in a margin: Edmund. And the boy was eating—honey bread, the rind left on, the way Maren made it, the trick of it—and he was laughing at something, and the memory was warm, warmer than any memory had a right to be, improved in the keeping, polished by a hundred years inside a hungry tower that had learned, slowly, clumsily, wonderfully, to hold things instead of eat them.

The shadow withdrew. On top of the cairn, where no hand had touched it, lay a fresh page of ledger paper. On it, in handwriting Sefa knew better than her own:

*He liked the rind left on. Tell her I'm keeping this one. —E.*

Sefa stood a long time in the gathering dark, under the returning stars, holding the page. Then she laughed—once, wetly, happily—folded it into her breast pocket next to the letter, and turned south toward the lights of Hollowmere, already composing the message she would send ahead to the city, to Vael, to Corvane, to anyone who would listen:

*The Spire is giving things back. It's giving Lior back. Come and see.*
