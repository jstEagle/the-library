## 40. Edmund and the Spire Again

The new ledger had a smell all its own.

The old one had smelled of ash and tallow and a century of secrets pressed flat between covers. This one smelled of fresh vellum and the cedar oil Sefa insisted on rubbing into the spine, because she said a book that was going to hold the future ought to smell like something worth keeping. Lior had pointed out that cedar oil attracted moths. Sefa had pointed out that Lior attracted trouble, and one of them had to be managed.

It was morning, or what passed for morning now that the stars came back. The Guildhall windows were open, and the light came in the way light was supposed to come in — slantwise, warm, gilding the dust motes instead of eating them. A year ago Lior would not have been able to tell you what a year ago felt like. A year ago Lior could not have told you what *breakfast* felt like, or their own name, or why the woman who brought them tea every morning looked at them with such fierce, patient tenderness.

Now Lior knew all of it, secondhand, the way you know a city you've only ever seen through another traveler's window. Sefa told the story every day. She had promised never to lie about who they were, and she never had, even when the truth was ugly — the bargain she'd made with Vael, the friend she'd nearly gotten killed, the apprentice who had run away from a teacher who loved her because every lesson cost a piece of the student. Lior had listened to their own life told back to them the way you listen to a song from the next room: familiar in shape, foreign in detail, aching somewhere the ache couldn't be named.

And the Spire, learning, had begun to give things back.

Not whole. Never whole. That was the covenant's first law, written in Lior's own hand on the first page: *What is given freely may echo back changed. Nothing returns as it left. This is not a flaw. This is the whole point.*

Lior dipped the pen and wrote the morning's entry.

*Entry the two hundred and sixtieth. The Spire asks, and is answered.*

It was a short entry, as most of them were now. The asking had become ordinary, which was the most extraordinary thing of all. Down in the border villages, the Spire's shadow still stretched long across the fields at dusk — it would always be a hungry thing; hunger was its nature, the way running was a river's — but now it *asked*. A farmer with a grief too heavy to carry might walk out to the shadow-line and stand there until the darkness touched his shoulder, and the Spire would take the grief, and in exchange it would send back, weeks later, some small echo: the smell of his mother's bread, the sound of a laugh he'd forgotten he'd ever heard, changed a little, softened at the edges, like a story retold by someone who loved it.

The people had a word for it now. They called it *trading with the tower*, and they said it the way they said *going to market* — casually, comfortably, with the ease of a thing that had always been done. Lior found this both wonderful and slightly alarming. Institutions, in Lior's experience, could make anything comfortable, including miracles. That was rather the Guild of Impossible Things' whole problem for a century.

Someone knocked. Well — someone *rapped*, in the particular three-beat rhythm that meant Vael, who had never in her life knocked like a person who expected to be told to wait.

"Enter," Lior called, because it was Lior's office now, technically. Former Inquisitor Vael had signed the paperwork herself, with the same terrible politeness she'd once used to seal the Guildhouse, and had remarked that it gave her great satisfaction to be on the other side of a seizure order for once.

She came in carrying a folder, which was how Vael carried everything, including soup, on one memorable occasion.

"The reformed inquisition's quarterly report," she said, setting it down. "The short version: the courts hate us, the villages love us, and the crown has stopped asking questions it's afraid I'll answer honestly. Also there is bread in the kitchen and Sefa has hidden the good jam again, which I mention only because you are the only person she feels guilty about."

"She shouldn't. I don't remember liking jam."

"You liked jam *enormously*," Vael said. "It's in your file. I read your file. It was the most humanizing document I have ever handled, and I once handled a warrant for a dragon's arrears."

Lior smiled. It was a strange thing, being known by people who had known you when you didn't know yourself. For the first months it had felt like living inside someone else's portrait — everyone else could see the likeness, and Lior could only see the brushstrokes. But portraits, Lior had learned, could be lived into. You grew toward the shape people loved in you, and one day you looked down and the shape fit, and whether it was the same shape or a new one hardly mattered anymore.

"Anything in the report about the north?" Lior asked. "The villages past the Grey Field?"

Vael's face did the thing it did instead of frowning — a small, precise stillness, like a scale settling. "Rebuilding. Slowly. The Spire asks them first, always. Some say yes. Some say no, and it withdraws, and — " she paused, choosing the word with a clerk's care, " — and it *waits*. Patiently. It has gotten very good at waiting. It had a hundred years of bad practice to unlearn."

"And the transformed folk? The ones who halted on the field?"

"Settling. They remember their names now. Not everything — the Spire can't give back what was never given, only what was. But names are enough to start with. Names are how everything starts." She tapped the folder. "There's a woman up there, a former warden of the old enemy, who has petitioned to *apprentice* at the guild. She wants to learn accounting."

"Of what?"

"Of debts," Vael said, "and how to forgive them." A beat. "I told her the position was filled, but we were always hiring."

She left the folder and went, and the office was quiet again, and Lior wrote the next entry.

*Entry the two hundred and sixty-first. The guild receives its first application from beyond the Grey Field. The war that never ended has ended. File under: things no one dared to hope for, sub-file: things we will now have to build shelving for.*

* * *

Sefa found Lior at midday, which was unusual, because Sefa ran the courier lines now and midday was when the couriers came in, a flurry of dust and saddle-leather and shouted reports from every road in the known world. For her to be in the Guildhall at midday meant either catastrophe or gossip, and with Sefa it was usually both.

"You have to come see the map room," she said.

"What's happened?"

"Nothing's *happened*. That's the point. Come on."

The map room had been Corvane's, once. They had debated for a long time what to do with it — the great table where the old master had plotted his century of borrowed time, the walls hung with charts of wards and borders and debts. In the end they had kept the room and changed everything in it, which Lior had come to believe was the guild's whole method, and possibly the world's. The maps on the walls now showed roads instead of wards, wells instead of watchtowers. The table was used for lunch.

Today it was covered in paper, and Maren was there.

Maren. Half a teacher still — that was the truth no covenant could undo. The spell at the ford had spent decades of her, and what remained was a woman who sometimes knew Lior and sometimes knew only *of* Lior, the way you know of a story you heard as a child. But she came to the Guildhall most days anyway, because her hands remembered ink even when the rest of her didn't, and because — she'd said once, in one of her lucid hours, with the old sharpness breaking through the fog like sun through cloud — "You don't stop teaching a student just because you've forgotten their name, dear. You just have to be more honest about the lessons."

She was standing at the table with a magnifying lens, and she was laughing.

"Maren," Sefa said, "show them."

Maren looked up. For a moment her eyes were clouded, searching Lior's face for a name, and Lior had learned not to flinch in that moment, had learned to simply stand there and be found or not found. Today she found them.

"There you are," she said. "Look. Look at the margins."

Lior leaned over the table. The papers were copies — careful, vellum-fresh copies — of pages from the *old* ledger. The oldest one. The one Corvane had pressed into Lior's hands as the Guildhouse burned, saying *carry the debt*, the one that had started everything. They had copied it a hundred times over, studying it, teaching from it, using it as the founding document of the new covenant. Lior knew every entry by heart, in the way you know a story you've told so often it wears smooth.

Except.

There, in the margin of the first page — the page recording the original theft, the heart of the Spire, held in trust, one (1) — there was a line of handwriting Lior had never seen in any copy.

Small. Slanted. Familiar in a way that made Lior's chest hurt before their mind caught up.

Beside the debtor's name — *the Spire, listed as a person, as all persons* — someone had written, in that small slanted hand:

*And beside the debtor, always, a keeper. E.*

Lior stared at it.

"That wasn't there," Lior said. "I've read that page a thousand times. That margin was blank."

"It was," Maren agreed cheerfully. "Yesterday it was. This morning it wasn't. The courier brought the copy down from the Spire archive not two hours ago. I thought I was seeing things — half-blind, you know — but Sefa confirmed it."

Lior looked at Sefa. Sefa was wearing the expression she wore when she was trying very hard not to say *I told you so*, which was most of her expressions.

"The Spire gave something back," Lior said slowly. "But I never wrote that. I never — "

"You did, though," Sefa said gently. "Or — you *will*. Or you *would have*. That's the thing about what you gave it, Lior. You didn't give it your memories the way a purse gives up coins. You gave it your *recorded past*. Everything ever written about you, everything you ever wrote. And the Spire has been learning to remember differently, the way you taught it. And things that echo back come back *changed*." She tapped the margin. "That's your handwriting. I'd know it anywhere. I spent a year reading it to you over breakfast."

Lior touched the page. The ink was dry. The ink had been dry for a hundred years, and also for no time at all, and both of these things were true, and Lior had long ago stopped expecting the Spire to respect the difference.

*E.*

"Edmund," Maren said.

The name landed in the quiet of the map room like a stone dropped in still water.

It wasn't entirely unfamiliar. That was the strange thing — the terrible, wonderful thing. Lior didn't *remember* it. There was no memory attached, no face, no moment. But there was a *shape* where a memory should be, the way an amputated limb aches with weather. Somewhere in the year since the Grey Field, in the fragments that had been surfacing — the laugh, the kitchen, the smell of ink — there had been this name, waiting at the bottom of the well like a coin someone had thrown in for luck.

"Tell me," Lior said. "Tell me what you know about it."

"Nothing," Sefa said. "That's the honest truth, and I promised never to lie to you. You gave away everything, Lior. Everything anyone ever recorded. There's nothing in any archive, any ledger, any mind in this city that remembers an Edmund. Except — " she nodded at the page, " — that. Except whatever the Spire kept, changed, and is now giving back. One margin at a time."

"It's your name," Maren said. Not a guess. Maren, even hollowed, had always been able to see the shape of a thing underneath its skin. "Or it was. Or it's the name of whoever you were before you were you — before you were the runaway apprentice, before you were the keeper. The Spire ate a hundred years of villages to stay alive, and everything it ate, it kept. And you taught it to give back. So it's giving back." She smiled, and for a moment the smile was entirely the old Maren, sharp and warm as a hearth iron. "You wrote yourself into the margin of the oldest book in the world, dear. It's the most impossible thing I've ever seen. And I once taught a child to remember tomorrow."

Lior laughed. It came out wet.

*Edmund.*

The name sat in Lior's chest like a key sitting in a lock it didn't quite fit — not yet. Not yet, the way Corvane had said it, a lifetime ago, over a stolen page and a stolen heart. Not yet. But *someday* had a different sound than *never*, and Lior had built a covenant on that difference.

* * *

That evening Lior walked out to the Spire's grounds.

It was permitted now, of course. It had always been permitted, in a sense — the Spire had never wanted to be a prison or a god, only a debtor and a neighbor, and neighbors could visit. But Lior still liked to walk the long way, through the rebuilt village at the shadow's edge, past the market where a woman sold light that remembered daylight and a boy sold maps of places that no longer existed and, lately, maps of places that *did*, which everyone agreed was the more impossible product.

The Spire stood black against the evening, as it always would. But its shadow had changed. It no longer erased color; it *deepened* it, the way dusk deepens a field before the stars come out. And the stars were coming out now, one by one, each one kindled — the people said — by a memory someone had chosen to give, freely, the way you light a lamp for a traveler you'll never meet.

Lior stopped at the shadow-line, at the place where the darkness touched the grass, and did what Lior always did: waited.

The Spire asked.

It didn't ask in words anymore. It asked the way the evening asks — a coolness on the skin, a stillness in the air, a question shaped like an open hand. *May I?* And Lior answered the way everyone answered now, freely or not at all.

*Yes.*

And something small came back. Not a memory, exactly. An echo. The smell of cedar oil — Sefa's doing, Lior's doing, someone's doing, changed a little, softened at the edges. And underneath it, faint as a name called from very far away, a feeling that had no story attached but had a *weight*: the feeling of being known completely and chosen anyway. The feeling Lior had spent a year learning to recognize from the outside, in Sefa's fierce patience, in Vael's guarded grace, in Maren's half-remembered tenderness.

The feeling, Lior suspected, of being Edmund.

"Thank you," Lior said aloud, to the tower, to the dark, to whoever had written in the margin a hundred years before Lior was born or a day after they died — time was a ledger, and ledgers could be kept in any order.

The Spire did not answer in words. It never had. But the shadow deepened, and the first star came out directly overhead, and it was, Lior thought, a particularly warm one.

* * *

Back in the office, with the lamp lit and the good jam (recovered from Sefa's hiding place through methods Vael had declined to document), Lior opened the new ledger to the fresh page and wrote.

*Entry the two hundred and sixty-second. A name has surfaced in the margins of the oldest account. It is not mine, and it is mine. It belonged to someone I gave away, and it is waiting to be earned back, and I have learned — from a tower, of all teachers — that what waits can be trusted, if the waiting is honest.*

Lior paused, pen hovering.

The guild's charter, the new one, hung on the wall behind the desk. It was short. Sefa had insisted on short; she said the old charter had been long enough to hide a war in, and she wasn't wrong. It read: *The Guild of Impossible Things trades in what cannot be owned. Nothing is taken that is not given. Nothing is given that is not honored. All accounts open. All debts forgivable. All names worth keeping.*

*All names worth keeping.*

Lior looked at the margin of the old ledger's copy, propped open on the desk corner where Maren had left it. *E.* Small, slanted, patient. A self waiting in the wings of Lior's own story, holding a script Lior had never read, ready to step forward whenever the keeper of accounts could make room on the page.

Lior did not reach for it. That was the old way — the hungry way, the way of the theft and the war and the century of borrowed time. The new way was slower and harder and better: you let things come back when they came back, changed, and you loved them in their changedness, and you did not demand the original.

But you could leave a light on.

Lior smiled, and kept writing.

*Entry the two hundred and sixty-third. Note for future keepers: the margin of every account is not empty space. It is where the story keeps what it isn't ready to say. Check your margins. Check them often. Something is always waiting there, and it has your handwriting.*

Lior set down the pen, and blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the starlight coming through the window — real starlight, kindled by chosen things, falling on the open ledger like a blessing or an invitation.

And there, in the margin of the fresh page Lior had just written, in ink that had not been there when the lamp was lit — small, slanted, familiar, patient — a single new word had appeared, beside the entry, waiting to be read:

*Soon.*
