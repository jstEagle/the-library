## 1. Salt Charms and Small Lies

The frost came to Greyhollow the way debt comes to the poor: quietly, early, and all at once. By the time the market bells rang the hour after dawn, it had silvered every awning in Tallow Row and turned the cobbles slick as oiled slate, and Petra Underhill was already at her stall, warming her fingers over a clay brazier no bigger than a cupped hand, arranging her wares so they would look like nothing at all.

That was the whole art of it, she had learned. Not the knotting — the knotting was simple, a crone could teach it in an afternoon, and Maren had taught her in less — but the looking-like-nothing. A luck-knot displayed plainly was evidence. A luck-knot hung among spools of dyed thread, beside buttons and bone needles and little clay whistles for children, was inventory. The fever-charms went into the basket marked *sewing notions*, wrapped in linen as if they were thimbles. The salt charms — those were her own invention, coarse grey salt packed into wax-sealed lozenges the size of knucklebones, good for keeping out cold drafts and worse things — she sold openly, because salt was honest. Salt was kitchen-work. No one had ever been hanged for selling salt.

She had been five years learning how to be small. She was very good at it now.

"Morning, mistress." The voice belonged to Goodman Hesk, the cooper, who came every market-day and bought a thread-spool he did not need and paid too much for it and never once met her eyes while doing either. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with sawdust permanently worked into the creases of his knuckles, and this morning his breath steamed and his shoulders were up around his ears like a man walking past a dog he didn't trust.

"Goodman Hesk." She smiled the smile she kept for him, warm and stupid. "Thread's three copper this week. The red's come in lovely."

"The red, then." He counted coins onto the board without looking at them either. "And — my wife says the winter's coming hard. Says the draft off the moor gets into the baby's room something dreadful. I thought — thread being what it is — you might have some notion of what keeps a draft out."

"A heavy curtain," said Petra.

"Course. A curtain. Only —" He rubbed the back of his neck. "Only my grandmother used to say there was a way. With salt. And I don't hold with anything, mind, I'm a churchgoing man, I've got no use for — only if a person happened to have such a thing lying about—"

"I sell salt," Petra said gently. "In lozenges. For the larder. Keeps the damp off the flour." She took four of them from under the counter and wrapped them in brown paper as she talked, her hands moving through the motions with the ease of long habit. "You'd want one above the door frame and one in each window corner. Kitchen wisdom. Nothing more than that."

"Nothing more," Hesk agreed gratefully, paying silver for what should have cost him two coppers, because the lie was part of the price and both of them knew it and neither of them would ever say so. "Kitchen wisdom. My wife will be glad of it."

He left. Petra watched him go, a broad careful back disappearing into the crowd, and felt the familiar small ache she always felt after these transactions — not guilt exactly, and not fear, but something that lived in the same room as both of them and paid no rent.

Because here was the thing, the thing she carried under her ribs like a swallowed stone: half the time, the charms did nothing at all. She made them honestly enough — the knots tied in the old pattern, the words murmured under her breath, the salt blessed with whatever thin residue of blessing still clung to her hands from five years at Maren's hearth. But the gods were silent. Everyone knew it. A generation ago they had stopped answering, stopped speaking, stopped being anywhere at all, and the Crown had declared magic outlawed not because it worked but because it didn't, because a thing that doesn't work is an insult, and insults to absent gods are the easiest kind to punish. What Petra sold was mostly hope with a knot in it. Placebo and prayer-wire. She told herself this every night, and most mornings she believed it, and believing it let her sleep.

*Mostly*, though. Mostly was a door with a crack under it, and Petra had spent five years pretending she couldn't see light through the gap.

* * *

The morning wore on the way market mornings do, in small transactions and smaller weather. A fishwife bought thread. Two apprentices from the tannery bought whistles and, when Petra's back was turned, one of the fever-charms, snickering, because boys that age buy charms to mock them. An old woman named Sorrel — the only customer who had ever said the word *charm* aloud in Petra's hearing, in a whisper, twenty years past caring — bought three luck-knots and pressed Petra's hand across the counter and said, "You've your mother's fingers," which was wrong on every count, since Petra had never known her mother and her fingers were Maren's fingers entirely, and Petra had to turn away and fuss with her brazier until the tightness in her throat passed.

Greyhollow spread around her as she worked, the market city built where three roads drowned in each other, all steep lanes and leaning houses and the smell of the river underneath everything like a held note. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys. Gulls fought over the fish-stalls' leavings. Somewhere a temple bell tolled the third hour — the temples still rang their bells, though no god answered them, the way a widow still sets out her husband's plate — and the crowd moved beneath the sound without lifting its head, the crowd of a city that had learned, generation by generation, to expect nothing from heaven and everything from the day's takings.

It was near noon when the woman came with the child.

Petra noticed the child first, the way you notice a candle guttering. A girl, perhaps six years old, riding her mother's hip with her head lolling against the woman's shoulder, and even across the width of the market row Petra could see the flush standing out on the small cheeks like a brand, could see the shallow fluttering effort of the breathing. Fever-child. Every hedge-wife within fifty miles knew that walk, that terrible slow hip-sway of a mother carrying a child too ill to sit upright, moving carefully, as though speed might shake something loose inside the little body that could never be put back.

They stopped at the apothecary's two stalls down. Petra watched the apothecary — a decent man, Master Coll, with ink-stained fingers — lean out, look at the child, look at the mother, and shake his head with a gentleness that was worse than rudeness. There was no physic for a fever that had passed three days and begun to speak in the blood. The mother stood a moment longer. Then she turned, and Petra saw her face do the thing faces do at that point, the folding-flat, the closing of accounts, and the woman began walking down the row of stalls with no destination at all, just moving, because stopping meant admitting.

Petra's hands were already moving before her mind caught up. That was the trouble with five years of careful living; it trained you wonderfully in caution and not at all in mercy. By the time she had thought *don't*, the fever-charm was out of the sewing-notions basket and unwrapped and sitting on the counter between her palms like a small pale egg.

The woman stopped at her stall without seeing it, eyes fixed on nothing, and the child's breath whistled wetly, and Petra heard herself say, in a voice pitched low and kind:

"Mistress. Your daughter's burning."

The woman flinched as if struck. "There's nothing—" Her eyes came up and found Petra's face and something in them broke open. "The apothecary says—"

"I know what he says." Petra glanced left, right. The row was busy; no one looked at a woman selling thread. "I can't cure her. Do you understand me? Whatever happens next, I cannot cure her, and I'll deny saying any different." The words tasted of ash and of Maren's kitchen, of the last lesson before the last fight before the running-away. *If you must work in the open, work small, work deniable, and never promise what the world won't deliver.* "But I have — a charm. Old household work. It's harmless. It costs nothing. If you'll take it."

"What does it do?" The mother's voice was flat with exhaustion, past hope, asking only to have asked.

"It's meant to cool a fever," Petra said. "Meant to. Whether it does—" She almost said *nothing*. She almost gave the woman the gift of expecting nothing, which was the kindest thing in her stock. But the child whimpered then, a small cracked sound, and Petra heard herself say instead, quietly, terribly: "Whether it does anything, I honestly don't know."

Five years of lies, and the truth came out of her like a splinter.

The mother took the charm. It was a knot, white thread over a core of river-clay and feverfew ash, tied in a pattern of nine crossings, with a word under it that wasn't a word in any language Petra could name — a word Maren had given her wordlessly, mouthed against her ear one winter evening while her hands guided Petra's through the crossings, a sound more felt than heard, like wind deciding which way to go around a hill. Petra had retied it that morning from memory, murmuring the shape of the sound if not the sound itself, and had wrapped it in linen and put it among the thimbles and thought nothing of it, because nothing was what charms did.

"Put it against her skin," Petra said. "Under the shift. And — talk to her. However the night goes. Talk to her."

The woman nodded, tucked the charm against the child's throat with fingers that shook, and stood a moment looking at Petra with an expression Petra couldn't read and didn't try to. Then she walked away down Tallow Row, slower now, as though carrying something fragile rather than something already lost.

Petra stood at her stall in the cold noon light and felt, distinctly, the sensation of having done something irreversible. She couldn't have said how she knew. There was no thunder, no sign. Just the ordinary noise of the market, and under it, faint as a hairline crack in a bell, a wrongness — the feeling of a door swinging somewhere far away that she had nailed shut long ago.

She sold thread for the rest of the afternoon. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; Maren had seen to that. But twice she caught herself staring at the empty space in the sewing-notions basket where the fever-charm had lain, and twice she made herself look away.

* * *

She closed the stall at dusk, as she always did, packing her wares into the same worn hamper, banking the brazier's coals, pulling the canvas over the frame and weighting it with the same three bricks, everything square and orderly and exactly as it had been for five years, a life assembled with the precision of a lock mechanism. She carried the hamper home through streets going blue with evening, up Cutpurse Stair, along the wall-walk where the city's oldest stones sweated frost, to her rooms above a chandler's shop on Candle Lane: one room, one window, one bed, one shelf of jars labeled in a plain merchant's hand — *saltpetre, alum, dried yarrow* — labels that were true and also, in the way of all her life now, incomplete.

She lit her lamp. She ate bread and honey standing up. She was reaching to bank the fire for the night when the knock came.

Not a loud knock. Three soft taps, the knock of someone who did not want to be heard knocking. Petra stood absolutely still, lamp-oil smell in her nostrils, and listened to her own heart counting.

Three taps again.

She opened the door on the chain — a habit older than her fear, or perhaps the same age — and the fever-child's mother stood in the corridor with the lamp behind her, and Petra saw her face and forgot how to breathe.

The woman was crying. Not grieving; crying the other way, the wrong way, tears of a kind Petra had not seen in five years, had not seen since Maren's kitchen, since the last time a working actually worked. Behind the woman, bundled at her shoulder, the child slept. Slept deeply, round-cheeked, breathing slow and even as a tide, the flush gone from her face as if scrubbed away, and against the small throat the white thread of the knot glowed — glowed, there was no other word, a soft heatless light like moonlight trapped in wool, already fading even as Petra watched, spending itself out, its work done.

"It broke at midnight," the mother whispered. "The fever broke at midnight, and the knot — it shone. Mistress, it *shone*. What was it? What did you give her? What are you?"

Every question in that string deserved a lie. Petra had five years of lies racked and ready, sorted and labeled, and she opened her mouth to use one, and found that none of them would come. Perhaps it was the child sleeping. Perhaps it was the light dying on the thread. Perhaps it was simply that five years is a long time to hold a door shut with your shoulder, and everyone's shoulder tires eventually.

"Come inside," Petra said. "Before anyone hears you."

The woman came in, and the child slept on, and Petra closed the door on the corridor and on the careful life, feeling it close behind her like water over a stone. She made the mother sit. She touched the child's brow with the backs of her fingers — cool, miraculously, ordinarily cool, cool as any well child in any house in Greyhollow — and the wonder of it frightened her more than any fever could have. Because she had tied that knot a hundred times. She had tied it for strangers and neighbors and once, desperately, for a drowning sailor dragged half-dead from the Grey, and every time, *every time*, nothing. Thread and clay and a word-shaped silence. The gods were silent. That was the law of the world, as fixed as the fall of stones.

And now a knot had shone.

"When?" she asked the mother, and her voice came out strange. "When did you tie — no. When did you set it against her? What hour?"

"Just past dusk. Why? Does the hour matter?"

Everything mattered. Everything had suddenly begun to matter in ways Petra had spent five years making sure it wouldn't. Dusk — the knot had gone on at dusk and the fever had broken at midnight, and Petra sat very still in her lamplit room doing arithmetic she did not want to do, remembering the hairline-crack wrongness under the market's noon noise, the sense of a door swinging somewhere. Remembering, with a chill that started in her scalp and ran down her spine like meltwater, something Maren had said once, late, half-asleep by the fire, in the voice she used for things too large for waking speech:

*When the old ones go quiet, girl, they aren't gone. They're holding their breath. And one day the breath goes out of them all at once, and every working on earth will catch like dry straw, and God help whoever's holding a match.*

Outside the window, Greyhollow lay dark and frost-bound under its smoke, ten thousand chimneys, three drowned roads, temples full of people praying to nothing. Petra looked at the fading knot, at the sleeping child, at the weeping mother, and understood that she was holding a match, that she had been holding matches for five years and calling them thread.

Somewhere across the city — she would swear afterward, though no one believed her — somewhere far off in the dark, a bell trembled on its rope.

It did not ring. Not quite. It shivered, the way a throat shivers before speech, and went still.

Petra sat in her small careful room, among her careful lies, and listened to the silence afterward, and for the first time in five years, she prayed.
