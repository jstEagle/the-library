## 2. The Bell That Rang Itself

Petra had learned to sleep like a hedge-witch sleeps: lightly, with one ear turned toward the door and one hand curled around the salt dish on her nightstand. Five years of careful living had taught her that trouble came at the hours when honest people were too tired to notice it. So when the bells began — long before dawn, before even the bakers had lit their ovens — she was awake within three heartbeats, sitting upright in the dark of her rented room above the chandler's shop, listening.

It was not one bell. It was all of them.

Greyhollow had eleven temples, counting the ruined shrine of the Twin Sisters down by the tannery run-off, whose bell had not swung in forty years because there was no rope left to pull and no priest left to pull it. Petra counted as they sounded: Saint Aldhelm's deep bronze note, the sharper silver voice of the Chantry of Tears, the cracked old bell of the Saltmarket chapel that always rang a half-tone flat, like a man singing through a broken tooth. Eleven voices, eleven temples, all ringing at once, all ringing *wildly* — not the measured tolling of a sexton's practiced arm but a peal like panic, like joy, like something enormous laughing in a language made of metal.

And beneath it, under the bronze and the silver, a sound she felt in her back teeth and in the hollow behind her sternum. A low vibration, the kind a great beast makes before it speaks.

She knew that sound. She had heard it described once, in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and burning hair, by a woman who talked about gods the way other people talked about weather.

*A god clearing its throat,* Maren had said, stirring something in a black pot, not looking up. *That's what you'll hear first, girl, if you ever hear anything at all. Not thunder. Not trumpets. A clearing of the throat. The small polite sound of something vast deciding whether to bother speaking.*

The bells rang for the better part of an hour. Petra sat on the edge of her bed with the salt dish in her lap and did not move until the last echo died against the hills east of the city. Then she dressed in the dark, took up her market basket, and went out into streets that were already full of people kneeling.

* * *

Dawn came up grey-gold over Greyhollow, and the city was on its knees.

That was the image Petra would carry afterward, the one she would return to in the years that followed whenever she needed to remember how quickly a careful life could come apart: the whole of Wren Street, from the chandler's corner to the fishmongers' arch, crowded with kneeling figures. Bakers still dusted with flour. Night-soil men with their carts abandoned mid-lane. A prostitute from the Lamb's Head in nothing but her shift and a shawl, kneeling in a puddle, weeping without any noise at all. They knelt facing no particular direction. Some had their hands pressed flat to the cobblestones, as if checking whether the ground itself had grown warm.

Nobody spoke. That was the worst of it. Greyhollow was a city that never stopped talking — haggling, cursing, gossiping, singing drunk on feast days — and now it was silent except for the drip of the eaves and, somewhere far off, a baby crying and being hushed too fiercely.

Petra stood in her doorway with her basket on her arm and felt the dread settle into her bones like cold water into wool.

Because she knew what this was. Or she knew what it wasn't. It was not piety. She had watched these same people walk past the temples every morning of the five years she'd lived among them, eyes forward, mouths set, the way you pass a house where someone once died. The old gods had gone quiet a generation ago — that was simply a fact of the world, like the color of the sky. The Crown had filled the silence with law; the Church of the Reformed Covenant had filled it with sermons about how the silence was itself holy, a test of faith, a clean room swept for a god who would return only when the country deserved Him. And the people of Greyhollow had done what people do. They had gotten on with things. They had stopped praying, mostly, or prayed the way you talk to a relative who never writes back — briefly, guiltily, expecting nothing.

Expecting nothing. And now, at dawn, every bell in the city had rung itself, and the nothing had answered.

A woman near Petra's step — the cooper's wife, Marta, a good customer, a buyer of fever-charms who always paid extra and never asked why they worked — looked up at her with a face washed raw. "Did you feel it?" Marta whispered. "In your chest. Like something *breathing* under the stones."

"I felt the bells," Petra said carefully. "Bells ring. Ropes slip. Birds land on them."

"Eleven bells." Marta's laugh was a strange, wet sound. "Eleven birds, then. All at once. All mad."

"Strange weather," Petra said, and it was the oldest lie in her repertoire, the hedge-witch's refrain, the words Maren had drilled into her until they were smoother than prayer beads: *strange weather, bad harvest moon, a trick of the light.* The lie you told so that ordinary people could go on believing their world was ordinary, because the alternative was to burn someone, and better it be no one than anyone.

But Marta wasn't listening anymore. She had turned back toward the east, where the sun was coming up the color of an egg yolk, and she was whispering something under her breath — and Petra, with a sick lurch, recognized the shape of it through the mumble. Not a Covenant prayer. Something older. Something with the rhythm of a name in it.

People remembered faster than they thought they would. That was the thing nobody understood yet, standing kneeled in the streets. You could forget a god for twenty years, but forgetting is only a door closed, not a wall built. And somewhere in the night, every door in the country had blown open at once.

* * *

By midmorning the wonder had begun to curdle, the way wonder does in a city with walls and taxes.

Petra opened her stall in the Saltmarket because not opening it would be stranger than opening it, and because her hands needed work the way other people's hands needed prayer. She strung her luck-knots on their usual pegs. She laid out the fever-charms in their little cloth nest, each one a twist of waxed linen around salt, ash, and a word spoken into it at the making. She arranged the dried herbs that were legal — chamomile, sage, yarrow — in plain view where any passing constable could see them, the way a fishmonger displays his freshest fish.

Her first customer of the day was a boy of eight or nine, the tanner's youngest, with a scabbed knee and coins clutched in his fist. He wanted a luck-knot for his brother, who was going down to the river flats to dig for clay and was forever falling into holes.

"For falling," the boy said solemnly. "So he doesn't."

"That's not really what luck does," Petra said, tying the knot with her fingers moving through the old pattern without her permission. Three loops, two crossings, a word breathed into the center. "Luck just tips things. Small things. A foot finds a root it would have missed. Nothing dramatic."

"It worked before," the boy said. "Yesterday. I bought one for our dog and yesterday he didn't die."

Petra's hands went still on the cord.

Charm-work hadn't worked in five years. That was the bargain the world had struck, and Petra had built her whole careful life inside its terms: she sold knots and twists of linen that were, functionally, string and cloth. People bought them because hope was cheap and because Petra had a knack for looking trustworthy, and the charms did nothing, and no one was ever burned, and everyone went home. Once — early, in her first frightened months — she had tested one herself, a finding-charm for a lost ring, and felt the familiar shape of the working slide off the world like water off glass. The magic was gone. Whatever the old gods had been, whatever current Maren had tapped in her kitchen full of smoke, it had been shut off, and Petra had been grateful. Grateful every single day. A witch whose magic didn't work was just a woman selling string.

"The dog," Petra said. "What happened to it?"

"Sick. Two days dying. Ma said we'd drown it come morning because he was suffering." The boy shrugged, matter-of-fact the way children are about the cruelties they've been raised in. "Come morning he was fine. Running around. Fat as you like. Ma cried." He held out his coins. "So. For my brother. For falling."

Petra took the coins. Her fingers were cold. She tied the knot and breathed the word into it and felt — distinctly, unmistakably, for the first time in five years — the word *take*. Felt it sink into the cord the way heat sinks into a stone held near a fire. A tiny answering pressure, like a hand closing around hers from the other side of a wall.

"There," she said, and her voice came out steady, which she counted as the finest working of her life. "One knot. Tell him to wear it under his shirt where his ma can't wash it by accident."

The boy ran off happy. Petra stood in the Saltmarket amid the ordinary roar of commerce — fish thrown, prices cursed, a cart wheel screaming on its axle — and quietly, thoroughly, panicked.

Because it wasn't just the bells. Now that she let herself look, the market was full of small wrongnesses wearing festival clothes. The flower-seller's bucket of winter-dead narcissus had come up green overnight. Old Tam the pie-man, who had burned his hand every winter for thirty years, was showing off a palm smooth as a child's. And twice that morning Petra heard it happen: a passerby murmuring a half-remembered invocation over spilled goods or a stubbed toe — *Sister of the Ford, look kindly* — and the spill righting itself, or the toe, impossibly, not throbbing. Small mercies. Sloppy ones. Miracles performed with the precision of a drunk man setting down a cup.

Maren's voice again, unbidden, from five years and a hundred miles away: *When they answer, girl, mind how they answer. The old ones don't think like us. You ask for rain, you'd best say which field, and how much, and for how long, and be ready to take the flood if you misspoke.*

The gods were answering prayers in Greyhollow. Badly. Enthusiastically. Like a lamp turned up too bright after years of dark, like a deaf man hearing suddenly and shouting over everything. And every answered prayer was a lit candle in a country where the Crown's edicts hung on every church door, and the penalty for unlicensed workings was the pyre, and the pyre had been hungry lately.

* * *

They posted the new edict at noon, and Petra was close enough to the temple steps to read it over the shoulders of the crowd.

A herald in Crown blue read it aloud in a voice trained to carry, while four inquisitors stood above him on the steps in their dove-grey cloaks, faces still as carved saints. The crowd had come to gawk and stayed to listen, and the listening had a quality Petra distrusted deeply: hunger.

*"By order of the Lord Protector and the Holy Office of the Reformed Covenant:"* the herald intoned, *"in light of the disturbances of this dawn, all persons are reminded that the working of miracles, charms, blessings, or any alteration of natural course by unlicensed means is forbidden throughout the realm. Any person receiving or witnessing such a working is bound by law and conscience to report it within one day to the nearest officer of the Holy Office. Reports may be given anonymously. Informants acting in good faith shall be protected. Those concealing knowledge of unlicensed workings share in their guilt."*

He paused, and the pause was a blade laid flat on a table.

*"Further: the bells of this city rang this morning with no hand upon them. This Office declares the event an act of mechanical sabotage and conspiracy, currently under investigation. Persons spreading superstitious interpretations — claims of divine intervention, resurrection, or the return of the old gods — shall be charged with sedition. There are no returning gods. There is only disorder, and disorder has authors. Find them."*

The crowd made a sound — not quite a murmur, not quite a sigh. Somewhere in it, Petra heard a woman whisper, *"There are no returning gods,"* in the tone of someone repeating a rule they intend to break.

An inquisitor stepped forward to the edge of the steps. He was tall and dry-looking, with a face like folded paper and eyes the pale grey of a winter sky, and he surveyed the crowd the way a surveyor surveys land he intends to regrade. He did not shout. His voice carried anyway, level and precise, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"You knelt this morning," he said. "I saw you. Half of you are still dirty at the knees. I want you to understand that kneeling is not a crime. Wondering is not a crime. Even hoping — even *praying* — is not a crime, though I will tell you plainly that prayer is a door, and doors open in both directions, and you do not know what is on the other side of this one." He let that sit. "Whatever rang those bells wants something. Things that want something come to you in shapes you trust. A dead husband's voice. A child saved. Luck, small and sweet. And by the time you realize the shape was bait, you have already swallowed the hook, and the Office cannot help you, because you hid it, because you loved it." His gaze moved across the crowd like a slow searchlight, and for one vertiginous moment it seemed to snag on Petra's stall, on the little cloth nest of fever-charms, on her. "Bring me what you hear. Bring it today. The first person through my door with truth will find me more grateful than any god has ever been."

He turned and went inside, and the crowd broke apart like a dropped pot, shards of conversation scattering in every direction.

Petra packed her stall with hands that shook only slightly, which she considered another miracle, and a worse one than the bells.

* * *

That evening she locked her door, shuttered her window, and sat on the floor with her back against the bed, doing the arithmetic she had been avoiding all day.

Item: the bells had rung themselves, and she had recognized the sound beneath them, which meant Maren had known what was coming, or something like it, five years ago.

Item: the charms worked now. Hers did. The boy's dog lived. Which meant the current was flowing again after five dry years, sudden as a burst dam, and everything she had buried her talent under was floating loose.

Item: the Crown had declared the miracle a conspiracy, which meant the Crown was afraid, which meant the Crown would start looking for authors, and there was exactly one charm-stall in the Saltmarket whose knots had started *taking* yesterday.

Item, and this was the item that made her put her head back against the bed frame and close her eyes: somewhere out in the dark, thousands of desperate people who had spent a generation praying into silence were discovering, this very night, that the silence had ended. And they were going to pray harder. And the answers were going to keep coming, loud and crooked and enormous, like a giant waking in a small room. And between the faithful who would love her for it and the inquisitors who would burn her for it, Petra Underhill — seller of string, keeper of small lies, woman who had spent five years building a life with walls thin enough to see through but thick enough to live in — was about to become the most important person in a country that had forgotten its gods and remembered them all in one terrible dawn.

She thought about running. She was good at running; she had references.

Then, through the floorboards, from the chandler's family below, she heard them saying grace — the new grace, the old grace, the one with the names in it that hadn't been spoken aloud since her mistress was young. Saying it clumsily, getting the words wrong, laughing a little, crying a little, saying it anyway.

The bells did not ring again that night. But lying awake in the dark, Petra could have sworn she felt it once more through the soles of her feet, faint and patient, like a knock at a very large door:

that low, vast, throat-clearing hum.

Something was deciding whether to bother speaking.

And in the morning, she knew with a certainty that settled over her like frost, she would have to decide whether to open the spellbook she had stolen five years ago and never once dared to read.
