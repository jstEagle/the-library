## 3. What the Apprentice Took

Five years before the bells rang themselves, on a night with no moon and a wind that smelled of coming frost, Petra Underhill stole from her mistress a book, a vial, and her own future — though at the time she believed she was only saving it.

She had planned the theft for three weeks. She knew this because she had counted the days in knots, tying one into the frayed hem of her sleeve each evening as she lay in the narrow bed beneath the eaves of Maren's cottage, listening to the house settle around its secrets. Twenty-one knots. She had told herself that if she reached twenty-one and still wanted to run, then wanting to run was not fear but knowledge, and knowledge was the only inheritance Maren had ever promised her.

The cottage stood at the crook of two hills where the road forgot itself, an hour's walk from the nearest village whose name Petra had already half-let go of, the way you let go of anything you intend never to see again. It was a low house, turf-roofed, its chimney built crooked so the smoke came out sideways like a lie told quickly. There were charms nailed above every door and window — not the pretty ones Petra would later sell in Greyhollow markets, all ribbon and plausible deniability, but the old kind: knucklebones wrapped in red thread, a horseshoe hung upside down *because down is where the dead walk*, bundles of rowan gone grey with weather. Maren had nailed them there herself, decades ago, and never explained them, because she believed explanations were a kind of invitation.

Petra had lived there four years by then. She had arrived at fourteen, delivered by a mother who did not look back down the lane, with a bundle of clothes and a birthmark on her wrist shaped like a keyhole. *She has the look*, her mother had said, and Maren had looked — long, and without kindness, and without cruelty either, the way you look at weather coming — and said, *Then she'd better learn what the look costs.*

Four years of learning. Four years of grinding grave-dust in a mortar until her wrists ached, of memorizing which herbs lied about their names, of standing barefoot in the herb garden at dawn reciting the names of things that had no names anymore because the gods had gone quiet a generation past and taken the words with them. Four years of being loved, she would later admit, in the specific and terrible way Maren loved things: completely, and as property.

That was what she had come to believe, in the twenty-one nights of knots. That was the crack the whole plan grew from.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with something small: a word overheard through a floorboard.

Maren kept her working room below, dug into the hillside like a root cellar, and she worked at night, always at night, muttering in a language Petra had been forbidden to learn — *you'll learn it when your mouth is ready and not before, girl, and your mouth isn't ready, your mouth wants things*. On the night of the first knot, Petra had gone down to bank the fire and found the door barred from inside, which had never happened before, and instead of knocking she had done the cowardly, human thing: she put her ear to the wood.

Maren was speaking to someone. Not muttering — speaking, in the pauses of a conversation, waiting between sentences for answers Petra could not hear.

*"Not yet,"* Maren said. *"She's not ripe. You don't pick a vessel green or it sours everything in the jar."*

A silence, long enough that Petra thought the conversation had ended.

*"Because I promised someone,"* Maren said, and her voice changed, went thin and old in a way Petra had never heard it go, even at funerals, even when she laid out the dead of the village and closed their eyes with two fingers and a word. *"I keep my promises. Even the ones I hate."*

Another silence. Then, softer, almost tender, which frightened Petra more than anything else that night:

*"Yes. I know what it will cost her. I've known since the day she came up my path with that mark on her wrist. Why do you think I took her in at all?"*

Petra had climbed back to her bed and lain awake until the birds started, and somewhere in the grey hour before dawn she tied the first knot into her sleeve, and her hands barely shook at all.

A vessel. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She turned it over night after night, examining every face of it. She knew the old stories — every hedge-witch's apprentice knew them, the way farm children know slaughter stories. The great workings needed fuel, and the best fuel was a life freely given, and the next best was a life *prepared*, grown toward the purpose like a pig toward winter. A vessel. A jar. Something you kept until it was time to pour it out.

*Why do you think I took her in at all?*

There it was. Four years of gruel and grammar and cold dawns, four years of the closest thing to family she had ever had, and underneath it all, the whole time, a woman fattening a jar.

Or so she believed. Belief, Petra would learn much later, is just a story you tell yourself with the doors locked. But that comes later. That comes after the running, after Greyhollow, after five years of selling luck to people who needed none and fever-charms that worked by suggestion and warm tea. That comes after the guilt has grown its own garden inside her, watered nightly.

On the twenty-first night, she ran.

* * *

She took three things, and she remembered afterward — she would always remember, with the terrible fidelity of shame — the exact order in which she took them.

First, the vial. Grave-dust, Maren called it, though it wasn't dust from any grave; it was finer than dust, darker than dark, and it weighed nothing and yet when you held the vial your arm grew tired, as if you were holding something that was holding you back. Maren kept it in the working room, in a niche behind the third stone from the hearth, and Petra had ground and sifted and re-ground it a hundred times without ever once being told what it was *for*. She took it because it was the most valuable thing in the house and because taking it felt like taking a hostage. If Maren meant to use her, then Maren would need this, and now Maren would have to come after her, and coming after her meant —

Meant what? Even then, crouched in the dark of the working room with the vial sweating cold against her palm, some honest corner of Petra's mind asked: *meant what? Chase me across three counties to reclaim an ingredient? Or chase me because the ingredient is useless without the vessel?*

She had shut that corner away and bolted it. She was good at bolting things. She would spend five years getting better at it.

Second, the book. Maren's spellbook — though *spellbook* was too clean a word for it. It was a ledger, really, bound in hide that might have been goat and might not have been, swollen fat with forty years of insertions: pages sewn in mid-binding, loose leaves folded in, receipts and recipes and warnings written in three different hands, because Maren had inherited it from her own mistress the same way everything in that cottage was inherited — along a chain of women who each added their weight to it. Petra had copied from it under supervision for years, always with Maren's hand flat on the page beside hers, always with the sense of reading over someone's shoulder rather than being shown. She had never been permitted to hold it alone. So she held it alone now, in the dark, and it was heavier than she expected, and warmer, and it seemed to lean into her grip like something that had been waiting a long time to be picked up.

She told herself she took it out of spite. Proof, evidence, leverage — if anyone ever came asking about the witch of the crooked hills, here was the whole confession in hide covers. But her hands knew better. Her hands held it the way you hold a letter from someone you love who has wronged you: carefully, and against your own judgment, and unable to put it down.

Third — last, and this was the part that shamed her most, because it was the part that proved the theft was not strategy but grief — she took the charm off the nail above her own bed. A small thing, a knot of braided hair and red thread no bigger than a thumbnail, that Maren had hung there Petra's first night in the cottage without a word of explanation. Protection, she'd assumed. Or ownership. A tag on a jar. She took it anyway. She wore it against her skin for five years and never once untied it to see what was braided inside.

Then she wrote the note. She had decided against a note seventeen times and for a note nineteen times, and the note won, because silence felt too much like agreement with whatever Maren had planned for her. She wrote it by rushlight at the kitchen table, her handwriting going worse as it went:

*I heard you through the floor. I won't be poured out for anyone. I'm sorry about the dust and the book. I'll pay you back someday. — P.*

She read it over twice. She almost added *thank you* and did not, because thank-you implied the debt was simple, and she could not bear to make it simple. She left it weighted under the salt dish, which was the household's sign for *read this before you speak*, and then she stood in the middle of the kitchen of the only home she had left and listened to the house breathe around her, and she said goodbye to it in the only language she trusted, which was silence.

Then she opened the door, and the night took her.

* * *

She walked east through the hills all that night and the next day and the next night, sleeping in hay-barns and once, memorably badly, in a shrine to a god whose name had worn off the lintel, curled under the altar with the spellbook for a pillow and the vial in her fist like a talisman against dreams. The gods had been silent for a generation; everyone said so; the temples in the towns had been converted to granaries and courtrooms. But the little roadside shrines still got offerings, out of habit, the way you set a place at the table for someone dead. Petra left nothing at the nameless god's shrine. She was carrying quite enough stolen holy things already.

On the third day she came down out of the hills and saw Greyhollow for the first time: a market city strung along a river like beads on a wet cord, smoke rising straight in the windless morning, temple towers with their bells hanging silent and green with age. It was big enough to be anonymous. That was all she wanted. She had a few coins sewn into her hem — Maren paid her, oddly enough, fairly, for the work she did, another fact that refused to fit neatly into the story of the fattened jar — and she spent the first one on bread and the second on a room in a lodging house above a dyer's shop, where the smell of mordant ate through everything and made her eyes stream for a week until she stopped noticing, which was, she understood even then, how most unbearable things were survived.

She did not open the book.

This is the detail that would have surprised everyone who later knew her — Sef, Anselm, the customers who trusted her charms, the inquisitors who hunted her. Petra Underhill, hedge-witch, keeper of a hundred small workings, had stolen the single greatest repository of craft she would ever hold in her hands, and she never read it. Not once. Not in the first terrified winter, when she was sick with fever and alone and would have given anything for Maren's voice, even the voice saying *your mouth isn't ready*. Not in the second year, when she began making charms to sell and found herself inventing them from scratch, reinventing, clumsily, things that were certainly written down in those fat pages in her mistress's careful hand. Not ever.

She told herself it was safety. Unopened, the book was contraband; opened, it was evidence. She hid it in the false bottom of her charm-chest, under the ribbons and the plausible deniability, and she told herself that too: *it's hidden, it's safe, I'm safe.*

But the truth was simpler and uglier, and she knew it on the nights she couldn't sleep, which were many. She didn't open the book because the book was the last conversation she and Maren had never finished. Every page was a sentence in Maren's voice. To open it was to hear her again — *grind it finer, girl; no, finer than that; patience is the whole of the craft, everything else is decoration* — and Petra could not bear to hear her, because hearing her risked understanding her, and understanding her risked discovering that the story of the vessel was wrong.

And if the story was wrong, then she had robbed the only person who had ever wanted her, and run from the only home she'd been offered, and knotted twenty-one lies into her sleeve for nothing.

Better the locked cover. Better the guilt you can carry than the guilt you have to read.

* * *

Five years passed the way water passes through cloth — slowly, invisibly, leaving everything damp and slightly faded. Petra built her small careful life. She learned the market rhythms of Greyhollow, which festivals drew crowds hungry for luck, which seasons brought fevers worth charming. She learned which customers wanted to believe and which wanted to pretend, and she learned to serve both with the same bland smile. She became known — quietly, deniably — as reliable. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that worked. A charm that worked was a confession, and in a country where magic was outlawed and the gods had gone quiet, confessions were dangerous.

She kept the vial in the false bottom of the chest too, unopened beside the unread book, two secrets keeping each other company in the dark. Sometimes, on bad nights, she took the chest out and touched the vial through the wood, the way you touch a scar to check it's still yours.

And sometimes — rarely, in the hour before dawn, in the gap between sleep and work — she dreamed of the cottage at the crook of the hills. In the dreams the chimney smoked sideways and the charms on the doors were all intact except one, the small knot of hair and thread from above her bed, and there was a nail-hole where it used to hang, and the hole whistled faintly in the wind, a sound almost like a voice, almost like a name, almost like *Petra, girl, your mouth isn't ready* — and she would wake with her wrist burning where the keyhole birthmark sat, and lie staring at the ceiling of the room above the dyer's shop, telling herself the oldest lie in her careful inventory:

*Whatever she meant to do to me, I did the right thing. I did the right thing. I did—*

Five years of knots. And then, on an ordinary market morning, in a stall that smelled of ribbon and dried lavender, a child's fever-charm would work — actually work, undeniably, miraculously work — and every locked cover Petra owned would begin, very quietly, to rattle on its hinges.

But that morning had not come yet. Five years ago, there was only the road east, and the book riding heavy against her spine like a hand between her shoulders, pushing her on — or holding her back. She never decided which. She never opened it to find out.
