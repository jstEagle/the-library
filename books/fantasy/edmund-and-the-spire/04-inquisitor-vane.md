## 4. Inquisitor Vane

The cell beneath the Assize Hall had been a wine cellar once, back when the Crown still trusted its magistrates with wine. Now it held a table, two chairs, one lamp, and a woman named Aldith Crane who had been kneeling on the flagstones for three hours because Inquisitor Vane had not yet told her she could stand.

He had not told her to kneel, either. That was the point. Kneeling was her own idea, and everything about her own ideas was what he had been sent to Greyhollow to understand.

"Say it again," he said. "From the beginning. Slowly."

Aldith Crane was a laundress by trade, a widow by misfortune, and — as of nine days ago — something else entirely. She was perhaps fifty, with hands gone soft and red from other people's linens, and she kept pressing them flat against her thighs as though they might otherwise rise on their own and do something. Her lips moved constantly, silently, shaping words that were not words. Prayers, Vane thought. Or apologies. With the faithful it was often difficult to tell the difference.

"She was sick," Aldith said. "My girl. Bess. Fever, the sweating kind, the kind that took her father in the spring of the last bad year. The physician came and looked at her and shook his head the way they do when they want you to stop paying them. And I sat by her bed all night, and I did what I've done every night since I was a girl. I prayed."

"To whom?"

She flinched. It was a small flinch, quickly mastered, but Vane saw it the way he saw everything: completely, and without mercy, and with a kind of grief underneath the seeing that he permitted himself only in the dark hours before dawn.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth, sir, I swear it on my husband's grave. We're told to pray to the Nine. I said the names I was taught. But it didn't feel like saying names anymore. It felt like — like calling down a well. Like there was finally water at the bottom."

"And the fever broke."

"The fever broke." Her voice went strange on the words, hollowed out. "It broke like a plate breaks, sir. All at once, and everywhere, and you're standing there holding pieces."

Vane turned a page in his ledger without looking at it. He had stopped needing the notes an hour ago; the account was written into him now, the way scripture is written into a boy who memorizes it before he understands it, so that understanding arrives later already bleeding.

Bess Crane's fever had broken at the hour of the ox, on the ninth night. So had the fevers of four other children within earshot of the temple bells — though the bells had not rung yet; that horror was still three days away, and Vane's superiors in the capital did not yet know how much worse this was going to get. Five children healed at once across half a city. The Crown's physicians called it coincidence. The Crown's priests called it grace. The Crown's edicts, posted by noon tomorrow, would call it sorcery.

Vane called it none of these things. Vane had read the list of what else had happened at the hour of the ox, and he knew that whatever had passed through Greyhollow that night had not been tidy enough to be grace.

"Tell me about your neighbor," he said.

Aldith's hands stopped moving. "Master Poll."

"Master Poll," Vane agreed. "The cooper. Whose wife left him for a tanner in Southreach, whose son died at sea, who owed you money for washing. You prayed for him too, didn't you. After Bess got better. You thought — since it worked once —"

"I only asked," she whispered. "I only *asked*."

"What did you ask?"

"That he be made humble." The word came out of her like a stone coughed up from the throat. "That's all. That he learn humility. It's in the litany, sir, it's practically *in* the litany — *let the proud be humbled, let the low be lifted* — I said it the way we all say it, I never thought —"

"Describe what happened to Master Poll."

She described it. Vane let her describe it slowly, because he needed her to hear herself say it, and because he needed to watch her face while she said it, and because some part of him — the part that had entered the priesthood at fourteen believing the world was a house with God in every room — needed to hear it again and again until it stopped feeling impossible.

At dawn on the morning after Bess's healing, Master Odo Poll had woken to find his tongue swollen to twice its size, so that he could not speak above a whisper and could not pronounce a single word beginning with a letter he had ever used proudly. His debts, every one of them, had been found that same week to be recorded in ledgers that had rotted through — the ink intact, the paper gone to mulch — so that no debt could be proven and no creditor could collect. And his house, his fine timbered house with the carved lintel he'd been so vain of, had settled overnight on its foundations so that every doorframe in it stood exactly four inches shorter than it had, forcing Odo Poll to bow his head to enter or leave any room he owned.

Humbled. Precisely. Literally. Down to the four inches.

"He can't say my name," Aldith finished. Her voice had gone thin as thread. "He tried to curse me in the street and the swelling — every time he gets to *Crane* his tongue — he makes this sound, sir, like a bell with a crack in it. Everyone laughs. I never wanted anyone laughed at. I wanted —"

"You wanted justice," Vane said quietly. "You got a carpenter."

She began to cry then, silently, the way the poor cry, having learned young that noise costs extra. Vane let her. He rose, crossed the cell, and poured water from the jug into the tin cup, and set it near her knee, and returned to his chair. These small courtesies were not kindness, he told himself. They were technique. A witness who has been given water tells longer stories.

It was a lie, and he was practiced at telling it to himself. It was kindness. It was also technique. He had learned long ago that he could hold both in one hand if he didn't grip either too hard.

* * *

The lamp burned low. Outside, somewhere above them, Greyhollow went about its evening — carts, arguments, the smell of frying onions drifting down through the ventilation grate along with the sound of a drunkard singing a song about a miller's wife. An ordinary city. Three days ago it had been an ordinary city, and now under its ordinariness Vane could hear the other thing, the thing under the floor of the world, shifting its weight.

"Your Grace said the words felt like calling down a well," he said. "When did they stop feeling empty? Exactly when?"

"I don't —"

"Think. This matters more than anything else you'll tell me tonight."

She thought. He watched her do it, watched her go back through nine days to that night at the bedside, and what surfaced in her face frightened her more than the memory itself.

"It wasn't sudden," she said at last. "That's the horrible part. It was like — you know when you knock on a door and no one answers, and you knock for years, and you forget there's ever been anyone behind it, knocking becomes just — a thing your hand does?" She looked up at him. "It was like that. And then one night the door opened. Not all the way. Just — a crack. And something breathed out."

"*Something.*"

"Not the Nine." She said it fast, guiltily, the way one betrays a friend. "Forgive me, but it wasn't. I know the Nine. I've said their litanies ten thousand times and they've never once — it wasn't them. It was older. It was like the difference between a man's voice and — I don't know. A hill's voice. Something that was here before the names we use. It heard me the way a wall hears you. Everything. All at once. Every word I've ever prayed and never meant, and every word I meant and never prayed, and it couldn't tell the difference, and it didn't care about the difference, it just — answered. All of it. Everything I'd ever asked for, sir. Everything since I was a girl."

Vane sat very still.

"Everything," he repeated.

"There was a dog I wanted when I was seven. There's a dog in our yard now. Big as a calf, sir, and it won't leave, and it looks at me like it's *waiting*, like it knows it's a debt come due after thirty years and it means to see the thing paid proper. My husband—" her breath caught— "my husband used to say he wished I'd sing more. Before he died. Two nights ago I woke up and I was singing. I hadn't started. I was just — already singing, in his voice, his exact voice, a song he used to hum, and I sang until dawn and I couldn't stop and I don't remember any of it except that the neighbors knocked on the walls."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim the wick.

*Everything since I was a girl.* Vane wrote nothing down. Some sentences were too heavy for ink; they bent the page.

This was the shape of it, then. Not a god returning — the priests would have wept with joy over a returning god, and gods, whatever else they were, had manners. This was something answering the way a drowning man answers a shout from shore: with everything it had, all at once, unable to choose, unable to measure, grabbing at every voice that called it. A generation of unanswered prayers, stacked up behind a door, and the door cracking open, and the whole hoard spilling out into the world at once — every wish ever whispered, granted literally, granted *old*, granted by something that had forgotten how human wanting worked, if it had ever known.

And the Crown, in its wisdom, would hang a laundress for it.

* * *

"Stand up, Mistress Crane."

She stood, knees cracking, and swayed, and he steadied her elbow briefly and let go.

"Here is what will happen," Vane said. "Tomorrow the edicts post. Unlicensed workings are capital offenses; answered prayers will be ruled evidence of witchcraft; those who confess and name others will receive amnesty. You will be brought before the magistrate, and you will confess to hedge-sorcery, and you will name the person who taught you the words you prayed — there is always someone, Mistress Crane; even prayer has a lineage — and you will serve five years' penance labor instead of the rope. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir." No relief in it. Only exhaustion, and under the exhaustion something that hurt him to look at. "Sir — may I ask you something? You keep asking about the well. About the voice at the bottom."

"Ask."

"Have you ever prayed to it?"

The question went into him like a needle finding the one unarmored seam.

Because yes. Of course yes. Every inquisitor he had ever known prayed; it was the profession's open secret, its dirty joke, its wound. You spent your days proving miracles false and your nights on your knees asking for one. Vane had prayed at his mother's deathbed for a reprieve that did not come. He had prayed, in the famine years, over fields that stayed gray. He had prayed every morning of his life the full litany of the Nine, precisely, perfectly, in a voice trained to carry to the back of a cathedral — and he had never once, not once in thirty years, heard so much as an echo.

He was the best hunter the Crown had, and he hunted because he had been hunting all his life, and the thing he hunted had never once turned around.

"No," he said.

Aldith Crane looked at him with the terrible gentle clarity of the exhausted, and he understood that she saw straight through the lie, and that she was too tired to hate him for it.

"You should try the old words," she said. "Not the Nine. The ones my grandmother used at the well, before the temples said to stop. Just — careful, sir. Because it answers. Whatever's down there, it answers *everything*."

They took her up to the cells then, two guardsmen with faces carefully emptied of expression, and Vane remained alone in the cellar with the dying lamp and his ledger full of things he had not written.

* * *

He did not sleep. He climbed instead to the Assize Hall's roof, where the city spread out below him in its ordinary dark, chimney smoke rising straight in the windless cold, and far off the black line of the hills where no lights showed, because there were no villages there anymore worth lighting. He knelt on the cold lead of the roof, alone, as he had knelt ten thousand times.

He should pray the litany. It was discipline; discipline was the spine of him; take away the spine and the whole body of his life collapsed. He opened his mouth to begin the familiar words —

— and instead heard Aldith Crane: *it answers everything, sir, everything since I was a girl,* and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

What would it answer of mine?

Thirty years of prayers, stacked and unanswered. Every petition, every plea, every furious midnight bargain. If the door cracked open over *his* life, what would come pouring through? What dog, what debt, what dead voice singing in the dark?

He was afraid. He noted the fear the way he noted all evidence, catalogued it, filed it — and then, kneeling on the roof of the Assize Hall with the whole sleeping country around him going soft at its edges like paper left in the rain, Inquisitor Edmund Vane folded his hands and did the one thing forbidden to a hunter.

He prayed to the thing he was hunting.

Not the litany. Not the Nine. He had no old grandmother's words; his lineage ran only through sanctioned texts and burned books. So he gave it the truth instead, plain and unadorned, the first honest prayer of his life:

*I have served you in ignorance my whole life. I have enforced the silence of heaven with a sword, and I would do it again tomorrow, because order is all I have to give. But if you can hear me — if any of it was ever heard — tell me one thing. One. Tell me why you stopped.*

The wind moved over the rooftops. Somewhere below, a temple bell — the great bell of Saint Verene's, chained and silent these twenty years, its rope locked in an iron cage since the Edict of Quiet — shivered in its yoke with a sound too small to be called ringing and too deliberate to be called anything else.

One note. Faint as a finger tapped on a door.

Then nothing.

Vane knelt a long time on the cold lead, heart slamming, telling himself it was the wind, knowing it was not the wind, knowing — with the certainty of a man who has spent his life distinguishing forged documents from true ones — that he had just been *answered*. Not with mercy. Not with doctrine. With a single note, like something vast clearing its throat on the far side of a door it was about to open.

By noon tomorrow the edicts would post, and he would seal the gates of Greyhollow, and begin the searches, and burn the charm-stalls, and offer amnesty to any witch who named another. That was his duty. Duty was the spine of him.

But he rose from the roof with a new entry in the ledger behind his eyes, unwritten, undeniable: *the prisoner is not the source. The prisoner is a symptom. Find the door.*

And find whoever, in this city of a hundred thousand souls, had taught a laundress's prayer to work — because in Greyhollow, Vane was certain, someone was selling luck-knots and fever-charms to customers who pretended not to know what they were buying.

He would start with the market at dawn.
