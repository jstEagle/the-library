## 5. The Priest Without a Parish

The bell had not rung again. That was somehow worse.

For three days Greyhollow held its breath and listened for it the way a child listens for a parent's footsteps after breaking something. The markets reopened because markets always reopen — hunger is more patient than fear — but the crowds moved differently now, hunched, quick-eyed, each person pretending not to look at every other person. The inquisitors' edicts still hung on the temple doors, their wax seals gone soft in the drizzle, and beneath them someone had chalked a prayer in a shaking hand: *Hear us. Whoever you are. Hear us.*

Petra read it on her way to the stall and felt her stomach turn over.

She had stopped selling charms. Not officially — officially she was simply ill, or grieving a fictional aunt, or short of thread — but her hands had refused the work. Every knot she tied now felt like shouting into a dark room where something had just woken up. She sold candles instead. Candles were honest. Candles only promised fire.

So it was with a basket of tallow and a lie about her aunt that she came to her corner of Tallow Row on the third morning after the bell, and found the priest already there.

He was sitting on her own stool, behind her own counter, as if he owned the lease. A big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had once been trimmed with care and was now trimmed with resignation, and vestments worn down to the weave — grey where they must have been black, black where they must have been embroidered. His collar was missing. Priests without parishes cut their collars off themselves; she'd seen it done. It was supposed to be a wound you showed people.

His hands were folded on the counter like a man at prayer, except that between them, laid out with terrible gentleness, were three of her luck-knots.

"I bought these," he said, "from a woman on Cooper's Lane. Tuesday last." He turned one over with one finger. "Her son's fever broke that night. She swears it was the knot. She's told four neighbors. They've all come back to Cooper's Lane looking for the seller."

"The seller's gone," Petra said. "I don't know anything about knots."

"Mm," said the priest. "And the charm I bought from a man near the fish market — a fever-charm, red thread, salt inside — that worked too. And the birth-knot a midwife showed me, worked. And the little salt-charm from a girl who wouldn't give her name, worked so well the child's cough went out like a pinched wick." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were tired and blue and entirely without menace, which frightened her more than menace would have. "You have a very distinctive knot-work, whoever you are. My mother was a fisherman's wife. I know a net-mender's hand when I see one."

"Then you know I mend nets," Petra said. "Move along, father. I've candles to sell."

"You're not selling any," he observed. "Your basket's still full. You've been standing there since I sat down, working up the nerve either to flee or to lie to me again, and I thought I'd save us both the time." He gathered the three knots and held them out to her across the counter, an offering, or a trap with its teeth showing. "My name is Anselm. I was rector of Saint Vered's, before Saint Vered's was closed. You may have heard of me. There was a scandal."

"I don't follow scandals."

"No," he said. "You follow none of it, do you? No temples, no tavern gossip, no parish registers. You live like a woman standing in a doorway — half in, half out, ready to run whichever way the wind goes." He set the knots down on the counter between them, deliberately, one by one. Click, click, click. "I'm not here to arrest you, child. I don't have the authority to arrest anyone. I barely have the authority to bless anyone, and I've been threatened with prison for trying."

"Then what are you here for?"

Anselm looked at his own hands for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice under the priest's voice.

"To ask you to teach me what you're ashamed of."

* * *

She did not answer him. She walked away, which she counted as a victory, and spent the afternoon telling herself the encounter was finished.

It was not finished. That evening, coming home through the Tanner's Walk with her hood up and her heart doing its old practiced rhythm — *no one sees you, no one sees you* — she found him again. He was standing at the mouth of her alley with a lantern, not hiding, not lurking, simply waiting the way weather waits, and when he saw her he did not step forward. He just lifted the lantern slightly, so she could see his face, so she could see he meant nothing by it.

"You followed me," she said.

"I followed your charms," Anselm said. "There's a difference. A working charm leaves a — I don't know what to call it. A warmth. Like walking past a house where bread is baking. I've felt it three times this week, always within a street of wherever you'd been. I used to feel it in churches, when I was young. Before the silence." He lowered the lantern. "It's been twenty years since I felt it anywhere. Then this week, suddenly, it's everywhere you walk."

"Coincidence."

"Three days ago every bell in this city rang itself at dawn," Anselm said. "I stood in my room with my hand on my door handle and wept like a boy, and I am fifty-eight years old and I have not wept since my ordination. Coincidence has stopped being an honest word, mistress. Whatever your name is."

"Petra," she said, before she could stop herself, and hated the small traitor tongue that said it.

"Petra." He tried the weight of it. "Will you hear an old man out? Five minutes. If you want me gone after, I'll go, and I'll never find you again — I couldn't, truly, I only ever had the warmth to follow, and if you stop working, the warmth stops too."

Every sensible thing in her said *run*. She had five years of sensible things stacked up like sandbags: the false name, the false aunt, the careful prices, the customers who pretended not to know what they bought so she could pretend they didn't buy it. Five years of building a life designed to be abandoned at a moment's notice, and here was a defrocked priest standing in the rain asking her to unbuild it.

But he had wept at the bell. That was the thing that undid her. Inquisitors heard the bell and posted edicts. Crowds heard the bell and knelt, hungry, wanting. This man had heard it and wept — not for what it might get him, but for what it might mean.

"Five minutes," she said. "Not in the alley. There's a shrine of Saint Osla behind the chandlery. It's roofed."

"It's a shrine," Anselm said carefully. "Shrines are being watched."

"Not that one. Nobody's prayed at Saint Osla in thirty years. Even the inquisitors forgot it exists." She almost smiled. "That's why I chose it."

* * *

Saint Osla's shrine was a stone alcove no bigger than a confessional, tucked between a chandlery and a cooperage, holding a weather-worn figure of a saint whose attributes — a wheel? a loaf? a key? — had eroded into suggestion. Someone had left a stub of candle there years ago. The roof leaked in one corner, patiently, onto the saint's head.

Petra lit two of her own candles, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and stood with her back to the alcove wall where she could see the alley mouth.

"Talk," she said.

Anselm ducked under the low lintel and filled the little shrine the way a large stone fills a stream — the space rearranged itself around him. He looked at the saint for a moment. "Osla. Keeper of thresholds and lost keys. Funny choice."

"It wasn't a choice. It was the closest roof."

"Of course." He settled himself against the opposite wall, which put them nearly knee to knee in the cramped space, close enough that she could smell wet wool and candle smoke and, underneath, something older — incense, maybe, soaked so deep into him it would come out in his grave. "Twenty-two years ago I was ordained. Twenty years ago I was given Saint Vered's, in the Weavers' Quarter. A good parish. Poor, but good. And from my first Sunday I noticed the thing everyone noticed and no one said aloud: the gods didn't answer."

"They hadn't answered in years by then," Petra said. "Everyone knows that. The Silence."

"Everyone knows it happened," Anselm said. "No one agrees on when, or why, or whether it ended. The Crown says the gods withdrew as punishment for our sins. The philosophers say they never existed and the Silence is just honesty arriving late. The temples say—" he made a small, bitter gesture "—the temples say whatever keeps the tithes coming. But I stood at that altar for eighteen years, Petra, saying the words, and I will tell you what I learned: the words still work. The liturgy still has weight. It's like shouting down a well that's been capped. You can hear your own voice come back wrong, and you can tell something is on the other side of the cap, listening, unable to answer." His jaw tightened. "Or unwilling."

"So you preached to empty gods. That's the scandal?"

"That's what the tribunal said." He was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the shrine roof. Somewhere down the alley a dog barked twice and gave up. "What I actually preached, in my last year, was that the gods had not fallen silent. That they had *left*. Deliberately. That the Silence wasn't a punishment or a mystery but a departure, and that we were singing to an empty house, and that it was cruelty to keep telling poor people their prayers were being received when they were piling up against a locked door." He looked at her. "They took my parish for despair, Petra. Not heresy against doctrine — doctrine they'd have argued. Despair. Telling the flock the truth as I saw it. They defrocked me for *despairing out loud*."

"And now the bells ring," Petra said softly.

"And now the bells ring." Something moved through his face — hope, and terror wearing hope's clothes. "And prayers are being answered. Badly. Cruelly. Have you heard about the widow on Bellows Street?"

"I've heard things."

"A woman prayed for her drowned husband back, and got him. He walked out of the canal on the second night, dripping, and he knew her name and their wedding day and nothing else, and he stands in the kitchen staring at the wall, and when she touches him he is cold as canal water, and he does not eat, and he does not sleep, and he will not die." Anselm's voice shook. "She came to me — to what's left of me — yesterday, because the priests won't touch it and the inquisitors want to burn her. She asked me: *did God do this, or did something else?* And I have spent twenty years telling people the house was empty, and now something is answering, and it is answering like—" he searched for it "—like a man waking from a long illness, deaf and blind and grabbing at whatever moves. Loud. Indiscriminate. *Desperate.*"

Petra's skin prickled. Her mistress's voice rose in her memory, unbidden: *a god clearing its throat.* Maren had known. Maren had seen this coming — had been building something against it, the night Petra ran.

"Why me?" she asked, to push the memory down. "If you want answers about answered prayers, why chase a charm-seller? Go to the widow. Go to the bells."

"Because the widow's miracle is a wound," Anselm said. "And yours is a craft. Whoever answered her husband did it raw, without skill, the way a child smears paint. But your charms — I've examined six of them now, I'll confess that much — they're *shaped*. Measured. Salt measured to the need, thread chosen for the ailment, knots counted. Someone taught you to aim. And if prayers are being answered again, even badly, then craft matters again, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I have spent my whole life on the receiving end of faith. Words spoken toward heaven. And now heaven is speaking back, and I find I understand nothing about how any of it works. The theology books are silent. The seminary trained me for a conversation that has resumed without me." He spread his big hands. "Teach me, Petra. Not to work charms — I'd be hopeless, my hands are for books. Teach me what it *is*. What moves through a knot of thread and salt. What I've been talking *to*, all those years, and what's talking *back* now. You know things the Church burned the records of. Hedge-craft. The old ways. The things that kept working after the altars went quiet."

"You realize what you're asking," Petra said. "What I do is outlawed. What you're asking me to teach you is outlawed. If the inquisitors find us—"

"If the inquisitors find us," Anselm said, "they'll burn you for witchcraft and me for relapse, and honestly, child, at fifty-eight, with no parish and no god and no use, I find the arithmetic less frightening than you do." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "Besides. You're assuming I might turn you in otherwise. I watched you decide whether to believe that, all afternoon. Let me save you the worry: I reported a woman once — a hedge-worker, when I was young and orthodox and certain. She was taken. I told myself it was law." His smile faded. "I have regretted it for forty years. I will not add you to the account."

The rain thickened against the shrine roof. Petra looked at the saint with the eroded face — threshold-keeper, lost-keys — and felt the careful life shifting under her like ice on a pond.

Because here was the truth she couldn't say: she wanted this. She had wanted it for five years and hated herself for wanting it. Someone to talk to about the work. Someone who knew the names of things. Someone who might, someday, be able to tell her what Maren had been building that night — because a priest would know the old lore, the spire-lore, the door-lore, the things Maren had muttered over her half-written pages while Petra eavesdropped with her heart hammering.

And here was the other truth: she didn't trust him. Trust was a currency she'd stopped carrying. A priest who'd reported one witch could report another. A man who wept at bells might kneel to edicts tomorrow. And if he was following her warmth, then others could learn to follow it too — others with worse intentions and better funding.

"Conditions," she said.

Anselm raised his eyebrows.

"One: you never say my true name aloud where walls could hear. Two: you don't preach about me, pray about me, or confess about me — I don't care what your rules say, you keep me out of your mouth. Three—" she hesitated, then committed, because there was no taking it back anyway "—three: you tell me everything the Church knows about the Spire."

The change in him was immediate and total. The tired priest vanished; something sharp and scholarly leaned forward in his face like a lamp being turned up. "Why do you know that word?"

"Why do *you*?"

"Because I've spent twenty years reading confiscated archives," Anselm said slowly, "and every map that shows it has been burned, and every manuscript that describes it ends mid-sentence, torn out, and the one thing every fragment agrees on is that it predates the kingdom — that the kingdom was built *around* it, the way a body grows around a splinter." He stared at her. "And you, a charm-seller who claims to mend nets, say the word like a woman checking a bruise."

Outside, far off across the city, a temple bell rang. One note only — not the self-ringing dawn peal, but a single struck note, the kind rung for a death, or an alarm. Both of them went still. In the silence after, Petra heard the sound underneath the sound, faint as a pulse through a wall: a hum, low and vast, coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground, from the stones, from the space between her ribs.

Anselm heard it too. She watched him hear it. His lips moved around a word he didn't say.

"Tomorrow night," Petra said. "The chandlery closes at dusk. Come to the shrine after. Bring nothing written." She picked up her candle basket, steadying herself with its familiar weight. "And Father — if this is a trap, if there are inquisitors behind any door you lead me to, I promise you the last thing you'll ever teach anyone is what a hedge-witch does to a man who breaks her trust."

"I believe you," Anselm said, and to her considerable alarm, he sounded delighted. "You know, that's the first honest threat I've received in twenty years. It's like hearing the liturgy in the old tongue." He ducked out under the lintel into the rain, then paused, half-turned, water running off his broken tonsure. "Petra. The hum. You heard it before today, haven't you. You've been hearing it for days."

She didn't answer.

"That's how I finally found you," he said gently. "Not the charms. The charms led me to Cooper's Lane. But three nights ago I woke at midnight and heard the whole city humming one note, and I walked toward it until dawn, and the note got louder and louder, and it led me to a market stall full of candles that nobody was buying." He smiled that tired crooked smile. "Whatever you're ashamed of, child — it isn't the knots."

He went out into the rain, and Petra stood alone in the forgotten shrine with two guttering candles and the hum rising through the soles of her feet like water filling a well, and understood that the careful life had cracked clean through, and that the sound she could hear underneath the city was going to get louder every day, and that sooner or later everyone in Greyhollow would be following it — the faithful, the inquisitors, the desperate — straight to whatever door it was opening.

She snuffed the candles. Behind her, in the dark, the faceless saint seemed to hold out an eroded hand.

*Come in,* said the shape of it. *Or lock the door behind you. Those are the only two things a threshold is for.*

Petra pulled her hood up and went home the long way, counting turns, losing herself on purpose, and all the while the hum sang under the streets like a second heartbeat, patient, enormous, and hers now — whether she wanted it or not — to answer.
