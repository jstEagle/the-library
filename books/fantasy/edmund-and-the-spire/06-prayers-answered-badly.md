## 6. Prayers Answered Badly

The widow's name was Aldith Crane, and she had prayed every night for eleven years in the same flat, worn voice she used for counting eggs.

She was not a wicked woman. She was not even, by the measure of Greyhollow's back lanes, an unusual one. She had buried a husband and two of her four children, and she had done what everyone did with grief too large for the house: she had carried it to the old gods' empty altars and left it there like coins on a well-bottom. She knew they were empty. Everyone knew. The priests said so from their pulpits, the edicts said so in their red wax seals, and the silence itself said so, that great generation-long nothing that had settled over the country like snow over a field.

So when Aldith knelt at her bedside on the third night after the bells rang themselves — when she pressed her cracked knuckles together and said, as she always said, *bring him back to me, bring Tam back to me, I will give anything* — she said it the way you hum a song you no longer believe in. Habit. The shape of a prayer without its heart.

The gods were not empty anymore. That was the trouble. The gods were dying, and dying things do not hear the way living things hear.

Aldith woke in the dark to the sound of water dripping on her floorboards.

It came from the doorway of her bedroom, slow and patient, drop after drop, in the rhythm of a thing that had been dripping a long time and would go on dripping longer still. She lay very still and told herself it was rain through the thatch. It had not rained in three weeks. She told herself it was the roof melting frost. There was no frost.

*Tam?* she said, because eleven years of habit is stronger than fear, and the word came out of her before she could stop it, small and hopeful and already, somewhere beneath the hope, knowing.

He stood in the doorway. He was wearing the coat he'd drowned in, the good grey wool with the horn buttons, and it hung on him wrong now, heavy and dark with river-water that ran off him in ropes, pooling around his boots, spreading toward her bed in a thin black tongue. His face was Tam's face. His eyes were Tam's eyes, except that they were full of water, actual water, standing in them like two cups filled to the brim, trembling but never spilling, and behind the water something looked out at her that was trying very hard to remember how to be a man.

*Aldith,* he said, and his voice was the sound a river makes under ice. *You called me.*

*I did,* she whispered. She had backed against the headboard. Her hands were pressed over her mouth. *Oh, love. Oh, love, I didn't mean—*

*You said anything.* He took a step, and the floor groaned with the weight of the water in him, though he had been a lean man, a light man, a man who danced at weddings. *I held my breath the whole way, Aldith. Eleven years. You can't hold your breath eleven years and come back right.*

She should have screamed. Later, when the neighbors found her sitting in the flooded room holding his cold hand, she would try to explain why she hadn't: because it *was* him, some part of him, the part that had heard her, and he sounded so tired. So terribly tired. Like a man who has walked a long way home only to find the house is not his anymore.

*What do you want?* she asked him. *What do you need, Tam?*

And the drowned man smiled with his wife's name still pooled in his cheeks, and said:

*More.*

* * *

Four miles east of Greyhollow, on a farm that had belonged to the Hedgely family for six generations, Osric Hedgely knelt in his own field and wept, and the field wept with him, or rather it answered him, which was worse.

Osric was not a praying man either, but this year had broken him in the ordinary agricultural way: blight on the barley, a lame ox, a daughter married off to a family two provinces away who might as well have been two kingdoms away, since no letters came back anymore from anywhere. On the morning after the bells, standing in his bare dirt field with his hands black to the wrist, Osric had shouted at the sky — not prayed, shouted — *give me back my harvest, damn you, give me back what I planted, I'll pay any price you name.*

The field took him at his word.

By noon the barley was up. By evening it was taller than a man. Osric walked the rows laughing and crying and touching the green gold stalks, and it was only when he reached the far corner, where the soil had always been poorest, that he stopped laughing.

They grew in rows, neat as anything he had ever drilled himself. Pale. Curved. Each one seated in its little hillock of turned earth like a seed set by a careful hand. They gleamed in the last light with the wet shine of things newly born, and each one was a tooth — human teeth, molar teeth, big square teeth with roots like white legs — and there were thousands of them, tens of thousands, whole acres of them, and as the sun went down they began, very softly, all together, to grind.

Not gnashing. Grinding. The sound of a millstone. The sound of something enormous chewing slowly, patiently, on nothing at all, waiting to be given something to chew.

Osric did not sleep that night. In the morning the teeth had grown a second row. He went door to door in the village, hoarse, half-mad, saying *the field, look at the field,* and the villagers looked, and crossed themselves in the old way even though the old way had been outlawed for a generation, and nobody would walk past the Hedgely land anymore, and the dogs howled at it from a mile off.

On the third day a tax assessor came from the city, saw the crop, and rode straight back to report it. On the fourth day men in grey coats with iron collars came and burned the field, and the teeth screamed while they burned, high and thin, ten thousand tiny voices, and Osric Hedgely sat on his fence rail and watched, and afterward he could not say whether the screaming had been relief or rage.

The ash blew west, toward Greyhollow. It tasted faintly of milk.

* * *

Petra heard about both of these things within a day of each other, and neither story frightened her as much as the way people told them.

That was the change she kept circling, unable to land on it. Greyhollow had always traded in rumor the way it traded in salt — steadily, in small measured amounts, with a fair price for quality. But rumor used to be currency. Now it was blood in the water. People spoke of the widow Crane's drowned husband in the fish market in voices pitched just below the level where the wrong ears might catch them, and their eyes went bright, and their hands shook, and underneath the horror — Petra could see it, plain as a coin at the bottom of a clear stream — underneath the horror was *wanting.*

A woman buying fever-charms from her on Tuesday gripped her wrist across the stall and said, low, urgent: *Is it true about the Crane woman? Is it true they come back if you ask?* And Petra said the safe thing, the practiced thing — *stories grow in the telling, mistress* — but the woman wouldn't let go. Her fingers were hot. Her breath smelled of cheap wine drunk in daylight. *My son,* she said. *My son went down with the ferry at Marlow Ford. If there's a way. If there's a price. Tell me the price.*

*There's no way,* Petra said, and pried the woman's fingers loose, gently, the way you'd calm an animal. *There's no price. What came back to Widow Crane isn't her husband. It's the river wearing her husband.*

But the woman only looked at her with those bright shaking eyes, and Petra understood, with a lurch, that the woman had heard every word and believed none of them, because wanting does not believe anything; wanting only reaches.

That night she climbed to her attic room and barred the door and sat on her narrow bed with her knees drawn up, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing for five days.

The bells had rung themselves. Charms were working — hers had worked, a child's fever-charm, five years of dead craft suddenly alive in her hands like a fish pulled gasping from a net. Prayers were being answered. And the answers were *wrong.* Not subtly wrong. Wrong the way a broken leg is wrong, wrong in the bones of the thing. A husband returned as drowning. A harvest granted as teeth. Every answer too literal, too greedy, too *hungry* — as if whatever listened had caught only the shape of the words and none of the meaning, or as if it understood the meaning perfectly and simply did not care, or —

Or as if it were weak. As if it were straining. As if it were a man going deaf catching fragments of a shout across a valley and guessing at the rest, and guessing badly, and being too proud — or too desperate — to ask twice.

Maren had told her once, in the early days, back when Petra still sat at the hedge-witch's feet and drank tea made of things she couldn't identify and loved her the way you love a teacher, helplessly and a little resentfully. Maren had said: *The old gods aren't like the new ones, girl. The new god is a king on a throne; you petition him. The old ones are like old farmers. They're half-deaf and they're stubborn and they'll give you exactly what you ask for and nothing you meant. You don't pray to them. You bargain with them. And you count your fingers after.*

Petra had laughed then. She was not laughing now.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not avoid, sitting alone in her attic with the barred door and the dark coming down: the gods had been silent for a generation, and silence from gods means one of two things. Either they are gone. Or they are *dying* — and a dying god, like a dying man, spends its last strength shouting. Loudly. Indiscriminately. Grasping at any voice that calls, answering any prayer it can half-hear, because soon it will not be able to answer at all, and it knows, the way the dying always know, that the window is closing.

*Loud, indiscriminate, desperate.* That was the phrase that formed itself in her mind, unwelcome, precise. The gods were not returning. The gods were calling out — flailing — answering everything at once because soon they would answer nothing forever. And every answered prayer, every bell rung by no hand, every working charm in every hedge-witch's basket across the country was not a miracle.

It was a death-rattle. And the whole country was leaning toward it, hungry, reaching, asking for more.

Petra put her face in her hands. Through the floor she could hear her landlady moving below, and the landlady's granddaughter practicing scales on a cracked fiddle, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary street in an ordinary city, and it struck her with physical force — a fist under the ribs — that all of it was balanced on top of something, the way a plate balances on a stick, and the stick was snapping, and nobody downstairs had heard it yet.

*It's not a blessing,* she said aloud to the empty room, testing the words, hating them. *It's not a return. It's a last breath. And we're all standing downwind of it, breathing it in and calling it mercy.*

The child's charm came back to her then — the fever-charm that had worked, actually worked, the knot tightening warm in her hand when little Wren's mother brought the girl back to her, cured, round-eyed, alive. Petra had told herself it was chance. Fever breaks. Knots are knots. Five years of craft and one coincidence.

But she had felt the warmth. She had felt something move *through* her, quick and vast and frightened, like a bird striking a window — something that had been flying a long time in the dark and had seen, far off, one small lit thing, and had thrown itself at it with everything it had left.

It had used her. Or it had begged her. With the gods, Maren might have said, it comes to the same thing.

Petra got up and lit her one candle and dug to the bottom of her chest, past the spare charms and the folded cloths, until her fingers found the shape she never touched: the spellbook, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with three knots she renewed every season and had never once untied. Five years. Five years she had carried it from town to town like a stone in her belly and never opened it, because opening it meant deciding what she believed about the night she'd run, and she had built her whole careful life on not deciding.

She sat with it in her lap for a long time. The candle guttered. Downstairs the fiddle stumbled through its scale and began again.

Somewhere out in the dark, she knew, more prayers were being answered. Somewhere a sick man was waking healthy and hollow-eyed. Somewhere a barren field was swelling with a crop that would make the Hedgely teeth look gentle. Somewhere someone was getting exactly what they asked for, and nothing they meant, and the giving would not stop, could not stop, because the giver was dying and did not know how to do anything anymore but give — wildly, wrongly, spending its last strength on a country full of people who had forgotten how to ask carefully.

And sooner or later — she could feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar — sooner or later one of those answers was going to point at her. At the witch in Greyhollow whose charms had started working. At the runaway with the stolen book. The desperate faithful would find her, or the inquisitors would, and both of them would want the same thing: *tell us how it works, tell us the price, tell us the way.*

She needed to know what she was carrying before anyone else decided it for her.

Her thumb moved to the first knot.

Then she stopped — because below her window, out on the night street, someone had begun to scream. Not in pain. In joy. A man's voice, ragged with weeping, shouting the same words over and over to whoever would listen: *It heard me! It heard me! I asked and it heard!*

Petra closed her eyes. Around her, faint as a pulse under skin, she could feel it again — that vast frightened presence brushing past, spending itself, answering, answering, answering, a dying giant throwing gifts at a crowd it could no longer see clearly.

*Quietly,* she whispered to it, absurdly, uselessly, the way you'd shush a wounded animal. *Please. Quietly. You'll draw the wolves.*

The presence passed on. The man below kept shouting. And Petra Underhill, keeper of a small careful life, sat in her attic with a stolen book in her lap and understood at last that the life was over — that the flood had already reached the floorboards, that the teeth were already in the ground, and that the only question left was not whether the shouting would stop, but what would be left standing when it did.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would buy dragon-scale from the girl at the tannery, the one the charm-sellers whispered about, the one who kept the old fire. If the world was ending loudly, she wanted to know what the quiet keepers knew.

She untied the first knot, and did not yet open the cover, and slept — or something near it — with her hand resting on the book like a hand on a sleeping dog that might, at any moment, wake.
