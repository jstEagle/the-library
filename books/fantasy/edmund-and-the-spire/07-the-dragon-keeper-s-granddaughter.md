## 7. The Dragon-Keeper's Granddaughter

The tannery district smelled the way it always had — of lime and rot and rendered fat, a stink that got into wool and would not wash out — but on the morning Petra went looking for dragon-scale, it smelled of something else beneath all that. Something hot. Something old.

She had told herself she wasn't going. She'd spent two days telling herself that, in fact, since Father Anselm had stood in her doorway with rain dripping off his defrocked collar and said, *You'll need scale if you're going to work anything worth working, and there's only one place left in Greyhollow that has it.* Two days of telling herself that buying dragon-scale was exactly the kind of thing an unlicensed witch should not do while inquisitors walked the market with lists in their hands. Two days of the careful life holding its shape around her like a jar with a cracked lid.

And then, on the third morning, she had woken to find every fever-charm in her stockbox humming. Not ringing. Humming — a low, warm note, like a kettle deciding whether to sing. She had laid them out on the table and watched them vibrate in their rows, forty small knots of red thread and salt and bone, all leaning slightly east, all leaning toward the tannery district, like iron filings finding a lodestone they'd been promised for years.

So she went. She wrapped three luck-knots in wax cloth, tucked Maren's spellbook — still unopened, still unread, five years of guilt with a locked cover — into the false bottom of her basket, and walked east through streets where people knelt at doorways and wouldn't meet each other's eyes.

* * *

The tannery yard was half-deserted. The Crown had taxed the pits into idleness years ago; most of the vats stood open to the sky, full of grey rainwater and greyer scum, and the long drying-frames were bare except for a few hides stiffening like flags of surrender. A dog with ribs like a harp watched her cross the yard without lifting its head.

The heat was strongest near the eastern wall, where a squat stone building squatted against the tannery's flank like a barnacle — low-roofed, windowless except for one round vent near the eaves, from which a thread of smoke rose so thin and so steady it looked drawn with a compass. No smoke in Greyhollow rose like that anymore. Every chimney in the city coughed and gusted and spat. This smoke went up straight as a plumb line, as if the air itself had agreed to hold still for it.

There was no sign on the door. There was a horseshoe nailed above it, though — hung points-down, which any hedge-witch knew was not for luck but for *keeping*, for holding a thing inside a threshold rather than letting it out. And under the horseshoe, chalked directly onto the wood in a hand so small and precise it might have been done with a pin:

**NO PRAYERS. NO PREACHERS. NO CROWN.**

Petra knocked anyway.

The door opened before her knuckles landed the third time, and a girl looked out at her — sixteen, maybe seventeen, with ash-brown hair cut off blunt at the jaw as if scissors had been involved but patience hadn't, and eyes the color of banked coals, deep brown shot through with a reddish gleam that Petra chose, firmly, to believe was firelight. Her forearms were bare to the elbow and black to the shoulder with soot, and she held a fire-iron in one hand the way a soldier holds a spear: not brandished, just present.

"You're the charm-seller," the girl said. "From Wednesday market."

"I'm—"

"The one whose charms started working." The girl's coal-colored eyes narrowed. "Half the city's talking about you. The other half is praying you get arrested before their neighbors find out what they bought." She looked Petra up and down, taking in the plain grey cloak, the basket, the careful nothing-to-see-here face. "You shouldn't have come here."

"Probably not," Petra said. "I need dragon-scale."

Something moved behind the girl's expression — quick, involuntary, gone again, like a fish turning under ice. "We don't sell scale."

"Everyone says the Underdown girl sells scale."

"Everyone says a lot of things." But she didn't close the door, and her gaze flicked — once, fast — toward the round vent and the straight thread of smoke, and back. "Scale's grave-goods. You don't sell grave-goods. You tend them until the grave is ready, and then you bury them proper, and then you don't talk about it."

"That," Petra said quietly, "is the first honest sentence anyone's said to me in a week."

The girl studied her a moment longer. Then she stepped back from the doorway — not a welcome, exactly. More like a decision she already resented. "You'd better come in before someone sees you standing there. Inquisitor Vane posted new edicts this morning. Third one's about harboring." She jerked her chin at the chalked words over her own door. "I wrote that yesterday. It's already out of date."

* * *

Inside was hotter than Petra expected and darker than it should have been, given the size of the room. The space was long and low, ceilinged in blackened beams, and down its whole length ran a trench of coals — not a hearth, not a forge, but a *bed*, a sleeping shape of embers banked and raked and tended into something between a fire and a nest. The heat came off it in slow waves, and the light came up from below, so that everything in the room — the hanging tools, the stacked split logs, the girl herself — seemed lit from underneath, like figures in a bad dream, or saints in an old fresco.

And in the far end of the coal-bed, filling it, was the dragon.

Petra had seen dragons before. Everyone over thirty had — distant, high, crossing the sky in ones and twos during the last years of the old reign, wings like torn sails, beautiful the way storms are beautiful. She had never seen one *close*. She had certainly never seen one dying.

It lay along the trench with its body half-buried in the coals, and it was vast even diminished — thirty feet of it, maybe more, folded into a space built for twenty — and it was *thin*. That was the horror of it. Scales that must once have shone bronze-and-green now hung loose as a coat on a starving man, dulled to the color of old pewter, and between them the skin showed dry and papery, stretched over a frame of ridged bone. Its wings lay along its flanks like folded funeral banners. One eye was open. It was the color of a coal's heart, and it did not look at Petra so much as *through* her, with the terrible unhurried attention of something that has stopped bothering with hurry because hurry is for creatures with futures.

"Well," Petra said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "That's a dragon."

"It's Edmund," the girl said, as if correcting a mispronunciation. "He's four hundred and eleven years old. He's been dying since before I was born, and he'll be dying long after I'm dead, probably, because he's stubborn." She set the fire-iron in its bracket and wiped her hands on her thighs, leaving them no cleaner. "Sef Underdown. You wanted scale. Say what it's for or get out."

"What's your name?"

"Sef. I said it."

"You said it like throwing something away." Petra kept her voice level, her eyes moving over the dragon — the rise and fall of its sides, slow as a tide, the way the coals around it glowed brighter where its body touched them and dimmer where it didn't, as if the fire itself were keeping time with its breath. "My name is Petra. I sell charms I'm not supposed to sell, and as of nine days ago they work, and I don't know why, and it frightens me. I came here because my charms are pointing at your cellar like compass needles, and I'd very much like to know why before the inquisitors figure it out too."

Sef's jaw tightened. For a moment Petra thought she'd be thrown out. Then the girl turned away, picked up a rake with a handle worn smooth and pale by generations of hands, and began working the edge of the coal-bed with short, practiced strokes — turning the embers, feeding the edges, the motions so automatic they were almost sleepwalking.

"My grandmother kept this pit," Sef said, to the fire more than to Petra. "Her mother kept it before her. Back when there were twelve dragons in Greyhollow and the keepers' guild had a hall and a charter and a seat at the winter council. They tended the fires and the dragons slept in them — that's the whole mystery, right? Dragons don't sleep in caves. They sleep in *fire*. Coals banked right, fed right, raked right, and a dragon can lie in them for a hundred years and come out in spring fat and shining." The rake paused. "There were rites. Feeding-times. Words you said over the coals at dusk and dawn. Songs, if you believe the songs, and I do, because I've sung them and he sleeps better. My grandmother taught me all of it before I could read. Made me say it back word-perfect every night, kneeling on that stones there, cold knees, till I dreamed it." She raked another stroke. "She died when I was eleven. Mother took over. Mother lasted six years — the fumes, mostly, and the nights, and the grief, because you can't tend a dying thing for six years without it getting into you. She's buried behind the wall there, under the rowan. So now it's me. Keeper of the last fire-pit in Greyhollow. Tenders' guild of one." She finally looked at Petra, and her coal-bright eyes were hard and wet at the same time, which Petra thought might be the truest face she'd ever seen. "I know every rite by heart. Every feeding-time, every dusk-word, every dawn-word. I hate every single one of them. Is that enough honesty for you, charm-seller?"

"Yes," Petra said softly. Because she understood it — understood it down in the marrow — the way a cage understands a cage. *Two keepers of dying things,* she thought. *She keeps a dragon. I keep a lie.*

* * *

They sat on either side of the coal-trench after that, because there were stools there, ancient and soot-blackened, set at the exact distance a keeper sits from her fire, and Sef poured them both tea from a kettle that hung permanently in the heat, tasting of smoke and something mineral, like drinking the inside of a mountain.

Edmund watched them. Petra could feel it — not threatening, just *attending*, the way the very old attend the very young, with time to spare and opinions banked.

"Why does he stay?" Petra asked. "If he's dying — the old stories say dragons go to the mountains at the end. Go up high and let go."

"The mountains are gone." Sef said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report weather. "Or forgotten. Same thing, maybe — you've heard what's happening out past the walls. Villages losing their names. Roads that stop. Grandmother said the world's been thinning for a generation, thin as broth, and the dragons felt it first because they're old enough to remember when it was thick." She sipped her tea. "He stays because the fire-pit is the last warm thing he knows, and because—" she hesitated, and for the first time the hardness cracked all the way through, "—because I asked him to. When Mother died. I was seventeen and I was alone in this cellar with a dying dragon and I said, *please don't leave me too.* Out loud. Like a prayer." Her mouth twisted. "Which is funny, isn't it? Half the city's praying themselves stupid these days and getting miracles, and my one prayer in my whole life gets answered by — this. By him staying. Dying slower, so I won't be alone." She stared into the coals. "That's the cruelest answered prayer I ever heard of. And I've heard the widow's story. Everybody's heard the widow's story."

Petra's hands had gone cold around the hot cup. *Prayers answered badly.* Anselm had said it too — the gods coming back like drowning men grabbing at swimmers. But sitting here, in the heat, watching a creature spend its last strength on a lonely girl's loneliness, Petra thought: *maybe some of the answers aren't cruel. Maybe some are just desperate. Maybe the gods aren't mocking us. Maybe they're holding on the way we are.*

"Your charms point here," Sef said abruptly. "You said that. Why?"

"I don't know."

"Guess."

Petra set down her cup. Below them, Edmund's eye had closed — or nearly; a slit of coal-light remained — and his breathing had slowed further, and the coals along his flank pulsed faintly, gently, in time. Like a heart. Like a second heart the fire had grown around him.

"He's not just dying," Petra said slowly, feeling her way along the thought like a hand along a wall in the dark. "A dying thing pulls. Everything living leans toward warmth at the end — I've seen it in sickrooms, in animals. But this is different. He's not pulling warmth *in*. He's—" she searched for it, "—he's *broadcasting*. Like a bell that's been struck and won't finish ringing. My charms are salt and bone and thread, and they're dumb as stones, they don't feel anything — unless something is *loud*. Whatever he's doing, it's loud. It's the loudest quiet thing in this city."

Sef was staring at her. "Grandmother said a dragon's death-fire doesn't go out. It goes *somewhere*. She said that's what the spire was for, the old spire, the one that's not on any map anymore — that the dragons gave their last fire to it, every one, all down the centuries, and that's why the kingdom stood. Fire for the door. Door for the fire." She laughed, short and bitter. "Keeper's nonsense. Fireside stories. Except now the bells ring themselves and prayers come true wrong and your dumb salt-charms are pointing at my cellar like dowsing rods, so maybe the fireside stories were the only true things anybody ever told."

The word landed in Petra like a dropped stone: *spire.*

Anselm had said it too, that night in her doorway, half-drunk on cheap wine and older griefs — *there's a tower, witch, older than the kingdom, and every map of it has been burned, and I think that's where all this starts and ends.* She had filed it away with the rest of the impossible things. Now it sat in a tannery cellar, breathing in coals.

"How much scale do you want?" Sef said suddenly.

Petra blinked. "You said you don't sell—"

"I said we don't sell grave-goods. We don't. But shed scale isn't grave-goods — he sheds, same as a snake, same as anything with scales, little bits every year, and I sweep them and I keep them because throwing them away feels like—" she stopped, jaw working, "—like throwing him away early. There's a box. A shoebox's worth, gathered over ten years. If you're doing whatever it is you're doing — if your charms work, if you're part of whatever's waking up — then take it. Take it and *do something*." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, visibly, furiously, hating her own crack the way she hated the rites. "Because I can keep a fire alive. That's all I know how to do. Keep a fire alive in a country that's forgetting how to be real. And I am so tired, charm-seller. I'm nineteen years old and I am so tired of being the only one who knows the words."

Petra reached across the coal-trench and put her hand over Sef's soot-black one. The girl didn't pull away. Under the grime her skin was furnace-warm.

"I'll do something," Petra said. "I don't know what yet. But I'll do something."

Below them, in the banked heart of the last fire-pit in Greyhollow, the old dragon opened his eye — fully, this time, both eyes, twin coals kindling in the dark — and looked at Petra Underhill for a long, long moment.

And when he spoke, his voice was the sound of a landslide remembering it used to be a mountain, and the first word he said was her mistress's name.
