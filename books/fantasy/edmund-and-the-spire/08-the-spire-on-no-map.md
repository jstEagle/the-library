## 8. The Spire on No Map

Father Anselm had spent eleven years learning how to be invisible in a city that had never once looked at him, and he had gotten rather good at it. He knew which gutters to walk beside so the smell of the tanneries would mask the smell of the incense that still clung, faintly and stubbornly, to his one remaining cassock. He knew which shrines had been stripped of their saints and which had merely been left to rot, and he knew that a defrocked priest standing too long before an empty alcove drew fewer eyes than a man loitering outside a bakery. Grief was permitted. Devotion was not.

So when he wanted to think — truly think, in the old way, with his whole mind laid open like a book on a lectern — he walked.

He walked now, in the hour after compline, when Greyhollow's lamps burned low and greasy and the fog came up off the canal with the smell of iron and old fish. Behind him, in the rented room above the chandler's shop where he'd been sleeping for three years, lay everything he owned: two books, a razor, a change of linen, and a map.

The map was the reason he was walking instead of thinking comfortably indoors.

It was not a large map. It was drawn on calfskin, cracked along its folds like old bread, and it showed the northern reaches of the kingdom as they had been charted some two hundred years ago, in the reign of a king whose name Anselm had to work to remember because the Crown had done such thorough work forgetting him. In the lower corner, beneath the cartographer's flourish of signature, someone had inked a small black shape: a tower, narrow as a needle, rising out of a valley marked only with a symbol that no living scholar could read.

Anselm could read it. He had spent four years of his life and most of his faith learning to read it.

*The door*, the old texts called it. *The last door.* And every copy of every document that named it had been confiscated or burned within a generation of the kingdom's founding, save the ones hidden by men like Anselm's own teacher, who had died penniless and disgraced and certain, to his final breath, that the tower on the calfskin was real.

Anselm turned down Tanner's Row, past the shuttered stalls, past the great vats where the hides soaked in lime, and stopped at the mouth of the alley behind the scale-seller's stall.

She was there. Of course she was there. He had followed her working charms through half of Greyhollow for six days now, tracking her the way a hound tracks — not her, precisely, but the warmth she left behind her, the small bright residue of magic that clung to doorposts and lintels and the wrists of sick children. The hedge-witch Petra Underhill left a trail through this city like a snail leaves silver, and tonight the trail ended here, at the cellar door of the dragon-keeper's granddaughter, and Anselm intended to knock.

He had a speech prepared. He had rehearsed it twice against the chandler's window glass. It began, *I know what you are*, and it went downhill from there, and he suspected — correctly, as it happened — that he would abandon it entirely the moment she opened the door.

He knocked.

The door opened perhaps a hand's width. One eye, dark and wary, appeared in the gap. "We're closed."

"Madam, I'm not here to buy." He held up both hands, palms out, the way you'd calm a spooked horse. "I'm here because I need to show you something, and I need you to believe me when I tell you that I have already lost everything worth taking, and so have you, if what I suspect is true."

The eye did not blink. "That's a terrible opening line."

"It's the honest version," Anselm said. "I had a better one prepared. It was lies all the way down."

A pause. Somewhere behind her, deep under the street, something breathed — a long, slow, labored sound, like bellows worked by a tired man. The girl's eye flicked toward the sound and back.

"You're the priest," she said at last. "The one who preached to empty churches until they took his collar."

"I preached to full churches," Anselm said. "They were empty of gods. There's a difference, though I grant it took me ten years to see it."

Another silence. Then the door opened wider, and Petra Underhill stepped out into the alley, pulling her shawl tight around her shoulders, and looked at him the way a deer looks at a hunter who has set down his bow.

"You've been following my charms," she said. Not a question.

"For six days. You salted Mrs. Corren's doorway on Tuesday. You hung a fever-knot over the cooper's daughter's bed on Wednesday. On Thursday you—" He stopped himself. "Yes. I've been following them."

"Why?"

"Because they work." He said it simply, and watched the word land on her like a stone dropped into still water. "Because nothing has worked in this country for twenty years, madam, and then, in the space of a fortnight, your luck-knots are holding, your fever-charms are breaking, and every temple bell in Greyhollow rings itself at dawn. And because I am a man who spent his life studying the machinery of heaven, and I recognize the sound of machinery starting up again after a long disuse. It is not a pleasant sound. It is the sound of a mill whose wheel has seized for decades suddenly turning again — groaning, cracking, shedding rust, grinding whatever is fed into it. Something has woken. And I believe I know what woke it, and I believe I know why, and I believe — this is the part you will not like — that we have very little time."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Then she glanced back toward the cellar door, and whatever she saw or heard there made her decision for her.

"Not here," she said. "And not tonight. Come tomorrow, after dusk, to the chandler's. Bring whatever it is you want to show me. If it's a trap, Father, I'll turn your bones to—"

"To what?" Anselm asked, genuinely curious.

"I don't know," she admitted. "Something unpleasant. I've never actually done it."

"That's very reassuring," said Anselm, and meant it completely.

* * *

He brought the map, and one other thing.

The chandler's back room smelled of tallow and beeswax and the ghost of a hundred candles' worth of prayers that had never quite risen. Anselm cleared a space on the worktable, brushed away the wax drippings, and unrolled the calfskin with the care of a man handling a relic — which, in a sense, he was.

Petra stood across the table from him, arms folded, firelight from the single lamp catching the sharp bones of her face. She had not touched the map yet. She was reading him instead, the way she probably read omens: looking for the flaw, the false note, the trick.

"This is a forgery," she said finally. "Or a fake. Or a decoration. Maps of anything north of the Ashford hills don't exist. The Crown burned them all eighty years ago. My mistress told me that. She said it was the first thing the new dynasty did — burn the maps, close the roads, put tolls on memory itself."

"The Crown burned the maps it knew about," Anselm said. "Your mistress was right about the burning. She was wrong about the completeness. There were always a few that survived. Hidden in seminary libraries. Sewn into altar cloths. Carried out of the country in the lining of a refugee's coat and carried back in again by his grandson, who didn't even know what he carried." He tapped the calfskin. "This one spent sixty years as a wall-hanging in a brothel in Marrowgate. The madam thought the little tower was decorative. I bought it for the price of a funeral mass and a promise I didn't keep."

"And the tower?"

"Look closer."

Petra leaned over the map at last. Her fingers hovered above the valley without touching it, the way you hover your hand over a flame to test its heat. The little black needle-shape sat in the fold-crack of the calfskin, half-eaten by time, but unmistakable: a tower, impossibly tall, drawn with a precision that made every mountain and river around it look like a child's scrawl.

"There's no name on it," she said.

"No. There wouldn't be." Anselm reached into his cassock and drew out the second thing he had brought: a slim volume, hand-copied, its leather gone soft as cloth with age. "But there's a name in this. Or rather, many names, none of them the right one. This is a commentary on the founding of the kingdom, written by a monk who lived two hundred years ago and was very nearly honest. He wrote that before the kingdom, before the old dynasties, before the gods themselves had names in this tongue, there was a tower in a northern valley. It was not built. It *grew*. It was here when the first people crossed the mountains, and they did not settle near it because they understood, in the way animals understand, that it was not for them. It was a door. The last functioning door between the country-that-was and whatever comes after."

"Comes after what?"

"After everything," Anselm said. "After the country ends. After the gods finish dying. After the last prayer goes unanswered and the last border dissolves and the maps stop agreeing with the ground they describe." He met her eyes. "You've seen it beginning, madam. You told me yourself — the widow whose husband came back dripping. The farmer's field of teeth. Prayers answered badly, loudly, indiscriminately. That is not waking, whatever the crowds kneeling in the streets believe. That is a death rattle. The gods are not returning. They are *leaving*, and as they go, they are pulling the country apart at the seams, because the seams were stitched with prayer, and the stitches are coming loose."

Petra had gone very still. "You said you know what woke it. What started the bells."

"I know what the bells are." Anselm laid his palm flat on the map, over the little black tower. "They are the sound of the Spire feeding. Every answered prayer — every cruel, literal, desperate miracle — is a meal. The Spire eats them. It has been starving for twenty years, since the gods went silent, and now that the dying has begun in earnest, it is gorging itself, and the bells are its digestion. And when it finishes eating—" He paused. He had rehearsed this part too, and abandoned it too, and now he simply said it plainly. "When it finishes eating, the door closes. And when the door closes, the country closes with it. Everything inside the borders at that moment becomes permanent. Final. A finished thing, sealed in amber, slowly forgotten from the outside in. Villages first. Then roads. Then names. Then people."

The lamp flame bent in a draft. Neither of them moved.

"You're saying the country is going to stop existing," Petra said.

"I'm saying it has already begun, and the Spire is the only way out, and the Crown is burning every map of it because the Crown knows exactly what it is and wants no one leaving." He straightened. "Every confiscation order, every auto-da-fé of cartography, every scholar imprisoned for owning an old road-book — it isn't superstition, madam. It's border control. The kingdom cannot afford witnesses to the fact that there is an elsewhere."

Petra stared down at the map. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — quieter, rougher, the voice of a woman talking to herself more than to him. "My mistress had a spellbook I stole. I never opened it. Five years, and I never once broke the clasp." She laughed, short and bitter. "I used to tell myself it was shame. Now I wonder if some part of me knew that opening it would be the first step of a road, and I wasn't ready to walk it."

"And now?"

"Now the bells ring themselves," she said, "and a child's charm worked for the first time in five years, and a drowned man walked into his widow's kitchen, and I bought dragon-scale from a girl who keeps the last fire in the city in a cellar." She looked up at him. "Now I'm ready, Father. Tell me where this tower is."

Anselm hesitated. It was the hesitation of a man about to burn the last bridge behind him, and he felt it in his chest like a physical thing — the specific weight of a secret that has kept you safe by keeping you useless.

"The valley is nine days north," he said. "Past the Ashford hills, past the salt marshes, into country so old the maps that survive don't agree on whether it belongs to us or to the dead. There are no roads. There are villages that appear on no chart and whose names, if they ever had any, have been eaten. And somewhere in the middle of it, growing out of the earth like a tooth, there is a tower with a door that opens for no key and no prayer and no crown." He rolled the map closed and pushed it across the table toward her. "Nine days. Perhaps fewer, now. The bells rang twice this morning. Last week, they rang once."

Petra took the map. Her hands were steady. Anselm found himself admiring that, and fearing it, in equal measure.

"There's a girl in a cellar under the tannery," she said, "keeping a dying dragon alive with rites she hates. And there's a spellbook with a locked clasp that I've been carrying like a sin for five years. If we're doing this, Father, we do it properly. We get Sef. We get the dragon. And I open that book."

"The dragon," Anselm repeated carefully. "You want to bring a dragon."

"I want to bring the last fire in Greyhollow," Petra said, "because I have a feeling — call it a hedge-witch's feeling, call it the same instinct that made me run from my mistress five years ago and made me stay in this city five years more — that a door like the one you're describing doesn't open for keys." She tucked the map into her shawl. "It opens for sacrifice. And I intend to be the one who decides what gets sacrificed."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring.

It was not dawn. It was not any canonical hour. It rang three times, slow and heavy, and stopped, and the silence afterward had a texture to it, like the silence after a scream.

Anselm closed his eyes. *Twice yesterday,* he thought. *Three times today.*

The Spire was getting hungry faster.

"We leave soon," he said quietly. "Sooner than nine days, I think. Sooner than we're ready."

Petra was already moving toward the door, the map a hard rectangle against her ribs, her shadow long and thin in the candlelight. At the threshold she paused, half-turned, and for a moment she looked — Anselm would remember this all his life — she looked less like a fugitive and more like a woman standing at the edge of a country she was about to redraw.

"Then we'd better go wake a dragon," she said.
