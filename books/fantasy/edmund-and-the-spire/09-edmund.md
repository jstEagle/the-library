## 9. Edmund

The tannery smelled the way old grief smells: salt and rot and something underneath it all that refused to die.

Petra had bought dragon-scale from Sef twice before — once for a fever-charm, once because a fisherman's wife wanted protection against drowning and Petra had nothing better to offer her than ground scale and a knot tied in the dark. Both times she had come to the back door of the tannery, both times Sef had met her in the yard among the lime-pits with her sleeves rolled and her hands cracked like drought-earth, and both times the transaction had been quick and wordless and conducted in the language of coin passed palm to palm without looking down.

This time Sef was waiting at the gate before Petra reached it.

"You came." The girl's voice was flat, but her eyes were not. There was something in them that Petra recognized from her own mirror on bad nights — the look of someone carrying a thing too heavy to set down and too precious to drop. "I thought you'd come yesterday."

"I came as fast as I could."

"No, you didn't." Sef said it without malice, the way you'd note the weather. "Nobody comes as fast as they can anymore. Everybody walks slow now. Like if they hurry, they'll arrive somewhere they didn't mean to be." She turned and pushed the gate open. "Come on, then. It wants to meet you."

Petra stopped in the gateway. The yard of the tannery spread out before her in the grey afternoon light, all its familiar ugliness — the pits, the racks of hides gone stiff as shields, the long low sheds sweating their stink into the air. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly where it should have been.

"It," Petra said. "It wants to—"

"The fire-pit's under the east shed. Under the floor. My grandfather dug the cellar before I was born." Sef was already walking, not checking whether Petra followed, which was either trust or a very sophisticated kind of trap. "You'll want to duck. He built the ceiling for himself and he was a small man."

"Sef." Petra caught up to her at the shed door, put a hand on the girl's arm, felt the muscle beneath go rigid as a bowstring. "What is it? What's down there?"

Sef looked at the hand on her arm for a long moment. Then she looked at Petra, and whatever she saw in Petra's face made her shoulders drop half an inch, some guard let down by degrees.

"A dragon," she said. "A dying one. And he's been asking for you since the bells rang."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than they should have. That was Petra's first thought, and her second thought was that no cellar under a tannery in Greyhollow ought to feel like descending into the throat of the world, and her third thought was not a thought at all but a sensation: warmth. Not the dead warmth of a banked hearth but something living, something with a pulse to it, rising up the stairwell like breath.

Sef lit lamps as they went — three of them, hung from iron hooks driven into the earth itself, and Petra noticed that each lamp was fed not with oil but with a curl of dried scale that glowed amber when the flame touched it. Old rites. Rites performed without thinking, the way Sef did everything, hating them and keeping them anyway.

"He hasn't eaten in four months," Sef said. Her voice dropped as they descended, the way voices do in sickrooms and churches. "He won't eat. He just burns what he has. Slower and slower. Grandfather said it would go like this, at the end. He said the fire doesn't go out. It goes *in*."

"In where?"

"That's what I asked him. When I was seven." Sef reached the bottom of the stairs and stood aside. "He said: inward is how gods are born. Then he laughed until he coughed blood, so maybe don't quote him."

The cellar opened around Petra like a held breath finally released.

It was vast — vaster than any tannery could justify, vaster than the building above could possibly contain, and Petra understood with a lurch that the space had been *made*, dug and shaped over generations by hands that knew what they were making room for. The walls were lined with scale-shale, the black stone that dragons slept on, and every surface of it shimmered faintly, catching lamplight and holding it the way water holds the moon. The floor was warm sand, raked smooth. And in the center, coiled around a pit of embers that glowed like a wound in the earth, lay the dragon.

He was smaller than Petra had imagined, though she couldn't have said what she'd imagined. Dragons belonged to stories, to the friezes on temple walls, to the banners of a kingdom that had once been proud of them and then quietly stopped being. This one was grey. That was the thing that struck her hardest — not gold, not crimson, none of the colors of legend, but grey, the grey of ash after the flame has moved on, of a winter sky, of hair going white. His hide hung loose over ribs like the beams of a burned church. One eye opened as they entered.

It was an old eye. That was the only word for it. Amber shot through with threads of white, lid heavy, iris slow to focus — and then focused, and Petra felt herself seen in a way that had nothing to do with light.

"Oh," she said. Stupidly. Just that.

"Don't do anything stupid," Sef said, which Petra took to be a general blessing.

She walked forward across the warm sand. She did not decide to; her feet simply went, drawn along a thread she couldn't see, and later she would remember that walking toward Edmund felt exactly like walking toward Maren's cottage on the last morning she ever spent there — that same terrible gravity, that same knowledge arriving ahead of her body.

The dragon watched her come. When she stood close enough to touch him — and she did not touch him; some instinct older than courtesy forbade it — his nostrils flared, drawing in her scent in two long, deliberate pulls, like a man tasting wine he means to identify blind.

"Hedge-witch," he said.

His voice was not what she expected either. It was not thunder. It was dry and quiet and cracked at the edges, a voice like wind through a ruined house, and it came not from his mouth but from everywhere, out of the scale-shale walls, up out of the embers, from inside her own skull where thoughts lived.

"Yes," Petra managed.

"Small magic. Knots and salts and little lies told kindly." The eye regarded her. "You smell of Maren."

The name landed in Petra's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall and fall and never heard it strike bottom.

"You knew her," she whispered.

"I knew her mother. I knew her grandmother. Your line has kept my kind's leavings for six generations — the scales we shed, the teeth we lost, the fire we left cooling in iron pits." A sound moved through the great grey body, deep in the chest, too slow and heavy to be called laughter but wearing its shape. "Maren came to me twenty years ago with a question. Do you know what she asked me?"

"No."

"She asked me how a door knows it's a door." The ember-light pulsed. "I told her a door is a door because something on one side wants through and something on the other side consents to be passage. She wrote that down. She was always writing things down. She wrote down everything except the one thing that mattered, and then—" The eye narrowed. "And then she sent you away."

"I stole from her." The words came out of Petra like splinters coming out of flesh, each one a small relief and a small wound. "I took her book. I took her grave-dust. I ran. I've run for five years and I never opened the book, I never—"

"Never opened it." The dragon's head shifted on its coils, closer, the heat of his breath washing over her face and smelling of iron and rain-on-hot-stone. "Five years, child. Five years of selling luck to people who pretend not to believe in it, and never once the courage to turn a page."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I have been dying in a hole in the ground for eleven years, and there is very little else to do but listen." The white-threaded eye held hers without mercy. "The country listens too, now. Did you know that? Since the bells. Everything asleep is waking up just enough to listen, and what they hear is you. The girl who ran carrying a book she wouldn't read. The whole dying land has turned its ear toward Greyhollow like a man turning toward a knock."

"That's not—" Petra's voice failed and restarted. "That's madness. I'm nobody. I sell charms in a market. Half of them don't work. Most of them never worked—"

"One did." The dragon said it softly, and the softness was worse than thunder. "Three days ago. A child. A fever. You tied the knot and spoke the words and for the first time in five years the words went somewhere. You felt it leave you. You felt it *land*." He drew another slow breath, and the embers in the pit flared with it. "Do you know why it landed, hedge-witch? Why your small magic suddenly works after five years of falling flat as coins on stone?"

Petra shook her head. She could not make her voice work.

"Because the doors are opening. All of them. Every crack between here and elsewhere is yawning wide as the gods fail and their last breath blows through the gaps. Magic isn't returning, girl. Magic is *leaking*. Pouring through the broken seals of a country that is ending. And you — you with the stolen book and the buried guilt and the mistress who sent you ahead — you are standing in the largest leak in the land." The great head lowered until the ancient eye was level with hers, near enough that she could see her own reflection doubled in it, small and wavering. "Or perhaps you are not standing in it. Perhaps you were placed in it. Maren was very clever about placement."

"I need you to stop talking about her," Petra said, and was surprised by the steel in her own voice. "I need you to tell me what you want. Sef said you asked for me. Dragons don't ask. In every story, in every frieze, in every lie the temples ever carved in stone — dragons take, or they burn, or they sleep. They don't ask."

Something moved across the grey face that might have been a smile, if smiles could be carved from granite and worn smooth by grief.

"So you do know the old stories after all. Good. Then you know the last one, the one the temples cut off the friezes when the Crown decided the gods were embarrassing." The dragon settled deeper into his coils, and Petra saw for the first time how much effort the movement cost him, how the great muscles trembled like an old man rising from a chair. "The last story says that when a dragon dies, its fire must be carried. Not extinguished. Carried. Passed hand to hand, keeper to keeper, down the long road of the world, until it finds a place worth burning for. My line of keepers carried my line of fire for nine hundred years. Her mother. And hers. And hers." His gaze slid sideways, briefly, to Sef, who stood at the foot of the stairs with her arms wrapped around herself and her jaw set hard against tears. "And now the fire has nowhere left to go, and I have perhaps a month of burning in me, and the door in the valley will starve shut forever unless someone carries it."

"The Spire," Petra breathed. Anselm's maps, Anselm's confiscated towers, Anselm's voice in the dark saying *the last functioning door*. "You're talking about the Spire."

"I am talking about the only door out of a country that has already died and doesn't know it yet. I am talking about the hollow in its heart shaped like a burning heart. And I am talking to the wrong person, which is the tragedy of my final month, because the right person would be someone brave, and instead I got you."

The words should have wounded. They did wound. But underneath the wound Petra felt something stranger, something almost like vertigo, like the moment on a cliff edge when you realize the drop has been there all along and you've been walking near it with your eyes closed for years.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why does everyone keep — Maren, the bells, the dreams Sef says the fire sends, you — why does everything keep pointing at *me*?"

"Because you ran," Edmund said simply. "Because you were given a burden and you refused it, and refusal, in a country of kneeling believers and obedient keepers and inquisitors who burn what they cannot explain, refusal is the rarest virtue left. The gods are dying, Petra Underhill, and do you know what killed them? It wasn't the Crown. It wasn't the silence. It was consent. Everyone consented. Every priest, every keeper, every faithful fool knelt down and agreed to be empty. And then along came a hedge-witch's apprentice who looked at the greatest working of her age and said *no*, and ran, and kept the door of her own heart shut tight for five years." The ember-light rose and fell with his breath, rose and fell, a slow tide. "A locked door is the most valuable thing in a dying land. Because a locked door can choose to open."

The silence that followed was enormous. Petra became aware of small things with terrible clarity: the tick of the lamps, Sef's ragged breathing at the stair-foot, the sand shifting faintly under her boots as the floor's warmth breathed upward.

"What are you asking me?" she said at last. "Say it plainly. I'm tired of omens and leaks and doors. Say it plainly."

Edmund's eye closed, then opened again, and in the space of that blink Petra saw eleven years of a dark cellar pass through it, and nine hundred years behind that, and something older still, something that predated names.

"When I die," the dragon said plainly, "my fire must be carried to the Spire. Sef can carry it — she is a keeper, born and bound, and she will offer, because offering is all she has ever been allowed. But the fire will not pass through the door alone. Fire needs a hand. A will. Someone to give it freely, and someone to pay the toll the door demands." His voice roughened, cracked like river ice in spring. "I am asking you to carry my death, Petra Underhill. I am asking the girl who stole a book and ran to finish the journey her mistress started. I am asking you to walk out of Greyhollow carrying the last living dragon-fire in the world, and I am asking it because you, of all the souls left in this unraveling country, are the one who understands that some doors should stay shut — and therefore the only one alive who can be trusted to open the right one."

"And if I say no?"

The eye regarded her, patient as stone.

"Then the door closes, the country finishes forgetting itself, and everyone you have ever sold a charm to — every fevered child, every widow, every fisherman's wife afraid of water — goes wherever forgotten things go. Which is nowhere. Which is the specific nowhere of never having been." He paused. "But you already know I will not need to argue. You picked up the lantern before you agreed to lift it. People like you always do."

Petra looked down at her own hands.

They were shaking. But they were also, she noticed — with the strange, cold clarity that arrives sometimes at the exact center of ruin — already cupped. Already curved around a weight that wasn't there yet. Already shaped like a woman carrying something.

Behind her, at the foot of the stairs, Sef made a sound. Not a word. Something smaller and more honest than a word, the sound a rope makes when the last strand parts.

And above them, muffled through the tannery floor, through the yard, through the streets of Greyhollow where edicts were being pasted to walls and neighbors were beginning to look at one another with new and careful eyes — a bell began to ring. One bell. No hand upon the rope. It rang three times, unhurried, like a knock.

Like something asking, politely, to be let in.

Edmund's ember-lit eye found hers one last time.

"There," the dragon said. "It remembers you too."
