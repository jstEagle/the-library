## 10. Vane Closes the Market

Inquisitor Vane had always believed that a city could be read like a confession. Greyhollow in the hour before dawn lay open on the page before him: the tannery stink rising off the river like a sinner's breath, the fishwives' carts already squealing toward the Salt Gate, the temple bells hanging silent and suspect in their towers. He stood on the customs house roof with his hands folded behind his back and watched the fog come off the water, and he thought: *here is a city that has begun to lie to itself.* A city that lied to itself was a city ready to betray itself, and betrayal, properly harvested, fed an investigation for years.

He had not slept. Sleep had become a country he could no longer find on any map — which was, he reflected grimly, more than could be said for most of the kingdom these days.

The edict was drafted. It sat in his breast pocket against his heart, three pages of Crown vellum, and it weighed more than his sword. By noon every gate of Greyhollow would be sealed. By nightfall his men would be going door to door with torches and lists. And somewhere in that fog, in some narrow house that smelled of salt and dried herbs, the witch who had answered a child's prayer was waking to learn what her miracle had cost her city.

Vane touched the pocket once, lightly, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

*Begin,* he told the fog. And the fog, being loyal to nothing, began.

* * *

The sealing of the gates was done by the book, because Vane did everything by the book; the book was the only thing left in the world he trusted absolutely. At the first bell after dawn — a bell rung by human hands this time, verified, witnessed, its rope inspected twice — forty soldiers in Crown grey took up positions at the Salt Gate, the Tanners' Gate, the river stairs, and the postern behind the chandlers' row. The great oak doors swung shut with a sound like a held breath finally released, and the bar came down, and Greyhollow became a closed sentence from which no word could escape unexamined.

The crowd that gathered at the Salt Gate did not shout. That was what unsettled him. He had expected shouting, fists, the ordinary animal fury of a penned population. Instead perhaps two hundred people simply stood in the drizzle and watched the gates close, faces tilted upward, and one old woman near the front crossed herself in the manner of the Old Rite — thumb to brow, brow to heart, heart to the hollow of the throat where the voice lives — a gesture banned for thirty years, performed in full view of forty soldiers, and not one soldier moved to stop her. Not even Vane. He stood at the gatehouse window and watched the gesture travel through the crowd like wind through wheat, hand after hand after hand, and he felt something turn over in his chest that he refused, for the moment, to name.

They were not angry, those people. They were *hopeful*. That was the horror of it. The Crown had just locked them inside a dying city, and they looked at the closing doors as if God Himself were coming down the road from outside.

Vane went down into the street.

"Disperse," he said, and his voice carried the way it always did, flat and cold as slate. "Return to your homes. Curfew begins at dusk."

The old woman turned to him. She had a face like a walnut shell, all creases and dark oil, and her eyes were wet but unafraid. "Inquisitor," she said, "my son drowned off the Weir nine years ago. I prayed for him back every night since. Last week—" Her voice caught. Not with grief. With something worse. "Last week I heard knocking."

"Then you heard the tide," Vane said. "It does that."

"It knocked *in time*, sir." She said it gently, the way one corrects a child. "Three knocks, then a pause, then three. Like a man who has forgotten how to knock but remembers he is polite."

Behind him, Vane heard one of his own soldiers shift his weight. He did not turn around. He looked at the old woman a long moment, and she looked back, and between them passed the understanding that passes between two people who both know a thing is true and have agreed, for different reasons, to call it something else.

"Go home, mother," he said quietly.

She went. But as she passed him she said, so softly only he could hear: "You pray too. I can tell. You've got the look of a man whose prayers are stacking up unanswered, and the stack's about to fall."

* * *

By midmorning the burning had begun.

Vane had ordered it done methodically: charm-stalls first, then the apothecaries who sold to charm-makers, then the private houses on the list his informers had compiled over five patient years. His men worked in pairs, one to hold the torch, one to hold the list, and Vane walked behind them with his ledger open, recording each seizure in his small, exact hand. *One stall, Saltmarket Row. Contents: forty-one luck-knots, red thread, knotted in the old pattern. One jar of fever-charms, wax and rosemary. One copper brazier, cold.* He wrote it all down because writing made it procedure, and procedure made it law, and law was the wall he had built his whole life against the ocean.

The luck-knots burned quickly. Red thread goes up like hair. The fever-charms popped as the wax split, releasing little breaths of rosemary smoke that smelled, obscenely, of Sunday kitchens and comfort, and Vane found himself standing in a cloud of it while a charm-seller's awning collapsed in flame, breathing in the smell of the thing he was destroying.

A woman was screaming at him from behind the soldiers' line. Not the seller — the seller had gone pale and silent, sitting in the gutter with her hands in her lap like a woman at a funeral. The screaming woman was a customer. Well-dressed. Merchant class. He recognized her vaguely; she bought charms every quarter-day and always pretended, while doing it, to be examining ribbon.

"You're burning *medicine*," she shouted. "My daughter wears one of those knots! She's worn it since she was born!"

"Then bring the knot in, madam, and it will be burned also," Vane said, without looking up from his ledger. "No charge will be laid against you. The amnesty extends to purchasers."

"Amnesty?" She laughed, high and cracked. "You mean if we name each other. That's what the criers are saying. Name a witch, save your neck." She pushed forward; a soldier caught her arm. "Well, here's my naming, Inquisitor: *everyone*. Everyone I've ever bought from, everyone who ever tied a knot over my baby's cradle, everyone who ever hung a horseshoe crooked on purpose. Half this city practices and the other half pays them to. Burn us all or burn your list, because they're the same fire."

Her words went through the assembled crowd like a spark through dry flax. He saw it happen — saw people glance sideways at their neighbors, quick and involuntary, the ancient flicker of the denunciation reflex — and he should have been satisfied. This was the harvest. This was exactly what the edict was designed to grow. Fear, divided out among neighbors, kept a city docile far better than soldiers did.

But instead of satisfaction Vane felt the sensation he had been carrying for six days now, since the kneeling woman in the interrogation room, since the prayer that had come true too literally, too cruelly, too *old*: the sensation of standing in a church during a sermon he did not believe, while everyone around him wept.

"Take her home," he said. "Gently."

* * *

The interrogations ran through the afternoon in the undercroft of the customs house, and Vane conducted every third one himself, because he did not trust his subordinates with the strange ones. The strange ones were multiplying. Every person dragged in now had a story, and the stories had stopped being about witches and started being about answers.

A cooper whose wife had prayed for rain got rain — for nine hours, black and warm and smelling of iron, falling only on their street, drowning their cellar and no other. A ferryman who had prayed aloud, drunk, for his dead brother's forgiveness woke to find muddy footprints crossing his floor from the river to his bed and back again, every morning, in sets of two. A girl of eleven who had wished on a coin thrown into the Drywell — the well that had been dry for a generation — reported that the coin had been thrown back.

Vane wrote it all down. His hand never shook. His hand was the steadiest thing in Greyhollow.

But late in the afternoon, alone in the undercroft between sessions, he did a thing he had never done in twenty years of service. He took out the small worn prayer-book his mother had left him — the Old Rite, the forbidden printing, the one he kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath his spare cassock — and he read the evening office silently, moving his lips, feeling like a man drinking from a well he had spent his life poisoning.

*Deliver us from the answering,* the old liturgy said, in the part the new Church had cut. *Deliver us from the god who hears.*

He had always assumed that line was metaphor. Some pious exaggeration, a rhetorical flourish against pride. Sitting in the lamplight with the screams of the cooper's wife still fading down the corridor — she had been brought in at dusk, soaked to the skin, though it had not rained since morning — Vane understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming across a pond, that his ancestors had meant it literally. That they had known something the new Church had burned away along with everything else. That being heard was not grace.

Being heard was a hook.

He wrapped the book away again, and when the next prisoner was brought down — a tanner's apprentice, trembling, accused of nothing yet, named by a neighbor who wanted the amnesty — Vane heard himself say, in a voice almost gentle:

"Tell me what you know. And understand that I am not your enemy. I am the only man in this city asking the same question you are."

* * *

He saved Underhill Street for last, and he told himself the ordering was administrative. The charm-seller there was small game — luck-knots, fever-charms, the hedge-craft of market women — and her file was thin. Five years in Greyhollow, no incidents, no complaints, payments of the unlicensed-practitioner fine made quarterly and on time, which was either remarkable honesty or remarkable caution. What put her street on his route was the child. The miller's boy. The fever that broke overnight, the charm that had *worked*, the first working charm in Greyhollow in five years — and Vane had learned long ago that miracles cluster like lightning. Where one struck, others were gathering.

He walked the street himself rather than sending soldiers ahead. He wanted to see the faces before the faces knew to arrange themselves.

Underhill Street was narrow and crooked, leaning houses, laundry strung between them like signal flags. And the faces — Vane catalogued them as he passed, professionally, the way another man might count coins — the faces were already wrong. A woman drawing water paused at her well and watched him with an expression he had seen a hundred times and never learned to stomach: the look of a neighbor deciding. A man mending a shutter let his hammer go quiet and did not resume it until Vane had passed. Two boys stopped their game of knucklebones and stared, and one of them whispered something to the other, and the other looked back over his shoulder at the small house at the end of the lane — the one with the blue door and the herb pots — and then looked at Vane, and then looked away very fast.

There it was. The noose, tying itself. He had not posted a single notice on this street. He had not needed to. The edict had done its work in a single day; fear had traveled faster than any crier; and now the neighbors of the charm-witch were already rehearsing, in the privacy of their own heads, the words they would say to save themselves. *I always thought there was something. She paid her fines, but who does that unless they're hiding something? My cousin's boy bought a knot off her once and his luck went bad.*

Vane had seen it in a dozen towns. It never took longer than a day. That was the thing no one understood about witch-hunts, the thing the ballads got wrong: the hunters were almost unnecessary. Give a neighborhood permission and a reason, and it would deliver its own devours. All the Crown had ever really contributed was the match.

And yet — he stopped, halfway down the lane, in the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone, and admitted it to himself honestly, because honesty in his own head was the one discipline he had never surrendered — and yet he did not want it to be her. He had read her file four times. Petra Underhill, hedge-trained, fled mistress unknown, five years of quiet commerce, five years of *nothing*. No botched workings, no dead clients, no desperate midnight rites. In a city suddenly full of prayers curdling into horrors — drowned husbands dripping on floors, fields blooming teeth, wells throwing back coins — hers was the only magic in Greyhollow with a clean record.

Which meant either she was innocent of the answered prayers, or she was the most disciplined practitioner he had ever hunted, and both possibilities made his chest ache in a way he declined to examine.

The blue door opened before he reached it.

She stood in the frame with a lantern in her hand — why a lantern, in daylight? — and she was smaller than he'd imagined, sharp-faced, calm in the way of people who have already run the arithmetic of their own arrest and gotten a number they can live with. Behind her, in the dim interior, Vane glimpsed a table swept bare, a hearth cold, a room prepared for departure. Prepared for flight, rather. And something in her stance — the weight slightly forward, the eyes already measuring the gap between her doorway and the alley mouth — told him she had been expecting this visit since the bells rang themselves, and had decided, sometime in the last six days, exactly how much fight she had in her.

"Inquisitor," she said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles on the lantern were white. "You'll want to search the house."

"I will," Vane said.

"And afterward?"

"Afterward," he said, "I will ask you questions. And you will answer them. And whether I believe your answers will depend entirely on whether anyone on this street has already given me better ones."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing, in an enemy's voice, the accent of a fellow prisoner.

"They will give you plenty," she said quietly. "They've been practicing all day. I could hear them through the walls, deciding what I am." She lifted the lantern a little, an odd, formal gesture, almost hospitality. "Come in, then. Mind the step. And Inquisitor—"

"Yes?"

"When you find nothing, and you won't find anything, ask yourself why a guilty woman doesn't run." Her chin came up. "Ask yourself what she's waiting for."

Vane stepped past her into the small, herb-scented dark, and behind him, out in the lane, he heard the neighbors' shutters begin, one by one, to open — quietly, carefully, like eyes.
