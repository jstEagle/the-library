## 11. Maren's Unfinished Spell

The book had traveled with her for five years the way a stone travels in a boot — always there, always felt, never once looked at directly.

Petra kept it at the bottom of her chest beneath three layers of ordinary clothing: shifts and stockings and a winter shawl that smelled of camphor, things chosen precisely because they were boring, because no inquisitor rifling through a hedge-witch's belongings would pause over wool. The spellbook lay under all of it wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was wrapped again in a charm of dulling — one of Maren's own designs, stitched into the fabric in thread Petra had spun herself on the night she ran. *Let it be overlooked,* the charm said. *Let it be nothing.* It had worked. It had worked so well that sometimes Petra herself could open the chest and look straight down at the bundle and feel her gaze slide off it like rain off waxed canvas, and she would close the lid again and go about her careful life.

Tonight she took it out.

She told herself it was because of the bells. Because of the widow on Tanner's Row whose husband had come back dripping. Because of the farmer's field of teeth two valleys over, which the criers spoke of in hushed, delighted horror as though it were entertainment rather than an omen. Because every answered prayer in Greyhollow rang against her skin now like a struck tuning fork, and she could not shake the feeling — irrational, unprofessional, beneath her — that they were ringing *for* her.

But that was only part of it, and she knew it. The larger part was simpler and worse.

Someone had written to her.

Not in ink. Not in words. But when she'd stood in the market that morning selling luck-knots to people who pretended not to know what they were buying, a woman had pressed a copper coin into her palm and said, *For my daughter, who is thinking of running away,* and the coin had been warm, and the warmth had crawled up Petra's arm and settled behind her sternum like a hand pressing flat against the inside of her ribs, and she had heard — not heard, *felt*, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar — a sentence half-finished hanging in the air of the world, waiting for someone to complete it.

Maren's sentences were like that. Half-finished ones. She used to leave them lying around the cottage like traps: a pot on the fire with no salt added, a letter begun and abandoned mid-line, a lesson started and then silence, watching, waiting to see if the apprentice would finish the thought or let it die. *Finish your workings,* she'd say. *A spell left incomplete is a door left open, and anything can walk through a door.*

Petra sat down at her table, moved the candle closer, and unwrapped the book.

* * *

The oilcloth fell away and the dulling-charm broke like a held breath released, and suddenly the room knew the book was there. She felt it in her teeth. Five years of being-nothing ended all at once, and the spellbook became the heaviest object in Greyhollow, heavier than the temple bells, heavier than the tannery vats, heavy as a body.

It was bound in grey leather gone soft at the corners, the color of the ash Maren stirred into everything. There was no title on the spine. There had never been a title; Maren said names were handles and handles were for other people to grab you by. On the cover, tooled so faintly you had to tilt it toward the light, was a single mark: a circle with a line drawn down through it, entering but not exiting. A door seen edge-on. A door that opened inward only.

Petra's hands were shaking. She noticed this with a strange detachment, as though the hands belonged to a customer across the stall, some frightened girl buying fever-charms, and she almost smiled at the irony before the smile collapsed under the weight of what she was about to do.

Five years. She had crossed a country with this thing against her spine, sewn it into hems, buried it twice, slept with it under her head through a winter in the Fells when she'd been so hungry she'd considered — briefly, shamefully — whether a spellbook might be edible if boiled long enough. And she had never opened it. Not once. Opening it would have meant admitting it was hers, and it wasn't hers, it had never been hers, she had *stolen* it, she had crept out of the cottage at moonset with the book and the vial of grave-dust and the clothes she stood up in, and she had run because —

Because she had woken in the night and found Maren standing over her bed with chalk in her hand, drawing a circle around the floorboards, around *her*, murmuring in the old cadence, and Petra had known — had *known* with the absolute certainty available only to the young and the terrified — what circles like that were for. Vessels. Doors. You drew a circle to hold something in place while you worked it, and the something was never supposed to be a person, except that old magic didn't care what it used, and Maren had been getting old, and her hands had been getting slow, and Petra had been right there, young and whole and full of exactly the kind of living blood that dying workings crave.

She had run. She had taken the book because taking it felt like justice, like evidence, like a knife pulled from a would-be murderer's belt. And then she had spent five years not looking at it, because looking at it might prove her wrong, and being wrong about that — being wrong about the single act that had made her life — was a thing she could not afford.

The candle guttered. Outside, far off, a temple bell gave one soft note with no hand anywhere near its rope, and the sound went through the walls of her little room like water through cloth.

Petra opened the book.

* * *

The first pages were old. Older than Petra's apprenticeship, older than Maren's own apprenticeship, maybe older than the kingdom, though the writing stayed stubbornly legible — Maren had re-inked the fading passages somewhere along the way, Petra realized, in her cramped sideways hand, preserving her own teacher's teacher's voice the way you'd preserve seeds. Remedies. Wards. A recipe for ink made from oak galls and grave-water. Instructions for speaking politely to a river. Page after page of the small, practical, unglamorous craft that Maren had tried to teach her and Petra had learned only well enough to sell in a market where everyone pretended not to believe in it.

She turned pages slowly. The candle flame leaned toward the book, she noticed. Not flickering — *leaning*, steadily, like a plant toward a window. She decided not to notice it harder than that.

Then, two-thirds through, the handwriting changed.

It changed gradually, the way a person changes — the letters growing larger, looser, the lines less ruled by habit. This was Maren in her last year. Petra knew that hand. She had watched it write invoices and warnings and, once, a letter to a cousin that Maren had burned unread in the hearth while weeping without noise, which was the only time Petra had ever seen her cry. The ink here was brownish. Iron-gall going rusty with age. Some of the entries were dated, and the last date was five years and two months ago.

Three weeks before Petra ran.

She should have stopped there. She knew that too, the way you know the depth of a stair in the dark a half-second too late. But her fingers turned the page.

The heading was written large, centered, underlined twice in a hand pressing hard enough to emboss the next leaf:

**TO HOLD A DOOR OPEN**

And beneath it, smaller:

*for the one who ran*

Petra stopped breathing.

The room did not stop. The candle leaned. Somewhere below her window a cart passed, wheels hissing on wet cobbles, and a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The world went on being ordinary with a terrible indifference while Petra Underhill read the first line of a spell addressed to her by name.

*for the one who ran.* Not "the thief." Not "the apprentice." Not "the ungrateful girl." *The one who ran* — spoken of the way you'd speak of a known quantity, an expected outcome, a thing already accounted for in the working. As if the running were not a betrayal to be cursed but a step in the procedure. An ingredient. Something the spell required.

Her eyes went back to the top of the page and began, despite everything, to read.

*The door will not stay shut. I have looked at what is coming and the door will not stay shut, and when it opens it must be held, or everything on this side of it goes through whether it consents or no.*

There was more. Maren's late hand, hurried now, the margins filling with notes in different inks — she had come back to this page again and again, over months, the way you worry a tooth. Diagrams crowded the lower half: concentric rings, a tower drawn in section like a tooth pulled from the earth, annotations in the old script that Petra could only half-read, her lessons having ended the night she ran. Words recurred that she knew from market-day rumor and inquisitor edict both: *spire*. *Threshold*. *The hollow at the root*. And one phrase, repeated four times in four places, each time underlined:

*willingly given or not at all.*

Petra read it standing, then sitting, then standing again, pacing the four steps her room allowed, reading by snatches, telling herself she was searching for the trap, the vessel-working, the circle-around-the-bed, the proof that Maren had meant to pour her like wine into something older and hungrier. That was what she was looking for. She was sure of it. She read the whole page twice, three times, hunting for it.

It wasn't there.

What was there was arithmetic. Costings. Maren had costed the spell out like a merchant costing cloth — *the holder must stand at the hinge; the hinge takes a life's weight; a life's weight may be paid in whole or in installments; installments shorten the payer.* Notes in the margin: *ask Edm—* and then scratched out, scratched out so hard the paper had thinned. *Ask the keeper's line about fire.* Another: *she will not forgive me. Let her not forgive me, so long as she lives.* And beside that, in fresher ink, almost an afterthought, almost gentle:

*If you are reading this, little thief, then my timing was wrong and yours is running out, and I am sorry for both.*

Petra put the book down.

She walked to the window and stood with her forehead against the cold glass and breathed until the shaking slowed. Below, Greyhollow lay under a thin fall of ash-colored fog, lamplighters moving through it like fish under ice. Somewhere in that fog a widow was hiding a drowned husband who wasn't her husband. Somewhere a bell was ringing itself. Somewhere an inquisitor was sharpening questions. And somewhere — she pressed her knuckles to her mouth — somewhere a dead woman had spent her final months writing a letter to the girl who stole from her and ran, and had hidden it inside the stolen thing itself, knowing — *knowing* — that guilt keeps better than any lock. That the one who ran would carry the book for five years and never open it, and that one day fear would grow heavier than guilt, and the book would open, and the message would arrive exactly when it was needed.

*My timing was wrong and yours is running out.*

"You knew," Petra said aloud, to the empty room, to the fog, to the dead. "You knew I'd take it. You wanted me to take it."

The candle flame stood perfectly upright, listening.

No. That was the old terror talking, dressing itself in new robes. Think. Think like a witch instead of a runaway. If Maren had wanted her to go, why the circle? Why the chalk around the bed at moonset, the murmuring, the —

The murmuring.

Petra closed her eyes and made herself remember it properly, for the first time in five years, without the terror editing the memory down to its scariest shape. The cadence hadn't been a working-cadence. Workings had a rhythm to them, a metronome beat, *so-gather-so-and-hold*. What Maren had been murmuring that night had no meter at all. It had been uneven. Halting. The rhythm of someone counting under their breath. Or reading.

Or copying something out, fast, before the chance was lost.

And the chalk — Petra remembered now, remembered with a nausea that came up from five years deep — the chalk circle hadn't enclosed the bed. It had enclosed the *floorboards beneath the bed*, and there had been a gap in it, a deliberate break in the ring facing the window, and she had fled through that gap without ever seeing it, and Maren had not followed. Had not called out. Had not raised one hand. Petra had run three miles before she let herself believe she'd escaped, and the whole way the night had been strangely, suspiciously easy — no dogs, no neighbors, no mist rising off the marsh the way it always rose, the road lying open ahead of her like a sentence inviting completion.

*A spell left incomplete is a door left open.*

"Anything can walk through," Petra whispered. "You left the door open. You left it open and I walked through it, and you counted on me walking through it."

The book lay on the table where she'd set it down, open to the page, patient as a well.

She went back to it. She made herself go back, because there was one thing left to check, one thing she had been circling the way you circle a wound, and avoiding it longer would be cowardice dressed as caution. She read the working from the beginning, properly this time, line by line, the way Maren had taught her to read a spell before casting — *first learn what it wants, then decide if you want the same thing.*

The spell wanted a door held open. The spell required a hinge, a life's weight, willingly given. The spell named its components: dragon-fire, freely surrendered; grave-dust of the caster, freely surrendered; and the holder — *the holder must be one who has already passed through a door she believed was shut behind her.* 

Petra laughed. One short, cracked syllable, with nothing in it of humor. Even that, even her guilt, even the running itself — catalogued, required, *used*. Maren had built her theft into the architecture. There was no version of these events in which Petra had acted freely, and no version in which she hadn't. The old woman had woven her apprentice's heart into the working the way you weave a warp-thread before the weft ever starts, and then died — or been killed, or simply worn out, the page didn't say — leaving the weft unfinished.

Because the page *was* unfinished. That was the last thing. She had saved it for last the way you save the worst medicine.

The working ran down the page in Maren's loosening hand, instructions following instructions, precise and terrible and tender, and then, near the bottom, the script grew faster, the letters crowding, as if the writer knew her time was shorter than her ink. The final passage began:

*When the fire is given and the dust is given, the holder speaks the closing words, which are these: that which was shut shall stand open, and that which ran shall stand —*

And there it stopped.

Mid-word. Mid-*word* — the pen-stroke trailing off into a long thinning line, the way a pulse trails off on a physician's chart. No flourish, no blot, no sign of interruption. Just ink failing at the same moment, apparently, as a heart. As if Maren had been writing the spell and writing the spell and the spell had been writing her, using up her remaining time word by word, and the account had run empty one syllable short of done.

*that which ran shall stand—*

Stand what. Stand *where*. Stand *in the door*? Stand *aside*? Stand *still*? Stand *witness*? Stand *in the fire*?

The difference between those endings was the difference between everything, and Maren had died — had *stopped*, at least — one syllable before saying which.

Petra sat very still. The candle had burned down to a stub; the leaning had stopped; the flame burned straight and small and honest. Outside, the fog was thickening, and far away across the city, faint as a memory of a memory, a bell rang once with no hand on its rope, and this time Petra felt the note pass through her chest and *catch*, snagging on something behind her sternum, the same place the widow's warm coin had pressed, and she understood with a cold, clear, unhurried certainty that the catching was the half-finished sentence. That it lived in the world now, unfinished, the way Maren had taught her that unfinished spells live — as an open door. That it had been ringing through every bell and every answered prayer and every wrong resurrection in Greyhollow, calling its missing ending out into the dark, waiting for the one who ran to come and speak it.

Waiting for her to choose the word.

"That's not fair," Petra said to the dead woman, and her voice broke on the second word, and she let it. "That's not fair, you can't — you don't get to make me finish it. You don't get to make me the answer."

The book said nothing. Books are good at that. But the page lay open in the stub-light, and the embossed pressure of the underline showed through from the leaf above, and the heading seemed to sit there with the patience of all dead teachers:

*for the one who ran.*

Petra wiped her face with the heel of her hand, the way Maren used to tell her to, *properly, girl, salt water ruins ink*, and reached for her pen and her pot of iron-gall, and found her hand hovering over the page and unable to descend, because whatever she wrote next she would have to mean, and meaning it would change everything, and not meaning it would change nothing, and she was so tired of changing nothing.

A knock at the street door. Three knocks, unhurried, familiar.

Through the fog-thick window she could just make out, below, the shape of a tall man in a priest's coat with the collar torn off, looking up at her light with the expression of a man who has followed a scent a very long way and finally stands at its source.

Petra looked at the page. She looked at the door.

Neither one, she suspected, was going to wait much longer.
