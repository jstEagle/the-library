## 12. The Road Out of Greyhollow

The city began burning its temples on the third night.

Petra watched from the loft window of Sef's tannery as the first fire went up — the little shrine of Saint Vessal on Cooper's Row, the one with the chipped alabaster hands. It burned green at the edges, which meant someone had salted it, which meant someone had decided that whatever had been listening in there was no longer welcome to listen. The flames stood very straight and made almost no smoke, and the crowd around them was silent in a way crowds are never silent, and Petra thought: *they're not angry. They're frightened. That's worse.*

"They started with the small ones," Anselm said behind her. He sat on a stool among the hides, his defrocked collar folded away into his pocket like something he'd stopped being able to afford. "They'll work up to the big ones by morning. The Cathedral of the Ninefold Bell last, if they've any sense of theater."

"Do they?"

"Greyhollow always has." He rubbed his eyes. "Sense of theater is the only sense this city has never lacked."

Below them, in the cellar, Edmund breathed. Petra could feel it through the floorboards now — she'd been able to feel it for two days, since the dragon had spoken its name and asked her, of all people, to carry its fire when it died. Each breath came slower than the last, long as a tide going out, and between breaths there was a silence so complete it seemed the whole cellar held itself still out of courtesy. The heart-flame waited in its iron lantern beside the great gray head, burning low and steady, the color of a coal remembered rather than seen.

Sef was down there. She hadn't come up in hours. Through the boards Petra heard her voice, low and rhythmic, reciting the old rites — the ones she knew by rote and hated, the ones her mother had died keeping. *Ember to ember, ash to ash, what was given is not taken back.* Petra didn't know all the words. She found herself learning them anyway, the way you learn a song stuck in your head against your will.

"We leave tonight," she said.

Anselm looked up. "I assumed we would. I confess I hoped otherwise. Hope's a bad habit of mine; I caught it from preaching."

"The gates close tomorrow. Vane posted the order at dusk — full seal, house-to-house searches beginning at dawn. Amnesty for any witch who names another." She said it flatly, because saying it flatly was the only way to say it without hearing Mrs. Corren's voice in her head, or the baker's, or old Tam Wicker's who'd bought fever-charms from her for four years and called her *love* and slipped her extra bread. *Amnesty for any witch who names another.* Every one of those faces was doing arithmetic tonight. She could feel it happening across the whole city, quiet as frost forming: neighbors counting neighbors, deciding what a charm was worth against what a name was worth.

"You could still go alone," Anselm said quietly. "You know that. A hedge-witch with your craft could walk through Vane's cordon like fog through a fence. Take the lantern, take the book, be over the wall before the fires reach the cathedral."

"And leave Sef."

"And leave Sef," he agreed. "And leave me, though I flatter myself I'm harder to kill than I look, which admittedly isn't difficult."

"She won't stay. She'll die here before she leaves that lantern, and if I take it without her she'll follow us into the cordon alone and get taken, and then everything Edmund gave us ends in an inquisitor's strongbox." Petra turned from the window. The green fire on Cooper's Row had begun to collapse inward, eating its own shrine. "Besides. You said it yourself, Father. The Spire is east, past the fens, in country that isn't on maps anymore. Three people traveling together look like pilgrims. One woman traveling alone looks exactly like what she is."

"A witch."

"A witch," she said. "I've spent five years pretending not to be one. I find I've lost the knack."

* * *

The corpse-wagon belonged to a man named Hobb who owed Petra a debt she had never intended to collect.

He came to the tannery after midnight, driving his cart slow through streets lit orange at both ends of every vista, and he did not knock but stood in the yard among the lime-pits until Sef fetched him in, because knocking drew eyes and eyes were the one thing Greyhollow had too many of now. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a carter's forearms and a mourner's face, and he smelled of lye and camphor and the particular sweet-rot undertone that no amount of washing ever fully left the dead-carters of a plague-seasoned city.

"You said three days," he rumbled. "It's been two."

"I said soon."

"You said three days, mistress, and I've had the wagon standing empty and my route undone, and the burial guild asks questions, and the guild asking questions is how heads end up on spikes." He shifted his weight. In the lantern-light his eyes went to the iron lantern itself — to the low coal-colored flame inside it — and away again quickly, the way men look at things they've decided not to believe in. "There's a body under the sacking. Plague-marked, or marked to look it. Nobody searches a plague cart twice. Not even Vane's people. They burn the contents and wave the driver through."

"That's the arrangement," Petra said.

"The arrangement," Hobb said, "was three days."

Petra took his hand. It was a working touch, quick and unshowy — thumb across the pulse-point, a knot tied invisibly in the air between their skins, luck braided into the sinew the way her mistress had taught her before Petra had understood anything her mistress taught was real. Hobb stiffened. Five years of customers pretending not to know what they bought, and still they flinched when the buying became undeniable.

"For the road," she said. "Axles don't crack, horses don't throw shoes, checkpoints see a tired honest carter and nothing else. That's worth more than three days, Hobb. That's worth your life, and you know it, or you wouldn't be standing in a tannery yard at midnight while your city burns its gods."

He worked his jaw. Outside, somewhere south, a bell began to ring — not rung, *ringing*, wild and arrhythmic, the sound Anselm had named a god clearing its throat, except now it sounded less like a throat clearing and more like a throat failing. Hobb crossed himself with the hand she'd just charmed, an old reflex, and looked faintly ashamed of it.

"The fen road," he said. "Past the leper gate. There's a checkpoint at the toll bridge but plague carts go through unopened. After the bridge I put you down at the crossroads and I turn back, and I never saw you, and if anyone asks I'll say you robbed me." He almost smiled. "Which will be true enough. You've robbed me of my quiet life, mistress Underhill. I hope whatever you're carrying is worth it."

Petra looked at the iron lantern, where Edmund's heart-flame sat burning its patient coal-color, waiting to become a door.

"So do I," she said.

* * *

They laid Sef in the body-box.

She insisted on it, which told Petra more about the girl than five weeks of buying dragon-scale ever had. The box was a proper coffin-cart bed, plague-stenciled, smelling of lime, and Sef climbed in with the lantern hugged to her chest like a child and lay down among the sacking and let them fold the plague-flag up over her face.

"If they open it anyway," she said through the cloth, "they'll see a dead girl holding a lamp, and they'll feel foolish, and while they're feeling foolish Father Anselm can hit them with the shovel."

"There is no shovel," Anselm said.

"There's a shovel."

"There is absolutely—" Anselm lifted the sacking, looked, and sighed. "There is a shovel. Why is there a shovel?"

"Because I put it there," Sef said. "Keepers plan. It's the one thing I like about it."

Petra rode up front with Hobb. Anselm rode in the back with the coffin, which he declared an old professional habit — *I have spent years preaching to empty pews; a box with one living girl in it is practically a congregation* — and then the wagon rolled out of the yard into streets that smelled of smoke and wet ash, because the city had begun, in its fear, to do the sensible terrible thing: it was raining, lightly, and nobody was putting the fires out.

The Cathedral of the Ninefold Bell was burning as they passed.

It went up slowly, majestically, the nine bells tolling as the heat warped them — each one giving off a different broken note, so that the whole ruin rang like a struck chord that refused to resolve. People stood in the square watching, hundreds of them, holding candles they had apparently brought to burn *with* the cathedral rather than against it, and Petra saw a woman kneel in the gutter and pray aloud, hands pressed to her mouth, and saw the prayer visibly fail to be answered, and saw the woman keep praying anyway, harder, the way you push on a door you already know is locked.

"Where are the answers going?" Petra asked, without meaning to say it aloud.

Hobb glanced at her. "What?"

"Nothing." But she felt it, riding in her chest alongside the slow tidal breathing from the coffin behind her: the prayers weren't stopping. All across the burning city people were praying harder than they had in a generation, and the gods were dying, and the Spire was east and humming, and somewhere between the mouths of the faithful and the black tower in the fens there was a channel running with light, and it was narrowing, and everyone could feel it narrowing, the way you feel weather change in an old wound.

At the leper gate the watch didn't even raise a lamp. A boy of maybe sixteen waved them through with his sleeve over his nose, shouting *plague cart, move, move*, and Petra looked at his young frightened face and thought of Maren's forgetting-dust in its vial against her breastbone, and was grateful, and hated the gratitude.

Then the toll bridge. Then the checkpoint.

There were six soldiers at the toll bridge, and a brazier, and an officer with Vane's new edicts nailed to a post beside him, and Hobb slowed the wagon the way a man slows when slowing is the law, and Petra tied her luck-knots tight and made herself breathe like someone bored.

"Papers," the officer said.

Hobb produced them. Burial guild seal, plague-route stamp, the whole careful fiction. The officer read by brazier-light, and behind him one of the soldiers had drifted toward the back of the wagon, toward the coffin, drawn perhaps by the smell of lime or by the dumb animal instinct that something in this cart was warmer than it should be.

"No one opens plague boxes," Hobb said. It wasn't a plea. It was a statement delivered with the magnificent boredom of a man who has driven corpses through six cities' panics. "Guild rules. You want what's in there, you can ride to the pit with it."

The soldier had reached the tailboard. His hand rose—

—and the lantern inside the box, muffled under flag and sacking, chose that moment to breathe.

Not flare. Breathe. A single pulse of heat like a heart beating once under a floorboard, and the soldier snatched his hand back as if the wood had bitten him, and crossed himself, and stepped away, and would not look at the wagon again. Petra watched him do it. Watched superstition do in one heartbeat what her charms spent five years pretending to do.

The officer stamped the papers. "Move along."

The wheels took the bridge planks one by one, and Greyhollow fell away behind them — the burning cathedral, the ringing bells, the kneeling woman in the gutter, Mrs. Corren and Tam Wicker and every face doing its arithmetic — and Petra did not look back, because looking back was a luxury shaped exactly like turning around, and she had chosen exile before exile chose her, and chosen things are not revisited at bridges.

* * *

They put her down at the crossroads an hour before dawn, where the fen road went east into country the maps had given up on.

Hobb unloaded them in silence, set the shovel down in the mud with exaggerated care, touched his forehead to Petra — an old gesture, half apology, half blessing — and turned the wagon north toward the pits and his ruined quiet life. They watched his lamps shrink and vanish. Then there were three of them, and a coffin, and a lantern, and a road.

Sef climbed out of the box stiff-legged, hauling the lantern, and stood looking east. The fens began a mile off — a darkness darker than the night, stitched with the last stars, and somewhere far out in that darkness, so faint it might have been imagination, a hum. Low. Patient. Getting louder the way a name gets louder when you realize someone is calling it.

"It knows we're coming," Sef said.

"Buildings don't know things," Anselm said, without conviction.

"This one does." Sef lifted the lantern. Inside, Edmund's heart-flame had changed — not brighter, but *straining*, leaning east like a plant toward a window, throwing shadows that pointed the way home. "It's been leaning like that since the bridge. Like it smells the rest of itself."

Petra shouldered her pack. The spellbook rode against her spine, five years unopened, heavier than paper had any right to be. Somewhere behind them Greyhollow's bells were still ringing themselves, and somewhere ahead the fens waited with their unmapped villages and their forgotten names, and somewhere in between — she could feel it, the way you feel a storm front — something was watching the road.

Anselm struck a light for the lantern-hook on the cart they'd bought from Hobb's cousin, a handcart barely fit for the coffin, and the three of them took up the traces like the world's smallest and strangest funeral procession.

They had walked perhaps a hundred yards when Sef stopped.

"Listen," she said.

Petra listened. Wind in the reeds. The hum, eastward, steady. And beneath it — faint, wrong, coming from the village they'd just left behind them at the crossroads — a sound she had heard once before, five years ago, on the night she ran: the sound of a bell ringing in a place where there was no bell.

Behind them, the crossroads chapel — abandoned thirty years, roofless, a ruin children dared each other to enter — was ringing.

Ahead, the hum of the Spire stuttered, once, like a heartbeat skipping.

"It knows we're coming," Sef said again, softly. "But I don't think," she lifted the lantern, and the flame inside leaned east, and trembled, "it's the only thing that knows."

Petra tightened her grip on the cart's handle, and they walked on into the dark.
