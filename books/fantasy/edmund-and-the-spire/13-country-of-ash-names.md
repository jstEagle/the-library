## 13. Country of Ash Names

Anselm had spent thirty years learning the shape of his country from inside a church, and he had thought himself a man who knew maps.

He had been wrong about most things — about the gods, about his own vocation, about the particular smell of incense that turns to rot when a congregation stops believing — but he had never doubted the maps. The kingdom was a body and he knew its bones: the Vell Road running north from Greyhollow like a spine, the river towns strung along it like vertebrae, the chapel at Harrow's Ford where he had preached his last sermon to pews full of people who would not look at him. He knew the distances between places the way a priest knows the intervals between bells. You could close your eyes and walk the country in your head.

Three days out of Greyhollow, the country began to disagree with him.

* * *

It started small, as dissolutions do. A milestone on the Vell Road with no inscription at all — not worn away, not chiseled off, but simply blank, the stone smooth as an egg, as if the name had never been cut into it. Anselm ran his thumb across it twice, then a third time, telling himself it was erosion, telling himself that stone forgets letters the way old men forget faces.

Sef crouched beside him with the iron lantern balanced on her knee. Inside it Edmund's heart-flame burned low and steady, a coal-colored light that threw no shadows so much as it *withdrew* them. She had carried the lantern for three days without once setting it down longer than she had to, and her arms had gone thin and trembling with the weight of it, and she had not complained, because complaining was not in her. She looked at the milestone the way she looked at everything now: with the flat, assessing gaze of someone whose whole inheritance had turned out to be a dying animal in a cellar.

"It's not weathered," she said. "Weather leaves edges."

"I know what weather leaves."

"Do you? You're a priest. I'd have thought you'd be glad of a blank stone. Nothing to pray at." But there was no cruelty in it. Sef said cruel things the way other people breathed; it cost her nothing and meant nothing, and Anselm had learned in three days of road that her sharpness was only the shape her fear took.

Petra came up the road behind them, leading the mule, and stopped when she saw them gathered around the stone. She looked tired in a way that sleep did not touch. Three days of walking, and every night she sat apart with Maren's spellbook closed on her knees like something she was guarding and something that was guarding her, and every morning she rose before the others and stood facing north with her hands folded as if in prayer, though Anselm had watched her long enough to know she was not praying. She was listening.

"Which village is this?" Petra asked.

Anselm opened his mouth to say *Harrow's Ford*, because the chapel bell-tower should have been visible from here, because the ford was half a mile east and the village straggled along it, because he had baptized children in that church and buried their grandparents and known, in the way a priest knows his parish, every crooked lane —

And found nothing.

Not a fog over the memory. Not a word on the tip of the tongue. Nothing, the way a socket is nothing after the tooth is pulled. He reached for *Harrow's Ford* and his mind passed through the space where it should have been and kept going, arriving nowhere, and the absence had a texture to it, a smoothness, exactly like the blank face of the milestone.

"There was a village," he said slowly. "East. There was a river crossing. There was a church with a leaning tower and a crack in the bell." He could feel those facts like objects in a dark room, real but unlit, belonging to no one. "I can't tell you its name."

"You preached there," Petra said quietly. "You told me. Your last sermon."

"I know I did." His voice came out strange, flattened. "I remember the sermon. I remember the faces. I cannot remember the name of the place where they were sitting." He pressed his palm flat against the blank stone. It was cold in the ordinary way of stone, which was somehow worse than if it had been cold in some other way. "I have walked past this milestone my whole life. I have read this name a thousand times."

The lantern flickered. All three of them looked at it. Edmund's flame bent toward the milestone — toward the *east*, toward the space where the village should have been — the way a candle bends toward a draft, or a dog toward a grave.

Sef stood up. "We should keep moving," she said, and her voice was very controlled, and Anselm understood that she had understood.

* * *

They came to the village at dusk, because the road insisted on it even if the maps did not.

That was the first wrongness: the road went there. The Vell Road curved east, obediently, confidently, down a slope toward a cluster of rooftops and chimney smoke, as if the place were on every map ever drawn. And yet Anselm knew — with the same smooth, toothless certainty — that no map in the Royal Cartographer's office in Greyhollow showed a village here. He had studied maps the way other men studied scripture. There was nothing here. There had never been anything here. And here it was.

It was a poor village, the kind that exists at the mercy of its soil: a dozen houses, a well, a shrine at the crossroads with a rain-worn figure that might have been any god or none. Smoke rose from chimneys. Goats muttered behind fences. A woman was drawing water in the blue hour, the bucket-chain clanking its slow rhythm, and the sound was so ordinary that Anselm felt his chest loosen with something like relief.

Then the woman looked up at them, and smiled, and asked, "Are you come about the names?"

Petra exchanged a glance with Anselm. "What names?" Petra said.

"The ones we lost." The woman set down her bucket. She was perhaps fifty, wind-browned, with the capable hands of someone who had done the same work ten thousand times. "Started last spring, my husband says. Or maybe two years. He says things started going missing and I say he's wrong, it was me that noticed first, but—" she frowned, and the frown had the quality of a person reaching into a pocket and finding it empty, "—I don't recall which of us it was, now. One of us. Anyway." She waved the confusion away like smoke. "Names go first. Then the rest follows. You'll want to see the shrine."

"Why would we want to see the shrine?" Anselm asked.

"Because you're the first strangers in—" again the empty pocket, the reaching, the nothing, "—in some time. And strangers always want to see the shrine." She picked up her bucket as if the conversation had concluded itself satisfactorily, and walked toward her house, and Anselm noticed that she did not look back at them, and that her footprints in the road-dust seemed shallower than they should have been, though he told himself that was the light.

At the crossroads shrine, the rain-worn figure stood in its little alcove, and beneath it, where villages traditionally carved the god's name, the stone was blank. Smooth as an egg.

But scratched into the alcove walls, in dozens of different hands, in charcoal and knife-point and berry-juice and one line that gleamed faintly silver as if written with a nail dipped in moonlight, were words:

*I lived at the foot of the mill hill.*

*My daughter is called— my daughter is called—*

*Bell rings at noon. Bell rings at noon. Bell rings at noon.*

*We are the village of— we are the village of—*

Dozens of attempts. Dozens of failures. The same sentences begun and abandoned, begun and abandoned, each hand trying to hold a name the way you cup water, and the water going through the fingers regardless.

"They write themselves down," Sef said. Her voice had gone very quiet. She was holding the lantern up so its light fell into the alcove, and the flame leaned toward the inscriptions, reading them, or mourning them. "They can feel it going. So they scratch it into the stone before it goes. And then—" she touched the wall below *my daughter is called—* and her finger came away grey with old charcoal, "—then they forget they wrote it. And they write it again."

"How many times has this same sentence been carved?" Anselm asked, though he could count.

"Eleven," said Sef, who had counted faster.

Petra had not spoken. She stood with her hand resting on the mule's neck, and Anselm watched her read the shrine the way she read everything — sideways, warily, as if the world were a page in a book that might bite. Then she stepped forward and laid her palm against the blank stone beneath the figure, and closed her eyes, and Anselm saw her lips move without sound, and the hair on his arms rose, because for one moment — one breath — the blank stone under her hand seemed to *lean* into her touch, the way a starving thing leans toward warmth.

She snatched her hand back.

"It's still in there," she said. "Whatever it was. The god. It's not dead. It's—" she searched for the word and hated the word when she found it, he could see that, "—it's *thinning*. Like broth stretched too far. Like a voice at the end of its breath."

"That's not possible," Anselm said. "Gods don't thin. Gods are. That is the entire definition of a god. That is the one thing they are *for*—" and he heard his own voice climb, heard the seminary in it, thirty years of doctrine rising like floodwater, and made himself stop. Made himself breathe. "Forgive me. Continue."

"There's nothing to continue." Petra was looking at the shrine with an expression he was beginning to recognize: the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want anyone to see. "Except that whatever is happening here started before the bells rang in Greyhollow. Before the prayers started being answered. This village has been forgetting since spring, you said. Last spring. Two years." She turned to him. "Father Anselm. What came first? The gods going silent — or this?"

He had no answer. He had a sermon-shaped hole where the answer should have been.

* * *

They sheltered that night in the village, because the villagers insisted, and because the alternative was the open road and the open road had begun to feel like walking on the skin of something that might flinch.

The village had an inn, or a building that served as one, and the innkeeper fed them barley soup and dark bread and asked them questions that circled and circled without landing. Where are you from — Greyhollow, they said, and the innkeeper nodded, and Anselm watched him try to hold the word *Greyhollow* overnight, the way you'd watch a man carry water in a sieve. Where are you going — north, they said, and the innkeeper nodded again, and asked north of *where*, and Petra said, toward the Spire, and the room went silent in a way that told Anselm everyone in it knew the word and no one in it could say why.

"The Spire," the innkeeper repeated, tasting it. "There's a song about the Spire. My grandmother sang it." He frowned. "Or my mother. Someone sang it. It went—" and he hummed four notes, and the notes were lovely and incomplete, a phrase that reached for its resolution and never arrived, and Anselm realized with a cold sliding feeling that the man was humming it exactly as he had received it: broken. A song with its ending forgotten. Perhaps the ending had been forgotten a generation ago. Perhaps the ending had been forgotten by the grandmother, who had learned it already-broken from *her* grandmother, and so on backward, a chain of broken songs stretching back through time to some moment when the country had begun, note by note, verse by verse, to misplace itself.

Later, by the fire, while Sef slept sitting upright with the lantern chained to her wrist — she would not let it out of arm's reach, and Anselm did not ask her to — Petra sat down beside him and spoke without preamble, which was her way.

"You felt it today. At the milestone."

"Yes."

"When you reached for the name. Describe it."

He considered lying to her, decided it was pointless — she had a hedge-witch's nose for lies the way he supposedly had a nose for sin — and described it honestly. "Like a socket. Like a tooth pulled. The memory isn't hidden. It isn't forgotten the way men forget, with the thing still buried underneath. It's *gone*. The space where it was is smooth." He looked into the fire. "I have forgotten things before, Petra. My mother's face, God forgive me, I couldn't tell you her face now if my life depended on it. But forgetting a face is like a room going dark. This was like the room being *unbuilt*. Walls, floor, the lot. No darkness. Just absence with good carpentry."

"And the village?"

"The same. Worse. They've lost more than a name. Did you talk to the miller's boy? He told me his father used to sing while he worked, and now the father stands at the millstone in silence, and the boy can't remember whether the silence is new. He can't remember the singing. He knows there *was* singing — someone told him, or he wrote it down somewhere — but it's hearsay to him. His own father's voice is hearsay." Anselm turned his hands toward the fire. They were an old man's hands. When had they become an old man's hands? "A country is not its stones, Petra. I learned that in seminary and never believed it. A country is its names. Its songs. The number of steps to the well. Which neighbor owes which neighbor what grudge. Take those away and the stones remain but the country—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the blank milestone, the blank shrine, the humming innkeeper, "—the country is a rumor told about stones."

Petra was quiet a long moment. Firelight moved on her face.

"In Greyhollow," she said finally, "when the bells rang themselves, you told me the gods were waking up. You said it like it was good news. You crossed yourself."

"I did."

"Were you wrong?"

Anselm laughed, short and bitter, an old habit. "I'm a defrocked priest, child. Being wrong is my entire biography." But then, because she deserved better than wit, because the fire was warm and the night outside the window was full of unnameable dark, he gave her the truth instead. "I think the gods are not waking. I think they are *drowning*. I think whatever is eating this country eats from the bottom up — from the names, from the small gods of wells and thresholds and mill-hills, upward, course by course, the way fire eats a house. And I think the great gods, the temple gods, the ones with bells — I think what we heard in Greyhollow was them shouting. Because drowning men shout. Because they can feel the water rising and no one will answer them, and so they seize the first rope that touches their hand, even if the rope is attached to a fever-charm sold by a frightened girl in a market stall. Even if the prayer comes back wrong. Drowned men are not careful, Petra. They grab."

"And the Spire?"

"Is either the shore," Anselm said, "or the boat. And I confess I no longer know which I'm praying for."

Across the room, the lantern's flame stood up straighter, all on its own, and burned a shade whiter, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it would have required deciding what it meant, and neither of them was ready.

* * *

In the morning, the village was smaller.

Not ruined — nothing so dramatic as ruin. Simply smaller. Two houses that had stood at the village's western edge yesterday were not burned, not collapsed, not removed. They were simply not *there*, and the space where they had been was grass, old grass, deep-rooted grass, grass that had clearly grown there for years, and a goat grazed on it unconcerned, and the goat's owner — the woman with the bucket, the one who had asked if they'd come about the names — stood looking at the grass with an expression of mild, unfocused puzzlement, like a woman trying to recall why she had come into a room.

"My goats liked that corner," she said, to no one in particular. To the air. To Anselm, who happened to be standing nearest. "Sheltered from the north wind, that corner."

"There were houses there," Anselm said carefully.

"Were there?" She considered this. She did not argue. That was the terrible part, he thought later — she did not argue, did not insist, did not defend her own memory against his. She simply weighed his statement and her own blankness in opposite hands, and both hands came up empty, and she shrugged. "Well. Grass grows where it likes." And she walked away, calling her goats, and Anselm stood in the deep old grass of a place where houses had stood yesterday, and felt the ground tilt under a truth he had been circling for three days.

It was not that the village was being destroyed. Destruction left edges. Weather left edges — Sef had said it about the milestone and she had been right about everything. Destruction left ash, rubble, grief, survivors with stories. This left *smoothness*. This left grass that had always been grass. This left a woman who could not mourn because she could not remember there was anything to mourn.

The country was not dying. Dying was what living things did, and left corpses, and corpses could be buried and wept over. This was something else. This was the country being *forgotten* — erased not by armies or plague or the wrath of heaven but by omission, undone the way a word is undone when the last mouth that knows it closes. Somewhere, somehow, the remembering had stopped. And a thing that no one remembers does not cease to exist dramatically, with trumpets. It simply becomes the grass. It simply becomes the smooth stone. It simply becomes a socket where a tooth was, with such fine carpentry that even the ache is sanded away.

He found Petra at the crossroads shrine, packing the mule. She had chalk in her hand — he saw it, a stub of white chalk such as charm-sellers use for marking prices — and she was writing on the blank stone beneath the rain-worn god, in a clear careful hand, the words:

*This village had a name. Ask the road. Ask the river. Ask the grass.*

Not the name itself. She couldn't give the name; no one could; the name was gone from every mouth and mind within a hundred miles. But she could leave a splinter in the world's memory. A hook. A *question*, carved where a fact used to be, so that some future traveler — some cartographer, some priest, some child with a curious finger — might stop and ask, and asking is the first act of remembering, and remembering is the first act of—

Of resistance, Anselm thought. Of war. Because that was what this was, suddenly, unmistakably: not a journey but a campaign, not flight but a fighting retreat along a front that no army could hold, because the enemy was not at the border. The enemy was in the mouths of the people. The enemy was the silence where a song's ending used to be.

"Sef's ready," Petra said, not turning around. "Edmund's flame leaned north all night. Toward the Spire. It knows the way even if we don't." She tucked the chalk away and finally looked at him, and her face in the morning light was grim and young and absolutely resolved. "How far to the next town on your maps, Father?"

Anselm opened his mouth. Closed it. Reached for the name of the next town north on the Vell Road — a market town, a real town, he had drunk wine there, he had heard confession there, it had a bridge with seventeen arches, he was sure of the arches, he was nearly sure of the arches —

The socket. The smoothness. The grass.

"I don't know," he said, and the words came out of him like teeth. "I don't know its name. I don't know if it's there. I don't know if it was ever there." He looked north up the road, which ran straight and confident toward a horizon with no smoke on it, no bells, no evidence of any human hand, and heard himself say the thing that had been true for three days and unsayable until now:

"We are not traveling through a dying country, Petra. We are traveling through a country that is being *unremembered* — and we may be the last people alive who know enough to miss it."

Behind them, in the lantern on Sef's wrist, Edmund's heart-flame burned white and steady, leaning north like a compass needle, like a pointing finger, like the last lit window in a house going dark room by room.

And far up the road, at the very edge of sight, where the morning mist still stood, something tall and black and patient waited — and hummed.
