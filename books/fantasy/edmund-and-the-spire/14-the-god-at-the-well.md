## 14. The God at the Well

The well stood where the crossroads should have been.

That was the first wrong thing. Petra had been counting crossroads the way other travelers counted miles — each one a small proof that the country still agreed with itself, that roads still met roads and went on together. But three hours past the last village whose name Sef could half-remember, the cart track simply stopped agreeing, frayed out into heather and old stone, and there in the middle of the nothing sat a well. Round-mouthed, waist-high, its coping stones worn smooth as knuckles. A roof of slate over it, sagging. A bucket on a rope gone green with moss.

No village around it. No foundations, no midden heap, not so much as a fence post to say *people once drew water here*. Just the well, alone in the heather, patient as a held breath.

"It's dry," Sef said, before anyone had touched it. She'd dismounted and leaned over the lip, and now she straightened up wiping her hands on her trousers as if she'd found something unclean. "I can feel it from here. Dry as a dead fire."

"Then why does it have a roof?" Petra asked quietly.

Anselm had gone still beside the cart. He was looking at the well the way he looked at altars — which was to say, the way a man looks at the place where something used to happen and might, against all sense, happen again. "Roofs are maintenance," he said. "Maintenance is love, in the grammar of old things. Somebody loved this well enough to keep the rain off it. And then somebody kept loving it after everyone else had forgotten there was anything here to love." He crossed himself, an old habit, then seemed embarrassed by it. "Or something did."

Petra walked closer. The afternoon light was the color of weak tea, strained through cloud, and the heather hissed softly in a wind that never quite became a breeze. Up close she could see the well's stones were marked — not carved, exactly, but rubbed. Generations of fingers, or one finger for generations, tracing the same small spiral at the same spot until the groove went deep enough to hold rainwater. Someone had prayed here. Many someones. For a long time.

She put her palm flat on the coping stone.

The cold that came up through her hand was not stone-cold. It was the cold of a cellar, of a river bottom, of the underside of ice — and beneath the cold, faint as a pulse in a wrist, something moved. Something *leaned* into her touch the way a starving animal leans into a hand, all desperate weight and no dignity left.

Petra snatched her hand back. The charm-bag at her belt — the little drawstring pouch where she kept her working knots, her luck-cords, the tools of the trade she'd built out of five years of careful smallness — grew warm against her hip. Warm and attentive, like a dog that has heard its name.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're still *here*."

"Petra?" Anselm had come around the well fast, one hand out, ready — she noticed — to pull her back or to pray, whichever proved necessary. Behind him Sef had taken up the iron poker they carried for the lantern's sake, holding it low like someone who knew exactly what iron was for. "What is it? What do you feel?"

"Not a ghost." Her mouth had gone dry. "Ghosts are echoes. This isn't an echo. It's—" She searched for the word and hated every word she found. "It's *small*. It's so much smaller than it wants to be."

The wind dropped. All at once, completely, the way a door closes.

And the well spoke.

Not with a voice, not exactly. The sound came up the shaft like breath through a pipe — a wet, hollow rushing, and inside the rushing, words, the way you can hear words inside a shell if you want to badly enough:

***thirsty***

Sef made a sound low in her throat. Anselm went to his knees without deciding to, his body remembering worship faster than his mind could veto it. Petra stayed standing, both hands pressed to the stone now, because letting go felt like abandoning something mid-drowning.

"I hear you," she said. And then, because five years of selling charms to people who pretended not to believe had taught her the liturgy of the skeptical heart, she heard herself add: "*I believe you.*"

The well wept. There was no other word for it. Condensation bloomed across the mossy stones, beaded, ran down in slow tracks, and pooled at Petra's feet in a spreading dark stain shaped — she would swear this later, to Anselm, who did not laugh — shaped like a kneeling person with their arms spread wide.

***dry so long*** the shaft breathed. ***so long. they came with copper cups. they sang at midsummer. a bride threw her ribbon down. i gave and gave and gave and then—*** the breath caught, hitched, thinned to almost nothing ***—then no one. no one came. and when no one comes, what is a god? a hole in the ground with opinions.***

Petra's eyes stung. She thought of Maren's kitchen, of the lesson that had frightened her more than any spell ever had: *Magic is attention, girl. Every god is just attention that learned to answer back. Starve the attention and the answering goes strange.* She had thought it a warning about hubris. Now, palms bleeding cold into a nameless well in a country forgetting itself, she understood it was a description of grief.

"What happened to your people?" she asked gently. "Your village?"

A long silence, full of the tick and drip of condensation. Then, with terrible mildness:

***what village?***

*

They made camp within sight of the well, because none of them could bear to leave it, though none of them said so. Anselm built a small fire behind a windbreak of stones. Sef unhitched the mule and brushed her down with the grim tenderness she usually saved for Edmund's scales. Petra sat on the well's coping with her boots dangling over the dark and talked to the thing inside it while the tea went cold in her cup.

Its name, when she finally coaxed it out, was barely a name. *Wella. Wyll. The Wet One. Grandmother Under-the-Stone* — the titles shifted like water finding its level, and Petra suspected the god itself no longer knew which was truest. It had been a minor power even in its prime: keeper of one village's water, granter of clear skin and easy births and rain in proper season, patron of the particular holiness of cold water on a hot day. It had never wanted more. That, it told her with something like pride, was why it had lasted this long. *The loud ones went first,* it breathed. *The storm gods, the war gods, the ones the kings built temples to. Loud gods burn bright and get eaten by the forgetting. I was small. I was easy to overlook. Even oblivion has bad eyes.*

"But not bad enough," Petra said.

***not bad enough.*** A ripple moved through the empty shaft, a sound like a sigh underwater. ***the country is going out like a lamp, witch-girl. wick first, then the flame, then the smell of the flame, then even the memory of warmth. i have watched whole parishes go. good people, walking around, tending pigs, kissing their children — and underneath, thinning. their prayers got shorter and shorter. do you know what a prayer is, from down here? it's a rope. every prayer is a rope thrown down a well. and when the ropes stop coming, you don't die. you just... keep falling.***

Sef had drifted over, poker across her knees, listening the way she listened to everything now — hungrily, suspiciously, like a girl relearning a language she'd been taught in grief. "Our village," she said abruptly. "Ashenford, it was called. Was. We passed where it should be yesterday and there was nothing. Not ruins. Nothing. How long?"

***how long since anyone prayed there?*** The question came back soft and merciless. ***that's the only clock there is, keeper's child.***

Sef looked away, jaw tight, and said nothing else for a long time.

Anselm fed the fire another stick. "In my last parish," he said to the flames, in the tone of a man confessing to a god because it was easier than confessing to himself, "we had a font. Old stone, older than the church around it. When I was defrocked they let me take one thing and I took a handful of water from it, and I carried that vial for six years telling myself it was spite, a souvenir of what they took from me." He turned the stick in the fire. "It wasn't spite. I think part of me knew I was carrying the last of something. The way you carry soil from a grave." He glanced up at Petra, and in the firelight his face looked older than his years and younger than his sorrows. "You're going to give it something, aren't you. I've watched you for two days. You have your merchant face on and under it you're doing sums."

Petra laughed, short and unhappy. "Am I so obvious?"

"To me," Anselm said. "I spent twenty years watching poor women decide whether they could afford the collection plate. You're wearing exactly that face."

She was. Because the well had asked — had been asking since she first laid her palm on the stone, politely, desperately, the way beggars ask who remember being givers — and she had been turning over her pockets in her mind ever since, and everything she owned was either needed or already promised, except one thing.

Her luck.

Not luck in the vague sense — she'd never had much of that. Luck in the literal sense: the slow accumulation of a charm-business worked honestly for five years. Every luck-knot sold carried a thread of it; every knot returned a little interest when its buyer prospered. It was the quiet capital of her trade, the reason her charms worked better than Tam Wicker's two streets over, the margin that had let her eat through bad winters and buy goodwill from neighbors who pretended not to know what she was. She had guarded it the way misers guard gold and mothers guard children. It was, she realized, sitting on the cold stone in the failing light, the closest thing she had to a hoard. To a nest. To the careful life itself, distilled.

If she poured it out, she would not starve. But her knots would be ordinary. Her fever-charms would take longer, fail sooner. She would become, overnight, merely skilled — a woman with a craft instead of a woman with a covenant. Five years of careful building, spent in an afternoon.

The well breathed up at her, patient, ashamed of its own hunger.

***you don't have to,*** it said, and the gentleness of it broke her worse than begging would have. ***i heard your thoughts, girl, i'm sorry, walls are thin when you're this thin yourself. you don't owe me. nobody owes a hole in the ground.***

"That," Petra said, "is exactly what's wrong with this country."

She slid off the coping and knelt, and unlaced the charm-bag.

*

Anselm stood watch at the edge of the firelight — not because he expected trouble, he said later, but because he could not bear to watch a sacrament he hadn't been invited into. Sef stayed close, because Sef had decided somewhere on the road that where Petra went, she went; the granddaughter of dragon-keepers knew a deathbed rite forming when she saw one, even a small one.

Petra turned the bag out onto a cloth.

Most of it was cord — knotted lengths of hemp and wool and, for the expensive customers, silk, each knot tied in the patterns Maren had taught her and she had refined into something entirely her own. But at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in waxed linen, lay the heart of it: a coin. Not gold, not silver. Lead, worn smooth, stamped with a sigil she had cut herself her first winter in Greyhollow with Maren's graver and Maren's stolen knowledge and hands that would not stop shaking. The luck of the business lived in that coin the way a flame lives in a coal bed. Every knot she tied, she touched the coin first. Every morning for five years.

It was warm in her palm now, warmer than the night justified, and it pulsed faintly — recognition, or protest, or both.

"You understand what I'm giving you," she said to the well.

***i understand it's yours.***

"And you understand I'll want receipts." She tried to make it a joke and mostly failed. "Information. You've been down here through the whole — the going-out. You've felt the shape of it from underneath, in a way we can't from the surface. Tell me what you know about the Spire."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The temperature around the well dropped several degrees, and the fire behind them bent sideways though the air didn't move.

***you've seen it,*** the god breathed, and it wasn't a question. ***or you're going to. the tall dark one. the tooth in the valley.*** A tremor ran through the shaft, fear or awe or the memory of both. ***every god left knows about the spire, witch-girl. it's the only thing left we all agree on. it hums, and when it hums, we feel ourselves thin. it eats what we're made of.***

"Eats prayers?"

***eats what prayers are *for*.*** The words came slowly now, dragged up from somewhere deep, a thing that had never been said aloud before and knew it. ***listen. a god is a bargain. we take their wanting and we give back answers, and the wanting flows through us like water through a channel, and that flow is our food and our shape and our name. but the bargains ended. the channels closed. and all that wanting — all that human wanting, which doesn't stop, which never stops, they still want rain and health and their dead husbands breathing — it has to go somewhere.*** The pause stretched. Somewhere in the heather a bird cried once and thought better of it. ***it goes to the spire. it's been going to the spire for a generation. every unanswered prayer in this country runs downhill into that tower like floodwater into a cellar. that's the humming. that's what you heard in the bells. it's not a god, girl, it's a *reservoir*. it's everything we used to be, backed up with nowhere to go.***

Anselm had abandoned his watch and come near, firelight jumping on his face. "The answered prayers," he said hoarsely. "The widow's husband. The fields. The bells. If the Spire is a reservoir—"

***a dammed river bursts,** the well said simply. **the pressure got too high. it started answering. it answers like a drowning man grabs — anything, everything, wrongly. those poor souls getting their prayers come true, they're not being blessed. they're being splashed.***

Sef's knuckles were white around the poker. "And the door. The door out. Anselm says the Spire's a door too. How can it be a reservoir *and* a door?"

***because it's dying too, keeper's child, and a dying thing will spend its hoard on one last miracle.*** The voice dropped lower, and Petra felt the words land on her like stones lowered into her chest, one by one. ***but doors don't open on pressure. doors open on payment. that's the oldest grammar there is — older than gods, older than wells. nothing opens without something being given. freely. willingly. the spire doesn't feed on sacrifice the way kings feed on taxes. it feeds on sacrifice the way a lock feeds on a key. the giving IS the turning.***

"Willingly," Anselm repeated. His voice had gone very quiet. "Not coerced. Not bought. Given."

***given knowing the price,*** the well said. ***given anyway. that's the coin it accepts. that's the only coin it accepts. everything else is counterfeit.***

Petra looked down at the lead coin in her palm. Five years of careful smallness, warm as a living thing. She thought about how she'd built her life the way you build a wall against weather — course by course, mortar by mortar, never beautiful, only sound. She thought about how the wall was already cracking, how the cracks had started the day a child's fever-charm actually worked, and how every day since had widened them.

"Well then," she said, to the coin, to the well, to the crack running through everything. "Let's practice."

She held the coin out over the dark mouth of the shaft.

"Grandmother Under-the-Stone," she said formally, because if this was a sacrament it deserved the old forms, and because saying the title aloud made the loss real enough to survive. "Keeper of one village's water, granter of clear skin and easy births and cold drinks on hot days. I'm Petra Underhill, hedge-witch, runaway, seller of luck-knots to people who pretend not to believe. I don't have much. I have this." Her hand shook. She let it. "Take my luck. All of it. The business's and mine — they're the same thing, I see that now, I always saw it, I just liked pretending the wall would hold. Buy yourself a drink. Fill your bones. Be wet again, even for a minute, even just once, so you can remember what you were before you go."

She opened her fingers.

The coin fell.

It fell a long time — far longer than the well was deep, far longer than physics or sense allowed, spinning down through darkness with a sound like a bell heard underwater, and Petra understood that she was not dropping a coin into a hole but pouring a measure into a vessel, and the vessel went down farther than down.

Then the well *answered*.

Water came up like joy. It rose roaring through the shaft, silver-black in the dying light, burst over the coping in a cold fountain that drenched Petra to the skin and kept rising, spilling, singing — actual singing, or the closest water has ever come to it, a chord of rush and ripple and the deep glad gurgle of a throat finally wet. The pool spread around the well in the heather, widening, reflecting stars that weren't out yet, and in its surface Petra saw — just for a heartbeat — a village: smoke from chimneys, a bride's blue ribbon, children's cupped hands, midsummer lanterns floating out across clean dark water.

Then it began to drain. Slowly, gratefully, without complaint — a tide going out that knew the moon's business was done. The singing softened to a murmur, the murmur to dripping, the pool shrank back ring by ring into the earth, and the last of it sank away with the unmistakable sound of a long sigh.

But before the silence closed, the well spoke one final time, and the voice was different — fuller, wetter, briefly young:

***thank you, daughter-of-the-door.***

Petra froze, soaked and shivering. "What did you call me?"

***you heard.*** A bubble of laughter rose through the last inch of water, popped, was gone. ***luck spends true, witch-girl. and now you know the price the spire asks, and now i know what you're carrying, and we're both poorer and both richer, which is the only honest arithmetic there is.*** The dripping slowed. The cold receded. ***go carefully. go *willingly*. and when you stand in front of that hungry tower with its hollow heart — remember the well. remember that the smallest gifts are given the same way as the greatest, and the tower cannot tell the difference, and neither, in the end, can the giver.***

Silence. Real silence, the ordinary kind, the kind with nothing underneath it.

The well was just a well again. Drier than ever, somehow — finished, in the way that a candle that has burned to its socket is finished, having done precisely what it was for.

Petra knelt at its edge a long time, hair dripping, charm-bag suddenly weightless on her belt, feeling the absence where five years of quiet capital had lived. It didn't hurt the way she'd feared. It hurt the way she'd hoped, which was worse.

Sef crouched beside her and, without a word, wrung out the hem of Petra's cloak. Then, still without a word: "Daughter-of-the-door?"

"I don't know what it meant." Petra's voice came out rough. "I don't know yet whether I want to."

Behind them, Anselm cleared his throat. When they turned, he was standing very straight, holding out the fire-tongs like a scepter, wearing the expression of a man who has just remembered what courage costs and is pricing it anyway.

"There's smoke on the northern road," he said. "A lot of it. Too organized for wildfire." His jaw tightened. "Crown purple in the smoke, if my eyes still work. Which means either the inquisitors are burning villages ahead of us—"

He stopped. They all heard it at the same moment, carried thin on the evening wind: hoofbeats. Many. Disciplined. Coming on.

"—or," Anselm finished quietly, "they're burning them behind us, and we're about to meet the men who light the fires."
