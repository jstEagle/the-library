## 15. Vane's Conversion

The road to Hollowmere was the kind of road Inquisitor Vane had been raised to distrust: too narrow for processions, too soft underfoot for marching, lined with hawthorn that leaned in over the riders like gossips leaning over a fence. His escort of six men rode in a clatter of harness and complaint. Vane rode ahead of them in silence, and the silence was his armor, and he wore it well.

He had been three days out of Greyhollow, following reports the way a man follows smoke — never quite reaching the fire, always arriving at the scorch. A village where a fever broke overnight. A field that came up green after a killing frost. A woman who prayed at her daughter's bedside and woke to find the child breathing easy and the sickness gone *somewhere*, though no one could say where, and the girl's left hand had gone cold as river-stone and stayed that way.

Prayers answered. That was the phrase in the reports, written by frightened constables in the careful language of men who did not want to be believed to believe it.

Vane believed it. That was the trouble. He had believed it since the morning every bell in Greyhollow rang itself, since he stood in the temple square with his hand on his sword-hilt and felt the sound go through him like a hand through water. The edicts he posted by noon were true law and good law — magic outlawed, unlicensed workings punishable by fire or rope, all answered prayers to be reported within a day — but he had posted them with ink that trembled, because some part of him, the part he kept locked below the waterline like ballast, had heard those bells and thought: *at last.*

He was forty-one years old. He had been praying since he was seven. Nothing had ever answered.

* * *

Hollowmere sat in a fold of hills where the road forgot itself, thirty houses around a pond the color of old pewter, a church with a roof that sagged like a tired shoulder. It was the kind of place the Crown governed the way weather governs a stone — technically, distantly, without effect. Vane had visited such places before. They lied to him politely, hid their grandmothers' charms behind loose bricks, and wept when he burned what he found, and he told himself each time that the weeping was guilt.

This time, they did not lie.

They were gathered in the churchyard when he rode in — not hiding, not scattering, just standing among the graves in the grey afternoon light, looking at him with an expression he had never been given before in eleven years of service. Not fear. Not defiance. Something worse.

Pity.

An old man stepped forward, hat in hands, joints like knotted rope. "You'll be wanting Widow Sorrel," he said. "She's at the Harrow house. But you should know—" and here the old man faltered, and looked back at his neighbors, and they nodded at him as if passing something heavy along a line, "—you should know she didn't do anything wrong."

"Everyone says that," Vane said. "It is almost never true."

"No," the old man said, and there was no argument in it, only a terrible gentleness. "I don't expect you'll think so. But I'd have it said anyway. She didn't do anything wrong. It wasn't her doing. She only asked."

*Asked.* Vane turned the word over as he dismounted and handed his reins to a boy who took them without meeting his eyes. In his reports, in his training, in the catechism of the Office of Inquiry, prayer was a private weakness and witchcraft was a crime, and the space between them — the space where a desperate woman opened her mouth in the dark and spoke to something that might actually be listening — that space was supposed to be empty. It had been empty for a generation. The gods were silent; the Church said so; the silence was doctrine.

The bells had rung themselves. And now a widow had *asked.*

The Harrow house stood at the village's edge, low and stone-walled, its door open. Inside, on a bed beneath a window, lay a girl of perhaps nine, asleep or something near it, her left hand resting outside the blanket on top of the covers — deliberately placed there, Vane realized, so that everyone who entered would see it. Cold as river-stone. Grey at the fingertips. The price, laid out honestly, where the whole village could look at it.

Beside the bed sat a small round woman with flour on her forearms and eyes red-rimmed from more than one kind of crying. She rose when he entered. She did not curtsy. She did not kneel. She looked at the badge on his chest the way one looks at weather coming in over a hill — assessing, resigned, already calculating what could be carried out of the house.

"You're the inquisitor," she said.

"I am Inquisitor Vane." He heard his own voice do its office-voice, flat and official, and hated it in his own ears. "You are Sorrel, widow, of this parish. You are accused of unlicensed working. You will answer—"

"She was dying." The words came out of the woman simply, without heat, the way one states that water is wet or winter is long. "My girl. Fever, four days, the doctor from Marleford wouldn't come — said the roads weren't safe, which is his way of saying we're poor. On the fourth night her breathing went wrong. Little sounds. Like a bird." Her hands folded together, flour and all. "And I got down on my knees beside this bed, and I did what my mother taught me never to do. I *asked.* Out loud. To anything. To whoever used to listen. I said: take it from her. Take the fever, take whatever you want, take it from her, please, please, please."

"And it did," Vane said quietly.

"It did." She looked at her daughter's grey hand. "But nobody tells you how these things work. Nobody remembers. It took the fever, and it put it somewhere, because nothing just goes away — my mother knew that much, even if she taught me nothing else worth having. It put it in the nearest thing that was hers. Her hand. Just the hand. The rest of her woke up hungry the next morning asking for porridge." For the first time her voice cracked, and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "So. Am I a witch? Is that witchcraft? Kneeling on a floor and begging?"

Under the edict, yes. Under the edict, any petition to any power outside the sanctioned rite was unlicensed working, and unlicensed working was death. Vane knew the wording. He had helped draft the wording. He stood in the little room with its smell of bread and fever-sweat and candle-tallow and found that the wording would not come into his mouth.

"The hand," he said instead. "Will it mend?"

"I don't know. It's been nine days. It doesn't hurt her. She says it feels like it's asleep, except it isn't asleep, it's just... elsewhere." The widow looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes were dry and very tired. "You can burn me if your law needs burning. But do it in the next village, and do it quick, because she'll need someone to make bread."

* * *

That night Vane did not sleep. He sat in the church — the priest had fled or died or simply stopped coming years ago; the vestry held dust and mouse-droppings and a lectern Bible gone soft with damp — and he read the parish register by candlelight, because reading registers was what he did instead of thinking, and had been for years.

The register told him things the reports had not. It told him that Hollowmere had buried fourteen children under ten years of age in the last decade, most of them to fevers, most of them in the cold months. It told him that the widow Sorrel had buried a husband and, eight years ago, a son aged six. It told him, in the cramped faithful handwriting of a country curate long gone, the names of children who had been baptized here, and confirmed here, and married here, and it struck Vane — sitting alone in a dead man's church with a candle guttering — that every one of those souls had been promised, in formal liturgical language, that their prayers were heard.

*Graciously hear us,* the rite said. *Hear us, O Lord, and answer.*

They had said it over dying children for a hundred years, and the children had died, and the doctrine of silence had settled over the land like snow, and everyone had learned to pray the way one talks to the deaf — kindly, hopelessly, for the comfort of moving one's own mouth.

And now the silence had broken, and the first thing to come through it was a fever lifted off a child and crammed into her hand, and the Crown's response was to send men with ropes.

Vane closed the register. He sat for a long time. Somewhere past midnight, without deciding to, without permitting himself to decide to, he knelt on the dusty chancel floor of the empty church, in full view of no one, and did the thing he had not done aloud since he was a boy at his grandmother's knee.

He asked.

Not for anything. Not for promotion, not for his mother's soul, not for the forgiveness he had stopped expecting decades ago. He asked the question underneath all his other questions, the one he carried the way other men carry a wound: *Are you there? Did you ever hear me? Was any of it received?*

The candle flame stood perfectly still. The damp stones breathed their cold. Nothing answered, and Vane knelt in the nothing, and understood that this — this kneeling in the dark with no assurance — was exactly what the widow Sorrel had done, and that the difference between them was not righteousness. It was only that something had finally, catastrophically, begun to listen to *her.*

He got up stiffly, blew out the candle, and went back to the Harrow house to sit watch outside a child's window until dawn, telling himself it was surveillance.

* * *

In the morning, the girl's hand was warm.

Vane was standing in the yard when the scream of joy came from inside — the mother's voice, cracked wide open — and he went in and saw it himself, because he made himself see it, because an inquisitor must verify. The left hand, grey for nine days, was pink. The fingers curled around the mother's thumb. The girl, awake now, wiggled them and laughed and said they'd been *tingling all night, like when your foot wakes up,* and went back to her porridge.

Nine days, and the debt called in and paid, and the account closed clean.

Vane walked out into the churchyard where the villagers had gathered again — they gathered easily here, he was learning; they were people accustomed to standing together in weather — and he faced them, and his escort of six men faced them behind him with the particular boredom of soldiers waiting for an execution.

"Widow Sorrel," Vane said, and his voice carried across the graves, "is cleared of all suspicion. The affliction of the child was natural in origin and has resolved naturally. There is no evidence of unlicensed working in this parish." He paused, and heard the falseness of it, sentence by sentence, each word a small smooth stone laid over something living. "There will be no further inquiry."

The old man with the hat looked at him for a long moment. Then he bowed — not deeply, not servilely, just the bow of one man acknowledging another's burden — and the villagers began to drift away toward their fields and their bread-ovens, and Vane stood among the graves feeling the crack run through him, quiet and structural, the way ice cracks on a pond before anyone hears it.

His sergeant, a blunt reliable man named Corven, fell into step beside him as they saddled. "No arrest, sir?"

"No arrest."

Corven glanced at him sidelong. "The report, then. What do I write?"

Vane looked north, toward Greyhollow, toward the offices where his reports were filed and cross-filed and read by men who measured the kingdom's safety in burnings per season. He thought about the wording. He had always been precise in his wording; precision was the last piety left to him.

"You will write," he said slowly, "that the reported miracle was investigated and found to be rumor compounded of grief and coincidence. That the child's recovery followed the natural course of the fever. That the widow Sorrel is a woman of good character whose prayers were ordinary prayers, and were not answered by anything." He mounted. "You will write that there is nothing here requiring the Office's attention."

Corven wrote it down. Then, carefully, not looking up: "Is that true, sir?"

Vane rode out of Hollowmere with the hawthorn closing over the road behind him, and the honest answer followed him like a bird that would not stop singing:

No. It was not true. Nothing here was true except a child's warm hand and a widow's knees on a cold floor and the vast, terrifying possibility that the old silence had ended — and that he, Edmund Vane, chief inquisitor of the realm, keeper of the edicts, believer policing a miracle, had just begun, with his own careful hand, to bury the evidence of God.

By the time they reached the crossroads at Marleford, he had composed in his head the fuller report he would file himself, in his own script, with his own seal: *Parish of Hollowmere investigated. No unlicensed working found. Recommend removal of the district from the list of active inquiries.*

And beneath it, unwritten, unpublishable, the report he would carry in his chest for the rest of his life: *A woman asked, and was answered, and the answer cost her daughter one cold hand for nine days, and I knelt in a dead church and asked my own question and heard nothing, and I do not know whether that means the gods are not there, or only that they have not yet gotten to me.*

Three days later, back in Greyhollow, a sealed dispatch reached his desk bearing the Crown's mark: new orders, urgent, regarding a hedge-witch named Petra Underhill, last seen selling charms in the market city — charms that, according to the attached testimony of no fewer than eleven witnesses, had recently begun to *work.*

Vane read the name twice. He had seen it before, months ago, in a minor file: a runaway apprentice, unremarkable, presumed harmless.

He took out a fresh sheet of paper, dipped his pen, and began — for the second time that month — to decide what the truth would be allowed to say.
