## 16. Sef's Inheritance

The lantern did not sleep.

Sef had learned that on the first night out of Greyhollow, curled in the straw of a hayloft with Anselm snoring like a landslide two bales away and Petra breathing slow and even in the dark beside her. She had expected the heart-flame to dim when Edmund's body was far behind them, to gutter down to an ember, to behave the way fire behaved — hungry, then quiet, then ash.

It didn't. It burned at exactly the same height it had burned in the cellar beneath the tannery, a thumb-length of white-gold light inside the iron cage, steady as a held breath. And all night, as she lay watching it through the lattice of her fingers, she had felt it *watching back*.

Not in a frightening way. That was the strange part. In the way a grandmother watches you from across a room while you pretend not to notice, warm and patient and faintly amused, waiting for you to come sit by the fire where you belong.

She belonged by the fire. She had always belonged by the fire. That was the whole of her inheritance, and she had spent nineteen years pretending otherwise.

* * *

The fire-pit beneath the tannery was older than the tannery, older than the street above it, older than Greyhollow itself if you believed her grandmother, and Sef believed her grandmother because her grandmother had been the last person alive who remembered why any of it mattered. The pit was a bowl of black stone sunk into the cellar floor, wide as a wagon wheel, its rim carved with spirals that predated every alphabet Sef had ever seen scratched onto a wall. And in the pit, coiled like a heap of cooling slag, had lain Edmund — though no one had called him that in three generations, until the night he opened one amber eye and said his own name aloud to a hedge-witch who smelled of salt charms and running.

Her mother had tended him. Her grandmother before that. And her great-grandmother before *that*, all the way back through a line of women whose names were written in a ledger bound in dragon-hide — a binding Sef had only understood the nature of when she was twelve, and had vomited into the coal scuttle, and had been told, gently, that this too was part of the keeping.

*You will tend him,* her mother had told her, the way other mothers told their daughters they would be married, or widowed, or buried. Not a threat. A weather report. *You were born for it. I felt it the day you came — how you reached for the warmth.*

Sef had been born in the cellar. That was the part nobody outside the family ever knew. Not in a bed, not upstairs among the tanning vats and the stink of lime, but down in the dark, on a pallet dragged within arm's reach of the pit, because her mother would not be parted from Edmund for so much as a labor. The midwife had been paid triple and sworn to silence and had taken the money and the oath and fled the city anyway, which Sef supposed was the sensible response to delivering a child in the presence of a sleeping dragon.

She had grown up measuring her life against his breaths. He breathed perhaps once a minute now, in these last years — a long, grinding inhalation like a millstone turning, a slow exhalation that stirred the coals and made the whole cellar smell of hot iron and something older underneath, something like lightning-struck sand. As a child she had played a game: hold your breath until he finishes his. She always lost. She had never once beaten a dying dragon at the simple business of breathing, and some stubborn, secret part of her had found that comforting. Whatever else failed — whatever the Crown outlawed, whatever the gods went silent over, whatever rotted in the tannery vats above their heads — Edmund kept breathing. The world still had one dragon in it, and she was the reason, and the reason was also a chain.

Because here was the thing her mother had told her at fourteen, sitting on the pit's edge with her feet dangling over the black stone, in the tone of voice other mothers used for explaining where babies come from:

*The keeping kills us, love. It killed my mother. It will kill me. And one day, unless the world ends first, it will kill you.*

Not dramatically. That was almost worse. The fire-pit didn't take keepers in a single burning; it took them the way a river takes a bank — grain by grain, year by year. Tending a dragon meant feeding the pit, and feeding the pit meant feeding it more than coal. Every rite in the hide-bound ledger ended the same way: *and the keeper gives of herself.* A drop of blood on the new moon. A fingernail at the solstice. Hair at the turning of each season, burned in the spiral-carved channels until the stone drank the smoke. Small payments, endlessly collected, and the ledger was very clear about what happened to keepers who fell behind. There was a page near the end, in handwriting gone brown and brittle, describing what her great-grandmother had looked like in her final year: *thin as a wick, and burning just as steadily.* The fire didn't hate you. The fire simply assumed, correctly, that you had offered.

Her mother had died at thirty-nine. Sef had been fifteen. There had been nothing dramatic about that either — no final conflagration, no grand gesture. Just a woman who got thinner and thinner across five winters, whose blood came slower each new moon, until one morning Sef came down the stairs with the breakfast tray and found her mother lying on the pallet with her hands folded on her chest, composed as a saint on a tomb, and Edmund's eye open and fixed on her, and the fire in the pit burning higher than Sef had ever seen it, and understanding arriving all at once and forever: her mother hadn't simply died. She had finished. She had given the last of herself, willingly, the way the ledger required, and the fire had accepted, and the dragon had watched, because there was nothing else for a dying god-beast to do but witness the small mortal holocaust that kept him alive.

Sef had fed the pit alone that day. Sixteen days past her fifteenth birthday. Her hands had shaken so badly she'd cut the pad of her thumb twice instead of once, and the fire had flared at the double offering, greedy and grateful, and she had hated it with her whole body, and had knelt there hating it, and had done the rite anyway, perfectly, because the rites lived in her deeper than her own heartbeat. Her grandmother had drilled them into her before she could read. *Say them angry if you must,* the old woman had rasped, near her own thin end. *Say them weeping. But say them true. The fire knows the difference between a lie and grief, child, and it forgives grief.*

Nobody had ever asked Sef whether she wanted any of it. That was the confession she could never make aloud, the one that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal: she had hated the pit, hated the ledger, hated the spirals and the new moons and the slow arithmetic of her own diminishment — and she had loved Edmund with a ferocity that shamed her, because loving the thing you were chained to felt like a betrayal of every version of yourself that had ever dreamed of running. She used to lie on the pallet where her mother died and imagine walking up the stairs and out the tannery door and into a city where nobody knew her name or her obligations, and the imagining was sweet, and then Edmund would breathe — that vast, grinding, patient breath — and the sweetness would curdle, because she could no more leave him than she could leave her own ribs.

A keeper. Not a keeper's granddaughter. Her mother had lied to her about that, kindly, for years — *you're just helping, love, until you're grown, until you choose* — but the ledger had never lied. Sef had read it cover to cover by lamplight the winter she turned sixteen, hunting for an escape clause, and found instead her own name already entered in the succession, in her mother's hand, dated the day of her birth. *Sef, daughter of Ilset, keeper born in the keeping.* The ink had been dry for fifteen years before she'd known it existed.

So when the bells rang themselves, and the prayers started coming true wrong, and the hedge-witch with the guilty eyes bought scale off her stall and then came back asking questions — when the world cracked open and Edmund spoke his name and asked *her*, of all people, to carry his fire when he went — Sef had said yes before Petra finished asking.

And now, lying in a stranger's hayloft with a god's heart burning in an iron cage a foot from her face, she was discovering something that should have been impossible:

She was free.

* * *

She got up before dawn, because the lantern needed tending — old habit, though the flame needed nothing, had needed nothing since the moment it left Edmund's chest. She knelt on the loft boards and lifted the lantern into her lap and looked into the white-gold light, and the light looked back, and she said, quietly, so as not to wake the others:

"I know what you are. I know what this is."

The flame leaned toward her, the way a cat leans into a known hand.

"Every rite I ever learned was a payment," she told it. "Blood for breath. Years for warmth. My mother paid everything she had and the ledger called it fair." Her throat tightened. "I'm not paying you anymore. Do you understand? If I carry you, it's because I choose to. If I feed you—" she hesitated, then made herself say it, made it true, "—if I give you anything, ever, it'll be a gift. Not a tithe. You can burn me down to a wick if that offends you, but I won't be *spent*. Not again. Not by anyone."

The flame did something she had never seen fire do. It dimmed — not in defeat, not in hunger, but the way a person dims when they're trying not to cry. And then it steadied, brighter than before, and the warmth that came off it was different from the cellar's warmth, less like a debt being collected and more like a hand being held.

"You remember her," Sef said. "My mother. You watched her go."

The flame pulsed once, slow, like a nod.

"She wasn't sad at the end. I need you to know that I know that. She was *finished*, and she was glad, and I have spent four years being furious at her for being glad." Sef's voice cracked and she let it. "But I think — I think maybe she was glad because she chose it. Because somewhere under all the ledger and the law of it, she gave freely, and giving freely is the only thing that doesn't feel like dying." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "I never chose anything until three days ago. Did you know that? Nineteen years, and every hour of it was inherited."

Above her, the loft beams creaked. Petra was awake; Sef could tell by the change in her breathing, the way it went from the deep tide of sleep to the shallow careful rhythm of someone listening and deciding whether to admit it. Sef almost laughed. The witch thought she was the only one in their little company carrying shame around like contraband.

"Come sit, then, if you're going to eavesdrop," Sef said, without turning around. "There's room by the fire. There's always room by the fire. That's the first rule and nobody ever taught it to me — I had to work it out myself."

Petra came. She moved quietly across the boards and settled cross-legged beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the lantern burned between them like a small patient sun.

"My mother trained me for a death," Sef said at last. "The whole of my childhood was preparation for a death — hers first, then mine. Rites and ledgers and how much blood the new moon wants." She turned the lantern slightly, watching the light crawl. "And now I'm carrying the actual death in my hands, walking toward the place where it has to be spent, and it's the first thing I've ever done that nobody ordered me to do. Isn't that mad? Edmund is dying. The country is forgetting itself village by village. And I am happier than I have ever been in my life."

"It isn't mad," Petra said. Her voice was rough with sleep and something else. "It's the difference between a cage with the door locked and a cage with the door open. Same bars. Different animal."

"That's it exactly." Sef looked at her. "You ran from your keeping. I stayed in mine. And we both spent years thinking the other one had it right."

Petra's mouth twisted — not quite a smile, not quite a wound. "Did your book tell you what happens at the Spire? What the door asks?"

"I've read every word Edmund ever let me read, and half of what he wouldn't." Sef held the older woman's gaze. "One passes through. Alone. Yes. I know."

"Then you know I can't—"

"Don't." Sef said it softly, but she said it like a rite, like something that had to be spoken true. "Don't decide for me. Everyone decided for me for nineteen years — my mother, my grandmother, the ledger, the fire. This is the one choice I get to make with clean hands, and I'm telling you now, before the road gets harder, before Vane or the Crown or the end of the world takes the deciding out of ours: when we get there, I'm going to stand in front of that hollow, and I'm going to offer, and you are going to let me." She lifted the lantern. The flame stood tall and white and unafraid. "He was mine to keep. He's mine to spend. That's not duty, Petra. Duty was the ledger. This is the first thing I've ever loved well enough to lose."

Petra didn't answer. She put her arm around Sef's shoulders, awkwardly, the way people hug when they've forgotten how, and Sef let her, and the lantern warmed them both, and neither of them slept again before dawn.

* * *

They walked all that day through country that was starting to frighten Sef in a way the cellar never had.

It began small. A milestone with the place-name chiseled off it — not worn, *chiseled*, deliberately, though by whom and when was anyone's guess. Then a hamlet where the people waved at them cheerfully and none of them, when asked, could say what the hamlet was called, or how long they'd lived there, or which direction the nearest market town lay. They smiled the whole time. That was the worst of it. They smiled like people in a dream who haven't yet noticed they're dreaming.

By late afternoon they crossed a bridge over a stream that flowed backward — gently, without violence, just a patient insistence on running uphill toward its own source — and Anselm stopped in the middle of the span and stood very still with his priest's hands hanging empty at his sides, and Sef saw him mouth something that might have been a prayer or might have been a curse, and decided it was probably both.

"The names go first," he said quietly, when they'd crossed. "That's how it works. I've been reading the inscriptions, the ones I can still translate. A place forgets its name, then its roads, then its neighbors, then itself." He looked back the way they'd come, at the cheerful nameless hamlet already sinking into the haze. "We are walking through a country being erased, and the erasure is moving faster than we are."

Edmund's flame flickered — the first time it had done anything but burn steady since leaving his chest — and Sef felt it against her palms like a pulse quickening. Not fear. Urgency. A dying dragon's last impatience.

"He feels it too," she said. "Whatever's eating the names. He can feel it getting closer."

They made camp that night in the shell of a wayside shrine, its god-statute faceless — not broken, Sef realized, running her fingers over the smooth blank stone, but *unfinished*, as if the sculptor had been interrupted mid-stroke and never returned, as if the memory of that particular god's face had been subtracted from the world and the stone had obediently forgotten along with everyone else. Anselm built a small ordinary fire in the ordinary way, and they ate ordinary bread, and Petra sat apart with Maren's spellbook closed in her lap, staring at it the way you stare at a letter you're afraid to open.

Sef set the lantern between them all, where its light could reach every face.

"We should talk about the checkpoint," she said.

Anselm looked up. "What checkpoint?"

"Saw the smoke this afternoon, west side of the valley. Cook-smoke, too many fires for a farmstead, laid out in lines." She tapped her own nose, the keeper's gesture for *smell and know*. "Soldiers cook in lines. And there's a road down there that's the only crossing over the ravine for twenty miles, and somebody's built a gate across it. I grew up under a tannery, Father. I know what a choke-point smells like."

Petra's head came up slowly from the spellbook. In the lantern-light her face had gone the color of the blank god's stone.

"Maren wrote about checkpoints," she said. "In the margins. Years ago. Things to use when the roads start closing." Her hand rested flat on the book's cover, on the lock that had never once been opened. "There's dust in the back binding. I swore I'd never touch it."

The flame in the lantern leaned toward the book — toward the sealed binding, the forbidden dust, the promise — and burned taller, and whiter, and Sef understood, with a keeper's certainty that needed no ledger, that Edmund's fire recognized what slept in that binding, and was hungry for it, and that the road ahead had just become a choice none of them could put off any longer.

"Well," said Anselm, into the silence. "Tomorrow, then."

"Tomorrow," Sef agreed, and drew the lantern closer, and kept watch.
