## 17. The Inquisitors' Roadblock

The road out of the ash-country narrowed where two hills leaned together like conspirators, and that was where the Crown had built its teeth.

Petra saw it first as a smudge of orange against the grey morning — lantern-light where no village stood — and then as shapes: a timber barrier across the road, a cookfire behind it, and four soldiers in the mud-brown of the Border Wardens, their spears planted crooked in the frozen ruts. Behind them, half-hidden by the hill's shoulder, a pavilion tent flew the grey-and-gold of the Inquisition, and beneath that flag a second banner she hadn't seen since Greyhollow: the new edict-cloth, black, with its single white sentence stitched in thread that caught the light wrong.

*No unlicensed working. No sheltering the same. Name a witch, keep your house.*

"They're checking everyone," Sef said from under the corpse-wagon's canvas, where she crouched with the iron lantern between her knees. The lantern did not glow — Edmund's heart-flame had learned, or been taught, or simply chose, to burn dark — but Petra could feel it the way you feel a sleeping animal in a small room. A warmth with attention inside it. "Checking for what?"

"Us," Anselm said. He had his priest's collar back on, the one he kept folded at the bottom of his pack like a knife he wasn't sure he'd use. "Or not us specifically. Anyone going east. The roads west are emptying, and empty roads make the Crown nervous. They'll want names. Papers. Reasons."

"I have a reason," Sef said. "I'm taking my grandfather to be buried."

Petra looked at her. The girl's face was set in that particular stubbornness Petra had come to know — the look of someone who had decided grief was a tool she was allowed to use.

"That's true enough," Petra said slowly.

"It's true *enough*." Sef patted the coffin slats above her head. The wagon smelled of lime and old straw and, underneath, faintly, of hot iron. "He's in there. What's left of him. I wrapped him myself. Nobody at a checkpoint asks a mourning girl to open her grandfather's shroud. It's bad luck."

"And if they do?"

"Then they'll meet a girl who cries very convincingly," Sef said, "because I won't be pretending."

* * *

They stopped the wagon a quarter mile short, in the shadow of the western hill, and walked the last stretch on foot — Anselm leading the mule, Petra beside him in her widow's grey, Sef driving with the lantern hidden in a basket of grave-linens. The morning was doing its best to be beautiful. Frost furred every fencepost silver; the hills breathed low clouds; somewhere a lark was insisting on spring. Petra hated it. Beautiful mornings made checkpoints worse, because the soldiers manning them were always slightly gentler, slightly more human, and it was harder to hate a man who offered you tea.

There were six of them, not four. Two at the barrier, two warming their hands at the fire, one walking the ditch line with a spear over his shoulder like a scythe, and one — young, barely more than a boy — standing at the head of the queue of travelers with a ledger under his arm and an expression of terrible earnestness.

The queue was short. That was the worst part. A tinker with his cart. Two sisters with a goat. An old man carrying a bundle of winter turnips as if they were the last turnips in the world, which, given what had happened to the provinces behind them, they might have been. Everyone shuffled forward, gave their names, showed whatever scraps of paper the Wardens would accept, and passed through into the eastern hills. Nobody argued. Arguing at a checkpoint was how you became interesting.

"Names," said the young soldier when they reached him. He had a voice that cracked on the second syllable and a face full of freckles that the cold had turned to rust. His eyes went down the ledger, up to Anselm's collar, and something flickered there — recognition, maybe, or its cousin, suspicion. "Priest. You'll have letters of travel."

"Defrocked, son," Anselm said mildly. "You can't defrock a dead parish. I'm escorting this family to a burial."

"Family name?"

"Underhill," Petra said, before she could stop herself, and felt the word leave her like a bird leaving a hand. Her own name. She'd used false ones at three other checkpoints — Miller, Fenwick, a name she'd invented on the spot about a dead aunt in Harrow Ford — but this one came out true, and she watched the soldier write it down in careful, schoolroom letters, *U-n-d-e-r-h-i-l-l*, and thought: *that's it, that's your name in a Crown ledger now, you fool, you tired fool.*

"Relation to the deceased?" The soldier looked at Sef.

"Granddaughter." Sef's chin trembled, and Petra knew it wasn't craft. "He kept the fire-pit at the tannery in Greyhollow. Forty years. There's no one else to carry him."

Something moved across the boy's face — a softening, a remembering. Maybe he'd had a grandfather. Maybe he'd had a fire-pit. Maybe he was simply cold and wanted this queue finished so he could go back to the cookfire.

"Purpose of travel," he said, quieter now.

"Burial," Petra said. "In the hill country east. His people came from there."

"Burial," the soldier repeated, writing it. And then, because he was thorough, because he was young and earnest and trying so hard to be the kind of soldier the edict-cloth demanded, he looked up at Petra with apologetic brown eyes and said the words she had been dreading since the hills first leaned together:

"You'll understand I have to ask. Are any of you practitioners? Charms, workings, hedge-craft of any kind? It's amnesty if you declare. It's—" he swallowed— "it's worse if we find out after."

* * *

Later — much later, years later, in the time after everything — Petra would try to remember exactly which moment she decided. She never could. Memory refused to give her a clean edge to cut along. There was only the boy's face, and the frost coming off her breath, and the weight of the little clay pot against her sternum where it hung on its cord beneath her dress, warm as a held coin.

She had sworn. That was the thing. Five years ago, running through the wet dark with Maren's spellbook against her ribs and the vial of grave-dust rattling in her pocket, she had sworn on everything she had — on her mother's memory, on the silent gods, on the road itself — that she would never use the dust. Not because it was forbidden, though it was, though it was the most forbidden thing Maren had ever ground between mortar and pestle. Because of what it *was*.

Forgetting-dust. Grave-earth taken from the head of a burial, milled fine, mixed with the ash of burned letters — letters the dead person had written and no one else had read. Maren had made it once, shown it to Petra once, held the little clay pot up to the candlelight and said, in her dry voice, like someone describing a tool she hoped never to need: *"This doesn't hurt them. That's what's monstrous about it. It takes only what you point it at. A face. A name. An hour. They walk away whole, minus the piece you took, and they never know there's a hole. You could steal a man's wedding day and he'd feel only a vague sadness at church. Never use it, child. Not to escape, not to save, not even to be kind. A witch who reaches into a mind is a thief in a house with no locks, and thieves get comfortable."*

And Petra had sworn. And for five years the pot had sat at the bottom of her kit, wrapped in wool, untouched, a stone in her shoe that she'd grown around.

Now the boy waited for her answer, pen poised, breath steaming, and behind him the Inquisition pavilion stirred in the wind like something waking, and in the basket at Sef's feet a dragon's heart burned dark and patient, and the lark went on singing its idiot spring song, and Petra heard herself say:

"No, sir. None of us. We're just poor folk taking an old man home."

It should have ended there. It nearly did. The soldier nodded, made his mark, and waved them toward the barrier — and then the sergeant by the cookfire looked up.

He was older, grey-whiskered, with the flat competent face of a man who had searched a hundred houses. "Girl," he called to Sef. "What's in the basket?"

"Grave-linens," Sef said. Her voice didn't shake. "Shroud-cloth. For wrapping."

"Set it down. Open it."

*He smells it,* Petra thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy. *Not the linen. The iron. The heat. Twenty years of searching houses and he knows what contraband warmth looks like.* The sergeant was already pushing off from the cookfire, wiping his hands on his coat, and the young soldier was turning back toward them with his ledger hugged to his chest, confused, the easy moment curdling, and Petra's hand was inside her dress and closing around the clay pot before her mind had finished voting.

*"Never use it, child."*

"Maren," she whispered — not a prayer, nothing so clean, just a name thrown at the silence where the gods used to be — and thumbed the wax seal.

The dust was finer than flour and darker than night, and it weighed nothing, and it weighed everything. She cupped it in her palm below the level of the wagon bed, and Maren's voice came back to her across five years and one betrayal: *you point it. Like a finger. Like a blade. Choose the smallest piece you can live with stealing.*

The smallest piece. Not his name — she couldn't take his name, that was too much, that was a whole life. Not his orders, not his post, not his memory of this morning. Just — she reached out with the dust cupped in her palm and found him, the way you find a candle flame in a dark room without looking directly at it, and she chose.

The moment the sergeant's shadow fell across the basket. The order to open it. The next ten minutes, no more. A hole the size of a hand, cut out of a stranger's day.

She blew.

The dust didn't scatter. That was the horror of it, the thing no one warned you: it went where you pointed it, all of it, every grain, sliding through the cold air like water finding a crack, and it touched the sergeant's forehead and vanished, and the man stopped mid-stride.

He blinked. He looked at the basket. He looked at Sef. He frowned — a small frown, the frown of a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why — and then he shook his head like a dog shaking off rain and said, "Cold morning. Get your cart moving, mistress, you're blocking the lane."

Sef stared at him. Then at Petra. Then, quickly, down at the basket, hiding whatever her face was doing.

"Thank you, sir," Sef managed.

The young soldier was still watching them. He wrote one more line in his ledger — Petra would wonder forever what line, what word, whether her true name sat there in schoolroom ink pointing at her like a finger — and then he stepped aside and waved them through the barrier, and the wagon wheels crunched over the frost-ruts, and the hills opened eastward, and they were through.

* * *

They didn't speak for a mile. The mule clopped. The lark gave up. Somewhere behind them the checkpoint shrank to a smudge of orange again, and then to nothing.

It was Sef who finally pulled the wagon up at a stream-crossing and climbed down and stood in front of Petra with her arms crossed and her jaw working, nineteen years old and furious in the way that only the very young and the very loyal can be furious.

"You had that the whole time."

"Yes."

"The whole *time*. At the gates of Greyhollow. At the ferry. At the crossroads where we hid in the ditch for three hours while that patrol passed." Sef's voice rose. "We could have just — you could have just—"

"Yes," Petra said.

"Why didn't you *say*?"

"Because I swore I'd never use it." Petra heard her own voice come out flat and tired, older than she felt. "Because my mistress told me what it does to the witch who uses it, and I believed her, and I spent five years being the kind of person who doesn't do that. And today I did it anyway, and it took less than a heartbeat, and that—" she stopped. Started again. "That's the part I can't explain to you, Sef. It's not that it worked. It's how *easily* it worked. Like opening a door I'd been leaning against for years. My hand knew what to do. My hand had always known."

Anselm had walked ahead to the stream and was filling the waterskin, giving them the privacy of his back. But he spoke without turning. "How much did you take?"

"A moment. The sergeant's moment. Him walking over, ordering the basket opened. Nothing else. He'll finish his shift and go home and never know there's a gap in his day." Petra wrapped the clay pot in its wool again, carefully, the way you'd bandage a wound. "That's what makes it monstrous. It's such a *small* theft. Small enough to justify. Small enough to do again tomorrow."

"Would you?" Sef asked. "Tomorrow?"

Petra looked at her. The girl's anger had already cooled into something more dangerous — curiosity, calculation, the practical mind of a keeper assessing a new tool. *She's thinking about the lantern,* Petra realized. *About what the dust could do for Edmund's flame, for the road ahead.* The dust was already spreading, the way Maren had said it would. Not through minds. Through hers.

"No," Petra said. "Ask me tomorrow and I don't know. Ask me today, right now, with my hand still smelling of it — no. Never again unless it's this exact moment again, and God—" she caught herself on the word, glanced at Anselm's back, and found she didn't want to take it back— "*God* keep me from ever standing in this exact moment again."

Anselm came back with the full waterskin and handed it around. When he spoke, his voice was gentle in the way that meant he was about to be merciless.

"You blew dust on a soldier to protect a stolen dragon-flame, a stolen spellbook, and a lie about a grave. Do you know what the old texts call that? Necessary sin. It's the doctrine I was defrocked for rejecting." He took a long drink. "I rejected it because I thought the gods were gone and nobody was keeping score. Standing here, watching you hate yourself for saving our lives — I'm starting to think somebody's keeping score after all. I just don't know whose."

"Mine," Petra said. "Somebody's keeping score in mine."

She climbed back onto the wagon and took up the reins, and the mule leaned into the harness, and the eastern hills rolled out ahead of them, green-grey and indifferent, getting closer to the Spire with every mile.

But that night, making camp in a hollow under a rowan tree, Petra took out the clay pot one more time and looked at it in the firelight. Lighter now. She could tell by the rattle. Some of the dust was gone forever, living now in a sergeant's missing ten minutes, and some of something else was gone too — some load-bearing wall inside her, quietly removed, the house still standing but standing differently.

And it was while she was rewrapping it that she noticed the book.

Maren's spellbook, at the bottom of her pack, where it had lain unopened for five years. Its cover was warm. Not fire-warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a page gets when someone has just finished writing on the other side of it.

Petra put her palm flat against the leather and held very still, and beneath her hand, faint as a pulse, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the book waited — as if somewhere inside it, in a margin, in a space between lines, her mistress had picked up the pen again.

And had written one more word.
