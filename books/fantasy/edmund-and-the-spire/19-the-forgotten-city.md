## 19. The Forgotten City

They smelled it before they saw it: woodsmoke and wet stone and something sweeter underneath, like bread left too long in a cooling oven. Anselm stopped on the ridge with his hand raised, the way he did when he wanted them silent, though there was nothing to be silent toward — no road, no walls, only a shallow valley holding a scatter of rooftops like coins at the bottom of a green purse.

"There's no city here," Sef said.

She said it without doubt, which was the strange part. She had the map open across her knees — the one Anselm had copied from memory of a memory, all its edges soft with handling — and the valley beneath them was blank vellum. Not unexplored. Blank. The parchment there was smooth as an egg, as if no cartographer's pen had ever so much as trembled over it.

"I know," Anselm said. "That's why we're going."

Petra looked at him. Five days on the road had worn him down to sinew and stubbornness; his vestments were gone, traded in Harrowmere for a farmer's coat that fit him badly, and without the black wool he looked less like a priest than like a man who had once been one, the way a riverbed looks like a river in dry years. But his eyes still had that quality she both trusted and feared: the look of someone who has already decided and is merely waiting for the world to catch up.

"It's not on any map," she said. "Nothing is on any map anymore. That's been true since the wells went dry."

"This is different." He folded the map away with the care other men gave relics. "The villages we've passed were *fading*. Names going soft, signs losing their letters, people forgetting which road led where. That's erosion. This—" he nodded at the valley, "—this was never written down at all. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Somewhere between the last survey and now, an entire city fell out of the country's memory so completely that even the paper forgot it. And yet it's still smoking its chimneys. Still baking its bread." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "I want to know who's doing the remembering."

* * *

The road down into the valley was a footpath, then a cart-track, then a proper cobbled street, appearing under their boots the way things appear in dreams — without transition, without explanation, each step simply finding more city beneath it than the step before. The houses were old in a way Petra had never seen. Not ruined-old, not picturesque-old. Old the way a grandmother is old: lived-in, patched, warm-lit, utterly indifferent to fashion. Thatch and slate and timber darkened by centuries of rain. Smoke rising from chimneys in unhurried columns. Laundry strung between upper windows, shirtsleeves turning slowly in the windless air.

And everywhere, silence of a particular kind. Not emptiness. The streets had people in them — a woman beating a rug from an upstairs window, two men mending a cart, children chasing something invisible along a gutter — but no one spoke. Or rather, they spoke constantly, mouths moving, hands gesturing, laughter rising in bright wordless bursts, and none of it arrived as language. It was as if the whole city conversed in a tongue made entirely of everything except words.

"They can't remember their names," Anselm said quietly. He had gone very pale. "Look at them. They're talking around the hole where language used to be."

A woman crossed the square toward them — middle-aged, flour to her elbows, the particular rolling gait of someone whose feet have known the same stones for forty years. She opened her mouth to greet them, and Petra watched the greeting die: the woman's lips shaped sounds, abandoned them, shaped others. Her face went through welcome, confusion, apology, welcome again, all in the space of three heartbeats.

Then her eyes found Petra's face, and everything changed.

The woman stopped. The flour-dusted hands rose halfway, hovering, trembling. And out of her mouth came — clear, whole, unbroken, the first fully-formed words Petra had heard since entering the valley —

"*Petra.*"

Not a question. A recognition. The name came out of the stranger the way water comes out of a spring that everyone thought had dried: sudden, cold, undeniable.

"How do you know my name?" Petra's voice came out smaller than she intended.

But the woman was already laughing — soundlessly at first, then with breath behind it, a real laugh, rusty as an unused gate — and she took Petra's hands in her floured ones and pressed them, and said again, "*Petra Underhill,*" tasting every syllable like a sweetness she'd been rationing, and then the rest of her speech collapsed back into shapeless sound, and she didn't seem to mind at all. She tugged. Come, come, come. The whole square had turned to watch. Wordless faces, dozens of them, and every single one wearing the same expression: the look of people seeing someone arrive whom they had somehow always expected.

"*She remembers you,*" Sef whispered. "They all do. Look at them. They know your *name*, Petra. They don't know their own."

* * *

They were given a house. There was no discussion of it; the flour-woman — they called her Baker, because she baked, and because a thing must be called something — simply walked them through streets that bent obligingly toward their destination and installed them in a cottage with a lit hearth and beds already made, as if the house had been waiting the way a chair waits. On the table inside stood bread, cheese, a jug of cider, and three bowls. Three. Not four. As if the house knew about Vane, wherever Vane was, and had decided he wasn't coming.

Or hadn't decided yet. Petra kept thinking about that fourth bowl all evening.

The city had a name once. You could see the absence of it everywhere: a blank space on the lintel above the well where a carved plaque had been pried away or had simply ceased; a flagstone in the square smoother than its neighbors, worn by generations of feet stopping to read something that was no longer there. The people gestured at these absences sometimes, in the wordless way, palms up, eyebrows lifted — *what was it? what was it?* — and then shrugged and went back to living. They had made their peace with the hole. They had built their lives around it the way a tree builds around a fence wire.

It was the children who frightened Petra most, and the children who undid her.

There were perhaps twenty of them in the city, born after whatever forgetting had happened, and they had invented a language of their own — a whistled, clapped, hummed thing, fluent and fast, passed between them like a ball. They could say anything in it except, apparently, names. When they wanted to refer to a person they used gestures, or objects — the Baker was a flat slap of palms; Anselm was two fingers walking, a man of long strides and books. And when the children wanted Petra, they pressed both hands flat over their hearts.

She learned this on the second evening, sitting on a doorstep while the light went amber and long. A girl of six or seven came and sat beside her, close, the way children sit when they have decided you are theirs. She pointed at Petra's chest. Then she pressed her own small hands over her own small heart, and looked up, waiting.

"That's me?" Petra asked. "That's how you say me?"

The girl nodded vigorously, delighted, and did it again, and then did something new: she took Petra's hand and pressed it, palm-flat, against the girl's own chest, and held it there. Nothing. No gesture. No whistle. The child's face flickered — not sadness exactly, but the shadow where sadness would live if she'd had the word for it — and then she shrugged, magnificently, the shrug of someone who has already survived worse than having no name, and ran off to rejoin the clapping game.

Petra sat on the doorstep a long time after dark.

* * *

Anselm spent his days among them with his notebook, and by the third day he had stopped writing in it, which told Petra more than any page could have.

"I thought I'd document them," he said that night, in the cottage, over the fire. His voice had the raw edge of a man arguing with himself and losing. "Names, customs, genealogies if any survived. Evidence. Proof that a place can fall out of the record and keep breathing." He turned his hands toward the flames. "But you can't document people who will forget your documentation. Whatever I write down stays written. Whatever they remember—" he shook his head. "Do you know what they remember, Petra? Out of everything? Every face they've ever seen, every kindness done to them, every song's melody if not its words. Everything except the categories. Except the scaffolding. They've lost the frame and kept the picture."

"The gods," Sef said from the corner. She was cleaning Edmund's lantern — the iron case with its sleeping coal of heart-flame, which she tended the way other girls tended hair. "You're dancing around it. Somebody remembered this city into hiding, or somebody forgot it into safety, and either way that's god-work. Old work. Before the Crown, before the temples got loud." She snapped the lantern's latch closed. "My grandfather used to say the first magic wasn't fire. It was forgetting. You hide the thing you love where even heaven can't find it."

"Then whoever hid this place," Anselm said slowly, "hid it from heaven."

"And from maps," Petra said. "And from taxes, and edicts, and inquisitors." She heard her own voice go quiet and strange. "From everything that's been hunting us since Greyhollow."

The fire popped. None of them said the next thing, which was: *then maybe being forgotten isn't the worst fate.* Maybe, said the silence around the hearth, it was the only shelter left.

* * *

On the fourth day, the Baker taught Petra to bake.

It was not offered; it was simply assumed, the way everything in the city was assumed — Petra found herself handed an apron, stationed at a trough of flour, shown by patient gesture how the dough should feel when it was ready, how the oven's breath should taste on your cheek when it was hot enough. The Baker could not explain in words. She didn't need to. Her hands explained, and Petra's hands listened, and somewhere in the middle of the morning Petra realized she was humming — some tune half-remembered from her mother, from before Maren, from before everything — and the Baker was humming harmony to it, note-perfect, though neither of them knew the song's name or its words, and the melody itself was the entire conversation.

*I could stay here.*

The thought arrived uninvited, and Petra set down the dough and stared at her own floured fingers as if they belonged to someone else.

Because that was the trap of the valley, she understood suddenly. Not malice. Nothing so dramatic. Just this: the city needed remembering the way a fire needs feeding, and it remembered *her* — her name, whole and sweet, the one thing it could hold — and in exchange it offered everything she had run five years to find. Belonging without conditions. Neighbors who knew her not as the witch of Greyhollow, not as the runaway, not as the woman whose charms worked too well lately, but simply as *Petra*, pressed flat over the heart. Here she was not hunted. Here she was not even remarkable. Here she was a girl who kneaded bread and hummed, and the whole city turned toward her like flowers toward a window.

Vane could not find this place. The Crown could not tax it. The dying gods, if they were shouting anywhere, were not shouting here.

She could put down the road forever. She could let the valley fold over her like a blanket. In ten years she would be the Baker's daughter in every way that mattered, and the hole where the city's name had been would feel like home.

"Petra."

Sef stood in the doorway, flour-dusted because everyone ended up flour-dusted eventually, and the lantern hung from her hand, and through its iron seams glowed that faint stubborn red, the last fire of the last dragon, patient as a banked hearth. Sef looked at her — really looked, the way she rarely did, nineteen years old and older than the hills in her eyes — and Petra understood that Sef had seen the thought land. Had maybe had it herself.

"We can't," Sef said. Not cruelly. Gently, which was worse.

"I know."

"They'd take us in anyway. That's the thing. They'd never ask where we're going. They'd just make us a fourth bowl." Sef's jaw tightened. "And we'd let the rest of the country finish dissolving while we baked bread."

Petra looked down at the dough, risen and soft and waiting, and felt the full weight of what belonging costs when you finally find it: it makes leaving a violence. She had spent five years believing she had no one to betray. Now she had a whole invisible city that knew her name.

* * *

They left on the fifth morning, at grey light, before the city woke enough to make leaving hard.

They almost failed anyway. Because the whole city was in the square.

Every soul — the Baker, the cart-menders, the children with their clapping language, the old men on the well's rim — standing silent in the mist, facing the road out, and none of them barring it. None of them speaking, because they couldn't. They simply stood, and as Petra passed, one by one, they did the thing the children did: pressed their hands flat over their hearts. All of them. The whole forgotten city saying her name the only way it could, a hundred silent salutes, and the Baker broke from the line and pressed a cloth bundle into Petra's arms — bread, still warm, enough for a week — and held her face in floured hands for a moment, and mouthed, wordlessly, unmistakably:

*Come back. Remember us, if you can't stay.*

Petra walked up the valley road with her eyes burning and did not let herself look back until the ridge, and when she did, the city below was already fading — not gone, never gone, chimneys still smoking, laundry still turning — but softer, dimmer, the way a face goes when you're falling asleep. Holding itself in existence, she thought, by threads. By bread and humming and children's games. By one name, hers, kept safe like a coal in ash.

"Someone hid them here," Anselm said at last, hoarse. "Someone loved them enough to erase them. And it's still costing that someone something, every day, or the city would have vanished outright instead of just... slipping." He looked east, toward the Spire, toward the hum only he seemed able to hear growing in the ground. "If forgetting can be a mercy, Petra, then remembering can be a weapon. And I think we're about to meet whoever knows the difference."

Behind them, the valley exhaled its chimney smoke into the morning. Ahead, the road bent toward a tower that grew on no map, and under Petra's arm, wrapped in cloth, the bread was still warm — proof, at least, that some things could be carried out of oblivion intact.

She just wished she believed they'd be allowed to carry them much further.
