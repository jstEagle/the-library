## 20. Vane Follows

The escort was meant to slow him down, and so he left it.

Vane told himself it was a matter of efficiency. Twelve men and a supply cart could manage a village search; they could not manage a pursuit across country that had begun, in the last fortnight, to misbehave. Roads that appeared on no surveyor's chart. Hamlets whose names his own men could not pronounce twice the same way. A province that had, according to the last dispatch from the capital, simply stopped answering its post.

*Verifying reports*, he wrote in the log, in his small precise hand, and signed it with the seal he had carried for nineteen years without once dropping it in mud or blood. *Inquisitor First Class Corvus Vane proceeds ahead of column to assess anomalous conditions in the eastern reaches. Column to continue scheduled searches under Sub-Inquisitor Hale.*

Hale was twenty-six and devout and would follow his orders exactly, which was the highest praise Vane could give any man and also, lately, the thing that frightened him most.

He rode out before dawn, alone, in a grey rain that smelled of iron.

* * *

He had been a believer once in the uncomplicated way of boys who have never been asked to define what they believe in. His mother had hung a salt-charm over the door — not because she thought it worked, she said, but because her own mother had, and a house should remember its dead. The Crown had taken such things gently at first, then less gently, and by the time Vane was old enough to ask questions the questions themselves had become suspect, and so he had stopped asking and started enforcing, which was what the asking turned into when you fed it enough years.

Nineteen years of edicts. Nineteen years of burning stalls and questioning widows and filing reports in a hand so neat it looked printed. He had told himself for nineteen years that order was a form of mercy — that a country without gods needed law all the more, the way a man with no heart needs a schedule.

Then the bells rang themselves, and the prayers began coming true like wounds coming open, and every certainty he owned developed a hairline crack down the middle that he could hear whistling when the wind blew through it.

* * *

The woman he had interrogated in Greyhollow — the widow Merrin, kneeling on cold stone while he read her the charges — had prayed for her husband back, and gotten him back, drowned and dripping and wrong, standing in her kitchen for three days before anyone dared speak to him. Vane had questioned the husband too. That interview lived behind his eyes now, surfacing at night the way drowned things surface: the man's skin cold as cellar stone, his voice coming out with water in it, saying, *She called me home. I heard her calling from very far away and I came. It was so far. It was so dark coming back.*

And Vane had sat there with his ledger open and realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he did not know what he was writing anymore. That the categories — *witchcraft, unlicensed working, threat to realm* — were words, only words, laid over something vast and grieving that was happening to his country whether he filed it or not.

The husband had looked at him at the end, with eyes like wet river stones, and said: *You pray too. I can tell. You have the look of a man carrying an unsaid thing.*

Vane had closed the ledger and left the room and not written anything down for two full hours, which his clerks still spoke of in whispers.

* * *

The road east degraded the way roads do when the land behind them is being forgotten. First the milestones lost their numbers. Then the ditches filled with a fog that did not behave like fog — it lay in long ropes along the ground, and his horse refused it, sidestepping and blowing, until Vane dismounted and led the animal through by the bridle, speaking to it in the low voice he used on suspects, which was also, he realized, the voice his mother had used on him.

By midday he passed a shrine at a crossroads — one of the old ones, pre-Crown, a squat stone hooded figure with a bowl for offerings. Someone had filled the bowl recently. Not with coins. With teeth.

He stood looking at them a long time. Small teeth, milk teeth, the kind a child loses and a mother keeps in a jar on a shelf because throwing them away feels like throwing away the child. Dozens of them, white in the rain, arranged in the bowl in a spiral.

A farmer's field blooming with teeth — that report had come through Greyhollow three weeks ago, and he had filed it under *anomalous agriculture, pending investigation*, and the clerk had laughed, and Vane had not laughed, and now here was the phenomenon following him east like a dog.

He did not touch the teeth. But he found himself, before mounting again, standing at the shrine with his head slightly bowed, the way one bows to a colleague one does not entirely trust, and the bow cost him nothing and gave him nothing and he made it anyway.

*Duty*, he told the horse. The horse flicked an ear.

* * *

He caught their trail on the second day, and it was not the trail he expected.

He had imagined fugitives: cold camps, buried fires, a path chosen for concealment. Instead he found generosity. At a burned-out tollhouse someone had left bread wrapped in oilcloth on the windowsill, weighted against crows, with a scrap of charcoal writing on the stone: *for whoever comes after.* At a ford, stepping-stones had been newly placed where the old bridge rotted — flat stones set carefully, tested, stable, by hands that had gone on walking without waiting for thanks.

And everywhere, faintly, the smell of charms working. He had learned it now, that scent — hot metal and crushed herbs and something underneath like the air before lightning. Father Anselm had described tracking Petra Underhill by it, back when Anselm was merely a name on a list of defrocked clergy, and Vane had put the detail in a file and forgotten it. Now he understood it in his body. The trail smelled like a door held open.

He followed it through country that grew stranger by the hour. A village where every door stood open and no one answered his hail, though he saw movement behind windows — people going about their business with the unhurried air of those who have decided something enormous. A hillside orchard where the trees bore fruit out of season, heavy branches bowed, and no one harvesting, and birds refusing to land.

Once, near dusk, he passed a milestone that read, in fresh-cut letters, a name he did not know, and when he looked back from ten paces the letters were smooth blank stone, and when he looked again they were there, and he rode on faster.

* * *

On the third night he made camp in the shell of a waystation chapel, roofless, its bell long since confiscated, and built a small fire because the alternative was sitting in the dark with himself, and he had begun to find that company unbearable.

He ate hard bread and harder cheese and took out his ledger to make the day's entry, and found himself writing:

*Column status unknown. Road conditions deteriorating. Trail of fugitives indicates —*

He stopped. Looked at the sentence. *Indicates what?* That the witches were feeding the countryside? That they were leaving bread for strangers and mending bridges? That the eastern provinces, officially in rebellion, were instead quietly taking care of each other in ways his edicts had never managed?

He struck the line. Wrote: *Nothing anomalous to report.* And then sat staring at the lie, because it was the first lie he had ever entered in nineteen years of ledgers, and it fit his hand as neatly as the truth had, and that ease terrified him more than the teeth in the shrine bowl.

The fire cracked. Rain began again, soft on the ruined rafters.

And there in the dark, alone, with no clerk to hear and no God officially listening, Inquisitor Corvus Vane found the unsaid thing rising in him like water finding a crack.

He had carried it since he was nine years old. His sister, Liss, fevered, six years old, three days dying in the bed beside his while his mother prayed and the priests came and went and the gods — the gods who were already going quiet, though no one said it yet — said nothing. She had died on the morning of the fourth day, and Vane, nine years old, had knelt at the window and made a bargain with whatever might be listening: *Give her back and I will serve you my whole life. I will do anything. I will be your instrument.*

No answer. Of course no answer. And so he had served anyway — served the Crown, served the law, served the great machinery of order that was the closest thing to a god the country still permitted — and he had told himself for thirty years that the prayer had been childish, that the bargain lapsed unanswered, that he owed the silence nothing.

But he had never taken it back. That was the thing. Thirty years, and he had never formally released the vow, and some clerk-part of his soul kept the account open, accruing interest, waiting.

The widow's husband had come back when she called. Drowned and wrong, yes — but he had *come*. From very far away. Through the dark.

Liss had not come. Or — the thought arrived like ice water — Liss had not been *called correctly*. Or the line had been cut. Or the gods had been too weak even then, dying by inches for a generation while the Crown congratulated itself on the silence.

Or they had heard, and could not answer, and had spent thirty years ashamed.

Vane knelt in the ruined chapel, in the rain through the missing roof, in the light of his own small fire, and did not pray. He was not ready to pray. Praying would mean admitting the addressee existed, and admitting that would burn down nineteen years of reports and edicts and neat printed-looking sentences, and a man cannot burn his life's work in a night.

But he said the name. Just the name, aloud, into the dark, for the first time since the funeral.

"Liss."

The fire leaned. The rain went on. Somewhere east, faint as a memory of a memory, a bell that had no rope rang once.

He stayed kneeling a long time. When he finally rose, his knees were soaked and stiff and he felt lighter than he had since the bells, and he hated the lightness, because lightness meant hope, and hope in an inquisitor was dereliction.

* * *

He overtook them on the fifth day, though he did not yet let himself know that was what he was doing.

The trail bent south toward the high country — toward, if his geography served, and his geography always served, the valley the maps did not show. Every map of that region had been confiscated by his own office eleven years ago, on orders from above, and the confiscation order itself had been classified above his clearance, which had annoyed him then and chilled him now. The Crown had known about the Spire. The Crown had known for years. And had answered the ringing bells and the blooming fields and the returning dead not with truth but with arrests.

His horse was failing; he traded it at a farmstead for a younger one, paying triple and leaving his seal-ring as surety, and the farmer's wife looked at the ring and then at his face and said, "You're the one that burns the charm-stalls," not accusing, just placing him, the way one places weather.

"Yes," he said.

"My boy's fever broke last week," she said. "We didn't pray. We'd given that up. But something answered anyway." She handed him the reins. "Ride careful. Country's thin out east. You can see through it, in places."

She was right. Twice that afternoon he saw through it — brief gaps where the hills went translucent as horn, showing not sky beyond but a kind of pale unfinished nothing, like a page where the illuminator died before painting. Both times his new horse shuddered and both times Vane spoke to it in the low voice, and both times he thought: *this is what I have been policing. Borders around a country that is evaporating. Law for a kingdom that is forgetting itself into mist.*

And the witch — Underhill, the hedge-witch, the runaway — was riding toward the center of it. Toward the Spire. With a priest and a dragon-keeper's girl and, if the reports held, a lantern full of the last fire in the world.

Every instinct of his office said: intercept. Arrest. Confiscate the flame for the Crown.

Every instinct of the man kneeling in the chapel said: you know what the Crown will do with it. You have read the classified orders. You know.

* * *

At dusk on the fifth day he crested a ridge and saw them below, three figures and a mule on the valley road, small in the last light, and he reined in behind a hawthorn and watched.

The girl walked ahead, quick, restless, carrying an iron lantern wrapped in sacking, and even through the wrapping he could see the glow — a deep red pulse, slow, like a heartbeat seen through a wall. The priest walked beside the mule talking, gesturing, sermonizing to an audience of one woman who mostly wasn't listening, and the woman —

The woman stopped. Turned. Looked directly up the ridge at the hawthorn where he sat, across half a mile of failing light, and Vane felt her gaze land on him like a hand on his chest.

She couldn't have seen him. No human eye could have. But she looked, and held, and then she raised one hand — not a curse, not a summons. Something almost like a greeting. Almost like an invitation. And turned back to the road.

Vane sat in the shadow of the hawthorn until the three figures dropped below the ridgeline, and his heart was going like a boy's, and the lantern-glow lingered in his vision long after it was gone, red on the inside of his eyelids.

He should signal. He should ride for the nearest garrison, raise the column, close the valley. That was duty. That was the whole architecture of his life.

Instead he checked his horse's feet, tightened the girth, and rode down into the valley after them, alone, telling himself — one last time, and hearing how thin it sounded — *verifying reports.*

Behind him, far off in the dark, a bell with no rope rang once more, patient as a door left unlatched, and this time Vane did not pretend he hadn't heard it. This time, without quite deciding to, without quite permitting himself, he answered it the only way a man with no prayers left can answer:

He followed.
