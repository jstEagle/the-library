## 21. The Spire at Last

They smelled it before they saw it.

Petra had thought, in the weeks of walking, that she would know the Spire by sight — a tower on a horizon, a black line against the sky, something a person could point to and say *there*. But it announced itself first through the ground. For two days the road had hummed beneath their boots, a vibration too low to hear and impossible to ignore, like standing on the chest of something enormous and sleeping. Sef felt it through her soles and said nothing for an hour. Anselm pressed his palm flat against a milestone and left it there until his fingers went white.

"It's not sound," he said finally. "It's the space where sound used to be."

The lantern rode between them in its sling of rope and iron, swinging with Sef's stride, and inside it Edmund's heart-flame burned a color Petra had no name for — not gold, not white, but the color of the moment just before a struck match catches, stretched out forever. It had burned brighter each day since they left the forgotten city. It burned brighter now, and the hum in the earth rose to meet it, and Petra understood, with the dull certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant, that the flame and the tower were already speaking to each other. They were only carrying the message.

* * *

They came over the shoulder of the valley at dusk, when the light was doing that thin, dishonest thing it did in a country being forgotten — gilding hills that might not be there tomorrow, laying long shadows across fields whose crops grew in rows no farmer remembered planting. Anselm stopped first. He made a sound that was half prayer and half the noise a man makes when he has been punched.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, it's *real*."

The Spire grew out of the valley floor like a tooth from a jaw.

That was the only honest way to describe it, and Petra hated how true it was: not built, not raised by hands, but *grown*, root-first, as if the earth had once bitten down on something and never let go. It was black — not painted black, not shadowed black, but black the way the inside of a closed eye is black, a blackness that seemed to drink the last of the sunset rather than reflect it. It rose perhaps three hundred feet, tapering to a point that bent slightly eastward, toward Greyhollow, toward everything they had left behind. Its surface was smooth in some places and ridged in others, and as the light failed Petra saw that the ridges moved — slowly, slowly, the way a nail moves on a living hand.

Around its base, the valley floor was bare earth for a quarter mile in every direction. No grass. No stones. No ruins of whatever had stood there before. Just soil packed hard and dark as if ten thousand pilgrims had knelt there for ten thousand years, which, Petra thought, looking at the worn-smooth ground, perhaps they had.

And it was humming.

Not the ground-hum anymore. The tower itself sang — or rather, it held a thousand songs at once without letting any of them finish. Petra heard a widow's grief in it, the raw syllable of a woman calling a name into water. She heard a farmer's fury, a child's small bargaining voice, an old man's thank-you repeated like a rosary. Every answered prayer from every desperate mouth since the bells rang themselves at dawn in Greyhollow — all of it lived here, layered and looping, a choir of everyone who had ever asked the silent gods for anything and been horrified to receive it.

"They're all still here," Sef whispered. Her hand had found the lantern's handle without her noticing; she gripped it like a railing in a storm. "The prayers. They don't go anywhere. They come *here*."

"Everything comes here," Anselm said. "It's the only door left. Doors collect what passes through them." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry and cracked. "I preached for twenty years that the gods were gone. I told my congregation they'd been abandoned. And the whole time—" He pressed his fist against his mouth. "The whole time they were *dying*, and I called the body an empty room."

Petra said nothing. She was listening past the choir, the way Maren had taught her to listen past a customer's words to the want underneath them. Beneath the thousand voices the Spire made one sound of its own, and it took her a long moment to recognize it because she had only heard it once before, five years ago, in a cottage that smelled of grave-dust and beeswax:

It was the sound of Maren breathing at the end of a working. That long, drawn, effortful exhale. The sound of something holding itself up at tremendous cost.

The Spire was tired. The Spire was dying too. Everything in this country was dying except the people, who simply couldn't manage it fast enough to matter.

"We camp here," she said. "Above the rim. We don't go down in the dark."

"You think it'll be different in daylight?" Sef asked.

"No," Petra said honestly. "But I will."

* * *

She didn't sleep. None of them really did. They made a cold camp among the wind-bent pines at the valley's rim, and the lantern sat between them like a fourth traveler, and the hum came up through the ground into their spines and kept them company in the way that a fever keeps you company.

Near midnight, Sef spoke into the dark.

"When my mother died," she said, "the fire-pit went quiet for nine days. Not out — quiet. Like it was deciding whether to bother with me. And I sat with it every day and did the rites, all forty of them, the ones nobody else in the family could remember anymore, and on the ninth day it flared up and burned my eyebrows off, and I knew it had chosen me." She turned the lantern slightly; the strange pre-flame color washed her face. "This is what it sounds like when it's deciding about someone. Did you know that?"

"I didn't," Petra said.

"It's deciding about you." Sef wasn't accusing her. That was the worst part. She said it the way she reported the weather. "It's been deciding since the cellar. Maybe since before the cellar. Edmund asked *you*, Petra. Not me. I've carried its heart across half a country and it still asked *you*."

"Edmund is old and sentimental," Petra said.

"Edmund is the last creature alive that remembers what this country looked like when it was whole." Sef lay back and put her arm over her eyes. "So am I wrong to be afraid? Tell me I'm wrong."

Petra couldn't. She lay awake instead, watching the Spire's silhouette against the stars — a gap in the stars, rather, a place where they refused to shine — and somewhere in the small hours she realized she had stopped hearing the thousand prayers individually. They had braided together into one voice, and the one voice was saying something, patiently, over and over, the way you speak to someone who is slow to understand.

*Come down,* it said. *Come down and knock.*

* * *

Dawn in a forgetting country was a gray, apologetic affair, but the Spire made its own weather of light. As the sun cleared the eastern ridge, the tower's black surface began to move with reflections that matched nothing around it — flashes of market squares, of temple interiors, of a kitchen with copper pots, of faces. Petra caught one glimpse of a stall hung with luck-knots and had to turn away.

They descended together, because whatever the door wanted, none of them would give it the satisfaction of taking anyone alone.

The quarter mile of bare earth took longer to cross than it should have. Petra counted her steps and lost count twice; the numbers slid away from her like fish. The humming deepened as they approached, resolving, becoming almost words, and by the time they stood at the tower's base, close enough to touch it, the choir had fallen silent altogether — all of it, every prayer, every grief, every bargain — leaving a silence so complete that Petra could hear Sef swallowing and Anselm's breath catching and, faintly, impossibly, her own heartbeat answered by a second heartbeat coming from inside the stone.

The wall was warm. That undid her more than anything. She had braced herself for cold, for the dead mineral indifference of architecture, but the Spire's surface gave off heat like a sleeping animal, and when she laid her palm against it the ridges shifted — barely, gently — toward her hand. Toward the warmth of a living palm. The way a plant turns to light.

"There's no door," Anselm said. He was circling the base, running his fingers along the stone, reading it the way he read old inscriptions, lips moving. "There's no seam. No hinge. No— wait." He stopped. "Petra. Here."

Here was a hollow.

It was set into the tower at the height of a human chest, and it was roughly the size of two cupped hands, and it was shaped — there was no other word for it — shaped like a burning heart. Not the neat valentine shape of woodcuts and cheap charms. A real one: lopsided, asymmetrical, the chambers uneven, the vessels trailing off into the stone like roots. The hollow was lined with the same hungry black, and the air above it shimmered, and when Petra leaned close she felt it pull — gently, politely, the way a beggar holds out a bowl — drawing at the warmth of her skin, at the breath between her lips.

A door with no handle. Only a hollow shaped like exactly the thing it wanted given to it.

"Well," said Anselm, very quietly. "It doesn't leave much room for misreading, does it."

Sef stepped forward and lifted the lantern.

The heart-flame surged. There was no other word for it — the strange pre-fire color flared white, then beyond white, and the lantern's iron frame groaned with heat, and inside it Edmund's heart beat visibly, hugely, once, twice, like a fist knocking on a door from the inside. The Spire's second heartbeat answered, faster now, eager, and the hollow's shimmer brightened, and the whole tower leaned — Petra would swear to her grave it *leaned* — a fraction of an inch toward the flame.

And then the flame steadied. Dimmed. Drew back into itself, small and stubborn, and went quiet.

Sef stood holding the lantern at arm's length, and her face was wet, though Petra hadn't seen her start to cry.

"It won't go in unless I open the cage," Sef said. "Unless I let it go. Unless—" She stopped. Started again, in the flat keeper's voice she used for rites. "Unless someone gives it. Freely. All the way. And whoever gives it—"

"Passes through," Anselm finished. His voice had gone hollow as the hollow. "Alone. I can feel it in the inscriptions under the stone. I can't read most of them, but that part is written so plainly even a defrocked priest can't pretend otherwise. One heart. One door. Everyone else stays on this side of it, in a country with maybe weeks left to exist." He turned and looked at Petra, and his eyes were terrible with understanding. "You felt it too. Last night. It's been calling your name since the rim."

The hum rose again, gentle, patient, insistent. *Come down and knock.* And Petra stood before the warm black wall with her palm still resting against it, feeling the second heartbeat quicken under her hand like a dog recognizing its master's step, and understood that the impasse was not the door's.

The door knew exactly what it wanted.

It was waiting to see if she did.
