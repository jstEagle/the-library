## 22. What the Door Asks

The Spire did not want them to read it.

Anselm understood this within the first hour, kneeling in the frost-brittle grass at the tower's base with his fingers pressed to the black stone, because the inscriptions moved when he tried to follow them. Not the way water moves, or light through leaves — they shifted like a sentence being spoken by someone determined not to be overheard, each line sliding out of reach just as comprehension gathered behind his eyes. He had spent thirty years reading dead languages in dead books. He knew what it felt like to chase meaning across a page. This was different. This was a wall that flinched.

"Stop that," he muttered, in the tone he had once used on novices who fidgeted during translation exercises. "I am older than you and I have nowhere else to be."

Above him the Spire went up and up into a sky the color of pewter, its summit lost not in cloud but in a kind of visual reluctance — the eye slid off it the way a tongue slides off a cracked tooth. The hum came through the ground, through his knees, through the bones of his hands: every answered prayer since the dawn of the world's last age, still ringing in the stone like sound trapped in a bell.

Behind him, Petra had built a fire from wet gorse and was pretending it warmed anyone. Sef sat apart with the iron lantern between her knees, one hand resting on its handle the way other people rested hands on sleeping children. She hadn't let go of it more than an hour since Edmund's body failed them in the valley below, and Anselm had watched her sleep sitting up twice now, chin on chest, fingers curled through the lantern's bars as if the flame inside might otherwise wander off without her.

He envied her that certainty. A keeper knew what she kept. He himself was crouched at the foot of the oldest thing in the world with ink-stained fingers and no idea what he was about to learn, only the certain scholar's dread that it would be worse than ignorance.

* * *

By midmorning the Spire relented — or perhaps simply grew bored of him, which was a distinction Anselm chose not to examine too closely. The letters stopped moving. They were old; that much he had known from the first touch, older than the Crown, older than the Church, older than the names of any god currently dying. But they were not the liturgical script he'd spent his life on. They were the writing that came before writing, the kind carved by people who had not yet learned to separate words from things, so that every character was also a small picture of itself: the sign for *door* was an actual door; the sign for *fire* was a flame with a mouth.

"It's pre-covenantal," he said aloud, mostly to prove to himself he could still speak. His voice came out rusted. "It's — this isn't temple script. This is the language temples were invented to remember."

Petra looked over. Her face in the grey light was all hollows and stubbornness, and there was ash in her hair that she didn't know about. "Can you read it or can't you?"

"I can read it the way you can hear someone shouting through a closed door. Slowly. Badly." He ran his thumb beneath the first band of inscription, where the stone was warm — warmer than it should be, warmer than the season allowed, warm as a living flank. "And it doesn't want me to. Which means I'm right to try."

"Why would a door refuse to explain itself?"

"Because," Anselm said, and the word came out before he could soften it, "it already knows what it has to say, and it knows we won't like it."

Sef rose without a word and came to stand at his shoulder, bringing the lantern with her. The heart-flame burned low and white within the iron, and where its light touched the stone the inscriptions brightened — eagerly, Anselm thought, the way a dog brightens at the smell of its master. The flame recognized this place. Or this place recognized the flame.

"Start at the bottom," Sef said. "Edmund always said you start reading a thing the way it grew."

* * *

He started at the bottom. He worked upward through the morning and into the afternoon, and the others drifted close and then away and then close again, the way people do around a difficult diagnosis. Petra brought him water he didn't drink. Sef held the lantern at whatever height he asked, arm steady as a pulley-rope, never complaining, though he saw the tremor start in her shoulder by the third hour and said nothing about it either. There was a courtesy among the three of them now, forged on the road out of Greyhollow and tempered in the forgotten city — a courtesy made entirely of things none of them said.

The lowest band was easy. History, almost. It told of the Spire's raising in terms so plain they were nearly domestic: here stood a door, built when the country was young and the gods walked it openly, a way out prepared against a day its builders hoped would never come. The day when the land itself should fail. When the names wore thin. When the prayers fell upward into silence instead of downward into answer.

A door against the end, Anselm translated. Built by people who loved their home enough to plan its funeral.

The middle bands were harder — theology, or whatever preceded theology — and he worked through them with his lips moving, tracing characters that meant *giving* and meant *willingness* and meant, repeatedly, with a frequency that began to feel less like emphasis and more like warning, *freely*.

"Freely," he said. "Everything hinges on freely. Fire given, not taken. Not bought. Not stolen." He glanced at Petra without meaning to, and she met the glance with a level look that told him she had caught it, and had decided, for now, to forgive him for thinking it.

"The dragon-fire," Sef said. Flat. Already ahead of him, because she had been ahead of him since the cellar under the tannery. Keepers always are.

"The dragon-fire." He laid his palm flat against the stone above the word for *freely*, where the characters swelled larger, formal, almost ceremonial. "*The door opens for the fire freely given.* That's the whole clause. There's no ambiguity. No hidden second meaning — I've checked it four times. The door is not clever. It's honest in the way very old things are honest. It says exactly what it does."

"Then say the rest of it," Petra said quietly. "You've been circling the top band for an hour. You read everything except the part at the top."

Anselm looked up.

The uppermost inscription ran in a single unbroken line around the Spire's base, at the height of a standing man's heart, as if the builders had wanted it level with the eyes of whoever came last. The characters there were the largest on the tower. The largest, and the simplest. No theology. No history. Just two clauses joined by a conjunction, the grammar of a bargain.

He had read it eleven times. He had understood it on the first.

"*The one who gives it passes through,*" he recited, because saying it aloud had become a kind of penance, a way of distributing the weight. "*Alone. All others remain.*"

The wind moved through the valley grass. The Spire hummed. Somewhere far off, in the direction they had come from, another piece of the country was forgetting itself — he could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel a tooth loosen, a wrongness in the general pressure of the world.

"Alone," Petra repeated.

"As in—" Sef started, and stopped, because there was only one way to finish it and they all finished it silently together, the three of them standing in a triangle around the sentence like witnesses around a grave.

One passes. Everyone else stays. Everyone else stays in the country that is coming apart at its edges, in the villages with no names, under the bells that ring themselves, in the land whose gods are spending their last breaths answering prayers badly and gloriously and too late. One person walks out through the oldest door in the world carrying a white flame, and everyone that person loves remains behind to be forgotten politely out of existence.

"That's the trap," Anselm said. His voice sounded very far away to him. "That's been the trap from the beginning. The door isn't broken. It was built perfectly. It was built to save exactly one."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. He would remember afterward that the argument lasted most of the afternoon and accomplished nothing, and that the strangest part of it was how calm everyone stayed, as if some part of each of them had already known and was merely going through the motions of protest.

Petra paced. Petra, he was learning, thought on her feet, wore grooves in the earth with her pacing, gestured with hands that were never quite still. "There has to be a reading you're missing. You said yourself the script predates the covenant — half those words won't have one meaning. *Alone* might mean *single*. Singular. As in—"

"As in one at a time?" Anselm shook his head. "I considered it. The character for *alone* here—" he tapped the stone, gently, respectfully, the way one taps a coffin lid, "—is drawn as a single figure inside a doorway with the door closing behind. Not *one at a time*. One, period. The door shuts. Look — here's the character for *closed*. It's in the same line. The builder wrote it deliberately: *passes through, alone, and it closes.*"

"So it opens once," Sef said.

"So it opens once."

"And after that?"

"And after that, as far as I can translate, the Spire has done what it was built to do. Its work is finished. Whether it crumbles or stands empty for ten thousand years—" he shrugged, and hated the shrug, hated how academic it sounded against the size of what it dismissed, "—the text doesn't care. Texts never do. The builders cared about the door working, not about what the door felt afterward."

Petra had stopped pacing. She was looking at the topmost line, at the large simple characters, and Anselm watched her lips move through them and watched her arrive, syllable by syllable, at the place he had arrived eleven times that morning. Her face did something complicated and then settled into something simple, and the simple thing was worse.

"Sef," she said. "Give me the lantern."

"No," said Sef.

"You haven't even heard—"

"I don't need to hear it." Sef's hand tightened on the iron handle until the knuckles whitened. Nineteen years old, and she had the voice of a woman who had buried her mother at a fire-pit and known since childhood the exact year of her own burial. "Whatever the sentence says, it ends with the fire going through the door, and I know whose job the fire is. It's been my family's job for six generations. It was supposed to be mine down in that cellar, tending the pit until it killed me the way it killed her. At least this way it buys something." Her chin came up. "At least this way it's a trade."

"It's not a trade, it's a—"

"It's a better deal than I was ever offered before," Sef said, and there was such terrible steadiness in it that Petra faltered, and Anselm found himself looking away, out at the valley, because some griefs are private even when conducted in front of witnesses.

* * *

He walked the perimeter as the light failed. It was a translator's habit — when the text defeated him, walk the walls of the building that holds it. Sometimes the architecture confesses what the grammar conceals.

The Spire's base was wider than a chapel and perfectly round, and the inscriptions wrapped it in concentric bands, and as the sun went down he noticed something he had missed all day, something so obvious it shamed him: the bands were not uniform. The lower ones were deep-cut, confident, the work of masons carving in full daylight with generations ahead of them. The upper bands grew shallower. More hurried. The final line — *alone, all others remain* — was cut barely a finger's depth into stone harder than anything he had ever touched, as if it had been added late. As if it had been an amendment.

Someone had argued with this door too.

He pressed his palm to the shallow-cut line and, for the first time all day, the stone answered him. Not in words. In pressure — a slow pulse against his hand, like a heartbeat heard through a wall, like something vast turning over in its sleep and registering, dimly, the small warmth of a human palm. And threaded through the hum, so faint he would later doubt it, he heard — or felt, or remembered, the distinction no longer mattered — a fragment of the old liturgy, the covenant-chant every priest learned and no priest believed anymore: *what is freely given cannot be withheld, and what is freely given cannot be compelled.*

Freely given. The phrase was everywhere on this tower. It was in the bottom bands and the middle bands and stitched through the great top line like a thread through cloth. He had taken it, all day, to mean only one thing: the fire must be given willingly, not seized.

But phrases that frequent are never only doing one job.

"What if it applies to more than the fire," he said aloud, to the stone, to the darkening valley, to no one. "What if the whole bargain has to be free. What if the one who goes through has to choose it — truly choose it, with the door watching, with everyone watching — or none of it works."

The pulse against his palm neither confirmed nor denied. Old things rarely do. But it didn't stop, either. And somewhere behind him he heard Petra's footsteps stop in the grass, and knew she had come looking for him, and knew she had heard.

"Father Anselm." Her voice was careful. She only used his title when she was frightened or furious, and he had long ago stopped trying to tell which. "Say that again. Slowly."

He turned. The lantern-light caught her from behind, so her face was all shadow and the white flame made a halo of the ash in her hair, and he thought — with the cold clarity that comes to tired men at dusk — *she has already decided. Whatever I say next, she has already decided.*

"I said," he answered, "that a door built on willingness cannot run on compulsion. Any part of it." He nodded past her, toward where Sef sat silhouetted with the iron lantern, keeping her vigil over a flame that had once been a dragon named Edmund. "If the girl gives the fire but her heart is breaking while she gives it — if the giving is grief wearing the shape of generosity — I don't believe the door will open. I believe it will sit there. Humming. Waiting. Until the gift is clean."

"And if the gift is never clean? If she wants to give it and I want her not to, forever?" Petra's laugh had no humor in it at all. "Then we stand here until the country finishes forgetting us. Is that it? The trap has a trap inside it?"

"The best traps do." He came closer, close enough to see her face now, and lowered his voice to the register he had once used for confessions. "Petra. There's something else. Something I haven't said, because translating it felt like handling a live coal. The top line — *all others remain* — it doesn't name who the one is. The door doesn't ask for the keeper. It doesn't ask for the granddaughter. It doesn't ask for bloodline or office or worth. Read it strictly: *the one who gives the fire passes through.* Nothing about who must carry the fire to the hollow. Only who gives it."

The silence stretched between them, long enough for the wind to move the grass twice.

"Anyone can carry it," Petra said slowly. "Anyone can give it."

"Anyone willing," Anselm corrected softly. "That's the whole of the law on this tower. Willingness. From first band to last, that's all it ever says. The builders weren't cruel, Petra. They were precise. They left the choice open on purpose." He looked up at the black mass of the Spire rising against the first stars, at the door with no handle, at the hollow shaped like a burning heart that waited somewhere above them in the dark like a held breath. "Which means the trap isn't the door. The trap is that now every one of us knows the price, and every one of us is free to pay it."

Behind them, in the quiet, the iron lantern flickered — and Sef, who had seemed asleep, opened her eyes.
