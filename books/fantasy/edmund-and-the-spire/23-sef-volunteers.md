## 23. Sef Volunteers

The Spire's hum followed them down into the valley like a second heartbeat.

Petra had thought that once they stopped walking she would sleep — three days in the corpse-wagon's reek, then the road through the nameless villages, then the forgotten city where people had pressed her hands to their foreheads as if she were a relic. She had thought her body would simply fold up and be done with it. Instead she lay on her bedroll beneath the black tower's shadow and listened to it hum, low and patient, the way a kettle listens before it sings.

The fire was small. Sef had built it from tamarisk scrub, and the iron lantern sat beside her knee like a sleeping animal, its seams glowing faintly at every exhale of wind. Edmund's heart-flame. The last dragon-fire in a country that had mostly stopped existing.

Anselm slept sitting against a fallen pillar, his breviary open on his chest, rising and falling. He had spent the evening copying inscriptions by lantern-light until his eyes failed him, muttering translations half into sense: *freely given*, *the one who gives*, *alone*. He had gone quiet after that last word, the way men go quiet when they've found the trapdoor under the floor.

Petra stared at the Spire instead of sleeping. It grew out of the valley floor like a tooth out of a jaw, black stone veined with something that caught the starlight and held it a moment too long. Up close, the humming had texture. It wasn't one sound but thousands — every answered prayer since the bells rang themselves, every drowned husband and toothed field and filled well, all of it drawn up into the tower like smoke into a flue. She could almost pick out individual voices if she tried. She made herself not try.

"You're doing the thing," Sef said.

Petra turned. The girl was awake, of course. Sef slept the way cats did, in shallow installments, one ear always working. Nineteen years old and she'd never once been surprised by the dark.

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-it thing. Like it'll blink first." Sef fed a twig to the fire without looking at it. Her hands were scarred in the particular pattern of someone who had worked live coals since childhood — shiny patches across the backs of the fingers, one burn along the inside of the left wrist shaped like a crescent moon. Keeper's hands. Petra had noticed them the first day in the tannery cellar and thought, *she's too young for those hands,* and then thought, *no, she's exactly the age for them; that's what's wrong.*

"Go back to sleep."

"You first."

"I'm not tired."

"Liar." Sef said it without heat, the way she said most things. She'd grown up reciting rites to a dying dragon in a cellar that smelled of tannin and ash; she had no patience left over for polite fictions. "You've got the look. Anselm's got it too. I've seen it before."

"Where?"

"On my mother. The night before she went into the pit." Sef's voice didn't change at all. That was the terrible thing about her; grief came out of her flat and level, pre-smelted, like ore already refined. "It's the look of somebody doing arithmetic they don't want anyone else to see."

Petra looked at the fire. The arithmetic was simple enough. Anselm had translated it off the door itself: dragon-fire freely given opens the way, and the giver passes through — alone. Everyone else stays in the dying country. Everyone else stays in a country that was being forgotten out from under its own feet, whose roads now ended in hedges, whose villages were dissolving like sugar in rain.

One person lived. Everyone Petra loved stayed behind to fade.

Except.

She hadn't let herself finish the thought, but it was there anyway, patient as the Spire: *except the flame doesn't have to be carried by a living hand.* Except Maren's spellbook lay against her ribs where she'd carried it for five years, unfinished, addressed to *the one who ran.* Except there might be a way to hold a door open with something other than a life — a charm, a working, a trick of hedge-craft strong enough to fool a tower older than kingdoms. She had been turning it over all night like a stone in the pocket, looking for the flaw, finding only the flaw-shaped hole where the missing lines should be.

"Sef," she said carefully. "When we go up tomorrow. Nobody does anything until we've talked it through. All of us. Together."

"Mm," said Sef.

That *mm* should have warned her.

* * *

Morning came grey and slow, fog pooling in the valley like milk in a bowl. They ate hard bread and dried apples and drank from the skin, and Anselm read over his notes one more time, lips moving, and then they climbed the valley's spine toward the tower, and the hum rose around them until Petra felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of old charms sewn into her hem, in the scar tissue of everything she'd ever burned herself on.

The door was worse in daylight. It had no handle, no hinge, no seam — only the hollow, set at the height of a human heart, shaped exactly like a burning heart, a flame rendered in negative space. Standing before it, Petra understood that the shape wasn't decoration. It was a socket. Something was meant to fit there, and the something was currently asleep in an iron lantern in Sef's arms.

"Well," Anselm said. His voice came out scraped. "I've said the words. There's nothing in the inscriptions about—"

"I'll do it."

Petra heard the sentence land before she understood it. Then she turned, and Sef was holding the lantern out in both hands, level, steady, the way you hold anything precious over long distances — elbows locked, breath measured, a keeper's carry.

"No," Petra said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"No. Put it down."

"It makes sense." Sef's chin came up. Fog drifted between them. Behind the girl, the Spire hummed, and Petra would swear afterward that the hum leaned in. "Listen to me. My family kept the fire-pit for nine generations. Nine. You know what that means? It means every keeper who ever tended it died in it. My grandmother. My mother. It's not a job, it's a debt, and it comes due, and the only question is whether you die near the fire or far from it. I was supposed to go into the pit when Edmund finally failed. That was always the arrangement. The fire takes a keeper; the keeper keeps the fire. So this—" she lifted the lantern a fraction, an offering, "—this is just the same debt, paid somewhere better. Paid somewhere it actually buys something."

"It buys your death."

"It buys everyone else's *life.*" For the first time Sef's voice cracked, just at the seam, and Petra hated how much it moved her. "Do you know what I was? In Greyhollow? I was a girl in a cellar feeding a dying animal on a schedule written by dead women, waiting for the day the schedule killed me, and everybody in the whole city knew it and nobody thought it was sad because that's just how it goes, keepers keep. And now — now the fire can matter. Now it can open a door and get Anselm out and get the widow Marta out and get every soul in that forgotten city out, people who don't even remember their own names, and all it costs is me, and I was already spent, I was *always* going to be spent, so don't you dare stand there and tell me it doesn't make sense."

The fog moved. Somewhere above them the Spire's hum changed pitch — a fraction warmer, a fraction more attentive — and Petra felt sick with the certainty that it was listening, that it had been listening the whole time, that doors this old heard everything said in front of them.

"It doesn't make sense," Petra said quietly. "It makes *bookkeeping.* There's a difference."

"Don't."

"Nineteen years old. You think I don't know what nineteen is? I ran away at nineteen. I know exactly how much of the world you've seen and how sure you are that you've seen enough of it." Petra stepped closer. The lantern-light painted Sef's face orange from below, and up close Petra could see the exhaustion under the girl's steadiness, days of carrying a heart-flame that weighed nothing and everything. "You want to pay a debt. Fine. But nobody in your family ever chose the pit, did they? They were born into it. Born owing. And the moment someone finally offers you a door out of the arrangement, you walk straight back into it and call it freedom."

"Because it *is* freedom!" Sef's voice rang off the black stone. "Choosing the fire — choosing it, with my eyes open, for something — that's the only free thing any Underhill-keeping, rite-reciting, cellar-living keeper ever got to do! My mother didn't choose. I'm choosing!"

"And I'm telling you no."

"You don't get to tell me no."

"I am the one Maren's spell is addressed to. I am the one the god at the well talked to. I am the one Edmund asked." Petra heard her own voice go strange and low, and realized she was saying things she hadn't decided to say. "This door has been talking to me since before we reached it, Sef. Whatever happens here, it happens through me. So yes. I get to tell you no."

They stood chest to chest in the fog, the lantern between them like a held breath, and Petra saw the exact moment the girl understood — saw it land in her face not as defeat but as betrayal, which was worse, which cut deeper than any argument.

"You knew," Sef whispered. "You've known since last night. You've been sitting on some idea — some *charm*, some trick — and you weren't going to say it, you were going to wait until I was asleep or Anselm was watching and then just — what? Swap yourself in like it's nothing? Like your life's worth more than mine?"

"It's not worth more."

"Then why!"

"Because—" and there it was, the truth, ugly and obvious, and Petra made herself say it. "Because I found you. Do you understand? Five years I built a life out of small lies and salt charms and pretended it was enough, and then the bells rang and everything cracked open, and the only real thing — the only belonging I've ever had — is standing in front of me holding a lantern and asking to die. And I will not trade that. I will not buy my escape with the one thing that made staying worthwhile. That's not sacrifice, Sef. That's just running again, dressed up nobler."

Sef stared at her. The fog beaded on her lashes. When she spoke, her voice had gone very quiet and very cold, the cold of banked coals, which is hotter than flame.

"You think you're the only one who found something?" she said. "You think I don't know what I found? I found two people who looked at me and saw something other than *the keeper's granddaughter.* Anselm asks me questions about the rites like they're worth answering. You bought scale from me and called it craft. Do you know what that was worth to me? And now you want to take the one thing I have to give — the only thing — because you've decided my life belongs to you too. Everybody wants to own the keeper. Even the kind ones. *Especially* the kind ones."

"That's not fair."

"No," Sef agreed, and her voice shook now, properly shook. "None of this is fair. That's rather the point of the door."

Behind them, Anselm cleared his throat — a small, careful sound, a man stepping onto ice he suspected was thin. "There may be a third reading," he said. "Of the inscriptions. The grammar is ambiguous in the clause about the giving. *Freely given* — it doesn't specify a giver. It doesn't even specify, precisely, that the giver must—"

"Must what?" Petra and Sef said together, and then glared at each other for it.

"Must be separate from the gift," Anselm finished gently. "Or from each other. Or—" he closed his book, "—or that the giving ends when the door opens. Old door-covenants are strange. They're less contracts than invitations. And invitations, sometimes, can be renegotiated."

The hum changed again. Petra felt it pass through her sternum like a finger testing fruit at market — considering, weighing, *interested.*

Sef looked at the tower. Then at Petra. The fury in her face hadn't gone anywhere, but something else had arrived beside it, wary and unwilling: hope, or the memory of hope, the way a burn remembers heat.

"If you're lying to me," Sef said, "if you're just finding words to keep me quiet while you work out how to do it yourself — I will put this lantern down on that stone and let the fire find its own way home. Swear it. Swear you won't decide for me. Whatever happens at this door, we decide together, or it doesn't happen."

Petra thought of Maren's page ending mid-sentence. Of the hollow shaped like a burning heart. Of the god at the well saying *the spire feeds on willing sacrifice* — willing, it had said, and she had heard *willing* and thought only of herself.

"Together," she said. "I swear it."

Sef held her gaze a long moment, keeper to whatever Petra was becoming, and then nodded once, sharp, and lowered the lantern between them like a border-stone.

And the Spire, above them, hummed a note none of them had heard before — high, brief, almost glad — and in the fog behind Anselm, far down the valley road, something metallic caught the light.
