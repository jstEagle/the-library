## 24. Vane Arrives

He had left his escort at the ford three days ago, and the lie he had written in his own ledger — *verifying reports along the northern ridgeline* — sat in his breast pocket like a stone he had swallowed whole. Inquisitor Aldous Vane had falsified documents before. He had signed death warrants with a steady hand and slept well after, or well enough, which was all the Crown ever asked of him. But this was the first time he had lied to himself in ink, and the ink did not dry. It bled.

The valley opened before him on the morning of the fourth day, and he stopped his horse where the treeline broke, because there was nothing in the Crown's cartography — nothing in any map he had ever confiscated, burned, or been ordered never to look at — that had prepared him for the Spire.

It rose out of the valley floor like a tooth out of a jaw: black, curved, wrong in the way that living things are wrong, so that his eye kept trying to file it as rock and kept failing. It was not built. He understood that immediately, the way a man understands a heartbeat is not a drum. It had grown, or it was growing still, its base broad as a cathedral and its crown lost in a low grey cloud that did not move with the wind. And it hummed.

Not a sound, exactly. A hum was too small a word. It came up through the ground, through his horse's hooves, through the bones of his pelvis and spine and skull, and it was made of voices — thousands of them, tens of thousands, layered so fine they blurred into one sustained note, like a choir heard through a wall, like every prayer ever whispered into every pillow in the country rising at once and refusing to stop.

Vane had spent twenty years policing prayers. He knew their sound the way a butcher knows breathing.

His horse would go no closer. That should have told him something. He dismounted, tied the animal to an ash tree whose leaves were trembling though there was no wind, and drew his sword — not because he expected a fight, but because his hand asked for the weight of it, the way a child's hand asks for a mother's skirt in a strange house. The blade caught the grey light and looked, for the first time in his memory of it, ornamental.

*You are an officer of the Crown,* he told himself, and began to walk down into the valley.

* * *

The last mile took him two hours, because the ground itself argued with him.

It was subtle at first. Paths that bent when he wasn't looking at them. A stream he crossed twice without ever crossing back. His own footprints appearing ahead of him instead of behind, pressed into soft earth, leading the way — and he followed them, God help him, because they were going where he wanted to go and he no longer trusted his own sense of direction in a place that hummed.

Then the whispers started.

They came from the grass, from the stones, from the air just behind his left ear where no one stood. They were not threats. That was the horror of them. Inquisitors learned early to expect the accusations of the guilty — *murderer, tyrant, butcher of Greyhollow* — and Vane had long ago made his peace with those words; they were simply the cost of order, coins paid from a purse he kept separate from his soul. But these whispers said none of that.

They said: *Aldous.*

They said: *you were seven.*

They said: *the fever year. The little bed by the window. You prayed until your knees were bruised and your voice was gone, and your sister died anyway, and you decided — you decided right then, seven years old, fists against the floorboards —*

"Stop." His voice cracked across the valley like a whip. The humming did not pause. "I am Inquisitor First Rank Aldous Vane, acting under the authority of the Crown, and I command—"

Command what? A tower? A chorus? The dead?

He walked on. The whispers, mercifully or cruelly, walked with him.

* * *

He found them at the base of the Spire, small against its vast black curve, like insects against a whale. Four figures. He counted them twice, professionally, before he let himself feel anything about them: the disgraced priest Anselm, gaunt as a winter branch, standing with his hands open at his sides in a posture Vane recognized from a hundred interrogations — surrender, or its performance. The dragon-girl Sef, iron lantern hanging from her grip, its light leaking between the bars like breath through teeth. The hedge-witch Petra Underhill, whom he had hunted across half a country, standing closest to the tower with her palm flat against its surface as though taking its pulse.

And the fourth figure was not a figure at all. It was a shape of light and grief at the Spire's hollow heart — a burning hollow, heart-shaped, exactly the size of something enormous being given away — and Vane understood, with the cold clarity that had made his career, that he had arrived at the end of something. He did not yet know whether it was the end of the country, the end of his duty, or merely the end of the man who had believed duty was enough.

"Sword," he said. His voice carried strangely in the humming air, flattened, as if the Spire were listening and found human speech quaint. "Down. All of you. By authority of—"

He stopped.

Because Petra Underhill turned around, and she did not look afraid. She looked tired — bone-tired, road-tired, tired in the way of someone who has already fought the battle that matters and is only waiting for the smaller ones to catch up — and she looked at him, and she said, gently, horribly:

"You've been praying the whole way here."

* * *

"I have not." The denial came out fast and hot, and he hated it, because it was the voice of a boy caught stealing, not an officer of the Crown. "I do not pray. Prayer is the disease. I am the cure."

"No," Anselm said quietly. The defrocked priest had not moved, but his eyes had gone soft in a way Vane distrusted more than hatred. "No, you haven't. Not aloud. Not in words. But I watched you come down that hillside, Inquisitor, and I'll tell you what I saw. I saw a man walking like a man carrying a question he's held so long it's grown into his skeleton. I buried enough of those men to know the shape."

"The gods are silent," Vane said. "They have been silent for a generation. That is doctrine. That is fact. Everything since the bells rang themselves is fever, witchcraft, contagion —"

"— and yet." Anselm spread his hands. "Here we stand inside the fever. Listen to it. Tell me that's witchcraft. Tell me one hedge-witch in Greyhollow could make a sound like that."

Vane's sword arm trembled. He noticed it the way one notices a tooth beginning to ache: with dread, and with the knowledge that the dread will only make it worse.

He had rehearsed this arrest for four hundred miles. He had composed the speech somewhere past the second burned village, refining it against the rhythm of his horse's hooves: *In the name of the Crown, in the name of order, in the name of the silence that keeps us sane.* He had imagined their faces — the witch's guilt, the girl's defiance, the priest's weary contempt — and he had armed himself against each one.

He had not imagined this: that they would simply stand there, in the shadow of an impossible tower, and let him hear himself.

"Twenty years," he said. The sword point drifted downward, degree by degree, and he could not say when he had allowed it. "Twenty years I have done the Crown's work. I have burned charm-stalls. I have taken witches to the pyre who wept and swore their workings were kindnesses — charms against fever, knots against nightmares — and I burned them anyway, because the law is the wall that stands between us and the dark, and a wall does not negotiate. I believed—" His voice failed. He tried again, lower. "I believed the silence was mercy. I believed the gods had died and left us free, and freedom meant rules, and rules meant me."

"And now?" Petra asked.

"Now the bells ring themselves." He said it flatly, a report, the only language he had left. "Now a widow in Marrowdale has her drowned husband back, dripping, wrong, standing at her door asking to be let in. Now a farmer's field blooms with teeth. Now the country is coming apart at its edges — villages I cannot find, roads that end in fog, letters returning unread — and my own reports describe a world I no longer recognize, and I sign them, and every signature feels like—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Like praying to the wrong god."

The humming swelled. Just slightly. Just enough.

And Vane — Inquisitor First Rank, breaker of the unlicensed, the wall that does not negotiate — felt his knees inform him, quietly and without appeal, that they intended to bend.

He went down onto the valley grass with his sword flat in the dirt before him, and it was not surrender, he told himself, it was positioning, it was tactics, it was anything except what it was, which was a man kneeling in a church he had spent his life burning down.

* * *

"Answer me," he said.

Petra crouched before him. Up close he could see the road on her — the dust in the seams of her sleeves, the salt-cracked lips, the burn scar on her left hand he had read about in a dozen confiscated letters. She looked, he thought absurdly, like every hedge-witch he had ever condemned, and nothing at all like the monster his edicts described.

"Answer you how?"

"The night the bells rang." His voice came out ragged, stripped of rank, stripped of everything but the thing underneath, the thing he had carried since he was seven years old with bruised knees beside a small bed by a window. "My sister. Her name was Liss. She was nine. The fever took her in six days, and I prayed — I was a child, I didn't know doctrine yet, I prayed the way children pray, with everything, with my whole body — and she died anyway. And the priests told me the gods had reasons too deep for us to know. And later the philosophers told me there were no gods at all, only weather and chance. And I chose the philosophers, because at least they didn't lie to my face." His hands were shaking. He let them. "But lately — since the bells, since the answered prayers, since the widow's drowned husband came home — I have lain awake in camp wondering, wondering like a child again, and I cannot — I need to know—"

He looked up at her. At all of them. At the impossible tower that hummed with ten thousand voices.

"Were we heard?" The word tore coming out. "All those years. All those people kneeling in the pews saying nothing back, believing anyway, building their whole lives on a silence — was anyone there? Did it matter? Was Liss's prayer sitting in some dark somewhere while she died, unread, unheard, while I got up off my bruised knees and decided the universe was empty because the universe had ignored me?" His voice broke entirely, then, and he did not try to repair it. "Please. You've spoken to them. The witch speaks to them. The priest — whatever he is now — you've all touched this thing. Someone tell me if we were heard. Even if the answer is no. Even if the answer is cruel. I have policed a miracle I don't understand for a season and I am telling you I would rather be damned with an answer than promoted with another silence."

The valley held its breath. Even the Spire's hum seemed to lean in.

It was Anselm who spoke first, and his voice was gentle in a way that must have cost him, coming from a man whose faith was anger wearing vestments.

"I preached to empty pews and emptier heavens for eleven years," Anselm said. "I told my congregation the gods had abandoned us on purpose, and they defrocked me for it, and I have hated them for it — hated the gods, I mean, with a clean and nourishing hate, the kind a man can build a life on." He knelt too, slowly, joints cracking, until he was level with Vane. "And then I watched a minor god of water beg a hedge-witch for an offering at a dry well. Beg. Do you understand? Not demand. *Beg.* Whatever is answering our prayers now, Inquisitor, it isn't a king on a throne ignoring petitions. It's dying men shouting down a corridor, hoping someone, anyone, calls back. And I think—" his voice wavered, "—I think it was always like that. I think the silence was never contempt. I think the gods have been drowning alongside us, and drowning men cannot answer, and we mistook their silence for absence for so long that we built an entire kingdom on the mistake."

"But heard?" Vane pressed. "Was the prayer *heard*?"

"I don't know," Anselm said. "I honestly don't know. But I'll tell you what I believe tonight, kneeling in the dirt next to the man who came to arrest me. I believe that a prayer is never wasted. I believe it goes somewhere. I believe it pools, the way water pools, waiting for a low place." He nodded toward the Spire. "Maybe that's what this is. Maybe every unanswered prayer for a hundred years pooled somewhere, and this is the low place, and it's finally spilling over. Maybe your sister's prayer is in there. Maybe they're all in there. Maybe that's the sound."

Vane turned to look at the Spire — really look, for the first time, not as a target or an anomaly but as a vessel — and the hum resolved, just for a moment, just barely, into something almost like words. Almost like names. Thousands upon thousands of names, held, remembered, kept.

His eyes were wet. He did not wipe them. There was no one left to perform for.

* * *

"Inquisitor." Petra's voice, quiet. "There's something you should know before you decide what happens next. This door opens for a fire freely given, and the one who gives it passes through alone. Everyone else stays in a country that's dying. We're arguing about who pays. So if you're going to arrest us, arrest us now, while it's still simple."

Vane looked at his sword in the grass. Twenty years of doctrine lay along that blade, and he examined it the way a man examines a tool that has begun to feel foreign in his hand.

"If I give the order," he said slowly, "my escort is three days behind me. They'd follow me here because I'm Inquisitor First Rank and they trust the wall." He laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "And I would be ordering them to drag a girl and a lantern away from the only door out of a drowning country, in the name of a Crown that may not exist by autumn. I know what my edicts say. I wrote them." He closed his eyes. "God forgive me. I wrote them."

"You're not arresting us," Sef said. It was not a question. The dragon-keeper's granddaughter stood with her lantern and her chin up, nineteen years old and already old in the way of people raised around dying things, and she looked at Vane with neither fear nor forgiveness — only assessment, keeper to creature. "You came all this way alone. You lied in your own reports. Men like you don't break by accident."

"No," Vane agreed. "We break the way walls break. Slowly, and then all at once, and usually from the inside."

He rose. His knees protested; he let them. He picked up his sword, and they all watched to see what he would do with it, and what he did was slide it back into its scabbard with a finality that sounded, in the humming air, like a door closing.

Then he unbuckled the scabbard belt altogether, and laid sword and scabbard both on the grass at the Spire's black root, the way a man lays down arms at a shrine — or the way a man sets down a weight he has carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"I have a prayer I've never said aloud," he said. His voice was steady now, scraped clean, emptied of everything except what remained. "Twenty years I've carried it. I'd like to say it here, if the listening things will allow it. And then—" he looked at the hollow burning heart of the Spire, at the witch, at the girl with the lantern, at the priest who had knelt beside him in the dirt, "—and then I'll tell you what's coming up that valley behind me, because the Crown's reinforcements are two days out with orders to burn this tower and everyone near it, and you are going to need every hour I can buy you."

Petra stared at him. "Why?"

"Because I asked you a question and you didn't turn me away," Vane said simply. "That's more mercy than I've shown in twenty years, and I find I want to owe less than I do."

He knelt once more — willingly this time, no pretense of tactics left — bowed his head, and spoke at last, into the humming dark, the prayer he had carried since he was seven years old and had never dared give a shape or a voice:

"*Liss. If you can hear me — wherever they keep the prayers — I'm sorry I stopped.*"

The Spire's hum deepened. For one impossible moment, in the space between one pulse of sound and the next, Vane would swear for the rest of his life that the note bent — bent warm, bent glad, the way a room changes when someone you mourn walks into it — and that somewhere in the ten thousand voices, one small voice said his name.

*Aldous.*

Then the moment passed, and the valley was only a valley, and the Spire was only a tower, and the reinforcements were two days out.

Vane stood, wiped his face with the heel of his hand like a soldier, and turned to the others.

"Now," he said. "Tell me what you need. Because we have very little time, and I intend to spend all of mine here."
