## 25. Maren's Ghost

The Spire did not let her sleep.

Petra had told herself she would rest — they all needed it, Sef curled around the iron lantern like a child around a hearthstone, Anselm sitting upright against the tower's black flank with his breviary open on his knees and his eyes closed in something that was not quite prayer. The valley was quiet. The Spire hummed. She had thought the hum would be like a hive, a drone to lose herself in.

It was not a drone. It was listening.

She felt it the way she had once felt Maren watching her over the rim of a mortar: that pressure between the shoulder blades, patient and total, the attention of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Every answered prayer in the country fed this thing. Every desperate voice in every half-dissolved village poured itself into the stone above her, and now the stone turned its attention down, and in, and found her.

*You,* the Spire said, without words, the way a well says *water*. *You are the one who ran.*

"I didn't—" Petra said aloud, and heard how young her own voice sounded, how much it sounded like a girl of nineteen standing in a kitchen doorway with a spellbook burning a hole through her apron.

The hum deepened. The hollow in the door — the hollow shaped like a burning heart, the one that wanted dragon-fire freely given — began to glow. Not red. Not gold. A color Petra knew from five years of dreams: the green-gold of foxfire in a jar, of will-o'-wisp over a marsh, of candlelight through honey. Maren's working-light. The light of a hedge-witch's kitchen at midnight.

The glow spread up the Spire's face like frost blooming across a window, and where it spread, the black stone went transparent, and beyond the transparency there was a kitchen.

Maren's kitchen. Down to the crack in the third flagstone that Petra had tripped over a thousand times. Down to the bundle of dried yarrow hanging from the beam, gone brown and dusty because no one had taken it down. Down to the smell — and this was the part that undid her, this was the part that made her knees go — woodsmoke and rosemary and the particular scorched-iron scent of a cauldron that had been used for dye as often as for charms.

"Come in, then," said the memory, or the ghost, or whatever the Spire had kept of her. "You're letting the cold out."

---

Five years fell away like a coat shrugged off.

She was not in her own body. She understood that distantly, the way one understands a dream is a dream even while drowning in it. She stood at the edge of the kitchen — near the door, always near the door, even in memory she positioned herself for running — and watched Maren Underhill work.

Her mistress had been old when Petra came to her, older than any hedge-witch had a right to be, and in five years Petra had never seen her look tired until the last month. Now she saw it plainly, seeing it as the Spire saw it, seeing it with the terrible clarity of hindsight: Maren had been tired for years. Tired the way a wall is tired, holding something up.

The spellbook lay open on the table. *Her* book — the book Petra had carried out of this room under her shawl, the book she had not opened in five years because opening it would have meant knowing what she'd done. It lay open now to pages Petra had never read, and Maren's hand moved across them, and Petra read over her shoulder the way she had read over it a thousand midnights, hungry and furtive and certain she was stealing.

Except.

Except the hand paused, and dipped the quill, and wrote in the margin — small, cramped, in the shorthand of craft that Petra could still read if she let herself — and what it wrote was:

*Not a vessel. Never a vessel. A door.*

Petra's breath stopped somewhere below her ribs.

On the table beside the book sat the vial. Grave-dust, dark as dried blood, stoppered with wax. Beside it, a knot of Petra's own hair, cut — when? She tried to remember anyone cutting her hair and found nothing, only waking one morning that final autumn to find her braid shorter by a finger's width and Maren saying the mice must have got at it, girl, you sleep too sound.

*A door,* the margin said again, in Maren's hand, in Maren's shorthand, and beneath it, in letters pressed so hard the quill had nearly torn the page:

*For the one who runs. Because she will run. Because I am going to make sure of it.*

---

The kitchen shifted. The Spire was not showing her one night; it was showing her the whole last season, compressed, layered, the way a tree shows its years in rings. Maren at the table by lamplight. Maren in the garden at dusk, cutting hair from a sleeping apprentice's braid with scissors that made no sound. Maren at the grave behind the cottage — *whose* grave, Petra had never asked, had never dared ask, had assumed the worst, had built five years of guilt on the assumption — kneeling in the wet grass with the vial open, whispering into it not a binding but a blessing.

*Keep her,* Maren whispered to the dust. *That's all I ask of you dead ones who owe me. Keep her when I can't.*

And then the nights came faster, and the visions came with them, and Petra watched her mistress see the future and be broken by it.

Because Maren saw it. That was the thing Petra had never known, the thing the Spire poured into her now like cold water into a lung: Maren had *seen* it. Not guessed, not feared — seen, in the basin, in the smoke, in the last functioning scrying of a god who was already dying but not yet silent. She saw the temples go quiet. She saw the bells ring themselves. She saw prayers come back wrong, husbands dripping, fields full of teeth. She saw the country begin to be forgotten, village by village, name by name, the borders fraying like old cloth. She saw the Spire.

And she saw Petra in it. That was the worst of it. In every version of the future Maren scryed, in every shape the coming ruin took, there was a girl with quick hands and a careful heart standing at the foot of a black tower, and in most of those versions the girl died there — died badly, died uselessly, died before she understood what the door was for.

Unless she ran first.

Unless she ran carrying everything she'd need and hating herself for it, because hatred would keep the book shut, and the book shut would keep the spell unfinished, and the spell unfinished would keep her alive long enough to reach the place where finishing it would matter.

"Oh," Petra said. Her voice came out of her own mouth, back in the present, back in the valley, and cracked in the middle. "Oh, you cruel old woman."

The kitchen-memory did not answer, but Maren-in-the-memory looked up from her writing, directly at the place where Petra stood at the edge of the scene, and for a moment the eyes were not the past's eyes at all. They were the Spire's. Green-gold. Knowing.

*You thought you stole it,* the gaze said. *Good. That was the point.*

---

The last night assembled itself around her piece by piece, and Petra could not look away, though she tried, though she dug her nails into her palms hard enough to bleed.

Maren packing the satchel. Not hiding it — leaving it by the door where a girl with quick hands would find it, would inventory it, would count the coins and the dried food and the charm-components and understand exactly what kind of journey it was provisioned for. Long. One-way. Alone.

Maren writing the letter and then burning it, because a letter could be argued with, and the argument would keep Petra in the house, and the house was going to be the first thing to go. Petra caught one line of it as it curled in the flames: *If you are reading this, I have failed, and I am sorry, and run anyway—*

Maren taking the spellbook off its shelf and opening it to the unfinished working and reading it aloud, softly, alone, the way a mother rehearses a speech she knows will break her child's heart. *A spell to hold a door open.* Addressed, in the heading, in formal script: *To the one who ran.*

Maren stopping. Setting down the quill. Looking at the page for a long time.

And then — and this Petra would carry longer than anything else, this she would carry past the end of the world if the world had an end left in it — Maren smiled. Small. Wry. The smile she'd worn when Petra burned her first batch of luck-knots and then sold them anyway to a farmer who swore his cows calved easier for a year after.

"You'll hate me," Maren said to the empty kitchen, to the future, to the girl standing at the edge of a memory five years wide. "That's all right. Hate keeps a body moving. Love makes her stay, and staying here is dying, my girl, staying here is being forgotten along with everything else. So hate me. Run. Take the book and don't read it, take the dust and don't use it, take my name and curse it every market day. And when you get where you're going — when you stand in front of the thing I saw —"

The memory flickered. The Spire's attention tightened; Petra felt it lean closer, felt the whole humming weight of the tower bend toward this moment, because this moment was the reason the Spire had called her here, this moment was the hinge on which everything since had turned.

"— finish the sentence," Maren said. "I couldn't. I'm not strong enough to hold a door and I know it, and the spell knows it, and that's why the page ends where it ends. It needs someone the door will actually listen to. Someone who chose to stay somewhere. Someone who belongs." The old woman laughed, low and rough. "Gods help me, I spent five years trying to teach you belonging and the only way you'd ever learn it was to lose it first."

She dipped the quill one last time. She wrote four more words at the bottom of the unfinished page — words Petra had never seen, because she had never opened the book, because she had never been able to bear it — and then she closed the cover, wrapped it in oilcloth, and set it on the table where a girl with quick hands would find it.

The four words, written in Maren's steady, unhesitating hand, were:

*I was never angry.*

---

The kitchen dissolved.

Petra came back to herself on her knees in the grass at the foot of the Spire, and she was weeping, and she did not remember starting, and she did not make any effort to stop. The sound that came out of her was not dignified. It was the sound of a girl of nineteen standing in a doorway with a stolen book, and of a woman of twenty-four kneeling in a valley with the same book in her pack, and both of them crying for the same old woman who had loved them in the only language either of them could survive: the language of being sent away.

Sef was awake. Of course Sef was awake; Sef slept the way a fire-pit sleeps, which is to say not at all, only banked. She crouched at the edge of the grass with the lantern held against her chest, its white heart-flame throwing her shadow huge up the Spire's face, and she didn't touch Petra, and she didn't speak, and Petra loved her for both.

Anselm had his eyes open now. He had closed the breviary. He looked at Petra the way he looked at scripture he couldn't parse — carefully, hungrily, aware that getting it wrong would cost him.

"What did it show you?" he asked.

"She sent me away." Petra's voice was wrecked. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and it didn't help. "Five years. Five years I thought — I took the book, I took the dust, I thought she meant to pour the gods' last breath into me, I thought I was saving my life by ruining hers, and she *packed my bag*, Anselm. She cut my hair while I slept and put it in the vial and blessed it. She wrote the spell *for* me. She ended it mid-sentence on purpose because it can't be finished by her, it has to be finished by—" She stopped. Looked at the hollow in the door, shaped like a burning heart. Looked at the lantern in Sef's arms.

"By someone the door will listen to," Anselm said slowly. "Someone who stayed."

"The spell holds a door open." Petra got to her feet. Her legs shook and held. "That's what it does. That's what it's *for*. Not escape. Not a vessel. A door that stays open — for everyone. She saw this coming a generation ago and she couldn't finish the fix, so she built me instead." A laugh tore out of her, ragged, half grief and half something that might, in time, become gratitude. "I've been carrying the answer in my pack for five years and I was too ashamed to open it."

Sef spoke for the first time. Her voice was very quiet. "What did she write? At the end. You said — you said she wrote something."

Petra looked at the girl who had inherited a death she hadn't chosen, and thought about inheritance, and about what gets passed down, and about how Maren had never once, in five years, sent word, because sending word would have been cruelty dressed as kindness, and Maren had never been cruel.

"She wrote: *I was never angry.*"

Sef's arms tightened around the lantern. The heart-flame inside flared — bright, brief, almost glad — and in its light Petra saw the Spire's hollow pulse once, twice, like a throat swallowing.

And then the hum changed key.

It rose. It sharpened. It went from listening to *urgent*, a sound like a bell struck and not allowed to fade, and far away to the north — so far they should not have been able to hear it, and heard it anyway, through the ground, through their bones — something vast and old simply stopped.

The Spire's glow guttered. Somewhere behind them, in the dark of the valley, Edmund lifted his ruined head and made a sound none of them had ever heard a dragon make, and Anselm said, in a voice stripped of everything but arithmetic:

"That was a province. Just now. That was a whole province, and everyone in it."

Petra picked up her pack. Inside it, against her spine, the stolen book felt different — lighter, warmer, like a hand resting between her shoulders, pushing her forward.

"Hold the door open," she said. "All right, old woman. Show me the sentence."
