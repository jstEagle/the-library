## 26. The Country Ends a Little More

The letter came back to her on the third morning, folded and stamped with a mark she had never seen before: a black circle, empty at its center.

Sef turned it over twice in the grey light of their camp beneath a leaning rowan. "That's not a postmark," she said. "That's nothing. That's ink where writing should be."

Petra took it from her. The letter was Sef's own hand — she recognized the cramped, stubborn slant of it — addressed to her cousin in Marrowfield, a town three days behind them on the road they'd traveled. The seal was unbroken. The wax had never been cracked because there had been no one to crack it. Across the front, in a clerk's careful bureaucratic script, someone had written: *No such address.*

"Marrowfield exists," Sef said. Her voice did something strange on the word *exists*, as if she were testing whether it would hold weight. "I've *been* to Marrowfield. My mother sold tallow there every autumn. There's a fountain with a fish on it that spits sideways when the wind comes off the hills."

Anselm crouched by the cold fire pit and fed a twig to the embers without looking up. "There's no wind off the hills anymore," he said quietly. "I noticed it two nights ago. The air has stopped moving through this country like blood stops moving through a dying limb."

Nobody answered him. Nobody wanted to answer him. The twig caught, flared, and went out again almost immediately, as though even fire had begun to doubt itself here.

* * *

Vane stood at the window of the provincial garrison at Coldharbour Ford and watched a courier ride into the yard and then, halfway across it, simply stop — not the horse, not the man, but the *riding*. The horse kept its legs moving for three more strides, an automaton of habit, and then both animal and rider stood still in the middle of the yard like figures in a painting of a courtyard, and did not move again.

"Fetch him," Vane said.

Two soldiers went out. They reached the rider, touched his shoulder, spoke to him. He looked at them with mild, pleasant incomprehension, the look of a man woken gently from a nap, and asked them — Vane read it on his lips through the frosted glass — *where is this?*

They told him. Coldharbour Ford. He shook his head slowly. He said something else, and the younger soldier turned back toward the garrison with a face gone the color of porridge, and Vane met him in the doorway.

"What did he say?"

"He said, sir—" The soldier swallowed. "He said he doesn't remember leaving anywhere. He says he can't remember the road he was on. He remembers his mother's name and the prayer for the dead and nothing between yesterday and the middle of the ford."

Inside the courier's satchel: dispatches from three towns. Two were ordinary. The third was from a place called Wethering Stile, and the paper it was written on was blank. Not empty — blank. The ink had not faded; it had never decided to be there. The clerk's handwriting simply stopped existing past the salutation, *To the Crown's Grace at Coldharbour, greetings,* and below that the page was smooth as lake ice.

Vane held it to the light. He held it to a candle flame, looking for the ghost of pressed letters. Nothing. Somewhere south of the Ford, a man had sat down with a pen and tried to describe his town to his king, and the country had declined to be described.

He thought of the kneeling woman in the interrogation cell, months ago now, whose prayers had come true too literally, too cruelly, too *old* — and how she had wept and said, *they're answering because they're going out, sir, you shout when the house is burning.* He had put it down to hysteria. He had written *hysteria* in his report himself, in his own precise hand.

He looked at the blank page for a long time.

Then he opened his ledger, and beneath the day's entries he wrote, small, in a corner where no superior's eye was likely to fall: *Wethering Stile — confirm existence. Send riders. Do not file until confirmed.*

It was the first report he had ever delayed. It felt like stepping off a cliff in the dark and finding, instead of ground, a question.

* * *

The province behind them was called the Vailmark, or had been. Anselm said the name the way you say the name of someone recently buried — carefully, so as not to wake anything or disturb anything.

"It was grain country," he told them as they walked, because walking was all there was to do, and talking kept the silence from getting ideas. "When I was a novitiate we processed through it every harvest. Forty villages. A bell tower in nearly every one. The Vailmark bell-ringers were famous — they could ring changes for six hours without repeating themselves." He smiled at the memory, and the smile hurt to look at. "Old Brother Cade used to say you could navigate the whole province blindfolded just by listening. Each tower had its own voice. Thistlebrook rang flat. Marrowfield rang sweet. And Wethering Stile—"

He stopped walking.

"Sef," Petra said. "What is it?"

"Wethering Stile rang sharp. Always half a tone high. Every year, without fail, and every year the bishop sent a man to tune it and every year within a month it drifted sharp again, and eventually they gave up and called it the bell's character." Anselm's hands had gone very still at his sides. "I haven't heard a single bell in four days. Have either of you? One bell?"

They hadn't. Petra realized it as he asked — realized the silence the way you realize a toothache only when someone presses the spot. Four days of road, and no bells, and no dogs barking at their passage, and no cock crowing at dawn, and yesterday no birds at all over a hedgerow thick with berries that no bird had touched.

"The villages aren't being destroyed," Anselm said. "Destruction leaves ruins. Ruins have names. This is—" He searched for it, and found it, and hated it. "This is what happens to a thing that was never quite believed in. You know how a dream fades? Not torn out. *Faded.* The edges go first. Then the middle. Then you're left holding the feeling of it without the shape."

"And the people?" Sef asked. Her voice was flat, which was how she sounded when she was most afraid; Petra had learned that about her, the way she'd learned the dragon's moods, reading the surface to find the depth. "The people in those villages. Are they fading too?"

Anselm didn't answer, which was an answer.

That night they came over a rise and saw, in the valley below, a village with lights in its windows — real lights, warm ones, woodsmoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless air. Relief went through the three of them like water through a cracked jug, and they hurried down toward it, and it wasn't until they reached the first house that Petra understood why no dog had come out to bark at them.

The door stood open. Inside, a table was set for supper. Three bowls, three spoons, bread cut and going stale in the windless air. The chairs were pushed back as if the family had risen mid-meal — but no, Petra thought, looking closer, no, they hadn't risen. The bowls were full. The spoons were clean. It was a supper set out by someone who remembered suppers but could no longer remember eating them, a ritual performed by muscle-memory alone, by a house dreaming itself.

In the second house, a woman sat by a hearth with a sleeping child in her lap, and she rocked it, and rocked it, and when Petra knelt in front of her and said *good evening, mistress,* the woman looked at her with eyes like wells and said, "You'll stay for supper? I've set out bowls. I think. Have I set out bowls?"

"How many children do you have?" Petra asked gently.

The woman looked down at the sleeping child. Her brow creased with genuine effort, the effort of a person doing sums in a language they used to speak. "One," she said at last, uncertainly. "Or. There was a christening gown. Why would there be a christening gown?" She began to rock faster. "Why would there be a gown if there was only one?"

Petra stayed the night in that house. She didn't sleep. She sat across from the woman and made her say things over and over — her own name (she got as far as *Maren* once, which made Petra's chest seize, before the woman corrected herself to something vaguer), her village's name (she offered three different ones, all plausible, none matching), the prayer for the dead (which she knew perfectly, word for word, better than she knew her own child's face).

By dawn the woman had forgotten Petra entirely, and greeted her as a stranger, and offered her supper.

They left bread and coin on the table. It seemed obscene. It seemed like paying rent to a ghost.

Outside, Sef was waiting with the iron lantern, and inside the lantern Edmund's heart-flame had changed. Petra saw it before Sef said anything — the flame that had burned steady gold since the cellar beneath the tannery now flickered in a rhythm, quick-quick-slow, quick-quick-slow, like something counting down.

"He can feel it," Sef said. "The flame's tied to the old country, the deep roots of it, bedrock and boundary-stones. He says—" she listened to something Petra couldn't hear, head tilted, lips moving — "he says the Vailmark isn't the first. There are holes all over. Like moth-work in wool. But the holes are joining up."

"How long?" Petra asked.

Edmund answered through Sef, and the girl's voice came out strange, doubled, as if the dragon spoke through some hollow place in her that grief had carved and left open. "*Weeks,*" Sef said. "*Perhaps fewer. The Spire holds the door, and the door holds the shape, and the shape is forgetting itself faster than any of us can carry the memory of it.*"

* * *

Anselm dreamed of the Spire that night, the way he had dreamed of it every night since they'd turned north, and in the dream the hum of it had changed key.

He woke with the melody stuck in his teeth. He was a priest; he knew music the way farmers know weather; and what frightened him was not the tune itself but what it resembled. Every sacred tradition kept one piece of music in reserve, one mode reserved for extremity — the tone you sang over the dying, the wail at the walls of a besieged city, the keening note that meant *the end is not coming, the end is here, sit down, weep, begin.* He had sung it twice in his life. Both times over actual bodies.

The Spire was singing it in its sleep, sixty miles away, and the hum was growing frantic the way a heart grows frantic, and he lay in the frost under the rowan tree and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only in the hour before dawn, that they were not racing an army or an edict or an inquisitor. They were racing a funeral. The whole country was a body, and somewhere in it a great organ was failing, and the question was not whether the patient would die but whether anyone would arrive in time to hear the last confession.

He got up quietly, so as not to wake the others, and walked to the edge of the camp, and did the thing he had sworn off doing years ago in a pulpit in front of a congregation that had stopped believing him.

He prayed.

Not the elegant prayers of the liturgy. The ugly kind. The kind you pray with your fists. *If any of you are still there — you dying, shouting, indiscriminate old powers — hear me. We are coming. We are bringing fire. Give us the weeks. Just the weeks.*

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. The gods were going out like lamps in a house where no one lives.

But far to the north, faint as a pulse under a wrist, he thought he felt the hum change — one beat, steadier, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep and listened.

Or as if he wanted it. He honestly could no longer tell the difference, and he suspected that was what faith had always been, and suspected Maren Underhill — wherever her ghost walked — would have laughed at him for taking a lifetime to learn it.

* * *

On the sixth day they reached the crossroads at Thistlebrook, and the signpost had grown a seventh arm overnight.

Petra counted it three times. Six roads met at Thistlebrook; she had the map in her head from Anselm's recitation, six arms, six destinations. The post now bore seven, and the seventh pointed off at an angle that matched no compass direction — slightly north of north and also slightly west of west, a direction that made her eyes water to follow, like trying to look at the inside of her own eyelid. The board at its end was blank. Not worn blank. Blank the way the letter had been blank. Wood that had never gotten around to having words.

And nailed beneath it, in a clerk's official hand, a Crown notice:

*By order of the Office of Inquisition: the settlement of Thistlebrook is hereby struck from the rolls. Travelers are advised that no such place exists. Any persons claiming residence, origin, or commerce therein are guilty of fraud against the Crown and will be treated accordingly.*

"They've declared it out of existence," Sef said. "Before it finished going. They've — they've *filed paperwork* on a hole in the world."

"No." Anselm touched the notice, and his fingers trembled. "Look at the date. This order is eleven days old. Someone in Coldharbour noticed the tax rolls failing, the tithe records emptying, and did the only thing a bureaucracy knows how to do with a problem it cannot understand." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Petra had ever heard him make. "We erase it officially so no one has to admit it erased itself. Gods forgive us. We're helping it along. Every roll struck, every name removed from every record — we're teaching the country to forget itself faster. We're the moth."

Behind them, far to the south, the horizon did something Petra had no word for and suspected no one alive had a word for. It didn't darken. It didn't blur. It simply became *less* — less detailed, less certain, the way a face becomes less when you try to recall it years later, all general impression and no particular feature. Where the Vailmark had been there was now a softness, a smudge, a region of the world rendered in the style of a bad memory.

Sef lifted the lantern. The flame inside had gone white at its core, and it was flickering quicker now, quick-quick-quick, and through the girl's mouth the dragon said, in a voice like coals settling:

"*Come. Whatever remains of me, come now. The door is the last thing remembering, and even doors forget.*"

They ran north, the three of them, toward the hum only Anselm could hear, while behind them a province the size of a small sea quietly, politely, without violence or complaint, stopped being — and ahead, growing louder with every mile like a heart heard through a wall, the Spire sang its frantic dying note into a country running out of time to have a name.
