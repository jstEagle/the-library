## 27. Edmund's Last Fire

The valley smelled of iron and rain the morning Edmund began to die.

Sef knew it before she knew she knew it — the way a keeper knows, in the marrow, in the soles of her feet against the ground. The fire-pit beneath the tannery had taught her that language years ago: the small tremors, the change in the air's weight, the way smoke stops rising and starts *leaning*. She woke in the lee of a fallen wall with the lantern cold against her hip and thought, *today*, and then lay still for a long moment because thinking it made it real, and there was no part of her that wanted it to be real.

Beside her, Petra slept sitting up, chin on her chest, the spellbook open-faced in her lap like a bird that had landed there and meant to stay. Anselm was already awake, of course. Priests woke at dawn even when they had no god to wake for; it was habit worn down to something like bone.

"He's calling," Sef said.

Anselm looked at her. "I don't hear anything."

"You wouldn't." She stood. Her knees cracked. Nineteen years old and her knees cracked like an old woman's; the fire-pit did that, or the grief did, or both were the same thing wearing different coats. "It's not sound. It's the opposite of sound. It's the quiet he leaves behind him getting bigger."

She unhooked the lantern from her belt and held it up. Inside, behind three layers of iron mesh, Edmund's heart-flame turned slowly, sullenly orange, like a coal deciding whether to be done with the world.

*He's calling,* she said again, to the flame this time, and the flame guttered once — yes — and she closed her eyes and let the morning have her.

* * *

They found him in the shallow bowl of the valley where the hills folded together like hands cupped around something precious, or something dying. He had dragged himself out of the ravine in the night. Sef could read the whole story in the ground: the furrows where his claws had dug, the wide sweep of his tail flattening the bracken, the places he'd stopped to rest and the grass had browned beneath him not from fire but from simple, enormous weight, the way a chair wears smooth under a man who has sat in it too long.

Edmund lay on his side with one wing half-spread across the hillside, a sail with no wind left in it. His scales, which had been the grey-green of deep water, had gone the color of ash that has been rained on. His chest rose and fell, rose and fell, each breath longer than the last, and between breaths there was a silence that kept growing, and Sef understood that this was what she had meant: he wasn't calling out. He was calling *in*. Drawing everything toward him. The way a fire draws air.

"Edmund." Petra went to him first, because of course she did, because Petra had never once in all the time Sef had known her hesitated at the edge of another creature's pain. She put her hand flat against the great ridge of his jaw, below the eye, where the scales grew thin and you could feel the blood moving underneath like a river under ice.

His eye opened. It was the size of a cartwheel and the color of old honey and it found Petra first, and then, rolling with terrible slow patience, it found Sef.

*Sef.*

Not sound. Never sound. It arrived in her the way heat arrives through a door — pressure first, then meaning.

"I'm here," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "I'm here, and I've got your fire, and I know the rites. All of them. Even the ones I hate."

A sound moved through him then, low enough that she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it with her ears, and she chose to believe it was a laugh, because the other thing it could have been was worse.

*You always hated them,* Edmund said. *Your mother hated them too. She used to say the words wrong on purpose, small wrongs, just to spite them.*

"She told me they had to be perfect."

*She told me they had to be perfect.* The honey eye blinked, slow as a season turning. *Keepers lie to their daughters the same way keepers lie to their dragons. It is how love survives being duty.*

Sef felt that go into her somewhere deep and unguarded, and she turned away from it, busying her hands with the straps of her pack, because if she let her face do what it wanted to do in front of a dragon who could see everything, she would come apart before noon, and the rites took until dark.

* * *

The old way went like this:

At midmorning, the washing. Not with water — water insulted a dragon, reminded it of the sea that had drowned its ancestors' pride — but with ash. Sef walked the length of Edmund's body with a basket of cold hearth-ash from every fire they'd camped beside since Greyhollow, and she rubbed it into the seams between his scales, working with her bare hands, saying nothing, because the washing was silent work. The ash drew out the last of the oil his skin made and gave his body back its old smell: woodsmoke and hot stone and, underneath, faintly, the green smell of the egg he had hatched from two hundred years ago. By the time she reached the tip of his tail her arms ached and her fingernails were black and Edmund had begun, very quietly, to purr — a vibration too deep for purring really, more a settling, the sound a house makes when its owner comes home.

At noon, the telling. This was the rite Sef hated most, and she hated it honestly, with her whole chest. The keeper knelt at the dragon's head and recited the line of descent — every keeper, every fire-pit, back and back until the names dissolved into the wordless time before names. Sef's mother had made her memorize it at six years old, kneeling on cold flagstones, striking her across the mouth when she stumbled. *Again. Again. You are the rope, girl, and if you fray, everything falls.* Fourteen generations. She knew them the way she knew her own heartbeat, and she resented every single name, and now, kneeling in the wet grass with Edmund's warm breath stirring her hair, she said them all aloud and found — to her fury, to her shame, to something that was neither and both — that she was glad of them. That the rope held. That her mother's voice lived in her voice, in the cadence if nothing else, and that Edmund's eye had closed halfway with pleasure at hearing the old litany spoken properly over him for the first time in thirty years.

"Marag of the Ashfall pit," she said, reaching her grandmother, "who kept the coals through the winter of the red snow. And Yeva of the Ashfall pit, who was my mother's mother, who—" her throat closed. She swallowed it. "Who sang to the eggs. And Tamsin of the Ashfall pit, who was my mother, who tended the last fire and died of it, and whose daughter stands here now, and is the last, and does not fray."

Edmund opened his eye all the way.

*Well said,* he said. *Tamsin would have struck you only twice for the pause.*

This time it definitely was a laugh. Sef laughed too, or sobbed, or did whatever the thing is that sits between them and sounds identical from the outside.

In the afternoon, the listening. The keeper asks the dragon for its hoard — not gold; dragons of the old pits had never been creatures of gold — its hoard of memory, everything it wished remembered after it was gone. And the dragon speaks as long as it can speak, and the keeper keeps the words however she can: carved, sung, stitched, or simply carried in the body like a stone carried in a shoe, hurting, present, hers.

Edmund talked until the light went long and amber. He talked about the Spire, which he had seen raised, though he refused to say when or by whom, only that it had been *smaller then, and louder*. He talked about Maren Underhill, and Sef watched Petra go very still at the edge of her vision while Edmund described a young witch with ink-stained fingers climbing down into the tannery cellar to argue philosophy with a dragon about whether doors were kind or cruel. (*She decided,* Edmund said, *that a door is neither. A door is a promise, and promises are only as good as the people willing to stand in them.*) He talked about fire — what it was, what it wanted, why it had chosen, of all the things in the world, to live inside the bodies of his kind. *Because we are the only ones who agree to carry it without asking it to be useful,* he said. *Fire is very tired of being useful.*

And near the end, when the sun was a red coin caught in the trees, he said Sef's name, and stopped, and waited, and she understood that the waiting itself was the last instruction.

"Yes," she said. "I'm keeping it. All of it. Every word."

*No,* said Edmund. *Give some away. That is the final rite and no one ever teaches it because no one who knows it lives long enough to teach. A hoard kept perfectly becomes a tomb. Give some away, keeper, or I will haunt you, and I am very large, and I am extremely bored.*

* * *

Night came down into the valley like water filling a bowl.

They built the pyre while he could still watch them — that was the law of the old way; a dragon must see its own resting place prepared, must approve it, must be able to say *yes, there, that will do* — and Edmund approved it with a rumble and a critical flick of one ear-spine, because the eastern side was too steep and he would roll, and they had to rebuild it, and Sef found herself almost grateful for the complaint because it was so exactly what he had been like in the cellar all those years, insufferable, magnificent, alive.

Petra worked beside her without being asked. So did Anselm, sleeves rolled, hauling timber with a defrocked priest's stubborn grace, murmuring fragments of funeral Latin under his breath until Edmund told him — kindly, Sef thought, but firmly — that the old way required no words he couldn't understand, and Anselm had gone quiet, and then, after a while, had simply started helping with his whole attention, and that was its own kind of prayer.

By full dark, Edmund could no longer lift his head.

Sef knelt at his jaw with the lantern between them. Through the iron mesh the heart-flame burned low and blue-edged, pulling inward, drawing down toward its own center like a star considering collapse.

"It's time," she said. "Isn't it."

*It is time.* A breath. The longest silence yet between breaths, long enough that Petra made a small sound and stepped forward, and then Edmund breathed again, deliberately, the way an old man lifts himself upright in bed to finish a conversation properly. *Sef. Last of the Ashfall pit. Listen, because this part is yours alone.*

"I'm listening."

*The flame is not mine. It was never mine. It passed into my egg from the world-fire, and before that from the dragon before me, and before that from the first thing that ever burned and wanted company. I am a doorway it lived in. You are the next one. Do not think of yourself as carrying my death. Think of yourself as carrying the world's oldest loneliness, which has decided, after all this time, that it likes you.*

Her eyes were burning. "That's a terrible thing to say to someone."

*Yes,* said Edmund. *Truth usually is. Now open the cage, keeper. I would like to see my own heart before I go. It has been behind iron for eleven days and I have opinions.*

Her hands shook on the lantern's latch. Three locks — her grandmother's design, her mother's reinforcement, her own addition — and each one opened harder than the last, as though the metal itself objected, as though the whole craft of keeping were built into the hinges and the hinges knew what came next. When the last lock gave, the door swung wide, and Edmund's heart-flame rose out of the lantern into the open air for the first time since Greyhollow.

It was small. That was the terrible thing, the thing no rite had warned her of. After everything — the cellar, the tanner's chains of custom, the road, the ash-names, the country dissolving behind them like sugar in rain — the last heart-flame in the world hung above the pyre no bigger than a candleflame, trembling, orange going gold going white at its center, and it looked at Edmund, and Edmund looked at it, two halves of one ancient creature regarding each other across four feet of cold night air.

Then it went into him.

There was no drama. That was the second terrible thing. It simply touched his breastbone and sank, the way rain sinks into ground that has been waiting all summer, and Edmund's whole body arched — wings flaring to their full ruined span, blotting out half the sky, claws scoring the earth — and he breathed in, one vast draught of the night, valleys and stars and rain and the small terrified faces of the three humans who loved him.

He breathed out fire.

Not the sullen furnace-glow of the cellar years. White fire. Fire like the inside of a struck bell, like the moment lightning decides. It went into the pyre and the pyre went up all at once, every stick catching in the same instant, and the heat came off it in a wall that drove them stumbling back ten paces, and Sef stood at the edge of that light with her face streaming and her arms wrapped around herself and watched two hundred years of Edmund burn upward in columns of white and silver and, at the very heart of it, a color she had no name for and suspected no one had ever had a name for, because it existed only in the space between a living fire and a willing keeper.

He did not cry out. Dragons of the old pits did not cry out; that was in the litany too. But as the flames took his wings, his head turned — slowly, with the last of his strength, the way a man turns in bed to look at his family — and the honey eye found Sef one final time.

*Keeper,* he said. *Last of the Ashfall pit. You were the freest thing I ever knew.*

And then he was fire, and then he was light, and then he was the smell of hot stone and green eggs and woodsmoke, hanging over the valley, refusing for a long, long moment to be only smoke.

Sef knelt in the scorched grass and wept the way the old book said keepers wept — loudly, uglily, without shame, because grief was fuel and fuel was not to be wasted — and behind her, faintly, she heard Petra begin to hum the tune Yeva had sung to the eggs, and Anselm's voice joining it badly, and she understood that this was the giving-away Edmund had ordered, and that she was doing it, and that it hurt exactly as much as it was supposed to.

The lantern, empty, lay on its side in the grass. Its iron mesh had gone black.

And in its heart, where the flame had lived, something remained: a single scale, no bigger than a thumbnail, white as the center of the fire, warm as a held hand — and pulsing. Slowly. Patiently. In time with footsteps that were coming up the far slope of the valley, many boots, many voices, torchlight beginning to move through the trees like a rumor of dawn.

Behind them, someone had found the Spire too.
