## 28. The Bargain Petra Makes

The Spire's hum had changed since dawn.

Petra felt it in her teeth before she understood it with her mind — a new note threaded through the old one, thinner and faster, like a pulse climbing toward fever. All night the tower had sung its patient song of answered prayers, every miracle they'd passed on the road folded into that sound like threads into cloth. Now something in the weave was fraying.

She stood at the base of it with Maren's book open against her chest, held there by both hands the way you hold a wound closed. The black stone rose out of the valley floor without foundation or seam, growing rather than built, and up close it was warm. Not sun-warm. Skin-warm. The hollow in the door — that cup-shaped absence shaped like a burning heart — breathed slow heat against her palms when she came near enough to touch it, and when she pulled away the warmth clung to her fingers like water that didn't want to let go.

Behind her, at the edge of the valley where the road gave out into grass that grew in no pattern any farmer would have tolerated, Sef sat with the iron lantern between her knees. Edmund's heart-flame burned inside it, white and enormous now, so bright that Sef had wrapped her hands in leather just to be near it. She hadn't slept. None of them had. Anselm stood apart with his broken prayer-book open to nothing, reading nothing, mouthing nothing, because that was how he thought — words moving under the surface like fish under ice.

They had argued until the stars went out. Sef had offered herself and the flame together, her voice flat as a ledger: *my life was promised to the fire before I was born; spend what's already owed.* Petra had refused, and the refusal had come out of her like a blade coming out of a body, ugly and necessary, and Sef had looked at her the way you look at someone who has taken a thing from your hands and called it help.

Now the argument was over and the silence it left was worse.

"You're going to do something," Sef said from behind her. It wasn't a question. Nineteen years old and raised on dying things; she could hear the shape of a decision the way she could hear the change in a dragon's breathing an hour before the end.

"I'm going to talk to it," Petra said.

"To a door."

"To whatever's listening." She turned the book over in her hands. The cover was cracked leather gone soft as skin, and beneath her fingers she could feel the faint ridges of Maren's handwriting through it, all those years of ink pressed into fiber. "Doors don't hum. Doors don't *want*. Something in there is hungry, and hungry things can be bargained with. I've fed hungry things my whole life. Gods, charms, customers who pretend they don't believe — you learn the shape of want, and you learn what it will trade."

Anselm closed his empty book. "The inscriptions say—"

"The inscriptions say one goes through." She said it before he could soften it, because softening it would have been a lie and she had spent five years building a life on small lies and it had nearly killed everyone she loved. "One passes. Everyone else stays in a country that is being forgotten off the face of the world. That's the deal as written. But deals are written by whoever writes first, Father, and I have my mistress's hand in mine, and my mistress never finished her sentence." She looked down at the book, at the page she had finally opened after five years of carrying it shut, at the last line trailing off mid-word the way a voice trails off when someone walks into another room. *"Hold the door with—"*

With what? With blood. With name. With fire. With the self.

She had spent two nights not sleeping, and somewhere in the second night the answer had come to her the way answers come to hedge-witches — not as revelation but as craft. You did not break a spell by opposing it. You broke it by completing it differently than intended. Maren had written a door for one. But Maren had also written *hold*, and *hold* was a word with room in it.

"Petra," Anselm said gently, and she heard the sermon coming, the defrocked priest's voice that could make even comfort sound like doctrine.

"Don't." She smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, the kind you show a horse before you bit it. "I've watched you give last rites to a man who didn't believe in them. I've watched Sef sing a dragon into its death with rites she hates. We're all of us doing the thing we swore we wouldn't, and doing it well. Let me do mine."

* * *

She approached the door alone.

The valley floor was soft underfoot, moss and ash-grass, and the closer she came to the Spire the more the hum resolved into voices — not words, exactly, but the memory of words, prayers worn smooth by answering. She heard the widow's drowned husband in it. She heard the field of teeth. She heard the god at the dry well saying *the spire feeds on willing sacrifice* in its voice like water finding a crack. Every prayer this country had ever shouted at its silent gods was in that stone somewhere, packed like grain in a granary, and the granary was full, and full things groan.

Ten feet from the door, she stopped and knelt, because you did not bargain standing over someone. Even a door. Especially a door.

"Maren Underhill wrote a spell," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very small against the humming, a single reed against a river. "Half of one. She died — or stopped, or was forgotten, I don't know which — before she finished it. I've carried it for five years thinking I stole it. I'm done thinking that." Her throat tightened around the next part and she made herself say it anyway, plainly, the way you'd state terms across a market stall. "I'm offering to finish it."

The hum did not change. Or it changed so slightly that she might have imagined it — a half-tone shift, the way a listener leans.

"Her spell holds a door open. Your door opens for fire freely given, and closes behind the one who carries it, and everyone else stays here to be unmade by forgetting. Those are your rules. I read them in your stones; your own priest translated them; I'm not pretending otherwise." She laid the book open on the moss before her, pages up, Maren's unfinished sentence facing the tower like a face. "But here's what your rules didn't count on. A spell isn't a rule. A spell is a *sentence*, and sentences can be finished by anyone holding the pen."

She drew the knife from her belt — Sef's knife, tanning-knife, kept sharp for work on hide — and opened her left palm along the old callus line where needle and thread had toughened the skin for years of charm-work. The pain was clean and ordinary and she was grateful for it, grateful for anything that belonged to the world she knew. Blood welled dark in the grey morning light.

"I'll complete it with this," she said. "And with Edmund's flame-ash. And with my name, which is the only thing I own outright. I'll stand in your hollow and I'll hold your door open from the inside — not passing through, not closing it behind me. Holding. For as long as it takes everyone to walk through. The widow and the farmer whose field grew teeth. The forgotten ones in the city no one remembers, who knew my name when they couldn't know their own. Vane, if he lives. Anselm, who doesn't believe in anything except showing up. Sef, who was promised to a fire and deserves to be released from the promise." Her voice cracked on Sef's name and she let it. "All of them. Not one. Everyone willing to remain."

The hum rose.

It rose the way wind rises before weather, the way a crowd's murmur rises before it becomes either riot or hymn, and the warmth of the door became heat and the heat became pressure, and Petra knelt in it with her bleeding hand pressed flat to the moss and did not flinch, because flinching was the first coin every bargain demanded and she refused to pay it.

Something considered her.

She felt it the way you feel a large animal considering you across a fence — a vast attention turning, slow and ancient, with no malice in it and no kindness either, only weight. It was older than the kingdom. It was older than the maps that had been burned to erase it. It had been built — or grown, or born — to be a door out of a country, and doors were simple things, and simple things did not easily understand being asked to become more.

*ONE,* the Spire said, and the word was not sound but pressure, arriving in her chest like a hand pressing on a bruise. *THE TERMS ARE ONE.*

"Terms are written by whoever writes first," Petra said through her teeth. "You wrote first. I'm writing back."

*THE DOOR OPENS FOR FIRE FREELY GIVEN.*

"And it does. And you'll have it. Edmund gives it freely — ask him, if you doubt it; he's been dying for thirty years and he told a runaway apprentice to carry his fire, which is more trust than most gods ever showed anyone. Take the flame. Take it gladly. But the flame opens the door. My spell decides what the door *is*." She lifted her bleeding hand and turned it palm-up toward the tower, an offering and a challenge in the same gesture. "Maren saw you coming, you understand. Five years ago she saw this — the silence breaking, the prayers answered badly, the country thinning out at the edges like cloth worn through. She wrote a spell to hold a door open and she addressed it to *the one who ran*, and then she let that one steal the book and run, because a vessel stays where it's put but a carrier chooses where to go. She wasn't making a trap. She was making a key. And keys—" Petra's breath caught, steadied. "Keys fit locks. They don't belong to them."

The pressure withdrew a little. Considered.

She could feel it testing her the way she tested charms before selling them — turning the working over, looking for the flaw, the place where the maker's hope outran the maker's skill. And she let it look, because there was no flaw to find, or the flaw was the whole point: she meant it. Every word. She had spent five years building a careful life out of small charms and smaller lies, and the life had cracked open, and what was left underneath was this — a woman kneeling in a valley with her blood on the moss, offering to become architecture.

*YOU WILL NOT PASS THROUGH,* the Spire said. It was not quite a question.

"No."

*YOU WILL HOLD.*

"For as long as it takes."

*DOORS THAT HOLD BECOME WALLS.*

"Doors that hold become *doors*," Petra said. "That's the whole trick of them. Ask any threshold. Ask any well. Ask the god of small offerings, who told me what you eat, and who filled with water for one honest gift and wept while it did." She laughed, briefly, wetly, surprised by it. "You've been feeding on sacrifice. Sacrifice runs out. There's always one less person willing, and then none, and then you're a silent tower in an empty country, and you know it — that's why you're humming so fast. But willingness doesn't run out. Willingness *breeds*. Someone sees a door held open for strangers and something in them stands up straighter. I've sold luck-knots for five years to people who pretended not to believe, and every one of them tied the knot anyway. That's the crop you should have been planting."

The hum went very quiet.

In that quiet she heard everything else — the wind in the ash-grass, Sef's breathing twenty feet behind her, the lantern's white flame hissing softly like rain on hot iron, and far off, at the edge of hearing, the sound of the Crown's reinforcements on the roads: hooves, wheels, the creak of tar-barrels. They had perhaps hours. Maybe less. The Spire's frantic note had been right about that too; the country was ending a little more with every mile those men rode.

Then the Spire spoke again, and it had changed its voice. The pressure was still vast, but it had found something almost like a tone a person could bear — the way a great dog learns to modulate its bark indoors.

*SHOW ME THE SPELL.*

Petra reached forward and turned the book around, pages facing the tower, Maren's half-finished sentence open to the air. *Hold the door with—*

The warmth of the hollow deepened. She felt the Spire reading — actually reading, attention moving along the inked lines like a finger — and she felt the moment it reached the break, the missing words, the place where a dead woman's voice simply stopped. Something moved through the great stone then that she had no better word for than grief. The grief of a door that had been waiting a generation for someone to finish a sentence.

*IT IS UNFINISHED.*

"So am I," Petra said. "Finish us both."

* * *

The consideration took the length of three breaths, or three years; time went strange against the warm stone, and afterward she could never have said which.

She became aware, distantly, that Sef had come up beside her — she must have crossed the moss without a sound, keeper-quiet, and now she stood at Petra's shoulder with the lantern held against her chest, and she did not speak, and she did not try to take the knife, and her presence said everything her argument had failed to say all night: *wherever you kneel, I kneel.* On her other side, further off, Anselm had sunk to his knees too, and his lips were moving, and whether it was prayer or argument with heaven no one living could have said.

*THE TERMS ARE AMENDED,* the Spire said.

Petra's heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a table.

*FIRE FREELY GIVEN OPENS THE DOOR. THE ONE WHO HOLDS IT STAYS. ALL WHO CHOOSE MAY PASS WHILE IT IS HELD.* A pause, vast as a season. *WHEN SHE CAN NO LONGER HOLD, THE DOOR CLOSES. WHAT REMAINS OF THE COUNTRY WILL REMAIN AROUND HER.*

"That's the bargain," Petra whispered.

*THAT IS THE BARGAIN.*

"And I want it witnessed." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "By the fire, and by the priest, and by the keeper. Bargains unwitnessed are just wishes."

Sef set the lantern down on the moss between them, and the white light of Edmund's heart-flame lay across the open page like a blessing, and the flame leaned — there was no other word for it — leaned toward the tower the way a person leans toward a door they have decided to walk through. Anselm said, in a voice scraped raw, "Witnessed," and then, because he was Anselm, "God help us, witnessed."

The Spire's hum changed one final time. The frantic note smoothed. The fever-pulse slowed. And in the hollow of the door, that cup-shaped absence shaped like a burning heart, a faint light kindled — not the lantern's white, but a color Petra recognized from her one glimpse inside Maren's working, the color of a candle seen through a hand held over it, warm and red-gold and patient.

Waiting to be filled.

Petra looked down at her open palm, at the blood already drying brown along the cut, and understood with perfect clarity what came next: the ash, the blood, the final lines written in a dead woman's book by a living woman's hand. She understood what it would cost, and that the Spire had not lied about the cost, and that the pain would be described honestly later or not at all.

She picked up the knife again.

Behind her, far down the valley road, the first tar-smoke of the Crown's vanguard rose above the ridge line — thin and black against the morning, like a sentence beginning.

"Sef," Petra said quietly, not turning around. "Bring me Edmund's ash. And then I need you to hold the book steady. My hand's going to shake, and the spelling can't."
