## 29. Vane's Choice

The column came up the valley road at dusk, and Vane counted them the way he had once counted sins: without mercy and without hope.

Two hundred men. Pikes, crossbows, a wagon of pitch-barrels behind four dray-horses, and at the head of it Captain Ossler of the Ninth, a broad man with a broad voice and the particular confidence of someone who had never once been asked to think. The Crown's seal hung from his neck on a chain thick enough to strangle a child. Behind him, the banners — the sun-and-scales of the Inquisition, the crowned tower of the Realm — snapping in a wind that smelled of ash coming down from the high passes.

Vane stood at the roadside with his hands folded behind his back and let them come to him.

He had sent no word ahead. He had told himself, all the way down the mountain, that he was still verifying reports, still doing his duty, still an instrument of order moving through a country sliding into forgetting. He had rehearsed the report he would give: *the fugitives located, the Spire confirmed, awaiting instruction.* Clean words. Words that kept their shape.

But he had also stopped, three days ago, at a village called — nothing; the name would not stay in his mouth — where a hedge-witch younger than his own dead daughter had knelt in a flooded barley field and prayed over a sick boy until the fever broke like ice underfoot. He had stood in the crowd with his hand on his sword hilt and watched a miracle happen in mud, and no one had burned, and no one had been punished, and the boy's mother had looked at him — at *him*, the inquisitor, the terror of Greyhollow — and mouthed *thank you* as if he were part of it.

He had not corrected her.

That was the crack. That was where the light got in. Everything since had only been the sound of the wall giving way.

"Lord Vane." Ossler reined up and saluted with a flourish meant for audiences. "Reports said you'd gone soft in the hills. Chasing ghosts." His eyes went past Vane to the valley floor, where the Spire rose out of the dusk like a black tooth against the last grey light, humming so low Vane felt it in his back teeth. "Well. It seems the ghost has a tower."

"The reports are accurate," Vane said. "The witch is there. The priest. The dragon-keeper's girl."

"And the dragon?"

"Dead." He had heard it that morning — or felt it, rather, a change in the Spire's hum, a note going taut as a drawn bowstring. The flame lived on in a lantern somewhere among the fugitives. He did not say so. He was already lying, then. He noted this about himself with the detachment of a man reading another man's confession. *Subject demonstrates escalating deception. Subject demonstrates loss of conviction. Recommend—*

Recommend what? There was no form for what he recommended now.

Ossler swung down and clapped him on the shoulder, comrade-fashion, familiar in the way of men who have never needed to fear each other. "Orders are simple, my lord. Burn the tower, burn the witches, salt the ground. The Chancellor wants this finished before the month turns. Half the provinces east of here have stopped answering correspondence — did you know that? Whole garrisons, silent. The Council's frightened." He grinned. "Frightened men burn things. It's the oldest medicine we have."

"It is not medicine," Vane heard himself say. "It is the disease agreeing with itself."

Ossler's grin faltered, recalibrated, held. "You've been alone too long, Lord Vane. Come. Walk the line with me. A man needs to see his work done properly."

*His work.* Vane walked the line because refusing would draw more eyes than complying. Two hundred faces turned toward him as he passed — young faces mostly, farm-boys and second sons, men who had signed on for bread and a coat and had been handed fire instead. One of them, a corporal barely shaving, had a charm-knot tied around his wrist under his gauntlet. Vane saw it when the boy adjusted his grip. Grey thread. Market-work. Greyhollow work.

He said nothing. The boy saw that he'd seen. Neither of them moved. Somewhere in the ranks, quietly, the country that was dying and the country that might remain looked at each other across six feet of trampled grass.

At the end of the line, Ossler stopped and gestured at the Spire with his riding crop, expansive, a showman unveiling the evening's entertainment. "There it is, then. Twenty years I've heard stories. They say it was old before the kingdom. They say the first king knocked on it and it wouldn't open." He laughed. "It'll open tonight. Things always open for fire."

Vane looked at the tower. The hum had changed again — faster now, urgent, like a heart before a race. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the evening air the way sound carries over water: voices. Singing. No — not singing. Praying. Many voices, braided, rising from the base of the Spire where the fugitives had gathered whatever half-forgotten souls the valley held.

They were praying *for* the soldiers. He understood that suddenly, the way one understands a blow after it lands. The prayers were not pleas for deliverance. They were blessings. Spoken aloud, unhidden, aimed at two hundred armed men marching to kill them.

"Lord Vane?" Ossler said. "You've gone pale."

"I need to inspect the tower's base before we commit," Vane said. "Alone. If there are workings laid on the ground, I should be the one to find them. You don't want your sappers walking into a ward."

That much was even true. There *were* workings on the ground. He could feel them from here, old and patient, the whole valley humming like a struck bell that had not yet decided to stop.

Ossler weighed it, then shrugged. "An hour. Then we light the barrels."

* * *

He walked down into the valley alone, and the hum rose around him like water rising around a drowning man, and by the time he reached the outworks of the Spire he was weeping and could not have said precisely when it began.

Not for the first time, he thought of the kneeling woman in the interrogation cell, years ago now — the widow whose prayer had returned her husband drowned and dripping and wrong, and who had knelt on the cold stone thanking God for it while Vane wrote down every word. *He came back. He came back and he was cold but he knew me, he knew my name, isn't that enough? Isn't knowing enough?*

He had written: *subject displays disordered attachment.*

He had gone home that night and knelt beside his own bed, as he did every night, and prayed to a god who had not answered in a generation, and risen in the morning and put on the scales-and-sun and gone out to punish people for hoping. He had done it for twenty-two years. He had told himself it was faith — faith disciplined, faith armored, faith willing to do the ugly work while softer men sang. But kneeling now in the grass below the Spire, listening to strangers pray for his soul in a language older than the kingdom, he finally named the thing he had actually been carrying all those years.

Fear. Dressed in vestments. Fear that if he ever stopped, ever listened, ever *asked*, the silence would be the answer — and that the silence would mean he had wasted his whole life guarding an empty house.

The hum deepened. The grass around him silvered with frost that was not cold. And Vane, chief inquisitor of the realm, breaker of the Greyhollow market, burner of charms, did the thing he had never done aloud in twenty-two years of policing other people's prayers.

He prayed.

Not formally. Not in the liturgy. He knelt in the wet grass with his sword across his knees and said, in the plain voice of a tired man: *"I don't know if You're there. I never knew. I hurt people because I couldn't bear the not-knowing. If You can hear anything — hear this. I was wrong. I was wrong the way a wall is wrong across a door. Take the wall down. Use the stones."*

The Spire's hum paused.

One breath. The whole valley holding it. Frost blooming up his arms like white feathers.

Then the hum resumed — and it had changed key. Warmer. Lower. Like a door unlatching somewhere very far away, or very near.

Vane stayed kneeling a moment longer. Then he stood, sheathed his sword, and began to walk back up the hill toward two hundred men and a wagon of pitch, and with every step he was aware that he was walking out of one life and that he had not yet decided what he was walking into.

He decided at the top of the hill, when Ossler met him with the torch already lit.

"Well?" Ossler said. "Wards?"

"There are wards," Vane said. "And something else." He drew his sword. Slowly, so the movement read as ritual, as inspection, as anything but what it was. "Captain, I am countermanding the order."

The torchlight found Ossler's face and Vane watched understanding arrive there in stages — confusion, disbelief, and then the flat animal calculation of a man deciding whether the thing he just heard was real. Around them the nearest soldiers had gone still. The wind dropped. Even the banners hung uncertain.

"Countermanding," Ossler repeated. "My lord. With respect. The orders come from the Chancellor."

"The Chancellor is giving orders to a country that may not exist by spring," Vane said. "I have seen what's happening east of here. I have filed the reports myself, and I lied in some of them, because the truth was worse than treason. There is a door in that tower, Captain. It is the last one. And the people at its base are not rebels or heretics. They are the ones willing to stay." He raised his voice, then, for the first time in his career raising it *against* the Crown's instruments, and it cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Every man who can hear me — look at the tower. Listen. Have you ever heard the gods answer anyone? Have you, in all your service? Listen *now*."

And the valley, obligingly, terribly, answered him.

The hum swelled. Every soldier heard it — Vane watched it land on them one by one, two hundred faces turning toward the Spire as the sound rose through boot-soles and breastbones, a chord too large for any bell, and above it, thin and clear, the praying voices blessing them by name. Not their names. Their mothers' names. Names they hadn't heard spoken since childhood, rising out of the dark like lamps being lit in windows.

The corporal with the grey charm-knot dropped his pike. It fell in the grass with a soft, final sound.

"No," Ossler said. "No. This is a working. This is witchcraft, this is exactly what—" and he thrust the torch toward the pitch-wagon, and Vane moved.

He had fought duels. He had executed prisoners. He had never once turned his sword on a brother of the service, and his body knew it — knew it the way a river knows a dam — and fought him for the length of one heartbeat, and lost. The blade took Ossler's torch-arm at the elbow. The torch spun away into the wet grass and died with a hiss like a curse swallowed.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines. This was a shattering — two hundred men splitting along a fault no one had known was there, some surging forward with Ossler screaming and clutching his arm, others standing rooted, others — a dozen, two dozen, the corporal among them — turning their backs on the wagon and walking downhill toward the Spire with their hands empty and open.

Vane stood between the factions with his sword red and his breath smoking, and he understood with perfect clarity that he had perhaps a minute of authority left in his life, and he spent it shouting the only command that mattered:

"To me! All who believe the gods can hear — to me! Hold the road!"

They came. Not enough. Forty, maybe fifty, forming a ragged line across the valley road between the pitch-wagon and the tower, and beyond them the rest resolving into two mobs, and Ossler on his knees in the grass howling for arbalists, and the first crossbow bolts already loosed into the dark, whistling wide, whistling at shadows, because no one could quite see straight anymore.

Vane raised his sword and set himself.

He thought, absurdly, of his garden — the small square behind the inquisitors' residence in Greyhollow where he grew bittercress and nothing else, because bittercress thrives on neglect. He thought: *if I live I will plant something sweeter.* He thought of the widow in the cell. *Isn't knowing enough?* Yes, he wanted to tell her. Yes. It is enough. It was always enough. Knowing is everything; I only learned it too late to be anything but a wall across a door, and walls, at least, can be stood upon.

The first wave hit the line like weather.

He did not remember most of it afterward — only fragments, the way one remembers a storm: the corporal at his left shoulder fighting with a broken pike and crying without shame; the frost-light of the Spire washing over everything until blood looked black; a voice — his own? — shouting words that might have been prayer or might have been orders, and no difference anymore between the two. He took a blade in the thigh and did not fall. He took one under the collarbone and did fall, partly, to one knee, and rose, because the line had not broken, because behind him the praying voices had risen to something like triumph, because the lantern-light at the base of the Spire had gone white — white as frost, white as the first page of a book — and the whole tower was beginning, gently, impossibly, to glow.

*They're doing it,* he thought. *The witch is doing it. Hold. Hold the road. Buy her the hour.*

A sword found him below the ribs, angled up, the way butchers know. He looked down at the young face attached to it — a farm-boy, terrified, striking because stopping was impossible now — and Vane did the last kind thing available to him: he gripped the boy's wrist and said, "Run east. When the borders move, run east," and let go, and the boy ran.

Vane sat down in the grass. The line held without him. Someone was shouting for pitch-men to stand down; someone else was singing; the Spire filled the valley with light like a second dawn arriving out of schedule, and Vane leaned back against the cold earth and watched it, and found that he was smiling.

Footsteps came down the hill through the chaos — unhurried, deliberate, robes trailing frost. Anselm. The defrocked priest knelt beside him in the ruined grass, and his face was the face of a man seeing clearly for the first time what he had refused to see for years.

"You mad old bastard," Anselm said. "You held the road."

"Somebody had to be the wall," Vane said. Blood made his mouth taste of iron and pennies. "Across the door. So the door could—" He coughed. "Fetch the witch. Tell her — tell her the inquisitor sends his confession. All of it. Unabridged."

"She's busy opening the end of the world."

"Then you'll do." Vane caught the priest's sleeve with a bloody hand. "I have never said the rites correctly in my life. I said them like a man locking a door. Say them for me like a man opening one. And Father — if nothing answers — say them anyway. Promise me. Say them anyway."

Anselm's jaw worked. Above them the Spire blazed; around them the battle guttered into scattered fires and running figures and men kneeling in the grass on both sides, praying to the same sky.

"I promise," Anselm said, and bent close, and began.

And for one breath — one breath, no longer — the hum of the Spire bent downward, personal, intimate, warm as a hand on a dying man's brow, and every hair on Vane's arms stood up, and he knew, with the last certainty of his life, that he had been heard.

His fingers found Anselm's wrist and pressed once. Then the light took the valley, and the wall came down, and the stones went wherever stones go when the door finally opens.
