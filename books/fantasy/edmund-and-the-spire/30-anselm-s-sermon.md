## 30. Anselm's Sermon

The wind off the valley had changed.

Anselm felt it before he understood it — a warmth where there should have been none, a sweetness in the air like incense burned too close to the flame. He had spent thirty years learning the language of weather and forty more unlearning the language of God, and now both vocabularies failed him at once. He stood at the mouth of the Spire's shadow with his hands empty and his vestments stiff with old sweat and road dust, and he watched the smoke of Vane's burning escort rise into a sky that seemed, for the first time in his life, to be paying attention.

He found the inquisitor twenty paces from the Spire's black base, propped against a fallen pillar older than any kingdom Anselm could name. The pillar was carved — he noticed this distantly, the way a drowning man notices the color of the water — carved with figures whose faces had been worn to smoothness by centuries of hands touching them in prayer. Vane had been laid there by no one. He had simply stopped walking, the way soldiers do, and folded down against the stone as if it were a pew.

There were four bodies between the pillar and the tree line. Crown men. Vane's own men, or men who had been his an hour ago, before he turned his sword on them to buy three strangers time they had not asked for. The blood on the grass was already going brown at the edges. Flies had begun their small, indifferent work.

Anselm knelt beside him.

Vane's eyes were open. That was the first terrible thing — that they were open, and clear, and looking at Anselm with something worse than hatred. Recognition. The look of a man who has been searching a crowd for one particular face and has finally found it, though the face belongs to someone he despises.

"You," Vane said. His voice came out in pieces, like coins counted out of a purse nearly empty. "Of all of them. The heretic."

"The defrocked priest," Anselm corrected gently. "Heretic implies I convinced anyone."

Vane tried to laugh. It cost him; Anselm saw the price of it in the way his hand spasmed against the stone, leaving a smear of red on the worn-smooth cheek of some forgotten saint. The wound was low, under the ribs, and deep. Anselm had seen men take such wounds on campaign roads and in tavern brawls, and he knew the arithmetic of them. Hours, perhaps. Less if the man kept talking.

"Stop talking," Anselm said.

"I have been silent," Vane said, "for thirty years."

* * *

They had met before, once. Anselm remembered it now with the strange clarity of things that meant nothing at the time. A synod in the capital, eleven years ago — before the defrocking, before Greyhollow, before the bells rang themselves at dawn and the world began forgetting its own name. Anselm had been young then, still believing in the architecture of the faith rather than its absence. Vane had been younger, a junior inquisitor with a ledger under his arm and a face like a locked door. They had shared a table at the evening meal because the hall was crowded, and they had spoken of nothing — the quality of the wine, the length of the bishop's sermon — and at the end of it Vane had said, without preamble, without looking up from his plate:

"Do you think they hear us?"

And Anselm, wanting to be kind, wanting to be correct, had said: "Of course."

Vane had nodded once, the way a man nods when a door closes, and finished his meal in silence, and they had never spoken again until this moment, kneeling in the blood-warm grass beneath a tower that hummed with every desperate prayer in a dying country.

"I asked you once," Vane said now, and his voice was thinner, stretched over the pain like cloth over a wound. "At the synod. Whether they heard us."

"I remember."

"You lied to me."

"Yes," Anselm said. "I did."

The Spire hummed above them. Anselm had thought, from a distance, that the sound would be terrible — the groan of a machine, the scream of a dying thing. It was neither. It was closer to a choir heard through a cathedral wall: all the words dissolved, only the longing left, rising and falling with a patience that broke his heart. Every answered prayer in the country fed into that sound. Every drowned husband returned wrong. Every field of teeth. Every child's fever broken by a salt charm sold in a market where magic was outlawed. The gods' last breath, drawn and exhaled, drawn and exhaled, and somewhere inside it a door that wanted opening.

Vane's breathing had gone shallow and quick. Anselm pressed his palm flat against the man's forehead — cool, already cooling — and Vane did not resist. That was the second terrible thing. Inquisitor Vane, who had burned charm-stalls and posted edicts and hunted Petra Underhill across half a dissolving country, lay under a heretic's hand and did not resist.

"My men," Vane said. "The ones I—"

"Are dead or fled. The ones you turned your sword against will be remembered as traitors. The ones you saved are alive because of it." Anselm paused. "I am told, these days, that memory is a fragile thing. Whole villages forget their names. But I think what you did today will hold. Some things hold better than others."

"What things?"

"Acts," Anselm said. "Arguments fade. Acts hold."

* * *

He had prepared sermons his whole life. He had preached to congregations of two hundred and congregations of six, to farmers and magistrates and once, memorably, to a flooded church where the congregation sat in waist-deep water and refused to leave until the blessing was done. He had preached after his defrocking to no one at all, standing in an empty chapel, saying the words to walls that did not answer — because that was the heresy, in the end, the true one, the one that got him cast out. Not doubt. Doubt was fashionable; half the bishops doubted privately and performed publicly. No. Anselm's crime was that he had told the truth aloud: *the gods have abandoned us, and they abandoned us on purpose, and we must learn to live in the silence.*

He had built his whole ruinous honesty on that foundation. The gods were gone. The silence was final. Faith was a habit the living kept out of respect for the dead.

And now he knelt beside a dying man beneath a tower full of answered prayers, and the foundation held, and he hated it, and he was grateful for it, and he did not know which feeling was the truer one.

"Father," Vane said.

"I'm not—"

"Father." Stronger this time. The word cost him everything he had left, and he paid it anyway. "I know what you are. I signed the document. I read the charges. I have read every charge against every witch and heretic in this country for fifteen years, and I know exactly what you are." His hand moved, fumbling, and found Anselm's sleeve, and gripped it with a strength that surprised them both. "I am asking anyway."

Anselm looked at him. The light was failing — the real light, the sun going down behind the western ridge, though the Spire's hum gave off a glow of its own now, faint and sourceless, like moonlight remembered rather than seen. In that glow Vane's face looked younger. It looked, Anselm thought, like the face of the junior inquisitor at the synod table, asking his one honest question into his plate.

"You want last rites," Anselm said. "From me. From a man who told a congregation of four hundred souls that God had left them deliberately, and meant it, and would say it again tomorrow if he had breath to spare."

"Yes."

"Why? You could wait. One of the others—"

"There are no others." Vane's grip tightened. "There is only you. There was always only—" He coughed, and the cough was wet and bad, and when it passed he lay very still for a long moment, and Anselm thought it might be over. Then: "I have prayed my whole life. Do you understand? Every morning. Every night. Before every execution, and I attended them all, I stood there and I prayed for the soul of the person I was helping kill. Fifteen years. Thousands of prayers. And never once — never *once*, Father — did anything answer. Not a warmth. Not a sign. Nothing. I began to believe the silence was my punishment. That I was specifically, personally, cut off. That somewhere in the great order of things there was a list, and my name had been struck from it, and every other soul in creation could feel the touch of God except me."

"And now?"

"Now I turned my sword on my own men to save a witch." He laughed again, or tried to; it came out as a rattle. "And I am lying here waiting to find out whether that was damnation or the first honest act of my life. And I want — I need — someone to say the words over me. Even if they're lies. *Especially* if they're lies. Because if the words are said, then I was part of something. If the words are said, then the silence around me was just silence, and not exile." His eyes fixed on Anselm's, and they were bright, and they were afraid, and Anselm had seen that exact fear in a hundred dying faces and had never once grown used to it. "Say them. Lie to me. I give you permission. I, Edmund Vane, chief inquisitor of the Crown, grant a defrocked priest absolution for the sin of comforting me. Write it in your ledger."

* * *

Anselm did not have oil. He did not have the breviary; it had been confiscated along with everything else eleven years ago, and he had rebuilt the rites from memory the way a man rebuilds a house from the memory of its shadows. He did not have salt or candle or the white cloth. He had dust on his hands and a dying enemy's blood soaking through his sleeve where the man still gripped him.

He began, therefore, with nothing, which is how all of it begins, if you go back far enough.

He made the sign. His fingers remembered it the way his feet remembered roads — without him, without permission, tracing the old geometry in the air above Vane's chest. He spoke the opening words, the ones that named the dying man not as a functionary or an enemy but as a soul: *servant of God, though the service was imperfect; child of God, though the childhood was long ago.* Vane closed his eyes. His breathing slowed. Around them the Spire hummed, and Anselm had the strange sensation of speaking into an instrument — that the tower was taking his words and adding them to its chord, folding a defrocked priest's borrowed liturgy into the vast unfinished music of a country's last prayers.

He confessed sins on Vane's behalf, because the man's lips were moving but no sound came, and Anselm had done this enough times to know the shape of a confession he couldn't hear. He named the executions. He named the burned stalls, the frightened neighbors, the edicts that turned love into evidence. He named the woman in the capital, years ago, whose prayers came true too well and whom Vane had questioned on her knees — Anselm had heard the story from Petra, secondhand, thirdhand, and he spoke it now into the record of the rite because a rite is a record, and records matter even when no one reads them. *For all of this, and for the silence you kept, and for the silence that answered you: mercy.*

Then he came to the absolution, and he stopped.

Because here was the thing. Here was the hinge on which his whole ruined faith had turned for eleven years. The absolution was not the priest's to give. Every priest knew this; it was the first secret they taught you and the last one you believed. The words were a vessel. The authority flowed through, from elsewhere, from the elsewhere that Anselm had declared empty. To speak absolution without the elsewhere behind it was not a sacrament. It was theater. It was a kindness performed with props, and he had promised himself, in the wreckage of his old life, that he would never perform it again — that he would comfort the dying with honest human words, hands and presence and memory, but never again dress emptiness in vestments and call it God.

Vane's eyes opened. "You've stopped."

"I've stopped."

"Is it the heresy?" Something almost like humor in the wrecked voice. "Have you finally decided I don't qualify?"

"It's that I don't believe in what comes next," Anselm said. "I believe in this. The grass. The blood. The tower humming. The girl inside it about to do something unbearable. I believe in acts, Vane. I told you. Arguments fade. I have no argument left for what I'm about to say. Only the act."

"Then do the act," Vane whispered.

And Anselm — who had crossed a country of forgetting, who had lost his God and found a witch and a dragon-keeper and a cause, who had spent eleven years preaching honestly to the silence — laid his hand on the inquisitor's forehead and spoke the absolution as if the heaven it invoked were real, as if the silence were not absence but attention, as if every word were a key turning in a lock he could not see.

*I absolve you. I absolve you. I absolve you.*

The wind stopped.

Not gentled — stopped, entirely, the whole valley holding its breath, and in the hush Anselm heard the Spire's hum change pitch. Rise. Bend toward them like a listener leaning close. The sourceless glow brightened, and the air filled with a scent that had no business existing: beeswax and old incense and rain on hot stone, the smell of every church he had ever loved, layered together, arriving all at once.

And for one breath — one breath, no longer, a breath so brief that afterward Anselm would spend hours, days, the rest of his life trying to decide whether it had happened — something answered.

It was not a voice. It was warmer than a voice and stranger. It was the sensation of being known completely and forgiven anyway, poured through him like water through an open window. It touched Vane. He felt it pass through his own chest and out through his palm into the dying man beneath it, and he saw Vane's face change — saw the fear leave it the way frost leaves glass when the sun finds it — and Vane smiled, a small and astonished smile, the smile of a man who has waited thirty years at a locked door and heard, at last, on the other side of it, footsteps.

"Oh," Vane said. "Oh — it was never—"

The breath ended. The wind resumed. The hum settled back into its long patient chord, and the glow dimmed to ordinary, and the valley was only a valley again, and Vane's hand slipped from Anselm's sleeve and lay open on the stone, palm up, the way hands are arranged for burial.

But the fear did not come back. Anselm watched him for a long time — minutes, perhaps an hour, while the light died in the west and the first stars found the places the constellations had begun to abandon. Vane breathed. Shallowly, rarely, but he breathed, and each breath came easier than the last, which was not how dying worked, and Anselm did not question it. Some gifts you accept without audit.

Near the end — near what should have been the end — Vane spoke once more, barely a movement of lips.

"She'll need time," he murmured. "The witch. The men who are coming — they'll burn the valley before dawn unless someone delays them at the ridge road. I know the ground. I can still—" His eyes found Anselm's, and they were calm, and they were clear, and they were, unmistakably, a believer's eyes. "Tell her. Tell her the door was worth it. Tell her I finally got an answer."

His hand closed, slowly, around something Anselm had not seen him holding: a scrap of paper, soft with handling, covered on both sides in a small precise script. A prayer, written out. The one he'd carried and never said aloud.

Anselm took it from his fingers with the care owed to relics.

* * *

He read it by the Spire's glow while the stars wheeled overhead and the tower hummed its vast unfinished chord. It was short. It was simple. It was the prayer of a boy, not an inquisitor — *let me be of use; let me be heard; let me, just once, be sure* — and it was signed, at the bottom, in the same small precise hand, with a date thirty-one years old.

Anselm folded it and put it inside his shirt, against his heart, where prayers belong.

Then he stood, joints cracking, an old man on an old battlefield beneath a tower full of God's last breath, and turned toward the Spire's entrance, where a faint light was growing — warm, gold-edged, flickering like a lantern carried up a long stair. Somewhere inside it, Petra Underhill was preparing to finish a sentence her mistress had begun five years ago. Somewhere inside it, a girl was holding a dragon's heart in an iron cage. Somewhere inside it, a hollow shaped like a burning heart waited to be filled.

Anselm picked up his staff. His legs shook. His faith, for the first time in eleven years, did not.

"Hold the door," he said to the darkness, to the tower, to whatever had leaned close and listened. "We're coming. And she's going to need every word you have."

Inside the Spire, the light flared — once, twice — like a heartbeat, like an answer, like a bell rung by no hand at all.
