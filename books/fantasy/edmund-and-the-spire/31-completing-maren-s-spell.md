## 31. Completing Maren's Spell

The Spire's hollow waited.

Petra had been staring at it for what felt like hours — the heart-shaped cavity in the black stone, wide enough to cradle a lantern, deep enough to swallow a hand, an arm, a life. It did not glow. It did not pulse. It simply *waited*, the way an empty chair waits at a table set for someone who is not coming home, and the patience of it was worse than any hunger could have been.

Behind her, the valley breathed. Sef sat with the iron lantern between her knees, both hands wrapped around its handle as if it were a bird that might startle. Anselm stood at the base of the tower with his translation notes spread across a flat stone, though he had stopped reading them long ago; he was watching her the way men watch weather they cannot stop. And somewhere down the road, past the ridge where the world still remembered its own name, Vane lay dying or dead or something between, and the sound of his men retreating had faded into the ordinary noise of wind through ash-grass.

Petra opened the book.

She had carried it five years without opening it — through Greyhollow's markets and her careful little life, through corpse-wagons and forgotten cities and the dry well where a god had begged — and now, with her hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs first, she turned to the last written page.

Maren's handwriting filled it top to bottom, close and slanting, the script of a woman who knew she was running out of room on the page and possibly in the world. Petra read it once at a distance, the way you'd read a letter from the dead. Then again, slowly. Then a third time with her finger under each line like a child learning letters, because some of the words were not words she knew, and some of them were words she knew too well.

*To hold a door open,* the spell began, *you must give it something that was never yours to keep.*

Below that: instructions. Components. Warnings written in Maren's driest voice — *do not attempt this working while weeping, the salt will clump* — and then, three-quarters of the way down, the sentence simply stopped.

*...and the one who holds the door must speak her true name into the flame, knowing that the door will take the name and keep it, and knowing that—*

Nothing. The ink ended mid-word. The rest of the page was blank, and beneath the blankness, faint as breath on glass, the ghost of more writing that had never been pressed hard enough to survive.

"She meant to finish it," Petra said aloud. Her voice came out strange, scraped thin. "She died before she could."

"Or she left it for you," Anselm said quietly. "A sentence isn't finished until someone finishes it. That may have been the whole design."

"That's not how spells work."

"No," he agreed. "It's how gifts work. I've never been certain the two are different."

Sef looked up from the lantern. The light of Edmund's heart-flame moved on her face, and for a moment she looked very much like her mother must have looked, and very much like no one at all — a keeper with nothing left to keep except this. "The flame's ready," she said. "It's been ready since the valley. It's just waiting for you to tell it what it's for." She swallowed. "They're always waiting for that. Nobody ever tells them."

Petra knelt.

The ground at the Spire's root was warm through her skirts, warmer than any ground had a right to be this far north, and when she laid her palm against the stone she felt it — the hum, the vast slow heartbeat of every answered prayer in a dying country, all of it funneling down through the black rock into this one hollow place. A widow's drowned husband. A field of teeth. A well that had filled for the price of a luck-charm. All of it pooled here, waiting to be spent, and the Spire could not spend it alone because a door, apparently, could only be opened from the inside by someone willing to become the hinge.

Maren had known. Five years ago, in a cottage that smelled of rosemary and burning hair, Maren had seen this coming — had seen the gods failing, the country thinning, the prayers going unanswered and then answered *wrong* — and instead of telling Petra, instead of explaining, she had built a spell with a hole in it shaped exactly like the girl who slept in her loft, and she had trusted that girl to run straight through the hole and out the other side carrying the book.

*I thought you meant to use me.*

*I did mean to use you,* the ghost-vision had said, gentle as a hand on the head. *That's what trust is.*

Petra took out her knife.

The blood part was easier than she expected. She had imagined drama — a long gash, a dramatic spill — but Maren's instructions were specific and unglamorous: *three drops from the left palm, freely given, no coercion of the flesh.* She drew the blade lightly across the meat below her thumb and watched the welling red, and it hurt exactly as much as a small cut hurts, which is to say it hurt almost nothing, and somehow that was the worst part. The end of everything she had been building toward, and her body barely noticed.

Three drops fell onto the page.

The blood did not soak in. It sat on the paper in three perfect beads, trembling, and then — slowly, like frost forming in reverse — it began to move. It crawled along the grain of the page to the place where Maren's sentence broke off, and there it stopped, pooling at the edge of the last word like water at the lip of a fall.

"It wants the ash," Sef whispered. She had come to stand beside Petra without Petra hearing her move, keeper-quiet. "Flame-ash. From the lantern. It has to be given by the fire's keeper — that's the rite. I know it. I hate that I know it, but I know it." She held out her hands. "Give me the book."

"You don't have to—"

"Sef," Anselm said softly, warningly, because they had fought about this already, twice, and the third fight had nearly come to blows in a forgotten city full of people who couldn't remember their names.

But Sef only shook her head. "Not that. I'm not volunteering for the door. I promised you I wouldn't." Her jaw tightened. "I'm doing my job. The flame is mine to carry and mine to give, and giving it to a page is not dying. It's *tending*." She knelt, opened the lantern's little iron door, and reached in.

Petra flinched — every instinct screamed that the white fire would take the girl's fingers off at the knuckle — but Edmund's flame closed around Sef's hand like a cat leaning into a scratch, and when she drew it back her palm was blackened with ash, fine and silver-grey, smelling of cold iron and hot copper and something older underneath, something like the first fire anyone ever lit.

Sef pressed her ash-blackened palm to the page, beside the blood.

"Now you," she said. "Your name. Your line. She wrote most of the sentence. You write the end of it."

"I don't know how it ends."

"Nobody does. That's why it's yours."

Petra looked down at the book — the stolen book, the guilty book, the locked cover she had carried like a stone in her chest for five years — and understood, finally, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the very bottom of things, that she had never stolen anything at all. You cannot steal what is put into your hands. You can only refuse it. And she had refused it for five years, and the refusal had cost her a mistress, a home, a truth, and nearly a country.

She picked up the knife again and dipped its point in the blood on her palm.

And she wrote.

The words came not from memory but from somewhere deeper than memory — from the hum of the Spire, from the warmth of the ground, from the three beads of blood that seemed to lean toward the blade like flowers toward sun. She wrote in Maren's style without meaning to, the same close slanting hand, as if the page were teaching her as she went:

*...knowing that the door will take the name and keep it, and knowing that the one who gives it does not pass through but becomes the passage — that she will not walk out of the dying country but will hold it open from within, that every soul who chooses to remain will find the borders drawn around them like arms, and that she will feel each of them pass, and know each of them, and love each of them, forever, in the small dark warm place where doors live.*

Her hand stopped. There was more — she could feel more, pressing at the back of her teeth — but the blade was shaking now, and the blood was running low, and some sentences should not be written by anyone who is still hoping to be talked out of them.

"That's not what she wrote," Anselm said. He had come around behind her, and his voice was ragged. "Maren's version — the fragments I found in the inscriptions — hers ends with one person passing through. Alone. That was the mercy she designed. You've changed it."

"Yes."

"Petra—"

"She designed it before she knew me." Petra's eyes stayed on the page. "Before she knew I'd have people. Before there was a Sef, or a you, or a dragon in a cellar who remembered her running. She built a door for one frightened girl. I'm building one for everyone who stayed." She looked up at him, and smiled, and the smile cracked something in her face that she let stay cracked. "You told me once that faith is an act, not an argument. Well. This is the act."

The page drank the last line.

It happened all at once: the blood sank in, the ash flared silver, and Maren's half-sentence and Petra's ending fused together with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater — and the book grew warm, then hot, then *light*, glowing from within like a lampshade, like a heart, like the thing it had always secretly been. Not a grimoire. Not a theft. A gift, wrapped in guilt for five years because Petra hadn't been ready to unwrap it.

"The hollow," Sef breathed. "Petra. The hollow."

The heart-shaped cavity in the Spire's black stone had begun to shine.

It started faintly, a glow like coals under ash, and then it brightened — and it was not white, as Petra had feared, nor gold, but every color at once, shifting and layering: the green of a fever breaking, the blue of a bell ringing itself at dawn, the red of a well filling for a grateful god, the warm hearth-orange of a charm-stall in a market where customers pretended not to believe and bought anyway. Every answered prayer in the country had passed through this stone, and now they were all rising to the surface at once, showing her what she had been made of.

"It's asking," Anselm whispered. He had fallen to his knees without seeming to notice, and his lips were moving — not translating now, but praying, actually praying, for the first time since his defrocking, maybe for the first time sincerely. "It's asking for the flame."

Sef lifted the lantern.

For a long moment she stood holding it, and Petra saw her saying goodbye — not to Edmund, whose body had failed days ago in the valley, but to the whole long line of keepers stretching back behind her, her mother and her mother's mother and all the ones who had tended fire until the fire took them, and who would, tonight, finally be released from that arithmetic. Sef's mouth moved. Whatever she said, she said it in the old tongue, the rite-tongue, and it sounded less like a prayer than like a lullaby.

Then she tipped the lantern, and poured Edmund's heart-flame into the hollow of the Spire.

The world went silent.

Not quiet — silent, the way the inside of a bell is silent a half-second before it rings. Petra felt the silence press against her eardrums, against her ribs, against the wet cut on her palm, and in that held breath she saw the flame settle into the heart-shaped hollow as if it had been cast for that exact mold, fitting perfectly, filling every crevice, and the light climbed the Spire's black length like dawn climbing a mountain —

— and the book in her hands spoke.

Not in words. In weight. The pages pressed themselves against her palms with sudden urgent purpose, and she understood without being told: the spell needed finishing. Not on paper. The paper had been practice. The final lines had to be spoken, aloud, into the light, by the one whose blood was on the page — and after that there would be no taking them back, no closing the cover, no running.

"Petra." Anselm's hand on her shoulder, warm and trembling. "You don't have to say it yet. The door will hold for a little while. Hours, maybe. Say goodbye first. Please. Let us—"

"If I wait," she said, "I'll find a reason. There's always a reason. That's how I ran from Maren — I found reasons for five years and called it a life." She stood, and her legs held, which surprised her. "There's no more time. You taught me that on the road. The country doesn't wait for goodbyes. Neither do I."

She stepped toward the hollow.

The light received her shadow before it received her, and her shadow went first the way a hand goes into water — testing, trusting — and where the light touched it, the shadow did not darken but *brightened*, as if Petra Underhill had been made of light all along and had merely been wearing the dark as a coat.

Behind her, Sef made a sound — small, strangled, the sound of a promise being kept against every fiber of the keeper's heart — and Petra did not turn around, because if she turned around she would see the girl's face, and she had written the ending herself, and she meant to speak it.

She raised the book over the hollow. The flame leapt up to meet it, eager, and the pages began to burn away even as she read, the words releasing themselves into the air like birds from a cage, and her voice — thin at first, then stronger, then ringing off the black stone and out across the valley and into the bones of a country that had forgotten its own name —

"—and the one who holds the door speaks her true name into the flame, knowing the door will keep it, and knowing that she does not leave but *becomes* —"

The light took the words as she gave them.

"— knowing that every border shall be redrawn around the willing, that the forgotten shall be remembered by whoever stays to remember them, that the door shall never close while one heart remains to knock —"

The book was ash now, drifting upward, silver-grey, smelling of cold iron and roses.

"— and my name, which the door asks for, is Petra Underhill, daughter of no one, apprentice of Maren, seller of charms in Greyhollow, runner, returner, keeper —"

The hollow blazed.

"— and I am *staying*."

Light erupted outward from the Spire like a ring of water from a dropped stone — silent, total, passing through the valley, through the ridge, through the burning temples of Greyhollow and the drowned villages and the fields of teeth and the dry wells, everywhere at once, touching every person in the country-that-was and asking them, gently, one by one: *will you remain?*

Petra felt the question leave her.

And then she felt the answers begin to come back — hundreds of them, thousands, arriving not as sound but as warmth, each yes a small coal settling into the new-made walls of her, and she understood, in the last instant of being only herself, that the door was already opening, and that she was the opening, and that it did not hurt at all, or else it hurt so completely that pain and joy had become the same word—

The light closed over her like a well filling.

Like a bell ringing itself.

Like a door, unlatching.

* * *

Anselm found the book's cover in the grass afterward, cool and unburned, with nothing inside it at all.

He held it for a long time. Beside him Sef knelt where Petra had stood, one hand pressed flat to the warm stone, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing in any language — living, dead, or divine — that fit the shape of the silence.

Then, far away, across the valley, a church bell that had hung mute for a generation rang once, all by itself.

And the horizon — the whole gray edge of the world — shivered, and drew in, like a lung filling.

Like arms, closing around everyone who had chosen to stay.
