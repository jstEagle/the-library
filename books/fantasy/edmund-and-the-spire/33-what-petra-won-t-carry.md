## 33. What Petra Won't Carry

The light in the Spire's hollow had not dimmed since Petra made her bargain. It burned the way a coal burns when you blow on it — steady, patient, waiting for what it was owed — and Sef could not stop looking at it.

She had spent her whole life around fire. She knew its moods the way other people knew weather. She knew the sullen heat of a banked pit and the bright greed of a fresh-fed one, knew how flame leaned toward a door left open and away from a voice raised in anger. She had learned all of it kneeling on cold cellar stones with her mother's hands over hers, learning rites she hated, for a dragon she was never allowed to love too much because loving it would make its dying worse.

But this light was none of those things. This light was a promise being kept slowly.

And promises kept slowly were the worst kind, because they gave you time to argue.

"Petra."

They stood in the valley below the Spire, where the grass grew silver-gray and did not rustle, because the wind here had been forgotten along with everything else. Edmund lay behind them, vast and still, his scales gone the color of old ash. The lantern sat between Sef's feet, and inside it the heart-flame burned white and enormous, so bright that looking at it directly left a green ghost on the inside of her eyelids. She had stopped flinching from it two days ago. Now it felt like standing next to a sleeping animal that trusted her. That was worse than any fear she'd ever carried.

Petra didn't turn. She was looking up at the Spire the way you look at a house you grew up in from the road, knowing you'll never live there again.

"Petra." Sef said it harder this time. "You can't do this."

"I can," Petra said quietly. "That's the whole problem, isn't it? I can."

"You *can't*—" Sef heard her own voice crack and hated it, hated the nineteen years in it, hated that every argument she'd ever won had been won by keeping her voice flat as slate and this one refused to stay flat. "You told me. In Greyhollow, the night we left — you told me the gods answer like dying men shouting. Loud and desperate and wrong. And now you're going to shout back at one?"

"It isn't shouting." Petra finally turned, and her face in the lantern-light looked older than it had five days ago, older than it had five years ago, lined in places Sef hadn't noticed before. "It's listening. There's a difference. Maren taught me that, or I stole it from her along with everything else. A charm is just a conversation you're brave enough to finish."

"Then be brave enough to walk through the door instead!"

"The door doesn't work that way and you know it. Anselm read the inscriptions twice. The one who holds it open stays on the hinge side." Petra smiled, and it was the market-stall smile, the one she gave customers who pretended not to know what they were buying, and Sef realized with a lurch that she'd been sold something too, all these weeks on the road, and the price was only now coming due. "You of all people should understand, Sef. Duty isn't a thing you choose. It's a thing you recognize."

"Don't you dare use my own words against me."

"They're good words. You got them from your mother, who got them from hers. That's what words are *for*."

Sef turned away. Her hands were shaking, and she pressed them flat against her thighs the way she did when the fire-pit spat, when the tallow smoked, when anything threatened to get out of control. Below them the valley floor hummed faintly, and she could feel it through her boot-soles, through her teeth, through the place behind her sternum where Edmund's flame had taken up residence. Every answered prayer in a dying country, singing in the stone.

"You promised me," she said to the silver grass. "In the corpse-wagon. When I couldn't stop shaking and you held the lantern because my hands wouldn't hold it. You said, *we'll see it through together.* Those were your exact words. I remember exact words. Keepers have to. Rites don't forgive paraphrase."

"I know what I said."

"Then say something else now and mean it, or—"

"Or what?" Petra's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the most infuriating thing about her. "Or you'll put the flame down and refuse? You won't. You can't. Not because I'm asking — because *it* is." She nodded at the lantern, at the white heart burning inside it. "Edmund gave you that fire because he trusted you to carry it where it needed to go. And it needs to go up there. We both know it. If you set it down out of anger, you're not punishing me. You're punishing everyone whose name is still written anywhere."

Sef's throat closed. Because it was true, and Petra knew it was true, and the cruelest thing about Petra Underhill — the thing Sef had loved since the first day in the market, when the hedge-witch had haggled fairly over dragon-scale instead of cheating a girl who clearly needed the coin — was that she never lied to make things easier. She let the truth sit there between people like a hot iron, and waited for you to pick it up.

"I hate you," Sef said, and her voice broke clean in half on the second word.

"No, you don't." Petra crossed the space between them and took Sef's face in both hands, the way her mother used to, the way no one had since. Her palms smelled of salt and ash and the fever-charms she'd been making all week without saying why. "You hate the shape of this. So do I. But listen to me now, because I'm only going to say this once, and then we're going to do it properly, keeper to keeper, and neither of us is going to cry until it's done. Can you do that?"

Sef nodded, because speech had abandoned her entirely.

"Maren sent me ahead," Petra said. "Five years ago. I thought I was running. I thought I was stealing. But she built that spell for me, Sef — a door, addressed to *the one who ran* — and she finished it the only way left to her, which was to leave it unfinished, so that I would have to come back and complete it myself. That's what a teacher does when there's no time left. She doesn't give you the answer. She gives you the question with your name on it." Her thumbs moved once across Sef's cheekbones, wiping nothing away, because Sef was holding to the promise and not crying yet. "So I'm going to do the same thing. I'm going to give you a question with your name on it, and you're going to hate me for it, and then one day — maybe ten years, maybe thirty — you're going to understand it, and you're going to forgive me, and you're going to teach someone else the same way."

"What question?" Sef whispered.

"*What does a keeper keep, when there's nothing left to keep?*"

The valley hummed. Somewhere above them, in the black tower growing out of the earth like a tooth, the hollow of light pulsed once, slow, like a heart deciding whether to beat again.

"That's not fair," Sef said.

"No," Petra agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is for markets. This is a rite."

* * *

They climbed together, because Sef refused to carry the lantern up alone and Petra didn't pretend not to understand why. The path to the Spire was not a path so much as an agreement the ground had made with itself — a seam of bare stone winding up through the silver grass, worn smooth in places by feet that no longer existed. Halfway up, Sef's boot found a step carved with letters so old they were more texture than language, and she knelt without thinking, the way she'd been taught, and read them by touch.

*We stayed,* they said. *Therefore this is ours.*

"Everyone who ever climbed this wrote that," Petra said behind her. "Or it wrote itself out of their staying. Anselm thinks the Spire doesn't record history. It records *remaining*. That's why the borders are redrawing around the people who won't leave. That's why it needs a hinge." A pause. "That's why it needs me."

Sef stood. She didn't look back. "You've been writing sermons with Anselm. Don't deny it. You even have the pauses now."

"He'd be pleased you noticed. He'd also be furious that you're spending your last hour making jokes."

"I'm a keeper. Jokes are part of the rite. My mother told forty of them the night before Edmund's father died, and she cried through every single one, and the fire burned cleaner the next morning than it had in a year." Sef adjusted her grip on the lantern's iron handle; the metal had grown warm enough now that she could feel each link of the chain's pattern in her palm. "Grief needs somewhere to go that isn't the flame. Otherwise the flame takes it, and then it burns wrong forever."

Petra was quiet for three steps. Then: "Will you tell me one? A joke. For the road."

Sef thought about it. The wind that wasn't wind moved the silver grass below them, or didn't. High overhead, past the Spire's crown, the sky had begun doing the thing it did at dusk now — showing constellations that weren't there, stars arranged in shapes the old maps had names for. Tonight one of them was long and sinuous and unmistakably winged, and Sef made herself look at it straight on, because keepers don't flinch.

"All right," she said. "A farmer prays for rain. The god answers. Next morning his field is full of fish. Live ones, flopping in the wheat. So he prays again — *thank you, but I wanted water, not fish* — and the next morning the fish are gone and the field is full of fishermen. Standing in the wheat. Very apologetic. They help him harvest, actually. Best crop he ever had."

Petra laughed. It came out of her like something breaking loose — short and startled and real — and for a moment she was just a woman on a hill laughing at a bad joke, and Sef memorized the sound the way she memorized rites, exactly, completely, against the day she'd need to reproduce it for someone who'd never heard it.

Then the path crested, and the Spire's door was in front of them, and there was no more room for jokes.

* * *

Up close, the hollow in the door was bigger than it looked from the valley — a recess shaped like a burning heart, wide as a shield, deep as a well, its edges carved with the same worn letters that ran down the whole tower. The light inside it breathed. In. Out. In. Waiting for fuel.

Sef set the lantern down between herself and Petra, and the heart-flame leaned toward the hollow like a plant toward sun, and she put her hand flat on the lantern's lid and held it there.

"Not yet," she said. "First the promise."

"Sef—"

"*First the promise.*" She made her voice into the voice her mother used at the death-rites, the low one, the one that brooked nothing. "Keeper to keeper. Kneel."

And Petra Underhill — who had argued with inquisitors, out-waited priests, bargained with a dying god and won — knelt in the dust on the threshold of the last door in the world, because a nineteen-year-old girl asked her to, and because some hierarchies are older than age.

Sef knelt opposite her. Between them the lantern burned, and above the lantern the hollow breathed, and Sef took Petra's hands in hers and felt how cold they were, how steady, how much like the stones of a fire-pit an hour after the coals are raked.

"Repeat after me," Sef said. "Exactly. No paraphrase."

"Exactly," Petra echoed, and her mouth twitched. "No paraphrase."

"*I take the fire that is given.*"

"I take the fire that is given."

"*I tend it until it is done with me.*"

"I tend it until it is done with me." Petra's voice caught on the second half, just barely, and Sef gripped her hands tighter and did not stop.

"*And when it is done with me, I give it onward — whole, and willing, and without grief in the flame.*"

Silence. The hollow breathed. Somewhere far below, Edmund's body lay in the silver grass, and Sef thought she felt him listening, the way he had always listened from the cellar dark, patient as geology.

"And when it is done with me," Petra said, "I give it onward. Whole, and willing, and without grief in the flame." She exhaled. "There. Sealed."

"That's not the whole rite."

"No?"

"The rite ends with a task." Sef swallowed. Her eyes were burning and she would not blink, because blinking was the first crack and cracks become floods. "Every keeper passes the fire with a task attached. My mother gave me Edmund and said, *keep him alive until he asks you not to.* So. Here is yours."

"Give it to me, then."

"When it's over — when the door opens and the country remakes itself around whoever stayed — you find the ones who remain. All of them. The stubborn ones, the frightened ones, the ones who stayed for bad reasons and good ones and no reason at all. And you don't guard them. You don't keep them. You *teach* them." Sef's voice was shaking now and she let it shake, because this was the part of the rite where shaking was permitted. "Teach them the charms. Teach them the knots. Teach them that a prayer answered carefully is worth a hundred answered loudly. Teach them the difference between a door and a wall. Because a country that exists only because people remained — that country will need keepers, Petra, and keepers aren't born, they're *made*, one apprentice at a time, usually against their will, usually by someone who loves them enough to be hated for it."

Petra's eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them. "That's your task for me."

"That's my task for you. Say it back."

"I will teach." Petra's voice came out rough as bark. "Not keep. Teach. One apprentice at a time, against their will if necessary."

"And the last line. The one my mother said to me, the night before she died, that I swore I'd never say because it's a curse dressed as a blessing—"

"*May you outlive everything you tend,*" Petra said softly.

"*—and never outlive what you taught.*"

For a moment neither of them moved. The lantern burned between them, white and enormous, and the hollow above breathed its slow patient breath, and high overhead the dragon-shaped constellation wheeled a degree closer to the Spire's crown, as if it were descending, as if it were coming home.

Then Petra leaned forward and pressed her forehead to Sef's, keeper to keeper, the oldest gesture in the oldest rite either of them knew, and held there for the length of four breaths. Sef counted them. She counted everything now. She intended to count for the rest of her life.

"Now," Petra said, pulling back, and her face was calm and wrecked and certain, all three at once. "Give me the fire."

Sef opened the lantern.

The heart-flame rose out of it like a bird released — no, like a bird that had always been free and had merely consented to travel in iron — and it hung in the air between them for one impossible moment, and in that moment Sef would have sworn, later, to Anselm, to anyone, that it looked at her. That it knew her. That Edmund, in whatever remained of him, which was everything that mattered, inclined his vast forgotten head.

Then it went into the hollow, and the Spire drank it, and the whole tower lit from within like a lamp behind black glass, and the humming became a chord, and the chord became a name.

Petra stood. She picked up Maren's book from where it had lain against her chest for five years and one final week, and she looked at Sef, and she smiled the real smile this time — the one from the first day in the market, the one with no sale in it at all.

"Mind the stall," she said.

And she stepped toward the breathing light, and the light reached for her like a hand, and Sef — keeper, granddaughter, heir to ashes — stood at the threshold of the last door in the world and did not follow, because following was not the task.

The task was to turn around, and walk down, and begin.
