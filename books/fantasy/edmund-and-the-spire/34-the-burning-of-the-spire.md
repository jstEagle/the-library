## 34. The Burning of the Spire

The hollow in the door was shaped like a burning heart, and it was warm.

Petra had expected cold. Everything about the Spire suggested cold — the black stone that drank the valley light, the hum that lived in the teeth rather than the ears, the way the air around it moved like water over a drowned thing. But when she laid her palm against the hollow, it was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a hearth is warm an hour after the fire has gone down: heat without flame, patience without promise.

Behind her, Sef was still crying. She had been crying since Edmund died, and Petra suspected she would cry for a year, and that was right and proper. Keepers wept. That was half the rite. The other half was carrying what remained.

"Say it again," Sef said. Her voice was raw as a scraped knee. "The spell. I want to hear it one more time before —"

"You'll hear it," Petra said. "You'll hear it every time you teach it."

"That's not the same."

"No," Petra agreed, and did not turn around, because if she turned around she would see Sef's face — nineteen years old, soot to the elbows, Edmund's iron lantern hanging from her hand like a lantern holding a piece of the sun — and seeing it would make this harder than it already was, and it was already hard past the measure of hard things. "It isn't. Nothing will ever be the same again. That's rather the point."

Anselm stood further back, at the edge of the shelf of rock where the Spire grew out of the earth like a tooth out of a jaw. He had his hands folded. He had been praying, or trying to; she could tell by the set of his shoulders, which were the shoulders of a man arguing with someone invisible. He had told her once that his faith was anger wearing vestments. She wondered what it was now. She wondered if there was a word for a man who had spent his whole life shouting at empty rooms and then, at the end, heard something answer.

She would not be there to ask him.

That thought arrived the way all her thoughts arrived now: quietly, from a long way off, like weather reported from another country.

* * *

The spellbook lay open on the flat stone before the door.

Five years she had carried it without opening it. Five years of guilt with a locked cover, five years of believing herself a thief, a runaway, a coward who had stolen her mistress's life's work because she feared becoming a vessel for something vast and hungry. And then the Spire had shown her the truth — Maren at her desk by candlelight, writing not a trap but a door, writing *for the one who ran*, sending her student ahead of the catastrophe the old woman had seen coming in her bones. Petra had not stolen the book. She had been posted, like a soldier, like a letter, like a seed.

Maren had known. Maren had always known. And the last line of the last working ended mid-sentence, because Maren had died before she could finish it, or because she had known that no one could finish it but the one it was addressed to, or — and Petra believed this most of all — because some sentences can only be completed by the person who becomes them.

Petra knelt. The stone was warm here too. Everything near the Spire's heart was warm now, since Edmund's death, since Sef had tended the great body through its final hours with the old rites spoken through tears, since the heart-flame in the iron lantern had burned white and enormous and hungry for purpose.

She dipped the quill — a goose-feather quill, absurdly ordinary, bought in a village whose name no one could remember from a woman who could not remember selling it — into the little dish of ash. Edmund's ash. Flame-ash, Sef had called it, ground from the scale that had fallen away in the dragon's last molting, burned white in the lantern's first full flare. It glittered. Ash should not glitter. Ash should be grey and finished. This ash was neither; it moved, faintly, the way embers move when breathed upon.

And beside the dish, a bowl of her own blood, let that morning from her own thumb with Sef's tanning-knife, because Maren's marginal note had been specific: *the ink must be yours twice over — once in fire, once in flesh.*

"The one who ran," Petra said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the Spire's presence, flattened, as though the tower were listening through her mouth. "I'm finishing your sentence, old woman. You'd better have left room."

There was room. There had always been room. Half a page of it, blank and waiting, the paper drinking the lamplight like a mouth.

She began to write.

*Let the door be held open,* she wrote, and the words went down into the paper like stones into deep water, and the Spire's hum changed pitch, rose, became almost a voice. *Not by wood. Not by stone. Not by the last fire of the last dragon, which was given freely and is spent. Let it be held by a willing thing. Let it be held by a name.*

Her hand shook. She steadied it against her knee and kept writing, and the quill scratched, and somewhere behind her Anselm stopped praying to listen, and Sef's breathing went quiet.

*I am willing,* she wrote. *My name is Petra Underhill. I ran from Maren Coldwater five years ago because I was afraid, and I built a small careful life, and I sold luck to people who pretended not to believe in it, and every charm I made was a prayer I didn't know I was saying. Let my name be the hinge. Let my staying be the bolt. Let everyone who remains pass through, and let the country remake itself around them — around the ones who stayed, who kept, who carried, who chose this poor broken place over whatever waits beyond any door. Let the borders find them. Let the borders hold them. Let me be the door they knock on, and let me open.*

The pen slowed.

Because the next part was the true part, and the true part was the price, and she had known the price since the well, since the minor god of water had looked up at her with eyes like the inside of a rain barrel and said, in a voice like a trickle over stones: *the spire feeds on willing sacrifice, hedge-witch. It has always fed on willing sacrifice. Why do you think the gods are dying? They gave, and gave, and gave, and no one gave back.*

She wrote: *and let the giving be whole.*

Then she put down the pen.

* * *

"It doesn't have to be you." Sef's voice cracked across the shelf of rock like a whip. "It doesn't — the spell says a name, it says a willing thing, it doesn't say *you specifically* —"

"It says the one who ran, Sef."

"Then I'll run! I'll go back to Greyhollow right now, I'll run so far —"

"You've never run anywhere in your life." Petra turned at last, and saw her, and wished she hadn't, and was glad she had. Sef's face was blotchy and furious and young. "You've kept everything you were ever given. You kept a dying dragon alive in a cellar for years. You kept rites you hated because hating them and doing them anyway is what keeping *is*. You're the least running person I've ever met."

"Then I'll learn!"

"Sef." Petra crossed to her and took the girl's face in both hands, soot and salt and all. "Listen to me. This is the last lesson, and then you teach the rest of them. Do you understand? That's the trade. That's the only trade I'll accept. You carry the fire forward — not Edmund's fire, that's spent, but the *keeping* of fire. Every hearth. Every forge. Every lamp in every window of every house that stays. You teach children the old rites until the rites stop being old. And when some frightened apprentice comes to your door with a stolen book and terror in her eyes—" she smiled, and felt the smile crack something in her chest, "—you ask her to stay. You hear me? Whatever else you do, however scared you are, however much she reminds you of someone — you ask her to stay."

Sef was shaking. "You don't get to make me the keeper of *you*."

"Someone has to keep me," Petra said. "Doors are kept. That's the whole of it. That's the entire mystery of doors, and I learned it from a god in a dry well, and I'm telling you now so you never forget: nothing opens unless something holds it open, and nothing holds unless it loves what's on both sides."

"I hate you," Sef said, and threw her arms around her, and held on with the desperate strength of a girl who had spent her life holding on to dying things, and Petra held her back and let herself feel it — really feel it, the belonging she had finally found after five years of careful smallness, the family assembled out of a defrocked priest and a dragon-keeper and a dead mistress and a dead dragon and a city full of customers who pretended not to believe — and understood, with terrible clarity, that this was exactly why it had to be her. Not duty. Not guilt. Love. The Spire fed on willing sacrifice, and willingness without love was just suicide, and love without willingness was just grief. She had both. She was, God help her, *qualified*.

Anselm came to stand beside them. He did not embrace her. He was not an embracing man. Instead he took her right hand, turned it palm-up, and pressed something into it: a small wooden bead, worn smooth, from the rosary he had carried since before his defrocking, since before his anger, since he was a boy in a village that might not exist anymore.

"A priest gives these away at the end," he said. His voice was steady, mostly. "For the journey. I've been waiting thirty years for someone worth giving it to."

"Anselm —"

"Don't argue with clergy, it's unseemly." His eyes were bright and wet and furious. "You know what I preached, the day they took my parish? I told them the gods had abandoned us on purpose. I was wrong. I've been wrong for twenty years. They weren't abandoning us. They were *dying for us*, one answered prayer at a time, and none of us noticed, because that's what sacrifice looks like from the outside — it looks like silence." He closed her fingers over the bead. "So I'm noticing now. Loudly. With witnesses."

Somewhere above them, the Spire's hum climbed another octave, and the hollow in the door began, very softly, to glow.

* * *

She walked to the door alone.

The others stayed where keepers stay — behind the line, at the edge of the working, where the ones who remain belong. Sef carried the lantern. Anselm carried the book, closed now, finished, its last sentence complete in fire-ash and blood. Petra carried nothing but herself, and the bead in her fist, and a name.

Up close, the Spire was not stone. She had always known that, in the way you know things you refuse to say. Up close it was *prayer* — compressed, layered, centuries of it, every answered wish and every desperate shout pressed into black shining mass, the dying gods' last breath given architecture. She could see faces in it if she looked sideways. She chose not to look sideways.

The hollow waited, heart-shaped, glowing faint gold, warm as a living chest.

"Well," Petra said, to the Spire, to the gods, to Maren wherever she was, to the small careful ghost of her own former life. "You asked. I'm answering. Try to be gentle — I never learned how to do anything halfway, but I never learned how to do anything *well* either."

She pressed her hand into the hollow.

The pain was honest. She would give it that.

It began as heat — real heat, searing, the flesh of her palm welding itself to the shape of a burning heart — and then it did not stay in her hand. It went up her arm like dawn going across a valley, and it took her with it. It was not fire exactly. Fire would have been merciful. It was *naming* — it was the Spire reading her, all of her, every page, the way she had read Maren's book at last: cover to cover, margin to margin, every lie she'd told customers about fever-charms, every night she'd cried into her pillow in Greyhollow missing a woman she'd believed wanted to eat her soul, every small kindness, every cowardice, every knot she'd tied in string while whispering *please* to gods she claimed didn't exist. The Spire read her, and reading hurt, and being read hurt worse, and then it began to *write*.

She screamed. She was not ashamed of screaming. Pain honestly described deserves honest sound, and she screamed the way the widow's drowned husband had come back dripping and wrong, the way the farmer's field had bloomed with teeth, the way dying men shout — loud, indiscriminate, desperate — except hers was not indiscriminate. Hers was aimed. Hers was a prayer with an address.

*Take the name. Take all of it. Make me the door.*

The Spire took.

It took her name first — she felt it leave, felt *Petra Underhill* peel away like bark, like a label steamed off a jar, and for one vertiginous moment she was unnamed, a woman-shaped absence standing in a doorway, and the terror of that was worse than the burning, and then — because the spell was Maren's, and Maren had loved her, and Maren had written *let the giving be whole* knowing what wholeness cost — the name did not vanish. It *spread*. It went out of her like light out of a struck match, out across the valley, out along roads that led nowhere, into villages with no names, into mouths that had forgotten their own words, and everywhere it touched, something remembered itself.

A road remembered where it went.

A village remembered it was called Harrow's End, and had a bell, and the bell rang once, by no hand.

A woman who had not known her own name for three years sat up in bed and said, "*Margit*," and wept.

And the borders — the borders *moved*. Petra felt them move because she was becoming them; that was the joke of it, the awful gorgeous joke, that the country's edge and the door's edge were the same edge and she was being stretched along it like dough, like light, like a horizon. The borders of the country-that-had-quietly-ceased-to-exist drew themselves inward and then outward again, hunting, seeking, finding every person who had chosen to remain — the farmers who had not fled, the priests who had kept ringing bells to empty skies, the hedge-witches in their hedgerows, the neighbors who had refused to name each other even to inquisitors, the forgotten ones in the forgotten city who remembered her name when they could not remember their own. Around each of them the border settled like a shawl around shoulders. Around each of them the country said, quietly, *you are in me. You stayed. Therefore I am.*

It hurt. All of it hurt. The burning did not stop and the spreading did not stop and she did not stop, because she had written *whole* into the spell with her own blood and the spell was holding her to her own contract with the impersonal fairness of all true magic. Somewhere in it — she could not say when — her body stopped being a body and became warmth in a doorway, and the pain transposed, the way a scream held long enough becomes a note, and the note became something almost like singing.

The last thing she felt as Petra Underhill was Sef's voice, thin and clear across the valley, speaking the closing words of the rite — the words Sef knew by rote, the words she had hated, the words she would teach now until they were new:

"*What is given freely is never lost. What is kept is never gone. Keeper to keeper, fire to fire.*"

And then the door opened.

* * *

They came through in ones and twos and families, dazed, blinking, stepping out of the Spire's golden light into a country that existed again — smaller than before, poorer, real, its edges drawn tight around the people who had earned it. Anselm counted them the way he counted everything, helplessly, a shepherd's habit: four hundred and eleven souls the first day, more coming down the mountain roads each morning, each one stepping through a doorway of warm air that stood open in the valley where a black tower had been, a doorway with no frame and no wall, just light shaped like an invitation.

No one asked how it worked. The faithful rarely do.

On the third day, Sef filled the dry well at the valley's foot with water from her cupped hands, as a rite, as a gift, as a thanks she didn't have words for — and the well filled, and kept filling, and never ran dry again, not in drought, not in winter, not ever. And sometimes, when the wind moved across its surface, the water seemed to form letters, brief and dissolving, and once — only once, but Sef swore to it all her life — it spelled out a single word in a handwriting she knew better than her own:

*Stay.*

Which was strange, because the next morning, at the rebuilt market in Greyhollow, Father Anselm — who had reopened a certain charm stall, and hung a certain luck-knot above it, and begun telling a story to customers who pretended not to believe — received a visitor he did not expect: a Crown courier, mud to the knees, carrying a sealed report marked with the broken seal of Inquisitor Vane, and a question the courier could barely bring himself to ask.

"The inquisitor," the courier said. "He was seen riding toward the Spire with the reinforcements. We found his sword on the road. We found his horse. We found his report — finished, signed, dated yesterday." The courier's voice dropped. "Father, the report says he resigned his office. It says he was going home to plant a garden. But no one has seen him since. And the report is dated *tomorrow*."

Anselm looked at the seal. He looked at the well-water gleaming in his bucket. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, he felt a door — somewhere very close, somewhere inside everything — unlatch, just a little, just enough, the way it always did now, for anyone who knocked.
