## 36. Vane's Report

The ink was the hardest part.

Not the words — Inquisitor Aldous Vane had written ten thousand words in his career, edicts and interrogations and condemnations, all of them composed in a hand so steady his superiors had once remarked on it. *A man who writes like that,* the Lord Chancellor had said, years ago, pressing a seal into warm wax, *writes like a man who is certain.* The words were not hard now. He knew exactly what had happened in that valley, every hour of it, every detail scored into him like a brand. He could recite the last three days of the world the way other men recited psalms.

What was hard was writing them down honestly.

He sat at the desk in his quarters above the Greyhollow garrison — his quarters for eleven years, stripped now of everything but the desk, the chair, a lamp, and a single sheet of good paper he had bought himself, because Crown paper felt wrong under an honest pen. Outside the window, Greyhollow went about its evening. Smoke from cookfires. A cart rattling over cobbles. Somewhere a child laughing, high and unguarded, the way children laughed before the bells rang themselves and the prayers came true and the country began to forget its own name.

His right arm ached where the sword-slash had gone through muscle to bone. The chirurgeon said he would keep the arm. He would not keep much use of it. Three fingers curled stiffly now, and the hand trembled when he lifted anything heavier than a cup. He had been told, with great kindness by men who did not understand, that he was lucky.

He looked at his hand on the paper and thought: *I am not lucky. I am alive, which is a different thing entirely.*

He dipped the pen. He wrote:

---

*To the Office of the Lord Chancellor, from Chief Inquisitor Aldous Vane, concerning the matter designated the Spire Heresy.*

*It is my finding that there is no heresy.*

---

He stopped there and read the sentence back, and something in his chest turned over, slow and painful, like a millstone beginning to grind after long disuse. Eleven years. Eleven years of reports, and not one of them had begun with what he actually believed. Every report had begun with what the Crown required to be true, and his work — his whole life's labor — had been the careful, disciplined, daily translation of one into the other.

There had been a woman in the cells beneath the Chancery, in the first weeks of the answered prayers. He remembered her still: kneeling, hands folded, face wet, saying over and over that she had only asked for rain. Her husband's crops had burned. She had prayed at a shrine everyone agreed was dead, and it had rained for nine days without stopping, and the river had come up through the floorboards of her house and drowned her youngest in his cradle while she stood in the yard watching the sky with her arms open, thanking it.

Vane had interrogated her for six hours. At the end of it she had looked up at him — this woman whose prayer had killed her child — and asked him, with terrible sincerity, whether he thought the god had heard her.

He had told her the truth then, too. It had been the first honest thing he'd said in years, and he had buried it immediately afterward in a report full of the usual language: *unlicensed working, culpable negligence, sentence commuted to penance service.* But in the cell, in the dark, he had said: *I don't know. I have never known if anyone is listening. I say the words every morning and I have never once known.*

She had smiled at him. That was the worst of it. She had smiled as though he had given her a gift.

---

*In the matter of the Spire:* he wrote, and his pen moved more easily now, the way a man walks more easily downhill, *the tower no longer stands. I witnessed its destruction personally. I will describe what I saw, and I ask that whoever reads this believe me, because I am describing it plainly for the first time in my career and I do not intend ever to do it again.*

*The Spire was not destroyed by fire or force of arms. It opened. What I understood in the moments before it opened — and I understand that this will be received as the testimony of a man concussed, wounded, and half-mad with grief, and I accept that reception — is that the Spire was a door, and that the door was held shut by nothing except our refusal to knock.*

*A hedge-witch named Petra Underhill gave herself to it. I watched her do it. I had come to arrest her. I raised my sword against my own men instead, and I will answer for that crime under whatever law Your Grace sees fit to apply, though I note that the men I fought are alive and well and have, to a soldier, declined to press charges. Sergeant Corren told me, as they carried me from the field, that he had seen the light come out of the tower and had understood, without being told, that we had spent eleven years guarding the wrong thing.*

*I do not claim to understand what happened. I claim only to report it. The borders of this country moved that night. Villages that had ceased to exist returned. Roads that led nowhere found their endings again. Letters that had come back unread were delivered, some of them years late, to houses where people wept over names they had almost lost. Whatever the witch did, she did not escape through the door. She became it. There is a well outside what used to be the forgotten city that has not run dry since, and the water tastes of iron and, very faintly, of salt — the kind of salt one finds in charms against fever. I have drunk from it. I intend to drink from it again.*

*The dragon is dead. Its keeper lives, a girl called Sef, who carried its heart-flame across half a dying country in an iron lantern and gave it up at the end without hesitation. Father Anselm — defrocked, heretical, and the finest priest I have ever met — also lives, and has reopened a charm-stall in the Greyhollow market in defiance of standing edict, which I am formally noting here so that no one else need bother discovering it.*

*As for the witch: she is dead. Or she is everywhere. I find these are no longer contradictory statements, and I no longer wish to live in a country where they must be.*

---

The lamp guttered. Vane trimmed the wick with his stiff fingers, wincing, and looked out the window again. Down in the street, a lamplighter was making his rounds, and the pools of light bloomed one after another along the lane like flowers opening in fast motion. Ordinary light. Human light. It had seemed, for eleven years, like a kind of warfare — light pushing back against the dark where magic festered. Now it just looked like lamplight.

He thought about the moment on the field when he had turned his sword around.

People would want to know about that. The Chancellor would want to know about it; the tribunal, if there was a tribunal, would certainly want to know about it. And the truth was so simple it sounded like madness: he had turned his sword because a girl had knelt down beside a nineteen-year-old who wanted to die for everyone, and put her hand on the girl's shoulder, and said something he hadn't been able to hear — and the witch had refused her. Refused the sacrifice. Refused the easy arithmetic of one life against thousands. And Vane, standing thirty paces off with drawn steel and a lifetime of doctrine in his mouth, had realized that he had spent his entire career enforcing an arithmetic, and that the first person to break it was a criminal hedge-witch, and that she was right.

He had not reasoned his way to treason. He had simply seen it, the way a man sees a wall is load-bearing only until someone shows him the arch behind it.

*Peace earned, not granted* — Anselm had said that to him, once, in the forgotten city, when Vane had caught up to them and instead of arresting anyone had stood in the street with his sword still sheathed, shaking, asking his question. *Do you think the gods ever heard me?* He had asked it of a defrocked priest, in front of witches and dragon-keepers, and his voice had cracked on the second word, and he had not cared. Anselm had looked at him for a long time and said, "You'll have to earn it. Nobody grants peace. Not even gods." And then, softer: "But it can be earned. That's the good news."

He had not believed it then. He believed it now, sitting at his desk with his ruined hand and his honest page, and the believing felt less like faith than like a scar: proof that something had happened, tender to the touch, permanent.

---

*Recommendations,* he wrote.

*First: that the edicts against unlicensed magic be repealed. I do not recommend this lightly, nor out of sentiment. I recommend it because the edicts have failed on their own terms. Magic did not stop. It waited. We built a dam against a sea and congratulated ourselves on the dry land, and the sea rose anyway, and the only people who knew how to swim were the ones we had been drowning. Petra Underhill saved this country. She did it with charms sold out of a market stall and a stolen spellbook and a courage I did not believe laypeople possessed. If there must be licensing, let the licensed be taught by those who kept the old knowledge alive in cellars and hedgerows while the Crown burned books. Let the girl Sef teach fire-keeping. Let Anselm preach. Let them watch us, if watching makes us feel safe, but let them work.*

*Second: that the Office of Inquisition be dissolved. I say this as its chief officer, and I say it without ambivalence. We were founded to guard against a threat that had already passed, and we became the threat. I have signed orders that I will carry to whatever judgment awaits me, and I do not ask forgiveness, because forgiveness is not mine to ask. But I ask that the office not outlive its purpose another generation. Burn the records if you must. Better, read them. Read them and be ashamed, and then close the doors.*

*Third: that no monument be raised to the witch. She would hate it. She sold luck-knots for two coppers and lied to her customers about what they were buying because she thought hope should be affordable. Raise her a market stall instead. Someone already has. Go buy something from it and pretend you don't believe, the way everyone does. That is the monument she would have chosen.*

---

He set down the pen. His hand ached abominably. The candle had burned down two inches, and outside the window the lamplighter had finished his round and gone home, and Greyhollow lay quiet under a sky that had stars in it again — real stars, fixed and ancient, not the frantic shifting lights of the months when the sky itself had seemed unsure of its boundaries.

One more section. The hardest one. He picked up the pen again.

*Finally, a personal statement, which Your Grace may strike from the record if it embarrasses the office. I expect it will.*

*I prayed for thirty-one years. I prayed the offices every day, matins and vespers and compline, in a voice trained to carry to the back of a cathedral, and I never once believed I was heard, and I continued, because duty was the only shape my faith could take. When the gods began answering — badly, cruelly, desperately — I policed their answers, because I could not bear that they had finally spoken and had said nothing to me.*

*At the Spire, in the last hour, I asked aloud whether they had ever heard me. I will not repeat here what answered, because it was not words, and words are all I have, and I would only diminish it. But I will say this: the answer was not comfort. It was commission. I understood that I had been heard all along, and that hearing is not the same as agreeing, and that a god who lets a man walk thirty years in the dark is not absent — he is trusting.*

*I resigned my commission this morning. It was accepted with suspicious speed. I suspect the Chancellor had drafted the acceptance before I drafted the resignation, and I bear him no ill will for it. Bureaucracy, too, is a kind of prayer: words repeated toward an authority that may or may not be listening. I wish the next man better luck with his.*

*I have taken a cottage outside the walls, near the well. The land is poor and stony and has not been worked in living memory, which means it has not yet learned to disappoint anyone. I am going to grow vegetables. I am told the soil remembers how. Everything here remembers how, now. That is the whole of the miracle, as far as I can tell: things remember how, and someone paid for it, and the least I can do is plant something.*

*This is the first fully honest document of my career. I find that it is also the shortest. May whoever reads it take from it the single lesson I paid thirty-one years to learn: that certainty is not faith, obedience is not virtue, and the door was never locked. We were simply all standing on the wrong side, holding it shut, calling it guardianship.*

*Under the old law I am a traitor. Under the new one I am unemployed. I prefer the new one.*

*Aldous Vane*

---

He signed it, blotted it, folded it, and sealed it with plain wax — no crest, no office, no rank. Just a man's name pressed into cooling red, the way a farmer marks a sack of seed potatoes.

Then he took off his inquisitor's ring and set it on the desk beside the lamp, where it threw a small crooked shadow, and left it there for whoever came to clear the room.

* * *

The cottage had a leaking roof, a hearth that smoked until you learned its temper, and a garden that was mostly stone with ambitions. Vane woke before dawn the first morning out of thirty-one years of habit, lay listening to the birds start up in the hawthorn, and realized with a strange vertigo that there was nothing he was required to do. No office to attend. No prisoners to question. No edicts to draft. The day lay open in front of him like fallow ground.

He got up anyway. Habit, he was learning, was not the enemy. Habit was just faith with the theology removed: you did the thing every day because doing it every day meant something, even if you never saw what.

He turned the first spade of earth while the sky was still grey. The soil underneath the turf was black and dense and smelled of everything that had ever rotted into richness, and when he broke it open, worms turned away from the light with unhurried dignity, going about their business as though kingdoms rising and falling were weather. He supposed, thinking of it, that they were.

He worked until his bad arm screamed and then he worked a little longer, because Anselm had told him peace was earned, and this — the ache, the sweat, the stones pulled out one by one and stacked into a border wall — this felt like earning. By midmorning he had a bed perhaps ten feet square, and he sat on the stack of stones, breathing hard, and ate bread and hard cheese and drank water from the well at the edge of his plot.

The well water tasted of iron and faintly of salt.

"Good morning," he said to it. He felt foolish. He said it anyway. Thirty-one years of speaking to an empty heaven had at least taught him how to talk to things that might not answer.

The water did not answer. But the surface, for just a moment, went perfectly still — stiller than water gets, still as held breath — and in it he saw not his own reflection but a brief image of a market stall, a striped awning, a woman's hands tying a knot in colored cord, quick and sure, and then it was only water again, and his own tired face looking back at him, older than he felt, younger than he deserved.

He finished his bread. He went back to digging.

* * *

The Chancellor's reply came a week later, delivered by a nervous clerk who clearly expected the cottage to contain skulls. Vane read it standing in his garden with dirt to the elbows. The office would be dissolved, effective at year's end. The edicts would be "reviewed." His treason would be "noted and set aside," which in Chancery language meant forgiven without the embarrassment of saying so. The final line, in the Chancellor's own hand, below the secretary's formal close, read simply: *They're saying the well never runs dry. Is it true?*

Vane looked at the well. Then he wrote back, on the back of the letter, in his stiff, scarred hand, the most honest sentence of his retirement:

*Come and see. Bring something to plant.*

He sealed it, gave it to the clerk, and turned back to the soil — where, at the edge of the turned earth, a single green shoot had come up overnight through the stones, thin and stubborn and utterly impossible, and it was not a vegetable he had planted, and it was not any weed he knew, and when he knelt to look at it closely, he could swear the small leaves were shaped like a knot of cord tied for luck.
