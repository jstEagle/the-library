## 37. Sef's First Student

The fire-pit had a new home above ground now, and Sef still woke before dawn to tend it, though the law no longer required her to hide.

She climbed the stairs from her room in the old tannery — scrubbed, whitewashed, its smell of ash and lime finally faded after a year of salt and sunlight — and pushed open the door to what had once been the drying yard. The fire-keeping hall stood where the stretching frames used to hang: rough timber, open rafters, a smoke-hole that let the morning sky look down on the coals. Anselm had argued for stone walls and a proper roof. Sef had refused both. *Fire needs to see the weather,* she'd told him, in the flat voice she used when she would not be moved. *That was half of everything my mother taught me, and I'm not losing it because you want a building that looks respectable.*

He'd conceded with the particular grace of a man who had learned, late and expensively, which battles were worth his pride.

The coals needed nothing dramatic at this hour. They never did. That was the first lie of fire-keeping that Sef had spent a year unlearning: everyone imagined the work as drama — bellows and leaping flame and a keeper's arms raised against the dark. In truth it was raking. It was listening to the pitch of the coal-bed and knowing whether it wanted air or ash. It was sitting with your hands near enough to burn and not flinching, because the fire could tell.

Edmund's heart-flame lived at the center of the bed now, no longer white-hot but banked to a deep rose-gold, the color of the hour just before sunrise. A year since the valley. A year since Sef had knelt in the frost with her hands inside a dying dragon's chest, weeping through rites older than any kingdom, feeling the last great heartbeat slow under her palms like a tide going out. She carried that memory the way the lantern had carried the flame — carefully, in iron, so it couldn't spill and couldn't be dropped.

*Feed it willingly,* Edmund had told her, in that voice like gravel rolling downhill. *Not your fear. Your wanting.*

She raked the outer coals back from the heart, checked the draft through the smoke-hole, added oak from the coppice south of town — Greyhollow's own trees, growing in soil that existed again, in a country that had decided, in whatever way countries decide things, to keep itself going. Then she sat on her stool, the low one, the one her mother had used, and let the silence do its work.

Outside, the city woke. Greyhollow had been rebuilt ugly and quick and then rebuilt again slower and better, and now it was the kind of place that smelled of bread and horse and river-mud, and if you knew where to look — everyone knew where to look now; that was the strange part — you could see people carrying luck-knots openly, tied in Petra Underhill's patterns, bought at a stall by the fountain where a defrocked priest told stories to customers who pretended not to believe a word.

Magic was still outlawed. The Crown's edicts still hung on the gate-posts, sun-faded and ignored, like old scarecrows left up out of habit rather than need. But the fire-hall had a charter — Anselm had fought for that word for four months, *charter*, inked and sealed — and three students came to it now, two boys and a widow named Corla who had lost her husband twice, once to drowning and once to something worse, and who tended coals with the steady hands of someone who had already survived the worst day of her life.

They wouldn't arrive until midmorning. The first hour belonged to the fire alone. That rule Sef kept absolutely, and she kept it because of Petra.

*You'll want to fill the quiet,* Petra had said, one of the last times they spoke in the ordinary way, before the Spire, before the burning. *Don't. The quiet is where the fire tells you what it is. Maren filled every silence with instruction. That's why I ran.*

So Sef sat in the quiet, and this morning the fire was telling her something unusual: that someone was coming up the hill. Not the students — their footfalls were known to her, Corla's uneven stride, the brothers' competitive racing steps. This was lighter, faster, stopping and starting, the gait of someone who kept deciding to turn around and then deciding not to.

Sef didn't move. She listened the way her mother had taught her to listen to coal: without wanting too hard.

The knock, when it came, was barely a knock at all. Knuckles brushing wood, once, as if the door might be offended by firmer treatment.

"It's open," Sef said. "It's always open. That's rather the point of the place."

The girl who came in was perhaps sixteen, thin in the way of someone who had been eating badly and sleeping worse, with road-dust to her knees and burrs in the wool of her cloak and a healing scrape along one cheekbone where she'd clearly gone down somewhere between here and wherever here wasn't. Her eyes went everywhere at once — the coals, the rafters, the charter nailed by the door, Sef herself — cataloguing exits, Sef noted, the way you learn to when running is the only skill anyone has bothered to teach you.

And under one arm, wrapped in oilcloth, held the way you hold a thing that is either precious or stolen or, as Sef well knew, both: a book.

The sight of it went through Sef like cold water.

"I heard—" the girl began, stopped, swallowed. Started over. "They say there's a fire-keeper in Greyhollow. A real one. Who teaches." Her chin came up, defiant about something Sef hadn't accused her of yet. "I'm not asking for charity."

"Good," Sef said. "There isn't any. Fire doesn't do charity. Sit down before you fall down."

The girl didn't sit. She stood there breathing, and Sef watched the exhaustion and the wariness fight each other behind her eyes, and recognized the battle so completely that for a moment she couldn't speak at all.

Because she knew this girl. Not her face, not her name — she didn't have those yet — but her shape. Sef had been that shape once: standing in a doorway holding everything she owned, certain that whoever opened the door would eventually demand the price of her grandmother's sins, her mother's grave, her whole inherited chain of duty. Certain that belonging was a trap with a warm interior.

"You're hungry," Sef said instead of anything wise. "There's bread and dripping on the shelf, and the kettle's been on since before dawn because I don't trust mornings that start cold. Eat first. Whatever brought you here will still be true after you've eaten. Most things are less frightening fed."

The girl's defiance flickered, guttered, and gave way all at once, the way a coal gives way when you finally rake the ash off it. She sat on the bench by the wall and ate the way the starving eat — fast, guarding the plate, ashamed of it — and Sef turned back to the coals and let her, giving her the mercy of not being watched while she broke down a little.

When the plate was empty and the girl's breathing had found its floor, Sef said, "Name?"

"Bryn."

"Just Bryn?"

Bryn's mouth twisted. "Just Bryn. Names are — complicated, where I'm from." She looked at the fire then, really looked, and Sef saw the hunger underneath the hunger, the one that food didn't touch. "Is it true? What they say? That the keeper here learned from the one who burned the Spire. That the fire in there—" she pointed at the rose-gold heart of the coal-bed, and her hand shook slightly, "—is dragon-fire. That it answered prayers, once. That it chose someone."

"All true," Sef said. "Mostly true. True enough."

"And you teach it? Openly? To anyone?"

"To anyone willing to get up before dawn for a year and rake ash without complaining," Sef said. "Which filters the applicants considerably."

Something in Bryn's face cracked then — not the defiance, something deeper, some load-bearing piece — and she pulled the oilcloth bundle into her lap and held it there with both hands as if it were trying to escape.

"I stole this," she said. Flat, fast, like pulling a tooth. "From my mistress. A hedge-witch, up past Marrow Ford, the kind that keeps girls to scrub and grind and fetch, and calls it teaching. Except — except she wasn't like the stories say. She was teaching me, actually teaching me, and then I found the spell she was writing, the one at the back, the vessel-spell, the one that needs a young body, a *willing* body, and I read it and I read it and I couldn't—"

She stopped. Her hands had gone white on the oilcloth.

"So I took the book and I ran. Three weeks. Sleeping in ditches. And the whole way I kept thinking — there was a witch, years ago, everyone tells the story — Petra Underhill, who stole her own mistress's book and ran, and it turned out — they say it turned out the book wasn't a trap at all. They say the mistress was sending her ahead. Saving her." Bryn looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "But I'm not Petra Underhill. I don't get a story. I just get to be a thief forever. So I came here, because you're the only person alive who might know — was there a way to tell? With Maren's book? Was there ever a way to *know* whether running was the right thing, or whether she just — ruined everything by not staying long enough to find out?"

The question hung in the smoky air of the fire-hall, and Sef felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders like an iron lantern.

She thought of Petra in the Spire's heart, choosing to burn so the door would stay open. She thought of Maren, whose ghost had built a door out of her apprentice's flight and called it love, and who had never gotten the chance to simply ask. She thought of her own grandmother's fire-pit, and her mother dying in it, and how nobody in three generations of Sef's family had ever once said the words *do you want this* to the girl holding the tongs.

Every keeper she had ever known had inherited their duty the way you inherit a debt. Every one of them had been assumed, conscripted, expected. Nobody had ever been asked.

Nobody, until now, had ever been given the chance to ask.

"The honest answer," Sef said slowly, "is that I don't know. Petra never got to ask Maren either — not until it was too late for asking to change anything. That's the tragedy of it. Two women who loved each other, and neither one could manage a single plain sentence." She rose, crossed to the coal-bed, and stood looking down into the rose-gold heart of the flame. "But I know something else. I know that the worst thing we can do with a stolen book is let it stay stolen. Books aren't theft, girl. Silence is theft. If that spell is what you think it is, it needs finishing — by someone who knows what she's doing — or it needs burning. Either way, hiding it in your lap for three weeks of ditch-sleeping serves no one."

Bryn stared at her. "You'd help me? You don't even know me."

"No," Sef agreed. "I don't." She turned. "But I know exactly what you are, because I've seen you before. In a cellar under a tannery, talking to a dying dragon. On a corpse-wagon out of a burning city. At the edge of a valley full of ash, holding a lantern that weighed more than my whole life." She crouched to bring her eyes level with the girl's. "You're a keeper, Bryn. You just haven't been asked yet. So here's the asking, plain as I can make it: I'm asking. Stay. Learn the fire properly — not as a servant, not as a vessel, not as somebody's answer to a prophecy. As yourself, with a name you choose and duties you consent to. Stay, and we'll read your mistress's book together, in daylight, with tea, and find out what she actually meant. Stay, and in a year you'll be able to judge her fairly — which is more than most of us ever got."

Bryn's breath caught. For a long moment she didn't move, didn't speak, just looked at Sef with the expression of someone being offered water in a country they'd believed was all desert.

"Why?" she whispered. "Why would you break the pattern? Everyone runs, everyone steals, everyone inherits something terrible and passes it on. Why stop with me?"

"Because someone stopped with me," Sef said. "A witch named Petra Underhill refused to let a nineteen-year-old buy everyone's escape with a life that was already promised to the fire. She fought me for it. She won. And the last thing she ever asked of me was a promise — that I'd teach, not just keep." Sef smiled, small and fierce, the smile of someone who has buried too many teachers and intends to honor them anyway. "You're the promise, Bryn. Or the beginning of it. So — yes or no? The fire's listening, and it hates being kept waiting."

Bryn looked at the coals. At the book. At the door she'd come in through — and Sef watched her measure it, the old reflex, the runner's arithmetic — and then watch her set the arithmetic down.

"Yes," Bryn said. "I'll stay."

"Then welcome to the fire-hall of Greyhollow," Sef said. "First lesson's at dawn tomorrow. Don't be late."

But as Bryn gathered herself to rise — as the oilcloth slipped, just slightly, and Sef glimpsed the spine of the stolen book beneath — Sef's blood went cold.

For pressed into the leather, worn shallow but unmistakable, was a sigil she knew. A spiral, broken by a vertical line. The mark of Maren's line. The mark of the lineage that had produced both Maren the hedge-witch and Petra Underhill the saint.

The same mark, Sef realized with a lurch, that had been burned into the last page of Petra's recovered spellbook — the page Anselm kept framed in his study, the page he'd shown her a hundred times, the page that ended mid-sentence:

*And to the one who comes after, the door remembers—*

The sentence had never been finished.

Until, perhaps, now.

Sef looked at Bryn — at the stolen book, at the sigil, at the girl who had run from a hedge-witch up past Marrow Ford — and understood, with absolute certainty, that the cycle was not broken at all.

It had simply come around again. And this time, it had knocked on *her* door.
