## 38. The God of Small Doors

The first thing to understand about being everywhere is that it is not like being anywhere.

Petra had expected — in the last half-second before the fire took her name and unspooled it through the bones of the country — that she would feel scattered. Broken. That she would be a handful of ash thrown on a great wind, each grain alone. She had braced for loneliness the way she had once braced for Maren's anger: eyes shut, shoulders up, ready to be nothing.

Instead she woke as a threshold.

Not *on* one. Not standing in a doorway with her hand on the latch. She was the doorway — the cool dark of it, the held breath between rooms, the place where a house decided what it would let in. And she was also the well at Crossway Bottom, and the gap under the tannery door where the wind came through singing, and the moment when a key turned in a lock and the tumblers gave up their small stubborn argument. She was every unlatching in Greyhollow. She was every hinge.

It did not hurt. That surprised her most. The burning had hurt — she remembered that honestly, the way she had promised herself she would remember everything honestly, because a god who lied to herself was only a charm-seller with better distribution. The burning had been white and total and had lasted exactly as long as it needed to, and then it had become something else entirely: a warmth distributed so thin and so wide that no single point of her could ache. Pain needs a place to live. She had no places anymore. She had all of them.

She was, she understood slowly, in the way one understands a room after the candles are lit, a door.

And doors, it turned out, heard everything.

* * *

The prayers came first as weather.

That was how she experienced them at the beginning — pressure systems moving across the country, low fronts of wanting. A widow in Greyhollow's Tanner's Row wishing her bread would rise. A boy on the Salt Road praying not to be beaten. A farmer near the Ash Names asking his dead wife's forgiveness in words he'd never managed while she lived. They washed over her like rain over a roof, and at first she simply listened, the way she had once listened to customers who pretended not to know what they were buying — patiently, kindly, without correcting them.

But listening was not enough. She had spent five years selling small mercies across a market stall; she knew the difference between sympathy and help. So she began, carefully, to answer.

Carefully. That word mattered more than any other word in her new existence. She had seen what happened when gods answered carelessly — she had walked through the middle of it, the drowned husband dripping on the hearthstones, the field of teeth, the whole terrible indiscriminate generosity of dying divinities shouting their love in the wrong grammar. The old gods had answered like men going deaf: too loud, too late, grasping at whatever prayer was nearest. Petra had watched a widow's honest grief be twisted into horror by a god who meant well and could no longer read the fine print of a human heart.

She would not be that. Whatever she was now — and she still did not have a word for it, though Anselm would eventually find three or four and argue about all of them — she would read the fine print.

So when the widow prayed for rising bread, Petra did not make the loaf swell to feed a street. She warmed the yeast. Just that. A little extra kindness in the dough, a rise that came easy instead of grudging, and the widow laughed out loud in her kitchen for the first time since the Spire, and set a corner of the loaf on the windowsill without knowing why.

When the boy on the Salt Road prayed not to be beaten, Petra could not unmake the man raising the strap — she was a door, not a fist, and some things were beyond even her remaking — but she unlatched the stable door beside him, and the horses shifted and stamped, and the carter turned to see to them, and the blow fell on empty air. The boy ran. He would run again another day and not always escape; Petra knew that too, and grieved it in the way doors grieve, which is to say by opening wider.

And the farmer asking forgiveness from his dead wife — that one Petra answered herself, personally, if a presence can be said to do anything personally. She let him feel, for the length of one evening, the particular quality of light that had filled his kitchen in the years his wife had been alive. Not her ghost. Not her voice. Just the light, warm on the table, the way it had been on the night he'd almost said what he meant and hadn't. He wept into his hands until the weeping turned into something cleaner, and in the morning he planted the east field, which he had sworn he would never plant again.

Small answers. Careful ones. Kind ones, mostly, though kindness and gentleness were not always the same thing, and Petra learned the difference fast. Sometimes the kind answer was the hard one. Sometimes a prayer for return was best answered with a locked gate. She was learning the trade all over again — the same trade she'd plied at her stall, luck-knots and fever-charms, except now her stall was the whole country and her hands were hinges and wellsprings and the space beneath doors.

She had wanted, all her life, to belong somewhere. She had run from belonging, then built a careful counterfeit of it, then found the real thing in a corpse-wagon with a defrocked priest and a girl who smelled of dragon-smoke, and then she had burned it all into the foundations of a nation. She had thought that was the price. Give up belonging to buy it for everyone else.

No one had told her that apotheosis was just belonging with the walls taken away.

* * *

She found she had favorite places.

This felt faintly scandalous — shouldn't a god be impartial? — but she had never been an impartial creature, and death had not improved her in that respect. She loved the well at Crossway Bottom best, the one that never ran dry now, the one whose water carried, if you looked long enough, a glint of something that might have been iron filings or might have been starlight. Children drank there and called it sweet. Old women drew from it and muttered thanks to no one in particular. Once a very small girl leaned over the edge, saw her own reflection waver into something older and kinder, and said, "Hello," and Petra, who had not been addressed directly in months, poured a little extra coldness into the water — the good kind, the kind that tastes of morning — and the girl giggled and told everyone at supper that the well had said hello back.

They believed her the way children believe things: completely, briefly, and without consequence. But Petra treasured it. She kept the memory the way she had once kept a lucky knot in her pocket — not because it worked, but because holding it felt like luck.

She loved thresholds. All of them. Wedding thresholds, where she gathered the lint of old superstition and made it true for one breath — a bride stepping over, feeling the house decide, instantly and irrevocably, to take her in. Prison thresholds, where she lingered longest, pressing against the locks, hating them, working at them with the patient erosion of water on stone. Some nights she got one open. Some nights a cell door simply failed to latch properly, and a guard shrugged, and someone walked out into the dark and kept walking, and by morning the country had quietly rearranged its roads so that the walker's path led somewhere survivable. The borders had redrawn themselves around the people willing to remain; Petra was the border now, in a sense, and she took a fierce private pleasure in redrawing small pieces of it nightly.

And she loved, with an ache that had no location because she had no location, the door of a certain stall in Greyhollow's market.

It stood shut. It had stood shut since the burning — since Sef had come back with a lantern gone dark and Anselm with ash on his sleeves and Vane carried on a cart, broken but breathing, and the whole ragged remnant of them had stood in the empty market square and understood that the woman who belonged behind that counter was now behind everything. The stall had been left as it was. Charms still hung from the crossbar, faded, their knots gone slack. A jar of salt sat where she'd left it. Someone — Sef, Petra suspected, from the smudge of soot on the left side — had chalked a small sign on the counter: *closed. not abandoned.*

Every morning Anselm walked past it on his way to whatever errand had replaced his parish. Every morning he paused. Every morning he looked at the shutters the way a man looks at a grave he refuses to call a grave, and every morning Petra, inhabiting the latch, the hinges, the whole patient wooden face of the thing, wanted to fling herself open and say *I'm here, you old fool, I've been here the whole time.*

But doors don't speak. Not yet. Not clearly. She was still learning the grammar of her new body, and speech was the hardest lesson — harder than the burning, almost. She could nudge. She could warm, cool, loosen, tighten, glimmer. Words required a throat, and hers was distributed across forty thousand doorways.

So she practiced in small ways. A shutter creaking a greeting as Anselm passed. His hat lifting in a wind that had no business existing on a still morning. Once, memorably, she rattled the stall's latch three times in the rhythm he used to knock on her counter — *rap-rap-rap, pause, rap* — and he stopped dead in the street, went pale, pressed his hand flat against the wood, and stood there for a long time while the market flowed around him like water around a stone.

"I know," he said finally, quietly, to the shutters, to her. "I know you're there. I'm working on it."

Working on what, he didn't say. But Petra felt the shape of the thought behind the words, the way she felt all thoughts now that touched her edges — and the shape was a plan, and the plan had a counter in it, and jars, and a voice telling stories to customers who pretended not to believe.

She rattled the latch once more, softly. Affectionate. Then she let him go, because a god who clings is only a lonely woman with better reach, and she had not burned herself into the bones of a country to spend eternity clinging.

* * *

Winter came, the first winter of the country-that-was-again, and it was a hard one, smaller and poorer than the old winters, realer. The maps had to be drawn again from scratch, and the mapmakers fought bitterly about villages that had come back from forgetting with their names intact and others that returned changed, as if existence had roughed them up a little. There was less of everything. Less road, less river, less history. The people who remained spoke of the Spire in lowered voices, the way you speak of a debt you're glad to owe.

Prayers thickened in the cold. Petra heard them all.

Most she answered small. A fever cooled here. A rope held there. A drunkard found, at the precise bottom of his ruin, a door that opened onto a lit kitchen where someone was willing to yell at him and then feed him. She was careful always, kind usually, and honest — she never answered a prayer with more than the prayer could survive. That was the rule she'd carved into herself, the law of the new god of small doors: *give them what they asked for, not what it would cost them to receive.*

But some prayers she could not answer at all, and those she collected.

A mother praying for a son lost on the Inquisitors' Road, whose body lay under a cairn two provinces wide from her. Petra could not bring him back; she was a door, not a resurrection. But she could, on the anniversary, fill the mother's kitchen with the smell of the bread he'd loved, and she did, every year, and hated herself a little for the cruelty of comfort, and did it anyway, because the alternative was silence, and she had heard the silence of the old gods for five years and knew precisely what it did to people.

An old soldier praying for permission to stop fighting a war that was already over. That one Petra answered by answering someone else — she loosened the tongue of the magistrate the soldier feared, so that when he came to ask, the words *you may go home* arrived easily, almost casually, as if they'd always been waiting.

And once — this one she kept like a coal in her cupped hands — a prayer from Sef.

Sef did not pray often. Sef had told Petra once, in a cellar that smelled of scale and sulfur, that praying was what you did instead of working, and keepers worked. But on the night of the first snow, in the rebuilt fire-house in Greyhollow where she taught openly now — taught! children sitting in rows learning the old rites without hiding, without flinching, the cycle broken and swept clean — Sef stood alone by the banked coals after the students had gone and said, to the ceiling, to the smoke-hole, to whatever was listening:

"She'd better not be bored. Wherever she is. She'd better be busy."

Petra laughed. It came out as a gust down the chimney, stirring the coals into a brief bright bloom, and Sef stared at the fire, and then smiled the crooked smile that Petra had crossed a dying country to protect, and threw a pinch of salt into the flames — the old offering, the hedge-witch's coin.

"For the stall," Sef said. "Open it again soon. Somebody should be selling luck in this town who actually has some."

* * *

On the last night of the year, Petra made a decision.

She had been practicing. All winter, in the gaps between prayers, she had been pulling herself together — gathering her scattered self out of ten thousand hinges and pouring it, carefully, the way you pour the last of a bottle, into one place. It was exhausting in a way that having no body should not permit, and it thinned her everywhere else; for three days afterward, doors all across the country stuck slightly, and farmers cursed their barns, and Petra apologized to each of them individually in the form of oiled hinges.

But she wanted to try. Just once. To stand in a doorway — *in* one, not *as* one — and be seen.

She chose the well at Crossway Bottom, because it was hers the way nothing else was, because the little girl had said hello to her there, because a well is a door that opens downward and she had always liked the symmetry of that. On the last night of the year, when the bells of Greyhollow rang midnight — rang themselves, once, softly, as they now did every year, and everyone in the city paused and no one could explain why and no one tried very hard — Petra gathered herself into the well-water and rose.

She did not take a form, exactly. She took a suggestion of one: a figure in the black water, made of reflection, a woman-shape with kind eyes and salt-grey hair, visible only to anyone who happened to be looking into the well at midnight and happened to be the sort of person the world sometimes showed its seams to. That night, the little girl was there — dragged from her bed by an older sister, both of them wrapped in blankets, both of them daring each other.

The younger one looked in first.

"Hello," Petra said. Her voice was water over stones, and it was her own voice, and hearing it nearly undid her.

The girl did not scream. Children in this country had learned not to scream at miracles; they'd been raised on the bad ones, and they could tell the difference. She leaned closer instead, breath fogging the air above the water, and studied Petra with the frank appraisal of the very young.

"You're the well lady."

"I'm the well lady," Petra agreed.

"Are you the lady from the story? The one Father Anselm tells? At the stall?" The girl's sister had begun selling charms there in autumn — Anselm's doing, Anselm's voice, Anselm telling her story to customers who pretended not to believe and bought anyway. "He says you're in the doors now. He says when a lock won't work right, that's you being kind."

"Sometimes," Petra said. "Sometimes it's just a bad lock. I'm not vain enough to take credit for all of them."

The girl considered this with enormous seriousness. Then: "My gran died in autumn. Did you open her a door?"

And there it was — the question Petra had been turning over all winter, the one prayer she could not answer carefully or kindly or small, the one that sat in her chest like a stone she had chosen to carry rather than dissolve. She had no door for the dead. She had looked. She had pressed against every wall of her new existence searching for the way through that Maren's spell had promised, the door out of the country-that-was, and she had found it closed behind her — sealed by her own burning, locked from the inside, one-way. Whatever came after death, it did not go through her. She was a door between the living and the living. That was the whole of her jurisdiction, and some nights it felt like the whole of her exile.

"No," she said gently. "I couldn't do that."

The girl nodded, unsurprised, unfrightened. "Gran said you wouldn't be able to. She said you'd want to, though." She dug in her pocket and produced, of all things, a luck-knot — frayed, badly tied, unmistakably one of Anselm's stock, sold at the reopened stall by a girl who tied her knots like she was strangling the string. "I bought this for you. You're supposed to give luck, right? But Father Anselm says giving goes both ways with gods. He says that's the whole trick."

She dropped it into the well.

Petra caught it — caught it, in hands made of water and memory, felt the cheap twine and the clumsy knot and the child's warmth still in it — and if a god can weep, she wept then, and the well overflowed by exactly one tear's worth, and the girl and her sister ran home through the snow telling everyone they'd seen the well cry, and no one believed them, and everyone bought a charm from the stall the next day anyway.

Petra held the luck-knot in the dark of the water for a long time.

Then she did what she had done all her life with things too precious to keep and too precious to lose: she gave it away. She sent it down through herself, through the hidden veins of the country, through the secret doors beneath doors, and it surfaced weeks later in a fire-house in Greyhollow, tangled in the coals Sef raked clean at dawn — a frayed, badly tied luck-knot that no student would confess to owning, smelling faintly of well-water and salt.

Sef picked it up. Turned it over. Went very still.

Then she laughed — loud, cracked, delighted — and hung it over the fire-pit where the students could see it, and told them it was a relic, and refused to say of what.

* * *

Spring came to the country that existed again. It came late and unevenly, the way real things arrive, and Petra felt it the way she felt everything now: as a great loosening, a season of unlatching, ice giving up its grip on ten thousand doorways at once. The prayers changed with the weather — fewer pleas, more thanks, and thanks were easier to carry. Thanks weighed almost nothing. She could hold a thousand of them in one hinge.

She was in the market stall's latch — her favorite perch, her old counter, her old life humming under her like a struck string — when Anselm arrived earlier than usual, before dawn, with an armful of new stock and an expression she recognized from the inside: the look of a man about to do something he'd already talked himself into and out of six times.

He set the jars down. Straightened the charms on the crossbar. Tied his apron — her old apron, washed thin, absurdly large on him — and then stood with his hands flat on the counter and spoke to the empty air in the voice he used for sermons and confessions alike.

"All right," he said. "I'm going to tell them the true version today. The whole story. The spire, the bargain, the burning — all of it. And at the end I'm going to tell them you can still be reached." He paused. His thumb moved over the counter's grain, over the old burn mark she'd made with a careless candle in her second year. "I don't know if that's theology or advertising. I've stopped being able to tell the difference. I imagine you have too."

Petra rattled the latch. *Rap-rap-rap, pause, rap.* Yes.

Anselm smiled — the crooked, disbelieving smile of a man who had preached to empty gods and finally caught one answering — and flipped the sign on the counter from *closed* to *open*, and the first customer of the morning was already waiting, pretending, as always, not to know what she'd come to buy.

Petra settled into the hinges, and the latch, and the space beneath the door, and got to work.
