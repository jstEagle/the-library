## 39. The Charm Stall Reopens

The stall had been Petra's for eleven years, and Anselm knew this because he had spent four of them pretending to browse it.

He had been a different man then — defrocked, unshaven, drifting through Greyhollow's morning market like smoke looking for a chimney. He remembered the exact angle of the awning pole that leaned left, the knot in the third plank from the end, the way Petra arranged her luck-knots in fives because she said anything in fours looked like a coffin lid. He had stood at this stall with coins sweating in his palm and asked, in the flat voice of a man buying nothing, whether the fever-charms actually worked.

*They work if you don't watch them too closely,* she had said. *Like most mercies.*

He had bought three. He had watched them anyway. That was his whole life, in a sentence.

Now he stood behind the counter instead of before it, and the awning pole still leaned left, and he had not fixed it because some things should be allowed to keep their habits even when their owners cannot.

* * *

Rebuilding Greyhollow was an act performed mostly by people who refused to say the word *aftermath*.

The city had burned its own temples in those last frantic weeks — Anselm had watched the smoke from a corpse-wagon window and thought, with the peculiar clarity of the exhausted, *well, at least they finally agree with me about something.* The temples were ash. But the market had survived, because markets always survive; they are older than gods and more stubborn than kings. Within two months the stalls had gone up again like mushrooms after rain, crooked and provisional and utterly certain of themselves.

Magic was still outlawed. This was the joke nobody laughed at. The Crown's edicts still hung on the gate posts, their wax seals faded to the color of old blood, forbidding what half the city carried in its pockets. Vane's final report — the honest one, the only one he ever wrote — had been filed and forgotten by clerks who preferred their threats abstract. No witch had been burned since the Spire. No witch would be, Anselm suspected, because there was no longer anyone to burn. The witch was in the wells now. The witch was in the doors.

And every second Thursday, the witch was in a charm stall in Greyhollow market, sold by a defrocked priest who had never learned a trade and was too old to start.

* * *

He opened at dawn, as she had. He set out the stock he'd made over the winter from her notes — and they *were* her notes, recovered from the stall's hidden drawer where she'd kept everything she never wanted found: recipes in her cramped left-slanting hand, salt proportions, the exact words you whispered into a luck-knot's center (which turned out to be less a spell than a suggestion, a polite request that the thread remember what holding together felt like).

He could not make them the way she did. He knew this the way he knew his own heartbeat, which was to say constantly and with mild irritation. Her charms had a warmth to them, a small animal aliveness. His were correct. Correct was not the same.

But the well water helped. He soaked every knot in it now, as Sef had taught him — Sef, who came by on Thursdays with iron filings and fire-salt and the particular scowl of a nineteen-year-old (she insisted she was twenty now; the year had been hard on everyone's arithmetic) who has inherited something enormous and is determined to carry it without letting anyone see her arms shake.

*It likes being used,* Sef had said, the first time she'd brought him water from the well that never ran dry. *She told me once that magic is just attention with somewhere to go. So give it somewhere to go.*

So he did. Every charm went into the well water. Every charm came out carrying, faintly, unmistakably, the sense of being *noticed* — the feeling, when you held one, that someone kind was aware of your name.

The customers pretended otherwise.

* * *

That was the ritual within the ritual, and Anselm had come to love it the way one loves a liturgy: for its beautiful, transparent absurdity.

The fishmonger's wife came every week for a fever-charm "for my sister, who has a cold, nothing serious, I don't hold with any of this." She paid full price. She touched the charm to her lips when she thought no one saw.

The cooper bought luck-knots in fives, always fives, and complained loudly each time about the price of rope. He hung them over every door of his house including the privy, which Anselm knew because the cooper's daughter had told him, giggling, while buying a sweetheart's charm she also claimed not to believe in.

Old Tam the tanner — Sef's neighbor, the man who had rented the cellar under his floor to a dying dragon for six years and told the tax assessor it was a root vegetable store — came for salt charms against bad dreams. "Placebo," he said, using the word the way a man spits. "I read it in a pamphlet. It means it works because you think it works."

"And does it?" Anselm asked.

"Course it does," Tam said, offended. "I sleep like the dead." He paused. "Better than the dead, probably. The dead these days—" and he stopped, because everyone stopped, now, when they got near that particular edge of conversation. The dead these days were not gone. Everyone had noticed. Nobody said it above a whisper.

Anselm wrapped the salt charm in brown paper. "Blessings of the door upon you," he said, which was the phrase Petra had used, and which he had adopted without shame because it was true and because it drove the Crown's censors mad trying to decide whether it constituted preaching.

"Same to you," Tam muttered, and paid, and did not look at the little brass bell above the stall's frame — the bell that had rung itself once, softly, as he approached, and would ring again as he left.

Everyone heard it. Nobody mentioned it. That was Greyhollow's new religion, practiced with perfect devotion: the faith of pretending not to have faith.

* * *

Midmorning brought the child.

Children were the hardest customers and the best ones. They had not yet learned the performance of disbelief; they wore their wanting openly, like chapped hands. This one was perhaps seven, a girl with flour on her sleeves and a copper coin gripped so tightly her knuckles had gone white. She stood at the edge of the stall for a long time, working up courage, watching Anselm the way children watch large animals — with hope and suspicion in equal measure.

"You're the priest," she said finally.

"I'm a shopkeeper."

"Mam says you're the priest who tells stories."

"Mam is right, as mothers usually are." He set down his ledger. "What can I get you?"

The coin came onto the counter with great ceremony. "A door charm. For my da. He works the night kiln and he comes home and he's — quiet. Mam says he's tired but he isn't tired-tired, he's *other*-tired, and I thought—" The sentence ran out of road. Children's sentences often did; they set out boldly and discovered the world was bigger than their words.

Anselm reached under the counter. He had a box there, and in the box were the charms he did not sell — the ones he gave away, when giving away was the right shape of mercy. He took out a small wooden disc, carved with a door slightly ajar, a sliver of light showing through the gap. Petra's design. He had copied it forty times and never gotten the light quite right; hers had seemed to actually glow. His merely suggested it. Most mornings, that was enough.

"This is for the other-tired," he said. "You put it under his plate at supper. Not on the table — under. Doors open from underneath, that's the whole secret of doors, though nobody believes me."

"That's silly," the girl said, taking it with both hands.

"Completely," Anselm agreed. "Most true things are."

She ran off clutching it, and the brass bell rang once, gently, like a sigh of satisfaction, and Anselm put the copper coin in the jar marked *FOR THE WELL* and sat back down and let himself feel, for exactly as long as it took to draw one breath, the enormous weight of everything that wasn't there anymore.

Then the next customer arrived, and he smiled, because that was the job.

* * *

The story was part of the stock. He had understood this early. People didn't only come for charms; they came to stand at the stall and hear, in a low voice, between transactions, the tale of the woman who stayed.

He told it differently depending on the listener. To the skeptical he told it dry, almost bureaucratic — a hedge-witch, an outlawed tower, a legal dispute with a metaphysical entity, resolved in the taxpayer's favor. To the grieving he told it slow, with all the terrible tenderness intact: how she had run for five years from a woman who loved her; how she had come back to the truth of that love written in a book she'd stolen; how she had fed herself to a door so that everyone else could walk through it into a country that existed again.

"And did it work?" they always asked. "Did it really—"

"The country's here, isn't it?" Anselm would say. "Smaller. Poorer. Real. You can drink the water. You know your neighbors' names. A year ago half the villages past the ridge couldn't remember theirs." He would let that sit. "Whether it worked depends on what you think she was trying to do. I think she was trying to teach us the difference between escaping and staying. I think she thought we needed the lesson more than we needed her."

"And do you think she's—"

"In the well," Anselm said. "In the thresholds. In the moment a locked door unlatches." He would tap the counter. "In this stall, most Thursdays. I feel her checking my inventory. She always thought I overcharged."

This got the laugh he wanted. The laugh was important. Grief that cannot laugh becomes a stone in the chest, and Anselm had carried enough stones.

But sometimes — not often, maybe one customer in twenty — someone would go quiet after the story, and look at the well-jar, or the leaning awning pole, or the space behind the counter where Petra used to stand, and their eyes would fill, and they wouldn't say anything at all. Those were the moments Anselm understood he wasn't running a shop. He was keeping a grave that happened to sell things. And like all graves worth keeping, it was visited most by people who claimed they didn't believe in it.

* * *

Sef came at noon, as she always did, smelling of charcoal and tallow, with Edmund's memory riding her shoulders like a yoke she had decided to carry standing straight.

She inspected the stock without greeting. This was their ritual too. She picked up a luck-knot, turned it over, sniffed it, grimaced.

"Thread's better," she conceded. "Watering's good. But you're still tying the fifth loop too tight."

"It's supposed to be tight. It's the binding loop."

"It's supposed to be *held*, not strangled. She showed you the difference a hundred times, I bet, and you nodded and did it wrong anyway. You priests are all the same. You think reverence is a substitute for practice."

"I was defrocked, actually. Reverence and I are estranged."

Something flickered across her face — the ghost of a smile, quickly corralled. She set the knot down. Then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe twice a month: "I dreamed about her last night. Petra. Not the god version. Just — her. At the stall. Tying knots. She didn't say anything. She just looked up when I came in, like I was expected."

"What did you do?"

"Woke up crying, obviously." She said it flatly, daring him to make it tender. He didn't. That was why she talked to him. "Anyway. The fire-keeping class — the one in the old tannery yard? Eleven students now. One of them's a magistrate's son. If his father finds out, I'm finished, and probably he is too."

"Will you stop?"

"No," Sef said, with the simple ferocity that was her inheritance from a line of keepers going back further than maps. "Edmund waited six years in a cellar for someone willing to stay. I'm not going to be the one who runs." She gathered her purchases — fire-salt, iron filings, and, wrapped separately, a luck-knot she pretended was for a friend. "Thursday," she said, and left, and the bell rang for her the way it always did: twice. Once for arriving. Once, softer, for being seen.

* * *

The afternoon thinned out the way afternoons did, the light going long and amber through the market smoke. Anselm counted the till, restocked the well-water basin, and allowed himself the hour he permitted himself every day: the reading hour, when he sat behind the counter with Maren's book.

The book had come home eventually — passed hand to hand among survivors like a relic, until Sef had deposited it on his counter one day with the words *"you're the one who talks, you keep it."* It was burnt at one corner now, scarred from the Spire's light, and the pages where Petra had written the final lines were stiff with something that might have been blood and might have been flame-ash and was probably both. Anselm had read it so many times he could recite the ending. He read it anyway, daily, the way he had once said matins — not for information but for the shape of the act.

*A door must be held from both sides,* Maren had written, five years and a lifetime ago. *One who opens. One who stays.*

And beneath, in Petra's smaller, steadier hand:

*I stay. Let them go through. Let the country be whoever remains.*

Let the country be whoever remained. Anselm looked up from the page at the market around him — the fish-stalls and the knife-grinder and the children stealing apples with the incompetence of the very young — at the whole stubborn, diminished, resurrected city, and thought: *look what you built, girl. Out of us. Out of the ones who wouldn't leave.*

He was not a sentimental man by nature. He had been a priest of a church that taught him sentiment was a tax on clear thinking. But he was seventy-one years old, and he had buried his God and dug Him back up again in a doorway, and he had earned the right to sit in the amber light and be sentimental for one hour a day.

* * *

Near closing, the soldier came.

Young. Too young. Crown blue, though the uniform had gone shabby at the cuffs, and the edict-patrols had gone lazy since the winter — everyone knew the law was a coat the country had outgrown and simply hadn't taken off yet. The boy stood at the stall's edge with his hand resting on his sword hilt in the way of someone reminding himself it was there.

"You're selling charms," he said. "Charms are illegal."

"They are," Anselm agreed pleasantly. "Would you like one?"

The boy's jaw worked. "I'm supposed to report you."

"Are you going to?"

A long silence. Somewhere across the market, the evening bells began — the human ones, rung by hand, the ordinary ones. But underneath them, faint as a pulse, the little brass bell above the stall gave one soft ring, unprompted, and the boy flinched and looked up at it and then at Anselm, and his face did the thing that faces all over Greyhollow did now: the collapse of the performance, the brief naked glimpse of belief, terrified and hungry.

"My mother's sick," he said. "Not — officially sick. The physicians call it melancholia. She sits by the window and watches the road like someone's coming, and nobody's coming, and she won't eat unless—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The women in my street buy from you. They say the charms from this stall are different. They say they're *warm*."

"They're soaked in well water," Anselm said. "From the well with the girl's name in it."

"I know which well." The boy said it quietly, like a confession. Everyone knew which well. Pilgrims came to it, though pilgrimage was also technically illegal, and dropped in offerings, though offerings to unnamed presences were also technically illegal, and the well accepted everything and never ran dry, which was the most flagrant illegality of all and absolutely beyond prosecution.

Anselm took a fever-charm from the shelf — one of the good ones, one where he'd gotten the warmth almost right — and wrapped it in brown paper, and slid it across the counter, and waved away the boy's coin.

"First one's free to the Crown," he said. "Consider it a bribe. Report me or don't, but tell your mother to hold it against her chest at dusk and ask — politely — for the door to open a little. That's all any of this ever was. Asking politely."

The boy took the package like it might bite him. Then, at the edge of the stall, he turned. "Father—"

"I'm not—"

"I know. Sorry. Sir." He struggled with it visibly. "Is it true? What they say about her. That she's still — that she hears—"

Anselm looked at him for a long moment. Behind the boy, the market was folding itself up for the night, awnings coming down, lamps being lit, the whole city performing its nightly miracle of continuing. Above the eastern ridge, the sky was turning the deep bruised violet it went these evenings, and Anselm found himself — as he did every evening, as the whole city did, though no one admitted it — glancing upward, checking.

"She hears," Anselm said. "She answers carefully. And kindly. Those were her two rules for everything, and death hasn't improved her discipline." He smiled. "Go home, son. Ask politely."

The boy went. The bell rang him out, soft as a blessing.

* * *

Anselm closed the stall alone, as he did every night. He banked the brazier, latched the shutters, and stood a moment in the darkening street with the book under his arm, looking east.

The stars were coming out. And there — low over the ridge, where it had appeared a handful of times that winter, always on the coldest clearest nights — a constellation that no astronomer would own to, no chart contained, and no child could resist pointing at: a long sinuous curve of burning white stars, winged, unmistakable, a dragon coiled across the dark as if sleeping above the town it had given everything for.

The first time Anselm had seen it, he had fallen to his knees in the market mud, seventy years of theology crashing down around him like a burned cathedral. Now he just stood and looked, the way you look at an old friend's window to see if the lamp is lit.

"Evening, Edmund," he said.

The constellation flickered — once, twice — and Anselm would swear until his dying day that the flicker had the rhythm of laughter.

He turned for home. And behind him, in the shuttered dark of the closed stall, faint through the wood, he heard it: the small sound of a bell ringing itself, once, in a shop where no one stood — the sound of a door, somewhere, opening just a little.

Tomorrow was the last night of the year.
