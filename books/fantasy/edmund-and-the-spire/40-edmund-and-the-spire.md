## 40. Edmund and the Spire

The last night of the year came down on Greyhollow like a held breath finally let go.

Petra felt it first in her thresholds — she was thresholds now, among other things — the way every door in the city leaned into its frame at once, as if the whole country had turned its ear toward the sky. She felt it in the wells, where the water shivered without wind. She felt it in the space beneath the tannery, where an old fire-pit had gone cold and clean and full of Sef's voice, singing the rites with students instead of grief.

She had no eyes anymore, not exactly. She had attention, which was better. She could be in ten thousand places at once if she spread herself thin enough, though she preferred to be nowhere all at once and somewhere entirely: the latch of a bakery door at dawn, the moment a locked shop relented for a boy with flour on his hands, the small mercy of a jar lid turning easy when a widow's wrists ached. Small doors. That was her parish now, and it was larger than any Anselm had ever preached to.

But tonight she gathered herself together, because tonight the sky was doing something that deserved witnesses.

It began as a warmth behind the clouds, low over the eastern hills where the valley had been. The clouds thinned the way breath thins on glass. And then the stars came out wrong — or right, depending on how long you had lived, and Petra had lived past the end of living — arranged in a shape no astronomer had ever charted, because it had never been there before.

A dragon. Vast and burning, wings spread across a quarter of the heaven, its long neck arched down toward Greyhollow as if in greeting, or blessing, or the particular curiosity of a creature who had once known a girl who sold luck-knots and lied about it.

Edmund.

Not the dragon itself — that body had failed in a valley two years gone, tended through death by weeping hands that knew every rite by rote. But fire remembers. Fire is memory in its oldest form, and Edmund's flame had fed the Spire, and the Spire had become Petra, and Petra had never let go of anything she loved. So the constellation burned above the city, star-bright and patient, and everyone in Greyhollow who looked up felt, without knowing why, that they were being remembered back.

In the market square, Anselm stood at the old charm stall — her stall, rebuilt plank by plank from memory, because he claimed he couldn't remember and everyone knew he remembered perfectly — and looked up with his mouth open. He was older now, grey to the roots, and he still wore the shape of vestments even though he'd stopped pretending they belonged to any order. He had told her story so many times this year that half the city believed it and the other half bought charms anyway, which amounted to the same faith wearing different coats.

"Look," he said to the crowd, uselessly, because they were already looking. "Look what stayed."

* * *

The bells began at midnight.

Every temple bell in Greyhollow rang itself once — one note, deep and simultaneous, rolling through the streets like the city clearing its throat. The same sound Petra had heard five years ago on a morning that cracked her careful life open, the sound Maren had described as a god waking. But this time it did not frighten anyone. This time the people in the streets laughed, and wept, and pressed their palms flat against their own front doors as if greeting something familiar.

One ring. Not a summons. A knock.

Petra heard it from inside every bell at once, which is a strange thing to describe and stranger to be. The bronze sang her name — not the name she'd been born with, nor the name she'd used at market, but the newer one, the one written in water at the bottom of a well that never ran dry: *the girl who stayed*. And beneath the ringing she heard what the bells were actually saying, the way she always heard what prayers were actually saying, underneath the words people dressed them in.

*Are you there?*

That was the whole prayer. It always had been. Every prayer she'd ever answered — the fevered child, the drowning farmer, the widow at the well — had been that one question wearing a thousand costumes. *Are you there? Are you there? Are you there?*

And the old gods, dying, shouting, indiscriminate, had answered like men going under: *HERE — HERE — TAKE THIS — I DON'T KNOW WHAT YOU NEED BUT TAKE IT—*

Petra answered differently. She had learned that from Maren's unfinished sentence, finished at last in blood and ash: you don't answer a knock by tearing the door off its hinges. You answer it by opening — just a little. Enough to show the light. Enough to let someone decide for themselves whether to push.

So when the bells rang, Petra opened.

Not the great door. Never that again; the Spire was gone, ground into the valley's bones, and the country's borders had settled around the people willing to remain like a coat settling around shoulders. But everywhere in Greyhollow, on this last night of the year, small things unlatched. Shutters swung open on houses whose owners had bolted them out of habit. Padlocks clicked loose on granaries, and no grain went missing, and the granary-keepers found themselves oddly willing to share. The heavy door of the old prison — mostly empty now, mostly reformed — drifted wide, and three men walked out into the snow and, of their own accord, walked to the constable's house to say they intended to do better and would like witnesses.

Just a little. For anyone who knocked.

And they knocked. All night, they knocked — not on wood but on the air itself, on the thin places, whispering into wells and chimneys and keyholes. *Are you there?* And Petra, who was everywhere she had chosen to be, answered each one carefully, kindly, imperfectly — because imperfection was the lesson of everything, and perfection belonged only to doors that closed forever.

* * *

There was one knock she waited for. There was one knock she always waited for.

Sef came to the well at the hour before dawn, as she did every year on this night, carrying nothing but a lantern. The iron lantern. It held no flame anymore — had held none since the Spire drank the last of Edmund's fire — but Sef carried it anyway, the way you carry a mother's ring or a dead man's knife. Some things you keep not for their use but for their weight.

She was twenty-one now, and she taught fire-keeping openly in the yard behind the tannery, seven students this winter, and the Crown's edicts still said magic was outlawed and the Crown's soldiers still bought warming-charms from Anselm's stall when they thought no one saw. Sef knelt at the well's rim and set the lantern on the stones between them.

"You're showing off," she said to the sky, to the dragon, to the door. Her voice was rough with cold and something else. "A whole constellation. He'd have loved it. He'd have been insufferable about it."

Petra rose up out of the well the way she liked best — not a body, exactly, but a shape of moving water and lamplight, a suggestion of a woman with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers. She could take fuller shapes. She chose this one, because it was the one Sef had known first, kneeling beside a dying dragon in a cellar, two keepers of dying things learning each other's names.

"He'd have demanded the constellation include his good side," Petra said.

"Dragons don't have bad sides."

"They have sides they *prefer*."

Sef laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle the way it always did on this night, and Petra let her lean against the well-stones and cry the way keepers cry — quietly, efficiently, with dignity, hating every second of it. The lantern sat between them, empty and warm. Petra had made sure it was always faintly warm. It was the smallest of her miracles and the one she performed most often, and if anyone in Greyhollow had traced the warmth to its source they'd have found a girl who once ran away, spending eternity on a kindness worth almost nothing and everything.

"I brought you something," Sef said at last. She reached into her coat and drew out a book.

Petra went very still, in the way that water goes still, which is to say: completely, and with enormous tension underneath.

The book was small, hand-bound, the leather scuffed white at the corners from years of a pocket. Sef turned it over in her hands. "From the girl. The runaway. Wren, she calls herself. She's been at my classes since autumn — sits in the back, pretends she isn't listening, listens to every word." Sef's jaw tightened. "She stole it from her mistress. A hedge-witch outside Harrowmere. Grave-dust spells, door-workings, the beginnings of something big and half-finished. She took it and ran because she thought—" Sef stopped. Started again. "Because she thought the mistress meant to use her. As a vessel. For something coming."

The snow fell between them without sound. Above, the dragon constellation burned on, patient as a promise.

"She asked me what to do," Sef said. "She's scared, and proud, and she thinks she's ruined her life. She asked me — me, because apparently I give advice now, the world has lost its mind — and I didn't know what to tell her. So I'm asking you." She looked up, straight into the shape Petra wore, unflinching, the way she had looked at a dying dragon and refused to look away. "What do I tell her? Because I know what happened to the last apprentice who ran with a stolen book. I know how that story ends. I've *seen* how it ends. And I can't—"

"Sef."

"—I can't watch her carry that guilt for five years. I won't. So tell me what to say, or tell me yourself, or—"

"*Sef.*" Petra's voice came out of the well-water and the lantern-warmth and the falling snow all at once, gentle as a latch lifting. "Tell her to stay."

Sef blinked. "Stay?"

"Tell her the book was never stolen. Tell her that some mistresses spend their whole lives building a spell they can't finish alone, and the cruelest thing they can do — the most loving thing — is send the student ahead without telling her why, because a messenger who knows she's a messenger will turn around, and a girl who thinks she's running for her life will run exactly far enough." Petra felt the old guilt move through her, the guilt she had carried up a mountain of years and finally set down in a room of light where a woman she had wronged and been wronged by had shown her the truth: *I was sending you ahead. I was always sending you ahead.* "Tell her to go back. Or write to her mistress. Or wait, if waiting is what the spell needs. But tell her the door isn't locked. It never was. That's the secret nobody believes until it's too late."

Sef stared at her. Then she looked down at the little stolen book in her hands, and her thumb moved across the cover the way it moved across the iron lantern, and Petra understood that Sef had already decided, days ago, what she wanted the answer to be. She'd just needed to hear it from the door itself.

"She'll ask why," Sef said. "They always ask why."

"Then tell her a story," Petra said. "You're good at those now. Tell her about a witch who sold luck-knots in Greyhollow and pretended not to believe in them. Tell her about a priest who prayed to empty gods until they weren't empty. Tell her about a girl who kept a fire that everyone said was already dead." The snow caught in the shape of Petra's hair and did not melt, because she willed it not to, because some things should stay. "Tell her the ending hasn't been written yet. That's the only true part, and it's always true."

* * *

Dawn came up gold behind the dragon, and the constellation faded the way all night-things fade — reluctantly, keeping one bright point visible long after the rest had gone, the way a lamp keeps burning in a window for someone expected home.

Anselm opened the charm stall at first light, as he always did, and hung out the sign he'd carved himself: LUCK-KNOTS, FEVER-CHARMS, SMALL MIRACLES, NO QUESTIONS ASKED. Magic was still outlawed. Everyone bought anyway. A soldier from the garrison bought a knot for his sweetheart and pretended it was just rope. A merchant bought a fever-charm for his daughter and pretended it was folk medicine. Anselm took their coins and told them, as he told every customer, the story of the girl who stayed — and they nodded along, pretending not to believe, and tucked the charms into their coats next to their hearts, believing.

At the well, Sef wrapped the empty lantern in wool and carried it home, and paused at her own threshold — because she always paused at thresholds now, out of habit, out of love — and said, quietly, to the air:

"Thank you. For staying. For both."

And Petra, who was the threshold, who was the well and the warmth in the lantern and the unlatched shutter and the moment a locked thing relents, felt the words go through her the way Edmund's fire had gone through the Spire: wholly, painfully, gladly.

*Love against duty*, the old story had always been called, as if they were opposites, as if you must burn one to feed the other. But Petra had learned otherwise at the cost of everything, and the learning was this: duty is just love that stays. Love is just duty that sings. She had chosen to burn rather than escape alone, and the burning had not ended her — it had *spread* her, out into every hinge and handle and half-open door in the country that remained, and she was not gone, and she was not trapped, and she was not alone. She was everywhere she had chosen to be, and she had chosen everywhere.

On the last night of the year, when the bells rang themselves once, she opened — just a little — for anyone who knocked.

And late that evening, after the bells, after the dawn, after Sef had gone home to sleep, there came one more knock. Faint. Hesitant. From the direction of the tannery yard, where a girl named Wren stood in the snow with a stolen book clutched to her chest, having walked all night, having decided something enormous and terrifying in the dark.

Wren didn't know she was knocking on anything. She just stood at the edge of the fire-yard, looking at the cold pit where a dragon had died and a class would gather at midmorning, and whispered to no one, to the sky, to whatever might be listening:

"Am I too late?"

Petra smiled, in the way that wells smile, in the way that doors smile — invisibly, and with the whole of their being.

The latch lifted. The door opened — just a little.

"No," she said, in the creak of the yard gate swinging wide, in the sudden warmth of the dead fire-pit stirring, in the small voice of every second chance ever offered. "Come in. We've been keeping your place."
