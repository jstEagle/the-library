## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The city of Vell had a hundred names for luck and not one of them was true.

Oscar Marchetti — though no one in Vell had ever heard that name; here he was Osric Fen, trinket-seller, late of nowhere in particular — knew all the false ones by heart. He sold them from a folding table at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where the morning market spilled out of the great covered shambles and into the grey light like water finding its level. Luck-knots woven from three colors of thread. Iron nails bent into rings "for keeping bad dreams out." Dried rowan berries strung on red cord. Fever-bags of camphor and salt that smelled sharp enough to make your eyes water if you held them too close.

None of it worked. That was the point. That was the whole architecture of his survival, load-bearing as a keystone: nothing he sold could possibly work, because magic did not work in Vell, because magic was *forbidden* in Vell, because fifteen years ago the Wardens of the Unmaking had burned the last hedge-witch east of the mountains in a public square while the city fathers watched with their hands folded and their faces arranged into expressions of solemn relief.

So Oscar sold folk trinkets. Harmless superstition. The kind of thing grandmothers bought for grandchildren and sailors bought before long voyages and nobody, *nobody*, could be arrested for.

He tied knots well. That was the trick of it, or part of it. His fingers had been trained on living thread, on wool spun from sheep that had grazed inside a warded field, and even stripped of everything else his hands still remembered how to make a knot sit *right* — balanced, symmetrical, pleasing to the eye in a way customers couldn't articulate but always felt. He charged two coppers for a small knot, five for a large, seven for a fever-bag, and he smiled the smile of a man who believed in nothing but the coppers.

"Morning, Osric." Marta the fishwife, broad as a door, dropping her basket at the edge of his table to free her hands. "Two luck-knots. The big ones. Tam's crew goes out past the shelf tomorrow and he swears the water's been wrong all week."

"Wrong how?" Oscar asked, already reaching for the red-and-grey thread. It was good business to ask. It made people feel seen, and feeling seen made them buy.

Marta shrugged, but her shrug had a shadow in it. "Cold, mostly. Cold in a way that isn't weather. And the gulls won't go near the eastern water. Won't touch it." She laughed, short and uncomfortable. "Fish are fine, mind. Fat as you like. Just the birds acting strange."

"The sea does what it likes," Oscar said comfortably, and began to tie.

His fingers moved without him. Over, under, over, the pattern his mother had used, then Mother Aldith after her, the triple-locked loop that was supposed to hold a blessing the way a jar holds honey — except that this knot held nothing, because there was nothing left in Vell to hold. He finished it, bit the thread clean, and set it in Marta's palm along with its twin.

"For the mast," he said. "And one for the hearth. Tell Tam the gulls know something we don't, and it never hurts to be polite about it."

Marta laughed again, easier now, paid him, and went. Oscar watched her go and felt the familiar hollow open behind his sternum, the one that opened every time he gave someone a piece of dead craft and watched them carry it off like it was alive.

Because here was the truth he kept folded up smaller than any knot: the charms *should* have worked. His hands remembered how. His blood remembered how. There were nights — more of them lately, whole weeks of them lately — when he woke in his rented room above the chandler's shop with his fingertips tingling and the air around the bed thick as pondwater, as if something old and patient were pressing against the other side of a membrane, asking to be let through. On those nights he got up, lit a candle, and read Warden proclamations until the feeling passed. There was always a fresh proclamation. The Wardens posted them weekly: *Magic is Unmade. Magic is a debt unpaid. Report resonance to your district office.* The ink was always black. The paper was always stiff. They smelled faintly of cold iron, and reading them was like pressing his face against a window in winter.

*Report resonance.* As if the gift were a sound you couldn't help making. As if the crime were breathing wrong.

* * *

The child came at midmorning, when the market was at its loudest and the sun had finally fought through Vell's permanent grey to lay a thin gold stripe down Cooper's Lane.

She was perhaps six, maybe seven, small for her age, with a crop of dark curls escaping a knitted cap and a look of terrible seriousness that children only get when they've been sent on an errand too big for them. She stood at the edge of his table for a long moment, working up her courage, and Oscar let her. You learned patience in the hedge-craft. Everything came when it came.

"Please," she said finally, in the careful voice of a rehearsed speech. "I need a fever-charm. For my brother."

"Fever-bag's seven coppers," Oscar said gently. "Or I've got dried rowan for three, if it's just bad dreams with the fever."

"It's a real fever." Her chin came up. "Three days. The physicker came and said to keep him cool and give him willow tea and that sometimes—" Her voice snagged. She pushed through it. "Sometimes they don't come back from it. Papa said get a charm anyway, because it can't hurt, and Mama said don't waste money on nonsense, and Papa gave me the coppers anyway."

Oscar looked at her. Seven coppers sat in her cupped palms, counted out exactly, and he understood suddenly that she had walked across half the city alone with her brother's life balanced in her hands, and that every adult she'd met on the way had given her directions instead of hope.

There was a version of himself — the version he'd been at nineteen, running down a hedgeroad with his heart hammering and his name burning in his throat — who would have told her the truth. *Charms don't work in Vell, little one. Take the coppers home. Sit with your brother. Hold his hand. That's the only craft anyone has.*

That version of himself had died somewhere on the road south, and good riddance, because he'd been a fool, and fools got burned in public squares.

"Rowan and the fever-bag both," Oscar heard himself say. "Five coppers total. Don't tell your papa I gave you a discount; he'll think I'm soft."

Her whole face changed, and it was like watching a lamp lit in a shuttered room. She counted out five coppers with excruciating care, and Oscar took the fever-bag down from its hook — camphor and salt and dried elderflower, sewn into blue linen — and then, because his hands were traitors and had always been traitors, he did something he had not done in fifteen years.

He held it a moment longer than he needed to.

Just a moment. Just long enough for his thumb to find the seam, and for the old words — not spoken, never spoken, only *shaped* behind his teeth like a breath held underwater — to pass through him and into the bag. *Cool the brow. Ease the burn. Let the fever find its way out like water finding a drain.* It wasn't even real craft, properly speaking. It was less than a whisper of what Mother Aldith could have done with a word and a bowl of spring water. It was the magical equivalent of a mother kissing a scraped knee: mostly love, barely magic, harmless as bread.

Except that Vell had no bread either. Vell had outlawed flour.

"There," he said, handing it over, and his heart was slamming against his ribs so hard he was surprised she couldn't hear it. "Under his pillow. Change it for a fresh one every third day. And the willow tea besides — the charm helps, but it doesn't replace sense."

"Thank you," the girl said, with the gravity of a duchess, and clutched the bag to her chest, and ran.

Oscar stood very still among his dead trinkets and felt the shape of what he'd done settle over him like a wet cloak.

It was nothing. It was a whisper. No Warden in Vell could detect a whisper; their instruments were tuned for workings, for castings, for the great resonant crimes that had gotten the hedges burned. A fever-bag kissed with a breath of intent was beneath notice. It was beneath *notice*.

He repeated that to himself four times while he served the next customer, an old man wanting a knot for his dog. Five times while he ate a roll from the pie-cart. Six times while he packed up unsold stock as the light went amber and the market thinned.

He was lying to himself, and some cold clear part of him knew it, the way you know rain is coming from the ache in an old scar. Because the gift didn't work like a coin purse, something you could open a crack and close again. It worked like a bell. Touch it anywhere, however lightly, and the whole bell rang — and in a city built on silence, a bell was a bell whether it rang loud or soft.

* * *

He felt the Warden before he saw her.

That was the worst part, later — that he'd had warning, that the gift itself had tried to save him, and he'd misread it. The tingling started in his fingertips as he was folding his tablecloth, that same pondwater pressure from his bad nights, and he thought: *tired, hungry, end of a long day,* and kept packing. Then the hairs on his forearms lifted, all of them at once, like grass before lightning, and the market noise seemed to flatten, sounds arriving slightly late, slightly wrong, as though the whole street had been dipped in wax.

Then the crowd parted, the way crowds do, without deciding to, and she came through.

She wore the grey coat of the Wardens of the Unmaking, high-collared, iron-buttoned, and she moved through Cooper's Lane the way a pike moves through minnows — unhurried, exact, the water closing behind her. She was younger than he expected. Late thirties, perhaps, with brown hair pulled back severely and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever once been permitted to relax. At her belt hung the instrument every child in Vell could draw from memory: the unmaking-rod, a foot of dull metal with no ornament and no shine, which did not glow or spark or sing when magic was near.

It simply grew *cold*. And things touched by it forgot what they had been.

Oscar's hands went on folding the tablecloth. Fold, crease, fold. He had practiced being invisible for fifteen years and he deployed all of it now: the slouch of a tired tradesman, the flat eyes, the small smile ready in reserve. Around him the market performed its own pantomime of innocence, vendors suddenly fascinated by their own goods, everyone radiating the desperate studied calm of people hoping very hard not to be interesting.

The Warden stopped at his table.

"Osric Fen," she said. Not a question. She'd read the license taped to the lamppost behind him; of course she had. "Trinkets."

"Luck-knots, madam. Fever-bags. Rowan against nightmares." He gestured at the table with the easy pride of a craftsman. "All honest nonsense, I'm afraid. Keeps the spirits of the age happy, costs almost nothing."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "Honest nonsense," she repeated. "That's a fair description of most of what's sold in this market."

"And most of what's sold in this market keeps me fed, so I speak fondly of it."

She looked at him then — really looked, a long level look that had nothing of cruelty in it and nothing of warmth either, the look of a person doing arithmetic. Her right hand rested near the rod at her belt, not touching it. The air between them was very cold. Colder than the evening justified. Colder than the season justified.

"You sold a fever-bag today," she said. "Blue linen. Elderflower and camphor. To a girl of about six, dark hair, green coat."

The bottom dropped out of Oscar's stomach, and he watched himself, from a great distance, continue to fold the tablecloth. Fold, crease, fold. *You are a man selling honest nonsense. You have never shaped a word behind your teeth in your life.*

"I sell thirty of those a week," he said. "Half of Vell's children have one under their pillow, if their mothers are the worrying sort. Is the child ill? I'm sorry to hear it. The bags ease symptoms, nothing more — I tell every customer that."

"I know what you tell your customers." She turned her head slightly, surveying the lane, the shuttering stalls, the ordinary evening assembling itself around them. When she spoke again her voice was lower, pitched for him alone. "The girl's brother's fever broke an hour ago. Three days of climbing, and it broke inside an hour of her hanging that bag. The physicker is baffled. The physicker reported it. We receive reports of anomalies, Master Fen. It is most of what we do."

*Run*, said a voice in him, fifteen years old, wild, already halfway down a hedgeroad. *Leave the table, leave the stock, leave the name, run—*

He stayed. Staying was the harder discipline and the better one. Running confirmed. Running was a confession written in footsteps.

"That's medicine for you," he said, and managed a chuckle. "Fevers break when they break. If my linen gets the credit, I'll take it, but I'd sooner the physicker take the credit; he'll use it better than I would."

For a long moment she said nothing. The cold deepened. Somewhere behind his eyes Oscar felt the old gift press against its prison, frantic, useless, a bird throwing itself at glass.

Then Isolde Crane — though he did not yet know her name — reached out and touched the topmost luck-knot on his display stack. One fingertip, brief, almost casual.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. It was thread and nothing else, and he had tied it with skill and no intent at all, and for one suspended second he watched her wait for it to be otherwise, watched the arithmetic continue behind her eyes.

"Honest nonsense," she said at last, releasing the knot. "Keep it that way, Master Fen. Vell has no room for anything else." She straightened her collar, a small precise motion, and turned back into the thinning crowd. "Good evening."

"Good evening, madam," Oscar said to her retreating back, and his voice came out steady, and he was prouder of that than of anything he had done in fifteen years.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He sat in his rented room above the chandler's shop with one candle lit and the Warden proclamations spread out on the floor, and he read them until the words swam, and then he read them again. *Magic is Unmade. Report resonance.* Outside, Vell settled into its night noises — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the chandler's dog complaining at cats — an ordinary city going about its ordinary godless evening, and none of it, none of it, explained why the stars looked strange tonight.

He noticed it around midnight, at the window with the candle guttering. The sky over Vell was always bruised with smoke and lamplight; you saw perhaps twenty stars on the best nights, the stubborn ones, the bright ones. Tonight he counted eleven. Then nine. Then, while he stood with his breath fogging the glass, a star just above the eastern rooftops — the mariner's star, the one stitched onto half the luck-knots he'd ever sold — dimmed like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and did not come back.

Not cloud. There were no clouds. The rest of the sky sat hard and clear as slate.

Somewhere far to the east — east, where the gulls wouldn't go, east, where the water was cold in a way that wasn't weather — something vast and old shifted in its sleep, and the wards that had held it for three hundred years sighed like an old man settling into a chair, and every buried thing in Oscar Marchetti's body woke at once and pressed its palms flat against the inside of his skin.

His fingertips were glowing. Faintly, softly, green-gold, the color of hedge-light, the color of a door opening in a dark country, and he stared at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, to a boy on a road fifteen years gone, and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*They're going out. The stars are going out, and it knows my name.*

He closed his fists. The light died. Below in the street, a church bell that hadn't rung since the war began tolling — slow, cracked, arrhythmic — and dogs started up in every yard of every district of the city of Vell, all at once, all howling east.
