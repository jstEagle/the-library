## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The bell came first.

Not a bell of bronze or iron — Oscar had heard those all his life in Vell, the carillon of the Mint Quarter, the cracked toll of Saint Ferrow's that rang wrong on foggy nights. This was deeper, older, a sound he felt in his teeth before his ears caught it, a single vast note struck somewhere far to the east where the maps went thin and the ink turned to guesswork. It rolled across the rooftops like a wave across a harbor, and every dog in the city began to howl at once.

Oscar was crouched over his worktable with a luck-knot half-finished between his fingers, three strands of red thread and one of black, and when the sound passed through him the knot *moved* — not in his hands but in itself, the threads tightening like sinew, like something waking and flexing.

He knew what it was. He had known since he was nine years old, kneeling in the dirt behind Mother Aldith's cottage while she pressed his palm against the bark of a distant tree he could not see and said, *Feel that? That's the border breathing. Long as you can feel it breathe, boy, the dark stays on its own side of the world.*

He could not feel it breathing now.

He could feel it *stop*.

The silence after the bell was worse than the bell. It was the silence of a held breath, of a heart between beats, and Oscar sat frozen in his rented room above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane with red thread biting into his fingers and his whole body straining eastward like a compass needle, listening for a breath that did not come.

Then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. There was not a wisp of cloud over Vell that night; he would remember that afterward, the terrible clarity of it, the way the sky had been strewn with cold clean light through his open shutters and then — between one heartbeat and the next — was not. Every star guttered out at once, as if a hand had passed over the world's candle, and the darkness that came down was total and physical, a weight, a blanket thrown over the face of the night.

Oscar screamed. He didn't mean to. The sound tore out of him raw and wordless, and he clapped both hands over his mouth and stood in the black shaking, because he had learned long ago that a man who lived by hiding must never make noise in the dark.

Below him, the city began to scream too.

* * *

Vell had no name for what happened next because Vell had no memory for such things. That was the point of Vell. Fifteen years among its cobbled streets and gaslit squares had taught Oscar that the city's founders had built more than walls and wards — they had built a forgetting, a great smooth surface over the past, so that nothing sharp remained to cut anyone. Ask a Vellishman about the last war and he'd smile vaguely and speak of harvests. Ask about magic and he'd laugh, kindly, the way you laugh at a child who still believes in the bogeyman. Magic was outlawed here, yes, but outlawed the way plague is outlawed — a thing officially declared impossible, its very history sanded away.

So when the stars died, the city did not know what to be afraid of, and therefore feared everything.

Oscar pulled on his coat in the dark, fingers finding the laces by touch, and went down the stairs two at a time. The chandler's shop was shuttered but light spilled under the door of old Mistress Hobb's rooms behind it, and her voice came through the boards, high and cracked: "Lamps! Lamps, someone bring lamps, the sky's been stolen—" And beneath her voice, from the street, the rising tide of the city: doors banging open, boots on cobbles, a child crying somewhere near the fountain, a man shouting that the end of days had come and another man laughing at him with a laugh that shook at the edges.

Oscar stepped out into Candlewick Lane and looked up.

Black. Utterly, perfectly black, a sky like the inside of a closed eye. He had seen overcast nights, storm nights, the smoke-haze of a hundred chimneys smearing the heavens grey — but this was none of those. This was absence. The stars hadn't been hidden. They had been *put out*, snuffed like tallow-wicks, and standing beneath that blind black vault Oscar felt small in a way he hadn't felt since childhood, felt the way an ant must feel when the boot comes down not on it but beside it, close enough to feel the wind of it.

And underneath the terror, treacherous and warm as brandy, something else stirred.

His hands were glowing.

Faintly — so faintly that only he could have seen it, green-gold light seeping up through the skin of his palms like dawn through curtains, tracing the old scars there, the ones Aldith had given him with a thorn-needle when she bound his apprenticeship. The light pulsed once, twice, in time with something vast and distant, and Oscar's whole body turned toward it the way a sunflower turns, helplessly, toward morning.

*Home,* whispered something deep in his bones. *Home is burning.*

He hated himself for the warmth of it. Fifteen years he had run from that word, had buried it under a false name and a false trade and a careful gray life, and here it came rising out of him anyway, sweet and shameful, because some part of him — the part he'd starved and never managed to kill — had spent fifteen years waiting for exactly this: a sign, a summons, a reason to go back.

"Oi." A hand seized his shoulder and spun him around. It was Tam Broadstairs, the cooper's son, a big lad of nineteen whom Oscar sold fever-charms disguised as knotted bracelets, and Tam's face in the lamplight spilling from Hobb's window was white as flour. "Marchetti — Marchetti, your knots, look—"

Oscar looked. At Tam's wrist, beneath the leather cord, the little braided charm Oscar had made him — three strands against fever, nothing more, a trinket, a placebo, a hedge-witch's sleight of hand stripped down to superstition — was *burning*. Not with fire. With the same green-gold light that lived in Oscar's palms, pulsing, straining eastward like a hound at the end of a leash.

Every charm he had ever sold in this city was doing the same. He could see them from where he stood: the luck-knots in windows up and down the lane, the fever-cords on wrists, the little woven stars mothers hung over cradles — dozens of them, dozens of his buried, deniable, deliberately-weak workings, all lit up like embers blown upon, all pulling east.

Toward the border. Toward the tree. Toward home.

"They're alive," Tam breathed. He wasn't afraid of the light, Oscar realized. He was awed. Nineteen years old and raised in a city that had forgotten magic existed, and some ancient hunger in him recognized it anyway, the way a foundling recognizes its mother's voice. "They're — Marchetti, what are these? What did you *make*?"

"Trinkets," Oscar said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Folk charms. Thread and knots and nonsense."

Tam stared at him. Around them the lane boiled with panic — a woman ran past clutching a chicken to her chest, someone fell and was hauled up cursing, the watch-bells of the Mint Quarter began to clang out some emergency pattern that nobody living remembered the meaning of — and in the middle of it Tam Broadstairs held up his glowing wrist and looked at Oscar Marchetti with the slow dawning expression of a man watching a mask slide off.

"You're one of them," Tam said quietly. "Aren't you. You're a *witch*."

"I'm a trinket-seller," Oscar said, "who is going inside now, and if you've any sense you'll do the same," and he turned and walked back to his stairs with his heart hammering and his palms still shining, and behind him he heard Tam say, to no one, wonderingly: "It's warm. Why is it *warm*?"

* * *

He barred the door and put out even the stub of candle he'd lit, and stood in the dark room with his back against the doorframe, and let himself feel it.

The wards were gone.

He made himself think it plainly, the way Aldith had taught him to think — plainly, precisely, without the softening fog of fear. The border wards of the kingdom were not walls of stone. They were a living thing, or had been: rooted in a tree, fed on memory, tended by keepers whose names were written in no book because books burned and trees endured. Mother Aldith had been the last keeper. She had taken an apprentice, a boy called — and here Oscar flinched, even alone in the dark, even after fifteen years — a boy whose name he did not use anymore. And the boy had run. Had fled east to west across the kingdom to the godless city, had buried his gift under trade-goods and lies, had told himself the wards would hold, that Aldith didn't need him, that a coward's survival was its own kind of wisdom.

Now the bell had tolled. Now the stars were dead. Now his palms glowed like signal-fires, and every charm he'd ever woven strained eastward like a dog howling at a master's grave.

*Aldith.* Was she alive? Could she be alive? She'd been old when he left, old the way yews are old, seemingly eternal and secretly hollow. If the wards had fallen tonight, then either she was dead, or she had failed, or —

Or she had been *fed to them*. The thought arrived unbidden and cold, and Oscar pushed it away, and it came back, and he pushed again, and it stayed. Because there was something wrong with the fall of the wards, something his trained senses kept snagging on like cloth on a nail. Wards did not simply fail. They frayed, they thinned, they guttered — they gave warning. A keeper's whole life was spent reading those warnings, patching, feeding, mending. For the wards to collapse all at once, with a single bell-note and no long dying beforehand, was like finding a man dead in his bed with no wound and no sickness.

It was like murder.

And beneath that thought, worse: the light in his palms was not grief-light. He knew ward-magic, knew its flavor the way a cook knows salt. This was different. This was *hungry*. The green-gold pulse in his skin wasn't mourning the wards' death — it was answering something on the other side of the border, something that had waited a long time behind a locked door and had just heard the lock click.

Oscar slid down the doorframe until he was sitting on the floorboards, knees drawn up, glowing hands hanging useless between them.

Outside, Vell panicked beautifully. He could hear the whole performance through the walls: the watch blowing whistles, the priests of the Unmaking ringing their own bells and chanting their creed — *nothing is forgotten, nothing is forgiven, all things return to the quiet* — which had always struck Oscar as the most sinister prayer ever devised, though he'd never dared say so. Crowds were gathering in the squares, he guessed, staring up at the starless sky and demanding explanations from a city that had spent two centuries carefully deleting every explanation it owned.

That was the cruelty of it, he thought. The founders had bought their peace with forgetting, and now the bill had come due, and no one in Vell had the first idea what they were paying for. They would blame the weather. They would blame foreigners. They would blame witches — the word moved through the crowd-noise below like a spark along a fuse, he heard it, *witches, it's witches, find the witches* — and the Wardens of the Unmaking would oblige them, because hunting was what Wardens were for.

And somewhere in that hunt, sooner or later, would come a knock on the door of a trinket-seller on Candlewick Lane whose charms were currently glowing bright enough to read by.

"I should leave tonight," he said aloud, to the dark room. His breath fogged faintly gold. "Tonight, before dawn, before they organize. Take nothing. Go south, then west, lose myself in the river country—"

His hands pulsed.

East, they said. Home, they said. *Come back, they said, come back, we are dying, come back.*

"Fifteen years," Oscar told his own palms, and his voice cracked on the number like ice on a pond. "Fifteen years, and you wait until I'm comfortable. You wait until I've almost managed to forget."

The light didn't answer. Light never does. But as he sat there in the ruined dark of the starless night, the pulse steadied, slowed, softened — until it wasn't a summons anymore but something smaller and more human, something almost like a hand resting on his shoulder, almost like forgiveness offered in advance to a man who hadn't asked for it.

And that was the worst thing yet. Because he knew only one person in all the world who forgave like that — generously, silently, before it was earned — and that person had been fifteen years old the last time Oscar saw him, standing in the doorway of a hedge-witch's cottage with an armful of kindling, watching his friend walk away down the hedgeroad and saying nothing, asking nothing, keeping everything.

*Edmund.*

The name rose in him like a bubble through deep water, and when it surfaced Oscar made a sound low in his throat, half sob and half laugh, because of course. Of course. If Aldith was gone, the tree would have needed a keeper. And who else would take it? Who else had stayed when Oscar ran? Who else had spent his whole boyhood learning the roots and the rites while Oscar learned card tricks and false names?

Somewhere east, under a sky with no stars, Edmund Hale was holding a dying wall up with his bare hands. Had probably been holding it for years. Alone.

Oscar got up off the floor. His legs were unsteady and his mouth was dry and every rational instinct he possessed — every instinct that had kept him alive and hidden and safe for fifteen years — was screaming at him to stay, to bar the door, to bury himself deeper, to wait out the dark like everyone else and hope the boot came down elsewhere.

He packed instead. Quickly, in the glow of his own hands: bread, cheese, his good knife, dry socks, the little tin of needles Aldith had given him that he'd never once used and never once thrown away. He left the luck-knots. He left the rent owed to the chandler. He left the whole careful gray architecture of the life called Oscar Marchetti standing empty behind him like a suit of clothes on a hook.

At the window, before he went down, he looked east one last time. Nothing there but blackness, the blind sky, the dead stars — and beneath the blackness, so faint he might have imagined it, a smudge of green-gold on the horizon, like the memory of dawn.

Like a tree, burning cold.

He slipped out the back stair and into the starless streets, and behind him, unnoticed in all the city's chaos, a courier in travel-stained leathers was already asking at the chandler's door for a man named Marchetti, carrying a letter sealed in green wax that smelled, faintly and strangely, of sap and thorns.
