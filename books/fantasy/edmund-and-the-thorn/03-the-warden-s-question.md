## 3. The Warden's Question

The cells beneath the Hall of Unmaking had no doors, only thresholds that Isolde Crane could feel with her teeth — a faint pressure behind her jaw whenever she passed through one, like biting tinfoil. She had been a Warden for eleven years and had never stopped noticing it. Most of the others had gone numb to it long ago. Warden Brannick said you stopped feeling the wards the way you stopped feeling your own heartbeat, which was exactly the problem with people, in Brannick's opinion: they got used to things.

Isolde did not want to get used to things.

She stood now at the threshold of Interview Room Four, a slate in her hand and a list of nine names on it, and she was tired in the specific way she always got tired after the stars went out — as though the dark above Vell had reached down through the smoke of ten thousand panic-fires and pressed its thumb against the back of her skull.

Nine names. Nine charm-sellers, luck-peddlers, hedge-doctors, and one woman who sold what she called *blessed thread* out of a cart on Cooper's Row. All of them flagged by the resonance-plates in the hours after the wards fell. All of them, if the plates were right, humming faintly ever since, like bells someone had struck and not been able to silence.

One of them, the plates insisted, was more than a peddler.

Isolde pushed through the threshold and let the pressure bite her teeth, and went to work.

* * *

The first six were nothing. She knew they would be nothing before she finished the first question; she asked anyway, because procedure was the spine of the Order and because sometimes nothing turned into something halfway through an answer.

A charm-seller named Doss Fennick wept before she'd finished unrolling his list of inventory. He sold knotted red string, he swore, he sold *string*, he didn't know what it did, his mother had shown him the knots and she'd learned them from her mother and none of them had ever done anything, he'd swear it on the Founders, on the Unmaking, on whatever she wanted.

"Sit down, Master Fennick."

He sat. He was maybe fifty, with a burn-scarred forearm from some accident years back, and hands that shook so badly the slate rattled when he gripped the table's edge. He looked less like a criminal than like a man who had spent the night watching the sky eat itself and had not yet recovered the use of his own face.

"Your knots," Isolde said. "Show me one."

His fingers were too unsteady. She took the string herself, unwound a loop of it, held it up to the lamp. It was cheap cord, dyed unevenly, the kind of thing sold three-for-a-copper at every market south of the river. But the knotwork was old — older than market-trinket work, older than fashion. Seven crossings, each one tucked under the last in a pattern that made her eyes slide off it if she looked too directly. She had seen that sliding-before in confiscated grimoires. She had felt it once, years ago, in a hedgerow outside the city when a bird flew through a gap in the branches and came out the other side going the wrong direction.

"Who taught your mother?"

"A — a woman. Out east." His voice cracked. "I don't remember her name. I don't even remember my mother's face, honestly, Warden, I—"

The words stopped mattering about halfway through, because Isolde had set the string down and laid two fingers lightly over it, the way she'd been taught, the way all Wardens were taught, and felt the answer come up through the wood of the table like heat through a stove-top.

Resonance. Faint. A dying ember's worth of it — the ghost of a working, or the memory of a memory of one. Whatever power these knots had once carried had drained out of them generations ago, the way color drains from cloth hung in a window too long. Doss Fennick wasn't a witch. He was a man selling the fossilized remains of magic to people who needed something to hold.

"Where east?" she asked anyway.

"I don't — past the border country? Nobody goes there anymore, Warden. Not since—" He gestured vaguely upward, at the ceiling, at the starless sky beyond it, at everything the whole city had spent one terrible night learning to be afraid of again.

"Master Fennick," Isolde said, and softened her voice by one deliberate degree, "you're going home. Take the strings with you. Sell them if you need the money. If anyone asks, a Warden cleared you, and here is the token that proves it." She pressed the small lead seal into his palm and closed his fingers around it, and watched him stare at it as though it might bite him.

"You're not — I'm not going to the pits?"

"No one is going to the pits, Master Fennick. The Order doesn't have pits."

"They did," he whispered. "My grandfather said they did."

Isolde thought of the sealed archive levels, four floors down, where the oldest records lived behind locks no one below the rank of First Questioner could open, and said nothing, and moved him out, and called in the next name.

* * *

By the fourth hour she had cleared seven of the nine. The blessed-thread woman turned out to be genuinely blessed, in the small and harmless way of women who talked to their hens and buried iron at their doorsteps, and Isolde sent her home with a warning about labeling her goods honestly rather than any charge at all.

Which left the eighth name. The one the plates wouldn't stop humming about.

Oscar Marchetti. Stall 31, Chandler's Lane. Seller of luck-knots, fever-charms, and — according to the plate-record, which Isolde read twice — *resonance consistent with trained working under suppression*. The clerk who'd transcribed the plate's report had underlined *trained* so hard he'd torn the paper.

Trained. In a city where the practice of magic had been outlawed for longer than most families could count generations, where the Wardens' whole purpose — the purpose printed on their badges and preached from their lecterns — was the Unmaking of the art itself, root and branch, so that what happened during the war could never happen again. Trained meant schooled. Schooled meant someone had broken the law for years, patiently, secretly, teaching a child to do the one thing Vell existed to prevent.

Or it meant the plates were wrong. They were old instruments. Everything in the Hall was old. Sometimes she wondered how much of the Order's certainty was just age wearing the costume of authority.

Isolde climbed the stairs from the interview rooms, crossed the echoing hall with its black banners, and went out into a city still smelling of smoke.

Chandler's Lane was quieter than it should have been at midmorning. Half the stalls stood empty, their owners either fled or simply too frightened to open. The ones that remained sold practical things — rope, salt, candles, nails — because the night the stars went out had reminded everyone in Vell, all at once and forever, that the world contained things candles were actually for.

Stall 31 was open.

The man behind it was younger than she expected — late twenties, maybe thirty, with ink-stained fingers and the careful, pleasant blankness of someone who has practiced being forgettable until he's good at it. He was arranging little bundles of dried herbs with the fussiness of a man who needed something to do with his hands. Feverfew, sage, something she didn't recognize. Luck-knots hung from the stall's frame in rows, red string, seven crossings each, and Isolde felt her jaw tighten at the sight of them — but these were different from Fennick's. These were new. And they were not dead.

She stopped three paces from the stall and did not announce herself. That was technique, not cruelty. You learned more from the first five seconds of a man's unguarded face than from an hour of his answers.

He looked up, saw the badge, and his face did everything right. Surprise, then wariness, then the quick smoothing-over of a tradesman preparing to be cooperative. Perfectly performed. Almost perfectly. There was a half-beat at the start — between seeing the badge and beginning the performance — where something else flickered through his eyes. Not fear. Recognition, almost. As if he'd been expecting her.

"Warden." He inclined his head. "Can I help you?"

"Oscar Marchetti."

"That's me." A small, easy smile. "If this is about the crowd last night, I gave everyone their money back. Seemed fair, under the circumstances."

"It's not about the crowd." She took out her slate. "It's about your charms."

"My charms aren't charms, Warden, I've told the tax-man the same thing. Folk trinkets. Herbs for fever, knots for luck. People buy them because they like the idea of them. I sell the idea."

"Mm." She picked up one of the luck-knots without asking permission and held it between finger and thumb. Warmth crawled up her arm immediately — not heat, but the other thing, the pressure-behind-the-teeth feeling, the threshold-feeling, except there was no threshold here, only string and a smiling man and a morning sky with no stars in it even though it was day, and somehow that absence had gotten into everything, had gotten under her skin—

Seven crossings. Tucked under, each one, in the pattern that made the eye slide.

She set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be awake.

"These are fresh work," she said. "Days old. Maybe hours."

"People re-string them when the dye fades—"

"The resonance-plates flagged this stall, Master Marchetti. Last night, within minutes of the wards falling. Your stall, specifically, among forty-one registered sellers of folk remedies in this district." She let that sit. Watched his face hold. "The plates read resonance. Yours rang like a struck bell."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — the smile went out of him entirely, and what was underneath it was so raw and so young that she felt an unexpected pang, somewhere below the badge, below the training. Then the smile came back, and it was flawless again, and she hated how much better it made her feel to see it, and hated that she noticed that at all.

"Then your plates are broken," he said gently. "I'm sorry. I know that's frightening, after last night. Everyone wants someone to blame. But I boil herbs, Warden. My mother was a midwife. That's the whole of my inheritance." He spread his ink-stained hands, palms up. An honest gesture. A rehearsed one. "I'd show you anything you want. Search the stall. Search my rooms above it, if you like. I have nothing to hide."

And there it was — the invitation, offered too quickly, wrapped in too much honesty. Every liar she'd ever interviewed offered her the search. It was practically a signature.

Except.

Except that she searched anyway, because procedure was the spine of the Order, and she found exactly what a guilty man would have hidden and an innocent man would have owned: nothing. Bundles of herbs cut and labeled in a neat clerk's hand. Red string. A ledger with honest margins. A cot, a cold hearth, one chair, one cup, one book — a primer on candle-making, spine cracked with use. The room of a poor man who owned his poverty without shame. Or the room of a man who had burned everything worth finding, and burned it well, and recently — the hearth ash, when she knelt and put her hand near it, was warmer than a hearth left cold since dawn had any business being.

She crouched there a moment longer than she needed to, looking at the ash, saying nothing, letting the silence do its work. Behind her she heard him breathing, and his breathing was controlled, and controlled breathing was itself a kind of confession, and also proved nothing, and also might mean nothing, and the plates said *trained* and the knots said *alive* and the ash said *burned letter* or *burned papers* or possibly *burned breakfast*, and the truth was that she had nothing. Nothing but a feeling in her teeth.

She stood. Turned. He was standing in the doorway of his own narrow stair, blocking it with his body in a way he probably thought looked casual.

"Master Marchetti," she said. "Do you know what happened to the stars?"

Something moved behind his eyes like a fish turning under ice.

"No one knows," he said.

"The founders knew. Once." She heard her own voice go flat and strange and stopped it there. "The Order teaches that the wards fell because they decayed. Because everything decays. Because magic is a rot, and rot spreads, and the Unmaking exists to keep the rot out of Vell. That's what the badges mean. That's what I've believed since I was nineteen years old and swore the oath with my hand on the Ledger of Founders." She walked toward him, slowly, and he did not retreat, which interested her. "But last night, when the wards fell, every resonance-plate in this city rang at once. Forty-one folk-remedy sellers, and not one of them hummed louder than a dying fly. Except you."

"If your instruments say so—"

"I haven't arrested you yet, Master Marchetti. Notice that. I've had cause for an hour, and I've turned your rooms inside out, and I haven't called the escort." She stopped close enough to see that his eyes were not brown at all but a deep hazel shot through with green, green like — like something she couldn't name, some color associated with a place she'd never been, and the association itched at her like the tinfoil-pressure of a threshold. "I'm going to tell you why. And then I'm going to leave, and you're going to think very carefully about whether to run."

His throat worked.

"The Order was built to guard something," she said quietly. "That's what the old records say — the few pages of them I've ever been permitted to see. Not to destroy magic. To guard it. To keep it *somewhere*. And last night, whatever it was guarding got out, or got taken, or got used up, and the plates rang, and yours rang loudest of all — which means either you broke the wards, which I do not believe, because you have ink stains and a candle-primer and the look of a man who has spent fifteen years being small on purpose — or the wards know you. Recognize you. Called to you." She tilted her head. "So here is my question, and I'll ask it plainly, because I find that plain questions get plainer lies, and plain lies are easier to catch: *where were you trained?*"

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the lane a shutter banged. He looked at her for a long time, and she watched him decide — she could actually see the decision moving through him like weather — and what fascinated her, what kept her feet planted when every regulation she'd sworn said *take him, take him now*, was that he seemed to be deciding whether to trust her, not whether to lie.

"I don't know what you mean," he said finally, softly. "I make candles and sell knots, Warden. I'm sorry about the stars. Truly."

It was a good lie. It was such a good lie that she believed him completely for almost two full seconds, and then she remembered the ash.

"All right," Isolde said.

She took off her glove — her right glove, the one she wore for this and nothing else — and before he understood what was happening she caught his hand and pressed her bare palm against the back of his, skin to skin, and let the craft flow.

Wardens were not supposed to be able to do this. The Order taught that Wardens *unmade*; they had no making in them at all. But every Warden learned, in the third year, alone with an instructor and a sworn oath of silence, that the Unmaking could be turned inward and used to *listen* — to feel the shape of another person's working the way a blindfolded man feels the shape of a coin. It was forbidden on civilians. It required a warrant. It was, she reflected dimly, one more rule she was breaking today, in a growing pile, in a week that had already eaten the stars.

What she felt nearly knocked her backward.

Not power — or not only power. *Depth.* The man's mind, touched through the skin, rang like a well: deep and cold and echoing, and down at the bottom of it something enormous lay coiled and sleeping, something trained and trained and trained and then *buried*, deliberately, methodically, layer upon layer of suppression-work laid over it like shrouds — self-taught suppression, she realized, appalled; he had done this to himself, over years, alone, without an instructor, which was like performing surgery on your own eyes — and beneath the shrouds, faint as a heart heard through a wall, the working *beat*. Old craft. Hedge-craft. The real thing, the country thing, the thing the city had outlawed and forgotten and pretended it had never known.

And threaded through all of it, wound around it like wire, grief. Grief so old it had become structural.

She let go of his hand and stepped back.

They looked at each other. His face had gone white, and this time there was no performance over it at all, and she understood suddenly that he knew she had felt everything, and that he was waiting — with a strange, exhausted dignity — to find out what she would do with it.

Isolde Crane had arrested eleven practitioners in her career. She remembered every one of their faces. She remembered the boy who cried, the woman who spat, the old man who thanked her, and she had told herself each time that the law was the law and mercy was not hers to give, and she had mostly, on ordinary days, believed it.

But the law said magic was rot, and last night the stars had gone out, and this morning her own Order's oldest secrets had begun, very quietly, to smell wrong to her — like meat kept past its keeping, like a story told too many times by people who needed it to be true. And this man in front of her had buried his own gift alive to survive in a city that would kill him for it, and had done it so thoroughly that the burial itself was the most disciplined piece of craft she had ever felt, and discipline like that was not the work of criminals. It was the work of the *afraid*. Of people hiding from something.

From whom? From what?

"Here is what happens now," she said, pulling her glove back on. "I found no evidence. The plates malfunction — I'll file it myself, and my filing will note instrument failure across the district, which will cover the other sellers too. You'll keep your stall. You'll keep your life." She leaned in slightly. "And you will stay in this city, Oscar Marchetti, where I can find you, because I am going to keep asking my question, and one day I am going to ask it at a moment when you are too tired to lie well. Do you understand me?"

He swallowed. "Why?" The word came out rough. "Why spare me?"

Because the ash was warm, she almost said. Because your knots are alive and your hands shook when I took them. Because you looked at me like a man deciding whether to trust me, and no criminal has ever looked at me that way, and I have wanted, for eleven years, for someone to look at me that way.

"Because I don't think you broke the wards," she said instead. "I think you *heard* them break. And I think whoever taught you would want to know why — which makes you, right now, the only person in Vell who might find out. And I want to know why, Marchetti. More than I want an arrest." She stepped back. "More, even, than I want to be right."

She turned and walked away down Chandler's Lane, past shuttered stalls and frightened faces, and she did not look back, because looking back was for people who hadn't already decided.

Behind her, she felt it — faint, unmistakable, the pressure-behind-the-teeth — as a threshold opened where no threshold should be, and closed again. He had craft enough to watch her go. Good. Let him watch. Let him run, even, if running would bring him home to whatever country had taught him, because the plates had rung *toward* the border when the wards fell, all of them, like compass needles, and Isolde Crane had read those readings eleven times and could no longer pretend they pointed anywhere else.

That evening she filed her report: instrument failure, no evidence, subject cleared. Then, in the margin, in her own private shorthand, the code she used for matters requiring personal follow-up, she wrote a single line:

*M. knows the border. Follow.*

And far across the city, in a narrow room above a candle-shop, a man sat by a cold hearth holding a handful of warm ash, deciding.
