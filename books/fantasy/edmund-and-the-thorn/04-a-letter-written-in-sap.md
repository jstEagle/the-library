## 4. A Letter Written in Sap

The courier came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the market stalls were still shuttered and the gutters ran with last night's rain.

Oscar was at his table in the back room of Mrs. Fenwick's boarding house, working a luck-knot through its seventh turn, when the knock came — three raps, spaced too evenly to be a neighbor's fist. He set down the cord. His hands had learned that particular caution long ago: anything that arrived with the regularity of a clockwork was either a bill or a summons, and he had no money for one and every reason to fear the other.

He opened the door a hand's width.

The courier was a boy, maybe fourteen, with mud to his knees and the flat stare of someone who had walked farther than he wanted to think about. He wore no livery. That alone made Oscar's chest tighten, because Vell's couriers all wore livery, and anyone who didn't was paid by somebody who preferred not to be known.

"Letter for Marchetti," the boy said. "Sign here."

"I didn't order anything."

"Don't care." The boy held out a slate and a stub of chalk, patient as a fencepost. "Sign or I stand here till you do. I'm paid by the day, not the delivery."

Oscar signed a name that wasn't his, because that was what the name was for, and took the letter. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. The boy was already clattering down the stairs, and Oscar stood in the doorway holding it like something that might bite.

It was wrapped in oilcloth against the wet, tied with twine, and sealed — not with wax, but with a bead of hardened resin the color of dark honey. He knew it before he knew he knew it. Thorn-sap. His thumb found the seal's edge and his heart did something ugly and sudden against his ribs, because there was only one tree in the world that bled sap like that, and only one person left alive who would have used it.

*Edmund.*

He carried it to the table without sitting. Turned it over. The handwriting on the front was cramped and slanting, letters leaning into each other like tired travelers:

**To O. Marchetti, luck-seller, Vell. If that is even your name.**

Oscar laughed — one dry note with nothing behind it — and then stopped laughing, because the sound came out wrong, scraped raw.

He should burn it unread. That was the sensible thing, the safe thing. A letter that knew where to find him was a letter that could hang him; a seal of thorn-sap was practically a confession pressed in amber. Fifteen years of careful living said *burn it now, before you read a single word, before it gets its hooks in you.*

Fifteen years of careful living had also taught him that he had never once been able to leave well enough alone.

He untied the twine.

* * *

The paper inside was cheap and rough, the kind milled in river towns, and it smelled faintly of smoke and green things. The writing filled both sides, dense as bramble, and it was Edmund's hand — unmistakably, achingly Edmund's, the same stubborn forward-lean the boy had pressed into his copybook lessons while Mother Aldith scolded him for his margins. But it was grown up now. Harder. The letters of a man who wrote rarely and meant everything he set down.

*Oscar.*

*I don't know if this reaches you. I don't know if Marchetti is you. I hope it isn't, honestly. I hope you're fat and happy somewhere under some other name and this letter goes to a stranger who throws it away. But the hedges say a charm-seller in Vell ties knots the way Aldith taught us, and the hedges are never wrong about knots.*

*So. If it is you:*

*I'm dying. Slowly, which is worse than quickly, because slowly leaves time to write letters. The cough started three winters ago and the blood came last spring, and I've seen what Aldith looked like at the end so I know how this story finishes. Don't waste pity on me. I chose my seat.*

*The tree is failing too. Faster than me, maybe. You'll have felt it even from there — don't pretend you haven't. Everyone with a drop of the old blood has felt it for years, like a tooth going bad in the world's jaw. The wards thin every winter. Things get through that shouldn't. Small things yet. The village doesn't remember them by morning, which is a mercy and also the whole problem, because nobody remembers anything anymore, Oscar. They forgot the war. They forgot Aldith. Last month Marta Tuller's boy asked me why we call it the Nameless Green, and then asked me what a name was for, and I couldn't make him understand.*

*You know why. Or you'd know, if you'd stayed. The founders fed the memory-magic to whatever's out past the border, and the wards were the lock on the pantry door, and the lock is starving because the pantry's empty and the keepers keep dying. Aldith died holding it. I took her place because someone had to and because I was fifteen and stupid and loyal, and I have fed it pieces of myself ever since. Memories, mostly. It drinks them. I gave it my mother's face first, because she was dead anyway and it seemed fair. Then other things. I'm running low. When I'm empty, the tree takes the rest, and when the tree goes, the border goes, and then everything the founders bought with their bargain comes due all at once.*

*I need a keeper. Or the tree does. I need hands that know the work, and there is no one else. I looked. For fifteen years I looked, and every road led back to a boy who ran away in the night and left me holding the shears.*

*That's not fair. I know it's not fair. I'm not angry. I want you to read that twice: I am not angry. Anger needs somewhere to go and I've nowhere left to put it. I mostly just want to know if you're alive. And I want — God help me, I want help. Come home, Oscar. Come home and hate me if you need to, hate the village, hate the tree, hate Aldith's ghost, but come home. The stars are going out one by one over the border and I can't hold the line alone much longer, and I am so tired, and I remember your face better than my mother's now, which tells you exactly how far gone I am.*

*If you don't come, I understand. I'll feed it what's left of me and buy the world another few years, and whoever finds this letter after can do what they like.*

*Burn this if it makes you feel safer. The sap-seal will tell you it's true regardless.*

*— E.H., keeper of the thorn, last of the line*

*P.S. Your knot-work has gotten good. Aldith would have boxed your ears for the shortcut in the seventh turn, but she'd have been proud underneath. She was always proud underneath.*

Oscar read it four times.

The first time his eyes moved and nothing landed. The second time the words went in like splinters. The third and fourth times he was looking for a lie, some flaw, some tell of forgery — because Warden traps came dressed as desperate letters sometimes, and Crane's people were clever, and *come home* was exactly the sort of hook a clever hunter baited for a runaway hedge-witch.

But there was no flaw. There was only the seventh-turn shortcut, which no Warden in Vell could possibly know about, because Oscar had invented it himself at sixteen, hunched over this same kind of table, showing off to a freckled boy who laughed at him and then secretly copied it.

He sat down. He discovered he was shaking.

*Come home.* As if home were a place and not a wound. As if he could walk back up that lane and find anything waiting but a dying man and a starving tree and fifteen years of evidence that Oscar Marchetti — no, not Marchetti, never Marchetti, the real name sat behind his teeth like a stone he'd swallowed so long ago he'd grown around it — that the boy he'd been had committed the one sin the old magic never forgave.

Desertion. Not of a village. Of a *ward*. Of a living wall that needed every pair of hands it could hold, and he had slipped out between one moonset and the next with a bundle of clothes and Mother Aldith's silver pin stolen off her mantel, because he was seventeen and the city glittered and the hedge smelled of mud and duty and he wanted *more*, he had always wanted more —

And Aldith had died within the year. Holding the line. Alone except for Edmund, who had been *fifteen*.

Oscar put his palms flat on the table and breathed until the shaking slowed. Outside, Vell went about its morning: cart wheels, a fishwife shouting prices, the bell of the Unmaker's chapel tolling the hour with its flat, toneless voice. A city that had outlawed magic and called the outlawing peace. A city built on the founders' bargain, though Oscar hadn't known the shape of that bargain yet — only that the stars over Vell had been dimming for years, and everyone pretended not to notice, and the Wardens swept the markets for charms with faces like men clearing rot out of a cellar.

*Come home.*

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, in a voice that cracked in the middle.

He took the letter to the hearth. The grate still held yesterday's ash. He struck a match with hands that were almost steady, touched it to the corner of the page, and watched Edmund's handwriting curl and blacken — *I am not angry, I am not angry, I am not angry* — the ink flaring briefly green-gold as it burned, because even the words were soaked in the old craft, even the words refused to die quietly.

The sap-seal resisted longest. It bubbled and spat and gave off a smell like crushed leaves and summer, and for one absurd moment the whole room smelled like the hedge-road in June, like the year he turned fourteen and Edmund fell out of the elder tree and they lay in the grass laughing until Aldith came looking for them with a switch, and Oscar's eyes stung and he told himself it was the smoke.

Then it was ash. All of it. A soft grey drift in the grate, and one fleck of unburnt seal, dark as an insect, which he crushed under the poker until it was ash too.

There. Done. Safe. The sensible thing, the careful thing, fifteen years of careful living vindicated in a handful of cinders.

He washed his face. He went back to the luck-knot. He worked three turns and botched the fourth, which had never happened, not once in fifteen years of selling trinkets to people who never knew what they were really buying, and he stared at the tangled cord and understood that something had torn open in him and was not going to close again just because he wished it shut.

Guilt, he had learned, was not like grief. Grief came like weather and passed. Guilt was a scar that lay quiet for years and then tore open along the old seam, bleeding fresh, whenever the scar tissue got pulled. And Edmund's letter had pulled it wide. *I remember your face better than my mother's now.* Fifteen years of solitary nights compressed into one line, offered without accusation, which was infinitely worse than accusation. Accusation he could have fought. Forgiveness just sat there in the ashes, patient, asking nothing, and it was unbearable.

* * *

He lasted until dark.

He tried to work. He sold two fever-charms to a laundress with a sick daughter and lied to her beautifully, told her to steep the charm in hot water and drink it, knowing full well the fever-bane woven inside would do more than any apothecary's syrup — small magics, hidden magics, the coward's charity. He ate half a loaf and couldn't taste it. He lay on his cot and listened to the city settle around him, the creak and murmur of Vell at night, and above the skylight the sky showed through, and the stars over the northern quarter — the quarter that faced the border, though most people had forgotten there was a border at all — were dim. Dimmer than last month. He was almost sure of it.

One of them, low near the rooftops, guttered while he watched. Flickered like a candle in a draft. Held. Flickered again.

*Things get through that shouldn't. Small things yet.*

He got up. He knelt at the hearth. He told himself he was only checking that the fire was properly dead, that embers in a boarding house killed people, that Mrs. Fenwick would have his head. This was true and also completely beside the point, and he knew it, and he knelt there anyway with the poker in his hand.

The ashes were cold. He sifted them gently, the way Aldith had taught him to sift river sand for iron — patience, patience, the craft lives in the fingers — and there, near the back of the grate, he found it: a scrap of paper the size of his thumbnail, curled at the edges, scorched brown but not consumed. It had sheltered in the angle of the grate-bar, tucked where the draft couldn't reach, and whether that was chance or whether the letter itself had wanted to survive, he couldn't say. With the old craft you learned early that some things didn't burn clean.

Four words remained, in Edmund's hard lean hand, from somewhere deep in the middle of the second page:

***the stars are going***

Oscar sat back on his heels in the cold hearth-light with the scrap trembling between finger and thumb.

Above the skylight, over the northern quarter of Vell, another star flickered — and this time it did not come back.

He closed his fist around the fragment of ash and letter, and it bit his palm like a burr, and he thought: *I burned it once. I can't burn it twice. There isn't enough of it left to burn, and there was never enough of me that could.*

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow he would decide like a rational man, weighing safety against duty, weighing fifteen years of survival against one dying friend's plea. Tomorrow he would weigh it.

But even as he thought it, he was already reaching under the floorboard for the go-bag he had kept packed since his first week in Vell — the hedge-witch's superstition, never unpacked, never quite believed in — and his fingers found the worn leather strap and closed around it, and somewhere far north, past the dimmed stars and the forgotten border, he would have sworn he felt a tree exhale.
