## 6. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The hedgeroad let him go at dawn, the way a hand opens.

One moment Oscar was walking between walls of blackberry and elder that whispered his mother's name, and the next he stood in open country under a sky scrubbed pale and starless, and the hedges behind him were only hedges — thorn and leaf and bird-noise, dumb as any hedge in any kingdom. He felt the silence of them like a door closed in his face. Three days they had carried him. Three days of fragments: Aldith's hands crushing elderflower into a kettle, the smell of beeswax and blood, Edmund at nine years old holding a lantern steady while the grown-ups shouted. The hedges had given him these things grudgingly, the way an old woman gives up coins she was saving for her own funeral.

Now there was nothing between him and home but four miles of frost-killed barley and a road with no memory in it at all.

He walked because stopping would have meant thinking, and thinking would have meant turning around. The road ran straight as a scar through fields that should have been golden with late harvest and were instead gray to the horizon, stalks collapsed in on themselves like men who had forgotten how to stand. No crows. He noticed that before he noticed anything else. Four miles of farmland and not one crow, not one starling, not even the small brown birds that get into everything. Whatever had gone out of the sky when the wards fell had gone out of the ground too.

*You could still turn back,* said the part of him that had kept him alive fifteen years in Vell. *Crane is behind you. The Wardens will have your description by now. A city is easy to vanish in. What is this?*

This, he told it, was a debt.

The village appeared out of the gray the way villages do — a chimney first, then a roofline, then a scatter of roofs — and Oscar stopped on the last rise and looked down at it and felt nothing, which frightened him more than grief would have.

It was smaller. That was the first thing. Fifteen years will shrink any place you loved, he knew that; Vell itself had shrunk when he went back to the street where he'd lodged his first winter. But this was different. Houses were *gone*. Not ruined — gone. Where the tannery had stood, with its stink he'd hated and its warmth he'd secretly loved on cold mornings, there was a bare rectangle of paler earth, as if someone had lifted the building away like a piece from a board game. The well at the crossroads was a ring of stones with nothing inside it, no rope, no bucket, no dark. And the church — Mother Aldith had been no friend of the church, but it had been there, squat and slate-roofed, its bell cracked from the war —

There was no church.

Oscar stood very still on the rise and made himself count what remained. Mill. Smithy, cold. Eleven houses where thirty had been. The long common wall along the north side, built after the war, that every child learned the names of the dead carved into — blank stone, uncarved, unweathered, as if it had been raised yesterday for no reason anyone remembered.

Because no one had.

He understood it then, all at once, the way you understand a fever has broken — not through thought but through the body. The village hadn't lost its memories. They had been *taken*, steadily, year upon year, spoonfuls ladled out of every skull in every house, and what remained was a place that no longer knew why it existed. The people down there plowed fields their grandparents had died defending and did not know their grandparents had died. They walked past the blank wall and felt vaguely that something was owed to it and did not know to whom.

And the village's own name — he reached for it and found his own mind smooth as river stone. He had lived here fourteen years and he could not recall what the village was called. He tried the road-sign in his memory and found the signpost blank too, wood worn smooth by weather and by nothing else.

"They'll have called it something," he said aloud, to hear a voice in all that silence. "People always call places something."

*Crossroads,* suggested nothing in particular. *Or the Barley. Or Nothing-at-all.*

His legs carried him down the hill while his mind lagged behind, still counting.

* * *

The first person he met was a woman drawing water — no. He corrected himself. A woman standing beside the empty well-ring, looking at it with the expression of someone who has forgotten why she came into a room. She was perhaps sixty, dressed in good wool gone thin at the elbows, and when she saw Oscar she smiled the way country people smile at strangers: openly, and with a flicker underneath that measured how far he might be from help if he turned out to be trouble.

"Morning," she said. "You've come far?"

"Far enough." He made his voice easy, city-flat, the voice he'd worn for fifteen years like a borrowed coat. "I'm looking for someone. Hale. Edmund Hale."

The smile stayed exactly where it was, which was wrong. Faces move. Hers didn't.

"Hale," she repeated, tasting the word. "That'll be the keeper's boy. Man now." She pointed with her chin toward the far end of the village, where a low house stood apart from the others under a single enormous yew — the only tree Oscar had seen since the hedges let him go. "Keeps to himself. Tends the old tree up past the mill."

"The old tree," Oscar said carefully.

"Thorn-tree." She frowned, and the frown had the quality of a person reaching for a thing on a high shelf. "Don't know why we say old. It's not old. Or it is. There was something about it." Her hand rose halfway to her temple and dropped. "My mother used to take flowers up there. I remember the walking. Can't remember the why."

"What was the village called?" he asked. "Before."

She looked at him with real puzzlement then, gentle and total. "Called? It's called home, isn't it. Called what it's called." She laughed, a small embarrassed sound, and he understood that to her his question sounded like asking what her own hands were called. "You're a strange one. You'll want bread, I expect. Everyone wants bread after the road."

"I—" His throat closed. Fifteen years of practice at lying, and the lie that came out was: "Yes. Thank you."

She gave him half a loaf wrapped in a cloth and a cup of milk and asked him nothing else, and that was the worst of it — the hospitality without curiosity, kindness without roots, a whole village of people who had been hollowed so gently they didn't rattle. While he ate she talked about the harvest, which had failed three years running, though she couldn't say why; about her son, who had gone to the city and written once and then never again, though she couldn't quite hold on to his face; about the stars, which had gone out a week ago, though everyone agreed that was probably nothing, probably weather, because what else was there to agree it was?

"Do you remember a war?" Oscar asked quietly.

She paused with the milk jug in her hand.

"There's a word," she said slowly. "I can nearly say it. It starts in the chest." She pressed her fist below her collarbone. "It lives here. But it hasn't got any letters left. You know how it is when a fire's burned down and there's a shape in the coals that was a log?"

"I know how it is," Oscar said.

"That's the war. A shape in the coals." She set the jug down. "We don't speak of it. We couldn't if we wanted to."

He looked at her — at the shape of her, the set of her shoulders, and something moved in him sideways, unwelcome. "Did you know a boy called Oscar? He'd have lived here. Apprentice to the herb-woman, Mother Aldith."

Her brow furrowed with honest effort. He watched her reach and find nothing, watch the nothing close over like water.

"Aldith," she said. "That's a name that makes my teeth ache. Was she real? The children used to dare each other to run past her hedge. Or — no. That's not right either. That's a story someone told me." She shook her head, smiling apologetically. "I'm sorry. I'm no use at all. We're none of us much use, when it comes to before. Old Tam up the lane keeps trying — he writes lists of things he thinks he ought to know, and the list gets shorter every year. He says the paper eats them. I think that's kind of him, finding somewhere to put it."

Oscar's hands had gone cold around the bread.

"And me?" he said. "Do you remember me? Just now, just today — do you think you'll remember me tomorrow?"

"Oh, aye," she said comfortably. "Till supper, likely."

* * *

He went up past the mill because there was nowhere else his feet would agree to go.

The path climbed through a stand of ash trees — living ash, he noted, the bark unmarked — and came out onto a low hilltop where the wind moved freely, and there was the thorn-tree.

Even diminished, even seen across fifteen years and a childhood of taking it for granted, it stopped his breath. It filled the hilltop the way a cathedral fills a square. Black branches spread wider than the mill was long, dense with thorns longer than his forearm, and though the season was turning and every other tree wore yellow, the thorn was bare — bare and black and, he realized as he came closer, *wrong* in a way that made his apprentice's training sit up and howl. There was no green anywhere in it. No lichen. No moss. Birds' nests sat in its forks like abandoned boats, old, crumbling, none recent. The grass beneath it grew in a perfect circle and stopped dead at the drip line as though mown by an invisible scythe.

And the trunk — the trunk was scarred. Dozens of scars, deliberate, surgical, each one sealed with a knot of dried sap the color of amber and old wine. He knew those scars. Every hedge-witch knew them. They were tapping-scars, harvest-scars, the marks of sap taken — except no one took sap from a ward-tree. Sap was the tree's blood and the tree's blood was the kingdom's memory and you did not *bleed* the border, you fed it.

Someone had been bleeding it. For years. For years and years, by the number of the scars, and lately often, by how fresh the newest ones were — one still tacky, glistening, days old at most.

At the base of the trunk, arranged with a care that hurt to look at, lay offerings. A child's shoe. A ribbon gone gray. A clay figure of a horse, thumb-pressed, fired crooked in some kitchen oven. Wildflowers, weeks dead, replaced so many times the stems beneath them had composted into a dark mat.

Edmund had been feeding it the only way left to a man with no craft: with meaning. With grief and tenderness and regularity, year after year, keeping faith with a tree that ate memories, hoping love might substitute for magic.

Oscar knelt and touched the nearest scar and the tree shuddered.

He felt it through his palm and up his arm — a vast slow flinch, the motion of something enormous trying to wake and failing. And in the flinch he heard, or felt, or was shown, one image: a lantern held steady in a nine-year-old's hand. Then darkness again, but a listening darkness, the darkness of a thing gathering the last of itself to pay attention.

"You're still in there," Oscar whispered. "Gods help us both, you're still in there, and he's been bleeding himself into you to keep you breathing."

The tree said nothing. Trees do. But the wind moved through its black branches and made a sound he remembered from his childhood and had never once thought of in fifteen years, and hearing it now he had to sit down in the dead grass and put his head in his hands.

When he looked up, the sun was going down behind the mill, and lights had come on in the village below — eleven houses, eleven hearths, a handful of flames against all that forgetting — and one light apart from the rest, under the yew, in the window of a low house whose door he could see from here, whose door he had been avoiding looking at all day.

Time to stop avoiding it.

* * *

Edmund's house sat where it had always sat, and it was the only thing in the village that had not been eaten.

Oscar saw it plainly now as he crossed the frozen garden patch. The house itself was ordinary — stone, thatch, a door — but it *held*. Other buildings in the village had that lifted-away, board-game-piece wrongness, edges blurred like wet ink. This house had edges. The thatch was old and mossy and entirely itself. The stones were worn and entirely themselves. Even the weeds in the dooryard grew with an insolent specificity, as if they knew their own names.

Aldith's work. It had to be. Before the end, whatever the end had been, she had thrown a ward around this one house the way a drowning mother throws a child from a burning boat — not to save it from fire, but to save it from the water.

And the door.

The door was oak, iron-banded, older than the house around it, and it had no handle. It never had — he remembered that suddenly, remembered being seven and furious that he had to knock at Edmund's door like a beggar while other houses let you just walk in. You knocked, and the door decided.

Oscar stood before it in the last blue light with the stars absent overhead and his heart going like a trapped bird, and raised his knuckles, and knocked.

Nothing.

He knocked again. From inside, faintly, came a sound — a cough, deep and wet and terrible, and then a voice, thinner than he remembered, rougher, but shaped the same:

"It doesn't answer strangers. Go round—"

"Edmund." The name came out of him broken in the middle. "Edmund, it's—"

And the door *moved*.

Not opened. Moved — leaned toward him by the width of a finger, the way a horse leans toward a familiar hand, and under his palm the oak grew warm as skin, and iron bands that had been cold a moment ago held heat like a body's. He snatched his hand back. The wood grain shifted under his eyes, slowly, like clouds, and rearranged itself into letters. Old letters. Deep ones, cut long ago and healed over, legible only because the door wanted them legible:

**OSCAR.**

His true name. Not Marchetti, not the three false names he'd worn in Vell, not the street-name the charm-buyers knew. The name Mother Aldith had given him at his binding, the name that lived under his tongue and in the root of him, the name he had not spoken aloud in fifteen years because speaking it felt like handing a stranger a knife.

The door knew it. Had always known it. Had been waiting with it, all this time, the way the hedges had waited, the way the tree waited — everything in this emptied country holding one small true thing in trust against the day he came back, and the day had come, and he was standing in front of a door with his own name written on it in wood-grain and he could not make himself push.

From inside, the cough again. Then footsteps, uneven, and a shadow across the gap under the door, blocking the lamplight.

The latch clicked — no handle, but a latch, answering to the door's judgment — and the door swung inward, and lamplight spilled out over the frozen weeds, and Edmund Hale stood in it.

He was thirty and looked fifty. Gaunt, gray-skinned, wrapped in a blanket against a cold no blanket could answer, one hand braced on the doorframe, the other stained to the wrist with dried sap. His hair had gone white at the temples. His eyes were the same eyes. That was unbearable — fifteen years, and the eyes unchanged, still the color of rainwater over river stones, still looking at Oscar with an expression that had no anger in it at all, only a relief so vast and so terrible that it was worse than hatred would have been.

"Well," Edmund said. His voice caught; he mastered it. "The door finally let you in."

"Edmund—"

"Took you long enough." And Edmund Hale smiled — a real smile, cracked and crooked and fifteen years overdue — and stepped back from his doorway, and coughed into the sap-stained cloth until Oscar feared he'd fold, and said, when he could speak again:

"Come in, Oscar. There's things you need to know, and I haven't got the strength to tell them twice, and less time than that."
