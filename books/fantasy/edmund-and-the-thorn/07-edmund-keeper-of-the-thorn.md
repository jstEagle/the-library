## 7. Edmund, Keeper of the Thorn

The blood came up in the grey of morning, as it always did now.

Edmund sat on the doorstep of his own house with a rag pressed to his mouth and waited for the coughing to finish, the way you wait for weather. When it was done he looked at what the rag held — dark as elderberry wine, darker than that — and folded it small so the tree wouldn't see. The thorn worried about him. That was the trouble with feeding a thing your memories: it learned your habits, and then it fretted over them like an old wife.

"Don't," he told the tree anyway, because it had begun to lean. "I'm fine. Eat your breakfast."

The great thorn stood at the crown of the hill behind the village, black-barked and vast, its branches spread like a hand trying to hold down the sky. In spring it had flowered once — white, sweet-smelling, loud with bees — but there was no spring in it anymore. Fifteen years of winters had gone into those roots. Edmund could name every one of them. He had poured them in himself, cup by cup, the way Aldith had taught him before she died with her hands in the soil and her eyes still open.

He climbed the hill on legs that shook, and knelt at the tree's root, and laid his palm against the bark.

*Give me something,* the tree said, which is not how trees talk, but this one had been drinking human memory for three hundred years and had learned to speak in whatever language the keeper carried. It said it in his mother's voice, because that was the coin it liked best. *Just one. Just the small one.*

"No." His voice came out rusted. "We've been through this."

*You are dying.*

"I'm aware."

*Then why hoard? A man drowning does not keep his pockets full.*

"Because they're not mine to spend carelessly." He pressed his forehead to the bark. The thorn was warm today — warmer than the air, warmer than him — which meant it was hungry, which meant the border behind it was thinning again. Somewhere north of here, past the fields nobody plowed anymore, the old wards ran along the roots like veins under skin, and where the roots went pale, the world went thin, and things that had been forgotten on purpose began to remember themselves back into being.

He gave it the morning instead. That was the ritual: he sat beneath the branches and thought, deliberately, hard, about ordinary things — the smell of bread, the weight of a scythe, the sound of rain starting on a slate roof — and let the tree sip them off the top of his mind without taking anything whole. It was like skimming cream. It kept the tree alive the way a crust keeps a beggar alive, and it cost Edmund almost nothing except the slow erosion of everything else.

Almost nothing. But not nothing. He could no longer quite recall the color of the door of his childhood room. He knew he'd had a childhood room; the shape of knowing remained, like a footprint after the foot has gone. He'd fed that door to the tree four years ago, a bad night, frost killing the late lambs, the wards flickering like a guttered lamp. He remembered giving it away. He could not remember the thing itself. That was the arithmetic of it, always: the loss stayed even after the memory left, a hollow tooth where a tooth used to be.

He was doing the morning's tending when the first of them crossed.

It came out of the north field at a loping, wrong-jointed gait, a thing shaped roughly like a dog if a dog were made of other people's leavings — a scrap of someone's wedding day, a splinter of a funeral, the taste of a meal some child had refused. Discarded memories, walking. They did that now, more and more, since the stars had gone out. The village had stopped asking what they were. The village, mostly, no longer remembered enough to be frightened properly; they took their pitchforks to the things and burned them like rubbish, and felt only a vague unease afterward, like a word forgotten on the tip of the tongue.

Edmund watched it come and made the sign Aldith had taught him, two fingers crossed over the heart, and spoke her binding-word — or tried to. The word came up empty. He'd fed that too. He fed it another memory instead, quick and cheap, the memory of a girl named Tansy who had kissed him at fourteen behind the tithe barn — and the thing at the edge of the roots flinched, lost its shape, and crumbled into the grass like ash off a log.

"Sorry, Tansy," he said to nobody. "Wherever you are now."

That was the day's accounting done, and it wasn't yet noon. He sat down under the tree with his back against the trunk and coughed into the rag until the world went white at the edges, and when it cleared, the light through the branches had gone long and gold, and a man was standing at the bottom of the hill looking up at him.

* * *

Edmund knew him at once, and that surprised him, because he had expected not to.

Fifteen years is a long time for a face. But memory-magic runs strange: the tree held everything Edmund had ever fed it, and though he had never fed it Oscar Marchetti — had guarded that name like a coal cupped in bare hands through fifteen years of Warden interrogations — the tree had seen the boy in Edmund's dreams, in the dreams it drank from him every night, and so the tree knew Oscar, and so, in the way of such things, Edmund knew him too, instantly, whole, the way you know your own hand.

Oscar Marchetti. Older. Thinner. Dressed in city clothes gone shabby, with the wary hunch of a man who has spent years making himself small. His hair had gone from brown to dark iron. There was a charm-seller's satchel slung across his chest, and Edmund nearly laughed at that alone — the hedge-witch's best apprentice, selling fever-knots to godless Vellites like a peddler of ribbons.

But it was the eyes that undid him. They were the same eyes. Frightened and clever and kind underneath it all, the eyes that had looked at Edmund across Mother Aldith's kitchen table a thousand mornings, and then, one October dawn, had looked at him once more across an open window, and then had looked at him no more for fifteen years.

Edmund laughed. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him rough and cracked from disuse, and halfway through it broke into weeping, and the two sounds tangled together in his ruined chest until he was coughing again, bent double against the roots, laughing and crying and bleeding all at once, and through it all he heard footsteps coming up the hill, hesitant, stopping, starting again.

"You look terrible," Edmund managed, when he could speak.

Oscar stood above him, ten feet off, hands half-raised as if to help and afraid to touch. "So do you."

"Aye, well." Edmund wiped his mouth with the rag, and saw Oscar see the blood, and saw him understand. "Fifteen years will do that. So will the other thing." He patted the ground beside him. "Sit down, Osc. You're letting all the cold in."

Oscar flinched at the name. Of course he would. Nobody living called him that; in Vell he was somebody else entirely, some false-named nobody, and hearing his true name spoken aloud on this hill — where names had weight, where the ground itself remembered — must have hit him like cold water. He sat. Slowly, carefully, the way you sit beside a sick animal or a lit fuse.

They said nothing for a while. The wind moved in the thorn's high branches, and far off in the village someone's dog barked twice and gave up on it.

"You got my letter," Edmund said.

"Burned it." Oscar's voice was flat. Then, quieter: "Dug the ashes out of the grate. Read what was left."

"That's very you."

"Don't." Oscar turned on him, and there it was at last, the anger Edmund had been braced for these fifteen years — but it collapsed even as it rose, folding inward like a wing, and what was under it was worse than anger. It was shame. "Don't be kind to me. I've rehearsed this, Edmund. On the road, every mile, I rehearsed what I'd say. You have every right to —" His voice failed and restarted. "Aldith died holding the wards and I was in Vell selling luck-knots. And you — you were *here*. You were *fifteen*."

"Sixteen in the spring."

"*Sixteen.*"

"And you were seventeen and terrified, and she sent you away on purpose, and you've never worked that out, have you?" Edmund said it gently, watching it land. "She made you go, Osc. She chose me because I was the one who couldn't leave — sickly, slow, no gift to speak of. She chose you to live. She spent her last hour making sure you'd run, so you'd hate her instead of staying to die beside us. And you did exactly what she built you to do." He shrugged. "So there's no debt. I checked. Every year on the anniversary I checked, and there's no debt."

Oscar stared at him. His jaw worked. "You're lying," he said finally, without conviction. "To make it easier."

"I'm dying, Osc. Lying's expensive now. I can't afford it."

Something in Oscar broke then, quietly, the way ice breaks on a pond in thaw — no drama, just a soft structural surrender — and he put his head in his hands, and his shoulders shook, and Edmund, who had imagined this moment in ten thousand lonely variations, most of them angry, most of them ending in slammed doors or drawn blades or the Wardens dragging them both away, found that none of the rehearsals mattered at all. He simply reached over and put his hand on the back of his friend's neck, the way you steady a sparrow, and Oscar leaned into it like a wall giving way.

"You kept my name," Oscar said into his hands. "When the Wardens came. Crane told me — she asked me, years ago, whether anyone in Thornmarch —" he stopped. "She said a boy had been questioned. Refused to answer. She said he smiled the whole time."

"He was an idiot," Edmund said. "Smiling at Isolde Crane. She could have taken the smile off with pliers." He paused. "It seemed important, at the time. That somebody know who you were. Even if it was only me and her."

"The village doesn't remember you either," Oscar said. "Or the war. Or its own name. I asked at the inn — they looked at me like I was mad. What happened to them?"

"The tree ate it. Not by choice." Edmund tipped his head back against the bark. "Wards need remembering to hold. That's the whole secret of the hedge-craft, the thing the city never understood when it outlawed us. Memory is the mortar. And the founders starved the kingdom of memory to buy their peace, and the wards started failing, and the only food left was..." He spread his hands. "Me."

"How much?" Oscar asked. He was looking at the tree now, really looking, and Edmund saw the apprentice wake in him — the boy who had been better at this craft than Edmund ever was, reading the flows, seeing the pale places in the roots. "How much of yourself is in there?"

Edmund considered lying, decided against it, and did the sum aloud, because saying it plainly was easier than carrying it. "My mother's face. My father's voice. Most of my childhood. Three girls whose names I wrote down so I'd know I'd loved them even though I can't feel it anymore. The taste of honey. How to swim. Which days of the week feel like what." He smiled. "I kept the important ones till last. Kept you till last. Figured I'd eat dessert when there was nothing else on the plate."

"Don't," Oscar whispered. "Don't talk like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's already decided."

"It is decided." Edmund said it kindly, the way Aldith had said hard things, and watched the words go into Oscar like nails. "The wards are down to threads. The stars went out, Osc — you saw it, everyone saw it — and the things are crossing, and every night the tree asks me for more and I've less to give. I'll die inside the month regardless; the cough's in my lungs now, I can hear it. So there's one good use left in me, and it's a big one. One keeper, poured whole, roots to crown — that rekindles the wards for a hundred years. Maybe two." He squeezed Oscar's neck once and let go. "I made my peace with it years ago. You arriving early just means I get to say goodbye properly. That's more than most men get."

Oscar was silent for a long time. When he looked up, his eyes were wet and absolutely steady, and Edmund felt the first thread of dread, because he knew that look. He'd seen it in a mirror once, young, stubborn, before duty had worn his edges smooth.

"No," Oscar said.

"Osc—"

"No. Whatever you've decided, un-decide it. We're going to argue about this. At length. Possibly for days." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, briskly, like a man getting back to work. "First — show me the roots. All of them. And then you're going to tell me exactly what the rite requires, every step, and don't soften it, because I'll know."

Edmund looked at him — this ghost come home out of a burned letter, this coward-turned-fool-turned-something-else — and the terrible relief in his chest turned over and became something heavier and warmer, something almost like hope, which was a dangerous thing to feel on this hill, in this year, under a sky with no stars in it.

"There's something else," Edmund said slowly. "Aldith left books. In the cellar. I've never been able to open the last of them — they're warded, and I haven't the gift. Never had the gift." He met Oscar's eyes. "But you always did."

Below them, in the darkening field, something forgotten dragged itself toward the fence line, and the thorn-tree shivered on its roots, and held.
