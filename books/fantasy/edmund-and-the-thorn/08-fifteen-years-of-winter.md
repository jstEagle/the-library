## 8. Fifteen Years of Winter

The first winter alone, I learned that the thorn-tree drinks in the dark.

I did not know it yet, that first night after Mother Aldith died. I sat with her body until the cold came up through the floorboards and into my knees, and then I went out to the tree because I could not think of anywhere else to go. The stars were out — all of them, back when there were stars — and the tree stood black against them like a wound cut into the sky. Its branches were bare. Its roots ran down past the cellar, past the well, past anything a spade could reach, down into the old stone of the border-wards themselves.

It was shivering.

Trees don't shiver. But this one was, every twig trembling, and when I put my hand against its trunk the bark was cold as a grave-lid, and something reached up through my palm and took hold of a memory before I even understood what was happening. It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like being *relieved* of something — like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying.

The memory it took was small. My sixth birthday, the one where Mother Aldith gave me a whistle carved from elderwood and I lost it in the stream the same afternoon. I stood there under the bare branches and reached for the memory and found only the shape of it, the way you find the dent left in grass by someone who has gone.

And the tree stopped shivering.

I want to say I understood, then, what I was doing. I didn't. I was fourteen years old and I had just buried the only person who knew what I was, and the tree was cold, and giving it my birthday made the cold go away for one more night. That is how it starts. Not with heroism. With one small memory on one cold night, and then another, because the next night is colder.

* * *

Mother Aldith had told me about the wards exactly once, three days before she died, and she told it to me the way she told everything — while her hands were busy, so that her mouth could pretend it wasn't saying anything important.

She was rendering fat for soap. I remember the smell of it, tallow and lye, and her hands moving in the pot, and her voice flat as a ledger.

"The wards aren't stones," she said. "Everyone thinks they're stones, because the founders built their pretty marker-posts along the border and let people believe it. Stones don't need feeding. The wards are a tree. One tree. And a tree is a living thing, boy, and living things get hungry."

"Feed it what?" I asked. I was thirteen. I thought everything had an answer you could write down.

"Memory," she said. "That's what the kingdom was built on, before the war. Memory-magic. Every village kept its remembering-women, every family kept its name-songs, and the wards drank from all of it through roots like these." She stirred the pot. "Then the war came, and the thing beyond the border got hungry, and the founders made a bargain. They fed it the kingdom's stored memories — whole centuries of them, names and songs and faces — and it slept, and they called it victory."

"And the tree?"

"The tree kept what was left. Someone has to tend it. Someone has to feed it by hand now, a little at a time, or it dies, and when it dies—" She stopped stirring. For just a moment her hands went still over the pot, and I saw something in her face I had never seen there before, and it took me years to name it. It was guilt. "When it dies, the bargain comes due. Everything owed gets collected."

"Why us?" I said. "Why does it have to be us?"

She looked at me then, and her eyes were the same grey as the winter sky, and she said: "Because we're the ones who know. Knowing is a burden, Edmund. It doesn't ask if you want it."

Three days later the thing beyond the border pressed against the wards in its sleep — a dream-twitch, Aldith called it afterward, though she never got the chance — and she went out to the tree at midnight and stayed there until dawn, and whatever she poured into it that night emptied her out like a cracked jug. She took to her bed. She held on for six days. On the last day she gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, "Don't tell anyone your true name. Don't tell anyone *his*. Feed it small things. Small things, Edmund. It can live on small things if you're careful," and then she said a word I didn't understand until much later, which was "forgive," and then she was gone.

I thought she meant forgive herself. Or the founders. It took me fifteen years to understand she might have meant something else entirely.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about keeping a secret burden: it makes you cruel in small ways, and kind in large ones, and both feel the same from the inside.

I fed the tree small things, like she said. A smell. A color. The name of a dog I'd owned. I learned its appetites the way you learn a sick animal's — what it could stomach, what it craved, what would make it shake and groan all night if I gave it too rich a meal. It loved warm things best. First kisses. Summer afternoons. I rationed those like a miser, and gave it winters instead, and arguments, and the memory of my father's funeral, which it took so gently I almost forgave it for existing.

And the village forgot, slowly, around me. That was the other half of the arithmetic, the part Aldith hadn't lived long enough to explain. The tree drew from the land as well as from me, thin threads of it, enough to keep itself alive but not enough to stay whole. Names went first. The village's own name went somewhere in my second winter — I woke one morning and asked Goodman Teller what our village was called and he looked at me with a pleasant blank face and said, "Called? It's called home, lad." The war went next. Then the reason the border-posts stood where they stood. People kept their cows and their christenings and their grudges, but the deep shelves of themselves stood empty, dusted and bare, and no one noticed because forgetting, done gently, feels exactly like nothing at all.

I could have stopped it. I knew that even then. If I fed the tree great gulps — if I gave it myself entire, the way Aldith had given herself on her last night — the village would have kept everything else. One keeper's whole life against a village's memory. It seemed a fair trade. I stood in the cellar with Aldith's journals in my hands many nights, reading her notes on the rite, telling myself *tonight, tonight.*

But I was a coward about it, and I'll say so plainly, because Oscar will read this someday if the tree lets anything survive, and he should know I was a coward. I wanted to live. Even a diminished life, even a life spent alone in a house full of other people's forgotten things — I wanted it. So I paid in installments. Small things. Winters. And the village grew a little barer every year, and the tree held, and I told myself that was stewardship instead of what it was, which was stinginess.

* * *

The Wardens came in my fourth year.

Two of them, grey-cloaked, riding up the hedgeroad on horses that didn't snort or stamp — Wardens' horses are like that, quiet as furniture. The tall one was called Crane; not Isolde, some other Crane, a cousin or an uncle, the family seems to grow them like nettles. He had a ledger under his arm and a way of looking at you as though you were a page he'd already read.

They came because the tree had flared. I'd fed it badly that autumn — a whole summer, greedy, because I'd been lonely and the tree takes loneliness beautifully, wraps it up and hums — and the flare had run down the roots and lit the old ward-stones green for a mile along the border, and someone in Vell had seen it, and Vell did not like being reminded that magic existed.

They knocked. I made them tea, because my mother taught me manners before she died and they've proven harder to kill than she was.

"You're the hedge-witch's boy," Crane said. Not a question. "Aldith. Dead four years now."

"Dead five," I said. "But yes."

"There was an apprentice before you." He opened the ledger. My heart went very still and very loud at the same time, which I didn't know was possible. "Name unrecorded. Taken in roughly fifteen years ago, aged eleven or twelve. Left the village at nineteen. We have reason to believe the resonance in your tree this autumn matches a signature we have on file from that apprentice's childhood work."

The tea steamed between us. Outside the window, the thorn-tree stood in the grey light, bare and patient, holding everything I'd ever given it — including, folded away somewhere in its rings, a boy named Oscar Marchetti, age eleven, laughing at something I'd said, which was the first memory I'd ever fed it and the one I guarded most jealously and would have fed it last.

"I wouldn't know," I said. "There was never anyone before me. Mother Aldith trained me from the cradle. Ask anyone."

Crane looked at me for a long time. Wardens have a craft for pulling truth out of people — I'd seen Aldith describe it in her journals, a compulsion woven from the Unmaking itself, the anti-magic, the thing the city built to unbuild — and I watched him decide whether to use it. His hand moved toward his cloak. Stopped.

"Ask anyone," he repeated softly. "Anyone in this village. Except that no one in this village remembers there was ever an apprentice before you, do they. Because you've been feeding this tree the village's memory of him for four years."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a rope.

He closed the ledger. "You understand what I could do," he said. "You understand what happens to unlicensed practitioners. To their families. To the villages that shelter them."

"My mother's dead. My father's dead. The village can't remember me long enough to shelter me." I heard my own voice come out flat and steady and marveled at it. "So there's nothing you can burn here, Warden, except the tree. And if you burn the tree, the border opens, and everything the founders bought with their bargain comes due, and you can explain that to your masters in Vell. Can you? Do they even know? Does anyone down there know what the wards actually are?"

Something crossed his face then — quick, quickly mastered — and I have thought about that expression for eleven years, because it wasn't anger. It was doubt. Just a flicker, a hairline crack in fired clay. He believed in the Unmaking, that man, believed in it the way farmers believe in rain. And for one moment my question had gotten under the belief and made it itch.

"We'll be watching the tree," he said, standing. "If it flares again—"

"It won't," I said. "I'm careful."

At the door he paused. He didn't turn around. "The apprentice," he said. "Oscar Marchetti. If he ever comes back here — and runaways sometimes do, when they're dying or destitute — you should know that there is a standing order on his name. Fifteen years old, that order. Older than his disappearance. Someone in Vell wanted him found before he ever left."

The door closed behind them. I stood in my kitchen with the tea going cold and understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand two things in the space of a single heartbeat and neither has room to breathe.

The first was that Oscar hadn't simply run away. Someone had been hunting his name since before he fled — and he must have known it, or sensed it, or been told in whatever way a boy tells himself the truth he can't bear. He hadn't abandoned me. He'd led the hunt away from me. Fifteen years of exile, fifteen years of a false name in a godless city, and I had spent four of those years quietly hating him for it.

The second thing was that I had lied to a Warden's face and he had let me walk away, and I did not find out until much later why — whether it was the crack of doubt, or something in the ledger, or simply a tired man choosing mercy he couldn't justify. But he had marked the village, and marked me, and marked the tree, and the watching never fully stopped after that. Grey riders on the hedgeroad twice a year, every year, checking that the wound in the world stayed stitched.

* * *

After that I stopped counting winters and started counting what the winters cost.

Year seven: I gave the tree my mother's voice. I had been hoarding it — the sound of her singing at the washboard, three bars of a song whose name the village had already forgotten — and one February night the tree shook so hard it threw ice off its branches like glass breaking, and I walked out barefoot in the snow and gave it up, and stood there weeping while it settled, and could not afterward remember whether she sang high or low. Only that she sang. Only that it mattered.

Year nine: the tree took my sense of smell for a season, without asking, because I'd been late with the feeding and it grew impatient. I got it back in spring, mostly. Roses still smell like nothing to me. I've made my peace with roses.

Year eleven: I tried to teach the village children, quietly, the old name-songs, to seed new memory into the ground the tree could draw from honestly instead of eating what was already planted. It worked for a summer. Then a grey rider came through, and asked questions at the inn, and by autumn the mothers were keeping their children indoors when I passed. Fear travels faster than forgetting. I wrote in Aldith's journal that night — I keep her journal, I add to it, someone should — *"Alone is not a strategy. Alone is just a slower way of losing."*

And every year, on the longest night, I went to the cellar and read the page in her journals about the rite. The full rite. The one where a keeper gives everything — memories, self, name, the whole continuous thread of a life — and the tree drinks it and the wards blaze bright for a generation and the village keeps everything it has left. One life, fairly spent. I read it every year and I closed the book every year, and every year the excuse was smaller and thinner and easier to see through. This year the village needs me to plan. This year the tree is unstable. This year, this year, this year.

Fifteen years of winter. That's what I called them, privately, the way you name a disease. Fifteen years of feeding a tree in the dark so that a city full of people who outlawed magic could go on not knowing what their peace was made of. Fifteen years of watching the stars through the branches and wondering which ones the founders had already sold. Fifteen years of a name I never spoke — *Oscar, Oscar* — kept alive the only place I could keep it, in the rings of a hungry tree, because saying it aloud felt like spending it.

I hated him for the first four years. I grieved him for the next five. By year ten I had graduated to something worse than either, which was hope — the dull, stubborn, humiliating hope that he was alive out there somewhere, that the false name fit him loosely enough to breathe in, that someday the hedgeroad would bring him back and I would get to be angry at him properly, to his face, with tea.

I practiced the conversation, some nights. In the kitchen. Out loud, like a madman. *You could have written. You could have come back. You could have taken half of this.* And then, because I always played both parts, his voice answering: *I did write. You burned it. You always burn them.*

Which was true. Which was the worst part. Letters came, maybe once every two or three years, unsigned, sealed with plain wax, postmarked from cities I'd only read about. I burned every one unread, because a letter was evidence, and evidence was a rope, and the Wardens watched the hedgeroad. That's what I told myself, anyway. The truth underneath was smaller and meaner: I was angry, and burning them was the only punishment I could reach.

Last month a letter came sealed with wax the color of thorn-sap, and my hands shook so badly I dropped it twice, and I knelt right there in the yard and read it in the snow.

*Edmund. I know what you've been carrying. I know whose shoulders I left it on. I'm coming home.*

Home. He'd written *home*, as if the word still applied, as if fifteen years hadn't rotted the floorboards out from under it. I read it forty times. I burned it, because I always burn them, and then I dug the ashes out of the grate with my bare hands and kept them in a jar on the mantel like a relic, which is the most honest portrait of me that exists: a man who burns his letters and worships the ashes.

Now the stars have gone out — all of them, at once, with a sound like the sky cracking a knuckle — and the tree is shaking worse than it has ever shaken, and my chest is full of blood that comes up pink in the basin every morning, and I am standing at the window watching the hedgeroad through the dark, waiting.

Because I know that walk. Fifteen years, and I know it still — the too-long stride, the shoulders hunched like a man walking into weather, the way he looks at the ground ahead of him as if the ground owes him money. The stars are dead overhead and the border is failing and my lungs are filling, and none of it matters, none of it, because there is a figure on the hedgeroad, and it has his walk, and it is coming home.

I put the kettle on. Some things you do even at the end of the world.

And then I open the door before he knocks, and I laugh, and I weep, and I say the name I haven't spoken in fifteen years, out loud, at last, spending it all in one breath:

"Hello, Oscar."
