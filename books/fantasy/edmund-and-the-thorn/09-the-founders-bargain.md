## 9. The Founders' Bargain

The door to the cellar was behind the hearth.

I should have known. Mother Aldith had never trusted walls that stood away from fire; she said a hearth was the one place in a house where a lie could not keep warm. Edmund knelt before the cold ashes with a lantern held low, and I watched him press his palm flat against the soot-blackened stone until his fingers found the seam — a hairline crack shaped, when you knew to look for it, like a thorn.

"It only opens for blood of the house," he said. His voice was rough from coughing, and he did not look at me. "Or for someone the house remembers."

"And it remembers me?"

"It remembered you the moment you came up the lane." He drew a small knife from his belt — my old knife, I realized, the one I'd left on the workbench fifteen years ago, its handle worn to the shape of another boy's hand — and opened the pad of his thumb without ceremony. He pressed it to the stone and spoke three words in the hedge-tongue, words I hadn't heard since Aldith died, and the hearth swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, because sound, Aldith used to say, was just memory leaving too fast.

Cold air rose out of the dark beneath the house. It smelled of tallow, dust, and something older underneath — the smell of a place where time had been told to sit still and wait.

"Thirteen steps down," Edmund said. "Mind the seventh. She never fixed it because she liked knowing who was paying attention."

* * *

The cellar was not a cellar. That was my first thought, standing on the seventh step with my heart going like a trapped bird. Cellars are for turnips and cider and the small dark economies of a household. This was a library that had been buried alive.

Shelves lined every wall, cut into the earth itself, and on them sat journals — hundreds of them, thousands, bound in everything from calfskin to birchbark to what looked suspiciously like pressed nettle. They were arranged not by date or subject but by *weight*, I realized, the way Aldith had arranged her apothecary: the dangerous things at the bottom, where they could be reached quickly if they needed burning.

Lantern-light moved across the spines as Edmund descended behind me. He was breathing hard now. I could hear the wet rattle in it that I had been pretending not to hear since the moment I saw him under the tree.

"She wrote all her life," he said. "You knew that."

"I knew she kept accounts." I touched a spine gently, as if it might wake. "Ledgers. Herb-rotations. Which cow sulked in which month."

"She kept those too. Upstairs, in the kitchen, where anyone could read them." He lifted the lantern higher, and the light fell across a shelf near the back where the bindings changed — finer, older, sealed with drips of green wax that I recognized with a lurch: thorn-sap, the same seal that had held the letter that brought me home. "These were the ones she said would get us killed. She meant it as praise."

"Why show me now?"

Edmund set the lantern on a shelf and turned to face me, and in the small gold light his face looked carved out of patience and exhaustion in equal measure.

"Because you asked me last night why the wards failed," he said. "And I told you they didn't fail. And you looked at me the way you used to look at Aldith when she gave you an answer that was true but not finished. So." He gestured at the shelves, at the whole buried kingdom of paper. "Finish it yourself. I've read these so many times I can't see them anymore. I need eyes that still know how to be angry."

* * *

We worked through the night.

It was not reading, exactly, not at first. It was archaeology. The earliest journals were Aldith's teacher's — a name scratched out so thoroughly the page had nearly torn, though whoever had done the scratching had missed one word in the margin of the first volume: *Vell*. The city's name, written in a hand that shook.

I read while Edmund slept in fits against the far wall, propped on a sack of what turned out to be dried yarrow, waking every hour or so to cough and to check that I hadn't stopped existing. Between his wakings, the story assembled itself out of fragments, the way a hedge assembles itself out of individual leaves into one long green argument about borders.

The war had not ended. That was the first thing. Every history I had ever been taught in Vell — the few they allowed the poor, chalked on slate in rooms that smelled of lamp-oil and obedience — spoke of the Last War as something concluded. Won, even, in the vague way the city won everything: by being elsewhere when the dying happened.

But Aldith's teacher wrote of a war that was simply *relocated*. Of a thing beyond the northern border that the old kings had held back not with walls but with remembering — the kingdom's whole craft of memory-magic, generations of stored lives poured into living wards, hedges and thorns and stones that *knew*, and knowing, refused passage. The border was not a line on a map. It was a mind. A very large, very patient mind made of everyone who had ever given their memories to keep it.

And then the founders of Vell had come to that mind with an offer.

I found the account in a journal bound in grey leather, its wax seal broken long ago and resealed many times after, each new seal a little more reluctant than the last. Aldith's handwriting here was young, furious, slanting downhill as though the words themselves wanted to escape the page.

*They did not defeat it,* she had written. *They fed it.*

*The city was starving. Not for bread — for time. The war had eaten thirty years of harvests and sons, and the founders' council met in the winter of the Breaking and decided that victory was a commodity like any other. The wards were full. The wards were, in fact, the richest thing left in the kingdom: centuries of memory, carefully banked, the whole inheritance of the realm stored in living wood. And there was a hunger beyond the border that had learned, over the long years of the war, to love the taste of us.*

*So they opened the gates of the wards themselves. They called it "strategic redistribution." They called it "peace in our time." They took the kingdom's memory — our grandmothers' faces, our songs, the names of our dead, the reasons we planted what we planted and mourned how we mourned — and they poured it out across the border like slops from a kitchen, and the thing beyond drank, and slept, and the war stopped.*

*They called it victory. There are statues.*

I put the journal down. My hands were shaking, and I noticed distantly that this annoyed me, the way it annoys a soldier to bleed during a battle he hasn't finished fighting.

Edmund woke at the sound of the book hitting the shelf. "You found the grey one."

"You knew." It wasn't a question, and I hated how much it sounded like an accusation anyway. "You've known all along."

"I've known since I was nineteen years old and coughing up the first blood." He pushed himself upright, slowly, the way he seemed to do everything now — as if his body were a letter he was very carefully not crumpling. "Aldith showed me the year before she died. She said someone in this village ought to carry the truth, since the truth couldn't carry itself." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tired and fond at once. "She also said you'd find it faster than me. She said you read anger better than duty. I've had fifteen years to decide whether that was an insult."

"What happened to the wards, then?" I asked, though the shape of the answer was already closing around my throat. "If the founders drained them—"

"They didn't drain them dry." Edmund's voice went quiet, the particular quiet of a man delivering a diagnosis. "You can't. The wards aren't a cistern, they're a body. Take enough and the body doesn't die — it *forgets how to be a body*. The founders left just enough remembering in the thorn to keep the border standing, the way you leave a candle burning in a window so the house looks lived-in. And then they walked away and built a city where magic was outlawed, because—" he laughed, and coughed, and laughed again, wetly, "—because a kingdom that remembers what was done to it might ask questions. Better that no one remember anything. Better Wardens whose whole trade is unmaking. Better stars that go out one by one and no one notices, because no one remembers how many there were supposed to be."

"The stars." I thought of that night in Vell, the struck-bell sound, the sky going dark like a page turned over. "That was the wards failing."

"That was the wards *finishing*. What the founders left, it finally ate. Fifteen years ago, almost to the season." He looked at me then, straight on, and I understood that we had both done the arithmetic and arrived at the same sum. "The same year you left."

* * *

I don't know how long I stood in that cellar before I could speak. Long enough for the lantern to burn low. Long enough for Edmund to rise, fetch a second candle, and light it from the first without a word, which was such an ordinary act of kindness in the middle of all that ruin that it nearly undid me worse than the journals had.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing I kept circling like a tongue returning to a cracked tooth.

Fifteen years ago, I had run. I had run from Aldith's discipline, from the hedge-tongue drills, from the slow gray weight of a village life measured in seasons — and I had told myself, on the road to Vell, in every cheap room and every hungry winter since, that I had fled *smallness*. That I had escaped a cage. That the city, with its laws and its lamplight and its careful forgetting, was the larger country.

And the whole time, the truth had been inverted. The village was not the cage. The village was the *keeper*. And Vell — Vell was the thing that had eaten the kingdom's soul and locked the door behind itself and called the darkness peace.

Every charm I had ever hidden. Every luck-knot sold as a trinket, every fever-charm whispered over like a shame. I had spent fifteen years burying a gift that my own city had already tried to bury for everyone — and I had helped. God help me, I had *helped*. I had been one more pair of hands pressing the kingdom's memory down into the grave, grateful for the darkness because in the darkness no one could ask what I was.

"Oscar." Edmund's hand on my shoulder. When had he crossed the room? "Breathe."

"They knew." My voice came out scraped. "Aldith knew, her teacher knew, the founders knew — and they let the whole kingdom forget the war ever happened. They let the villages forget their own names." I thought of the innkeeper's wife, smiling blankly when I'd asked what the village was called. "There are people upstairs, Edmund, who cannot tell me where they live because their address was *spent*. It was spent buying a hundred years of quiet for people who never had to live in it."

"Yes."

"And the Wardens—" I was pacing now, between the shelves, among the sleeping books, and my fury had a rhythm to it, a walking rhythm, like a drum. "The Wardens of the Unmaking. They're not guarding us from magic. They're guarding the *lie*. Anyone who remembers too much, anyone whose gift stirs the old crafts awake — hunted. Marked. Because every working charm is a loose thread, and pull enough threads and someone eventually asks what the cloth was hiding."

"That's why they'll come for you," Edmund said simply. "Why they came for Aldith, at the end. Why they'll come for me, if they bother to check whether I'm still breathing." He said it without fear, and somehow that frightened me more than fear would have.

"Then we take it to them." I turned to face him, and the words came out of me hot and fast, fifteen years of buried gift finally catching flame. "We take the journals to Vell. We put the grey book in the square where every soul in the city can read what their precious founders bought and what it cost. Let them choke on their statues. Let them—"

"Oscar."

"—let them remember, Edmund, that's the whole crime isn't it, that's the whole rotten bargain, they stole *remembering* itself, and we can give it back, we can—"

"*Oscar.*" His hand closed on my arm, gentle and immovable, and I stopped because beneath the gentleness his grip was shaking. "Look at the tree."

I looked at him instead. "What?"

"The thorn. Look at what's happening to it. The wards didn't fail because of a lie sixty years old — they failed because the lie ran out. The founders bought peace with a meal, and the meal is finished, and the thing beyond the border has woken up hungry again." He nodded toward the ceiling, toward the night above the house, toward the starless dark pressing down on the village like a thumb on a bruise. "Telling Vell the truth is right. It's necessary. But it will take years, and the border has *nights*. Whatever we mean to do about the lie, we must first do something about the hunger — or there won't be a Vell left to tell, nor a village, nor anyone to read the accusation."

The fury in me guttered, caught, guttered again — and then settled into something colder and more durable, the way a bonfire settles into coals. Coals hold heat longer.

"All right," I said. "Then we do both. We feed the wards and we keep the proof. Both roots, one wall."

Something moved through Edmund's face at that — quick, involuntary, gone before I could name it. Later I would learn what it was: the memory of a plan he had already made alone, a price he had already agreed to pay in secret, in the dark, without witnesses. But that night I only saw a tired man looking at me with an expression I couldn't read, saying, quietly:

"Aldith would have liked that. Both roots, one wall." He picked up the grey journal and held it a moment, weighing it, the way you weigh a stone you're deciding whether to throw or to build with. Then he set it back on the shelf, spine outward, easy to find. "Sleep now. Tomorrow we read the rest — there are three volumes after the grey one, and the third one..." He paused. For the first time all night, he looked away from me. "The third one is about the price of refilling what the founders emptied. What it costs a living mind to feed the thorn."

"Edmund—"

"Tomorrow," he said, and blew out the candle, and the cellar held us both in the dark among all those buried truths, and somewhere far above us, beyond the roof beams, beyond the hills, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to listen.

We did not hear the hoofbeats on the hedgeroad until morning.
