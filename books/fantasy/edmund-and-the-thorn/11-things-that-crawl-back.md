## 11. Things That Crawl Back

The first thing to come over the border was a smell.

Oscar noticed it on the second starless night, standing in Edmund's doorway with a bowl of barley broth he'd been trying for an hour to get into the dying man. The air off the fields had gone wrong — not foul, exactly. *Unfamiliar.* That was the horror of it. Every wind carried something: cut hay, woodsmoke, the river's mud, the thorn-tree's iron-and-honey scent. This wind carried nothing at all. It was a hole shaped like a breeze, and when it touched his face his skin prickled with the animal certainty that something had been *removed* from the world and was now walking toward them wearing the absence like a coat.

"Edmund," he said. "Something's coming."

Edmund was propped in his chair by the window, gray as old tallow, hands folded over the blanket as if they'd already been buried. He didn't open his eyes. "I know. I felt it at dusk. They always come on the dark nights." A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "Get the villagers inside. Bar the doors. And whatever you see — don't try to remember it. That's how it takes you."

"Remember what? You haven't told me what—"

The bell began to ring.

Not the chapel bell. The old ward-bell that hung from a bracket on the thorn-tree itself, cast before anyone alive could say, a dished bronze thing green with age that no one had struck in living memory because no one could remember why it hung there. It rang by itself now, a cracked, arrhythmic tolling, and with each stroke Oscar felt the sound pass through him like a hand through water.

He ran.

* * *

The village — nameless, because its name had been eaten along with everything else — gathered in the lane between the well and the smithy, holding torches that guttered in the empty wind. There were maybe sixty souls left of what had once been a market town: farmers, a cooper, two sisters who kept the alehouse, old Tam Wicker who had forgotten his own wife's face but still set out two bowls every evening. They stood in their nightclothes and work coats, blinking up at a sky with no stars in it, and Oscar understood with a lurch that not one of them knew what was happening. They had come out because the bell was ringing and the bell had never rung, and some deep animal part of them knew that when a thing that has never happened happens, you stand together or you are taken alone.

"What is it?" someone called. "Is it raiders?"

"It's the border," Oscar said, and heard his own voice crack like a boy's. "The wards have fallen. Things are coming across."

A woman laughed — high, uncertain. "There's no border. There's just the moor."

"There *was*," Oscar said. "There was a war. There were wards. Your grandmothers sang songs about it." He looked around at their blank faces and felt the full weight of what the founders had done settle onto his shoulders like wet wool. These people weren't ignorant. They were *amputated.* Somewhere under their ribs were stumps where whole centuries used to be.

Then the things came up the moor road, and there was no more time for explaining.

They did not walk so much as *arrive*, in stages, like figures emerging from fog that wasn't there. The nearest one stood at the edge of the torchlight and it was made of — Oscar's mind skated off it, twice, three times, like a thumb pressed on wet ice. It had the shape of a man but the substance of other people's leavings. He caught fragments: a wedding dance seen from the back of the room; the smell of a specific bread baked by a woman dead four hundred years; the exact weight of a child asleep on a shoulder; a soldier's last thought, which was of plums. The pieces didn't fit together. They didn't try to. The creature wore them the way a magpie wears stolen silver — because they shone, because they were *kept*, and keeping was the one thing it could never do for itself.

It opened what might have been a mouth and spoke in a chorus of voices that did not belong to it.

"*Maren. Maren. Who laid the blue cloth on the table. Maren.*"

Old Tam Wicker dropped his torch.

"Maren," he whispered. His face went through a transformation Oscar would remember all his life — the slow sunrise of a man remembering his dead wife after thirty years of setting out her bowl. "That's — she used to — the blue cloth, Sundays—"

"TAM!" Oscar lunged, but the old man was already walking forward, arms out, weeping, into the thing's embrace. And where it touched him it began to *drink*. Oscar saw it happen — saw the memory pour out of Tam like light through a cracked jug, saw the creature swell and brighten with the color of a life it hadn't lived — and then Tam sagged, and the sunrise on his face set again, and he looked at the creature with polite puzzlement, the way you look at a stranger who has stopped you in the street.

"Do I know you?" Tam said.

The creature made a sound like a page being torn out.

"BACK!" Oscar screamed. "Everyone back! Don't let it touch you! Don't listen to it — it only has what you've lost, it can't give anything back!"

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and orders and the clean arithmetic of force. What followed was sixty frightened people discovering, in the space of twenty minutes, that they were the last wall between the world and the unrememberable — and fighting anyway, with what they had.

What they had was pitchforks, a scythe, the cooper's maul, boiling water from the alehouse sisters, and prayer. The prayer surprised Oscar most. He had thought these people too hollowed-out to pray, their gods eaten with everything else. But faith, it turned out, lived lower than memory, down in the marrow where the eating hadn't reached. The miller's widow stood in the lane with her arms raised and recited words half-worn away — "*hallow and hold, hallow and hold*" — and where her voice fell, the creatures flinched, as if the words themselves were a fence they remembered being built to keep them out.

"They hate that," panted Bram the smith, swinging his maul two-handed at a thing that had gotten into the sheep pen. The maul passed through it without effect — you couldn't hit what was made of absence — but the *intent* seemed to matter, the refusal. "Why do they hate it?"

"Because it's yours," Oscar shouted back. "It's the oldest thing you've got left. Everything they're made of is *stolen* — your prayers are the one coin they can't counterfeit!"

It was true, and knowing it changed the fight. The villagers couldn't wound the things, but they could *refuse* them, and refusal turned out to be a weapon. When a creature crooned a dead daughter's name at the cooper, he ground his teeth and said, "No. She's mine. You don't get her," and the thing recoiled as if struck. When it offered the alehouse sisters their childhood dog — *Biscuit, Biscuit who slept on the hearth* — the younger sister sobbed and said, "I don't remember any dog," and lied, and the lie held the creature at bay like a drawn blade. Forgetting, Oscar realized, could be armor too. The founders had taught these people that without meaning to.

But there were more coming. Always more. Up the moor road they arrived in twos and threes now, drawn by the feast, wearing scraps of a hundred vanished lives — a harvest song here, a christening there, somebody's grief for somebody's drowned son worn like a sash. And each villager they touched came away lighter, emptier, smiling vaguely at nothing. Old Tam sat down in the middle of the lane, contented as a child, and could not be persuaded to move.

"We can't hold the lane," Oscar gasped to Bram. "Fall back to the tree. The tree — the thorn — it's still got some strength left, I've seen it burn them off—"

"The tree's dying, isn't it? That's why you're here?"

"How did you—"

"Edmund talks in his sleep." Bram spat, wiped his mouth. "Half the village hears him through the wall. We just don't remember it by morning." He hefted the maul. "So it's true, then. All of it. The wards, the war, the — whatever's out there."

"All of it."

"Then I'm glad somebody finally came home to help him." And the big man turned and bellowed the retreat, and the village fell back through the lanes by torchlight, carrying their wounded — which meant carrying their *emptied*, the ones who stared and smiled and asked, gently, over and over, *where am I, who are you, what is this place.*

* * *

They made their stand in the square beneath the thorn-tree.

It rose over them in the dark, vast and black and hurting — Oscar could feel its pain the way you feel weather in a bad tooth. Its branches had gone bare since the stars went out; its bark split and wept sap that glowed faintly gold and smelled, unbearably, of Edmund's childhood. But it was not dead yet. When the first creature crossed into the shadow of its canopy, a root moved — slow as a glacier, faster than anything that size had a right to move — and swept the thing off its feet, and where the root's thorns pricked it, the creature shrieked in a hundred stolen voices and came apart like smoke in wind.

The villagers took heart. They formed a rough ring at the edge of the canopy's reach, pitchforks outward, the widow's prayer going around the circle from mouth to mouth like a bucket line, *hallow and hold, hallow and hold*, and the creatures circled them hungrily, unable to close.

Oscar stood at the trunk with his palm against the bark, doing the only magic he dared. He had spent fifteen years burying his gift, and now he dug it up with both hands and gave it everything. Not power — he had none, not really, not against things like these. But he knew charms. He knew knots and bindings, the hedge-craft Mother Aldith had beaten into him before he threw it away, and he worked it now into the tree itself, whispering the old structures into the sap, feeding the ward-remnant every scrap of structure he could remember. *Hold. Hold. Hallow and hold.* The tree drank his whispers and its glow strengthened by a hair's breadth, and the ring of villagers stood inside that glow like children inside a mother's skirt.

An hour past midnight, the creatures stopped circling.

They drew back to the edge of the torchlight and stood in a loose, waiting crescent, dozens of them, wearing the village's whole eaten history — and Oscar felt the change in the air and his stomach dropped, because they had stopped being hungry and started being *patient*, and patience was worse.

"They're waiting for something," he said to Bram.

"For what?"

And then Oscar knew, because he could feel it through his palm, through the roots, through fifteen years of guilt — a thread of attention from far beyond the moor, vast and cold and amused, turning its regard upon the little ring of torches. The things below the border were not raiders. They were *foragers*. And somewhere out in the starless dark, the thing the founders had fed — the thing that had eaten a kingdom's memories and called the meal peace — had noticed that a door it thought sealed stood ajar, and had sent its scavengers ahead not to take, but to *test*.

"It's measuring us," Oscar said quietly. "All this — it's just it counting our spoons."

The creatures in the crescent turned their borrowed faces upward, all at once, in perfect unison, and spoke together in the voices of everyone the village had ever lost:

"*Keeper. Keeper. Come out and be remembered.*"

Behind him, from the direction of Edmund's house, Oscar heard a door bang open and a body fall in the lane, and someone scream for help, and he was running before the sound finished — running toward the coughing, toward the blood, toward the boy he had abandoned twice now, once at nineteen and once tonight, by leaving him alone in that house while the dark came up the road.

* * *

Edmund lay in the mud outside his own door, on his knees, one hand braced against the step, and even now — even doubled over with blood on his lips — he was laughing.

"You absolute fool," Oscar gasped, hauling him up. "You absolute — I told you to stay in bed—"

"Had to see it." Edmund's eyes were bright with fever and something wilder. "Fifteen years I've tended that tree and never once seen them come. Wanted to know what I was — what I've been *for*." He coughed, and gripped Oscar's sleeve with fingers like winter branches. "Did you see them, Osc? Did you see what they're made of?"

"I saw."

"That's what's left. That's all that's left of everything, wandering around wearing it." His voice broke, and steadied. "We fed the wards with remembering, and now the remembering walks around outside wearing other people's faces, and the only thing standing between it and the rest of the kingdom is—" he gestured weakly at the square, at the torchlit ring, at the widow still praying, at Bram with his maul, at the great black tree glowing its stubborn gold "—is *this*. Pitchforks and an old woman's prayer and a runaway apprentice."

"And a dying keeper," Oscar said.

"And a dying keeper." Edmund smiled, and it was the smile from fifteen years ago, from the hedge-row, from the day Oscar had climbed over it and not looked back. "You know what the worst part is? Watching them fight tonight. They don't know what they're defending. They can't name it. Half of them will forget by morning. And they stood in the lane anyway." He shook his head slowly. "Ordinary courage. Nobody writes songs about it because nobody remembers it long enough. But it's the only kind there ever was."

A bell tolled from the square — the ward-bell, still ringing its cracked arrhythmic alarm — and beyond it, up the moor road, the crescent of waiting things stirred, parted, and bowed low toward the darkness beyond the torchlight, the way courtiers bow.

Something was coming up the road that the scavengers made way for.

Edmund felt it too. His grip on Oscar's arm tightened until it hurt, and all the laughter went out of his face at once, and he said, in a voice gone suddenly very young:

"Oscar. It's not sending scavengers anymore. It's coming to negotiate — or to eat. And I don't think we get to choose which."
