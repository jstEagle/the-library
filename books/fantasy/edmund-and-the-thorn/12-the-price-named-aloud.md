## 12. The Price Named Aloud

The cough came at dawn, as it always did, and Edmund Hale knelt in the frost with his fist against his mouth and waited for it to pass. It had been getting worse since the stars went out. He had a theory about that — a small, bitter theory he kept folded away like a letter never sent — that the thorn was taking more from him now because the wards were failing faster, and the wards were failing faster because there was less of him left to give. A circle drawn smaller and smaller until the pencil wore through the paper.

He spat into the snow. Red bloomed there like a dropped rose.

"Well," he told the tree. "You could have warned me I'd live long enough to see him again."

The thorn-tree said nothing. It never had, not in words. But its branches shifted overhead — black limbs heavy with frost, each thorn as long as his forearm — and the sound they made was the sound of an old house settling, which Edmund had learned to read as well as any language. *Patience,* it might have been saying. Or perhaps: *Hurry.*

Fifteen years of listening to a tree, and still he couldn't be sure which.

He rose, joints cracking, and went inside to make tea he would mostly waste. The cottage was small and dark and smelled of ash and dried yarrow, and every surface held some small monument to a memory he no longer owned: a carved wooden bird whose name he'd forgotten, a tin cup he knew had belonged to someone he'd loved, though love without a face is just a shape. He kept them anyway. The tree took memories whole, but it didn't take objects, and Edmund had decided long ago that a man who couldn't remember his own life should at least be surrounded by its evidence — like an archaeologist digging through himself.

The kettle sang. Outside, Oscar's voice carried across the frozen garden, low and careful, talking to the village children who had followed him around for two days now like ducklings imprinting on something strange. Edmund listened to it through the window glass and felt the old ache turn over in his chest.

Oscar Marchetti. Back. Fifteen years late and half-dead with guilt, and still walking like a man who expected the ground to be pulled out from under him — which, Edmund supposed, was fair. The ground had been pulled out from under him once already. Edmund had watched it happen. Had stood at the edge of the hedge-road and watched the boy he loved more than the sun walk away into the fog with a bundle over his shoulder, and had not called after him, because Mother Aldith's last instruction had been burned into him like a brand:

*Someone must stay. Someone must stay, or everything we were is lost.*

So he had stayed. And he had not hated Oscar for leaving, not even in the worst winters, not even the night the Wardens came asking questions and he had to lie with a straight face while his heart broke quietly behind his ribs. Hate required energy, and energy was currency here, and every coin went to the tree.

But today the arithmetic changed. Today he had to tell Oscar what staying cost.

* * *

He found Oscar by the roots on the north side, where the great taproot broke the earth like the shoulder of a buried giant. Oscar had his hand flat against the bark — he'd been doing that all morning, touching the tree in small stolen moments, and Edmund knew why. The thorn showed things to those who touched it. Fragments. Leaks. Whatever it wasn't drinking fast enough to keep down.

Whatever it was showing Oscar now made him go pale and step back.

"You shouldn't do that," Edmund said, coming up through the frost. "It's rude to eavesdrop on someone's nightmares."

Oscar turned. In the grey light he looked older than fifteen years should have made anyone — hollowed under the eyes, wind-burned, his hedge-witch's hands scarred in ways apprenticeship hadn't scarred them. But when he looked at Edmund, there it was. The same look from the night he arrived. Guilt wearing the mask of concern.

"Edmund." His voice cracked on the name. "The tree just showed me — there was a kitchen, and someone was crying, and I couldn't see who—"

"Probably me." Edmund sat down heavily on the root, patting the place beside him. "Sit. We need to talk, and my legs are done pretending they're good for standing conversations."

Oscar sat. Of course he did. Fifteen years gone and he still obeyed Edmund like it was yesterday, and something in Edmund's chest twisted at that — tenderness and grief braided so tight he couldn't tell which strand was which.

"The coughing," Oscar said. "I've heard you at night. That's blood, isn't it. Don't lie to me; I sold fever-charms in Vell for a decade, I know what dying sounds like."

"It's blood," Edmund agreed. "And yes. I'm dying. Have been for years, honestly, but the last month has accelerated the schedule considerably." He smiled, because if he didn't smile Oscar would start apologizing, and Edmund had spent fifteen years making sure this conversation would never need apologies. "Which brings us to what I wanted to talk about."

"Then let me help. There must be something — Aldith's books, your journals, anything. There are still workings I remember, real ones, ones I never used in Vell because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because using them felt like admitting I'd run."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. The frost ticked in the silence. Somewhere beyond the hedge, one of the village dogs barked at nothing — at the dark treeline where the things had stopped moving but hadn't stopped *waiting*.

"Oscar," he said gently. "Do you know how the wards work? Truly? Not the theory Aldith taught us — the practice. What I've actually been doing out here every night for fifteen years?"

Oscar's jaw tightened. "Feeding it. Memories. She said the founders built it to drink from the kingdom's stores, and when those ran dry, the wardens fed it themselves."

"She told us that much, yes. But she didn't tell us the rest, because she thought — she hoped — there'd always be two of us, and we'd each discover it slowly enough to bear." Edmund laid his palm on the root beside him. "There isn't a ration. There isn't a tithe. You don't choose which memories go. The tree takes what keeps the wall standing, and the wall has been standing on less and less for years, and lately it's begun taking things I need. Yesterday I forgot which hand I write with. Last week I forgot my mother — not her face, Oscar. Her. The whole shape of having had one."

Oscar had gone very still. "That's why the cottage—"

"That's why the cottage looks like a museum of a stranger's life. Yes." He laughed, short and dry. "I keep evidence. It's undignified, but a man should get to know what he paid for."

"And the blood?" Oscar's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Is that the tree too?"

"No. The blood is just my body objecting to being emptied. A vessel drained past a certain point cracks. Aldith cracked in the end — you saw her, that last winter. You thought it was age." He watched Oscar flinch. "It wasn't age."

The silence stretched. Above them the black branches moved, and frost sifted down between them like slow snow.

"So what do we do," Oscar said finally. It wasn't a question. It was a man gripping a rope. "There has to be another way. The journals — you said the founders fed it stored memories, a kingdom's worth. Maybe there are stores left, maybe in Vell, maybe under the city, maybe—"

"I've thought about Vell for fifteen years," Edmund said. "Every angle. Every desperate alternative. And here's what I've concluded, sitting alone in the dark with a tree: the founders didn't build a machine, Oscar. They built a mouth. And mouths don't care what goes in them, only that something does. Stored memory, living memory — it's all the same meal. The stores ran out because the founders fed the thing everything the kingdom remembered about the war, and then they locked the pantry and called it peace and went home to found a city and forbid magic so no one would ever learn to check the locks." He shook his head. "There is no storehouse left. There's only us. There was only ever going to be only us."

"Then we split it," Oscar said quickly. "Both of us feed it. Half each. You'll last twice as long—"

"And be twice as empty, half as fast, and die anyway, and take you down with me." Edmund reached over and took Oscar's hand — the scarred one, the charm-seller's hand — and held it the way you hold something you're afraid will pull away. "Listen to me. Please. Just listen, all the way to the end, before you start building castles out of maybes. Because I've had fifteen years to think about this ending, and you've had two days, and I need you to hear the version I've made peace with."

Oscar didn't pull away. His fingers tightened instead, and he nodded once, and Edmund let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding since the fog swallowed a boy with a bundle on his shoulder.

"Aldith died keeping the wall up alone," he said. "She lasted three days past the point where she should have collapsed, because she poured everything — not just memories. Everything. The mind goes with the memories, Oscar. The self. The tree doesn't take your past and leave your present; it takes *you*, root and branch, and uses the whole of you as fuel, and what's left afterward is a body with nobody home. That's the price. That's what it costs to stand the wall back up when it's fallen this far. One person, entire. Poured out to the last drop."

The color had left Oscar's face entirely. "No," he said. "No, that's — Aldith wouldn't have hidden that from us—"

"She hid it because she loved us. Same reason I'm telling you now: because I love you, and I'm out of time for kindness." Edmund's voice stayed calm. He'd practiced this calm in the dark for years, rehearsing it like a speech, and now that the performance had come he found it held better than he'd feared. "Here is what I've decided. The wall needs rebuilding within days — you've seen the things at the treeline; they're waiting because they can smell how thin it's gotten. And I am the natural candidate. I'm already half-poured. I'm dying regardless; the tree has simply been spending me in installments. If I go into it whole — all at once, everything remaining — the wall stands for another generation. Maybe fifty years. Long enough for someone smarter than either of us to find a third way. Long enough for the children in that village to grow up behind a green border instead of a failing one."

"No." Oscar pulled his hand free and stood, and his voice came out ragged. "Absolutely not. You are not doing that. We will find—"

"Oscar."

"—something, there is always something, Aldith taught us a hundred workings, I'll spend every memory I *own*, I'll go into Vell and dig under the founders' halls myself, I'll—"

"*Oscar.*"

He stopped. Stood there shaking in the cold, fists clenched, and Edmund saw it all move across his face — the panic, the fury, and underneath both, the thing he'd carried across fifteen years and a hundred miles: *I left, and someone stayed, and staying killed him.*

"Sit down," Edmund said softly. "Please. I'm not finished."

Oscar sat. It cost him visibly, like a man lowering himself into deep water.

"Thank you." Edmund gathered himself. This was the part he'd never rehearsed properly, because he'd never let himself imagine saying it to Oscar's face. "I know what you're feeling right now. You're doing the arithmetic of guilt. *If I hadn't left, we'd have shared it, and he'd have lasted longer.* And maybe that's true, and maybe it isn't — Aldith might have died either way, the founders' theft might have caught up with us regardless. But here's what I need you to understand, and then I need you to stop doing that arithmetic forever, because it's bad math and worse company." He leaned forward. "Staying didn't kill me, Oscar. Staying was the best fifteen years available to anybody in this story. I got to keep the wall up. I got to watch children grow up safe. I got to protect your secret when the Wardens came, and I'd do it again tomorrow. The only tragedy in my life is that you weren't here for it — and you're here now. So don't you dare spend my last days drowning in a debt I never charged you."

Oscar made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the opposite. "You always talked like this. Like a man reading out terms and conditions for loving him."

"Somebody had to. You'd have signed anything without reading it." Edmund smiled, and let the smile fade slowly into something plainer. "One more thing, and then I'm done, and you can rage all you like. I'm telling you this not because I want permission — I stopped needing permission the day Aldith died — but because you deserve to hear it aloud, from me, while I still remember your name."

"Don't." Oscar's voice broke clean through. "Don't say it like a farewell."

"Everything I've said today is a farewell. That's rather the point." Edmund looked up at the tree — the whole dark height of it, thorns gleaming with frost, patient as stone. "Tomorrow night, when the moon is new and the wall is weakest, I go into the roots. All of me. And it will hurt, I expect, for about a minute, and then it won't hurt anymore, and the border will burn green-gold from the sea to the mountains, and somewhere in the wood of it there'll be a piece of me that remembers a boy running down the hedge-road in the fog. I've asked the tree for that. Trees keep their promises. It's people who don't."

The silence after was enormous. Frost ticked. Far off, the dog barked again at the patient dark.

Oscar bowed his head over his scarred hands, and his shoulders shook, and Edmund reached out and put his arm around him and held on — held on the way he'd held the wall together for fifteen years, stubbornly, tenderly, past all sense of his own strength.

"I hate you," Oscar whispered into his shoulder. "I hate you so much. Fifteen years and you're still — you're still *deciding things for both of us*—"

"I know," Edmund said. "It's my worst quality. Second worst. The first is that I'm usually right."

A wet, broken laugh against his collar. Then, muffled, terrible, quiet:

"There has to be another way, Edmund. Tell me you'll at least let me try to find another way. One day. Give me one day."

Edmund closed his eyes. He had rehearsed refusals for this. He had rehearsed everything except the weight of Oscar begging against his chest.

"One day," he said. "Tomorrow at moonrise, whatever we've found or haven't found — I go to the roots. That's the term, and this time you read it before you sign."

Above them the black branches stirred, and the frost came sifting down between two men holding each other in the shadow of the price, and neither of them said the word for what the day ahead was.

They didn't need to. The tree already knew.
