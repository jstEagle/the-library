## 13. An Argument at the Root

The rain came down through the thorn-branches in a fine grey mist, and beneath the tree the world smelled of iron and old sap and the sweet rot of things that had been forgotten for too long.

Oscar knelt at the roots with his hands buried to the wrist in cold soil, feeling the ward-lines hum against his knuckles like plucked strings slowly going slack. He could read them now — Aldith had taught him that much before he ran, and fifteen years of hiding had not burned it out of him. The wards were not broken. They were *starved*. Every thread of them thin as winter honey, every knot in the great root-lattice frayed where Edmund had been picking it apart strand by strand, feeding himself into the tree like a man feeding his own flesh to a fire to keep a house warm.

"You've been doing this alone," Oscar said. His voice came out strange, scraped raw. "Fifteen years. Alone."

Edmund sat against the trunk with his back to the bark, knees drawn up, looking smaller than any grown man had a right to look. He was thirty now, only two years older than Oscar, but the coughing had carved him down to something birdlike, something that seemed held together by habit and stubbornness and the tree's slow green will. He wiped blood from his lip with the back of his hand, matter-of-fact as a farmer wiping away rain.

"Alone's a strong word," he said. "I had the tree."

"The tree."

"She talks. In her way." A ghost of a smile. "Mostly she complains about the weather."

Oscar pulled his hands from the soil. The ward-hum faded from his bones, and what rushed in behind it was worse than silence: it was arithmetic. He could do it without wanting to. Fifteen years of memories, fed a spoonful at a time into a dying god-tree, and Edmund still sitting here breathing, which meant he was nearly finished, which meant —

"How much is left?" Oscar asked. "Of you. How much have you given her?"

Edmund looked up through the dripping branches at the starless sky, considering, the way another man might consider how much flour remained in the barrel.

"Enough," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting."

And something in Oscar — something that had been coiling tighter since the moment he'd walked through the door that knew his name, since the moment Edmund had laughed and wept at the sight of him all at once — something in him finally snapped its tether.

"Don't you *dare*," he said.

Edmund blinked at him, mild. "Dare what?"

"Dare to sit there and be gentle with me. Dare to make jokes. I left." The words tore coming out, and he let them tear; he was done swallowing them. "I left you here. I walked out that hedge-gap when we were fifteen years old and I told myself I'd send for you, I told myself Mother Aldith would understand, I told myself a hundred comfortable lies, and you — you *stayed*. You stayed and you died by inches for a border full of people who don't even remember your name, and now you're going to sit there and tell me *enough*—"

"Yes," Edmund said.

The word landed like a stone in still water.

"Yes," he said again, quieter, and pushed himself upright against the trunk, and for a moment the rain-mist parted around him and Oscar saw the whole shape of what fifteen years had made of him: not a martyr, not a saint. A keeper. Tired, bloody-mouthed, unrepentant. "Yes, I stayed. Yes, I'm nearly spent. And no, Oscar, you don't get to stand there and make it your tragedy. You weren't here. You didn't watch her die."

Oscar flinched. "Don't—"

"She held the wards with her bare hands for three days when the founders' theft finally caught up with us. Three days and nights in the rain, and the tree drinking her down, and do you know what she said to me at the end?" Edmund's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it. It never rose. It just went deeper, like water finding stone. "She said, *the boy who ran will come back when the stars go out. They always come back when the dark comes.* She believed it. She died believing you'd return, and I stood at her grave and I hated you so much I couldn't see straight, and then I came back here the next morning and started feeding the tree, because hating you was a luxury and the wards weren't."

The rain ticked softly against the leaves. Somewhere out past the hedgerow, in the starless fields, something made a sound like a bell heard underwater.

"I'm sorry," Oscar whispered.

"No," Edmund said. "You're not. Not yet. You're guilty, and guilt's a cheap coin — you can spend it anywhere. Sorry comes later, when you've actually paid for something." He coughed, folded forward, and pressed a red-smeared hand against the root beside him. The bark drank the blood without comment. "I forgave you years ago, Oscar. It wasn't hard. You want to know why?"

"Why."

"Because running was the truest thing you ever did." Edmund looked at him then, straight on, and his eyes were the same eyes from fifteen years ago — brown, quick, always half-laughing — except now they were lit from behind by something vast and patient and dying. "Aldith chose me because I was steady. Steady as a fencepost. But you — you were the one who asked why. Why the wards ate memory. Why the founders got to trade ours away and call it victory. Why we kept the village small and dim so the city could burn bright. You asked why until asking got dangerous, and then you ran, and I have spent fifteen years wondering if maybe the tree needed a keeper who'd stay, or whether what it really needed was someone who'd leave and come back carrying questions."

"So you're glad I abandoned you."

"I'm saying nothing about you was wasted." Edmund's jaw tightened. "Which is more than I can say for me. Which brings us to tonight."

And there it was. Oscar felt it coming the way you feel a storm front — pressure behind the ears, the birds gone quiet.

"Tonight," Oscar repeated.

"The rite doesn't wait for me to die naturally. If I wait, the wards gutter out first, and everything the thing beyond the border has been sniffing at comes through the gap at once. So tonight, at moonset, I finish it. I pour the rest in. What's left of me goes into the roots, and the wards blaze green again, and the village sleeps safe, and Edmund Hale becomes a story the tree tells itself in the winter." He said it the way he'd say he planned to mend a fence. Calm. Settled. *Done.*

"No," Oscar said.

"Oscar—"

"*No.*" He was on his feet without remembering standing, rain running down his face, hands shaking. "You don't get to decide that alone either. That's the whole — that's the entire *point* — you've been deciding alone for fifteen years. Alone you kept the wards. Alone you hid my name from the Wardens. Alone you're going to walk into a tree and die, and you think because you've forgiven me that makes it noble? It doesn't. It makes it the same wound, cut open again. I am not letting you die alone twice."

Something flickered across Edmund's face — pain, quickly mastered, and under the pain something that looked horribly like tenderness.

"You already paid once," Edmund said. "That's what you don't understand. When you ran, you paid. You paid with Vell, with the false name, with fifteen years of selling luck-knots to people who'd burn you if they knew what you were. You paid with your whole life, Oscar. I watched the bill come due from a distance and I swore — I swore on her grave — that you'd never pay it twice. The rite takes a whole mind. Everything. Your mother's face. The sound of rain in Vell. The taste of the first charm you ever cast. All of it, poured out, and what's left is a body walking around with a stranger's patience inside it. I won't—" His voice cracked, just once, and he mastered it. "I won't watch you come out of that tree not knowing who I am. I did the arithmetic a long time ago. One of us empty is a tragedy. Two of us empty is just waste."

"Then we find another way."

"There is no other way. I've had fifteen years and a cellar full of Aldith's journals to look for one. There is the rite, and there is the dark, and there is nothing between them but us."

"Then we both go in."

Edmund stared at him. "That's not how it works. The rite takes one keeper. One mind, whole, given freely. Two minds muddled together in the roots — the wards would tangle, the border would fray, you'd unmake in a season everything I—"

"Then tell me exactly how it works," Oscar said, stepping closer, boots sinking in the black loam, "because I've been reading her journals too, Edmund, and I haven't finished them, and neither, I think, have you — because you stopped reading the night you decided to die, and people who've decided to die stop looking for doors."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The thorn-tree creaked overhead, branches shifting, and a single drop of sap fell between them, glowing faintly gold where it struck the soil — the tree listening, the way it apparently always listened, patient as geology.

"You always were insufferable when you thought you'd caught me in an error," Edmund said at last, and there was a tremor under the dryness.

"And you were always unbearable when you thought being steady was the same as being right."

They glared at each other, two boys of thirty-five and thirty-seven, mud to the knees, rain in their hair, and then — absurdly, terribly — Edmund laughed. It turned into coughing halfway through, and Oscar caught his arm without thinking, the way he'd caught it a thousand times on the hedgeroads of their childhood, and Edmund leaned into the grip for one unguarded second before pulling himself straight.

"Fifteen years," Edmund said hoarsely, "and the first thing we do is fight in the rain like we're twelve."

"We're good at it. We practiced."

"We did." He looked at Oscar then, and all the calm armor of the keeper slipped, just for a breath, and underneath it Oscar saw the boy he'd left — frightened, exhausted, sixteen years old and holding a burden that should have killed him, holding it anyway, holding it *alone*, and never once, not in fifteen years, not to a single Warden with fire in his hand, giving up Oscar's name. "I don't want to forget you," Edmund said simply. "That's the truth of it. I've fed her everything else. My mother's lullabies. The war. My own wedding-day-that-never-was. I kept you till last because I'm a coward about exactly one thing, and it's you. And if you step into that rite with me, I lose even that. Do you understand? You're asking me to give up the only thing I've been saving."

Oscar's throat closed. He knelt, slowly, until they were level, knee to knee in the wet grass at the roots of the tree that was eating them both.

"Then here's what I'm asking instead," he said. "Not tonight. Not yet. Give me until moonset tomorrow. One day. Let me finish her journals — all of them, cover to cover, the pages you stopped reading. Because I felt something in those wards today, Ed. Something in the lattice that doesn't match anything she wrote about the old rite. A second channel. Faint, half-grown, like a path that was started and abandoned. Somebody tried to build another way into this tree once. Maybe she did. Maybe she finished it. And if there's a door, I'm not walking through the wall just because you've decided walls are traditional."

Edmund was silent for a long time. Rain dripped. Far off, the underwater bell sounded again, closer.

"If you're wrong," he said finally, "we've spent a night we might not have."

"If I'm right," Oscar said, "neither of us spends the other."

Edmund closed his eyes. When he opened them, some of the terrible settledness had gone out of them, replaced by something younger and more frightened and more alive — the look of a man who has carried a coffin for fifteen years and just been told he might be allowed to set it down.

"One day," he said. "And Oscar — if the journals hold nothing, if there's no door, you swear to me you'll let me do what must be done. Swear it on her grave. On the hedge. On whatever you still believe in."

Oscar thought of lying. He found, to his surprise, that he couldn't — not to this man, not here, not anymore.

"On the hedge," he said. "But Ed — I felt that channel. I *felt* it. And there's one page I haven't found yet. Her last one. The journal breaks off mid-entry, like she was interrupted, and I mean to know what interrupted her."

He rose, legs stiff, and turned toward the dark shape of Aldith's cottage at the field's edge — toward the cellar where the dead woman's handwriting waited in the lamplight.

Behind him, soft as rain, Edmund said, "She was writing the night she died. I always wondered what she was trying to tell us."

* * *

The cellar lamp burned low, and Oscar read with dirt still under his fingernails, page after page of Aldith's tight, furious script, until near dawn his finger stopped on an entry dated three days before her death — an entry Edmund had never seen, because it was written not in ink but in something brown-red that had aged to the color of dried apples.

Thorn-sap.

*The rite need not take everything,* she had written, the letters wobbling, written fast, written scared. *A burden shared between two keepers, given willingly, divides. Half a mind each. Half a life each. No one has ever tried it. No one has ever had two. But I have two boys, and I am an old fool, and I should have taught them this instead of teaching them to be afraid.*

Below that, the entry broke off mid-word.

And below *that*, pressed into the page as though the pen had fallen from a dying hand, was a single line in a different hand entirely — shakier, newer, written recently, in fresh sap still faintly fragrant:

*I found this page three nights ago. I was going to burn it. I couldn't.*

It was signed with a small, crooked drawing of a thorn.

Edmund's hand.
