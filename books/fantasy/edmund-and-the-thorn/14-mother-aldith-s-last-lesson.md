## 14. Mother Aldith's Last Lesson

The cellar smelled of earth and tallow and fifteen years of silence.

Oscar had found the door behind the flour shelf in Edmund's pantry — a low hatch, oak gone silver with age, banded in iron that hummed faintly against his palm like a struck tuning fork. Warded. Of course it was warded. Mother Aldith had warded her jam pots; she would hardly leave her books naked to the world. But the ward had known him. It had tasted his name — his true name, the one he hadn't spoken aloud since he was nineteen years old and running — and opened without a whisper, the way a dog opens its eyes when the one person it loves comes up the path.

Now he sat on the cold flagstones with journals spread around him in a broken ring, candlelight guttering each time the house above shuddered. Edmund was sleeping — or pretending to sleep — in the room overhead, and every few minutes Oscar heard the wet rattle of his breathing come down through the floorboards like something drowning slowly in another room.

He had been reading since dusk.

Aldith's hand changed over the years. In the earliest volumes it was quick and confident, letters leaning forward like a woman walking into wind: *Ward-holding is not casting. Casting spends; holding *is*. You do not pour yourself into the thorn all at once. You lend it, drop by drop, the way a well lends water to a bucket.* By the middle years the letters had grown cramped, pressed hard into the paper as though she were afraid the words would escape if she wrote them lightly. And by the last volume — a thin thing, its cover stained dark at the corners where hands had held it too often — the writing had begun to fail altogether, trailing off mid-sentence, resuming days later in a different ink, a different strength.

Oscar turned pages with fingers that shook more than he wanted them to.

*The founders came again today,* she had written, in what he guessed was the fortieth year of her keeping. *They bring honey in their mouths and knives behind their backs. They want the thorn's roots for their city walls. They want the memory-vaults emptied into their new "archive." They call it preservation. I have seen what they preserve. I have seen what they feed.*

There was more, much more, but it circled the same wound: Vell had been built on a bargain. When the last war went badly — when the thing beyond the border had grown fat on the kingdom's fear and begun to reach across the wards — the founders had done the unthinkable arithmetic. The thorn-tree drank memory to live. The vaults beneath the city held the kingdom's stored remembering, generations of it, harvested and hoarded by hedge-witches and court-magicians alike against just such a night. And so the founders had opened the vaults. Not to the tree. To the other side.

*They fed it,* Aldith wrote, and here the pen had torn the paper. *They fed it everything we saved, and called the silence that followed peace. It sleeps now because it ate. It will wake hungry. And when it wakes, there will be nothing left between it and us but one old woman and a tree.*

Oscar set that volume down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw green-gold ghosts.

Fifteen years he had believed the wards failed because magic failed — because the old ways were dying, because the world was moving on, because cities like Vell were right to outlaw the craft that could no longer protect anyone. He had built his whole exile on that belief. He had buried his gift under a false name and sold luck-knots to children because believing the work was dead made leaving bearable.

It hadn't died. It had been *sold*. Eaten. Traded away by men in good coats who then wrote themselves into history as saviors.

His fury rose so hot and fast that the candleflame leaned toward him, and he caught himself doing it — catching the fire's attention the way you'd catch a child's — and made himself let go. Not here. Not with Edmund dying upstairs and the stars still black and the things at the border still waiting. Fury was a luxury. Fury was what he'd been spending instead of grief for fifteen years, and look where it had gotten everyone.

He reached for the last journal.

It was small enough to fit in two hands. The leather had cracked along the spine; someone — Edmund, surely, Edmund with his careful hands — had stitched it back together with waxed thread. The final pages were the worst to read, because Aldith's handwriting was failing in them the way a voice fails, letter by letter, the way a wall fails, stone by stone.

*I am tired,* she wrote. *The tree asks more of me each winter and I have less to give. I have fed it my mother's face. I have fed it the name of the river I swam in as a girl. Last month I fed it the smell of bread, all bread, everywhere, and now when Edmund bakes I smell only smoke. This is the price and I pay it gladly, but gladly does not mean forever.*

Then, several pages later, in a hand so weak it was nearly a scrawl:

*Edmund asks me what happens when I cannot pay anymore. He is sixteen and he already knows the answer better than I do. Someone must take up the keeping. That is the whole of the craft, in the end. Someone must.*

And then — Oscar almost missed it, because it was tucked at the very bottom of a page otherwise filled with a recipe for willow-bark tincture, written sideways in the margin as if she had been ashamed to give it room:

*There is another way. I think there has always been another way.*

Oscar went still. Above him, the house creaked. Somewhere far off — miles off, past the starless fields — something howled in a voice made of other people's childhoods, and the sound slid through the shutter-cracks and lay down on the floor beside him like a dog that would not be sent out.

He read the marginal note again. Then again. Then he turned the page, and found the rest.

It was the last full entry in the book. She had dated it — the date meant nothing to him, some autumn long ago — and beneath the date she had written, slowly, with the care of a woman setting down something breakable:

*A ward is a promise, and promises can be shared. Every keeper before me has poured alone, and so every keeper has believed the price is total: one mind, given whole, to hold the border. But the thorn does not actually want a whole mind. It wants enough. It wants a life's weight, yes — but a life's weight divided is still a life's weight. Two keepers, giving willingly, side by side, each pouring half — the cost divides. Neither is consumed. Both are diminished. Both remain.*

*I have never tried it. There has never been a second keeper willing to stand at the root with me — not because the villagers are cowards, but because I never told them. I told no one, because if I told them and they refused, I would have to go on knowing they knew. Pride, at my age. Pride, at the end of the world.*

*So I write it here, where only a keeper will ever read it, and I say plainly: the rite of one keeper is a choice, not a law. If any who comes after me reads this — hear me. You do not have to be eaten whole. Find the other. Ask. The asking is the hardest spell there is, and I never cast it.*

*Forgive me. — A.A.*

Oscar did not know how long he sat there.

The candle burned down a thumb's width. The howling outside rose and fell and rose. His hands, when he finally looked at them, were white where they gripped the little book, and there were tears on his face that he had no memory of starting, which frightened him more than anything else in the cellar — because tears without memory were exactly the kind of payment the thorn took, and for one mad moment he wondered if the tree was already drinking from him through the floorboards, through the roots that ran under the village like veins under skin.

*You do not have to be eaten whole.*

Hope arrived in him the way floodwater arrives in a dry ditch — not gently, not gradually, but all at once and with debris. And it was unbearable, because hope this late was worse than despair. Despair had a shape he'd lived with for fifteen years; it fit him like an old coat. Hope tore the coat off and showed him the man underneath, the man who might have stayed, the man who might have stood at the root beside Edmund all these years so that neither of them would have to stand there alone —

— except Edmund *had* stood there alone. Fifteen winters of it. Feeding the tree his mother's face, his river's name, the smell of bread, drop by drop by drop, alone, because the one person who should have asked — who should have *been asked* — had run.

"No," Oscar said aloud, to the cellar, to the dead woman, to himself. "No, don't — don't you dare make this better. Don't you dare."

But the words on the page didn't care about his guilt. They just lay there, patient as seeds: *the cost divides. Both are diminished. Both remain.*

Both remain.

He thought of Edmund upstairs, coughing blood into a rag he hid under the mattress because he was still, after everything, protecting Oscar from the knowledge of what the keeping cost. He thought of Edmund saying, three nights ago by the light of a lamp with no star above it, *I'll finish it tonight, before the tree takes me anyway — it's cleaner this way* — calm as a man discussing fence posts. He thought of the fight they'd had afterward, the shouting, the terrible gentleness underneath it, Edmund refusing to let Oscar pay for a choice Oscar had already made once by running, and Oscar refusing to let Edmund die alone again, and both of them stubborn as hedgerows, love coming out of their mouths shaped like anger because neither of them had ever learned another shape for it.

And all the while the answer had been sitting in a margin. All the while Aldith had written it down and hidden it from herself, from shame, from pride, from the simple human terror of asking and being refused.

*Find the other. Ask.*

Oscar laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and echoed off the low ceiling, and he clapped a hand over his mouth like a boy.

He gathered the journals — all of them, stacking them with shaking hands — and climbed the ladder, and pushed open the hatch, and stood in the pantry breathing bread-smell and dust while his eyes adjusted to the dark.

Then he took the lamp from its hook and went up the stairs to Edmund's room, two steps at a time, because hope had arrived too late to be comfortable and he was done being comfortable.

Edmund was awake. Of course he was awake. He was propped against the headboard with a blanket over his knees, and in the lamplight his face was gray as ash and translucent at the temples, and he looked — Oscar's heart broke cleanly, quietly, like ice on a pond — he looked *relieved*, the way a man looks when the bell finally tolls.

"You found her last page," Edmund said. Not a question. His eyes went to the little book in Oscar's hand, to the way Oscar was gripping it, and something flickered across his face — hope, quickly smothered, the way you smother a spark in a hayloft because a fire now would kill you faster than the cold. "She told me once there was another way. When I was young. She said she'd never write it down." A thin smile. "Liar. She wrote everything down."

"Edmund." Oscar's voice cracked. He crossed the room and knelt by the bed, and put the journal on the blanket between them like a bridge. "Listen to me. The rite doesn't have to take everything. Two keepers, sharing willingly — the price splits. Half and half. We'd both lose. We'd both *stay*."

For a long moment Edmund said nothing. The howling outside rose again, closer now, and beneath the floorboards, deep beneath the village, Oscar felt the thorn shift in its sleep like a great animal turning toward a sound.

"I know," Edmund said at last, very quietly.

Oscar's blood went cold. "You — *what?*"

"I've known for years. I found the page when I was twenty-two." Edmund looked at his own hands, folded on the blanket, scarred and sap-stained and steady. "I never said anything because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because you weren't here, Oscar. Because the rite needs two willing keepers, and there was only me, and knowing there was a way to survive it and no way to *use* it was—" His breath caught, and he rode through it. "It was the cruelest thing she ever taught me. So I decided to die clean instead. One keeper, whole price, finished. No halves. No one else dragged under."

"And now?"

"Now you're here." Edmund raised his eyes, and in them Oscar saw fifteen years of solitary nights, and one memory of a boy running, kept tenderly, and something new — something terrified and blazing that looked, impossibly, like hope arriving too late to be comfortable. "And I don't know how to ask," Edmund whispered. "She was right. The asking is the hardest spell there is."

Downstairs, in the pantry, behind the flour shelf, the warded hatch began to knock.

Three slow blows, iron on oak — not the rhythm of the house settling, not the rhythm of anything natural. Oscar was on his feet before his mind caught up, journal clutched to his chest, and Edmund's face had gone white around the mouth.

"That's not the tree," Edmund said. "The tree doesn't knock."

From the yard beyond the window came a horse's breath, and a lantern's swing, and a voice — level, weary, unmistakable — calling through the starless dark:

"Keeper Hale. Open your door. Warden Crane is at the root, and I need to speak with whoever is in your cellar."
