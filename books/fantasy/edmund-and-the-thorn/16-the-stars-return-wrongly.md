## 16. The Stars Return, Wrongly

The night after the rite, no one in the village slept.

They had earned the right to, if anyone ever had. The border had flared green-gold at moonset and held through the small hours while the forgotten things pressed against it like moths against a lamp, and when the pale light finally dimmed to a steady ember, the villagers had stumbled back to their houses with pitchforks still in their hands, too frightened to put them down, too exhausted to do anything but sit by their cold hearths and wait for morning.

Oscar did not go inside.

He sat on the low stone wall at the edge of Edmund's garden with a blanket around his shoulders that someone — Marta, he thought, though his memory of her was a torn page now, edges missing — had pressed into his hands without a word. His body ached in ways that had no names. There were hollows in him where whole years used to live. He could feel them the way you feel a pulled tooth: not pain exactly, but an absence with weight, a socket where something had been.

Edmund slept inside, or what passed for sleep in a man who had given half his mind to a tree. Oscar had watched him breathe for a long time before daring to leave the room. Each breath came slow and even, and each one felt borrowed.

Above him, the sky was empty.

That was the thing no one had said aloud yet, though everyone had seen it. The stars had come back during the rite — Crane had shouted it from the yard, her voice cracking on the word *back* — but they had not come back *right*. Oscar looked up now and confirmed what his heart already knew. The stars were there, hundreds of them, burning in their old white and silver. But they hung in arrangements he did not know. The Ploughman was gone. The Sisters were gone. The Weaver's Loom, which Mother Aldith had taught him to read before he could read letters, was nowhere in the sky, and in its place sprawled a constellation like a closed hand, knuckles down, as if the night itself had made a fist over the world.

He knew every star Mother Aldith had named. He had spent fifteen years in Vell looking up between the rooftops and finding them, taking comfort in their fixedness, their indifference, their terrible reliability. Stars were the one thing in his life that had never changed.

Now they had.

*It's renegotiating,* he thought, and the thought arrived fully formed, cold as well-water, as if some part of him that remembered things he no longer remembered had handed it up to the surface. *Whatever is out there. Whatever the founders fed. It took the bargain we offered and it is rewriting the terms.*

A floorboard creaked behind him. He didn't startle; he'd stopped startling sometime in the last two days, worn past it.

Crane came out into the garden with her Warden-grey coat over her shoulders and no badge on it anymore. She had torn the badge off herself, he'd heard, somewhere in the fighting — or rather she hadn't torn it off at all, she had simply stopped wearing it, which in Isolde Crane amounted to the same act performed more thoroughly. She sat down on the wall beside him without asking, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, far enough that they didn't.

"You should be sleeping," she said.

"So should you."

"I don't trust the dark." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she reported facts. "I stood watch over a lie for eleven years, Marchetti. I'd like to stand watch over something true for one night, even if nothing comes."

"Oscar," he said. "You can call me Oscar."

She considered this. "I'll keep Marchetti for now. It's the name I hunted." A pause. "And I find I don't want to lose track of him yet."

They sat in silence. Somewhere beyond the hedge-line, out where the fields gave way to the grey country of the border, the wards hummed. Not the green-gold flare of the rite — that had settled hours ago — but a low, continuous note, like a struck bell that refused entirely to fade. Oscar could feel it in his teeth. He suspected he would feel it in his teeth for the rest of his life.

"The stars," Crane said finally. "You've been staring at them since you came out here. Tell me what's wrong with them. You're the only one left who'd know."

Oscar lifted his hand and traced the fist-shaped constellation against the black. "That shouldn't be there. Nothing should be there. And there—" he moved his finger east, to a scatter of bright points arranged in a long descending line, "—that's new too. It looks like a stairway going down. Or a spine."

"A spine," Crane repeated.

"I didn't say it was a friendly spine."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her, brief and grim. Then it died, because both of them felt it at the same moment: the hum of the wards changed key.

It was subtle. A listener who hadn't spent the night attuned to it would have missed it entirely. But the note that had been holding steady all night — the bell-note, the *held* note — bent downward, softened, and became something else. Something almost like a voice clearing its throat.

Then, across the whole eastern sky, the stars began to move.

Not fall. Not flicker. *Move* — sliding along invisible tracks, smooth as beads on a wire, regrouping, rearranging. Oscar was on his feet before he knew he'd stood, the blanket fallen in the dirt. In the space of ten heartbeats the false constellations dissolved and reformed into shapes that made his stomach drop, because they weren't abstract anymore. They weren't fists or spines.

They were letters.

Old ones. The kind cut into boundary-stones, the kind Mother Aldith had called *the first promises*, the script the kingdom had used before writing learned to be humble. And the letters spelled, across a quarter of the heavens in light that any child could read:

**WE ACCEPT.**

"Oh no," Oscar whispered.

"What?" Crane was standing too, her hand on a knife she'd surely know was useless. "What does it say? What does it want?"

"It accepted." His mouth was dry. "We fed it names instead of memories. We renamed it small enough to fit behind the wards. And it's saying—" he made himself finish, "—it's saying yes. It's agreeing to stay."

"That's good." Her voice climbed. "Marchetti, that's *good*, isn't it? That's what we wanted—"

"It's what we wanted, yes." He couldn't look away from the sky. The letters held for a long moment, patient as carved stone, and then unraveled, the stars drifting back into their wrong constellations, the closed fist reforming directly overhead as though to underline the point. "But Crane — bargains aren't accepted. Bargains are *renegotiated*. When a merchant says 'we accept' to your price without haggling, it's because he intends to make his profit somewhere you haven't looked yet."

The words came out of him with a certainty he didn't own. They belonged to Aldith, or to the journals, or to whatever sliver of himself he'd poured into the thorn and gotten back changed. He understood, distantly, that this was what Edmund lived with — knowing things without knowing how he knew them, wisdom arriving like weather.

Below them, in the village, doors were opening. People had seen it too. Of course they had — you didn't need to read the old script to understand sky-writing. Light that organizes itself into language is language, whatever alphabet it uses. Figures emerged into the lane, faces tilted upward, and a sound went through the crowd that Oscar had never heard from a group of people before: not a scream, not a cheer, but a single collective intake of breath, drawn and held, the sound of three hundred people deciding together whether to hope.

Marta pushed through to the wall, the blanket-giver, a broad woman with flour still on her forearms from baking she'd done in the middle of the siege-night because terror and habit look alike at four in the morning. She stared at the sky, then at Oscar.

"It said accept," she said. "My gran taught me the old letters, rest her. It said *we accept*. That's mercy, isn't it? That's the thing giving us mercy?"

Oscar looked at the fist overhead. At the stairway-spine descending in the east. At the wards' low hum, steady again, obedient again, as though nothing had happened.

"Yes," he said. "It's giving us mercy."

Marta smiled with relief, and it broke his heart, because she believed him, and because he had not told her the whole truth, and because the whole truth was this: *mercy is what it looks like when something very large decides you are worth keeping alive.*

*For now,* said the part of him that remembered. *Mercy, for now.*

* * *

Dawn came, and it was wrong too.

Oscar noticed it first as a color problem. He was standing at the hedge-line with Edmund — Edmund upright now, moving slowly, wrapped in two cloaks and his own stubbornness, refusing the bed Aldith's old house offered — when the east began to lighten. And the light came up not gold, not grey, not any dawn-color Oscar's bones recognized, but a pale greenish silver, like moonlight diluted in milk, like the underside of a leaf held against the sun.

The villagers came out to see it and called it beautiful. Some of them wept with relief. Children who had never known a starless sky laughed and pointed at the fading stars as if the whole night had been a festival staged for their benefit.

Edmund said nothing for a long time. He stood with his ruined hands folded on his walking stick, watching the false dawn climb, and when he finally spoke his voice was quiet enough that Oscar had to lean in to catch it.

"Fifteen years," he said, "I watched the sun come up over that hedge and it was always the same sun. Ordinary. Rude, even. Woke you whether you wanted waking or not." He turned his head, and his eyes — one of them clouded now, the price of a memory he wouldn't name — found Oscar's. "This one's polite. Do you see? This dawn is *asking permission*."

Oscar saw it. Once named, he couldn't unsee it. The light lay across the fields like a guest laying a coat over a chair — carefully, deferentially, taking up exactly the space offered and no more. Real dawn shoved. Real dawn spilled. This light waited to be invited.

"It's showing us what we want to see," Oscar said. "A gentle sky. A kept promise. It's dressing the cage in ribbons so we won't count the bars."

"And the worst part?" Edmund's mouth twisted, half-smile, half-wince. "I think it means it. I think it genuinely wants us comfortable. A farmer doesn't hate his pigs, Oscar. He keeps them warm."

The words landed in Oscar's chest like stones dropped down a well. He thought of the founders, sitting in their sealed archive in Vell, feeding a kingdom's memories to the dark and calling the silence that followed *victory*. He thought of Crane's eleven years of faithful service to a lie dressed as duty. He thought of himself, fifteen years selling luck-knots under a false name, telling himself that hiding was survival, that the buried gift was safe, that the dark he'd fled was content to be fled from.

Everything in his life had been this. Everything, always, had been dread wearing mercy's clothes.

The hum of the wards shifted again — softer now, almost a lullaby — and along the hedge-line the thorn-trees stirred without wind. New growth, impossible growth: the branches that had flared green-gold during the rite had put out buds in a single night, tight and pale, thousands of them, row upon row down the border like candles waiting to be lit.

"Buds," Oscar said. "In what month is this? What season?"

"None," Edmund said. "Thorn doesn't bud till spring, and spring's months away. Those aren't ours." He rested his hand, gently, on the nearest branch, the way you'd calm a horse. "It's growing them. On our side of the wall. It's planting something in our garden."

"For what?"

"For spring, I'd imagine." Edmund withdrew his hand. For a moment his composure slipped and Oscar saw underneath it: the boy who had been left behind, the man who had fed himself to a tree alone, the keeper who had just survived his own sacrifice and now had to survive the aftermath. He looked very tired, and very young, and very much like someone reading a letter he already knows is bad news. "Flowers open, Oscar. And things that grow flowers usually grow fruit after. And I would very much like to know what a thorn-tree fruits when the gardener is the thing on the other side of the wall."

* * *

By midmorning the village had decided, collectively and without discussion, that the danger was over.

Oscar watched it happen the way you watch a fever break in someone you love — with relief and horror in equal measure. It started small. Someone laughed in the lane. Someone else began sweeping their step, an ordinary broom, ordinary dust. By noon the tavern had opened, and by afternoon there were children playing at the well, actually playing, shrieking and chasing, and the sound of it was so human and so welcome that Oscar felt his own fear loosening its grip despite everything he knew.

That was the trick of it. That was the elegant, awful genius of it. The mercy was *restful*. After days of siege, of starless dark, of creatures made of other people's discarded memories crawling the hedgerows, the village had been offered peace, and peace was a warmth they sank into like a hot bath. Who wouldn't? Who could blame them?

Only three people in the village seemed immune, and they stood together in Aldith's cellar that afternoon, surrounded by her journals, and none of them pretended otherwise.

Crane had spread the founders' documents across the table — her copies from the Vell archive, cross-referenced against Aldith's handwriting until the two matched like broken halves of a seal. Edmund sat with his eyes closed, listening. Oscar stood at the window slit, watching the hedge-line, where the buds had begun, impossibly, to swell.

"Read me the last clause again," Edmund said.

Crane's finger found the line. "'In consideration of the cessation of hostilities,'" she read, in her flat Warden cadence, "'the undersigned parties agree to renewal of terms at intervals to be determined by the greater party, such renewal to include, but not be limited to, adjustment of payment, adjustment of boundaries, and adjustment of—'" She stopped.

"Of what?" Oscar asked, though he already knew. He'd read it that morning. He'd read it five times.

"'Adjustment of memory,'" Crane finished. "Whose memory isn't specified."

"Because it doesn't need to be," Edmund said, eyes still closed. "It never specifies. That's how it writes. The founders thought they were signing a treaty. They were signing a menu." He opened his eyes. "The last war ended because it ate the kingdom's stored memories and got sleepy. Our rite ended because we renamed it small and it got *patient*. Sleepy and patient are different animals, my friends. A sleeping thing wakes up groggy. A patient thing wakes up hungry, and it wakes up having had time to think about exactly what it wants next."

"The stars," Oscar said. "The new constellations. I think they're not messages. I think they're *bookkeeping*. It's reorganizing the ledger of the sky. Closing old accounts." He turned from the window. "And opening new ones."

Silence in the cellar. Dust motes drifted through the green-silver light from the window slit, and even the dust looked polite.

"There's one more thing," Crane said. She reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper — not archive paper, not journal paper, but fresh stock, Vell-made, the kind the Wardens used for official summons. "This was pinned to the village notice-board this morning. Nobody saw who put it there. Nobody in the village thinks it's strange, which frightens me more than anything else I've seen this week."

She unfolded it on the table among the dead founders' lies. The handwriting was fine, formal, and somehow familiar in a way Oscar couldn't place — familiar the way the new stars were familiar, known and unknown at once.

It read:

*To the Keepers of the Renewed Border, with thanks.*

*The account stands open. Payment deferred. Interest accrues.*

*A delegation will call at the flowering.*

*Please receive them kindly.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Below the final line, where a seal would go, someone had pressed a single thorn-bud, pale and tight and perfect, into the paper, and it had taken root there, in the dry Vell stock, and put out one small green leaf.

Edmund looked at it for a long moment. Then he began, very quietly, to laugh — the same laugh Oscar had heard when he'd first come home, the laugh that was half weeping, the laugh of a man who has carried a burden so long that even worse news lands on it with a kind of comfort.

"At the flowering," he said. "Well. At least it writes polite letters." He met Oscar's eyes across the table, and beneath the exhaustion and the humor there was steel, the same steel that had held a border alone for fifteen years. "We've got until spring, then. However long it decides that is."

Outside, above the village that had already forgotten to be afraid, the false stars wheeled in their wrong constellations, patient as bookkeepers, and the first of the thorn-buds, down along the hedge-line, quietly began to open.
