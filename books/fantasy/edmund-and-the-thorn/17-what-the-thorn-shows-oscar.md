## 17. What the Thorn Shows Oscar

The thorn-tree stood at the center of everything, as it always had, though Oscar had forgotten — had *made* himself forget — how large it truly was.

It rose from a mound of pale roots at the village's heart, behind Edmund's crooked house, and its trunk was wide as a granary, black-barked and seamed with scars that glowed faintly green when the wind moved its branches. There were no leaves this deep in winter. There were only thorns, thousands upon thousands of them, each one long as a finger and dark as iron, and between them hung things that made Oscar's breath catch: small lights, like fireflies caught in amber, drifting slow among the branches.

Memories. He knew it without being told. The wards were memory, and the tree held the wards, and the tree was dying, and so the lights guttered like lamps running out of oil.

Edmund stood beside him in the snow, wrapped in two blankets and still shivering, his cough held behind clenched teeth. "You don't have to touch it," he said. "Not yet. I only thought — you came all this way. You should know what you'd be giving yourself to."

"You said it shows what it holds."

"If it wants to. If it trusts you." Edmund's mouth twisted. "It's picky. It took me three years before it showed me anything but Aldith's last morning."

Oscar pulled off his glove. His bare hand looked absurdly small against all that black bark, all those waiting thorns.

"Fifteen years," he said quietly. "I owe it fifteen years of looking."

He pressed his palm to the tree.

* * *

The world went green.

Not the green of spring — the green of light through bottle glass, of foxfire, of the phosphorescent rot that glowed on drowned wood. He was standing somewhere without walls or sky, and the memories came at him not as pictures but as *weathers*, each one arriving whole, pressing against his skin like changes in the air.

A wedding. He felt it first as warmth: two young people under an elder-tree, garlands of hawthorn, a fiddle badly tuned, and the crowd's joy braided together into something the tree drank and kept and turned into ward-light. A birth — terror and triumph and the small furious cry of a child announcing itself, and the mother's exhausted laughter feeding the roots. A funeral, gray and slow, grief measured out over years rather than days, and even grief, he understood, was food. Even sorrow kept the border bright.

This was what Mother Aldith had built. Not a wall of force. A wall of *lived life*, harvested willingly, season by season, from people who never knew they were paying because they never missed what they gave. The best hours of their lives, skimmed like cream, poured into the roots.

And the founders of Vell had sold it. Sold the harvest, sold the harvesters' children, sold the whole slow miracle, to buy themselves one more winter of comfort.

Fury rose in him, hot and clean, and the tree seemed to lean into it, drinking that too.

Then the weathers changed, and he stopped being a guest among strangers' joys.

* * *

Snow. Always snow.

He was standing in Edmund's kitchen, except he wasn't standing anywhere; he was *inside* the memory, wearing it like a coat, seeing through Edmund's eyes. A younger Edmund — twenty, twenty-one, his hands already cracked from cold — sat alone at a table set for one, eating bread and salt pork by the light of a single tallow candle. The chair across from him was empty. It was always empty. In fifteen years of nights, Oscar realized, the chair had never once been filled.

*You could have someone*, Aldith had told him once, in another memory, her voice thin as paper. *No one would blame you, boy. No one would even know.*

*I'd know,* Edmund had answered. And that was all. That was the whole of it.

The memories came faster now, winter after winter, and Oscar could not look away and did not want to and could not bear to, all at once.

Edmund learning to bleed the tree properly, the way Aldith had taught — choosing which memories to feed, rationing himself, taking the dull ones first, the lonely ones, hoarding the sweet ones like a miser hoards coins. Edmund sick with fever for a month, delirious, crawling out into the snow anyway because the tree needed tending and there was no one else. Edmund sitting up all night with a rifle across his knees during the first starless autumn, watching the hedgerow where things moved that had no right to move, and no one to relieve him at dawn, because there was no dawn relief, there was never any relief, there was only Edmund, and the tree, and the dark.

Edmund writing letters he never sent. That one stopped Oscar's heart. He saw the words form under Edmund's pen — *Dear Oscar, the plums did well this year, I know you hated the plums, I planted them anyway* — and then saw the letter folded away into a tin box, and then another letter, and another, forty of them, sixty, none sent, all kept, because the tree couldn't take them. Some memories Edmund refused to feed it. He fed the tree his loneliness, his fear, his long gray years — but he kept every thought of Oscar for himself, paid for them out of his own failing flesh, let them eat him alive from the inside rather than give them up.

*Why?* Oscar wanted to scream into the green dark. *Why keep those? They're killing you.*

But he already knew why. Because they were the only warm thing in fifteen winters. Because a man can live on almost nothing if he keeps one coal lit.

Then the memories shifted, and the coal became a knife.

* * *

He was in the hedge-road at night, and he was seventeen, and he was running.

He had forgotten how it sounded — his own footsteps, frantic on frozen mud, his breath tearing out of him in white rags. He had forgotten the bundle on his back with everything he owned in it, and the way Vell's distant glow stained the clouds orange ahead of him like a promise, and most of all he had forgotten the moment he stopped. Just past the third milestone, where the hedgeroad bent and the village dropped out of sight behind him, he had stopped, and turned, and looked back.

In the memory, through Edmund's eyes — because this was Edmund's memory, Edmund had been there, Edmund had followed him as far as the milestone and no farther — Oscar watched himself turn around. Watched the boy he had been stand in the moonlight with his stolen satchel and his stolen freedom and his face full of shame and longing and the terrible arithmetic of a coward doing sums.

Watched himself open his mouth. Watched himself say nothing. Watched himself turn away and run, and run, and grow smaller, and vanish into the dark toward the orange-glowing city that had outlawed everything he was.

And then — this was the part that broke him — the memory did not end. Edmund had stood at that milestone until the cold drove him home, and in all the years since, whenever the tree grew hungry and Edmund had nothing left worth feeding it, he gave it this. He gave it the boy turning around. He gave it the boy saying nothing. He gave it the boy running, over and over, hundreds of times, and Oscar felt now what Edmund had felt every single time: not anger. Not bitterness.

*Tenderness.* A fierce, aching, protective tenderness, the kind you feel toward something small and wounded, and underneath it a single stubborn thought, repeated like a prayer across fifteen years:

*He'll come back. He'll come back when it matters. And when he does, I won't make him sorry.*

Oscar came back to himself on his knees in the snow, both hands pressed flat against the bark, weeping without any dignity whatsoever.

* * *

"Hey." Edmund's hand on his shoulder, careful, as if Oscar might shatter. "Hey. Breathe."

"He followed me." Oscar's voice came out wrecked. "To the milestone. You *followed me*, and I didn't even—"

"I followed everyone who left." Edmund lowered himself down beside him, into the snow, because his legs wouldn't hold him any longer and he'd stopped pretending otherwise. "Most of them I watched go and forgot by morning. Fed it straight to the tree, that kind. Easy food, leaving-memories. Sharp." He coughed, wet and long, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Yours I kept."

"Why?" It came out as an accusation, which was unfair, and Oscar knew it, and said it anyway. "Why would you keep *that*? Why torture yourself?"

"Because if I fed it away, I might stop believing you'd come back." Edmund said it simply, the way he said everything, as though the truth cost nothing, when Oscar knew it had cost him everything. "And somebody had to believe it. The tree couldn't. Aldith was dead. Somebody had to hold that one little piece of faith, or what was the point of any of it?"

Oscar knelt in the snow and understood, finally and completely, the size of what he had done. Not the leaving — he had punished himself for the leaving for fifteen years, worn it smooth as river stone, made it familiar. This was worse. This was the discovery that while he had been hiding in Vell, flinching from Warden patrols, selling luck-knots to people who could never know what they bought — while he had been merely *surviving* — Edmund had been *keeping*. Keeping the wards. Keeping the secret. Keeping a chair empty and a box of unsent letters and one memory of a boy at a milestone, polished by handling, tended like a flame.

"I'm sorry," Oscar said. It was inadequate. It was the only thing there was.

"I know." Edmund patted the bark beside him, companionable, absurd. "Tree knows too. It's been listening to you apologize for ten minutes. Thinks you're very repetitive."

Despite everything — despite the grief sitting on his chest like a gravestone — Oscar laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

* * *

They stayed until the cold drove them in, and then they sat in Edmund's kitchen, at the table with the two chairs, and Oscar filled the empty one, and neither of them remarked on it.

The tin box sat between them. Edmund opened it and dealt the letters out like cards, and read some aloud — the ones about the plums, about the roof leaking, about the year the creek froze so hard the miller skated on it — and Oscar listened, and grieved, and slowly, grain by grain, something in him finished setting.

Because here was the thing the tree had shown him, beneath all the weathers and winters, the thing he had carried out of the green light like a coal cupped in both hands: the wards were not power. Power was what the founders had wanted, power was what they'd sold the kingdom's soul to keep, and look where that had gotten everyone. The wards were *attention*. Someone watching. Someone staying. Fifteen years of one stubborn man refusing to leave a chair empty in his heart, and that — that alone — had held the dark off a starving kingdom longer than it had any right to.

One keeper, half-dead, had nearly sufficed.

Two keepers, willing, sharing the price—

Oscar's eyes went to the journals stacked on the sideboard, Aldith's cramped handwriting filling their margins, and to the final page he had found only that morning, the page Edmund hadn't known existed: *the ward need not take everything; shared between two keepers, willingly given, the cost divides; but no one has ever tried it, and I am too old to be the first.*

No one had ever tried it.

"No one's ever tried it," Oscar said aloud.

Edmund looked up from a letter, wary. He knew that tone. He'd heard it from Aldith, probably, in the years before Oscar was born. "Tried what?"

"The rite. Two keepers." Oscar laid his hand flat on the table, on the wood, on the ordinary mortal wood, and felt his own pulse hammering under it. "You were going to do it alone tonight. Don't lie, I saw your face when Crane asked how long the tree has left. Days. Maybe hours." He leaned forward. "So we do it together. Tonight. Before the dark figures out the door's unlocked."

"Oscar—"

"It divides the cost, Edmund. *Willingly shared.* Aldith wrote it down. She was too old, too alone, too proud to ask anyone — but she wrote it down, she left it like a key under the mat for whoever came home." His voice shook, and he let it. "I came home."

Edmund stared at him for a long moment. Outside, beyond the frost-furred window, the starless dark pressed close against the hedgerows, and somewhere out in it, patient as hunger, the thing that had eaten a kingdom's memory was waiting for the last light to fail.

"You understand what you're offering," Edmund said at last, very quietly. "Even split, it takes. Faces. Years. Whole stretches of who you are. You'll wake up with holes in you, and I'll wake up with bigger ones, and neither of us will remember agreeing to this."

"Then we'd better write it down first," Oscar said. "Both our names. What we chose, and why. So whoever wakes up can read it and know they weren't robbed."

Something moved through Edmund's face then — hope, maybe, or fear, or the two of them tangled past separating, the way they had been tangled for thirty years. He reached across the table and gripped Oscar's wrist, hard, his fingers burning with fever.

"Moonset," he said. "We tell Crane. We tell the tree. And God forgive us both — we begin."

Above the house, above the dead stars, the darkness leaned in to listen.
