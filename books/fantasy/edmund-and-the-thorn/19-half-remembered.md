## 19. Half-Remembered

He woke to the smell of sap and the sound of someone breathing his name wrong.

"Oss—Oscar. *Oscar.*" The voice stumbled over it like a man crossing a stream on wet stones. "Is that right? Is that your name?"

Oscar opened his eyes.

The sky was wrong. He knew that immediately, before he knew anything else — before he knew his own hands, which lay pale against a bed of woven rushes; before he knew the room, low-beamed and smelling of woodsmoke and dried yarrow. The sky beyond the window was full of stars, but they hung in patterns no sailor or shepherd had ever drawn, constellations like spilled salt, like a sentence written in a language that had been dead for a thousand years. Green-gold light still pulsed faintly along the horizon, the way a bell keeps humming after the clapper falls silent.

The wards were lit. He could feel them — no, he couldn't. That was the thing. He could feel where they had been, the way an amputee feels a hand.

"Edmund," he said, and the name came easily, which surprised him, because so little else did.

A shape moved in the chair beside the bed. Edmund Hale — thinner than the tree had shown him, grayer, but alive, alive, his chest rising and falling with the stubborn rhythm of a man who had been dying for fifteen years and had apparently decided not to make a habit of it. His eyes were open and fixed on Oscar with an expression of desperate concentration.

"I know you," Edmund said. It did not sound like recognition. It sounded like a man reading a map by lightning-flashes. "I know I know you. Your name is Oscar. You ran away when we were — when we were young. There was a hedge. There was a woman called—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "There was a woman."

"Aldith," Oscar said.

Something went through Edmund's face like wind through wheat — grief, relief, terror, all at once. "Yes," he whispered. "Yes. Aldith." And then, horribly, hopefully: "Who was she?"

* *

They sat up together through what might have been night and might have been something else, because the stars outside were rearranged and time felt like water running uphill. They took inventory the way soldiers take inventory after a battle: item by item, naming what survived.

Oscar had his hands. That was the first discovery — he still knew knots, still knew how to braid luck into cord, how to steep feverfew, how to whisper a charm into the space behind a child's ear. Fifteen years of craft lived in his fingers even where it had died in his head. When he flexed them, they moved with knowledge his mind couldn't account for, a stranger's muscle-memory wearing his skin.

He had Edmund. Not the memories — those were mostly gone, burned away like fog off a morning field — but the *fact* of him, the way you know your own heartbeat. Whatever the thorn had taken, it had left the load-bearing walls standing: *this person matters. This person stayed.*

What he didn't have was worse, and it got worse the longer he looked for it.

His mother's face. He reached for it and found a warm shapeless glow where a face should have been — he knew she had laughed, knew she had hummed while she worked, knew the exact texture of being small and safe, and none of it had a picture attached. He knew he had run away at seventeen because he'd been afraid, and he could remember the fear perfectly, sharp as a thorn-tip, but the reason for it was a hole shaped like a reason.

And Vell. Fourteen years in Vell, gone like breath off glass. He knew he had sold charms under a false name. He could not remember the false name. He could not remember the room he'd slept in, the customers' faces, the taste of the city's bad wine, the particular gray of its winter light. A whole life, and all that remained was the summary: *I hid. I was lonely. I was good at hiding.*

"It's fair," Edmund said quietly, when Oscar told him this. "That's the joke of it. You spent fourteen years building a life out of pretending, and now the pretending's just — gone. All that's left is true things."

"What's left for you?" Oscar asked, and then wished he hadn't, because Edmund's silence answered before his words did.

Edmund counted on his fingers, slowly, like a child learning arithmetic. "The tree. The rite. Mother Aldith's hands — I remember her hands. The winters. Feeding the roots." He paused. "Your name. I kept your name. I don't remember keeping it, but I have it, like a stone in my pocket." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and laughing all at once. "I gave more. I fed it deeper. I thought — going in, I thought one of us should keep everything, and it shouldn't be me, because I'd only keep the winters anyway. So I drank first. And now I can't remember why I thought that was a kindness."

"You wanted to protect me," Oscar said. "You've always wanted to protect me. Even from myself. Especially from myself."

Edmund stared at him. "Did I?"

"Yes." Oscar said it with total certainty, and then heard himself, and started to laugh — a cracked, helpless sound that hurt his ribs. "Gods forgive us. We traded our pasts to a tree and I'm telling you your own motives. We're two old men of twenty-five and thirty-two, sitting here remembering each other on behalf of each other."

Edmund laughed too, and then stopped, and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "It's not funny."

"No," Oscar agreed. "It's not."

But they were both still half-laughing, the way people do at funerals, the way people do when the alternative is screaming.

* *

Somewhere below them, in the village that had forgotten its own name, a dog began to bark, and did not stop.

They noticed it the way you notice weather — distantly, at first. Then the other dogs joined it, and then the geese, and then there were voices, human voices, pitched in the register that has no words in any language, the sound people make when the world stops making sense.

Edmund stood too fast and swayed. Oscar caught his arm — caught it without thinking, the way you catch a thing you've caught before — and for a moment they just held each other upright, two halves of one debt.

"The border," Edmund said. "It's holding. I can feel—" He frowned, pressed his palm flat against his own sternum as though listening to a distant drum. "Holding. But it's *thin*. Like ice over a river in spring. Something's leaning on it."

"The thing the founders fed," Oscar said. The knowledge surfaced whole, cold and complete — Aldith's journals, the founders' bargain, the kingdom's memories poured down a hungry throat to buy a peace that was really just a delay. He remembered all of it. Of course he did. He had poured it into the thorn himself, hours ago, years ago, a lifetime ago. "It didn't get its meal. We cheated it. It's come to collect."

"Then it can wait until I can stand without falling over," Edmund said, with a flash of the stubbornness that must have carried him through fifteen solitary winters, and Oscar loved him for it so suddenly and so completely that it frightened him.

They made it down the stairs together, Edmund's weight against his shoulder, and out into the star-wrong night.

* *

The village had gathered at the edge of the green, near the great thorn-tree, whose branches now blazed faintly green-gold from root to crown, every thorn tipped with light like a candle-flame seen through smoked glass. The villagers stood in a rough half-circle, pitchforks and billhooks in their fists, faces turned upward toward the impossible stars. An old woman was praying. Several children were crying. Nobody was running, which Oscar thought was the bravest thing he'd ever seen from people who could not remember why they were afraid.

Crane stood between the villagers and the tree.

She looked wrecked — her Warden's gray coat scorched along one sleeve, ash in her cropped hair, a shallow cut across her cheekbone that she had clearly decided wasn't worth mentioning. In her hands she held her Warden's iron, the Unmaking-rod, but she held it low and loose, the way you hold a tool you're no longer sure how to use.

"You're awake," she said, when she saw them. No relief in it. No blame either. Just report. "The wards flared at moonset. Green-gold, visible from the hills. Then they settled." She turned her head slightly, listening to something Oscar couldn't hear. "And then something on the other side started *pushing*."

"Can you feel it?" Edmund asked.

"I can feel it in my teeth." She said it flatly, but her knuckles were white on the rod. "Fifteen years I served the Unmaking. I was taught the things beyond the border are errors. Mistakes. Things the world forgot on purpose, and the Wardens exist to keep forgotten." Her jaw tightened. "Nobody ever taught me they could be *hungry*."

The pressure came again while she spoke. Oscar felt it this time — a slow, vast lean against the inside of his skull, the way you feel a change in weather before the clouds arrive. The green-gold light along the thorn dimmed, flickered, steadied. Somewhere far away, beyond the fields, beyond the hills, something enormous shifted its weight.

The old woman's prayer faltered. The dogs had gone silent — which was worse than the barking.

"It's testing us," Edmund murmured. "Like a wolf circling a fence. Looking for the weak board."

"Then we find the strong ones," Oscar said. "We found them once tonight already." He looked down at his hand, at the faint green stain of sap still sunk into the creases of his palm, and then at Edmund, who was looking at the same hand. "Two keepers. Half a price each. If the thorn needs feeding again—"

"Don't," Crane said sharply. "Not yet. Not unless—"

"Not unless it comes to that," Oscar finished. "I know. Believe me, Warden, I am in no hurry to give that tree another course of my life. I've got very little left, and most of what remains belongs to him." He nodded at Edmund, who had the grace to look embarrassed.

But even as he said it, Oscar was reaching — carefully, gingerly, the way you probe a bruise — for whatever the thorn would show him. Because there had been a moment, at the bottom of the rite, when the tree had opened to him like a book, and he had understood that it was not just a wall. It was a *library*. Everything the kingdom had ever stored in it. Every memory the founders had sold. And somewhere in those shelves, if the thing beyond the border was made of discarded memories—

Then maybe memories were not only its food.

Maybe they were also its name.

"Edmund," he said slowly. "When Aldith built the wards. Did she ever write about *naming* things? About what names do?"

Edmund blinked at him. "She wrote about everything. Why?"

"Because I think I just remembered something important," Oscar said, "and I'd very much like to remember the rest of it before that thing finishes circling the fence."

Above them, in constellations no one had ever drawn, the wrong stars wheeled slowly — and one of them, low on the northern horizon, went out.
