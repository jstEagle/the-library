## 22. Edmund and the Thorn

The first thing I remembered of that spring was the smell of the earth turning over.

I had spent so many winters with my nose full of blood and sap and the copper tang of a dying tree that I had forgotten soil could smell like anything else — like rain about to happen, like worms waking, like the green insistence of things that intended to live whether or not anyone remembered them. I stood at the edge of the thorn's shadow with my hands in the dirt up to the wrist, and I laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough passed, as it always did now, leaving me lighter instead of emptier.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. I had given the tree fifteen years of myself in slow sips, and then, on the last night, I had opened the whole vein of me and let it drink. I should have been nothing. A coat with no man inside it. Instead I woke in the grey of that endless morning missing my mother's face and the taste of plums and every winter between my twelfth year and my twentieth — and found that what remained was enough to stand on. Less than a whole man. But *wholly* present, as Oscar liked to say, pressing his shoulder against mine as if to prove we both still had shoulders.

"Edmund." His voice came from the far side of the tree, where he'd been talking to the roots again. He talked to them the way some men talk to horses — low, constant, half nonsense. "Come look at this."

I came around the trunk. The great thorn filled the hollow below the churchyard the way it always had, black-limbed and patient, its bark scarred with old lightning and older griefs. But this spring it was flowering. It had never flowered before. Small white blossoms clustered along the branches like stars that had come down to rest after their long wrongness in the sky, and among them moved bees — actual bees, fat and drowsy, the first any of us had seen since the stars went out.

Oscar crouched at the base of the trunk, and there, pushing up through the leaf-mould not three hands' width from the old roots, was a seedling. A thorn sapling, thin as a whip, two leaves spread wide, trembling in a wind too gentle to trouble anything.

"It came up on its own," he said. "Or the tree made it. I can't tell which, and I've stopped pretending there's a difference."

I knelt beside him. My knees cracked like green wood in a fire, and he winced on my behalf, which was its own small comedy — the hedge-witch's apprentice who had buried his gift alive for fifteen years now flinching at the ordinary sounds of an ordinary old body. We were both less than we had been. That was the price, and we had paid it together, split down the middle like a loaf, and neither of us had starved.

"Do you remember," I said, "when you ran away?"

He went still. It was one of the few memories either of us had recovered intact, because the tree had kept it — kept it tenderly, wrapped in sap like a fly in amber, and when I touched the bark that first morning after the rite, it had played itself back to me unasked: a boy of seventeen slipping out a window with his boots in his hand, looking back once at the lit kitchen where I sat mending a harness, and the look on his face had not been freedom. It had been apology, already forming, already too late.

"I remember the window," Oscar said quietly. "I remember the frost on the sill. I don't remember why I thought Vell would want me."

"Vell didn't deserve you."

"Vell didn't know what it had outlawed." He touched the seedling's leaf with one finger, and the leaf leaned toward him, the way flowers lean toward light. "Isolde says the city's remembering now. Slowly. Like a man waking from a fever who isn't sure yet whether he wants to be awake."

* * *

The village remembered its name on a Tuesday.

I know it was a Tuesday because Marta Hale — no relation, though she claimed one loudly and often — came pounding down the lane waving her arms and shouting before she even reached us. She was the miller's widow, sixty if she was a day, and she had spent the whole winter unable to say what village she lived in. None of us could. We called it *the village*, or *home*, or sometimes just *here*, and the word sat in our mouths like a stone with the shape worn off.

"It came back!" she cried, skidding in the mud at the foot of the thorn. "It came back in the night! I dreamed of my grandmother's kitchen and when I woke it was *there*, the name, sitting in my head like it had never left — Thistlecombe! We're Thistlecombe! We were always Thistlecombe!"

She said it over and over, laughing and crying at once, and by noon the whole village had caught it like a cold. Old Tam the smith wept into his ale because he suddenly remembered his father's face, which he had lost two years ago along with everything else the founders' bargain had eaten. Children who had gone nameless in the registers were named again by mothers who woke in the night with the words on their tongues. The church bell rang until the rope frayed.

Not everything came back. The war stayed dark, mostly — the last one, the real one, the one the founders had bought their way out of with a kingdom's memory. And the thing beyond the border stayed dark entirely, which was a mercy, though Isolde Crane, when she passed through on her rounds, said it was also a warning.

"It's not forgiveness," she told us, standing under the flowering thorn with her Warden's grey coat brushed clean but her badge torn off. "It's patience. What we fed it on the last night — the names, the renaming — it swallowed those too. Everything we give it, it keeps. One day it will hand our own words back to us, and we'll have to be ready to hear them."

"You're a cheerful guest," Oscar said.

"I'm an honest one. It's new. I'm still practicing." But she smiled when she said it, and she stayed for supper, and she left a copy of the founders' journals with Marta Hale, sealed in wax, "so that Thistlecombe never again forgets what was done in its name." The village built a shelf for it in the church, next to the register book, and children were taught both — the names of the dead, and the names of the liars — and taught that these were the same lesson.

Crane had remade the Wardens, they said in Vell. Not disbanded — remade. Wardens of truth now, archivists and auditors, sworn to dig rather than bury. Some of the old guard had refused and been refused in turn; some had wept with relief. She spoke of it the way converts speak, carefully, as if the ground might still shift under her. Her faith in the Unmaking had cracked the night she stood before the thorn and asked what she could do, and what grew in the crack was something sturdier, because it had to be chosen every morning instead of merely inherited.

* * *

We planted the second thorn at moonset in late April, because Aldith's journals said moonset, and because Oscar had developed a superstition about honoring her instructions even the trivial ones. "She's dead," I told him. "She won't know if we plant at noon."

"She'll know," he said. "Dead teachers always know. It's the only power they have left and they use it ruthlessly."

We dug the hole together at the eastern edge of the hollow, twenty paces from the mother tree, close enough that the roots would find each other within a season and far enough that each would keep its own shape. The sapling we took was the one that had come up unbidden, and Oscar carried it cradled against his chest the whole way across the grass like a sleeping child, murmuring to it. I recognized the murmur. It was half hedge-craft and half nonsense, and it was exactly what Mother Aldith used to sing to her cuttings, and hearing it come out of him did something to my chest that I chose not to examine.

"Say the words with me," he said.

"I don't remember the words."

"You remember some of them. You kept the tree alive alone for fifteen years; you remember more than you think." He held the sapling over the hole. "Start anywhere. The hedge fills in the gaps. It always did."

So I started anywhere. I said the parts I knew — *root to root, wall to wall, what one holds the other recalls* — and where my memory went dark, his voice came in underneath like a bass note, and where his went dark, mine rose, and the words braided themselves whole between us. Neither of us knew the entire charm. Together we knew all of it. I looked at him over the little tree and saw that he understood the same thing I did, at the same moment, and we both laughed, and the laughter didn't turn into a cough this time.

We set the roots in. We packed the earth with our hands, no tools, because Aldith had written *the tree should know your touch before it knows your iron*. Oscar poured water from the well — the sweet well, the one whose water had tasted of nothing all winter and now tasted faintly of iron and honey — and I pressed my palm flat against the sapling's single stem, and I felt it take hold. Not dramatically. There was no flare of green-gold light, no bell-sound. Just a small tightening, a grip, like a child's hand closing around a finger. Two roots. One wall.

"There," Oscar said softly. "Now when one of us is having a bad decade, the other can pick up the slack."

"That was your bad decade. I was having a bad fifteen years."

"We'll split the next one."

We walked back to the house in the dark, and the dark was kind. Above us the stars burned in patterns no one remembered, constellations with no names yet, and the village had begun calling them new things — the Ploughwife, the Two Brothers, the Long Row of the Remembered Dead. Astronomers would come eventually, from Vell, with their instruments and their wardens-of-truth credentials, and they would chart the sky properly and give the stars serious names. But the village names would stick. Serious names always do; true ones last longer.

At the door — the door that had known my name when I was eleven and still knows it, though I sometimes have to stand there a moment waiting for it to come to me — Oscar paused.

"Edmund. Do you ever miss it? The pieces you gave away?"

I considered honestly, which was another thing I was practicing, having learned it from watching him fail at it first.

"I miss my mother's face," I said. "I miss it every day, and it doesn't hurt less for being shared. But here's what I have instead." I put my hand flat on the door, and it swung open onto firelight and the smell of bread, because Oscar had learned to bake in the winter out of pure stubbornness and had gotten alarmingly good at it. "I have a house that isn't empty. I have a village that knows its own name. I have a tree that flowers. And I have you, standing in my doorway arguing with me about whether to plant at moonset, which is a conversation I would not trade for every memory the founders ever sold."

He ducked his head the way he did when a kindness landed too directly — fifteen years in Vell had taught him to deflect, and he was unlearning it slowly, one undeflected kindness at a time.

"The bread's burning," he said, and went inside, and I followed.

* * *

Summer came, and the second thorn took, and the mother tree dropped its blossoms and set fruit — small bitter crabapples of thorn-fruit that the old books said could be made into a preserve that restored one specific memory per jar. Oscar made twelve jars. We ate them slowly, ceremonially, one a month, trading back and forth: he gave me the taste of plums, and I gave him a rainy afternoon in Aldith's kitchen when she'd let him fail at a charm and laugh about it. We gave each other back our mothers' faces last, on midwinter's eve, and wept, and it was good weeping, the kind that waters something.

The border held. Green-gold in the deep places, quiet at the surface, and the things beyond it quiet too — waiting, as Crane said, but waiting is not hunger, and hunger is not victory, and we had learned the difference at a cost we were still counting. Some nights I walked the hedgeroad alone, just to the first bend and back, and the blackberry and elder whispered to me the way they always had, fragments of other people's yesterdays caught in their thorns. I listened the way you listen to rain. Not for information. For company.

And on a morning in the second spring, when I came down to the hollow with the watering pails, I found Oscar already there, kneeling in the grass between the two trees, and growing in the turf around him — dozens of them, scattered like spilled seed — were new thorn seedlings, rising everywhere the mother tree's roots had reached, reaching toward the young tree's roots in turn, a lattice beginning, a wall becoming a weave.

He looked up at me, and his face was doing several things at once, the way it did when wonder and terror arrived in the same post.

"Edmund," he said. "They're not stopping at the border."

I followed his finger east, past the hollow, past the churchyard, down the long slope toward the low hills where the old wards used to end — and there, marching away from us across the open country in a ragged green line, thorn seedlings were coming up in the hedgerows, in the ditches, in the cracks of the abandoned boundary stones, mile after mile, following the ghost of the old border like ivy following a wall that no longer existed.

The tree was not content to hold the line anymore.

The tree was going to build a garden.

Oscar started to laugh — that helpless, delighted, slightly frightened laugh I had missed for fifteen years without knowing its name — and I sat down in the wet grass beside him among the seedlings, an old keeper and a half-remembered witch, and I laughed with him until the sun cleared the hills and lit the whole flowering road gold.
