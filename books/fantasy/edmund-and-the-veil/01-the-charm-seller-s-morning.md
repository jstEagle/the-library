## 1. The Charm-Seller's Morning

The bells of Greyhollow rang the hour before dawn the way they always did — badly. Three of them, cast from different foundries in different centuries, had never agreed on a single note between them, and so the city woke each morning to a sound like a quarrel in a churchyard. Sebastian Fairweather had slept through worse. He slept through this, too, until a cold nose pressed itself against his ear.

"Up," said the fox.

Sebastian pulled the blanket over his head. "It's not light."

"It's not light because you live in a cellar with one window the size of a hymn book. It's past light everywhere else." The nose returned, colder, more insistent, wedging under the blanket's edge like a debt collector. "Also, there are crows on your roof. Nine of them. They've been arguing about you since before the bells."

"They argue about everyone."

"They don't follow everyone to work."

That got him up. He sat on the edge of his cot in the grey half-dark, rubbing his eyes with the heels of his hands, and listened — really listened, the way he did when the day promised to be complicated — to the small chorus of Greyhollow waking around him. The rats in the wall behind the flour bin were gossiping about a cat that had moved into the tannery lane, a big one, silent, with a taste for ambition. The pigeons under the eaves were complaining about a boy with an airgun. And somewhere above, on the roof slates, nine crows were indeed having what could only be called a meeting.

He couldn't understand them the way he understood speech. It wasn't words. It was more like standing in a room where everyone spoke a language made of weather — moods and intentions arriving all at once, whole and unmistakable. From the crows he got a picture: black wings over a square, a crowd, something bright being nailed to wood. A proclamation. The image carried a flavor he'd learned to dread, the taste of iron filings and old fear.

"They're excited about something," he said.

"The crows? Crows are excited about corpses and weddings, and it's often hard to tell which is which." The fox sat in the middle of the cellar floor, tail wrapped over his feet, orange as a struck match even in the dark. His name was Sorrel, though he had never told Sebastian this himself; Sebastian had bought him from a peddler three years ago for two coppers and a pork pie, and the peddler had sworn the animal was named anything but. "Get dressed. You have charms to sell, and I have a reputation to maintain by pretending I don't know you."

"You're my fox. People expect you to be here."

"People expect a lot of things. That's why most of them die disappointed." But when Sebastian pulled on his coat, the fox was already at the door, waiting, which was as close to affection as Sorrel ever admitted to in public.

* * *

Greyhollow at dawn was a city holding its breath. Fog came off the river Mole and pooled in the lower streets like spilled milk, and out of it rose the gables and chimney-pots and leaning signposts of the Tanners' Quarter, where Sebastian had kept his stall for six years. By day the quarter smelled of lye and hides and horse. At this hour it smelled only of wet stone and coal smoke, and the gaslamps still burned in their cages, each one wrapped in its own private halo.

His stall stood under the broken arch of the old wool exchange, a plank table, a striped awning gone the color of dishwater, and a hand-painted sign: FAIRWEATHER'S REMEDIES & SMALL FORTUNES. Everything on the table was honest rubbish. Willow-bark tea for headaches. A salve of goose fat and comfrey that genuinely worked, because goose fat and comfrey worked whether or not anyone blessed them. Dried lavender in sachets stitched by his own crooked hand. And then the rest — the luck-charms, the love-knots, the little tin hares for safe travel — which were also honest rubbish, because Sebastian Fairweather sold no magic at all.

He couldn't afford to. Nobody in Greyhollow could. Magic had been outlawed for two hundred years, ground out of the city generation by generation, until the word itself had gone soft and embarrassing, something spoken of the way one spoke of plague pits or the old king's madness. There were no witches in Greyhollow. There were healers, midwives, herbwives, and charm-sellers, and the difference between a charm-seller and a fraud was mostly a matter of how good his lavender was.

Sebastian's lavender was excellent. So was his patter.

"Morning, Mrs. Hobb!" he called, as the first shape resolved out of the fog — a broad woman in a fishmonger's apron, pushing a barrow with one wheel that sang a single mournful note. "How's the hip?"

"How d'you think?" she said. "Raining in the bone."

"Comfrey salve. Last jar till Thursday. I'll not tell you it works miracles, because miracles are above my station, but I'll tell you Widow Crane swears by it, and Widow Crane has buried three husbands and outlived every doctor in the parish, so she knows a thing about endurance."

Mrs. Hobb bought the jar. She always bought the jar. Behind her came the day proper: apprentices with bread under their arms, dockhands smelling of tar, a clerk whose cough had opinions, two girls who giggled over the love-knots without buying any, which was the correct way to treat love-knots. Sebastian sold, joked, flattered, remembered names, remembered hips and coughs and dead husbands, and all the while the fox lay under the table like a discarded coat, one eye open, doing nothing whatsoever and being worth more than the whole stock.

Because here was the secret of Fairweather's Remedies & Small Fortunes, the secret no customer ever guessed and no Veilwarden would ever have believed: the animals told him everything.

Not on purpose, mostly. The sparrows under the awning chattered about who had quarreled with whom. The cart-horses on Mole Street announced their drivers' debts and drinking. The rats — God help him, the rats knew everything, saw everything, and had the moral discernment of a dropped pie. When Mrs. Hobb's husband had been cheating on her two winters back, it was a rat in her own pantry who'd described the woman from the dye-house in such loving detail that Sebastian had spent a week constructing an elaborate fiction about omens to steer Mrs. Hobb toward the truth without getting anyone hanged. When the baker on Cordwainer Row was going to fail, the mice had known a month early, because the mice knew the flour was being watered. Sebastian's stall thrived not on magic but on the oldest magic there was: information, freely given by creatures nobody bothered to lie to.

And if sometimes, late at night, a moth landed on his hand and he felt its whole brief world unfold against his skin like a letter read in a single glance — well. That was between him and the moths.

* * *

Midmorning brought the sun, thin and apologetic, and with it the crows.

They didn't land. That was the first wrong thing. City crows landed; they strutted, they begged, they stole. These nine circled the wool exchange arch in a slow wheel, once, twice, and then settled along the ridge of the roof opposite, in a row, spaced with a formality that made the hair on Sebastian's neck stand up. Nine crows sitting in a row is not a flock. It's a jury.

Sorrel lifted his head from under the table. "Told you," he said, without moving his lips, which was a trick Sebastian had never asked him to explain.

"What do they want?"

"To be looked at. Go on. Look at them. They've been rehearsing."

Sebastian wiped his hands on his apron, stepped out from behind the stall, and looked up. The nearest crow — big, scarred at the breast, one eye clouded like a bad pearl — dipped its head and gave him the picture again, deliberately this time, pressing it into his mind the way a child presses a stamp into wax. Black wings. A square. A crowd pressed tight as wool. Something bright hammered onto a board, and beneath it, spreading through the crowd like ink through water, a feeling he had no better name for than *the end of something*.

Then the crow did something no crow should do. It looked at him — not the flat yellow stare of a bird, but a look, aimed, weighted — and it said, in the voice of intention rather than words:

*Fairweather. Listen.*

"I'm listening," Sebastian murmured. Mrs. Hobb, passing with her jar, gave him the tolerant look Greyhollow reserved for men who talked to themselves.

*Tomorrow,* said the crow. *Proclamation Square. All the gifted. All the marked.* And underneath the intention, faint as a watermark, came an image that stopped his breath: a hand, human, wearing a white glove, drawing a circle in the air — and inside the circle, briefly, horribly, a shape like a door.

The crows rose all at once, nine black hinges swinging open, and were gone south over the rooftops.

Sebastian stood in the street with his heart going like a drum in a parade. Around him the market went on — a man argued about the price of onions, a child chased a hoop, the fog burned off the river — and none of them had heard what he'd heard, because there had been nothing to hear. Only a bird behaving strangely, and a charm-seller staring after it like a man who'd seen his own funeral.

"Sorrel," he said quietly. "What's tomorrow?"

The fox emerged from under the table, stretched with theatrical slowness, and sat. For a moment — just a moment — his amber eyes held something older than a peddler's fox had any right to hold, something that made Sebastian think, not for the first time, of library dust and closed doors.

"Tomorrow," Sorrel said, "is the first of Frostmonth. Which means nothing to you, because you sleep through half your life. But to certain parties, it is a convenient date for announcements." He turned his head toward the south, toward the high quarter where the crown's offices stood behind their iron fences. "There were wagons on the bridge road last night. Covered ones. I counted axles. Eleven wagons, heavy-laden, coming into the city at midnight, escorted by riders in grey."

"Veilwardens don't ride in grey. They ride in white."

"These weren't Veilwardens." Sorrel began walking toward the stall, unhurried, tail level. "These wore no colors at all. Which is either very humble or very confident, and nothing about eleven loaded wagons at midnight is humble." He paused at the table's leg and looked up. "You should sell your lavender, Sebastian. Sell all of it. And then you should come home early, bolt the door, and ask yourself who among your charming customers might be about to become a problem."

"That's not ominous at all."

"I'm a fox. Ominous is free."

But Sebastian barely heard him, because across the square, coming down Cordwainer Row with the fog still clinging to his hem, was a figure he knew. Tall, spare, dressed in good grey wool with a silver chain at the collar — the chain of a Warden's clerk, though Master Aldous Vane had not worn his chain in public for years. He walked like a man carrying a full cup, carefully, and his eyes found Sebastian across the crowded square with the certainty of a hawk finding a vole.

Vane had been his patron once. Had paid for this stall, in fact, or at least for the license that let it exist. Had taught him which palms to grease and which doors knocked politely. Had never, in six years, come down to the Tanners' Quarter uninvited.

The crowd parted around the clerk without quite noticing they were doing it. He arrived at the stall, set both gloved hands on the plank, and smiled the smile of a man about to deliver news he had been practicing.

"Master Fairweather," he said. "Close your stall. There's something you need to hear before the whole city does — and I would rather you heard it from me than from a bell."

Behind Sebastian, low under the noise of the market, Sorrel made a sound he had never made before: a long, slow exhale, almost like a sigh, almost like a word.

"So it begins," said the fox.
