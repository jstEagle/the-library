## 2. What the Crows Knew

The crows of Greyhollow woke before the bells did.

Sebastian heard them from his cot above the charm-shop, a ragged chorus gathering on the rooftop across Gilders Lane, and he lay still for a long moment with his eyes closed, listening the way other men listened to weather. Crows spoke a language of angles and appetites — *food here, danger there, the tall one with the stick again* — but this morning their chatter had a strange grain to it, a frequency beneath the frequency, like a bell struck slightly off its true note.

*They're counting us,* said an old crow with a voice like a hinge. She was the matriarch of the cathedral spire flock, and she held court every dawn above the chandler's shed. *The tall ones with the grey cloaks. Counting the chimneys. Counting the doors.*

*Why count doors?* asked a younger bird, all impatience and hunger.

*Because someone means to open them,* she said, *or break them down. Doors are only counted by men who intend to come through.*

Sebastian opened his eyes. Grey light seeped through the warped shutter, striping the floorboards, catching dust motes that hung like suspended snow. He could hear Tam already below, clanking about among the jars and copper wire, humming his tuneless morning hymn to Saint Ferrow the Unlettered. The smell of tallow and dried lavender drifted up through the floor gaps, ordinary and warm, and for a moment the crows' words seemed like nothing more than crow-words — the anxious arithmetic of birds who saw omens in every shadow because shadows meant hawks.

But he had been listening to Greyhollow's animals for twenty-six years now, ever since the winter his mother found him sitting in the frozen garden telling the hedgehog its own name, and he had learned the difference between a bird's noise and a bird's news.

He dressed quickly, pulled on the coat with the frayed cuffs that he pretended not to be embarrassed by, and went down into the shop.

* * *

Tam Hobb was sixty if he was a day, a barrel-chested man with forearms like mooring rope and a face weathered into permanent good humor. He'd been Sebastian's father's partner once upon a time, back when Fairweather & Hobb sold charms openly, back when the sign above the door read something prouder than *TALLOW, WICKS & SUNDRIES*. Now the charms were hidden behind false bottoms and mislabeled drawers, and the sign was a lie both men had agreed to live inside.

"You look like you slept in your boots," Tam said without looking up. He was sorting wick-cord into bundles, his thick fingers moving with surprising delicacy. "Bad dreams?"

"Crows," Sebastian said. "They say grey-cloaked men are counting doors in the Warrens."

Tam's hands did not stop moving, but they slowed, just barely — a hitch no one but Sebastian would have caught. "Crows say a lot of things."

"They were right about the granary fire. They were right about the ferryman."

"The ferryman," Tam said, "was right about himself, seeing as he did it." He set down the wick-cord and finally looked up. His eyes were pale hazel, almost colorless, and they had gone very still. "Seb. Listen to me. Every autumn the Wardens walk the low quarters and count things. Chimneys, doors, heads, hearths. It's what they do instead of doing anything useful. It doesn't mean a purge. It hasn't meant a purge in nine years."

"Nine years ago it nearly did."

"And didn't." Tam's voice stayed level, but he came around the counter and put one broad hand on Sebastian's shoulder, and the weight of it was its own kind of sentence. "Your father taught me something the year before the Repeal. He said: *a man with a secret gift has two choices — he can spend his whole life flinching at shadows, or he can decide which shadows are worth flinching at.* Your gift is small, Seb. A whisper to a dog. A word to a crow. No flames off your fingertips, no storms. If it comes to lists, you'll be so far down the list we'll all be dust first."

Sebastian wanted to believe him. That was the trouble with Tam — he made believing feel like warmth from a stove, easy and immediate, and you only noticed later how carefully he'd built the fire.

"Then why," Sebastian said quietly, "did you check the false drawer twice yesterday? I watched you do it. Once after closing, once before bed."

For a long moment Tam said nothing. Somewhere out in Gilders Lane a cart rattled past, and the shop's crooked shelves shivered, and a jar of pickled quinces caught the early light and threw it back in a dull amber gleam.

"Old habit," Tam said at last. "That's all. Now fetch the lavender oil — Mrs. Callow will be here within the hour wanting her sleeping draught, and if we're short she'll talk for forty minutes about her nephew in the militia, and I haven't the fortitude."

* * *

Mrs. Callow took thirty-eight minutes. Her nephew, it transpired, had been promoted, though to what was unclear even to her, and there was some question of a uniform allowance, and did Sebastian know that the Veilwardens had been seen at the Tanner's Gate at odd hours, three nights running?

Sebastian's hands paused over the mortar.

"At the gate?" he said, keeping his voice idle as milk. "Doing what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Mrs. Callow leaned across the counter, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman who had waited her whole life for a conspiracy worth whispering about. "My cousin's boy delivers fish to the garrison mess, and he says the grey-cloaks have requisitioned carts. Carts, mind you, and lanterns with shutters, and — this is the part — *ink.* A dozen pots of the good iron-gall ink. What does a Warden want with ink enough to write out a library?"

"A list," Sebastian said.

It was out of his mouth before he could weigh it. Mrs. Callow blinked at him. "A list of what, dear?"

"I don't know. Debtors. Delinquent taxes." He ground the valerian harder than it needed, forcing the thought down where it couldn't be seen. "The city loves a list."

She left eventually, draught in hand, and the bell above the door gave its tired little jingle, and Sebastian stood in the sudden quiet with his heart going too fast for the hour of the morning.

Outside, on the roof of the cooper's shop across the lane, three crows sat in a row watching the window with the flat black patience of tax collectors.

He went out into the alley and looked up at them. "*Well?*" he said under his breath, in the way that wasn't quite sound — the pressure of meaning shaped into the old speech, felt in the teeth rather than heard.

The eldest cocked her head. Her eye was wet and bright and utterly without mercy. *"The tall ones went to the Chancery at dawn,"* she said. *"Before the bells. Before the clerks. Nobody goes to the Chancery before the clerks unless the order came from above the Chancery."*

*"From the crown."*

*"From whatever wears the crown these days."* She preened once, ruffled, resettled. *"There are more of them in the city than there were last moon. I counted forty-two new feathers' worth of grey cloaks coming up the coast road. And boy—"*

She stopped. That was the worst part, the stopping. Crows never stopped mid-thought; they were creatures of momentum, of endless commentary. For one to pause meant the thing it had to say had weight enough to make even gossip hesitate.

*"And what?"*

*"One of them carried a cage,"* the crow said. *"A small cage, barred with cold-iron mesh, lined in wool. And in the cage was a wren. Alive. And the tall one fed it with his fingers, gentle as a nursemaid, and the wren sang for him. Sang everything it had heard. Names. Streets. A door that opens at night on Candlewick Row."*

The alley seemed to tilt. Sebastian put a hand against the damp brick.

*"A singing wren,"* he managed. *"They've made a listener out of it."*

*"They've made a collaborator out of it,"* the crow corrected. *"There's a difference, and it matters. The wren gets seed and warmth. The rest of the small birds get watched through it. You understand me, boy? Whatever the grey-cloaks are building, they've built ears into it. Everything that sings is theirs now."*

Sebastian stood in the alley a long time after the crows flapped off toward the market refuse heaps. The morning smelled of horse dung and frying onions and river mud, the great honest stink of Greyhollow, and none of it could settle him. His gift — his small, harmless, deniable gift — suddenly felt less like a coin sewn into a hem and more like a lit candle cupped in his palms, in a city full of people learning to sniff for smoke.

* * *

He told Tam at noon. All of it: the counting, the ink, the wren.

Tam listened while slicing soap into blocks, and his knife never wavered, and that frightened Sebastian more than anything else, because Tam's hands always told the truth his face refused to. Steady hands meant steady mind. Today the hands were stone.

"So," Tam said finally. "Wardens with listening birds and ink. Could be nothing. Could be the usual theater — scare the quarter, shake out a few unlicensed hedge-mages, fine them, move along. Happens every few years. The crown likes its people afraid in measurable doses."

"And if it isn't theater?"

"Then it's the Repeal with teeth." Tam set the knife down. He wiped his palms on his apron slowly, thoroughly, the gesture of a man buying time. "Seb, sit."

Sebastian sat. Tam rarely used that tone; it was reserved for dead customers and tax assessors.

"There's a thing I've kept from you," Tam said. "Not out of deceit. Out of — arithmetic. Your father made me swear, and I've spent eleven years deciding each morning whether today was the day the arithmetic changed." He rubbed his jaw. "This morning I think it might have."

The shop was very quiet. Through the window, Gilders Lane went about its business: a water-seller crying his rounds, two children chasing a hoop, the eternal gray drizzle beginning to stipple the cobbles. Ordinary. Unsuspecting. A city of doors that someone, somewhere, was writing down.

"Your father didn't only sell charms," Tam said. "You know that much. But you don't know what he was, really, or why a man with his talents ended up bottling sleep-draughts in Gilders Lane. And you don't know what he left with me, the night before they took him. Because he said the time would come when the grey cloaks started counting doors." Tam's colorless eyes fixed on Sebastian's. "And he said when that day came, there wouldn't be much time at all."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "Tam—"

"After closing," Tam said. "When the shutters are up and the lane's gone dark. Not before. Fear makes bad listeners, and I need you listening." He picked the knife back up, and his hands, at last, trembled — just once, just slightly, before he steadied them. "Now hand me the lavender oil, lad. Mrs. Pell's coming at two, and if we're short she'll cry, and neither of us has earned that."

Above the street, unnoticed by either of them, a single grey-cloaked figure paused at the corner of Gilders Lane, consulted a slim ledger bound in black, and made a small, precise mark before walking on.
