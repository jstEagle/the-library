## 3. The Proclamation

The crows had not lied.

Sebastian woke before dawn on the third morning with his heart already hammering, as though some part of him had spent the night listening for hooves on cobblestone. The garret above the charm-seller's shop was cold enough to see his breath. He lay still in the grey light and counted the sounds of the city waking: the groan of the tannery pumps, a milk cart's iron-rimmed wheels, gulls quarreling over the fish-market gutters. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds.

Then, beneath them all, faint and far off, a sound that did not belong — the deep brazen voice of the Curfew Bell rung out of season.

He was dressed before he finished thinking about it.

Downstairs, Maren Cobb was already at her counter, arranging her little deceptions: luck-charms of painted bone, love-knots of braided horsehair, fever-bottles filled with river water and confident lies. She was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and she had been selling magic that wasn't for thirty years precisely because it wasn't. Her one true gift — if you could call it that — was knowing which customers needed hope more than they needed honesty.

"You heard it too," she said without looking up.

"The bell."

"Rung twice." Now she looked up, and her face in the lamplight was older than her years. "Once for the Warden's arrival. Once for what the Warden brings. That's the old code, boy. Nobody under forty remembers it, but my grandmother did."

Sebastian pulled his coat from its peg. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, everything was moving like floodwater. *They are coming. They know. The crows said they know.*

"I'm going to the square," he said.

Maren set down the charm she was holding very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off. "No, you're not."

"Maren—"

"You're going to stand behind my counter, where a shop-boy belongs, and you're going to hand parcels to grandmothers until this whole thing blows over like weather." But even as she said it, her eyes went to the window, to the street beyond, and Sebastian saw her read the morning the way he had learned to read animals — by the angle of the shoulders, the set of the ears. And the street was wrong. People were walking toward Coronet Square instead of away from their errands. Nobody walked toward a Warden's bell unless the fear of not-knowing had grown bigger than the fear of knowing.

"They'll be counting faces," Maren said quietly. "That's how it starts. Not with arrests. With counting."

"Then I should be counted," Sebastian said. "A man who hides is a man with something to hide."

"A man who hides is a man who lives." She gripped his wrist, her fingers surprisingly strong, smelling of tallow and dried lavender. "Sebastian. Whatever your grandmother left in your blood — leave it sleeping today. Do you understand me? Today you are deaf as a post and dumb as a stone. You hear nothing. You say nothing. You are a shop-boy with chilblains and no prospects."

He almost smiled. "You make me sound irresistible."

"I make you sound *survivable*." She let him go. "Go on, then. I can see you'll go whether I chain you to the floor or not. But walk there slow, and come back faster."

* * *

Coronet Square had been built for glory and used for everything else. Its statue of King Aldous the Unifier stood green with verdigris, one arm raised forever over a city that had stopped looking up. By midmorning the square was packed shoulder to shoulder — fishwives and factors, apprentices perched on the fountain's rim, clerks still holding their ink-stained ledgers as though the world might resume and they'd be blamed for the interruption.

Sebastian worked his way along the edge, keeping the butcher's awning at his back, keeping his hood up against a drizzle that wasn't really falling hard enough to justify it. Around him the crowd murmured in the register of held breath. He could feel it through his boots — not the people, but the animals massed at the square's margins: dogs tied to rails, a carthorse stamping, pigeons so dense on the eaves they darkened the gutter-lines. Every animal within a quarter mile had come to look, the way they always came when men gathered in numbers, and every one of them was singing the same song.

*Fear,* the horses said. *Fear and the smell of iron.*

*Iron and fire,* said the dogs. *Men who smell of iron and fire and other men's blood.*

And the crows, high on the statue's raised arm, said nothing at all, which frightened him most of all. Crows were never silent unless they were being respectful, and crows were only ever respectful of two things: death, and power.

At the ninth hour the Veilwardens came.

There were twelve of them, riding in double file through the Coppergate arch, and they were nothing like the city watch, who swaggered and shoved and could be bought for a pint. The Veilwardens rode grey horses — actual grey, the color of slate and ash, and Sebastian's stomach turned when he understood why. No horse was born that color. Those were horses whose colors had been *taken*, drained to a uniform dun-grey by whatever the riders carried, the way a candle flame goes flat near a strong magnet. The animals moved with terrible docility, heads level, ears slack. They didn't spook at the crowd. Nothing about them was left to spook.

The riders themselves wore long coats of oiled black leather and half-masks of pale porcelain shaped like smooth, featureless faces — no mouths, no expression, just two narrow eyeholes apiece. Where their bare hands showed at the cuffs, the skin was marked: a thin scar circling each wrist like a shackle worn into the flesh.

Twelve riders. They drew up in a line before the statue, and the crowd's murmur died so completely that Sebastian could hear the fountain dripping.

One of them dismounted. He was taller than the rest, and where the others' masks were blank, his bore a single mark burned into the porcelain above the brow: a circle bisected by a vertical line. The Seal of the Veil. He carried a scroll case of black lacquer, and he opened it with the unhurried care of a man who had done this in a hundred squares and would do it in a hundred more.

When he spoke, his voice was amplified — not shouted, *carried*, rolling to the far corners of the square as though the air itself had been ordered to deliver it. Sebastian felt the pressure of it against his skin, felt the horses flinch in unison, and understood: the man was using magic to announce the destruction of magic. The obscenity of it was so clean it took his breath away.

"Citizens of Greyhollow," the Warden said. "By decree of the Crown, and under authority of the Edict of Severance, hear now the Proclamation of Purge."

A woman near Sebastian crossed herself with an old sign nobody used anymore. Somewhere a child asked its mother a question and was hushed.

"For three hundred years the kingdom has endured the residue of the Sundering — the unlicensed workings, the hedge-crafts, the traffics with beast and bogie that fester in the dark corners of honest cities. The Crown has been patient. Patience is over.

"Effective at dusk today, the practice, concealment, or harboring of the gifted arts is a capital crime. All persons possessing such gifts shall present themselves at the Warden's stations for Assessment within nine days. Those who present themselves freely shall be assessed, registered, and — where their gifts prove trivial — released under bond. Those who do not present themselves will be found. And those who shelter them will share their fate."

He let the silence sit. Then, softer, in that same carrying voice, almost gentle:

"There is no shame in the gift. There is only shame in hiding it. The Crown does not hate the gifted. The Crown *collects* them."

*Collects.* The word went through the crowd like a fishhook through a net, snagging everyone. Sebastian heard the woman beside him whisper, "Collects them *where?*" and heard no answer, because there was none.

The Warden rolled up the scroll. Before he sealed the case, he lifted his head, and though the mask had no features, Sebastian felt — knew, with the certainty of his gift — that the man was looking directly at him across four hundred bodies. Looking, and marking.

Then the Wardens wheeled their grey horses and rode out the way they'd come, and the square exhaled all at once, a thousand voices starting up in a ragged roar of questions, denials, prayers.

* * *

He didn't remember walking back to Cobb's. He arrived with his heart going like a trapped bird and found Maren standing in the doorway of the shop, arms folded, watching the street.

"Well?" she said.

"Nine days. Voluntary assessment. 'The Crown collects them.'" He pulled the hood back. "Maren, the horses. Their horses were grey because the color had been *drained out of them*. I've seen beasts drained before — poachers do it to hares sometimes, bleed the wildness out so the snares don't scare them. It's the same working. Same stink. These people don't suppress magic. They *eat* it."

Maren closed the door and shot the bolt, which she hadn't done in daylight since the cholera year. "Sit down."

"I can't sit down."

"Sit down anyway." She pushed him onto the stool by the cold stove and stood over him, and for once she didn't pretend to be merely a seller of false charms. "Nine days. So we think. First question: do they have names?"

"Not yet. The crows said lists were being written in the Citadel, but—" He stopped.

"But what?"

"But the crows also said something I didn't tell you. They said the first name on the list was written in gold ink." He rubbed his face. "I thought it was crow nonsense. Crow prophecy is mostly nonsense with a few true bones in it, like a stew. But a list with a golden name — that's not a purge list, Maren. That's an *invitation*. Somebody important is gifted, and somebody more important wants them handled gently."

"Lord Crane," Maren said flatly.

Sebastian looked up. Aldous Crane — Master of the Royal Menagerie, patron of naturalists, the great philanthropist whose money kept half the city's charities breathing. Sebastian's own patron. The man who had paid for his apprenticeship, who dined him twice a season, who had once said, wine-warm and laughing, *"Your gift is wasted on horses, boy — you should see what I could show you."*

"No," Sebastian said. "Crane hates the old craft. He's said so a dozen times. He calls it superstition and filth."

"He calls it that publicly." Maren's eyes were sharp and pitying at once, which was worse than either alone. "Boy, you've taken the man's coin for six years. Has he ever once asked you to *use* it? Truly use it, where he could see?"

Sebastian opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the answer, now that he reached for it honestly, was no. Crane had asked him to describe what animals told him — endlessly, greedily, in detail — but never to *work*, never to speak through a beast or draw one's senses into his own. As though Crane wanted the fruit of the gift while making sure Sebastian never learned what else grew on the tree.

"He knows," Sebastian said slowly. "He's known what I am since the beginning. He didn't buy an apprentice. He bought a *specimen*."

"And now the Crown is collecting specimens," Maren said, "and Lord Crane sits on the Privy Council, and the first name on the Warden's list is written in gold." She began pacing, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the track worn into her floorboards by thirty years of bad news. "So here's what happens next. Either Crane protects you — in which case you belong to him body and soul, and God help you. Or Crane gives you up — in which case you hang. Or Crane uses the purge to flush out every gifted soul in Greyhollow *except* the ones he wants, and builds himself something out of the wreckage. And I've met that man at charity suppers, and I'll tell you which of the three he is."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "What do I do?"

"Tonight? Nothing clever. Tonight you sleep, and tomorrow you sell charms, and you keep your ears shut and your mouth shut." She stopped pacing. "But Sebastian — listen to me now. If it comes to running, you don't run blind. A hunted man needs a map, and not one drawn by any mapmaker in this city, because every mapmaker in this city reports to the Citadel."

"What, then?"

Maren was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a woman stepping onto ice she'd avoided for decades.

"Old Hollis. The cartographer. Up on Chandler's Stair. He came into my shop last winter wanting a charm against forgetting, and I sold him one, because I sell anything, and because he paid in old silver. And when he'd gone I found he'd left something on my counter besides coin." She looked at Sebastian steadily. "A scrap of map. Hand-drawn. Of country that doesn't exist — not on any chart I've ever seen. And in the corner, in a shaking hand, one word."

She leaned close, as though the walls might be listening, and perhaps they were.

"*Veilmark.*"

The name landed in Sebastian's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He had never heard it. He had no memory of it. And yet somewhere far below thought, in the animal-dark place where his gift lived, something stirred and turned toward the word the way a sleeping dog turns toward its master's step.

Outside, the drizzle thickened to rain. Somewhere across the city, in the Citadel, ink was drying on a list of names — and one of them, written in gold, was his.

* * *

That night Sebastian did not sleep. He sat at the garret window with the lamp unlit, watching the wards of Greyhollow smolder under the rain, and listened.

The city talked to itself the way it always did — dogs reporting to dogs, rats gossiping in the walls, the carthorses dreaming their anxious dreams. But threaded through it all now was a new note, faint and getting fainter, coming from everywhere and nowhere: the sound of the gifted going quiet. He could feel them the way you feel stars vanish behind cloud — a charm-wife in Tanner's Row snuffing her little hearth-spell; a boy who talked to rats pulling his shutters closed; an old man in the Salt Quarter singing his last song to the gulls and then, deliberately, forgetting how.

One by one, the small lights going out.

And beneath it, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the city, at the edge of *everything* — something else. Something that was not a dog or a rat or a gull. Something sitting just past the lamplight with the patience of stone, watching his window, and humming to itself a tune that no animal had ever taught it.

Sebastian leaned forward, heart slowing strangely, and looked down into the wet street.

Two amber eyes looked back.
