## 5. Crane's Kind Offer

The rain caught them at the edge of the Ash Orchards, where the city's cobbles gave way to a road of packed grey mud and the trees stood in rows like mourners who had forgotten whom they were burying. Sebastian had never been past the orchards. Few in Greyhollow had. Beyond them the map said there were hills, and beyond the hills the wilds, and beyond the wilds — if the dying cartographer's ink could be believed — the door.

He did not look like a man carrying a door's secret. He looked, he was fairly sure, like a drowned rat with a stolen compass.

Sorrel trotted ahead through the wet grass, unbothered by the rain, which seemed to strike the fox and simply give up. His fur lay sleek and dry as though water had signed some private agreement with him years ago.

"You're staring," Sorrel said without turning his head.

"I'm wondering why you're not wet."

"Discipline," said the fox. "Yours is lacking. You breathe too loudly, you run on your heels, and you've checked that compass eleven times since the bell began ringing. The twelfth will not change its answer."

Sebastian shoved the compass back inside his coat anyway. It knocked against his ribs, warm as a held bird. *North-northwest,* it insisted, the way it had insisted since the cartographer pressed it into his hands with the last of his strength, *and hurry.*

The orchard road bent around a hillside, and from its crest Sebastian turned to look back one last time. Greyhollow lay below in the rain, all slate roofs and chimney smoke, the great grey bulk of the Wardenhouse squatting over it like a toad on a stone. He could see the market square, or imagined he could — the spot where he'd stood not three hours ago buying charm-ribbon off old Marta, before the crows went mad, before the proclamation, before the bell. Somewhere down there was his stall. His tools. The little brass bells he tuned for dairy cows so they wouldn't spook at thunder. Eleven years of small honest work, and he had left it all standing open under the awning like an abandoned sentence.

"They'll burn the stall," he said.

"They'll inventory the stall," Sorrel corrected. "Then they'll burn it. Wardens are thorough before they're cruel. It's what makes them worse than mere cruelty."

Sebastian pulled his collar up and kept walking.

They made four miles before noon, following hedgerows rather than the road itself, Sorrel insisting — reasonably enough — that roads were for people who wished to be found. Sebastian's boots were soaked through by then, and his stomach had begun composing angry letters, and it was somewhere in the middle of a long argument with himself about whether foxes could smell bread at five miles' distance when he heard the sound behind them.

Hooves. One horse, moving at an easy canter, unhurried.

Sorrel stopped so abruptly that Sebastian nearly trod on his tail.

"What?" Sebastian whispered.

"Quiet." The fox's ears swiveled. "One rider. Coming up the road behind us. Not hurrying." A pause. "That's the part I dislike. Pursuers hurry. This one is *strolling.*"

"Could be anyone. A farmer. A—"

"A farmer," said Sorrel, "would have passed us already, because we are walking at the pace of a funeral procession and farmers have things to do. Get off the road. Now."

They dropped into the ditch on the orchard side, into wet bracken and black mud, and Sebastian flattened himself against the earth and breathed as quietly as a man could breathe with his heart hammering on the compass. Through the stems of the bracken he watched the road.

The rider came into view beneath a dripping alder tree. Grey cloak, hood back despite the rain. A broad, weathered face, forty-some years old, with a short iron-grey beard and eyes that moved over the landscape the way Sebastian's own fingers moved over an animal's flank — reading, cataloguing, gentle and thorough at once. The horse was a rangy dun mare, mud to the hocks, and she carried saddlebags that bulged like a peddler's.

The rider drew rein. Looked at the road ahead. Then, without any apparent reason, looked directly at their stretch of ditch.

"Master Fairweather," he called. His voice carried easily under the rain, low and unhurried. "You can come out, or you can lie in that ditch until dark, and I'll still be here at dark. I'm told the bracken leaks."

* * *

Sebastian came out because there was nothing else sensible to do, and because — this was the part that unsettled him more than the hooves had — he knew the voice. Not well. From years ago, at the edges of rooms, at his father's elbow. A voice from the time before the quiet years, before the silence that had fallen over his family like snow.

The man dismounted and stood holding the mare's bridle, letting her crop at the hedge. Up close he was broader than Sebastian remembered, with the settled heaviness of someone who had spent decades walking. Rain beaded on his cloak and did not seem to trouble him either, which Sebastian filed away next to the mystery of the dry fox.

"You don't remember me," the man said. It wasn't quite a question.

"You look familiar."

"I should. You had your father's chin even at seven." He extended a hand. "Aldous Crane."

Sebastian took it. The grip was dry, calloused, strong. "Crane. The Wardenhouse sent you?"

Something flickered across Crane's face — quick, quickly gone. Amusement, maybe, or something older and more tired wearing amusement's coat. "The Wardenhouse," he said, "sent no one. The Wardenhouse believes you are still in Greyhollow, hiding in a cellar in the Tanner's Quarter, because that is what their trackers believe, and their trackers read reports instead of ground. I am not here for the Wardenhouse."

"Then why—"

"Because I knew your father." Crane said it simply, the way a man says *I knew your father* when the sentence carries thirty years of freight. "And because you are walking north-northwest with a dead man's compass and no food, no bedroll, no oilcloth, and one fox." He glanced at the hedge, at the exact spot where Sorrel sat concealed, invisible to any ordinary eye. "Two companions, counting the one in the ditch. Who may come out as well; my mare doesn't mind foxes, and I keep no dogs."

Silence. Rain on bracken. Then Sorrel flowed out of the ditch as if he'd merely been waiting for a polite invitation, shook himself elaborately — still dry — and sat down between Sebastian and Crane with the expression of a creditor attending a debtor's funeral.

"Well," said the fox. "This is interesting."

"You know him?" Sebastian asked.

"I know *of* him," said Sorrel. "Which worries me more."

* * *

They walked, because Crane would not talk standing in the rain and there was a field-barn half a mile on, an old drystone affair with a sagging roof where orchard laborers once stored ladders. Inside it smelled of apples gone to cider and years of patient rot. Crane unsaddled the mare, rubbed her down with a wisp of straw, and then, with the unselfconscious competence of a man performing a ritual he had performed ten thousand times, produced from his saddlebags a small kettle, a spirit-lamp, tea, bread, hard cheese, dried apples, and a jar of honey.

Sebastian watched this unpacking with the dazed hunger of a starving man watching a miracle. He hadn't eaten since yesterday morning. He realized it now with a lurch.

"Eat first," Crane said. "Talk after. Questions keep better than hunger does."

So they ate, sitting on ladder-boards in the ruined barn, while the rain worked at the roof and the mare dozed hipshot by the door. Crane poured tea into tin cups without asking how anyone took it, and it was hot and strong and slightly smoky, and Sebastian drank two cups before he remembered to be afraid again.

Sorrel ate dried apple with delicate disgust, as though the apple had personally failed him.

"Now," Crane said, settling back against the wall. "You want to ask what I am. Ask."

"The proclamation says the gifted are to surrender themselves or be purged," Sebastian said. "It says anyone aiding them shares their crime. You found me within hours. That's not luck. That's craft. And craft like that belongs to the Wardens."

"It belonged to the Wardens," Crane said. "Past tense. I wore the grey cloak for nineteen years. I was the best tracker they ever had, and I say that without pride, because I know precisely what the skill was worth. I read names like tracks, boy. Give me a man's history and I'll tell you which door he'll bolt through. Give me his childhood and I'll find him in a week." He turned his cup slowly in his broad hands. "I taught half their current trackers everything they know. Which is why I know exactly how they'll hunt you — and exactly how badly they'll do it without me."

"Why did you leave?"

For a moment Crane didn't answer. The rain filled the silence, drumming soft and endless on the sagging roof.

"Because nineteen years ago I tracked a girl of sixteen to a kiln village in the eastern hills," he said at last, quietly. "She had a gift. Small one. She could make fire sit still — bank it like a memory, hold it in cold ash for days. Her neighbors loved her for it. Baked bread that stayed warm till evening." His thumb traced a slow circle on the tin cup. "I brought the Wardens. I followed procedure. I wrote my report in full and honest detail, because I was good, and my goodness was the worst thing about me." He looked up, and his eyes in the dim barn were very steady. "There were nine people in that village who tried to stand between the Wardens and one frightened girl. Nine. I stood aside and let procedure happen. I have been paying for it ever since, in the only coin I have, which is knowing how the machine works from the inside."

Sebastian set down his cup. His hands weren't entirely steady. "And now? What do you want from me?"

"To walk with you as far as the mountains," Crane said. "To feed you, teach you how to move, how to sleep cold, how to leave no track and read everyone else's. To keep you alive until you reach whatever it is you think you're walking toward." He nodded at Sebastian's coat, where the compass lay. "I know whose that was. Elias Fairweather was the finest cartographer the old kingdom ever produced, and the last. When Veilmark fell, he was the only man alive who'd mapped the door. I spent six years after his death trying to buy that map from every cutpurse and relic-dealer between here and the coast. I never found it. I begin to suspect he gave it to his son along with everything else he never explained."

"He didn't explain anything," Sebastian said, and heard the bitterness crack his own voice. "My father died when I was twelve, and he left me a house full of locked drawers and a childhood of *don't ask, boy, and never whistle near the treeline.* I learned my gift from a stray dog, not from him. Whatever he knew, he took it into the grave with him."

"No," Crane said. "He took it into exile. There's a difference, though I grant it won't comfort you."

Sebastian stared at him. "What do you mean?"

"I mean," said Aldous Crane, "that your father didn't die in Greyhollow. I mean that the man who taught me to read tracks sat across a fire from me twenty-two years ago and told me what he was going to do, and asked me — asked *me*, boy, because I was the only Warden he trusted — to make sure the records said he was dead." Crane leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the rain suddenly sounded very far away. "Elias Fairweather is buried in the Wardenhouse registry under a false name and an honest date. But he walked out through the rat-runs the night before, heading north-northwest, toward the mountains, toward the last door of Veilmark. Toward home."

The barn seemed to tilt slightly. Sebastian became distantly aware that he was gripping his tin cup hard enough to bend the rim, and that Sorrel had gone very still beside him, ears forward, amber eyes fixed on Crane with an intensity that had nothing cynical left in it.

"That's impossible," Sebastian said. "I stood at his grave. I was *twelve*. I—"

"Mourned a coffin full of stones and a record book full of kindness," Crane said. "Yes. Your father asked me to let you grieve honestly rather than grow up hunted, and God forgive me, I agreed, because it was easier than telling a child the truth and harder than I have ever admitted since." He spread his big scarred hands. "I don't know if he lived. I don't know if he reached the door, or what he found there. But I know the direction he walked, and I know the direction your compass pulls, and I have spent twenty-two years learning every fold of those northern hills. So here is my offer, freely given, no price attached: let me take you to him. Or to his grave, if the mountains kept him. Either way — let me carry you there faster and safer than you could ever go alone."

The silence stretched. Rain. Rotting apples. The mare shifting her weight by the door.

"And the Wardens?" Sebastian managed finally. "If they learn you've found me—"

"They'll learn I retired to fish," Crane said. "Or they'll learn I'm dead, if it comes to it. I've died before; it's restful work." He rose, stiffly, joints cracking like green wood, and began repacking the saddlebags with the same unhurried competence. "Think on it tonight. We shelter here regardless — no one tracks through this rain, and the orchard road's empty since the blight. In the morning, you decide: alone, or with me."

He paused at the mare's side, one hand on the worn leather of the saddle, and did not look back at Sebastian.

"One more thing, Master Fairweather. Your father asked me a question, that night by the fire, that I've carried unanswered ever since. He asked me: *if a man builds a wall to protect his children, and the wall crushes them instead — does he tear it down, knowing what comes through the gap?*" Crane swung up onto the mare with an old man's care. "I never had an answer for him. Perhaps you'll find one in the mountains. Perhaps that's the real inheritance."

He rode out into the rain and was gone, hoofbeats fading down the orchard road, unhurried as ever.

Sebastian sat among the ladder-boards with his cold tea, his bent cup, his hammering heart. Beside him, Sorrel licked honey from one paw with great deliberation.

"Well," said the fox at last. "Either that man is the truest friend your bloodline has left in the world — or he is the single finest liar I have met in a very long life, and I include myself in the comparison." He fixed Sebastian with one amber eye. "So, boy. Do we trust him?"

Sebastian looked out at the rain, where the road ran north-northwest, toward hills he had never seen and a father he had wept for at twelve years old. The compass knocked against his ribs, warm and insistent.

And far behind them, back along the grey road toward Greyhollow, another rider had crested the orchard hill — cloaked not in grey but in the deep blue of the Wardenhouse proper, riding fast now, the strolls and the patience finished — and was following, name by name and track by track, the trail of a kind offer made in the rain.
