## 6. The Cartographer's Bequest

The mapmaker lived above his shop on Tallow Row, in a room that smelled of ink and camphor and the particular sweetness of a body quietly failing. His name was Aldous Vane, and Sebastian had known him for eleven years without ever once being sure he liked him.

He climbed the stairs slowly, because the fox had told him to.

*Slowly,* Sorrel had said that morning, sitting on the windowsill of the garret with his tail curled over his paws like a man folding his hands. *Old men hate to be rushed. And dying men hate it more. You'll want to get to the point — everyone does — and the moment you do, he'll know you came for the thing and not for him. So don't. Let him come to it.*

*And if he doesn't?*

Sorrel had yawned, showing teeth that were too many and too fine for any honest fox. *Then you'll have spent an afternoon with a dying man and learned nothing, which is more than most people manage in a lifetime.*

Now, at the top of the stairs, Sebastian knocked twice on a door whose paint had gone the grey of old dishwater, and waited.

"Come in before my tea goes cold," said a voice like dry paper folding. "It's been cold an hour, but I like to say it."

The room was small and ferociously ordered. Maps hung from every wall — not the pretty kind sold to merchants' wives, but working maps, annotated in a hand so small it looked like insect script, corrected and re-corrected until some of them seemed less like pictures of places than arguments about them. A cot stood against one wall, its blankets drawn up with military precision. In a chair by the window sat Aldous Vane, and Sebastian had to stop himself from staring.

He had seen the cartographer three months ago, hunched and irritable and alive. The man in the chair was a rumour of himself. His skin had gone the colour of the tallow below, translucent, blue-veined. His eyes were still sharp — that was the terrible thing — sharp as a draftsman's compass, set in a face that was already half gone.

"Fairweather." Vane's mouth did something that might once have been a smile. "You look worse than I do, and I'm dying. Sit down."

Sebastian sat. There was only one other chair, a drafting stool, and it put him lower than the old man, which he suspected was deliberate.

"I heard you were asking after me," Vane said. "Through the bird-girl at the fountain market. The starling that sits on her shoulder — yours?"

"Not mine. We're acquainted."

"Mm." Vane looked out the window. Below, Tallow Row went about its morning: a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking, a woman arguing with a fishmonger, two Veilwardens walking their rounds with the unhurried confidence of men who had not yet been given orders worth hurrying for. "You'll have heard the proclamation."

"Everyone's heard the proclamation."

"And yet here you are. Sitting in the open window-light of a licensed mapmaker's room, chatting, as though the crown hasn't just announced it means to burn through Greyhollow like fever through a poorhouse." Vane turned his head, and those compass-point eyes fixed on Sebastian with uncomfortable precision. "Either you're a fool, or you've decided the list won't reach you. Which is it?"

"The list reaches everyone," Sebastian said. "That's rather the point of a purge."

"Good answer." Something moved behind the old man's eyes — approval, or perhaps only relief. "A fool would have said *I'm not gifted*, or *they'll never find me*. Both lies. Both the first lies everyone tells." He coughed then, a long, ruinous business, and when it passed he sat looking smaller, as though the cough had taken payment. "Your mother sent you, didn't she. Before the end. Eleven years ago. She said — what did she say — *if ever the world turns on my son, take him to Aldous Vane on Tallow Row.*"

Sebastian's throat tightened. "How do you know what she said? I never—"

"You told the starling-girl, who told the starling, who told the pigeon on my sill, who told me. Your beasts gossip worse than washerwomen, Fairweather. Has no one ever told you that?" He waved a translucent hand. "Yes. I knew your mother. Sit there and be quiet a moment; dying takes concentration."

So Sebastian sat and was quiet, and watched the light move across the hanging maps, and listened to the knife-grinder below, and thought about his mother, who had been dead eleven years and had apparently spent her last careful days arranging her son's survival like a woman packing a trunk for a journey she wouldn't take.

When Vane spoke again, his voice had changed. It was quieter, and it had lost the performance.

"Do you know what a cartographer actually does, Fairweather? Everyone thinks we draw the world. We don't. We draw what people agree the world is. Every map is a treaty between what's true and what's useful." His finger rose, trembling slightly, and pointed at the largest map on the far wall — a survey of the northern reaches, all mountains and blank spaces. "Take that. Official version. Commissioned by the Crown Survey, forty years ago. Note the region past the Marrow. Blank. *Unsurveyed*, the legend says. Do you know why it's unsurveyed?"

"No one's mapped it?"

"No one who came back has mapped it." Vane smiled thinly. "I went. Younger, harder, stupider. I spent two years in those mountains, and I will tell you what I found, because I am dying, and dying men can afford honesty."

He levered himself up — Sebastian started forward, was waved back down — and crossed to the great map on legs that shook like a foal's. His finger found the blank space past the Marrow and tapped it, twice.

"There is a valley there that does not appear on any chart because it refuses to be charted. Compasses sulk. Birds fly around it — you'd know that better than I, beast-speaker; ask them sometime, ask them what they see when they cross the high passes, and watch how they answer. I walked into that valley because I was twenty-four and immortal, and I walked out of it nine days later with no beard, no memory of six of those days, and a conviction I have carried for forty years." He turned. His eyes were wet, but his voice was iron. "Magic did not die in this world, boy. It was *put away*. Like a sword in a scabbard. Like a body in a tomb. And the tomb has a door."

The knife-grinder's wheel shrieked below and fell silent. In the quiet, Sebastian heard his own heartbeat.

"A door," he repeated.

"A door." Vane came back to his chair and lowered himself into it with the care of a man handling fragile cargo. "Your mother knew. That's why she named me. Not because I could hide you — I couldn't hide a teacup — but because I was the only man in Greyhollow who had stood close enough to the truth to feel its heat." He closed his eyes. "She was Veilmark blood, your mother. So are you. Don't argue; you argue with your eyebrows, it's very rude. The gift you carry — the speaking, the listening — it isn't a trick the world played on you. It's an inheritance. The last coin of a kingdom that spent everything else."

Sebastian's mouth was dry. "Veilmark's a story. A fable mothers tell—"

"So is every kingdom, eventually." Vane opened his eyes. "But stories leave paperwork."

He reached beneath the cot, moving with agonizing slowness, and drew out a flat case of oiled leather, cracked with age, tied with a cord. He held it on his knees and did not offer it.

"Before I give you this, I want you to understand what you're agreeing to. Because agreement matters. It mattered to them, and it will matter to whatever waits behind that door." His voice dropped. "Inside this case is a map. Not mine — older. Older than the Crown, older than Greyhollow, older than anything with a name you'd recognize. I bought it from a corpse in the valley, forty years ago, and I have spent my life keeping it and cursing myself for it. It shows the way to the door. It also shows — if you read it rightly, and few can — what the door costs. Your mother wanted you to have it *if you needed it*. Not before. Not for curiosity. Need, Fairweather. Do you understand the difference?"

Sebastian thought of the proclamation nailed to the courthouse doors, the wax seals gleaming like fresh wounds. He thought of the charm-sellers already gone quiet in their stalls, of the rat-runs and the whisper-networks, of Crane's letter sitting unopened in his coat pocket these three days because he could not decide whether it was rescue or bait.

"Yes," he said. "I think I'm beginning to."

"Then take it." Vane held out the case, and his hand trembled, and his eyes did not. "And take one more thing, since you're here to rob a dying man anyway."

* * *

The second thing was a key. Small, black iron, shaped like a bird's foot.

"It opens a lockbox at the Dead Letter Office," Vane said. "Under the name *Meridian*. What's inside is not for you — it's for whoever comes after you, if you fail. Letters. Evidence against certain powerful men who have spent years buying up every whisper of Veilmark they could find. Names, dates, payments." He watched Sebastian's face carefully. "You know one of the names already. I saw it land when I said *powerful men*. Go on. Say it."

"Crane." The word came out flat. "Ambrose Crane."

Vane nodded slowly. "Your patron. Your friend, I'm told. The man who found a gutter-born beast-speaker and made him respectable." He sighed. "Crane came to me eight years ago, Fairweather. Very charming. Very generous. He wanted to buy a copy of my northern survey — the real one, with my annotations. I refused. He raised the price. I refused. And on his third visit, he stopped smiling, and he asked me a question I have never forgotten." The old man leaned forward, and the lamplight made his hollowed face into something from a masque. "He asked me: *Master Vane, if magic were a door, would you open it, or lock it?* And I said, *that depends who holds the key.* And he smiled again and left, and I lay awake that night understanding that I had answered wrong. He wasn't asking about doors. He was telling me he intended to hold the key."

Outside, a bell began to ring — the courthouse bell, the one that rang for proclamations and executions. Three strokes, four, five. Somewhere down Tallow Row, shutters were closing.

"They posted the first names today," Vane said quietly. "You didn't know. I can see you didn't. The bird-girl's starlings are quick, but even gossip needs an hour." He reached out and pressed the key into Sebastian's palm, closing the young man's fingers over it with hands that felt like winter twigs. "Get out of the city tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight. The rat-runs, the river gate, whatever your beasts arrange — go. And Fairweather—" He caught Sebastian's sleeve as he rose, and his grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "The door in that valley. When you stand in front of it, and you will, remember what I told you about treaties. Every map is a bargain between the true and the useful. So is every life. Decide which side of the bargain you're standing on *before* you touch the handle. Afterward is too late."

"What's behind it?" Sebastian asked. "What's actually there?"

Vane smiled, and for a moment the dying fell away and he looked like the man who had walked into unmapped mountains at twenty-four.

"That," he said, "is the one question the map won't answer. It only shows you the way in. What comes out —" he released Sebastian's sleeve, and settled back, exhausted, "— depends entirely on what walks in."

* * *

Sebastian descended Tallow Row with the case under his coat and the key biting its cold shape into his palm, and the world had rearranged itself while he'd been upstairs. The knife-grinder was just a man again. The fishmonger's argument was just noise. But the Veilwardens on the corner — their grey coats, their unhurried stride — had become something else entirely, and so had the shuttered windows, and so had the bell still tolling somewhere behind the rooftops, counting out names he hadn't heard yet.

Sorrel was waiting for him in the mouth of the cooper's alley, sitting in a patch of sun as though he had nowhere in the world to be, which was exactly the impression he liked to give.

"Well?" the fox said.

"He gave me a map."

"Obviously. What else?"

"And a key. And a warning about Crane." Sebastian crouched, keeping his voice under the street-noise. "And he said the first names were posted today. I need to hear them, Sorrel. Before dark. If anyone I've charmed for, anyone I've *spoken* for is on that list—"

The fox was already on his feet, ears forward, and for once there was nothing lazy in him at all.

"Then we'd best hurry," Sorrel said. "Because I heard the crier an hour ago, little beast-speaker, and the third name on the list is a girl who keeps starlings."
