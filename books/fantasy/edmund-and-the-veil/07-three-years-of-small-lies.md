## 7. Three Years of Small Lies

The lies had started small, the way rot starts in a beam — a softness no one notices until the floor gives way.

Sebastian had learned to lie before he'd learned to lie well, and there was a difference between the two that cost him every night when he lay awake in his narrow room above the charm-shop and counted the day's deceits like coins he was ashamed to own.

The first lie had been to his mother, though she was three years dead and could not hear it. *I have nothing of the old blood in me,* he had told her grave, kneeling in the ash-grey grass of the Cinderfield cemetery with his palms flat on the cold earth, because even dead ground felt like listening sometimes. *Nothing. Whatever Grandmother was, I am not her.* The earth had said nothing back. Earth never did, in Greyhollow. Only beasts answered him, and beasts could not be trusted to keep quiet about it.

That was the trouble with his gift, if you could call it a gift. It did not announce itself. It did not glow or spark or leave scorch-marks on the curtains for the Veilwardens to find. It was only this: animals spoke to him, and he understood them, and they understood him, and between those two facts stretched a chasm of danger wide enough to swallow a man whole. A dog who loved you would tell the Veilwardens' hounds where you slept. A rat who hated you would sell your name for a crust of bread. In Greyhollow, where magic had been outlawed for sixty years and the punishment for it was the grey cloak and the long walk to the Ashgate, a beast-speaker survived by being silent — and silence, Sebastian had discovered, was just another word for lying.

* * *

He thought about it most mornings now, standing behind the counter of Fairweather Charms & Curios while the shop filled with its usual traffic of gulls, grifters, and grandmothers. His mother had run the shop before him, and her mother before her, and somewhere back along that line — three generations, maybe four — the blood had come in, thin as watered wine but present all the same. He had never known which ancestor had carried it. He had never dared ask. Asking questions about the old gifts in Greyhollow was like poking a sleeping dog: mostly it stayed asleep, but once in a while it woke up and bit, and the bite always came with a grey cloak attached.

So he lied. Small lies, daily lies, lies worn smooth as river stones from handling.

He lied to Mrs. Tallow at the bakery, who asked every week whether her cat had "said anything" lately — she meant it as a joke, the way people in Greyhollow joked about the old days, safe jokes with no teeth in them — and he laughed and said cats don't talk, Mrs. Tallow, more's the pity, while the cat itself sat on her windowsill telling him in vivid detail about the tomcat down the lane and the state of the fishmonger's bins.

He lied to the dockhands who wanted a charm against rats and got, instead, a sachet of dried mint and a confident smile, because mint genuinely worked on rats and he had told the harbor rats himself, in a long and surprisingly civil negotiation conducted at midnight among the pilings, that the grain stores were off-limits and the wharf-cats were to be tolerated as an unpleasant necessity. The rats had agreed. Rats, whatever people said about them, were reasonable creatures. It was people who weren't.

And he lied to Crane.

That was the lie that sat heaviest, the one with weight and edges, the one he carried in his chest like a swallowed hook.

* * *

Aldous Crane had found him four years ago, in the winter Sebastian's mother died, when the shop was failing and the creditors were circling and Sebastian, nineteen years old and hollowed out with grief, had stood on Marrow Bridge in the freezing rain and seriously considered stepping off it.

Crane had appeared beside him the way rich men appear everywhere — suddenly, and as if the weather had improved specifically for their benefit. He was a broad, silver-templed man in a coat worth more than the whole bridge, with a voice like warm brandy and eyes that missed nothing.

"You're Fairweather," he'd said. Not a question. "Your grandmother mended my sister's falcon once, thirty years ago. A kestrel with a broken wing. Everyone said it would never fly again." He had looked out at the rain-dark river. "It flew. I've often wondered how."

Sebastian had said nothing. His heart had been doing something slow and terrible in his chest.

"I'm not a Warden," Crane had said mildly. "If I were, I'd hardly open with the falcon story." And then, turning up his collar against the rain: "Come and work for me, boy. Not for charity. I collect things — books, maps, oddities. Animals too, occasionally. Creatures are poor patients and worse guests, and I need someone who can... manage them."

*Manage them*, he'd said. As if it were nothing. As if it were stable-work.

Sebastian had gone with him. That was the truth he didn't like to look at directly: grief-drunk and desperate, he had walked straight into the finest cage in Greyhollow and called it salvation. Crane had paid off the creditors. Crane had restocked the shop. Crane had sent him, twice a year, on "collecting errands" — a sick hawk here, a lame carriage-horse there, once a circus bear with a growth in its ear that Sebastian had talked through its surgery while the beast wept great shuddering tears and told him about the forests it could barely remember.

And Crane had never once asked what Sebastian heard. He simply watched, with those patient brandy-warm eyes, and waited, and paid generously, and let the silence do his asking for him.

Three years of small lies. Because the biggest one — the one Sebastian told Crane every single day without saying a word — was that he was merely useful. That he had a knack with animals, a gentle hand, a way with frightened creatures. Knack. Hand. Way. Words chosen like loose clothing, draped over the thing underneath.

Because Sebastian had understood, almost from the first month, what Crane was really collecting. Not hawks. Not bears. *Him.*

* * *

"You're brooding," said the fox.

It was evening, the shop shut, the lamps lit low. Sebastian sat on the floor behind the counter with his back against the shelving, and the fox sat across from him like a small rust-colored gentleman, tail curled over his paws, amber eyes catching the lamplight.

"I'm thinking," Sebastian said.

"That's what brooding calls itself when it wants to sound respectable." The fox — Sorrel, though that name had come later, offered by the fox himself with the air of a man selecting a table at an inn — tilted his head. "You've been counting your lies again. You get a particular wrinkle. Here." He nodded at his own forehead, which was not wrinkled at all, being covered in fur.

"I don't have a wrinkle."

"You have three. One for Crane, one for the Wardens, and one you haven't named yet. The third one is the interesting one." Sorrel scratched his ear with a hind foot, entirely unbothered. "Shall I tell you what I think about Crane?"

"No."

"He's building something," the fox went on, because Sorrel had never in his life respected a boundary, including the ones Sebastian set, the ones other dogs set, and once memorably the ones set by a very large and humorless mastiff belonging to the city watch. "Men like Crane don't buy loyalty by the yard unless they're sewing something big. He asks you nothing and pays you everything. That's not kindness, cub. That's investment."

"He saved my life."

"He bought yours. There's a difference, and the difference has teeth." Sorrel settled his chin on his paws. "Ask him sometime what happened to the last person who worked for him."

"There wasn't a last person."

"Mm," said the fox, in a tone that contained an entire library of information he declined to check out.

Sebastian threw a cork at him. The fox caught it out of the air, examined it, and dropped it with disdain. "Waste of good cork. Now — the real business. The pigeons have been talking."

"The pigeons are always talking. Half of it is about bread."

"This half isn't." Sorrel's ears came forward, and the lazy gentleman fell away from him all at once, the way a curtain falls, leaving something older and sharper sitting in its place. "There are new men at the Ashgate. Grey cloaks, but wrong — new stitching, new boots, and they don't drink at the Tollhouse like the old garrison did. They keep to themselves. And yesterday one of them bought every bird in the market square."

"Bought pigeons?"

"Hired them. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone." The fox's eyes held his. "They're counting us, Sebastian. All of us. Feathered kind first, because we're everywhere and nobody looks at us — but a census of birds is a census of everything the birds see. And what birds see, sooner or later, is *you*, talking to sparrows on your own back step like a man greeting old friends."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "The Wardens don't use beasts. It's against their own doctrine. Magic for the hunt, they say — it's the first rule—"

"Yes," Sorrel said softly. "Isn't it interesting, then, that they seem to be breaking it?"

* * *

He should have run that night. He knew it later, looking back across the ruin of everything, the way a man knows which plank was rotten after the bridge is down. He should have taken the fox, taken the little hoard of coin under the floorboard, and walked out through the Rat-Runs into the wilds and never seen Greyhollow again.

But the shop had been his mother's, and her mother's. And Crane had been — Crane. Four years of warm brandy and generous pay and the closest thing to family the world had left him. Three years of small lies had built a house inside Sebastian, and he had furnished it with hope: *he doesn't know, he suspects nothing, if it ever comes to it I'll lie bigger.*

Lies, he would learn, are load-bearing. Pull one out and everything comes down.

So instead he banked the fire, checked the locks, and went to bed in his narrow room above the shop, with the fox curled at the foot of the bed like a rust-colored secret, and outside in the dark the pigeons of Greyhollow wheeled over the rooftops in slow, uneasy circles, counting chimneys, counting windows, counting — though none of them knew why yet — the houses where certain people slept.

Two streets away, in a tall house with lights burning late, Aldous Crane stood at his window with a letter in his hand bearing a seal he was not supposed to possess, and read it twice, and smiled.

* * *

Morning came grey and ordinary, which is how the worst mornings always come. Sebastian opened the shutters, fed the gull that had appointed itself the shop's doorman, and was grinding dried valerian when the bells began.

Not the hour bells. The Proclamation bells — the deep bronze voices from the Citadel that Greyhollow heard perhaps once a decade, and always about death: war, plague, purge.

Sorrel came off the bed like thrown flame and landed on the sill, ears flat. "Cub," he said. "Listen."

Down in the street, people were stopping. Doors opening. Faces tilting up toward the Citadel hill, where a crier in grey stood on the high balcony with a scroll, and the wind carried the words down through the waking city in fragments, sentence by terrible sentence:

*"By decree of the Crown... the gifted... concealment is itself a crime..."*

*"Registers shall be opened... names shall be given..."*

*"The Veilwardens thank the loyal citizens of Greyhollow for their vigilance—"*

Mrs. Tallow stepped out of her bakery across the lane, flour still on her hands, and looked up with her mouth open. Somewhere a child began to cry. The gull on the doorstep said, in its flat, unconcerned voice, *"Bad weather coming. The big kind."*

Sorrel's tail had gone perfectly still.

"Sebastian," the fox said quietly. "Get the map money. Get the coat. We may already be too late."

Below the window, at the corner of the lane, a man in a grey cloak with new stitching stood very still beside a pigeon perched on his gloved fist — and the pigeon's round black eye was turned upward, fixed on the shuttered window where Sebastian Fairweather stood frozen in the half-light, caught in the act of existing.

The bird cocked its head.

And spoke.
