## 8. The First Name on the List

The rain came down on Greyhollow like a verdict.

Sebastian Fairweather stood beneath the crooked awning of Aldous Finch's charm-stall in Tallowmarket Row and watched the Veilwardens nail the proclamation to the courthouse door. There were six of them, grey-cloaked, their hoods beaded silver with wet, and they moved with the unhurried certainty of men who had done this before — in other cities, perhaps, or other years that history had been encouraged to forget. The hammer struck four times. The parchment unfurled. And the crowd, which had gathered the way crowds always gather at executions and accidents, went quiet all at once, like a held breath.

Sebastian could not read it from where he stood. He did not need to. He could read the faces of the people who could.

Old Marta the fishwife's mouth went thin as a knife-cut. The cooper's boy stopped chewing. A woman near the front crossed herself twice, then a third time for good measure, though Greyhollow had not been a city of the old faiths in two hundred years. And beside him, under the awning, Aldous Finch — charm-seller, fence of small wonders, a man whose stall displayed luck-knots and hagstones and bottled whispers to anyone who asked and nothing at all to anyone who didn't — went very still, the way a rabbit goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"Read it out," someone called from the back. "Some of us can't."

A Veilwarden turned. He was young, broad through the shoulders, with a voice trained to carry over wind and battle both. He unrolled the parchment again and read in a flat, official cadence that made even the rain sound warmer.

*"By decree of the Crown Regent, in the matter of unlawful arts: let it be known that within thirty days, all persons practicing, harboring, or concealing the forbidden gifts shall present themselves for assessment at the Hall of Registers. Those who comply shall be catalogued and released under bond. Those who do not—"*

He paused there. Not dramatically. The pause was simply part of the text, and he gave it no more weight than any other line, which somehow made it worse.

*"—shall be found. And what is found shall be taken."*

The crowd made a sound that was not quite a murmur and not quite silence. It was the sound of three thousand people deciding, each one alone, whether they were safe.

The young Warden rolled up his parchment. His eyes swept the row — the shuttered windows, the leaning stalls, the faces — and Sebastian felt the sweep of that gaze pass over him like cold water. It lingered half a heartbeat on Finch's stall. On the hagstones. On the little brass cage where Finch kept a cricket that was, according to the sign, *charmed against melancholy*, and was according to Sebastian an ordinary cricket with a flair for evening performances.

Then the Wardens moved on, six grey shapes dissolving into the grey morning, and the crowd exhaled, and the rain kept falling, and somewhere above the rooftops a crow lifted off the courthouse eaves with a scrap of parchment in its beak.

* * *

"They'll come here first," Finch said, twenty minutes later, in the back room behind his stall, where the walls were lined with drawers and every drawer held something that would hang him if the wrong person saw it. "Tallowmarket. They always start with the sellers. You don't hunt rabbits by chasing them through the warren, Seb. You watch the burrow doors."

"You've been through this before," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. He'd known Finch for eleven years, since he'd been a boy selling charms his grandmother had left him, and in all that time Finch had never once explained where his stock came from, or why a man who claimed to deal only in trinkets flinched at the word *magic* the way other men flinched at the word *debt*.

Finch opened a drawer, looked at what was inside, closed it again. Opened another. He was looking for something, or looking away from everything, Sebastian couldn't tell which.

"I was in Vell's Crossing when they did this," Finch said finally. "Twenty-two years ago. Different crown, same parchment. I watched them take a woman who could make bread rise with a word. That was her whole crime, Seb. Bread." He laughed, but it came out wrong, more cough than laugh. "They took her in a cart with the blinds drawn so nobody had to see her face. Her neighbors signed the list of witnesses. All of them. People she'd baked for."

"What happened to her?"

"No one knows. That's the trick of it. That's the whole trick." Finch shut the last drawer and leaned against the wall, suddenly old. "They don't kill you where people can see. They take you to the Hall of Registers, and after that you're a name in a ledger, and ledgers can be burned, and burned names never haunt anybody."

Sebastian's hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice weather — distantly, with a kind of helpless interest. "I'm not on any list," he said. "I talk to animals. Nobody knows. Nobody *can* know. It's not like I light fires or—"

"Nobody knew about the bread woman either." Finch looked at him then, directly, and there was something in his face Sebastian had never seen before: fear offered freely, without disguise, like a man showing you a wound because he wants you to understand how deep it goes. "Listen to me. Your gift isn't fire. Fire leaves scorch marks; yours leaves friends. Every rat in your cellar, every dog on your street, every pigeon that tilts its head when you walk past — that's evidence, boy. You're surrounded by witnesses who can't testify and can't lie either. One Warden with a sharp eye watches how animals act around you, and you're finished."

Sebastian thought of the crow on the courthouse eaves. Thought of how, that very morning, before the proclamation, it had landed on the gutter above him and said — in the flat, clattering way crows say things — *grey men, grey men, paper coming.* He had assumed it meant tax collectors.

"How long have you known?" he asked quietly.

"That the purge was coming?" Finch shrugged. "Weeks. There are rumors before proclamations the way there's lightning before thunder. Why do you think I've been selling off stock? Why do you think I asked you to take that carton of things to your cellar last month and told you not to look inside?"

"Because you trusted me."

"Because you're the only person in this city I trust," Finch said, "and trusting you may yet be the death of us both. Go home, Seb. Go home, and don't speak to anything on the way, and don't let anything speak to you where a window might see."

* * *

He broke Finch's rule before he was out of sight of the stall.

It wasn't deliberate. It was the cat.

She lived in the alley behind the chandler's shop, a scarred grey molly with one torn ear whom Sebastian had fed through two winters. She was waiting for him on the wall, as she often was, and when he passed she did what she always did — sat up, curled her tail around her feet, and spoke.

*"Small-king. Bad day. Dogs are quiet. Birds are quiet. Everyone quiet."*

He hadn't taught her the word *small-king*. No one had. It was hers — her name for him, assembled from whatever cats understood of human hierarchies, and he had long ago stopped questioning it. But today her voice had a different texture. Today she wasn't chatting. She was reporting.

"Why is everyone quiet?" he asked, barely moving his lips, eyes forward, a man talking to himself on a rainy street.

*"Grey men walked the wall-top at dawn. Marked doors. Chalk."*

His stomach dropped. "Marked which doors?"

The cat stretched, unhurried, and looked at him with the ancient indifference of her kind. *"Doors of people who smell of the old blood. Not yours. Yet."* Then, softer, in the tone cats use for things they find distasteful: *"You should go. Small-king should go far. Cats know what cages look like. We were wild once too."*

He walked home the long way, counting chalk marks without meaning to. Three on Chandler's Lane. One on the tanner's. Two on the house of the widow Osgood, who everyone knew talked to herself in the garden — though Sebastian had heard her once, years ago, and she hadn't been talking to herself at all. She'd been talking to her roses, and the roses had been answering.

The widow Osgood's door bore two marks. His own door, when he reached it, bore none.

Not yet.

* * *

Inside, the fox was waiting.

This was itself an impossibility he had stopped examining weeks ago. The fox had appeared on his doorstep the night after the dying cartographer, Merrick Vane, had pressed a oilcloth-wrapped map into his hands and died saying *the door remembers your name*. The fox had walked in like a creditor, installed himself by the hearth, and begun offering commentary on Sebastian's life with the weary authority of a tutor saddled with a slow student. He was red as rust, large as a hound, and his eyes were the color of old gold coins. He had never given his true name. He answered to Sorrel, when he answered at all.

Tonight he was sitting on the table. Not beside the table. On it, squarely in the middle of Sebastian's supper space, tail wrapped over his paws, watching the door.

"You're wet," Sorrel observed. "And late. And you've spoken to a cat, a stall-keeper, and at least forty pigeons between here and Tallowmarket, if the state of your coat means anything. Pigeons are filthy gossips. Half the city's birds think you're some kind of saint. The other half think you owe them bread."

"The proclamation's posted," Sebastian said. "Thirty days. Assessment, or—"

"*Or what is found shall be taken.* Yes. I heard. Crows carry news faster than clerks." The fox's ears flicked. "Did you count the chalk marks?"

Sebastian went still. "How did you—"

"Cat on the chandler's wall has a loud mind. Loud minds leak." Sorrel yawned, displaying teeth that seemed, just for a moment, too many. "Two marks on the widow Osgood's door. None on yours. Which tells you the Wardens work from rumor first and talent second — the widow talks to her garden in daylight, poor fool, while you've had the sense to keep your conversations indoors. For now."

"There's a list," Sebastian said. "There must be a list. Names. Someone wrote names before the ink was dry, or the proclamation wouldn't exist. Someone decided who we are."

"Now you're thinking like a hunted thing instead of a fool. Progress." The fox tilted his head. "Yes. There's a list. There is always a list, Sebastian. Purges are not spontaneous. They are *compiled*. Somewhere in the Hall of Registers there is a book with your name in it — maybe your true name, maybe just a description, *fair-haired man, east quarter, speaks to beasts* — written by someone who has watched you longer than you'd like to imagine."

The fire popped. Rain ticked against the shutters. Sebastian sat down slowly across from the fox, the map's oilcloth bundle heavy on the shelf above the hearth where it had sat untouched for weeks, because he was afraid of it, because every time he came near it the air changed, because the map hummed — not audibly, but in his teeth, in the roots of his molars, the way a tuning fork hums against bone.

"Crane sent word today," he said. "Before the proclamation. A runner, with a note asking me to call on him tomorrow. He says he can protect me."

Sorrel's tail stopped moving.

That was the thing about the fox. He was mostly motion — the restless economy of a creature built to hunt — and so when he stopped, it landed like a dropped glass.

"Crane," Sorrel repeated. "Your patron. Master Aldery Crane, collector of curiosities, keeper of the Museum of Antiquities, friend of the Crown Regent, owner of the third-largest house in Greyhollow and the largest collection of locked rooms." The fox's voice had gone smooth and cold, like river ice. "Let me guess. His note was kind."

"It was kind," Sebastian admitted. "He said the times are dangerous. He said a man of my... particular talents... would be safer under his roof. He said he'd already spoken to the Registrar."

"Of course he did." Sorrel unfolded himself from the table and paced to the window, where he stood looking out at the rain with his back to Sebastian, which foxes cannot naturally do, and which was therefore a message in itself. "Thirty years ago, when your gift was young and you were younger, Crane found you. Do you remember how?"

"He bought a bird from me. A starling with a broken wing. I'd fixed it — I didn't know fixing it was anything strange — and he saw, and he paid me ten times what the bird was worth, and he became my patron. Paid for my apprenticeship, my lodgings, my mother's medicine when she was ill. He's been generous my whole life."

"Generous," Sorrel said, "is what power calls itself when it's cultivating something. Crane has spent thirty years watering you like a rare plant, Sebastian, and plants grown in pots stay small, and small things are easy to move. He has kept you dependent, isolated, and grateful. Gratitude is the strongest chain ever forged, because the prisoner polishes it himself." The fox turned his head, just enough to show one gold eye. "Now the kingdom burns the gardens, and Crane comes to offer you his greenhouse. Ask yourself what a collector does with the last of a species."

"The last of a—" Sebastian stopped. "What do you mean, the last?"

The fox looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the shelf above the hearth, at the oilcloth bundle that hummed against the bones of the house, and said nothing at all, which from Sorrel was the loudest answer he owned.

"Tomorrow," the fox said finally, "you will go to Crane, because you are going to go regardless of what I say, and because there is something you need to see with your own eyes — the fear in a powerful man's face, and what he does with it. But you will go having eaten, having slept, and having listened to me first. And you will remember three things. One: Crane's kindness has always had a price tag folded inside it. Two: the list exists, and someone fed it. Three—"

He paused. Outside, the rain thickened, and down the street a dog began to bark — not frantically, not angrily, but in the steady, deliberate rhythm of an animal trying to say something to someone who understood. Sebastian's ears pricked without his permission. He knew that rhythm. He had heard it once before, years ago, from a shepherd's dog outside Vell's Crossing, and it had meant *runners on the road, armed, at night.*

"—three," Sorrel finished softly, "they've already started."

Sebastian crossed to the window. Through the gap in the shutters, at the far end of the lane, lantern-light swung — the particular swinging arc of a carried lantern moving fast, held by someone walking with purpose. Two figures in grey. And ahead of them, at the widow Osgood's gate, a third figure in a nightgown, standing barefoot in the rain, arms spread wide in front of her own door like a woman defending a hearth from wolves.

Her voice carried down the lane, thin and clear and utterly unafraid.

"You'll not touch my roses," the widow Osgood was saying. "You'll not touch my roses, you grey-faced boys, they've done nothing but bloom—"

The lanterns stopped at her gate.

In the hearth, the map hummed. In the street, the first name on the list stood barefoot in the rain. And Sebastian Fairweather, beast-speaker, heir of nothing and nobody, watched the Veilwardens raise their hands toward an old woman's door and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that thirty days was a lie.

The taking had already begun.
