## 9. Out Through the Rat-Runs

The lanterns did not burn the widow Osgood's roses.

That was the first thing Sebastian learned about how it would be done — that there would be no fire, no smashing, none of the theatre of cruelty he had imagined all his life when he pictured what the Veilwardens were. They were polite. That was the horror of it. They knocked on her gate as if they were tradesmen come to measure for shutters, and one of them even bowed to her, and she stood in the rain in her bare feet with her nightgown plastered to her spine and told them about the roses because she could not think of anything else to say, because no one had ever taught her what a person says when the law arrives at her door at midnight with its hands already open.

They put something over her mouth. Not a gag — a strip of grey silk, fine as mourning ribbon, and where it touched her lips the words stopped. Not muffled. *Stopped.* Sebastian heard the shape of her last sentence die inside her throat like a bird striking glass, and then the widow Osgood was silent in a way he had never heard any living creature be silent, not even the dying.

He was across the lane and three houses down, pressed into the recessed doorway of the cooper's shop, and every animal within earshot was screaming.

Not aloud. In the way they screamed to him — a flood of grey terror pouring through the dark, rat-thoughts and cat-thoughts and the frantic pulse of pigeons bursting upward from their roosts under the eaves. The dogs of Lantern Row howled, and the Veilwardens did not look at them, and that frightened him worst of all. Anyone else would have looked. These men moved through a street full of screaming animals as though the animals were weather.

*Come away,* said a voice at his ankle, low and dry and furious. *Come away now, or I will bite you, boy, and I want you to understand that I have never said that to anyone I liked.*

The fox sat in the shadow between two rain barrels, and his eyes caught the lantern-light and gave back nothing.

"They're taking her," Sebastian whispered. "She's just an old woman. She grows roses."

"Yes," the fox said. "And in thirty days' time they'll have done it to everyone whose blood remembers, and your map will be a curiosity in some Warden's cabinet, and I will have wasted a great deal of my considerable patience on a boy who watches through windows." His tail flicked once, sharp as a blade being drawn. "The taking has begun. The thirty days are for the ones who run slowly."

Sebastian's hand found the doorframe behind him and gripped it. Inside the cooper's shop, past the dark windows, he could hear the heartbeat of a dog chained in the yard — fast, fast, fast — and beneath it, faintly, the hum of the map against his chest, steady as a second heart of its own.

"If I go out that gate," he said, "if I make them stop—"

"Then you will be the second name on the list instead of the first, and the list does not care about the order." The fox rose, unhurried, rain sliding off his coat. "Grief later. Running now. There is a difference between the dead and the stupid, and I am trying very hard to keep you on the correct side of it."

Down the lane, the Veilwardens were walking the widow Osgood away. She went between them without struggling, small and barefoot and silent, and the rain fell on all of them equally, which seemed suddenly like the cruelest fact in the world. At the corner she turned her head, just once, back toward her gate and her roses and the dark shape of her own front door, and Sebastian saw her face and knew that he would see it again — every night, probably, for the rest of his life, whether he lived long enough to have one or not.

Then the corner took her, and the lanterns followed, and Lantern Row was only rain again.

*Now,* the fox said, and ran.

* * *

They went down through the city the way water goes down through a roof — finding every flaw, every seam, every place the builders had never meant anyone to fit.

The fox led and Sebastian followed, and it was strange, he thought as he dropped from a wall into a tanner's yard, how little he had ever truly known the city he'd been born in. He had known the streets, the honest streets, the ones with names and lamps and shopfronts. But beneath and behind them lay another Greyhollow entirely — the rat-runs, the fox called them, with something close to affection — the drainage cuts and coal-chutes and collapsed cellar passages that the poor had always known and the rich had always paid to forget. A gap behind the smokehouse wide enough for a thin man sideways. A culvert under Tanner's Lane where the grate had been loose since before Sebastian could remember, because every child in the district knew it and no adult ever bothered to check. Stairs that went down off a courtyard and came up somewhere no staircase should logically be, in a city old enough to have been burned, rebuilt, burned again, and built over its own bones three times.

"The Wardens will seal the gates by dawn," the fox said, flowing along a wall-top with his belly low. "That's how it is done. You take the first name publicly, so the city sees, and quietly you close every road out, so the rest learn that seeing was a kindness. By morning, Greyhollow will be a jar with a lid on it."

"Then we go over the wall."

"We go nowhere near the wall. The wall is where the lid is tightest." The fox paused atop a drying-shed, ears forward, rain beading on his whiskers. "There is a place where the city wall is a lie. Under the old fullers' yards, east quarter, there was once a water-gate. The river moved, two hundred years ago, after the floods. The gate stayed. It opens on a culvert that runs out under the foundations, and it is guarded by exactly no one, because the men who drew the new maps did not bother to walk the ground they were drawing."

"You know this how?"

"I am old," the fox said simply, "and I pay attention. Both are rarer than magic, these days."

They crossed the east quarter through the backs of dye-works and derelict granaries, and Sebastian began, gradually, to notice what the night was telling him. Every dog in the east quarter was awake. Every cat sat in high places, facing the same direction — south, toward the Wardenhouse — like a congregation all turned toward an altar. And everywhere, threaded through the fear, he felt the other thing. The listening. Animals looking at *him*, marking him, a ripple of attention passing ahead of them through the dark like wind through wheat.

"They know me," he said quietly. "All of them. They're — passing word."

"They've always known you. Tonight they know what you are *for*." The fox did not slow. "A city full of beasts has been waiting a long time for a man who can hear them. Do not let it flatter you. Gratitude in animals is a short coin and spends badly."

But when they dropped into the drowned yard of the old fullers' works, something was waiting for them anyway: a big grey rat, sitting upright on a broken washing-stone in the rain, utterly unafraid, watching Sebastian with small black eyes that held something no rat's eyes should hold.

*East door,* the rat thought at him, and the thought arrived clean and deliberate, shaped like a gift. *Water-gate. Bar lifted three nights past. We remembered.*

Sebastian stared at it. "You — you lifted it? For me?"

*Rats lift things,* the rat thought, with the faint dry edge of something almost like laughter. *It is what we are. Go, speaker. The city is going to be a bad place to be a friend of yours.*

It flowed off the stone and into the dark, and Sebastian stood a moment in the rain with his heart doing something complicated in his chest.

"They've been preparing," he said. "The rats. Three nights — before the proclamation. Before Crane—" He stopped. The name sat in his mouth like a stone.

The fox looked up at him, and for a moment said nothing at all.

"Yes," the fox said finally. "Some things were moving before the move became visible. Come. The culvert won't wait, and neither will the dawn."

* * *

The water-gate was exactly where the rat had promised: a squat arch of black stone at the base of the old wall, half-drowned in the rechannelled river's sluggish remnant, its iron grille swung open like a jaw. Beyond it, a culvert ran straight into the hillside under the wall's foundations, tall enough to crouch, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and deep water and distance.

Sebastian hesitated at the lip of the arch.

Behind him: Greyhollow, the only city he had ever known. The crooked rooftops, the bell-tower of Saint Ferrel's leaning like a tired old man, the lamplighter making his rounds even tonight, even now, because lamplighters lit lamps — that was the whole of the profession. Somewhere back in those streets was Crane's house, with its good wine and its warm study and the chair Sebastian had sat in a hundred evenings while the old man talked about trade routes and patronage and the value of discretion. Somewhere back there was the widow Osgood's garden, and the silence where her voice used to be.

"I can't ever come back," he said. It wasn't a question. He said it anyway, to hear it in the open air.

"Not through a gate," the fox agreed. "Doors can be reopened. Gates get watched. There's a difference, and it matters." He stepped onto the wet stone of the threshold, delicate as a dancer, and looked back. "Well, heir of Veilmark. Your kingdom starts with a drain. Most kingdoms start worse."

"That's not funny."

"No," the fox said. "It isn't. Which is why I said it."

Sebastian pulled the map's case tighter under his coat, touched the hum of it once through the oilskin like a man touching a talisman, and crouched into the dark of the culvert.

It ran for a quarter mile under the earth, and it was the longest quarter mile of his life. Water sucked at his boots. The dark was total except for the faint grey suggestion of the exit growing by degrees ahead, and beside him the fox moved as a shape and a presence and occasionally a low voice counting turns — *left here; mind the drop; this is where the ceiling remembers it used to be a chimney* — until at last the culvert spat them out through a screen of alder roots onto the bank of the old river channel, and Sebastian stood up under open sky for the first time in hours.

Greyhollow sat behind him on its low hill, a smudge of lamplight and smoke under the rain. From out here it looked small. That was somehow worse than everything else — that the whole weight of it, the whole trap, could shrink to a smudge in ten minutes' walking.

They walked north along the dead channel until the city's light failed entirely, and then the fox stopped.

"There is a farmstead," he said, "two nights out, at the edge of the ash orchards. Before that, nothing but road and rain. We move at night and lie flat by day. And boy—" the fox's voice changed, lost its dryness, went quiet and level in a way that made Sebastian stop walking, "—before the road takes your legs and the fear takes your head, you should decide what story you are telling yourself about Crane."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the proclamation named the purge before the Wardens announced it. I mean the cartographer who made your map died conveniently before he could tell you more than half of what he knew. I mean a patron with Crane's money and Crane's reach knew exactly which door in Greyhollow to leave unbarred, and which window, and which lonely apprentice beast-speaker to feed." The fox's eyes glinted in the dark. "I have watched men hunt for a long time, boy. The cleverest ones never chase. They open gates and wait."

Sebastian's hand closed around the map case until the oilskin creaked.

"He taught me to read," Sebastian said. "When I was fourteen. He bought me boots when mine split. He—" His voice did something unsteady and he hated it. "He can't be. Because if he is, then everyone who was ever kind to me was a trap, and I can't — I won't live in a world like that."

"No," the fox agreed softly. "You won't. That is rather the point of traps." He turned and trotted north along the black ribbon of the old road, and his voice floated back through the rain, dry again, almost gentle. "Two nights to the orchards. Walk, Fairweather. Grieve with your feet."

And Sebastian Fairweather — beast-speaker, heir of a drowned kingdom, first name on a list that had no end — wiped his face with the back of his hand, pretended the rain explained the wetness on it, and walked north out of the light of the only home he had ever known.

Behind him, far off in the dark of the city, a bell began to ring.

Not the hour-bell. Not the alarm.

The gathering bell — ringing the way it rang when the Veilwardens wanted every lantern in Greyhollow brought out at once, every street searched, every door knocked upon. Ringing, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach, for him.

The fox paused at the edge of the fields, ears swiveled back toward the sound.

"Hm," he said. "Faster than I expected. Someone wants you found *quickly*." He glanced at Sebastian, and there was something in the look that was almost — almost — sympathy. "Run, then. The orchards are two nights out, and I suspect we are about to learn exactly how much of that distance is safe."

They ran, north, into the rain, and the bell rang on behind them like a heart that would not stop counting.
