## 10. The Road of Ash Orchards

The city let them go the way a fist lets go of sand — grudgingly, grain by grain, and not without leaving its mark.

Sebastian had slipped out through the rat-runs before dawn, Tallow trotting at his heel with the map rolled into a hollow reed strapped along the fox's back like a soldier's bedroll. It had been Tallow's idea. Of course it had been Tallow's idea. The fox had opinions about everything — about the weight of Sebastian's pack, about the wisdom of bringing the brass compass, about the particular stupidity of men who waited until their world was burning before they thought to leave it.

"You realize," Tallow had said, three streets from the wall, his voice a dry rasp under the hiss of the cisterns, "that we are doing exactly what every doomed creature does. Moving at night. Trusting a dead woman's ink. Walking toward the thing that killed her."

"I realize you could have stayed," Sebastian said.

The fox considered this with the flat golden stare he gave to all of Sebastian's arguments, as though each one were a mouse that had made the mistake of showing itself. "Yes," he said finally. "Well. Boredom is also a kind of death, and slower."

Now the wall was behind them and the sun was up, and the road ran white and empty between fields gone to seed. Greyhollow's smoke still smudged the southern sky, a gray thumbprint on the horizon that Sebastian kept catching himself looking at, the way a man worries a loose tooth. Somewhere back there were Marta and the others from the charm-market, the ones who hadn't run fast enough or hadn't run at all. Somewhere back there was Crane, with his kind offer and his soft voice and his eyes like ledger entries.

"Don't," Tallow said, without turning his head. He had learned to read the set of Sebastian's shoulders the way other creatures read wind.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Your left shoulder dropped. That's the thinking-about-going-back slump." The fox paused at the roadside to mark a fencepost with a businesslike flick of his nose. "You can't go back. Not because of the Wardens. Because of what you're carrying." He nodded his sharp chin at Sebastian's chest, where the map's second copy lay folded against his skin, ink-side in. "Dead women don't write letters twice."

* * *

By midmorning the fields gave way to the orchards, and Sebastian understood at last why the old road was called what it was called.

Ash trees. Thousands of them, planted in ranks so straight and so ancient that they made the eye ache, marching away eastward in long gray-green avenues toward a horizon that never seemed to arrive. Their bark was pale as bone in the slanting light. Their leaves turned late and fell early, and when they fell they came down in a slow silver drift that looked, from a distance, like snow falling upward. The air beneath them was cool and dim and smelled faintly of smoke, though no fire had touched this place in living memory.

"They were planted by Veilmark," Sebastian said quietly. He had worked it out somewhere around the third avenue of trees, watching the way the rows bent — not randomly, but in long shallow curves, like water finding a channel. "The whole orchard. It's not an orchard. It's a diagram."

"It's a map," Tallow said. "A big one. Laid out on the ground for anyone who could read it." He sat down in the middle of the road, which was a thing only Tallow would do, and looked up through the canopy. "Which is why your cartographer came this way, I expect. Ilsa Vane didn't find the door because she was clever. She found it because she could read trees."

Sebastian took out his copy of the map and held it up against the rows. The curves matched. The great bend where the road swung north matched the sweep of a dry streambed two fields over. Even the little annotation in Vane's cramped hand — *the trees remember the river* — resolved itself suddenly into sense. The ash rows followed the course of a river that had died centuries ago, and the door of Veilmark, if Vane's ink was true, lay somewhere along that buried water, in the hills where the orchards thinned and the mountains began.

"The trees remember the river," he repeated. His voice sounded strange to him, hushed, as if the avenue itself were listening. "Then the trees might remember more."

"Mm." Tallow stretched, yawned enormously to make the point that none of this impressed him, and got up. "Trees remember. Trees don't talk. There's a difference, and the difference is why you need me."

They walked on. The light came down through the leaves in coins and moved over them as they went, and for a while — an hour, perhaps two — the road was kind to them. Sebastian ate hard bread and a pear bought from a farmwife who had looked at him too long and asked no questions. Tallow hunted the ditch-banks and came back once with nothing and once with a vole he ate in four contemptuous bites. They passed a shrine at a crossroads, one of the crown's new ones, whitewashed and raw, bearing the Veilwardens' sigil: a closed eye above crossed keys. Someone had thrown mud at it. Someone else had washed the mud off. The argument was written there in dirt and clean stone, and both sides had lost.

It was Tallow who heard them first.

He stopped so abruptly that Sebastian walked two paces past him before he noticed, and when he turned, the fox was standing rigid in the road, ears forward, nose working the air out of the north.

"What?"

"Horses," Tallow said. "Three. Maybe four. Coming up the road behind us, but not on the road — alongside it, in the tree-shadow, walking slow." A beat. "Riders don't walk slow unless they're looking for something. Or someone."

Sebastian felt the cold go through him like well-water. "Wardens."

"Or Crane's people. He'll have sent riders after us the moment he understood the map was gone. He's sentimental, our patron, but he's not stupid." Tallow's tail had gone low and still, which on Tallow was the equivalent of another creature baring its teeth. "Either way, we are currently a pair of very visible shapes standing in the middle of a bright road having a conversation. Move."

They moved. Off the road, into the deep leaf-mold between the trunks, where the silver drift of fallen ash-leaves muffled their steps and the dimness folded around them. Sebastian went down behind a trunk wide enough to hide a kneeling man and pressed himself against the bark, heart going like a drum in a procession. Tallow poured himself flat beside him, all edges vanished, just another shadow with eyes.

The riders came. Three of them, walking their horses along the verge, and even at fifty paces Sebastian knew the cut of their coats — the slate-gray of the Veilwardens, the closed-eye badge dull on their shoulders. They moved through the avenues the way men move through a house they believe is empty, heads turning, one of them trailing a hand near a lantern that wasn't lit yet. Not hunting at speed. Sweeping. As if the road itself were a corridor to be cleared, room by room.

And then the strangest thing happened, and Sebastian would carry it with him for the rest of his life.

The horses stopped.

All three, nearly together, within the space of a breath. The lead rider gathered his reins and put his heels to his mount and the horse simply would not — it sidled, it tossed its head, it planted its feet in the leaf-litter and stared off into the trees with its ears pinned flat, staring at nothing, staring at —

At Sebastian. At the place where Sebastian knelt.

"It's me," Sebastian breathed. "Tallow, they know. The horses can smell it — my gift, the beasts can always—" His gift had never hidden well. Dogs had loved him too much and deer had feared him too much and there had never once, in thirty years, been an animal whose behavior around him was ordinary. Now, in the moment he most needed to be invisible, the whole living world insisted on pointing at him.

"Yes," Tallow said softly. "I know. Hold still."

"Hold still? They'll—"

"Hold. Still." And then the fox did something Sebastian had never seen him do. He stood up, deliberately, in plain view of the riders, stepped out of the shadow-line into a stripe of light, and sat down in the middle of the open ground like a cat presenting itself to a window.

The lead rider saw him instantly. Of course he did. A fox, sitting in the open, in daylight, looking directly at armed men — it was wrong in the specific way that things are wrong when magic has brushed past them. The rider's hand went to his sword. Then stopped. Because the fox was only a fox, thin and ragged and unremarkable, and because the horses had begun, slowly, to relax — the lead mare dropping her head, blowing out, her fear draining away like heat from a stone.

Because Tallow was drinking the attention. Drinking it out of the air. Sebastian could feel it happening, the way you feel a draft from a opened door — the pressure of being-perceived sliding sideways, off him, onto the fox, and the fox wearing it lightly, a borrowed coat, letting the riders' eyes snag on his small rust-colored body while the larger shape behind the tree went soft and unfelt.

The riders argued among themselves for a moment — Sebastian caught fragments, *nothing out here, birdsong, waste of a day* — and then walked their horses on, north along the avenue, and the sound of them faded into the silver light and was gone.

Sebastian stayed frozen a full minute longer than he needed to. When he finally turned, Tallow was grooming dust off his foreleg with elaborate unconcern.

"How," Sebastian said. "How did you do that."

"Do what?" said the fox.

"That. Whatever that was. You took their eyes off me. You took the *horses'*—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real meant it had to fit somewhere in his understanding of the world, and it didn't fit. Beasts spoke to him. That was his gift, the whole of it, the small crooked inheritance he'd built a life inside. What Tallow had done was something else. Something older.

Tallow finished with his foreleg and looked up, and for once there was nothing flippant in his face at all.

"You've been asking yourself what I am since the night we met," he said. "Underneath the jokes. You ask it the way you ask everything — politely, in the corner of your mind, where it won't disturb you." His tail curled around his feet. "Keep asking. But not today. Today we have miles to cover and those riders will report a fox and no man, and by tomorrow Crane will know that trick of mine exists, and he'll send someone cleverer." He rose and shook himself, all business again, the moment folding shut behind him like water closing over a stone. "Walk. North. Where the trees stop remembering, we start digging."

* * *

They made nine miles before dark, following the ghost of the dead river deeper into the orchards, and camped in the hollow root-bole of an ash so old that three men joining hands couldn't have ringed it. Sebastian risked no fire. They ate cold food and watched the last silver light drain out of the canopy, and the silence of the orchard settled over them like a blanket with weight to it.

It was almost full dark when Sebastian heard the singing.

Faint. Far off to the east, threading through the trees — not a human voice, or not only. Something between a voice and a wind through wires, rising and falling in a pattern that repeated, and repeated, and was not quite the same each time. It raised the hair on his arms. Every instinct he owned as a beast-speaker told him the sound was alive, and every instinct he owned as a man told him to lie down and be small until morning.

"Tallow."

"I hear it." The fox had come to stand at the lip of the root-hollow, facing east, utterly motionless. "Been hearing it for an hour. Decided you didn't need to know until you'd slept some." He glanced back, and his eyes caught the starlight and threw it back flat and gold. "It's the trees."

"The trees are *singing*?"

"The trees are remembering out loud. Old ones do, sometimes, in places where a lot happened." Tallow's voice had gone quiet in a way Sebastian had learned to distrust, because quiet Tallow was honest Tallow, and honest Tallow said things that rearranged furniture. "This whole valley was a border once. Veilmark's edge. Everything inside the orchard line belonged to the kingdom, and the kingdom taught its trees to keep the tally. Counting what came in. Counting what went out." A pause. "Counting what was taken."

Sebastian sat with that. Above them the ash canopy stirred without wind, and the song threaded down through it, and now that he listened with the listening his gift gave him, he could almost catch the shape of it — not words, nothing so kind as words, but numbers, seasons, the long arithmetic of a country keeping its own books.

"It knows we're here," he said. "Doesn't it. The whole time — the horses, the riders — the orchard saw all of it."

"Oh, certainly." Tallow said it the way another creature might say *the sun came up*. "We've been announced. To whatever's left to receive announcements." He turned his head east, toward the black mass of the hills where the orchards ended, where somewhere in a hollow the last door of Veilmark waited under roots and stone and however many years of forgetting. "Which means everything between here and that door already knows we're coming. The birds. The badgers. The things in the hills that aren't quite either." His tail moved once, slow. "Some of them will want to help you, Doorkeeper. Some of them will want to eat you. And at least one of them, if the stories I grew up on are worth the breath, has been waiting at the edge of these orchards for a very long time for someone with your blood to come walking north."

"What kind of someone?"

"The bargaining kind," Tallow said. "Sleep. Tomorrow we cross the shallows at the old ford, and after the ford, the stories start coming true."

Sebastian lay back against the root-wood and pulled his coat over him and did not sleep, not really — drifted instead in that half-country between waking and dream where the orchard's song came clearest, numbers and names and a grief so old it had become music. And in the grey hour before dawn, at the exact moment the singing changed — shifted key, quickened, became almost urgent — he opened his eyes and saw, standing at the edge of the root-hollow in the mist, a figure that was not a fox and not a bird and not anything the city had ever taught him a name for.

It looked at him. It smiled, in the way that things without human faces can smile.

Then it beckoned, once, toward the north and the ford and the hills beyond — and was gone, and the orchard held its breath, and Tallow said quietly from the darkness:

"That'll be Sorrel. Come on, then. Time to hear the price."
