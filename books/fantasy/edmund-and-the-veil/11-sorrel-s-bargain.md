## 11. Sorrel's Bargain

The fox led them north through the orchard for the better part of an hour, and it never once looked back to see if they followed. That, Sebastian thought, was its own kind of message: *I know you'll come. I've known since before you knew.*

Tallow walked close at his heel, and even she kept her voice low.

"Don't bargain with it," she murmured as they picked their way between the ash-white trunks. "Not yet. Let it speak first. Bargainers who open their mouths first always pay more."

"You've done this before."

"I've been poor my whole life, boy. Same thing."

The trees thinned. The ground rose in long grey swells toward a line of hills that looked, in the failing light, like the backs of sleeping animals. And there, where the last of the orchard gave way to open moor, stood a stone.

It was not a standing stone, exactly — those were common enough on the old roads, leaning relics of some faith nobody remembered. This one was *seated*. It had the shape of a hunched figure, weathered nearly smooth, its face worn to a suggestion of features — two hollows for eyes, a long groove for a mouth. Moss grew along its shoulders like a mantle. Someone had once set offerings in the cup of its lap: a bird's skull gone to chalk, a rusted coin, a twist of dried flowers that might have been marigolds a decade ago.

And sitting on the stone's knee, tail curled neat around his feet, was the fox.

Up close he was larger than foxes had any right to be, and his coat was not one color but many, shifting between russet and ash and a deep ember-red depending on how the light caught him — or perhaps depending on what he wished the light to catch. His eyes were amber, but old amber, the kind with things trapped inside.

"Sebastian Fairweather," the fox said. His voice was dry and pleasant, like leaves moving over leaves. "Beast-speaker. Charm-seller. Liar of small necessity. And — as of three days ago — a dead man walking, if the Veilwardens have their arithmetic right."

"You know my name."

"I knew your grandmother's name, which is more to the point." The fox tilted his head. "You may call me Sorrel. Everyone does eventually. Some call me other things first."

Tallow's hackles had risen from her neck to her tail. "What *are* you?" she said flatly.

"A question with a long answer and a short one." Sorrel licked one paw with maddening calm. "The short one is: I am the last servant of a kingdom that no longer exists, bound to wait until someone came looking for its door. The long one can wait until we're somewhere with a fire. Which is not here, because by tomorrow's dusk there will be Warden dogs on this moor, and they are *very* good dogs, and I say that with professional respect."

Sebastian crouched slowly, bringing himself level with the creature. He had spoken to animals all his life — sparrows and cart-horses, rats in the walls, once, memorably, a dying swan — and every one of them had answered in the same tongue, wordless and warm, all feeling and little grammar. But Sorrel's mind, when Sebastian reached toward it, was like touching a locked door from the outside. There was something behind it, watching him reach.

"You're shielding," Sebastian said quietly. "I can't hear you. Not really."

"No," Sorrel agreed. "You can't." Something flickered behind the amber eyes — not quite warmth, but the memory of it. "That is the first honest thing anyone has said to me in forty years, so I'll give you something honest back: your gift is louder than you think, Fairweather. You've been whispering to beasts your whole life and calling it nothing. In Veilmark, men with a tenth of your voice commanded garrisons of hawks."

"Veilmark." The name came out of him half-strangled. "The map. The cartographer — he wrote—"

"I know what he wrote. I told him to write it." Sorrel rose, unhurried, and dropped from the stone's knee. "Which brings us, neatly, to the price."

* * *

They walked while he talked, because Sorrel refused to talk standing still — "Standing still is how prey dies," he said, "and also how conversations die, which is worse" — following a track that no cart would ever find, up over the moor's brown shoulders toward the darkening line of the hills.

"The kingdom of Veilmark," Sorrel said, "was real. It sat in these mountains when Greyhollow was a fishing camp and the crown was a bandit with better manners. Its people did not *have* magic the way your Wardens use the word — spells, charms, fire from fingers. They had something older. They could *speak*, as you do. To beast and tree and river and stone. Everything alive has a voice, Fairweather. Most folk are simply deaf. Veilmark's kings were the ones who listened."

"And then?"

"And then the last king made a mistake that kings are built to make." The fox's tail flicked. "He feared losing what he had. So he built a door — a great working, woven through every gifted bloodline in the land — a door that could hold all the speaking of Veilmark inside it, safe from thieves and tyrants. A vault for magic. And when the crown's armies came over the passes, four hundred years ago, the Doorkeeper of that age opened the vault and poured everything in, and shut it, and the kingdom went silent. No voices. No gifts. Just people, suddenly ordinary, waiting for an army."

"It didn't save them."

"It saved the *magic*. That was the design flaw." Sorrel glanced back at him, and for a moment the ember in his coat burned very bright. "The door still stands. The magic is still behind it. And the door opens only for the bloodline of the Doorkeepers — thinned, scattered, forgotten, but not broken. Your bloodline, Sebastian. The cartographer found the last thread of it and spent his final years mapping the way. Why do you think a dying man gave a charm-seller his life's work? Sentiment? He was *paying a debt*. To me."

The moor dropped away into a shallow valley, and there — black against the last violet of the sky — stood the ruin of a shepherd's bothy, its roof fallen in, its walls shoulder-high. Sorrel trotted toward it without breaking stride.

"Here we make our fire and our bargain. Sit."

* * *

Tallow found dry bracken; Sebastian coaxed a flame from his kit with flint and patience, the old way, the way that got a man arrested in Greyhollow if a Warden saw the sparks. When the fire had settled to a low gold heart, Sorrel sat across from them, outlined in ember-light, and became very still in the way of creatures about to be entirely honest.

"Now," he said. "The price."

Sebastian folded his hands. Tallow had told him not to speak first. He didn't.

"There are two doors ahead of you, boy, and only one of them is stone." Sorrel's ears angled forward. "The first is the Warden road. Crane's men will sweep the orchards by week's end, then the ford, then the hill country. If they take you, you hang — or worse, because Crane doesn't want you dead, he wants you *cooperative*, and Crane's methods of cooperation are why grown men in Greyhollow leave the city rather than owe him money. The second door is mine. North, through Marrow Ford, into the mountains, to the hollow where the door stands. Open it, and everything changes."

"And your price for showing me the way?"

"My price is not coin." Sorrel let the silence stretch until the fire popped. "My price is a choice, made freely, at the threshold. When you stand before the door — and you will stand before it, because you're already too far in to turn back, we both know it — you will choose. Seal it forever, and the gift dies in the world: no more beast-speakers, no more Veilwardens hunting them, no more magic for anyone ever again. Your friends live small and safe and deaf. Or open it, and the speaking returns to the world — all of it, everywhere, uncontrolled. Every gifted child born after that night wakes with a voice. Every tyrant with a drop of the old blood wakes with one too. Including Crane, whose grandmother, I happen to know, was a Veilmark woman who fled south."

Sebastian felt the cold come up through the ground into his bones. "You want me to open it."

"I want you to *choose*," Sorrel said sharply. "I have waited four hundred years for a Doorkeeper to walk up that mountain, and I have watched lesser men than you be offered less than this and prevaricate until the moment passed. I am tired, Fairweather. I am so profoundly tired. My price is that you choose honestly — not out of fear, not out of Crane's knife at your throat, not because a fox talked you into it on a cold hillside. You choose with your eyes open, knowing what each path costs. That is the whole of my bargain. That, and one thing more."

"Which is?"

"When you pour the gift through the door — *if* you pour it through the door — it will take something from you. The door does not run on goodwill. It runs on the Doorkeeper's own voice. All of it." Sorrel's gaze held his, unblinking. "You will walk down that mountain the most ordinary man in the world. Deaf to every sparrow, every horse, every rat in every wall, for the rest of your life. That is the cost, and I will not soften it, and I will not lie about it, and if you say yes tonight you say yes to *that* as much as to anything."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, a nightjar called, and Sebastian heard it the way he had heard ten thousand nights of birdsong — clearly, intimately, a small mind saying *warm-dark-moths-good* — and understood that he was hearing it, perhaps, for one of the last few times in his life.

Tallow spoke first. "And if he refuses? If he chooses to seal it?"

"Then I show him the way anyway, and he seals it, and I go back to sitting on stones for another four centuries." Sorrel shrugged, a ripple through his coat. "A bargain is a bargain. I don't get what I want. I get what was promised. The promise was: a Doorkeeper, brought to the door, choosing freely. Nothing in it says he must choose as I'd wish."

"Why?" Sebastian asked. It came out rougher than he meant. "Why does it matter to you so much? What are you, truly, that you've waited four hundred years?"

Sorrel looked into the fire for a long time. When he answered, the leaf-dry voice had something under it, old and worn.

"I was the king's speaker," he said. "His voice to the beasts of Veilmark, when the kingdom lived. When the door was sealed, the last king bound his servants into the working so that the magic would have wardens until it woke again. I have been a fox for four hundred years, Fairweather. I remember being a man the way you remember a house you lived in as a child. I would like, before the end, to stop being a fox." His eyes lifted. "If the door opens, the binding breaks. That is my hope. That is my sin. Make of it what you will."

Silence, except the fire and the wind coming down off the hills, carrying the smell of rain and stone.

Tallow nudged Sebastian's ankle — a small pressure, deliberate. Through the beast-bond, faint as breath, came her feeling: not words, never words, but a shape of meaning. *Fear. And beneath it, stay-with-me. And beneath that, whatever-you-choose, I-go-with-you.*

He put his hand on her head and looked at the fox.

"If I say yes," Sebastian said slowly, "you guide us past the Wardens, past Crane, through the ford and the mountains. You tell me true, all the way — no more half-truths dressed as riddles."

"Agreed."

"And if I learn you've lied to me — about the cost, about the door, about *anything* —"

"Then you may leave me on the mountain," Sorrel said simply. "I won't resist. A four-hundred-year-old fox is not hard to leave."

Sebastian breathed out. The whole day lay behind him — the proclamation nailed to the Warden-house door, the crowd's faces turning toward him, the cartographer's map heavy against his ribs, Crane's name in his mouth like a bad tooth. Three days ago he had been a charm-seller. Now he was a bloodline.

"Yes," he said. "I'll choose at the door. Freely. Eyes open. That's my oath."

Sorrel closed his eyes briefly, and for just a moment the fox looked less like a trickster and more like a soldier relieved of a long watch.

"Then it's sworn," he said. "Sleep now. We move at first light, and we move fast — because, Fairweather, I must tell you the worst news of the evening, and I saved it for after the bargain, as bargainers do."

Sebastian's stomach dropped. "What worst news?"

"The Wardens aren't sweeping the orchards by week's end." Sorrel opened his eyes, and the amber in them caught the firelight like a trap catches sun. "Crane's riders passed through the valley below us at dusk. They're already on the moor. And they're not searching for you, boy."

"They're not?"

"No." Sorrel rose, shook himself, and trotted to the lip of the hollow, where he stood silhouetted against the starlit hills, ears pricked toward the north. "They're riding for Marrow Ford ahead of you. Crane knows about the door. He's known for years. And by tomorrow night, unless we outrun them—" the fox turned his head, and smiled the smile of old, tired things, "—there will be a man waiting at the tollhouse who is not the tollman."

Far off, down the valley, thin on the wind, came the sound of hooves.
