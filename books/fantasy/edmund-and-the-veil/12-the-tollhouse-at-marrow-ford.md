## 12. The Tollhouse at Marrow Ford

The rain found them an hour before Marrow Ford did.

It came down out of the western hills in a grey wall, first as a whisper in the ash orchards, then as a drumming on the dead branches, then as a cold that worked through Sebastian's coat and settled against his skin like a debt being collected. The orchard road had been empty for two days — rows of blackened trees marching beside them like mourners at a funeral no one would name — and now even the trees seemed to hunch away from the weather.

"We should have taken the ferry at Corben's Landing," Sebastian said, for the third time that morning.

"The ferry keeps lists," said the fox trotting ahead of him, tail low, ears flat against the rain. His name was Wick, though he had not offered that freely; Sebastian had pried it out of him somewhere outside Ashford, along with perhaps a tenth of everything else the animal knew. "Lists have names on them. Names get read by people with grey cloaks and bad intentions. Would you like to be on a list, Sebastian?"

"I'd like to be dry."

"You'd like to be alive. Dryness is a luxury of the unimportant." Wick shook himself, flinging water in a bright arc, and stopped so abruptly that Sebastian nearly trod on his tail. "There. Look."

Through the rain-haze, where the orchard road bent down toward the river, stood a tollhouse.

It was a squat stone building crouched at the edge of the ford like a toad at the lip of a well, its roof sagging under centuries of slate, its single chimney breathing a thin ribbon of smoke into the wet air. A lantern burned in the window — the first light Sebastian had seen in two days that wasn't lightning. Beside the house, the river ran high and brown and fast, chewing at its banks, and across the water he could just make out the far road climbing into hills that were not ash-grey but green.

Green. He had almost forgotten what color living country was.

"A tollhouse," he said slowly. "On a road nobody uses."

"On a road nobody *admits* to using," Wick corrected. "Which is different, and better, if you're the sort who collects tolls." The fox's eyes caught the lantern-light even from this distance, amber coins in a wet grey world. "Someone lives there. Someone cooks there. And someone, I'd wager my second-best lie, sells passage across that ford to people who can't afford to be seen crossing it."

Sebastian looked at the river. It was wide here, thirty feet at least, and swollen with the rain, white-capped where it broke over submerged stones. The ford itself was marked by leaning posts, most of them drowned to their tops. Even in summer it would have been a hard crossing. Now it looked like a way to die politely, with your boots filled first.

"I can't swim that," he said. "Not with the pack. Not in this current."

"No," Wick agreed pleasantly. "You can't."

"So we knock on the door."

"So we knock on the door," the fox said, and for just a moment something moved behind his eyes — something old and wary and calculating, gone before Sebastian could name it. "But let me do the talking about prices. You do the talking about anything else. Agreed?"

"Why does that matter?"

"Because," said Wick, starting down toward the lantern, "you're a terrible liar, and toll-keepers are the finest liars God ever made. It's best we each stay in our lane."

* * *

The woman who answered the door was not what Sebastian expected, which he supposed was the point of her.

She was small and broad and sixty if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope and a face weathered to the color of the stones her house was built from. She wore a man's oilskin coat over a dress that might once have been red, and she held a boat-hook in one hand the way other women held knitting needles — casually, comfortably, with the clear implication that she knew exactly how to use it.

She looked at Sebastian. Then down at the fox. Then back up at Sebastian.

"Toll's three coppers," she said. "Four if you're carrying anything worth stealing. Five if the fox talks."

Wick's ears went back. "I *beg* your pardon—"

"Five," the woman repeated, unmoved, and stepped aside to let them in.

Inside, the tollhouse was warm and close and smelled of woodsmoke, tallow, and river mud. A fire cracked in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. Copper pots hung from beams. A kettle sang quietly to itself, and a cat — enormous, orange, and missing half an ear — regarded them from atop a barrel with the flat disdain of minor royalty.

"Sit," the woman said, pointing the boat-hook at a bench by the fire. "Tea's a copper extra. Stew's two. Don't touch the cat."

Sebastian sat. His whole body ached with gratitude for the heat. Wick flowed onto the hearthstones, arranged himself with his back to the flames, and watched the woman with the fixed attention of a creature doing arithmetic.

"You'll cross at first light," she said, ladling stew into a bowl without asking whether he wanted any. She set it in front of him. It was good stew — barley, river fish, some root vegetable he didn't recognize — and he was halfway through it before he remembered to be cautious. "Water's too high tonight. Ford's a liar when it's high. Looks passable. Isn't."

"And the boat?" Sebastian asked.

"There's no boat."

"But you take tolls."

"I take tolls for the *ford*. The river takes whatever it likes." She said it without a flicker of humor, and Sebastian understood that this was, in fact, the entire business model. "I've pulled eleven people out of that water in nineteen years. Buried four. The rest learned to wait for morning like sensible folk."

Wick made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "And the five coppers buys us the privilege of waiting in your kitchen."

"The five coppers buys you the privilege of waiting in my kitchen *instead of on the road*, where the Veilwardens' riders passed not six hours ago, heading east, asking after a young man traveling with an animal that answers questions." She refilled his bowl. Her face gave nothing away. "That'll be another copper. The information was free. The second helping isn't."

Sebastian's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Riders," he said carefully. "How many?"

"Six. Grey cloaks. Horses too fine for the road they were on." She sat down across from him, unhurried, and poured tea into three cups — Sebastian noticed, and filed away, that one of them was set down by the fox without comment or surprise. "They showed me a paper with a drawing on it. Young fellow, dark hair, city clothes gone shabby. Asked had I seen him." She sipped her tea. "I told them I see nobody. Nobody crosses at night, and daylight folk aren't worth remembering."

"What did they say to that?"

"They said the reward for the boy was fifty crowns." She looked at him over the rim of her cup, and for the first time something moved in her face — not kindness exactly, but its rough country cousin. "Fifty crowns is a lot of money for a toll-keeper, boy. You want to know why you're still sitting in my kitchen eating my fish?"

"Yes," Sebastian said honestly.

"Because fifty crowns spends the same whether I earn it or not, but a dead river only forgives so many betrayals." She shrugged, massive shoulders rolling under the oilskin. "Superstition, maybe. Or maybe I've just never liked men in grey cloaks. They tipped badly."

Wick laughed outright at that — a real laugh, rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter. The cat on the barrel opened one eye, decided the fox was beneath comment, and went back to sleep.

"What's your name?" Sebastian asked.

"Marrow Kate. My mother was Marrow Kate too, and hers before her. We keep the ford. That's the whole of it." She jerked her chin at the window, where the rain had thickened into sheets. "Sleep by the fire. First light, I walk you across. And boy—" she waited until he looked up, mouth full, "—your fox is right about one thing. You're a terrible liar. When the riders come back, and they will come back, don't try to talk. Just look stupid. Nature's already done half the work."

* * *

He woke once in the night, and the kitchen was dark, and the voices were low.

"—not your concern," Wick was saying, from somewhere near the door. His voice had changed. Sebastian had heard the fox be sardonic, be weary, be wryly kind in his sideways fashion. He had never heard him sound like this: flat, careful, old. "It's mine. It's been mine since before you were whelped, Kate, and you know it."

"I know what you told me," the woman said. "Told and shown are different animals. Nineteen years I've kept this crossing, and in nineteen years you've come through maybe ten times, always alone, always going the same way — west, toward the mountains, toward the hollow country. Never once have you brought company."

"He's not company. He's cargo."

"Cargo doesn't dream by my fire. Cargo doesn't thank me for stew." A pause; the kettle ticked as it cooled. "What is he, Wick? Truly. Not the story you tell him."

Silence, long enough that Sebastian nearly gave himself away by breathing wrong.

"He's the last of a line that shouldn't exist," Wick said finally, very quietly. "And the door I've been walking toward for longer than you've been alive is going to open for him or it isn't. And if it opens—" the fox's voice did something strange, something that might have been fear, "—then everything I am gets renegotiated. So let me keep my secrets one more week, Kate. After that, ask me anything you like."

The floorboard by the hearth chose that moment to creak under Sebastian's elbow.

The silence that followed had weight. Then Marrow Kate said, loudly, in her ordinary gravel voice: "Second helping's still two coppers, boy, dreaming or not," and padded past him to bank the fire, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone.

Sebastian lay staring at the rafters for a long time afterward, listening to the rain, turning the fox's words over like a coin he wasn't sure was gold or brass.

*Everything I am gets renegotiated.*

He didn't sleep again until near dawn.

* * *

First light came grey and reluctant, the rain thinned to a drifting mist. The river had dropped a hand's width overnight; the tops of the ford posts stood clear of the water now, dripping.

Marrow Kate walked them across herself, as promised — boat-hook in one hand, Sebastian's pack strap gripped in the other, shouting instructions over the rush of the current. *Left foot there. No, THERE. The water lies about depth, trust the posts.* Halfway across, the current hit Sebastian at the hip and tried to fold him sideways, and it was Kate's grip and Wick's teeth in his collar together that kept him upright and moving. Then the far bank rose to meet them, green and dripping, and he stood on living ground coughing river out of his lungs while the toll-keeper watched him with something dangerously close to amusement.

"Fifty crowns," she said, shaking her head. "The world's gone mad." She pressed something into his hand — a small tin token, stamped with the mark of the ford. "Show that at the kiln villages up-valley. There are people past the pass who remember who kept them fed in the famine years. It won't open every door. It'll open enough."

"Kate," Sebastian started. "Your friend, the fox — he said—"

"I know what he said." She cut him off gently, already turning back toward the water. "Nineteen years, boy, I've learned there's two kinds of travelers. The kind running *from* something, and the kind running *toward* something. The ones running from, you help and forget. But you—" she pointed the boat-hook at him, then at the fox sitting damp and inscrutable at the tree line, "—you two are running toward the same thing, and neither of you has told the other what it is. Sort that out before the mountains. Rivers I can get you across. Lies, you drown in on your own."

She waded back into the brown water and was gone into the mist, and the ford took her shape and swallowed it.

Wick was already moving up the trail.

"Wick." Sebastian hurried after him, boots squelching. "Last night. I heard—"

"I know you heard." The fox didn't stop, didn't turn. Mist beaded his fur; his breath smoked. "Kate talks too much for a woman whose trade is silence."

"You said I'm cargo."

"You are. For now."

"And you said — what did you call it — *renegotiated*. Renegotiated how? With whom?" He caught the fox's shoulder, felt the surprising solidity of the small body under the wet fur, and turned him around. Up close, Wick's amber eyes were unreadable as river stones. "You've known what I am since before I did. You came to my shop. You led me to Maren's map. You've been herding me west for weeks, and I have followed you because — because you were the only thing in Greyhollow that made sense when nothing else did. So tell me. What happens at this door?"

For a long moment the fox simply looked at him. Rain dripped off the alder leaves. Somewhere up the valley, a bird sang — the first birdsong Sebastian had heard since the orchards began, thin and startled, as if the land itself was remembering how.

"When Veilmark fell," Wick said at last, quietly, "it didn't die. Kingdoms don't die, Sebastian. They go *inward*. Behind the last door. Everything the bloodline carried — the speaking, the shaping, the old agreements between human speech and beast — it all went home behind the veil, and the door shut, and the world got quieter and crueler and called it law and order." His tail moved once, slow. "A door that shuts can open. And a kingdom that's been holding its breath for three hundred years is going to exhale. When it does, everything borrowed goes back. Every gift. Every spark. Including yours."

"Including mine," Sebastian repeated. His voice sounded far away to himself. "So that's the choice. The door, or the gift."

"That's the arithmetic," said Wick. "Whether it's a choice depends on what you're willing to pay, and that, cargo, is the one question I genuinely cannot answer for you." He turned and started up the trail, and his voice drifted back through the mist, lighter now, almost his usual dry self. "Come along. The pass closes by evening, and there's a village past the treeline where an old acquaintance owes me a favor — assuming she hasn't heard about the bounty either."

Sebastian stood a moment longer at the foot of the green hills, the ford-token cold in his fist, the word *cargo* lodged under his ribs like a splinter.

Then the mist up-valley shifted, and he saw what lay ahead: the trail climbing toward a dark line of peaks, and on the nearest ridge, faint but unmistakable in the breaking cloud, a thread of smoke rising from a place where no chimney should be.

Wick had stopped too. He was looking at the smoke, and his ears were flat, and he was very still.

"That," said the fox, "is not the kiln village."
