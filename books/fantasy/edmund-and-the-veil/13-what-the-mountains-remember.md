## 13. What the Mountains Remember

The road ended where the ash orchards ended, and beyond that the mountains began in earnest — a wall of grey and white rising so sheer against the morning sky that Sebastian's neck ached just looking up at it.

"They don't look real," he said.

"They're real enough to kill you," Sorrel said from his shoulder. The fox had ridden there more often than not since Marrow Ford, claiming it was easier on his paws, though Sebastian suspected it was easier on the fox's pride to be carried past the places where the road grew narrow. "Everything past this point is real enough to kill you. Welcome to the Spine."

The pass ahead was called the Shepherd's Tooth, according to the map — a notch in the rock no wider than a cart track, stitched with switchbacks like a badly mended seam. The cartographer had drawn it with a careful hand and then, in the margin beside it, written a single word in that cramped, dying script: *listen.*

Sebastian had been turning that word over for two days now. *Listen.* Not *beware*, not *turn back*. Listen.

The wind came down off the heights carrying snow-dust and something else — a sound beneath the sound, the way a bell keeps ringing after the clapper stills. He stopped at the foot of the first switchback and tilted his head.

"You hear it too," Sorrel said. It wasn't quite a question.

"It's like..." Sebastian searched for the shape of it. "Like voices in another room. Like when the rats used to talk under the floorboards of the tannery and I could only catch the edges of what they said."

"The mountains talk." Sorrel's ears were flat, his amber eyes narrowed against the wind. "Older than Greyhollow. Older than the crown, older than the Veilwardens, older than every law they ever wrote down. Stone has a long memory, boy, and nothing up here has ever told it to forget."

Sebastian looked at him. "You've been here before."

The fox said nothing for a long moment. The wind filled the silence, and beneath the wind, faint as breath on a window, that other voice went on murmuring.

"I've been a great many places," Sorrel said at last. "Climb."

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the higher they went, the louder the mountains became.

It wasn't words, exactly. It was impressions — the way a dream leaves a feeling behind after the images have dissolved. Sebastian felt grief pooled in certain hollows of the rock like rainwater. He felt watchfulness along the ridgelines, patient as lichen. And threaded through all of it, growing stronger with every switchback, he felt a current of something he could only call *waiting* — a vast, geological patience, as if the whole range were holding its breath for an appointment made long ago.

At midday they stopped in the lee of a leaning stone pillar, and Sebastian ate hard bread and dried apples while the fox picked apart a grouse he'd caught in one of the scree fields below. The pillar was carved. Sebastian hadn't noticed until the light moved across it — lines worn nearly smooth, but unmistakably deliberate: a spiral, coiling inward toward a center that someone had once chiseled into the shape of a door.

"That's Veilmark work," he said.

Sorrel didn't look up from his meal. "Everything up here is Veilmark work. You're walking through their graveyard and their pantry and their churchyard all at once. The kingdom didn't end at some border-stone, boy. It ended everywhere at once, the day the door closed. These mountains were its spine. Its heart was somewhere south of here, in the low country, where your Greyhollow stands now on top of it like a cork in a bottle."

Sebastian set down the bread. "Then why does the map lead north?"

"Because hearts can be moved." Sorrel licked a claw clean. "Or because whoever drew your map knew something about doors that the histories forgot. Either way, we go where the ink says."

Sebastian put his palm flat against the pillar. The stone was cold, but beneath the cold — faint, far down, the way warmth lingers in a hearthstone hours after the fire dies — he felt the spiral humming. Not magic, not exactly. Memory. His gift had always been a kind of listening; beasts spoke to him because he could hear the shape of what they meant. And the mountain, it turned out, meant things too.

"What happened to them?" he asked quietly. "The Veilmark people. My people, if the blood in me means anything."

For once, the fox answered without sarcasm.

"No one knows. One autumn they were everywhere — their kilns smoking in every valley, their roads running straight as arrows, their door standing open so that magic flowed through the world like water through a channel. And then the door shut, and the water stopped, and everything that depended on it began to die. Not quickly. Slowly. The way a tree dies slowly after the roots are cut. Their songs went thin. Their children were born without the gift. Within three generations there was nothing left but ruins and stories, and within ten, not even the stories. Just laws. Laws forbidding the thing that had already gone."

"And my family kept the gift anyway."

"Your family kept the key," Sorrel said. "Which is worse, or better, depending on the day."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and near dusk the mountain began to speak clearly.

It happened at a bend in the path where the rock fell away to the west and the whole world opened out — valley after valley falling away into violet shadow, the last light lying along them like spilled honey. Sebastian stopped, and the voice of the stone rose up around him all at once, and this time it was not impression but image.

He saw fires. Hundreds of them, terraced down these same slopes, smoke rising straight in a windless evening. He saw figures moving between the fires — tall, robed in undyed wool, their hands raised, and light answering from their palms the way heat answers from a forge. He saw beasts among them, not driven but walking freely: wolves at the shepherds' heels like hounds, eagles perched on doorposts like cats, bears hauling timber with ropes of woven grass and no chains at all.

And he heard — not with his ears, with whatever it was inside him that heard — a single word repeated across the whole bright valley, passed from mouth to mouth, fire to fire, like a bell being rung:

*Doorkeeper. Doorkeeper. Doorkeeper.*

Sebastian gasped and staggered, and his boot skidded on frost-slick stone, and suddenly Sorrel's teeth were in his sleeve, hauling him back from the edge with a strength no ordinary fox possessed.

"Careful," the fox snapped. "The mountain shows off for company. It hasn't had any in a hundred years. Don't go leaning into it like a drunkard at a window."

"What was that?" Sebastian's heart was slamming. "What did I see?"

"The valley of Kel Doreth, the night before the closing." Sorrel released his sleeve and sat, wrapping his tail over his paws, looking out at the darkening valleys as if he could see the fires still burning there. "The last night. Every soul in the kingdom gathered to watch their Doorkeeper do the thing they'd spent a century arguing about. Half of them begged her to close it. Half of them begged her not to. She closed it."

"How do you know all this?" Sebastian turned on him. "You talk about it like you were—"

"Like I read a book," Sorrel said smoothly. "Foxes read. It surprises people."

But he wouldn't meet Sebastian's eyes, and the lie sat between them on the cold stone like a third traveler neither would acknowledge.

* * *

Night came fast in the high country, hard and star-riddled. They sheltered in a hollow beneath an overhang where an ancient avalanche had left a cave of tumbled slabs, and Sebastian built a small fire of scrub pine while Sorrel curled into a knot of orange fur at the cave mouth, facing outward, always outward.

Sebastian lay awake a long time, listening.

The mountain did not sleep. That was the thing he understood now, lying in the dark with his cloak pulled to his chin. Cities slept — Greyhollow snored and muttered and dreamed its anxious dreams, and even its magic had felt half-asleep, muffled under the outlawry and the fear. But the mountain went on remembering at full volume, every hour, every age, stacked like rings in a felled tree. The fires of Kel Doreth. Before those, older fires. Before those, something vast and formless, a time when the stone itself had been young and singing.

And underneath everything, deeper than anything, the waiting. The held breath. As if the mountain knew the door's location the way a dog knows where its master has buried a bone, and had been sitting by the spot for four hundred years.

"Sorrel," he whispered. "Are you awake?"

A sigh from the cave mouth. "Regrettably."

"When we find the door. What happens to the magic that's left? In people like me. In the city. If Veilmark takes it back—"

"Then it's taken back." No softness in it. "That's the arithmetic, boy. You pour the gift through the door, and the world goes quiet, and everyone the Veilwardens are hunting becomes safe precisely because there's nothing left to hunt. Your friends stop being criminals. Children stop being taken. All it costs is everything you are."

"All it costs," Sebastian echoed bitterly. "You say that like it's small."

"I say it like it's true. Small and large are the same price up here." The fox's eyes caught the firelight, twin embers in the dark. "Sleep. Tomorrow we cross the shoulder of the Tooth, and the mountain gets steeper, and the memories get older, and you'll want your strength for both."

Sebastian closed his eyes. But sleep, when it finally took him, was full of doors — doors in hillsides, doors in the roots of trees, doors standing alone in empty fields — and behind every one of them, a light like the last ember of a fire that someone, somewhere, was trying very hard to keep alive.

* * *

They crossed the shoulder of the Shepherd's Tooth at dawn, and that was where they found the graves.

There were dozens of them, maybe hundreds — cairns of stacked stone marching along the saddle of the pass in long, orderly rows, each one no bigger than a sleeping child, each one crowned with a flat slab carved with the same spiral-and-door mark Sebastian had seen on the pillar. Frost silvered them. Wind combed through them. They had been tended once; weeds grew in the joints now, but the stacking was still deliberate, still loving, the work of hands that had grieved.

Sebastian walked the rows slowly, his breath smoking, his hand trailing over the cold cairns. And the mountain, gentle for once, gave him only fragments: a fever moving through a refugee camp. Snow. Hunger. A woman in undyed wool singing to a child who would not wake, her song getting quieter and quieter until it was only the wind.

"They came north," he said hoarsely. "After the closing. They came north looking for the door, trying to reopen it, and they died in the pass."

"Some of them," Sorrel said. He stood apart from the graves, and for the first time since Sebastian had known him, the fox looked small. "The ones who still had enough gift left to feel the door's absence. It pulled at them. Like a tooth-ache in the blood. They couldn't stay in the low country with that ache, so they came home to the mountains to die near it." He paused. "The ones without the gift stayed. Forgot. Built your Greyhollow on the ruins and called forgetting wisdom."

Sebastian crouched beside the smallest cairn. A child's grave. On its slab, beneath the spiral, someone had scratched words in the old script — and to his own astonishment, he found he could almost read them, the letters stirring something in his blood the way a familiar smell stirs memory:

*We kept the key. Forgive us. We kept the key.*

His throat closed. "Kept it how? Kept it where?"

"The way a lamp keeps fire," Sorrel said quietly. "In something small and living, passed hand to hand. Generation to generation. Down and down, thinner each time, like wine cut with water." He looked at Sebastian then, and his gaze was old, unbearably old, older than any fox's eyes had any right to be. "Until one family in one gutter-city had a boy who could hear rats in the walls."

The wind moved through the pass, keening between the cairns, and for a moment Sebastian would have sworn the sound was voices — hundreds of them, the dead of Kel Doreth and the dead of the pass, all murmuring the same word they'd sung on the last bright night:

*Doorkeeper. Doorkeeper. Doorkeeper.*

He stood up. His legs shook, but he stood.

"Then we shouldn't wait," he said. "If the mountain remembers, if the dead are pointing — we shouldn't spend another night on this ridge. Which way?"

Sorrel turned his head north, along the descending line of the pass, toward a valley choked with dark pine and, far down in its throat, a smudge of smoke that had no business being there — no shepherd burned fires this deep, no hunter wintered this high.

Smoke meant people. People meant danger. But smoke also meant the map's next mark was close, because the cartographer had drawn a little cluster of squares in that exact valley and labeled them with a word that had been nagging at Sebastian for days:

*Kiln.*

"There," Sorrel said. "Down through the pines. And boy — when we get there, whatever you see, whatever you hear, however strange the people seem—" The fox hesitated, and something flickered across his face that Sebastian had never seen there before. Something that looked almost like guilt.

"—remember that not everyone who survived the closing survived it honestly."

He leapt down onto the northward path without another word, tail low, and Sebastian followed him down into the dark of the pines, toward the smoke, while behind them the cairns of the pass stood silent in the frost, keeping their four-hundred-year vigil over the key they had died trying to use.
