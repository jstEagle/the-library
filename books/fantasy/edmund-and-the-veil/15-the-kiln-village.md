## 15. The Kiln Village

They smelled it before they saw it: woodsmoke and wet clay, the sour-sweet breath of a kiln at work. After nine days in the high country — nine days of wind-scoured ridgelines, frozen streams, and Sorrel's increasingly creative complaints about the absence of mice — the smell hit Sebastian like a memory of another life.

"Smoke," he said, stopping on the trail where it bent around a shoulder of grey stone. "Real smoke. Not lightning-struck pine. Someone's firing something."

Sorrel sat down in the snow and regarded him with the flat patience of a creature who had long ago stopped expecting his human to make sense. *You said that about the goat.*

"The goat was real."

*The goat was a rock.* The fox shook melting snow from his ears. *You spoke to it for ten minutes. It told you about its childhood.*

Sebastian didn't answer. He was listening past the fox, past the wind, into the place where his gift lived — the quiet room behind his ribs that had been growing louder as they climbed westward, away from Greyhollow's iron and its Veilwardens and its caged songbirds. For three years he had kept the gift small, folded up like a letter never sent. Out here it unfolded on its own, the way a hand opens in sleep.

And what he heard now, threading through the wind, made his breath catch.

Not one voice. Dozens.

Chickens muttering their dim eternal grievances. A dog singing a low watch-song to itself. Goats, of course, goats always. And beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, a human voice — no, not a voice exactly, but the shape of speech, the rhythm of someone talking to animals the way he talked to them, easily, without shame.

"There are others," he whispered.

Sorrel's ears came forward. *Others like you?*

"I don't know. Like me enough." He looked at the fox, and the hope in his own chest frightened him a little, it was so large and so sudden, like a bird beating inside a closed room. "A whole village of it, maybe. Hidden up here where the Veilwardens' writ runs thin."

*Or,* Sorrel said, *it's a trap. You'll notice we have been fleeing a government purge. You'll notice there is a price on your head that could buy this entire mountain range, if mountains were for sale. You'll notice—*

"Noticed," Sebastian said, and started down the trail.

The village appeared all at once, the way mountain villages do — one moment forest and rock, the next a bowl of terraced hillside holding perhaps forty buildings, low-roofed and timbered, clustered around a great domed kiln whose chimney breathed a slow grey ribbon into the afternoon sky. Potters' racks stood outside the houses, rows of bowls and jugs glazed in colors Sebastian had no names for: a green like deep water, a blue like dusk just before the first star. Snow lay on everything, but it lay thinly here; the kiln's warmth reached even the outer lanes.

He heard the village before anyone saw him. That was the thing about his gift — it arrived ahead of him, an ambassador announcing his coming. The dog in the nearest yard lifted its head and sang out two bright notes of *stranger, stranger, but not wrong*. A child chasing a hoop across a lane froze mid-step, turned, and stared at him with the unblinking directness of the very young.

Then a woman came out of the nearest house, wiping her hands on a clay-dusted apron, and Sebastian knew before she spoke.

She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, grey going on white at her temples, with a face like a river stone — worn smooth, hard to read. But when she looked at him she did not look at his face. She looked slightly past him, at the space over his left shoulder, the way people do when they're listening to something only they can hear.

"You brought a fox," she said. Her voice was dry and warm, like kindling. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"He'd say both."

*I'd say worse,* Sorrel muttered, but he sat down neatly at Sebastian's heel, tail wrapped over his paws, presenting himself with the courtesy of a diplomat.

The woman studied them both. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, caution, and beneath those, carefully buried, a hunger. "My name is Maren Cole. I keep the kiln." She paused. "You're Fairweather blood."

It wasn't a question, and it knocked the ground sideways under him. "How could you possibly—"

"Because the sparrows told us you were coming." She said it the way another person might say *the postman told us*, utterly without drama. "Three days ago. A flock came through heading east, and they carry news the way rivers carry silt. A man from Greyhollow, they said. A beast-speaker running from the Wardens. With a fox who talks back." Her mouth twitched. "The sparrows were very impressed by the fox."

*Naturally,* said Sorrel.

Maren turned and called over her shoulder, in the ordinary carrying voice of someone used to being obeyed: "Tam! Put the kettle on. We've got a guest." Then, to Sebastian: "Come in before you freeze. And leave your sword by the door — not because I fear you. Because my dog will feel obliged to guard it otherwise, and he takes his work seriously."

* * *

The house was warm and close-smelling, full of clay dust and drying herbs and the particular coziness of a home built against hard winters. The dog — an enormous, grave-eyed mastiff named Bracken — inspected Sebastian at length, decided he was acceptable, and draped himself across the doorway with the finality of a portcullis.

There were six others at the table when the kettle went round. Maren introduced them without ceremony: Tam, her husband, a silent man with forearms like knotted rope who nodded once and never stopped whittling; a young couple, Pell and Isbet, who between them kept the village's goats and bees and who finished each other's sentences in a way that made Sebastian suspect the bees did most of the talking; old Hollis, nearly deaf in the human way but fluent in crow, who had been a Veilwarden's clerk in Greyhollow forty years ago before walking out one morning and never coming back; and a girl of perhaps fourteen named Wren, who sat with a moth perched on her finger like a ring and watched Sebastian with an expression of fierce, evaluating hope.

They were all gifted. Not one of them hid it. Here, in this bowl of mountain shadow, they spoke to their animals openly, wove charms into the glazes they fired — Sebastian could see it now, once he knew to look: the pots on the shelves practically hummed, each one holding a small knot of intention, protection stitched into clay. Jugs that kept milk sweet. Bowls that warned of poison. A great storage jar by the hearth that Bracken seemed to regard with professional respect.

"We trade them down-valley," Maren said, following his gaze. "Charmed ware sells well among folk who don't ask questions. The money buys grain and salt and silence." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "We've been here sixty years, Fairweather. My grandmother founded the kiln after the first purges. The rule has always been the same: small magic, quiet lives, nothing that draws the eye. No fire-calling. No storm-work. Nothing the Wardens would cross a mountain for."

"And me?" Sebastian said quietly. "I'm crossing mountains for."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's rather the problem."

The room went still. Even the moth held its position.

Wren leaned forward. "Is it true you can talk to anything? Anything alive?"

"Wren," Maren said.

"But is it true?"

"It's true," Sebastian said. "Though 'talk' flatters it. Mostly it's listening. Animals mostly want to tell you about food and weather and which way the danger lies." He hesitated. "And sometimes about older things. Things underneath."

Something passed across the table then — a glance between Maren and Hollis, quick as a fish turning. Sebastian caught it, and caught too the way Wren's moth lifted off her finger and resettled, agitated.

"Older things," Maren repeated carefully.

"My gift isn't mine," Sebastian said. He had been circling this for days, saying it aloud only to Sorrel, in fragments, and now it came out of him in a rush, relief and terror together. "I thought it was. I thought I was born wrong, or blessed, or cursed — I spent three years in Greyhollow pretending to be a charm-peddler, selling love-knots and luck-strings, keeping my head down. And then the cartographer Aldous Vane died and left me a map, and the map led here—" He stopped. Started again. "Veilmark. The kingdom that vanished. My family came from there. The gift is an inheritance. There's a door somewhere in these mountains, the last door, and everything — the Wardens, Crane, all of it — comes down to whether it opens."

Hollis set down his cup with a click. The old man's voice, when he finally used it, was rusty as a gate hinge. "Vane. Aldous Vane." He looked at Maren. "Told you. Told you the birds said *Fairweather*, and there's only ever been one line that mattered."

"What do you know about Vane?" Sebastian asked.

"Knew him," Hollis said. "Before your time, boy. He came through here too, thirty years back, asking the same questions you're asking. About the door. About what's behind it." The old man's clouded eyes fixed on him. "We told him nothing. He left angry. Word came later he'd gone back to Greyhollow and started drawing maps for the crown." A pause, heavy as snowfall. "Maps are how the Wardens find places like this, boy. A good mapmaker is worth more to a purge than a company of soldiers."

The cold that went through Sebastian had nothing to do with the weather. He thought of the dying old man in the crooked house above the tannery, pressing the oilskin map into his hands with trembling fingers. *Find it before they do.* He had believed it. He had staked everything on it.

But men were complicated, and dying men most of all, and Vane had served the crown for thirty years.

*You're doing the thing,* Sorrel observed from beside his chair, *where your face goes pale and your thoughts go loud. Stop it. You're upsetting the dog.*

Bracken did indeed look upset.

"The map," Maren said, and her voice had changed — flattened, careful. "Do you have it on you?"

"Why?"

"Because if the Wardens took Vane's maps before he died, every road, every ford, every hidden valley between here and the spine of the mountains is already drawn on their tables. Including," she said, "this one."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to their beds and the kiln's fires were banked to a red breathing glow, Maren walked him out to the terraces above the village. Snow had begun again, fat slow flakes that fell straight down in the windless dark. Below them the houses lay dark except for a few embers of lamplight, and the whole valley smelled of clay and cold.

"Sixty years," Maren said. "Small magic, quiet lives. And in sixty years the Wardens have never come. Do you know why?"

"Luck."

"Geography, mostly. Luck, some." She turned to face him, and in the light spilling up from the banked kiln below, her river-stone face looked suddenly ancient. "But also because we never let anyone stay who carried big weather in them. Your Vane came sniffing at the door, and we sent him away, and thirty years later I've half convinced myself we were wise and half convinced myself we were cowards." She exhaled, a plume of white. "Now you come, and you're not sniffing, are you. You're meant for it. The bloodline. The doorkeeper's line."

"I don't want to be meant for anything," Sebastian said. It came out rawer than he intended. "I want to go back to selling luck-strings. I want my friends in Greyhollow to be safe. I want the Wardens to be somebody else's problem."

"No one gets that," Maren said. "Least of all the meant-for ones." She was quiet a moment. Then: "There's something you should see before you decide anything. Tomorrow, when it's light. Wren can show you — she found it, two summers ago, and she's been bursting to tell someone ever since, and I've forbidden her, because telling is how villages die." Her jaw tightened. "But you're past the point where forbidding helps. If the door is truly waking, it's been calling things for a while now. Calling them here."

"Calling things?"

Maren looked out at the darkness beyond the terraces, where the mountain rose black against the stars, and for a moment Sebastian felt her gift reach out alongside his — felt her listening the way he listened, wide and wary — and what came back down the thread of the world made the hair rise on his arms.

From high on the mountain, faint and rhythmic, came the sound of digging.

"Tomorrow," Maren said. "Sleep if you can. And Fairweather — whatever Wren shows you, whatever you hear up there — remember that we have forty-one people in this village, and nineteen of them are children, and none of them asked to inherit anything."

She left him alone on the terrace. Sebastian stood a long time in the falling snow, listening to the small sounds of the sleeping village — Bracken's slow dream-breath, a horse shifting in its stall, the kiln ticking as it cooled — and beneath all of it, patient as roots, the sound from the mountain.

Digging. Digging. Something that had waited a very long time, digging its way toward the last door of Veilmark.

At his feet, Sorrel sat with his ears pricked toward the peak, and for once the fox said nothing at all.

* * *

Morning came white and silent. Sebastian woke in the loft above Maren's kitchen to find Wren already standing at the foot of the ladder, dressed for the cold, a moth riding her shoulder like a badge of office.

"Maren says I'm to take you up," she said. Her chin was set in a way that suggested she had argued her way into the job and intended to defend it. "She says I'm to show you the hollow and then bring you straight back, and she says if you go near the digging she'll skin us both."

"Sounds reasonable," Sebastian said.

"She also says—" Wren hesitated, and for the first time the fierce confidence flickered, and he saw the fourteen-year-old underneath, scared and trying not to be. "She says you're the one the birds have been singing about. The last of the Fairweathers. She says if anyone can tell us what's coming, it's you." She swallowed. "So. What *is* coming?"

Sebastian pulled on his coat and looked past her, through the frost-furred window, at the mountain — at the place where, even now, even in daylight, he could feel the rhythm of it, steady as a second heartbeat.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I think we're about to find out."

They were halfway up the switchback trail, Wren chattering nervously about the hollow, when the digging stopped — and in the new silence, from somewhere above them, clear across the snow, a voice that was not a voice at all said his name.
