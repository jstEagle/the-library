## 19. The Choice of the Doorkeeper

The door was smaller than Sebastian had imagined.

After everything — the ash orchards, the ford, the burned village, the mountains that remembered — he had expected something vast, a gate fit for a kingdom's grief. Instead it stood in the hollow like a cottage door left out in the weather: grey wood gone silver with age, iron hinges furred with lichen, a lintel carved with letters worn nearly to whispers. It did not tower. It simply *was*, and the hollow bent around it the way a room bends around a hearth.

And it was open. Only a crack — a hand's width of darkness between door and frame — but from that crack came a sound Sebastian had been hearing in his dreams for three years without knowing its name. A low, patient hum, like a hive asleep in winter. Like a voice deciding whether to speak.

Sorrel sat down on his haunches three paces away and began, deliberately, to wash his face.

"Well," said the fox. "There it is. Smaller than I remember."

"You've seen it before."

"I said smaller than I *remember*, didn't I? Which implies remembering." He licked a paw, dragged it over one ear. His eyes never left the crack of dark. "It was taller when Veilmark still stood behind it. Doors shrink when no one uses them. Everything does."

Sebastian stepped closer. The hum rose to meet him, and beneath his skin every small borrowed voice — the fieldmice in the hedgerow, a kestrel turning somewhere high above, the worms in the cold soil — went silent at once, as if they had all turned to look.

He could feel the magic in himself leaning toward the gap. Not eagerly. The way a tired man leans toward a lit window.

"Sebastian." Crane's voice came from behind him, and it was wrong — too smooth, too careful, the voice of a man arranging words like furniture. "Before you do anything, you should know what you're looking at."

Sebastian did not turn. "I know what I'm looking at. I've known since Marrow Ford. Since the kiln village." He heard Crane shift his weight in the wet leaves. "You should know that I watched you stand in that burning street and lie to my face about who gave the order."

"The order came from the Crown—"

"*You* read the list, Crane. You knew the names. You drew the map of every village with gifted children in it, and you handed it to men with torches, and then you rode ahead of us so you could be here first, wearing the face of a friend." Now Sebastian turned. Crane stood at the mouth of the hollow, mud to his knees, his fine coat ruined, his hands empty and open the way a man holds them when he has decided openness is his best weapon. Behind him, far down the valley, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of horns. The Veilwardens had found the road.

"I did," Crane said quietly. "All of it. And I'm not going to insult you by weeping about it. But you need to listen to me now, because there are perhaps ten minutes between us and a company of Wardens with iron chains, and what happens in those ten minutes decides whether Greyhollow ever breathes free again."

"Or whether *you* breathe free again."

"Yes," Crane said. "Both things can be true. Listen anyway."

* * *

The hollow was a bowl of grey stone and black water, ringed by birches that had grown crooked, all of them leaning away from the door as if from a draft only trees could feel. Mist moved along the ground in slow ropes. Sorrel had stopped washing and sat utterly still, ears forward, and when Sebastian glanced at him the fox's eyes were old — older than any fox's eyes had any right to be, gold shot through with green, the green of deep water.

Crane came forward slowly, boots squelching, and stopped an arm's length from the door itself. Up close, Sebastian could see that the man was shaking. He hid it well — he had hidden everything well for years — but his jaw was tight as a drawn bowstring, and sweat stood on his upper lip despite the mountain cold.

"Do you know why the Veilwardens fear this place?" Crane said. "Not because of what's behind the door. Because of what's in front of it. You. Every gifted soul in Greyhollow is a leak, Sebastian — a crack in the seal your ancestors made. The Crown calls it contagion. But it isn't contagion. It's *inheritance*. The magic didn't die when Veilmark fell. It just stopped having anywhere to live, so it lived in people. In cartographers' daughters who could sing birds out of the air. In charm-sellers. In beast-speakers." He looked at Sebastian steadily. "In me."

The word landed like a stone in still water.

"You," Sebastian said.

"A very small gift. Useless, really. I can tell when a lock wants to open." Crane smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all. "Do you understand what that means, in a city built on locks? On doors, and ledgers, and locked rooms where men decide other men's lives? I was eleven years old when a Warden caught me opening my master's strongbox without touching it. He didn't arrest me. He made me an offer. And I have been making that same offer to other people for thirty years, and telling myself each time that it was mercy."

The horns sounded again, closer. Sorrel's tail flicked once.

"So this is the confession," Sebastian said. "Chapter fourteen, come late."

"This is the *bargain*," Crane said. "Confession is what you give when you have nothing left to trade. I still have something." He gestured at the door, at the thin line of dark humming in the grey wood. "That door answers to blood. Your blood. The last Doorkeepers sealed it from this side and bound it to their line, which means it will open wide for you and stay shut for everyone else — including the Crown, including me, including every Warden marching up that valley with orders to burn whatever they find. Unless—" he paused, and chose the next words with visible care, "—unless you pour your gift through it. All of it. The whole inheritance, back through the Veil, back to the kingdom that's been waiting three hundred years to take it home. Then the leaks stop. The gifted become ordinary. The Wardens lose their reason to hunt, and Greyhollow goes quiet and safe and *free*, in the way that a bird in a cage is safe and free from hawks."

"And powerless," Sebastian said. "Every one of us. Permanently. That's the price you're offering me — my friends' souls, traded for their safety."

"It's the only price on the table. The alternative is that the Wardens reach this hollow, find the door sealed against them, and conclude that the only way to end the leak is to end the leakers. All of them. Every name on every list, starting with the ones they've already caught." Crane's voice dropped. "Mira is in a Warden cell right now, Sebastian. The charm-seller's girl, the one who sings to moths. They took her two days ago. She's alive because they want her to lead them somewhere, and she hasn't given them your name yet, and she won't, and when they finally believe she won't—" He let the sentence die. It died loudly enough.

Sebastian's hands had closed into fists without his permission. Through the bond that never slept, he felt the kestrel above circling tighter, agitated, echoing him.

"You're lying," he said. "About Mira. You say whatever opens this door."

"I am lying about many things and always have been," Crane said, with terrible calm. "But not about her. Check the crows if you don't believe me. They see into the Wardenhouse yard. Ask them what the cages look like."

* * *

Sebastian asked the crows.

It was not hard — the hollow's birches held three of them, hunched and silent, waiting like the rest of the world for the door to decide something. He reached out along the old thread, the thread that had been his whole life before it became his whole inheritance, and the nearest crow tilted its head and gave him the yard: grey flagstones, iron-barred pens along the east wall, a girl of maybe fifteen sitting with her back straight and her chin up, singing under her breath to the moths that gathered on her bars even in winter. Two Wardens watching her with the flat patience of men who had done this many times. A brazier, unlit, stacked beside the pens with wood already in it.

The crow showed him all of it in six heartbeats, and then said, in its dry rusted voice, *"Cold coming. Cold coming to the little singer."*

Sebastian came back to himself kneeling in the wet leaves, Sorrel's warm flank pressed against his side.

"Now you understand," Crane said softly. "Open the door. Give it everything. Save her. Save all of them. It's the choice your ancestor couldn't make, and it's why Veilmark fell — the last Doorkeeper loved his power more than his people, and the kingdom paid, and the world has been paying interest ever since. You can be the one who stops the payment." He spread his empty hands. "I can't make you. God knows I've spent thirty years making people do things, and I'd deserve whatever you did to me for it. But I can tell you the truth, which is more than anyone ever told me at your age: *there is no third option*. Seal it or lose them. Choose."

The hum from the door had changed. Lower now. Warmer. Almost — Sebastian thought, and hated himself for thinking it — almost comforting. The way a mother's heartbeat sounds through a wall.

*Sorrel,* he said silently. *You've been quiet too long. Even for you.*

The fox opened his eyes. For a moment the green in them was very bright, and very deep, and Sebastian understood — finally, completely — that he had never once been talking to a fox.

"He's going to offer you a fourth option," Sorrel said aloud. "Aren't you, Aldous?"

Crane went white.

"That name," he whispered, "is buried."

"Names don't bury well in Veilmark soil." Sorrel rose, stretched, and walked — unhurried, insolent, magnificent — to the base of the door, where he sat and wrapped his tail over his feet like a cat at a fire. "Go on, Sebastian. Ask him what he didn't put in the bargain. Ask him what happens to the magic after it goes through. Ask him who taught the first Doorkeepers to seal doors in the first place, and what they were sealing *out*."

"What?" Sebastian said. "What's behind the door?"

"Home," said Crane, too quickly.

"Behind the door," Sorrel said, "is the reason the door exists. Veilmark wasn't destroyed by its enemies, boy. It was destroyed by its own gift — by what the gift woke up, deep under the mountains, when the kingdom got fat and bright and careless with it. The Doorkeepers didn't seal the magic *in*. They sealed the rest of it *out*, and the magic was the lock. Take the lock away—" the fox's eyes glittered, "—and ask yourself what three hundred hungry years have done to the thing on the other side."

The hum from the door stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than any sound — a silence with attention in it, a silence that leaned. The crack of darkness between door and frame seemed, very slightly, wider than it had been.

"Choose quickly," said the door, in a voice like frost forming on glass, and it was not Crane's voice, and it was not Sorrel's, and it was not Sebastian's, though some ancestral corner of him recognized it the way one recognizes a face in a family portrait. "The hunters are near, little heir. And I have waited so long to be *opened*."

Sebastian Fairweather, beast-speaker, last of the Doorkeepers, stood in the hollow with a liar on one side, a secret older than kingdoms on the other, and a girl in a cage singing to moths — and made his choice.

He put his hand on the door.

It swung inward, wide and welcoming, onto the dark —

— and the dark was full of eyes.
