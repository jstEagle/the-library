## 21. After the Silence

The torchlight at the bottom of the hill belonged to a cookfire, and around the cookfire sat eleven people, and every one of them turned when Sebastian came out of the dark.

He had imagined this moment on the way down — had rehearsed it, in the numb way a man rehearses anything when his hands won't stop shaking — and in every version he had said something wise. Something that would make the giving make sense. *I gave it away so you could live.* Or: *It was never ours to keep.* But standing in the firelight with ash to his knees and his gift gone out of him like a tooth pulled from the jaw, all he managed was:

"Is there any soup?"

Maren made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and crossed the distance between them at a run. She hit him hard enough to stagger, her arms locked around his ribs, her face buried against his shoulder, and she did not say *you fool* or *you hero* or any of the things he deserved in either direction. She just held on, and after a while he held on back, and the fire crackled, and somewhere behind them the mountain kept its silence.

"You're grey," she said finally, pulling back to look at him. Not frightened-grey. Not Veilwarden-grey. A new grey, the grey of a thing burned down to its honest bones.

"I know," Sebastian said.

"No — I mean—" She stopped. Her eyes went wide and wet. "Sebastian. The birds. The crows used to land on you like you were a fence post. There were sparrows in your kitchen. I heard you talking to rats once, actual rats, in the cellar of the Gilded Hart, and you told me they were *colleagues*—"

"They were colleagues."

"And now?" Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Now what do you hear?"

Sebastian listened. It was the first time he had truly listened since the door closed, and what he heard was the world as everyone else heard it: wind in the dead orchards, the pop and settle of the cookfire, a nightjar calling somewhere up the slope, and beneath it all the small animal sounds of eleven frightened people trying to be quiet. No voices under the voices. No crow-language braided through the wind. No rat-gossip seeping up through the earth.

Nothing. Just the world, speaking plainly, saying only what it said.

"Nothing," he said. And then, because Maren's face was doing something terrible and pitying, he added: "It's very restful."

She laughed — a broken, startled laugh, but a real one — and someone by the fire snorted, and the tension over the little camp cracked like river ice in spring. Hands pulled him toward the flames. A bowl was pressed into them. Old Tam, the lamplighter whose gift had been knowing where lost things fell, looked at him across the fire with an expression of profound professional respect and said nothing at all, which from Tam was a eulogy.

They were eleven because they had been forty-one. Sebastian counted them while he ate, the way he had once counted crows, and found the arithmetic unbearable, and made himself look at each face anyway. Maren, who had no gift except stubbornness and had carried three children out of Greyhollow on her back. Tam. The Widow Ashe, whose talent for mending had been small and warm and now was only hands. Two brothers from the tanneries. A girl of maybe nine named Pip who had never shown a gift at all and had been swept up in the purge anyway because her grandmother had one, once, and blood was enough for the Veilwardens.

Pip was staring at him.

"Pip," Maren warned gently.

"It's all right," Sebastian said. He set down his bowl. "Go on, then."

"You're the beast-speaker," Pip said. "You talked to animals."

"I did."

"What did they say? Before. What did they always say?"

No one had ever asked him that. In thirty years of the gift, through a childhood of hiding it and an adulthood of selling it cheap — charm a mule, quiet a mastiff, ask the gulls where the fish ran for a copper a question — no one had ever simply asked what the animals said. They had asked what he could *get* the animals to *do.*

"They said," Sebastian said slowly, "mostly, that they were hungry. That they were cold. That their feet hurt. That the place they slept last night wasn't safe anymore. And that they loved whoever fed them, even when the feeding was late." He smiled, and found it didn't hurt. "Animals are not mysterious, Pip. They're just honest in public."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "That's sad," she decided.

"Yes," Sebastian agreed. "It is."

* * *

They moved before dawn, deeper into the orchard country, away from the road. Word had come up the valley with a peddler two nights past — the peddler himself long gone, his news staying like a smell — that Greyhollow had emptied its prisons into the ash pits, that the Veilwardens' Grand Warden Crane was dead, that the purges had stopped, that nobody knew why, that nobody much cared why, that the bells had rung the Relief and half the city had wept in the streets.

The peddler's version of Crane's death involved assassins, a curse, and a duel on the palace roof. The truth, which Sebastian kept behind his teeth, was quieter and worse: an old man kneeling in the snow before a seam of light, asking forgiveness of a kingdom he had helped bury, and the mountain taking him the way the mountain took everything — completely, without malice, without even noticing.

*He wanted the door's power,* Sebastian thought, watching the frost smoke rise off the orchard rows. *And in the end he got exactly what the door gives everyone. Nothing. Everything. Same thing.*

Maren walked beside him. She had appointed herself his keeper without discussion, the way she appointed herself everything, and he had stopped objecting somewhere around the second mile.

"The others are going to ask," she said quietly. "About Crane. About how it ended. Tam already tried; I headed him off. But people need a story, Sebastian. You can't give them silence. Silence curdles."

"I know something about that."

"So what will you tell them?"

Sebastian walked a dozen paces before answering. An apple tree stood at the field's edge, dead these three years, its branches black and bare — and yet at the end of one branch, absurdly, impossibly, a single bud had swelled overnight, tight and green and utterly unexplained. He stopped and looked at it for a long moment. It was not magic. He was quite sure it was not magic. Trees did strange things in the year after a fire; the old growers said so; it meant nothing.

He touched the bud anyway, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you could no longer speak to.

"I'll tell them the truth," he said. "The parts of it that help."

"That's not the same as the truth."

"No," he said. "But it's the same as mercy, and I've got less of the one than I thought, so I'm spending the other carefully."

* * *

They wintered in a ruined kiln village in the hills — not the kiln village; that one was ash and memory and a grave he visited alone — but a sister settlement two valleys over, its chimneys still standing, its roofs half-collapsed, its wells sweet. Refugees trickled in the way water finds a low place. By midwinter there were sixty of them, then ninety. Someone organized a wood detail. Someone else organized everything else, and that someone was Maren, because of course it was.

Sebastian became useful in the ordinary ways. He could mend a wall. He could butcher a deer without flinching. He discovered, with genuine surprise, that he remembered how to talk to animals the mundane way — slowly, patiently, with food and stillness and lowered eyes — and that a horse brought along in trust was nearly as good as a horse charmed, and lasted longer.

But the thing he became, the thing the village actually needed him to be, was the one who sat with the gifted.

Because they came to him. The ones whose gifts had been torn out at the Veilwardens' altars before the order collapsed — the menders, the finders, the weather-sensitives, a woman who had once spoken to rivers. They came limping into the village with a hole in them shaped exactly like the hole in him, and the healers could do nothing, because there was nothing wrong, and that was precisely the horror of it.

He sat with them in the evenings, in the longhouse, by the stove. He did not tell them it would pass. He did not tell them it was for the best. He told them the truth that helps.

"The first month is the worst," he said to the river-speaker, a gaunt woman named Odile who had not slept properly since her severing. "You'll wake reaching for it. You'll dream in it. You'll hate the quiet so badly some nights you'll want to walk into the hills and scream at the door itself, if there were a door, which there isn't." He let that sit. "Then one morning you'll hear a bird, just a bird, just a bird making noise about worms, and you'll realize you understood it anyway. With your ears. Like a person. And it will feel like a small death and a small birth at the same time, and after that it gets bearable. After that it gets almost good."

"How do you stand it?" Odile whispered. "You gave up more than any of us. You gave it *willingly.*"

"Did I?" Sebastian looked into the stove. "There was a moment on the mountain when I could have kept it. Held the door shut from my side and stayed full. I want you to understand that, because I want you to understand that I'm not brave. I stood there with everything I'd ever been in my hands, and I thought: if I pour this out, I'll be *nobody.* Just a man. Just Sebastian Fairweather, who talks too much and owns one knife." He turned his scarred hands over in the firelight. "And then I thought: yes. Exactly that. Finally."

Odile cried. Most of them did, eventually, in that room. He had come to think of the crying as a kind of weather — necessary, passing, leaving the ground softer than it found it.

* * *

Sorrel came back at the turn of the year.

Sebastian woke in the grey hour before dawn to a weight on his chest and two amber eyes an inch from his own, and his heart performed a maneuver he hadn't authorized.

"You," he croaked, "are supposed to be a legend. Or a god. Or whatever it was you admitted to being on the mountain, and I was too tired to follow."

"I'm a fox," Sorrel said, settling his chin on Sebastian's sternum with the entitlement of a cat twice his size. "Everything else is gossip."

"You told me you'd walked beside every doorkeeper since the first. You told me you *were* the memory of the place—"

"Mm. And you poured your gift through the door, and the door shut, and everything on the far side of it went to sleep. Including, presumably, whatever I was on the far side of it." The fox yawned enormously, showing white needles. "What's left over is me. This. Fur, fleas, opinions, and a truly magnificent capacity for finding mice. I've been testing it extensively. The mice are adequate."

Sebastian lay still, looking at him. Grief and relief arrived together, the way they had been arriving together all winter, twins who refused to be separated. "So you don't remember. Any of it. The kingdom. The kings. What you were."

"Oh, I remember *of* it," Sorrel said. "The way you remember of a dream at noon. There was a garden. There was someone singing. There was a debt I owed, and I paid it, and I couldn't tell you to whom or for what, and honestly the not-knowing suits me. Less to be cynical about." His tail flicked. "Almost."

"Why did you come back? Really."

The amber eyes regarded him for a long moment, and for once there was nothing sardonic in them at all.

"Because everyone else in the world hears you the new way," Sorrel said. "Plain speech, ears only, person to person. And I find I miss being heard the old way. Even by a man with no gift left. Especially by him." He stood, stretched until his spine curved like a drawn bow, and hopped off the bed. "Also the mice here are excellent and you leave crumbs. Don't make it sentimental. I have a reputation."

He was gone out the window before Sebastian could answer, red tail flashing against the snow, and Sebastian lay in the cold grey light and laughed until Maren banged on the wall and told him some of us are trying to sleep.

* * *

Spring came up the valleys like a rumor turning true. The ash orchards blossomed — sparsely, lopsidedly, half the trees dead and the other half flowering as though apologizing for their neighbors — and the village planted, and argued about drainage, and buried two elders and welcomed four babies, which by the arithmetic of those years was winning.

On an evening in late April, Sebastian climbed the ridge above the kilns with a slate and a stub of chalk he'd traded three fishhooks for, and sat with his back against a warm stone, and looked west toward the mountains.

The hollow where the door had been was invisible at this distance, of course. It was probably invisible at any distance. But he knew where it was the way you know where a tooth was, and he found his eyes went there anyway, the way the tongue goes to the gap.

*Keeping everything I gave it,* he thought. *Keeping it forever.*

He had expected to feel robbed. He had braced for it all winter — the moment when the scale tipped and he regretted it, when he'd wake cursing the choice, when the hunger for the gift would finally win. The moment had not come. Some nights he missed the crows the way you miss a friend who moved overseas: sharply, specifically, then not at all for weeks. But regret — the deep, structural kind — never arrived. Whatever he had poured through the door, it turned out he had not poured himself. There had been more of him than the gift. He was still checking. He suspected he'd be checking for years, and that this too was a kind of health.

He turned the slate over. On it, in his careful cartographer's-handwriting — learned from a dying man's map, in another life — he had been writing, a few lines each evening, for six nights now.

Not a chronicle. He'd seen what happened to chronicles; Crane had taught him that much. Chronicles got rewritten by whoever held the chalk next.

A story, then. The kind you told a child by a stove, the kind that survived because people *wanted* it. About a city where magic was outlawed. About a man who spoke with beasts and thought that was all he was. About a fox, a map, a betrayal, a door. About what it cost to give a thing away, and what it cost to keep it, and how you could not know which cost was yours until you stood at the threshold with your whole life in your hands.

He looked at the first line for a long time. Then he touched the chalk to the slate and wrote, underneath it, a title — the only title that had ever been true:

*Edmund and the Veil.*

Pip's voice floated up from the foot of the ridge, indignant, carrying beautifully in the evening air: "Sebastian! Supper! Maren says if you're up there *talking to rocks again* she's giving your share to Tam!"

"I'm coming!" he called back, and got up, brushing off his trousers, slate under his arm, the mountain at his back and the lamplit village below with all its plain-speaking, hungry, honest human noise.

Behind him, high in the western dark, a single crow crossed the face of the rising moon and called out once — just a crow, just a crow making noise about the day being done.

Sebastian smiled, and understood it anyway, and went down to supper.
