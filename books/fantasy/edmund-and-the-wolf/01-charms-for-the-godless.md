## 1. Charms for the Godless

The rain came down on Vell like a debt being collected.

Kestrel Yarrow heard it first — she always did — a low gathering pressure behind her teeth, the faint metallic taste of a storm deciding where to fall. She pressed her tongue to the roof of her mouth and swallowed it down, the way she had every day for twelve years, and turned back to her counter and her little rows of hollow things.

Charms for the godless, she called them in her head. Copper coins stamped with spirals that meant nothing. Knotted red thread that would hold together nothing but itself. Dried lavender sewn into linen sleep-sachets that smelled of better nights than they could ever deliver. She sold them from a narrow shop wedged between a chandler's and a coffin-maker's on Candlewick Row, and the people of Vell bought them because hope was cheaper here than anywhere else in the city, and because magic was outlawed, and outlawed things leave an appetite.

"Protection charm," said the woman at the counter, a fishwife with cracked knuckles and eyes gone flat with worry. "For my son. He works the night boats."

Kestrel took down a copper disc on a leather cord, one of forty identical discs in the tray. She held it a moment too long, the way she always did — a small theater of gravity, as if the thing were heavy with meaning rather than with copper.

"He'll come home," she said. "Wear it under your shirt so no one sees."

The woman paid, thanked her twice, and left clutching the cord like a rope thrown over dark water. Kestrel watched her go through the fogged window and felt the familiar sour taste of it. The charm would do nothing. It had never done anything. That was the whole point of hollow charms: they were legal precisely because they were empty. The Vigil had burned the last true charm-seller in Vell nine years ago, in the square by the fountain, and the crowd had watched in silence because that was what safety cost.

Outside, the rain thickened. Kestrel felt it the way a man with an old wound feels weather — not pain exactly, but attention. Somewhere over the harbor, lightning considered its options. She gripped the edge of the counter until her knuckles went white and breathed slowly, counting, until the pressure behind her teeth dissolved into ordinary rain.

*Not tonight,* she told the storm, or told herself. *Never again.*

* * *

She was trimming the candle stubs at closing when the boy came in.

He was small for his age, maybe ten, maybe eleven, soaked through despite a coat two sizes too big, with the kind of thinness that comes from giving meals away. He stood in the doorway dripping, staring at the shelves of charms with the desperate arithmetic of someone counting coins he didn't have.

"We're closed," Kestrel said, without looking up.

"Please." His voice cracked on it. "They said you're the only one who — they said your charms are different."

"They're not." She set down the knife. "They're tin and thread and dried flowers. Whoever told you different was trying to be kind."

"My mother's dying." He said it plainly, the way children say terrible things, because they haven't yet learned to wrap them. "The fever. The physickers won't come anymore; we can't pay. The priest at Saint Aldhelm's says prayers are answered according to heaven's will, but heaven hasn't answered in—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over. "Everyone prays and nothing happens. Nothing has happened my whole life. But you sell charms, so you must know something. Please. Make her prayer work."

Kestrel looked at him then, really looked, and something old and tired shifted in her chest. He had rain in his hair and a prayer clenched in his fists like a stone he'd been carrying for miles.

"What's your name?"

"Edmund."

"Edmund." She came around the counter and crouched to his height, which put her eye-level with the door and its little bell, and with the grey street beyond. No Vigil coats in sight. "Listen to me. There is no charm I can sell you that will save your mother. Anyone who tells you otherwise is stealing from you, and you've been stolen from enough."

"But—"

"I'm sorry." And she was. It surprised her how much. "Go home. Sit with her. Hold her hand. That's not nothing. That's the realest magic left in this city, and it's free, which is why nobody believes in it anymore."

His face did something complicated — the collapse of a hope he'd carried across half of Vell — and then, worse, it hardened. Children shouldn't harden like that. She'd seen it before, in another life, in faces around another fire, and it had never once ended well.

"You're lying," he said quietly. "You're hiding it. I can tell. You flinched when the thunder almost came. You were listening to it."

Kestrel went very still.

"The storm," Edmund said. "It stopped raining over your roof for a minute. Just your roof. I saw it from the lane."

"That was wind."

"It wasn't wind."

For a moment neither of them moved. Outside, the rain drummed its patient fingers on the awning. Somewhere down the street a Vigil patrol rang its bell against the curfew hour, two notes, flat and final. Kestrel rose slowly, every joint announcing the years, and looked out the window at the emptying street.

"You need to go home," she said. "Now, before the patrols thicken. Take the lavender sachet." She pressed it into his hands, and a coin besides, and hated herself for how much it felt like a bribe. "For your mother. So she sleeps well. That's all it does. That's all anything does anymore."

He took them. He didn't leave.

Instead he closed his eyes right there in her shop, among the hollow charms, and prayed.

She heard it because he whispered it aloud, the way children do, the way people do when they've stopped believing anyone is listening and have decided to speak anyway:

*"Please. I don't want anything for me. I just want her to not hurt. Please. Please. Please."*

And the candle flame on the counter stood up.

Kestrel had seen a great many impossible things in a life she mostly refused to remember. She had called lightning out of a clear sky. She had spoken with things older than mountains. She had watched the bond between realms shatter and known, in the marrow-deep way you know a bone is broken, that she had done it — though even that knowledge lived behind a locked door in her mind, a door she had never found and never dared look for.

But she had never seen a candle flame stand up straight and still in a drafty room, taller than its wick, burning white instead of gold, and bend toward a child like a listener leaning close.

The flame spoke.

Not with sound, exactly. With heat, with pressure, with a voice that arrived inside the skull the way thunder arrives inside the chest. Kestrel felt it pass through her and knew, with sick certainty, that Edmund heard it too — that it was speaking *to* him, and that she was only standing in the radius of something vast turning its attention upon the world after twelve years of silence.

The voice said:

***"True-prayed. True-heard. Little speaker, who taught you to pray so honestly?"***

Edmund's eyes flew open. He stared at the flame. The flame stared back, if fire can be said to stare, and did not go out.

"It talks," he breathed. "It — miss, it *talks*—"

"Be quiet," Kestrel whispered. Her heart was slamming against her ribs like something caged. "Don't answer it. Don't move."

But the voice rolled on, gentle and enormous, an ocean learning to whisper:

***"We hear so little now. The road between is broken, and we starve on crumbs. But yours came through clean. Yours came through like a bell."*** A pause, and then the flame flickered, guttered, steadied — and the next words dropped through Kestrel like ice water. ***"Ask us, true-speaker. Ask anything. And tell the woman behind you — tell the keeper of the broken road — that the gods remember her name, even if she does not."***

The candle snapped back to its wick. Ordinary gold. Ordinary small. The rain resumed its ordinary drumming, and the shop smelled of tallow and lavender and, beneath it, faintly, of ozone — the smell of a sky about to break open.

Kestrel realized she was on her knees. She didn't remember falling.

Edmund was looking at her, wide-eyed, drenched, clutching a lavender sachet worth three coppers, and his lips were moving around the question she most dreaded in all the world:

"The keeper of the broken road," he said. "Miss — who's that? Why was it talking about *you?*"

"I don't know what you mean." Her voice came out steady, which was a miracle in itself, a small dead god of composure answering her own true prayer. "It was a draft. Candles do strange things. Go home to your mother, Edmund. Go home and don't come back here, and don't tell anyone — anyone — what happened tonight."

But even as she said it, she was looking past him at the candle, at the curl of smoke rising from it, straight and unbothered by any draft.

Because the smoke was spelling letters. Slowly, deliberately, in a hand eerily like her own childhood handwriting, it wrote a single word on the air, held it there long enough to be read, and dissolved.

The word was: **KESTREL**.

Her name. Not Yarrow — the name she'd chosen, the safe name, the alias worn smooth by twelve careful years.

Her other name. The one she'd buried so deep she'd genuinely believed it lost.

The bell above the door gave a soft ring, though no one had touched it, and somewhere far off across the rooftops of Vell, thunder finally made up its mind.
