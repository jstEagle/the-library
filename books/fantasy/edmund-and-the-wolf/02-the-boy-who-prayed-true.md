## 2. The Boy Who Prayed True

Edmund ran.

He didn't remember deciding to run. One moment he was kneeling in the gutter outside the charm-seller's stall, the candle's flame still burning blue in his mind's eye, and the next his feet were carrying him down Tanner's Row with his mother's medicine bottle clutched to his chest like a second heart.

Behind him, a whistle cut the evening air. Short. Sharp. Twice.

Vigil.

His legs knew what the whistle meant before his head did. Every child in Vell learned it young — two notes, and you made yourself small, made yourself nothing, made yourself a shadow pressed against a wall until the grey cloaks passed. But Edmund couldn't be small tonight. The Vigil had *seen* him. He was sure of it. The tall one with the iron-banded staff had turned at the exact moment the flame spoke, and his face had done something complicated — surprise first, then hunger, then that flat, patient look Edmund had seen on cats watching mouse-holes.

*You prayed true,* the voice had said. *We have waited so long for someone who prays true.*

Edmund ducked into the tannery alley, where the stink of lye and rotting hides covered everything like a blanket, and pressed himself between two rain barrels. His breath came in ragged pulls. Above him, laundry lines sagged under the weight of fog. Somewhere a dog barked, then stopped, as if it too had heard the whistle.

The medicine bottle rattled against his ribs as he breathed. He'd gotten it. That was the whole point, wasn't it? He'd gone to the charm-woman because Ma's cough had been drowning her for three weeks, because the physicker wanted four silver marks they didn't have, because the charms from the temple stalls were just painted glass and dried flowers and everyone knew it but nobody said it. And the charm-woman — Kestrel, she'd said her name was, though she hadn't looked at him when she said it — had sold him a knot of braided thread for a copper and told him, not unkindly, that it wouldn't work. That nothing worked. That prayers were just words you said into the dark so the dark felt less empty.

But he'd prayed anyway. Right there in front of her stall, holding the worthless charm, he'd closed his eyes and prayed the way he prayed every night at Ma's bedside — not the temple words, not the sanctioned liturgy with its careful gaps where the gods' names used to be, but his own words, ugly and desperate and true. *Please. I don't care which of you hears. Please don't let her drown.*

And the candle flame on the charm-seller's table had stood up straight and spoken.

Not flickered. Not bent. *Spoken.* A voice like wind through a door left open in winter, cold and old and glad all at once. It had said his name — his whole name, Edmund Marsh, the way Ma said it when she was frightened for him — and it had said:

*She will not drown tonight. And you — little lamp — you are the first true prayer in twelve years. Tell the keeper we are waking.*

Then the flame had gone out, and the charm-seller's face had gone white as bone, and the Vigil man had turned around, and Edmund had run.

Footsteps now. Boots on cobbles, unhurried, deliberate. Grey cloaks didn't hurry; hurrying meant fear, and the Vigil was never afraid. Edmund shrank deeper between the barrels. A rat brushed his ankle and he bit down on the scream.

The boots stopped at the mouth of the alley.

"Boy." The voice was a woman's, dry as paper. "I can hear your heart from here. It sounds like a drum at a hanging."

Edmund squeezed his eyes shut. *Don't find me. Don't find me. Don't—*

"Interesting," the woman said softly. "It's doing it again."

Silence. Then the boots moved on, unhurried, toward Tanner's Row, and Edmund realized with a jolt that the prayer had *worked*. Again. Something out there had heard him twice in one night, after twelve years of silence, and the thought filled him with a terror and a joy so tangled together he couldn't tell which one was making him shake.

He stayed between the barrels until the fog thickened into full dark. Then he did the only thing he could think of.

He went back to the charm-seller.

* * *

Her shop was a cellar off Candlemaker's Wynd, down seven steps behind a door painted the grey of old dishwater. No sign. No name. Just a smell that drifted up through the cracks — beeswax and river-stone and something faintly electric, like the air before lightning. Edmund had passed it a hundred times without ever once noticing it, which he understood now was probably the point.

He knocked the way she'd told him to knock when he bought the charm: twice, pause, once. The door opened a hand's width. One grey eye looked out.

"You brought them to my street," Kestrel Yarrow said.

"I'm sorry. I didn't know it would—"

"Did anyone follow you?"

"I don't think—"

"*Did anyone follow you.*"

"No," Edmund said. "There was a woman. She talked to me in the alley. But she walked away."

Kestrel's eye narrowed. "Describe her."

"Tall. Grey cloak. Iron bands on her staff. Hair like — like ash, sort of. She had a scar through one eyebrow."

The door opened the rest of the way, fast, and a hand shot out and pulled him inside so hard he stumbled down the steps. Behind him the door shut, and three bolts slid home, and something heavy — a bar, maybe, or a beam — dropped across it with a thud that sounded final as a coffin lid.

"Candles," Kestrel said. "Now. All of them."

The cellar was low-ceilinged and crammed: shelves of charms in every direction, bundles of herbs, jars of river water and grave dirt, wax tapers by the hundred. Edmund lit them with hands that wouldn't quite steady while Kestrel moved through the room doing things he didn't understand — salt across the threshold, a knife laid flat on the windowsill, a bowl of water set in the center of the floor where it went perfectly, impossibly still.

When everything was lit and warded and still, she turned to face him, arms crossed, and looked at him properly for the first time. Up close her face was older than he'd guessed, lined around the eyes, with a scar of its own — a thin white brand at her wrist, half-hidden by her sleeve, shaped like a fork of lightning.

"Tell me exactly what happened," she said. "Every word. Leave nothing out. If you lie to me even a little, I'll put you out that door and let the Vigil have you."

"I won't lie."

"Everyone lies. Start anyway."

So Edmund sat on an overturned crate among the candles and told her. The prayer at her stall. The flame standing up. The voice — and here Kestrel went very still, stiller than the bowl of water — and the words it had used. *Little lamp. First true prayer in twelve years. Tell the keeper we are waking.*

"The keeper," Kestrel repeated.

"That's what it said. Tell the keeper."

"And then?"

"Then it—" Edmund hesitated. This was the part he'd been turning over and over in his head since the alley, the part that made no sense, the part that had sent him back down the seven steps to her door instead of running for the city gate. "Then it said goodbye. But not to me."

The candle flames leaned, all of them at once, the way grass leans under wind.

"It said, *farewell, Vessamaru.*"

The silence that followed had weight. Edmund felt it press on his ears like deep water. Kestrel Yarrow stood among her hundred candles and did not move, and in the stillness of the water bowl he saw reflected a hundred small flames and one woman's face going slowly, carefully blank — the way a person blanks their face when the alternative is letting it break.

"I don't know that name," she said finally.

"Yes you do," Edmund said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because her head snapped around and her grey eyes were suddenly terrifying, storm-colored and wide.

"Say that again."

"You do know it. When it said the name, your face did a thing. My ma does it. When someone says my da's name." He swallowed. "Like a door opening onto a room you boarded shut."

For a long moment he was sure she was going to throw him out. Her hand actually rose — toward his collar, or the door, he couldn't tell — and then it fell, and she laughed instead, one short bitter note with no humor in it at all.

"Twelve years," she said, mostly to herself. "Twelve years I built this little life. Salt and wax and painted glass. And one candle talks to a boy and it all comes apart in an evening." She looked at him. "Do you understand what you've done, Edmund Marsh? Do you understand what's coming for you now?"

"The Vigil."

"Worse than the Vigil." She crouched to his level, and up close he could see how tired she was, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "The Vigil takes magic-breakers to prison. That's the kind thing they do. But a boy whose prayers get *answered* — a boy the gods speak *through* — you're not a criminal to them, boy. You're evidence. You're proof the outlawing failed. And there are people above the Vigil, people in grey robes the grey cloaks never talk about, who have spent twelve years making sure the gods stay dead. They will want to take you apart very carefully to find out why you didn't stay quiet."

Edmund's stomach turned over. "Ma," he said. "I have to get home. She's sick, if they come to the house—"

"They'll already have been to the house." Kestrel said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "A answered prayer leaves a mark, boy. A warmth in the air. They'll have traced it to the stall, and from the stall to your street, and from your street to your door. By now there's a grey cloak drinking tea in your kitchen and asking your mother very polite questions about her son."

Edmund was on his feet without remembering standing. "Then I have to go *now*—"

"Sit. Down." The word cracked like a whip, and the candle flames all snapped upright, and Edmund sat. Kestrel closed her eyes and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Your mother is being watched. Which means the road to your house is a trap, and you walking into it helps no one — least of all her. What keeps her safe is you staying free and staying *quiet*, and the only place in Vell you can be either is here, in a cellar warded six ways against scrying, under the nose of a woman who has spent twelve years being no one." She paused. "I'll send word to her. There are ways. Medicine too — real medicine, not the painted glass I sell upstairs."

"Why?" Edmund asked. "Why would you help me? You don't know me."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice.

"Because whatever spoke to you tonight," she said slowly, "used a name I buried so deep I'd swear on any god you like that I never knew it. And either I'm losing my mind, or something out there thinks I'm someone I'm not." She straightened, and began snuffing candles one by one, plunging the cellar into a soft brown dusk. "Either way, boy, you and I are both in the same sinking boat now. Sleep. There's a cot behind the herb shelf. We'll talk more when it's dark-dark."

* * *

He woke to knocking.

Not at the shop door — at the *street door*, the plain wooden door at the top of the seven steps, the one that looked like every other door on Candlemaker's Wynd. Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks. Patient as dripping water.

Edmund lay on the cot in the blackness, heart hammering, and listened to Kestrel move past him in the dark, silent as smoke, a knife appearing in her hand from somewhere he never saw.

"Charm-seller," called a voice from above, muffled by wood and earth. Dry as paper. Ash-grey hair. A scar through one eyebrow. "Open in the name of the Vigil. We know the boy came here. We watched him knock."

Kestrel looked back at Edmund through the darkness. In the last unlit candle's dying ember-light, her face was calm — but her eyes were doing the thing again, the door-opening-onto-a-boarded-room thing, staring at something far away that Edmund couldn't see.

"Under the floor," she breathed. "Move. *Now.*"

And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the stone foundations of the city itself, Edmund heard it again — that cold, old, glad voice, like wind through a winter door:

*Tell the keeper: the wolf is coming.*
