## 4. Road of Small Gods

Edmund had never seen a god before the candle. Now he couldn't stop seeing them.

They left Vell before dawn, three shadows on the pilgrim road — Kestrel with her charm-cart rattling behind her like a coffin on wheels, the wolf pacing ahead as if it knew every stone of the way, and Edmund in the middle, trying not to look back at the city that wanted him dead or worse. The Vigil's horns had sounded twice in the night. Kestrel said they'd be hunting the boy who prayed true.

"Keep your prayers small," she'd told him, gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Small enough to fit through a keyhole. If you pray big out here, everything will hear you."

He was trying. But the road made it hard.

The pilgrim road ran south through country Edmund had only known from his mother's stories — orchard country, mill country, the kind of place where nothing happened except weather and weddings. Except now things were happening. He could feel it in his teeth, a low hum like the moment before lightning, and he could see it in the way travelers passed them going north: too fast, too quiet, eyes fixed on the middle distance.

The first village was called Bracken Hollow, and its god lived in a well.

* * *

They smelled it before they saw it — a sweetness like honey poured over rot. The village square was empty except for an old woman kneeling at the wellhead, her forehead pressed to the wet stones, whispering. The well itself was wrong. Water should have smelled like cold and iron. This water breathed. It rose and fell in the shaft like a chest taking slow, patient breaths, and when Edmund leaned over the lip — against every instinct screaming at him not to — he saw a face looking up at him from far below. Not reflected. Looking up. Smiling.

"Don't," Kestrel said, and pulled him back by the collar. Her voice was flat and careful, the voice she used when she was frightened and didn't want the fear to spread. "Well-gods are supposed to be the smallest there are. A cupful of divinity. They grant you clean water and nothing else." She stared down into the breathing dark. "This one has been fed."

"Fed?" Edmund asked. "Gods eat?"

"Everything eats. Most gods sip. Prayers, mostly — thin gruel, but it keeps them alive." She crouched, touched two fingers to the wellhead stone, and snatched them back as if burned. "Someone is feeding this one meat."

The old woman lifted her face from the stones. She was smiling too, the same smile as the thing below, wide and serene and empty as a painted mask. "He answers now," she said. "Our little god answers. I wished for my son to come home from the war, and he came home. He came home *forever*." She laughed, a bright, delighted sound that made Edmund's skin crawl. "He sits by my fire every night. He never leaves. He never *ages*. Isn't it wonderful?"

Kestrel stood very slowly. "Where is your son now?"

"In the house. Being wonderful."

Edmund looked toward the cottage at the square's edge. Through its window, in the firelight, he saw a shape sitting in a chair — man-shaped, but too still, too symmetrical, hands folded in its lap like a doll arranged for display. Its head turned toward the window. Toward him. It smiled with the same wide, hollow smile.

It raised one hand and waved, slowly, fingers moving like reeds underwater.

Edmund did not wave back. His legs carried him backward until he bumped into the cart, and then Kestrel was hauling him away, and the wolf was growling low in its throat, a sound like distant thunder deciding where to strike.

"What did it do to him?" Edmund managed, once they were past the last house.

"It gave him what was wished for," Kestrel said, "and nothing else. No love in it. No life. Just the wish, wearing a body." Her jaw was tight. "That's what happens when gods get strong without getting wise. A starving god grants wishes like a starving dog takes food — teeth first, no manners. Someone has been stuffing these small gods full of something rich, something *human*, and now they're drunk on it and granting prayers like a drunk man spends coin."

The wolf stopped in the road ahead of them. It turned its grey head and spoke, in that voice like wind through a broken window:

"*They remember being loved. That is what they were fed.*"

Kestrel went white. Edmund watched her hand drift to the brand on her wrist — the scar shaped like a wolf's jaw that she'd had since childhood, though she'd told him once, in a rare unguarded hour, that she had no memory of how she got it.

"You know something," Edmund said. "About who's doing the feeding."

"I know nothing," Kestrel said, too quickly. "I know less than nothing. Let's keep walking."

But the wolf looked at Edmund as they passed it, and its yellow eyes held something that felt almost like apology.

* * *

The second village was worse because it was happier.

Millbrook sat in a green fold of hills, and from the ridge above it, Edmund thought at first they'd found the one good miracle in the world. The fields were heavy with grain out of season. The people in the lanes were laughing. Children ran past them barefoot, rosy-cheeked, singing a skipping song about the Lady of the Millpond, and every single one of them turned to smile at Edmund as they passed — every one, in perfect unison, the same smile, the same tilt of the head, before running on.

"They're all blessed," he whispered. "Look at them. Nothing's wrong here."

"Everything's wrong here," Kestrel murmured. "Count the smiles, Edmund. Count how many different ones you see."

He counted. There was one. One smile, worn by two hundred faces, exactly the same, like coins struck from a single mold.

An old miller welcomed them into his home with open arms and a table heavy with bread and stew, and Edmund was so hungry and so tired that he almost forgot to be afraid. Almost. The miller talked while they ate — talked endlessly, joyfully, about the Lady of the Millpond, who had woken after a hundred years of silence and begun granting wishes. Wishes for good harvests. Wishes for healthy children. Wishes for happiness.

"And did everyone get their wish?" Kestrel asked lightly.

"Oh, yes!" The miller beamed. "My wife wished we'd never quarrel again. And we never have! Not once in four months! We agree on everything!" He laughed. "She agrees with me so completely now, sometimes I forget she's in the room. Sometimes *she* forgets she's in the room. She just stands wherever I'm looking and smiles." He leaned forward, confiding, delighted. "Isn't that happiness? Isn't that exactly what wishing is *for*?"

Under the table, Edmund's hand found the wolf's fur. The fur bristled beneath his fingers.

Afterward, outside, in the blue dusk, Edmund finally said it. "The wishes aren't twisted. That's the horrible part. They're granted *exactly*. And exactness is the twist." He heard himself and flinched — that wasn't how he usually talked, plain and sharp, but the road was teaching him a new language. "A prayer is supposed to be a conversation. You ask, and something decides how to answer, with mercy in the gap between. These gods have closed the gap. There's no mercy in it at all. Just... delivery."

Kestrel stopped walking. She looked at him for a long moment, and in the fading light her expression was strange — not surprise, but recognition, like watching someone play a song she'd written and forgotten.

"Twelve years ago," she said quietly, "the gods answered prayers exactly like that. Every wish granted to the letter, and every letter a knife. That's why the Vigil outlawed magic. That's why the realms were sealed." She shook her head. "You're describing the old tyranny, Edmund. And it's coming back, village by village, well by millpond. Something is pouring memories into these small gods — human memories, whole lives — and memory is making them strong, but it isn't making them *good*. It's making them hungry. A god that remembers being human wants to be worshipped the way humans want to be loved. Completely. Without gap. Without mercy."

"Then who's feeding them?"

"That," said Kestrel, "is the question I've been dreading since Bracken Hollow." She glanced at the wolf. "Because whoever it is needs a *lot* of memories. Villages' worth. Maybe cities' worth. And there's only one way to harvest minds on that scale."

The wolf spoke, soft as snowfall: "*Follow the fat gods downstream. Find the hand that feeds.*"

* * *

They found the third village by its silence.

No laughter this time. No song. Ashmoor Road, the crossroads hamlet was called, and as they approached in the grey of morning, Edmund noticed the birds first — hundreds of them sitting motionless on the fences and rooftops, all facing the village center, all silent. Then he noticed the people. They stood in their doorways and their fields and their lanes, standing perfectly still, all facing the same direction: toward the old shrine at the crossroads, where a statue of some minor hearth-saint had stood for centuries.

The statue was weeping. Thick rivulets of something dark and glistening ran down its stone cheeks and pooled around its feet, and the pool was growing, spreading across the road in a slow black tide, and the villagers stood at its edge and watched it come with expressions of perfect, rapturous longing.

"Their god is answering," Kestrel breathed. "All at once. All of them together. Look at their eyes — they're mid-prayer. All of them. It's pulling their prayers out of them like thread off a spool."

Edmund felt it then — felt it *tug* at him, a gentle pressure behind his breastbone, a voice made of every voice saying *come, kneel, ask, we have so much to give, we have been given so much to give* — and beside him the wolf's hackles rose and it planted itself between Edmund and the shrine and *roared*, and the sound was not a wolf's sound at all but thunder cracking the sky open, and every bird took flight at once, and the villagers swayed like wheat in wind—

—and the statue's head turned. Stone ground against stone. The weeping face found them on the road, found Edmund, and its mouth opened, and out of it came a voice braided from a hundred stolen voices, men and women and children speaking in terrible harmony:

"*Keeper.*"

Kestrel made a sound Edmund had never heard from her — small, wounded, involuntary.

"*We know what you buried,*" the choir-god sang. "*We taste it in what we were fed. Come to the Altar, keeper. Come and see what grows from what you broke.*"

Then the tide of dark reached the wolf's paws, and it recoiled, and Kestrel seized the cart and Edmund and dragged them both off the road and into the trees, and they ran, and ran, and did not stop until the village was miles behind and Edmund's lungs were fire.

When they finally halted in a hollow full of ferns, Kestrel sank down against a trunk, shaking. Edmund crouched beside her. Above them, the wolf stood watch, ears pricked south.

"It called you *keeper*," Edmund said softly. "And it said you'd buried something. And it wants you at an altar." He swallowed. "Kestrel. What did you break?"

For a long time she said nothing. Then, in a voice like a woman reading someone else's letter:

"I don't know. But I think—" She pressed her palm flat against the brand on her wrist, and the wolf lowered its great head and rested its muzzle, gently, on her knee. "I think it's the same thing they're feeding them. I think whatever's harvesting memories out there..." She looked up at Edmund, and her eyes were terrified and ancient and certain. "...it started with mine."
