## 6. Feast of Borrowed Faces

Edmund smelled Ashmoor before he saw it.

Not smoke, not dung, not the sour reek of a market town — but sugar. Warm bread and honeyed wine and roasting meat, a sweetness so thick it hung in the air like fog, drifting down the road to meet them a full mile before the walls rose out of the autumn mist. His stomach growled. The wolf's hackles rose.

"That's not food," Kestrel said quietly. She stood with her hood back, wind pulling at her grey-streaked hair, staring at the town as if reading something written above it that Edmund couldn't see. "That's memory. Someone is cooking memories."

"You can *smell* that?"

"I can smell that I'm hungry," she said, "and that this hunger isn't mine." She pulled her hood up again, and her hand went to the charm-bag at her belt — the one she hadn't sold from in weeks, because the charms had started whispering back.

They walked in through the south gate without challenge. There were no guards. There was no need for guards, apparently, because no one in Ashmoor seemed capable of suspicion. The streets were swept clean. The houses were painted in cheerful colors — butter yellow, robin's egg, the pink of early dawn. Children ran past them laughing, and every single one of them was laughing, and Edmund noticed that they laughed the way his little cousin used to after Father made him practice: correctly, on cue, but a half-beat late.

"Good morrow!" called a baker from her doorway, flour to her elbows. "Are you come for the Feast?"

"What feast?" Kestrel asked.

"The Feast of the Giver, of course." The baker beamed. Her eyes were the problem, Edmund decided. They were warm and soft and utterly empty, like windows painted onto a house where nobody lived. "He gives us our lives back. Every year He gives them back better than before."

The wolf growled low in its chest. The baker didn't hear it. Nobody heard anything they didn't want to hear in Ashmoor; Edmund was beginning to understand that.

* * *

They found lodging at an inn called the Honeyed Hearth, because everything in Ashmoor was named after sweetness or warmth. The innkeeper, a round man with a face like a risen loaf, charged them nothing. "Money's for strangers," he said fondly. "You'll stay three nights, and by the third you won't be strangers anymore."

"We're leaving tomorrow," Kestrel said.

"Of course you are," the innkeeper agreed pleasantly, and showed them to rooms that smelled of lavender and old, old magic.

Edmund shared a room with the wolf, which slept across the doorway like a drawn bolt. From the window he could see the town square, where carpenters were raising long trestle tables under strings of lanterns. At the far end of the square stood a shrine — no, not a shrine. A *throne*. A chair of white wood and silver ribbon, empty for now, draped in cloth-of-gold, facing rows and rows of benches.

Below the window, two women passed arm in arm.

"—and do you remember," one was saying, "the summer we swam in the mill pond, when we were girls? And Marla lost her shoe and the miller's boy fished it out and—"

"I remember," the other sighed happily. "I remember it perfectly."

And Edmund felt the cold walk down his spine, because he'd seen the mill pond on their way into town, and it wasn't a pond at all anymore. It had been drained years ago — there were sheep grazing in the dry basin, and a farmer mending the sluice gate. Whatever those two women remembered, it had never happened. Or it had happened once, somewhere, to someone else, and been taken like a coat off a line and given away twice.

That night he dreamed of his mother. That wasn't new. But in the dream she was well — sitting up in bed, color in her cheeks, asking him to bring her tea — and when he woke with tears on his face he lay in the dark listening to the distant sound of the town singing rehearsal hymns to its empty throne, and he understood what the Giver gave.

It gave you whatever you could not bear to live without. It just never asked whether you should have it.

* * *

In the morning, Kestrel was gone from her room.

The wolf found her — the wolf always found her — standing in the square before the golden throne, among a crowd gathering for the Feast. She wasn't moving. She was listening to a priest, if priest it was: a tall figure in saffron robes with a face so kind it looked sculpted rather than born.

"—and He asks so little in return," the priest was saying to her alone, though the crowd pressed around them all. "Only your sorrow. Lay it down, daughter. You have carried it twelve years. We have watched you carry it. Would you not set it down, just for one evening? Just to remember how light feels?"

"I don't—" Kestrel's voice cracked. "What would I remember instead?"

"Whatever brings you joy." The priest smiled. "A childhood without the cold. A teacher who loved you. A city that never turned you out. A name spoken kindly. Choose any life, keeper. We have such shelves of them."

*Keeper.* The word went through the crowd like a fishhook and Edmund saw Kestrel flinch — but the crowd only smiled, because the crowd heard nothing it didn't wish to hear.

"Kestrel!" He shoved forward. "Kestrel, don't."

She turned and looked at him, and it was the worst thing he'd ever seen, worse than the Vigil's hounds, worse than the twisted wish-villages on the road. Because she was crying without knowing it, and her face was full of longing so naked it hurt to witness, and she said, softly, "He's offering me my mother, Edmund. A version of her that stayed. A version of me she never sent away. Twelve years, and I could have tonight with her, and it would feel more real than real."

"It wouldn't *be* real."

"No," she whispered. "But it would *feel* like it. Forever, if I stayed. Every morning waking happy. Do you know what I would give for one morning of that?" Her hand drifted toward the priest's offered palm. The wolf snarled, and the priest's smile widened a fraction, and Edmund realized with horror that the wolf's snarling meant nothing here — the Giver wanted the wolf too. Wanted the divinity in it, the great shelved memory of a god, the richest dish that had ever walked into Ashmoor on four legs.

"Kestrel." He caught her wrist. Her skin was ice. "Your charms. You told me — back in Vell, the first night — you told me hollow charms are crueler than no charms, because people believe in them and the believing breaks them worse when they fail. You *said* that."

Her eyes focused slowly, like someone surfacing from deep water.

"This is a charm," Edmund said. "This whole town. It's your own trick, done by a god, done to a god — done to *you*. And it doesn't work. It can't work. It's just a pretty lie wearing your mother's face."

The priest's kindness curdled, just slightly. "The boy is tired," the priest said to the crowd. "The road has been hard on him. Perhaps the Giver should ease him too — give him back the mother he prays for every night, whole and healthy, forever—"

Edmund's breath stopped. Because that was the trap inside the trap, and gods below, some traitor part of him *wanted* it, wanted it so badly his knees nearly buckled — his mother well, his mother laughing, a house that never went quiet—

But Kestrel's hand closed over his fist then, hard, her nails biting his skin, and her voice came out rough and awake and furious: "*No.* He's mine to protect. And I'm mine to grieve. Both of those are true things, and you can't cook them into something sweeter."

She stepped back from the throne. The crowd murmured — confused, wounded, the way children look when a game ends. Above the golden seat, the air shimmered like heat off a stove, and for one instant Edmund saw the thing beneath the festival: something vast and pale and *hungry*, all mouths, wearing the shape of generosity the way a lure wears the shape of a worm.

Then Kestrel spat on the ground between herself and the throne — an old storm-caller's insult, he'd learn later, older than the covenant — and took his shoulder, and turned them both toward the gate.

Behind them, the singing started anyway. The Feast would happen with or without them. It would happen every night, forever, because the citizens of Ashmoor had given their doubts away years ago, and doubt, Edmund now understood, was the only coin the Giver couldn't counterfeit.

* * *

They made three miles before dusk, walking fast, the wolf ranging ahead with its nose down. They camped in a hollow of blackthorn, no fire. Kestrel sat sharpening a knife she didn't need, and after a long silence she said:

"When it offered me my mother — I almost took it. Not almost. I did take it, for about ten seconds. In my head I was already home."

"I know," Edmund said. "I almost took mine too."

"And that's what frightens me." She looked at the blade, not at him. "The choir on the road, the villages, the wish-twisting — I thought the gods were being fed memories to make them strong. But Ashmoor isn't strength. It's *appetite*. Something out there has learned that the sweetest harvest isn't fear or worship. It's gratitude. People will feed a god willingly, endlessly, if the god makes them happy. No chains required."

The wolf lifted its head from its paws and spoke — that grinding, echoing voice like stones rolled in a riverbed, the voice that still made Edmund's skin crawl even after weeks of travel:

"*South,*" it said. "*The altar-road. And on it — cages. Wagons of cages. They are moving the harvest ahead of the frost.*"

"Cages of what?" Edmund asked, though he already dreaded the answer.

The wolf's eyes caught the last grey light, and flared gold.

"*Prayers,*" it said. "*Living ones. And one of them is singing yours, boy. They have your mother's voice.*"
