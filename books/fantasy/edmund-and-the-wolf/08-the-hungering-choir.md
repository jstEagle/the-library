## 8. The Hungering Choir

The road into the Hollow Vale smelled wrong.

Kestrel had known a hundred bad smells in her years of exile — rot, ash, the copper tang of blood spilled on stone — but this was none of them. This was the smell of *absence*, of air scoured clean of everything that had ever lived in it. The pine trees still stood along the valley walls, but their needles were grey as old hair. A stream ran beside the road, clear and bright, and held no fish, no frogs, no reflection of the three of them walking its bank.

Only Edmund's face was missing from the water. Hers and the wolf's showed plainly enough.

"Maybe he's just—" Edmund started.

"Don't," Kestrel said. She kept her voice level, though her hands had gone cold inside her gloves. "Don't make excuses for it. Look at it. Really look."

He looked. She watched him do it — watched the color leave his cheeks the way it left the trees. He was twelve years old and he had prayed a candle into speech, and since then the world had been teaching him, lesson by lesson, what answered prayers cost. She hated the world a little for it.

The wolf pressed close against Kestrel's leg, its shoulder warm through her coat. It had not spoken since dawn. When it did speak, it spoke in her own stolen memories, and lately those memories had been arriving faster, sharper, like a debt collector closing in.

*You knew this valley,* it said now, low, almost gentle. *You camped here with Sorrel, the winter you were nineteen. You caught trout with your bare hands because you were showing off.*

"I don't remember," Kestrel said automatically.

*No,* said the wolf. *But your body does.* And it turned its head toward the far end of the vale, where the grey pines gave way to something pale and vast: a structure of white stone rising out of the valley floor like a ribcage picked clean. Arches upon arches, tiered and hollow, and from within came a sound.

Kestrel had heard choirs before. Cathedrals in Vell, before the Vigil burned them; pilgrims on the road; her mother, once, singing to a hearth-god so small it lived only in their kitchen. Choirs were made of voices braided together, each one distinct beneath the whole.

This was not that. This was thousands of voices singing a single note, and every voice in it was *empty*. Not harmonized. Erased. As if someone had taken ten thousand people and sanded them down until nothing remained but the sound they could make.

Edmund's hand found hers. Small, chapped, gripping hard.

"That's where they are," he whispered. "The ones who took the memories."

"Yes."

"And the people singing?"

Kestrel looked at the white arches, at the water that would not show a boy's face, and told him the truth because he had earned truth and nothing less.

"The people singing are the memories," she said. "Or what's left when you take everything else away."

* * *

They should have run. Kestrel knew it, and the wolf knew it, and some small surviving animal part of Edmund knew it too — she could feel it in the way his hand trembled against hers. But the road bent toward the choir-structure the way all roads in the god-touched lands now bent toward it, drawn like water to a wound, and by the time they understood they were being pulled rather than walking, the arches stood over them and the light inside was the color of milk.

Priests moved between the pillars. They wore robes the grey of the dead pines, and their faces —

Their faces were *wrong* in a way Kestrel had no word for until the wolf gave her one.

*Masks,* it breathed. *They're wearing masks made of other people's faces. Borrowed faces. That one is wearing a farmer's joy. That one is wearing a bride's grief.*

It was true. Each priest's expression was perfect and total and entirely static — an emotion worn like a plate of armor, nailed on. One passed close enough to touch, and Kestrel saw that behind the borrowed smile, the priest's actual eyes were flat as coins.

"Halt," the priest said, in a voice like a bell struck underwater. "Pilgrims. Have you come to give?"

"We've come to pass through," Kestrel said.

"Everyone comes to give." The smile did not waver. "Some give gladly. Some give screaming. The Choir is not particular. The Choir is only *hungry*." Its coin-flat eyes moved to Edmund, and something behind them shifted — hunger sharpening into recognition. "*Oh.* Oh, the Choir will want this one. Do you know what you have, little pilgrim? Do you know what grows in you?"

"He's my son," Kestrel said, and heard how easily the lie came, and hated herself for how easily it came, and reached for the storm anyway.

She got half a breath of it. Lightning gathered in her palms, blue-white, glorious — and then a second priest set a hand on her shoulder and the storm *drained*, poured out of her like water through open fingers, and she dropped to her knees in the milk-white light with her muscles turned to string.

"Storm-caller," the first priest said, almost tenderly. "Yes. We know your kind. We know *you*." The borrowed smile widened a fraction. "The Choir remembers you, covenant-keeper. Even when you do not."

They took Edmund. He fought — gods, he fought, biting and kicking and praying aloud, and his prayers made the white arches shudder and groan like a ship in ice — but there were nine priests and one boy, and the wolf was pinned under four sets of hands that did not bleed when it tore them, and Kestrel lay on the cold stone floor watching her boy — *not her boy, but hers now, hers by choice* — dragged up the tiers of the Choir, and the last thing she saw before the light took her was his face turning back toward her, mouth shaping her name.

Then nothing. Then the wolf's voice, far away, in the dark:

*Kestrel. Kestrel. Get up. GET UP.*

* * *

She woke on the stream bank, hours later, rain falling. Real rain — she could taste the storm in it, wild and unclaimed, and she drank it from cupped hands like a woman dying of thirst.

The wolf sat over her, hackles raised, watching the white structure downstream. From here the Choir's note was softer, and she could almost pick individual threads out of it — and that was worse, because some of the threads were *familiar*. An old woman's laugh. A soldier's lullaby. Somewhere in that endless note, a mother's love for a sick son, harvested and weaponized, sung forever.

"They're building something," Kestrel rasped. "A god. They're feeding it."

*Not feeding,* the wolf corrected. *Crowning. The memories aren't food, Kestrel. They're claims. Every stolen life is a vote. Every harvested mind is a hand raised in favor. They are assembling a god out of consent that was never given — a tyrant stitched from ten thousand stolen yeses.* Its yellow eyes turned to her. *And your boy is the purest yes they have ever found. He prays true. Whatever he asks for, the old powers want to answer. Crowned atop that Choir, his voice would sanctify the whole rotten thing.*

Kestrel stood. Her legs shook. Her hands still ached where the storm had been ripped out of her, and underneath the ache was the older ache, the one that never closed: twelve empty years, a self she'd paid as toll and could not buy back.

Once, she would have raged. Once — she was beginning to remember — she had been the kind of power that raged, and the raging had broken a world.

So instead she did the harder thing. She thought.

"The Sundered Altar," she said. "How far?"

*Three days' walk. Four, wounded, hunted, without a road.*

"And the Choir's priests — can they drain me again? Take the storm?"

*They take what you offer or what you surrender. They drained you because you reached for power like a woman grabbing a sword hilt. Offered power, freely shaped, freely released — that they cannot hold. It slips. It was always slipping through their masters' fingers. That is why they fear the old covenant, and why they need Edmund to break it properly this time.*

Kestrel looked down at her own hands. Charm-seller's hands. Exile's hands. Hands that had sold hollow brass and painted glass to a city that outlawed wonder, because wonder had burned her once and she had sworn never to carry it again.

"So I stop fighting like a sword," she said slowly. "I fight like a storm. Like weather. Like something that doesn't get held."

The wolf's tail moved — not a wag, nothing so simple. An acknowledgment. *There she is,* it said softly. *There is a piece of her.*

"There's more to bargain for than technique," Kestrel said. "Three days across country crawling with that Choir's collectors. I need roads that don't exist. Rivers that flow uphill. I need the small gods."

*They'll want payment.*

"I know what they'll want." She crouched, pressed her palm flat to the wet earth, and let a thread of lightning run down her arm into the ground — not a strike, an offering, a libation of raw sky. "Hearths and doorsteps and crossroads," she called, in the old cadence the wolf fed her syllable by syllable. "Little ones. Hungry ones. Forgotten ones. I'm the covenant-keeper come home, and I'm asking passage to the Altar. Name your price."

The ground warmed under her hand. All around the stream bank, small lights kindled — in the hollow of a dead tree, under a bridge-stone, in the eyes of a crow that had been watching from a fencepost with far too much attention. Greedy lights. Twisted lights. Gods who had spent twelve years starving and had learned, in the famine, to eat badly.

One spoke from the crow, voice like dry leaves: *The keeper returns. The breaker returns. What will you pay, breaker? Your name? Your years? Your pretty boy's voice?*

"None of those."

*Then nothing you have is worth three days of our protection.*

"My memory of the sun," Kestrel said.

The lights flickered. The crow tilted its head.

"I'm told I loved it once," she went on, and her voice cracked and she let it crack, because these small hungry things respected cracks, respected wounds, respected payment that bled. "I'm told I used to stand in it for hours. I don't remember. Take the memory of sunlight — the feeling of it, the wanting of it, all of it, from every year of my life. In exchange: passage. Three days. Get me to the Sundered Altar alive, and keep the Choir's collectors off us, and it's yours."

*That is a cruel price,* said the crow-god, with something like admiration.

"It's the only honest thing I have left."

Silence. Rain. Then, one by one, the small lights bowed — doorstep-lamps and crossroad-candles and the green glow under the bridge, a dozen minor divinities bending toward her like grass in wind, and the crow spread ragged wings.

*Done, keeper. Done, breaker. Follow the crow. Walk in the rain — the rain is ours now, and ours alone, and the Choir's long hands cannot reach through it.* The crow lifted off the fencepost. *But hurry, covenant-keeper. The Choir has begun to sing your boy's name into the note. And when a god learns a name—*

"—it owns the mouth that speaks it," Kestrel finished.

She pulled her coat tight, checked the knife at her hip out of habit, and stepped into the rain with the wolf at her side. Behind them the Choir sang on, and threaded somewhere in that vast erased note, faint but growing clearer with every hour, was a boy's voice — Edmund's voice — learning, note by stolen note, to sing a prayer he had never chosen.

Kestrel walked faster.

Three days. She had bargained away the sun to buy them, and she meant to spend every one of them like a miser.
