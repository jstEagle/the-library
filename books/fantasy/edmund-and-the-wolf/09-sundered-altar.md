## 9. Sundered Altar

The mountain did not want them there.

Kestrel felt it in her teeth from the moment they crossed the tree line — a low, grinding disapproval, as if the stone itself remembered what had been done upon its crown and resented being asked to witness it again. The path up was not a path so much as a wound: black rock split by an ancient channel where the old covenant-blood had once run, now dry and furred with frost. Above them, through the tearing wind, the Sundered Altar hung against the sky like a broken jaw.

Edmund climbed ahead of her, small and stubborn, his breath ragging out in white plumes. The wolf pressed close at Kestrel's hip, its grey flank warm against her leg, and every few strides it looked up at her with those amber eyes — *her* eyes, she knew that now — as if checking that she still intended to go through with this. As if it already knew how much of her wanted to turn around.

She did intend to go through with it. That was the horror of it. Twelve years ago she had stood on this same stone and made a choice, and the price had been herself, and some buried, traitorous part of her had been walking toward this summit ever since. Not to undo it. To finish it.

"Kestrel." Edmund stopped, bracing against the wind. "Do you hear singing?"

She heard it. She had been hearing it for an hour and pretending otherwise. It came down off the mountain in threads, sweet and wrong, a choir of voices that did not breathe between notes. Each thread was a voice she almost knew. A market crier from Vell who had vanished last spring. A pilgrim woman they'd passed on the road, gone gray-eyed and smiling. And beneath all of them, woven through like a spine—

"Mama," Edmund whispered.

* * *

The altar itself was a ring of standing stones, each taller than a chapel, each sheared cleanly in half — top halves missing entirely, carried off or crumbled in the sundering, no one living knew which. In the center, where the covenant-stone should have stood, there was only a pit of darkness that gave off light instead of shadow. And rising out of it, unfolding like smoke learning to have bones, was the god.

It wore faces the way a conqueror wears banners. Kestrel saw a farmer's weathered cheeks dissolve into a scholar's pale brow; saw a child's mouth open and speak with a grandmother's timbre. Hundreds of harvested lives stitched into one ascending shape, and at its heart — held up like a jewel, like bait, like a blade — burned a memory Kestrel recognized instantly because Edmund had described it a hundred times on the road, haltingly, the way children describe wounds.

His mother, in the last week of her dying, braiding his hair by candlelight because her hands were too weak for anything else, telling him: *Whatever happens, you were the best thing I ever grew.*

The god held that memory up and let it shine, and Edmund made a sound Kestrel would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life.

"You brought me fuel," the god said. Every face spoke together, and none of them moved. Its voice was warm. That was the worst of it — after the choir's cold harmony, the god itself sounded like a hearth, like soup, like someone calling you home through a door left open. "The boy who prays true. Do you know what your prayers taste like, little one? Like bread. Like the first rain after drought. They have been starving us for twelve years, and you—" the faces rippled, tender, terrible— "you fed yourself to us a spoonful at a time."

"He's not yours," Kestrel said.

The god turned its attention on her, and every stolen face focused at once, and she felt twelve years of exile compress into a single instant of being *known*. "Kestrel Yarrow. Charm-seller. Liar. And underneath all that—" the warmth curdled into delight— "*Covenant-keeper.* You broke us. You unmade the bridge between realms and left us starving in the dark, and you paid for it with your own name, and do you know what we have decided, in our long hunger?" It leaned closer, a cathedral of borrowed faces bending down. "We have decided to forgive you. We have collected enough of the dead and the emptied to rebuild what you shattered, and when we are whole again, we will be *gentle*. Come. Merge with your wolf. Become what you were. Help us raise the covenant anew — and this time, you will be on the right side of it."

And oh, it was a good offer. That was the trap of it. Kestrel felt it slide into her like a key into a lock she'd forgotten she had. *Gentle.* She thought of the villages on the road, the greed-twisted wishes, the small gods gorging wrongly — and understood suddenly that they had been starving animals, and here was the shepherd come to order the flock. All she had to do was stop fighting what she was. All she had to do was take back everything she'd paid away.

The wolf growled low beside her, but it wasn't warning *her*. It was warning the god. *Her*, too. Both at once. It had always been both at once.

"She says no," Kestrel said.

"She is half of you. Half is not whole, and whole is what the covenant requires." The god's voices dropped, and now each face spoke separately, a chorus becoming a crowd, a crowd becoming a mob. "But we do not need your consent, keeper. We need only your shape."

It reached for Edmund.

* * *

What happened next, Kestrel would never be able to tell as sequence. It was a storm, and storms do not happen in order.

The god's will came down on the mountain like a hand, and she met it with her own — not the charm-seller's tricks, not the buried storm-calling, but something older that rose out of her the moment she stopped pretending it wasn't hers. Lightning answered her before she called it. The frost on the stones leapt up in a blinding halo. And the wolf — the wolf *unfolded*, grey fur streaming into grey cloud, amber eyes widening into two suns, until what crouched beside her was half a divinity wearing the shape of everything she had amputated from herself twelve years ago.

*Merge,* the wolf said, inside her skull, inside her ribs, everywhere. *Now, while he holds the boy. Now, or we lose.*

"Together?"

*Together. And Kestrel — listen to me, because there will not be time later. When we are whole, you will remember everything. Every tyranny we propped up. Every prayer we ate. You will feel how *good* it was.* The wolf's voice cracked, human and divine at once. *That feeling is a lie. Promise me you'll remember it's a lie.*

"I promise," she said, and did not know if she was lying, and hated that she didn't know.

They merged.

There was no other word for it. The boundary between them went soft as wet paper and then it went, and Kestrel Yarrow became — became *vast*. Memories poured home like a river returning to a breached lake: her mother's kitchen, her first storm, her ordination at seventeen, the gods' faces when she told them *no more*, the sundering itself — her own hands tearing the bridge, the scream of a hundred deities, the price named and paid. She was whole. She was the covenant-keeper entire, power rolling off her in tides, and the mountain bowed, and the clouds arranged themselves into reverence, and it felt —

It felt *magnificent.*

That was the lie. She could feel it even as she loved it, the way you can feel poison working while your mouth waters. Whole, she looked down at the god in its pit and saw not a monster but a *problem*, easily solved, and beneath that a resource, and beneath that a subject, and she understood with glacial clarity how the old tyrannies had begun: not with hunger, but with competence. With power so complete that mercy became a mood rather than a duty.

The god struck at her with everything it had stolen. It threw the dead at her — faces, voices, final moments, a thousand harvested lives screaming their love and grief and terror as weapons. It threw Edmund's mother at him, the braid, the candlelight, *the best thing I ever grew*, reshaped into chains. Edmund fell to his knees on the stones, sobbing, reaching for a ghost.

And whole-and-divine Kestrel nearly let him kneel. Nearly calculated, coldly, that a grieving boy was a manageable boy.

That calculation was what saved him. Because the part of her that flinched at her own arithmetic — the mortal part, the charm-seller, the woman who had spent twelve years selling hollow charms to people who needed real ones — that part *revolted*. She seized the god's weaponized memory out of the air and unmade its edges, and gave it back to Edmund clean: not a chain, just a memory, his mother's hands in his hair, nothing attached to it but love.

"Take it back," she told him. "It was never theirs. Nothing they took was ever theirs."

The god shrieked with a thousand mouths, and the mountain shook, and the battle of wills tipped — because a god built of stolen things cannot survive being *returned to sender*. Memory by memory, life by life, the harvest tore loose and flew home, to graves and empty beds and gray-eyed villagers far below, and the great shape thinned from cathedral to bonfire to candleflame.

But the pit remained. The hunger remained. And the covenant — the covenant required a keeper, whole, standing at the sundered stone, choosing.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the pit with the storm of herself roaring in her chest, and the intoxication sang its beautiful lie — *stay whole, stay vast, rule gently, you of all beings could rule gently* — and the wolf-half within her waited, trembling, to learn whether she had lied with her promise.

Below her, small and hoarse and alive, Edmund got to his feet, wiped his face, and said:

"Kestrel? What happens now?"

She looked down at him. She looked into the pit. And she began, at last, to understand what the sealing would cost — and what it would require her to forget.
