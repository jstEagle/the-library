## 2. Tonight's Date, Written Long Ago

The lock on the prophecy book was not a lock. Mirabel had understood this for years, in the way one understands a scar—without ever asking how it got there.

It was a band of black iron, seamless, wrapped around the book's girth like a shackle around a wrist, and it bore no keyhole. Corvane kept the key anyway, on a chain beneath his shirt, and every morning he touched it once through the fabric of his waistcoat, a small superstitious gesture he probably believed no one noticed. Mirabel noticed everything. It was his trade. A Ledgerkeeper who did not notice was a Ledgerkeeper already dead.

So when the iron band lay open on the reading table that night—unlocked, unclasped, hanging slack as a broken jaw—Mirabel stopped three steps into the archive and did not breathe for a long moment.

The hall above had gone quiet hours ago. Old Hespera had retired to her room with her tinctures and her coughing; Tam Vetch had left at dusk without saying where, which was what Tam Vetch always did; and Corvane was shut in his study with the good brandy and the bad ledgers, doing whatever guildmasters did when their guild was dying by inches. The archive belonged to Mirabel alone, as it had belonged to him alone for six years now, since the night the last apprentice before him walked out with everything she owned in one satchel and never wrote to say why.

He should have closed the book. He knew that even then, even as his feet carried him forward across the cold flagstones. There were rules. There were *always* rules, and the rules existed because the Ledgerkeepers traded in impossible things, and impossible things did not forgive carelessness. Rule the fourth: nothing in the deep archive opens itself.

But it had opened. And the candle in his hand leaned toward it—not flickering, not guttering, but *leaning*, the flame drawn sideways like a compass needle finding north, like something hungry turning toward the smell of food.

"Rule the fourth," Mirabel said aloud, to hear a human voice in the room, even if it was only his own. "Nothing opens itself."

The book said nothing. Books rarely do. But the silence it made was a particular silence, dense and attentive, the silence of a held breath, and Mirabel—who had spent his whole life learning to read silences the way other men read letters—understood that he was being waited for.

He set the candle down. His hands were steady. That surprised him. Inside, some small animal part of him was screaming the way animals scream before an earthquake, but his hands were steady, because they had been trained to be steady over ten thousand entries, over bottled echoes and debts paid in years and a jar of last light from a village that no longer existed anywhere but in glass. Steadiness was the one inheritance the guild had given him that could not be sold or stolen.

He opened the cover.

The paper inside was a century old at least, vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, and it smelled the way old magic smells: like rain on hot stone, like the moment after lightning. The early pages were dense with a script Mirabel did not know—not any alphabet but something older, lines that curved like water finding its way downhill. Then, gradually, page by page, decade by decade, the script resolved into common letters, as though the book itself had learned to speak the language of whoever was meant to read it.

And then, near the end—the end, though there were still perhaps thirty blank pages remaining, and Mirabel's eye measured them the way a condemned man measures the distance to the wall—he found it.

The date.

*The seventeenth of the Waning Moon, in the year the ash fell on Harrow Fen.*

Tonight.

His own handwriting could not have been more familiar to him. But this was not his hand. This was a century-old ink, browned at the edges of each stroke, sunk so deep into the vellum that when he tilted the page against the candlelight the letters cast shadows—actual shadows, tiny ridges of dried ink standing up off the surface like scars.

Someone had written tonight's date one hundred years ago. Someone had known.

And below the date, in the same patient, sunken hand:

*Mirabel Iversen, last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers, will find this entry.*

Not *may find*. Not *should find*. *Will.*

He read it four times. His lips moved. The candle flame leaned harder, straining toward the page like a dog at the end of its rope, and somewhere in the walls of the archive a pipe ticked as the day's heat left the stones, and Mirabel stood very still in the middle of all that quiet and felt the floor tilt beneath him without moving at all.

There was more. Of course there was more. Below his name, the ink continued in that same terrible certainty:

*On this night the door will be knocked upon twice. The knocker carries the last voice of the Choir of Binding. The cage upon the king is failing. Thirteen turns remain, and fewer than half are theirs.*

*The keeper of the book must choose: the record, or the world.*

Mirabel sat down. Not gracefully—he simply stopped being able to stand, and the stool was there, and he was on it, and for a while he did nothing but look at the words and listen to his own heart, which had gone strange and loud, like footsteps in an empty house.

*A door will be knocked upon twice.*

The guild door had been knocked upon twice exactly once in his memory. Three years ago, a debt collector, drunk, who had pounded and pounded until Corvane went down and explained, in a voice like a closing ledger, that the guild owed nothing to anyone anymore. Twice. Knock knock. Everyone else knocked once, politely, the way you knock at the house of people who might not want you.

Tomorrow—or tonight, or within some span the prophecy had not deigned to specify—someone would knock twice. Someone carrying *the last voice of a choir*. Mirabel knew the name Choir of Binding the way every Ledgerkeeper knew it: from the margins, from the cautionary entries, from the single line in the guild charter that no one ever taught aloud but everyone absorbed the way children absorb fear. *Let no member traffic with the Choir, nor restore what was sundered.* No reason given. Reasons, Corvane liked to say, were for grocers.

*Thirteen turns remain, and fewer than half are theirs.*

Fewer than half. Fewer than six turns of the moon, and the moon was already waning outside the high window, worn thin as an old coin.

Mirabel looked at the remaining blank pages. Thirty of them. Enough, perhaps, for thirteen turns' worth of prophecy, if the book wrote sparsely. Enough for an ending. He reached out to turn the page—to see whether the future continued past tonight, whether there was anything written beyond the knock, beyond the choice—and his finger had barely brushed the corner when the page refused him. Not stuck. *Refused.* It resisted the way a living thing resists, with a small muscular reluctance, and Mirabel snatched his hand back as though he'd touched a stove.

"All right," he whispered. "All right."

He did not remember deciding to go upstairs. He simply arrived, at some point, in the corridor outside Corvane's study, holding the book against his chest with both arms like a child carrying something too heavy, the iron band dangling loose and clinking softly against the boards with each step. The sound seemed enormous in the sleeping hall. From behind the study door came the smell of brandy and lamp oil and, faintly, underneath, the smell Corvane himself carried these days: dust, and old paper going brittle, and something scorched.

He knocked once. Then, because his hands were shaking now, finally, uselessly, he knocked again.

Twice.

The realization arrived a half-second too late, and he stood frozen in the corridor, staring at his own knuckles as though they belonged to someone else. *A door will be knocked upon twice.* But no—that was the guild door, the outer door, the great iron-studded door that faced the street. This was an interior door. This meant nothing. This was coincidence, and coincidence was the currency of people who had never read a ledger.

From within: "Enter, or don't."

Corvane was at his desk with the brandy decanter uncorked and two glasses out, which was wrong—one glass was wrong, two glasses was worse—and he looked up when Mirabel came in with the expression of a man watching weather turn. His eyes went first to Mirabel's face. Then to the book. Then they changed.

They did not widen. They did not narrow. They did something worse: they went flat, the way water goes flat before it freezes, and Mirabel watched his guildmaster—a man he had loved the way you love a roof over your head, without thinking about it, until the night you notice the leak—watch the book in his arms and become someone else entirely.

"Where," Corvane said quietly, "is the band."

"Open."

"Open." He said it the way a physician repeats a symptom. "You opened it."

"It was open. When I came down. The band was—" Mirabel heard his own voice climbing and dragged it back down. "The band was hanging loose. Rule the fourth, master. Nothing in the deep archive opens itself."

"No." Corvane rose. He moved slowly, deliberately, the way men move when they are buying time to think. "No, it does not. Give it to me."

"You told me it couldn't be opened. You told me the key was lost with Master Ellery, that the book was sealed until—" 

"I told you what kept you safe." Corvane crossed the room and held out his hand, palm up, and his fingers were trembling. Mirabel had never seen Corvane's hands tremble. Not at funerals. Not the day the tax assessors came with their wax seals and their polite threats. Not ever. "Give it to me, boy. Tonight. Do not read another word of it."

"I've read it."

The words came out of Mirabel before he could weigh them, and having come out, they would not be recalled, and he watched them land on Corvane like stones dropped into still water. The ripples went out. The flatness in the old man's eyes cracked, just slightly, and beneath the crack was something Mirabel had never seen there before and would spend many nights afterward trying to name.

Fear. Raw, undiluted, animal fear.

"What did it say?" Corvane asked, and his voice had gone soft, gentle almost, the voice he used on dying clients, and somehow that gentleness was the most frightening thing in the room. "Tell me exactly. Word for word. You have the gift, Mirabel—you keep things word for word. So keep this one true."

And Mirabel, because he did have the gift, because he had been trained since childhood to hold language whole and unbroken, gave it back to him entire. The date. The name. *Will find this entry.* The knock upon the door, twice. The choir. The failing cage. The king. *Thirteen turns, and fewer than half are theirs.* And last, the sentence that had been sitting in his chest since the archive like a swallowed coal:

*The keeper of the book must choose: the record, or the world.*

When he finished, the lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as though it too were listening. Corvane had not moved. The brandy sat untouched between the two glasses, and Mirabel understood suddenly, absurdly, why there were two glasses: Corvane had been waiting up for someone. Or drinking to someone. Or drinking *about* someone, and the second glass was grief, poured and never filled.

"The choir," Corvane said at last. "It said the choir."

"The last voice of the Choir of Binding. Coming to the door. Carrying—" Mirabel faltered. "Master, what is the Choir of Binding? Truly. Not the margin warnings. Not the charter line. What did we do to them?"

"We?" Corvane laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as pages turning. "We did nothing, boy. We weren't born. The guild that wrote those warnings wasn't even this guild—it was the mother guild, the first hall, before the purges, before—" He stopped himself. His jaw worked. "Before the fire."

"Before my parents died."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. It pressed on the room. Corvane picked up the empty second glass, turned it once in his fingers, and set it down again without pouring anything into it.

"Yes," he said. "Before that."

"And the book knew. A hundred years ago, the book knew tonight, and it knew my name, and it knew—" Mirabel's voice did something humiliating then, cracking in the middle like a boy's, and he hated it, and he pushed through it anyway. "It knows what's coming, master. Something is coming to our door. Something about a king and a cage and thirteen turns of the moon, and it says I have to *choose*, and I don't even know what the choice is, and you're standing there telling me to hand it over like it's a misfiled invoice—"

"Burn it."

Mirabel stopped.

"What?"

Corvane had turned away. He stood facing the window, where the waning moon hung over the rooftops like a fingernail paring, and his shadow lay long across the floorboards, reaching almost to Mirabel's feet.

"Burn the book. Tonight, before the knock comes. Burn it in the yard stove, watch it to ash, and bring me the ashes, and we will speak of it never again. That is not a request, Mirabel. That is the last instruction your guildmaster will ever give you, and if you have one shred of the sense I tried to beat into you over six years, you will obey it and live a long, dull, unremarkable life, which is more than—" His voice caught. He mastered it. "Which is more than most keepers get."

"You want me to burn a prophecy."

"I want you to burn a *death warrant*." Corvane spun on him, and for one instant Mirabel saw the man he must have been thirty years ago, before the dust and the brandy and the slow collapse of everything: hard, bright, dangerous. "You think that book is history? History is dead, boy, history can't hurt you. That book is a *contract*, and contracts bind both parties, and the party on the other side of that particular signature has been waiting a hundred years for a keeper foolish enough to read his name aloud. Every word you spoke in this room just now, you *signed*. Do you understand me? Reading is signing. That is the first law of the craft, the one law, the law under all the others—"

"Then why teach me to read at all?"

It came out quiet. Quieter than he intended. But it landed, and he saw it land, and Corvane's face went through several weathers in the space of a breath—anger, grief, something that might have been pride arriving at the worst possible moment—and settled at last into a weariness so profound it seemed geological.

"Because someone had to carry it," Corvane said. "After me. Because a craft dies when no one remembers it, and I could not—" He turned back to the window. "I could not let it die with me. Whatever else I've done. Whatever I'm about to do. I could not let it die."

*Whatever I'm about to do.* The phrase snagged in Mirabel's mind like a fishhook, and he would think about it later, much later, turning it over in the dark—but not now. Now there was only the book in his arms, warm now, actually warm, warm as a sleeping animal, and the loose iron band clinking against it, and the waning moon in the window, and the two glasses on the desk, one full and one empty, and the question he had never in six years dared to ask, rising in him now like floodwater.

"No," Mirabel said.

Corvane did not turn around.

"I said burn it, boy—"

"I heard what you said." Mirabel's heart was slamming against the book's cover, against the old warm vellum, and his voice shook, and he let it shake, because hiding it had never once worked and he was done pretending otherwise. "Six years I've obeyed you. Six years I've shelved and catalogued and swept and kept silent, and I never asked why the guild has three members instead of thirty, and I never asked what happened to the others, and I never asked about the fire, and I never asked about my parents, because you said asking was dangerous and I believed you. I believed you because believing you was easier than being alone." He drew a breath that hurt. "But the book has my name in it. Written before I was born. And you want me to feed it to a stove and go back to sleep, and I can't. I can't do it. For the first time since you took me in, master—I am not going to do what you say."

The lamp flame leaned. Just slightly. Toward Mirabel, toward the book, the way it had leaned in the archive, and Corvane saw it, and something in the old man's shoulders went out—not collapsing, but *banking*, like a fire left to smolder overnight.

"So it's begun choosing sides already," Corvane murmured. "The book. The flame. Everything leans toward you now." He was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice was the old voice, the teaching voice, and that was somehow the saddest thing of all. "Then hear the last thing I will ever teach you, and may it keep you better than I have. Prophecies are not fortunes, Mirabel. They are invoices. Something, somewhere, has already rendered the service, and the bill always comes due, and the bill is always larger than the client expects." He picked up the full glass at last and looked into it rather than at his apprentice. "Go to bed. Leave the book on the table. If someone knocks twice—if such a creature exists—do not answer. Let the hall stay dark. Let the record close. Some trades are best defaulted."

Mirabel stood in the doorway a moment longer, the book against his chest, its warmth seeping through his shirt like a second heartbeat.

"Goodnight, master," he said, which was not agreement, and they both knew it was not agreement, and neither of them said anything more.

He did not take the book to his room. He carried it back down into the archive, past the shelves of bottled echoes and jars of last light, past the ledger of debts paid in years with its thin, patient columns, and he set it on the reading table beneath the leaning candle, and he sat down across from it, and he waited.

Outside, the waning moon climbed. The hall creaked and settled. Somewhere far off, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

And then—faint, distant, unmistakable, ringing up through the stones of the archive like a bell struck underwater—the sound of the guild door.

Knock.

Mirabel was on his feet before the echo faded, heart hammering, the candle flame straining toward the stairs like a pointing finger.

Knock.

Twice.

* * *

He took the stairs two at a time, the prophecy book clutched to his chest, its warmth pulsing against his ribs like a second heart. The corridor was dark. The great hall was darker. At the far end, the guild door stood in its frame of black oak, iron-studded, ancient, and through the frosted pane beside it he could see a shape—a silhouette, slight and still, waiting in the streetlamp's amber spill.

Hespera's door remained shut. Tam Vetch's room stood empty, as it always did at this hour. Corvane's study was silent, the lamp beneath the door extinguished—which was its own kind of lie, because Mirabel had heard the floorboard above his own ceiling sigh not five minutes ago, and knew the old man was awake, listening, letting his apprentice walk toward the door alone.

Coward, Mirabel thought, and then, immediately, unfairly: *or a man protecting the only thing he has left.*

He reached for the bolt.

His hand hovered. Through the frosted glass, the silhouette shifted—a tilt of the head, listening, the way you listen when you know someone is on the other side of a door deciding. And Mirabel became suddenly, acutely aware that he was about to do the thing the prophecy had named, the thing Corvane had forbidden, the thing that could not be undone once the iron swung wide. Reading is signing. Opening is worse.

The book against his chest grew warmer. Not painfully. Persuasively. Like a hand resting on his shoulder, like a voice saying *yes, this one, this is the door, this is the hour, and you were always going to open it—written so, a hundred years before you drew breath.*

"Last chance to default," Mirabel whispered to himself, to the book, to the century-old ink that had already spent his courage for him.

He drew the bolt. The lock turned with a sound like a bone setting.

The door swung inward on the night, and the cold rushed in smelling of rain and river and something else—something Mirabel had no word for yet, something that made the candles in the hall lean all at once, every flame in the building bending toward the threshold like grass before wind.

On the step stood a woman wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate, hood thrown back, rain beading in hair the red of banked coals. She was younger than her eyes. Her eyes were old the way the vellum was old, sunk deep with ink and patience, and when she looked at Mirabel—looked *through* him, at the book against his chest, at the warmth bleeding through his shirt—she smiled the way people smile at a chord they have been searching a lifetime to resolve.

"You kept it open," she said. Her voice did something to the air as she spoke; the rain at the threshold hesitated mid-fall, hung a half-heartbeat, then remembered itself. "A hundred years, and every keeper before you nailed it shut, chained it, buried it, burned the wings off the messenger birds. But you—" She stepped forward, one boot crossing the threshold, and every candle in the hall stood up straight, attentive, as if called to order. "You read your own name aloud. Good. Brave. Foolish. All three. I've come a very long way to find the man who would do all three at once."

Behind her, far down the street, a second figure detached itself from the shadows and began walking toward the guild door with the unhurried purpose of someone who has all night and no soul. The woman did not look at it. But her voice, when she spoke again, dropped low and quick, a thread pulled tight:

"Shut the door behind me, keeper. Quickly. And then you'll want to sit down—because I am the last voice of the Choir of Binding, the cage on your king is breaking, and we have less time than even your book dares to say."
