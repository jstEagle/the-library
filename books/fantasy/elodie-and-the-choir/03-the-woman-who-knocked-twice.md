## 3. The Woman Who Knocked Twice

The road to the Ledgerkeepers' hall had been eating itself for years, and Elodie walked up its ruined spine in the last grey hour before dawn, when the fog lay so thick against the cobblestones that each step felt like wading through milk. She had come a long way to knock on this door. She had come, if she was honest with herself, because there was nowhere else left to knock.

She stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up.

The hall had been grand once. She could read its bones beneath the rot: the colonnade's stumps still wore the ghosts of gilt; the great doors, though weathered to the color of old teeth, were carved deep with ledgers open like wings, with scales weighing things no scale should hold. Above the lintel, half the motto had fallen away, leaving only *WE KEEP* in letters worn smooth by a century of rain.

*We keep,* Elodie thought. *And what do you keep, when everyone who kept is gone?*

She climbed the steps. Her boots left dark prints in the fog-wet stone, and behind her, faintly, the street seemed to listen.

Twice. The prophecy—she had read it, or rather she had *heard* it, once, in a voice that came out of a locked book three hundred miles away and would not stop singing to her until she came—had been specific about this. Not once. Once was a beggar, a peddler, a debt collector. Twice was an omen. Twice meant the door would answer.

She raised her hand and knocked.

The sound went through the door and did not echo. It went *in*, the way a stone goes into well-water, swallowed whole, and for a long moment nothing answered at all. Somewhere beyond the wood she heard a candle gutter—a small, startled sound, wax and flame and breath—and then footsteps, hesitant, growing surer, and the scrape of bolts drawn back one by one, more bolts than any honest door required.

The door opened six inches. A chain caught it there, taut as a held note.

One eye looked out at her. Young. Dark-ringed with sleeplessness. Suspicious in the way of someone who has learned that suspicion is cheaper than grief.

"We're closed," the eye said.

"I know," Elodie said. "I knocked twice anyway."

The eye blinked. Behind it, something shifted—not a person moving, but the air itself, the way a room shifts when a long-sealed window is opened somewhere far away. She felt it against her skin like the first pressure-change before a storm: the hall knew she was there. Whatever slept in its ledgers had turned over in its sleep.

"The chain," she said gently. "Please."

"You could be anyone."

"Yes." She smiled, and it cost her something, because smiling was not the first thing her mouth remembered how to do. "That's rather the point of me. No one, from anywhere, belonging to nothing. But I can prove what I am better than any name could. May I?"

The eye considered her for the length of three heartbeats. Then the chain slithered loose, and the door opened, and Elodie stepped into the Ledgerkeepers' hall for the first time in her short, impossible life.

* * *

Inside, the dark was thick with dust and older things. Candles burned in iron sconces along the entry corridor—only a handful, their light rationed like bread in a siege—and their flames stood perfectly upright, disciplined, the way flames burn when they have been trained by people who believe even fire must keep accounts.

Elodie walked past them, and the candles leaned.

All of them, together, in perfect unison, bending toward her like wheat toward wind, like flowers toward sun, like the drowned toward the surface. The flames stretched tall and thin and quivering, and the shadows on the walls swung wide, and the young man behind her made a sound low in his throat—the sound of a belief being broken and hastily reassembled.

"Stop that," he said. It took Elodie a moment to realize he was talking to the candles. It took her another moment to realize they obeyed, straightening slowly, reluctantly, the way children straighten when a parent clears their throat—but they kept leaning again the moment his attention moved elsewhere, straining toward her like dogs toward a whistle only they could hear.

"They do that everywhere I go," Elodie said. "Fire, water, birdsong. Things that are made of rhythm." She turned to face him fully for the first time.

He was younger than she'd expected—younger than the weight he carried, which sat on him visibly, in the set of his shoulders and the ink stains worked so deep into his fingers they looked like bruises. He held a candlestick before him like both weapon and shield. His eyes moved over her the way she imagined his pen moved over a ledger: cataloging, assessing, entering her into some internal column marked *threats* or perhaps *miracles*, and she suspected that in his private accounting those two columns shared a page.

"You're the ledger boy," she said.

Something flickered across his face—wound and pride in equal measure. "I'm the apprentice. Mirabel Iversen. There's a difference." He lifted his chin. "Who told you that name?"

"A book," Elodie said. "A locked one. With tonight's date written in it, and yours."

The candlestick trembled. Just slightly. Just enough.

"So you've seen it too," Mirabel said quietly. "It fell open on its own. Last night. A hundred years of sealed pages, and it opened itself to tonight, like—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "Why am I telling you this? You could be a crown agent. You could be anything."

"I told you. That's the point of me."

"Say something true, then. One true thing I can check."

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. Around them the candles leaned and strained, and the dust hung motionless in the air as though even it were waiting, and she thought: *here it is, then. The moment where I say it aloud for the first time and find out whether saying it makes it real or breaks it.*

"One true thing," she said. "Listen."

And she hummed.

It was a single chord—three notes braided into one, low and high and between, a sound like a door opening and a door closing at the same time, like a chain being forged and a chain being broken. She had never sung it for a living soul. It was not a song; songs were for pleasure, for mourning, for the ordinary business of hearts. This was older than purpose. This was the sound the world made the day someone decided it should stay.

The candles did not lean this time. They *bowed*. Every flame in the corridor bent flat and low, and the shadows collapsed into them, and the darkness rushed in close and reverent, and somewhere deep in the hall—down past the counting rooms and the vaulted archive, down where the oldest ledgers slept under lock and cloth—something answered. A resonance. A single sustained tone rising out of the stones themselves, faint as memory, matching her chord note for note the way a tuning fork answers its twin.

Mirabel's face had gone white. The candlestick shook openly now in his grip.

"What was that," he whispered.

"That," Elodie said, letting the chord die slowly, unwillingly, the way you unclench a hand you've held closed for years, "was the Choir of Binding. Or what's left of it. Which is me."

* * *

He brought her to a counting room off the main corridor—a small room, built for many and holding one, with a scarred oak table and chairs enough for a dozen clerks and dust enough for a hundred years. He lit no new candles. The existing ones simply leaned toward her wherever she sat, and he had given up pretending not to notice.

"Sit," he said, and then, catching himself, "please. Sit. Please." He rubbed his ink-stained fingers across his mouth. "I'm sorry. I haven't—I don't receive visitors. No one receives visitors. We're three people in a hall built for four hundred, and we spend most of our time deciding which rooms to let die next."

"Three," Elodie said. "Out of how many, once?"

"Once? Thousands. Every city had a chapter house. Every impossible thing in the world passed through a ledger like—" He gestured vaguely upward, at everything and nothing. "Bottled echoes. Debts paid in years. Rain sold to drought countries. We kept the accounts of magic, and magic kept us in return, and now—" His voice did something complicated and settled on bitter. "Now we're three. Corvane, our guildmaster. Old Tam, who mostly sleeps. And me."

"And you're the last apprentice."

"I'm the *only* apprentice." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "There's a difference in that too."

"There is," Elodie said softly. "I know all the differences there are, in being the last of something."

He looked at her then—really looked, past the suspicion, past the ledger-columns in his eyes—and she watched him arrive at the question without asking it, the way careful people do. So she answered it anyway.

"You want to know what I mean. The Choir of Binding was nine voices. Nine singers, trained from childhood, whose harmonies could hold shut anything that needed holding. Gates. Plagues. Griefs. Kings." She laid her hands flat on the table, and the wood hummed faintly beneath her palms, sympathetic, listening. "A hundred years ago there were nine. Twenty years ago there were four. Ten years ago, two. I buried the second-to-last voice in spring, in a grave I dug myself because there was no one left to help me." She heard her own voice go flat and careful and hated it, and continued anyway. "I am the choir now. All nine parts, in one throat. And a throat is not a cathedral, Master Iversen. A throat leaks."

"Leaks what?"

"The binding." She met his eyes. "You've seen the king. Everyone has. Aldric the Restorer, they call him, the beloved, the wise. Have you ever wondered why he hasn't aged properly in twenty years? Why his portraits have to be repainted from older faces? Why his eyes, in the freshest coin-strikes, look like windows with the shutters closed?"

Mirabel had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak.

"The crown," Elodie said. "Not the crown as ornament. The crown as *cage*. My choir bound something inside it, a lifetime ago—bound it into the gold itself, into the circlet's song, every note of ours hammered into the metal like nails. And it has held. It has held for a hundred years while the thing inside grew patient and the kings outside grew comfortable and forgot why the crown was heavy." Her hands curled slowly closed. "But the last voices died, and the binding runs on memory, and memories fade. The cage is failing, Mirabel. It has been failing for years. And in—" she closed her eyes, hearing the number the way you hear a bell, "—thirteen turns of the moon, it will fail completely. Unless it is unbound properly. Released, not loosed. Sung free, not shattered. Because if it is merely broken open—"

"—then whatever's inside comes out hungry," Mirabel finished. His voice was barely above a whisper.

"You see why I came to the ledger keepers."

"I see why you came to *me*." He stood abruptly, pacing the little room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a clerk's cage of a walk. "Because the book opened to tonight. To my name. Because—" He stopped pacing. He stared at her with an expression she recognized with a jolt, because she wore it herself most mornings: the expression of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish. "The prophecy names me. Doesn't it. In your book, in the dates, in the—whatever it is that's been writing itself for a hundred years. It names me as the one who unbinds him."

"It names you," Elodie said. "Yes."

"And you walked three hundred miles on the strength of a singing book, to the door of a dying guild, to put your last hope in the hands of an apprentice with ink on his fingers and no army, no magic worth the name, and—" his laugh cracked in the middle, "—and two colleagues, one of whom is asleep most of the day."

"Yes," Elodie said.

"Why?"

The word hung in the candlelight. The flames leaned toward her, waiting for the answer too.

"Because you're the last keeper," she said, "and I'm the last voice, and I thought—" She faltered. She had rehearsed a dozen speeches on the road, all of them strong, all of them certain, and none of them survived the actual young man in front of her, with his sleepless eyes and his trembling hands and his terrible lonely competence. What came out instead was the truth underneath all the rehearsals. "I thought maybe the last of one thing and the last of another might recognize each other. Might be less last, together. That's all. That's the whole of my grand strategy. I knocked twice because I didn't know what else to do, and the book said the door would answer, and I was so tired of being the only—"

She stopped. Closed her mouth. Looked down at her hands.

The silence stretched. Then she heard him move—not away, but around the table, and a moment later a ledger thumped down onto the wood in front of her: an old one, fat and cracked-spined, its cover stamped with the open-winged books of the guild's arms.

"If the book wrote my name," Mirabel said, "then it wrote what I'm supposed to do. And if it wrote what I'm supposed to do, then it wrote *how*." He opened the cover with hands that were no longer quite trembling. "The Choir of Binding had nine voices. How many notes does the unbinding need?"

"Five," Elodie said. "Five voices, five notes. Four are scattered—the choir broke apart when it broke, each singer carrying their note away like a coal from a dying fire. They'll be hard to find. Harder to convince. And the fifth—" She hesitated.

"The fifth what?"

"The fifth part isn't sung by a member of the choir at all. In the old notation it's marked for a voice that didn't exist yet when the song was written. The part is labeled—" she sang the old words under her breath, translating, "—*'the keeper of the book.'*"

Mirabel stared at her. Outside, somewhere in the sleeping hall, a clock that had not run in years cleared its throat and struck the hour—one clean, impossible chime rolling through the corridors like a stone dropped down a well.

Neither of them moved.

"Well," Mirabel said at last, very quietly, closing the ledger with a soft decisive sound. "I suppose we'd better find the other four before the moon finishes counting."

And down the corridor, in the dark of the sealed archive, the prophecy book turned its own page—eagerly, the way a reader turns a page when the story finally begins.
