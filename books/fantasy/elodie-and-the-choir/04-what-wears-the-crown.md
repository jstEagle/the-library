## 4. What Wears the Crown

The ledgers did not like to be read at night.

Mirabel had learned this in his first year, when he was still small enough to sleep in the alcove behind the catalog shelves and foolish enough to try. The older volumes grew restless after midnight, their pages warming under his fingers like the flanks of sleeping animals, their ink shifting when he wasn't looking—settling, rearranging, the way a dreamer shifts beneath blankets. Corvane said it was nothing. Corvane said most things were nothing, in the flat voice of a man who had stopped believing it decades ago and could no longer afford to say otherwise.

But tonight Mirabel wanted the ledgers awake.

He sat at the long reading table with a single candle and the third ledger of the reign of Aldric's grandfather, open to the entry he had found an hour past and not yet dared to touch. It was written in the old hand—the true old hand, the one from before the guild began to forget its own grammar—and it read:

*One crown, worn by two. Entry to be kept open for observation.*

An open entry. Mirabel had catalogued four thousand trades and never once seen an open entry. Every contract in the guild's keeping was a closed thing: terms met, price paid, the ink dried hard as scar tissue. An open entry meant the trade was still happening. Still *ongoing*. Somewhere in the world, something was being exchanged even now, this very minute, while he sat with cold feet and a guttering candle.

He wet his thumb, as the old instructions in the margins prescribed—he had copied those instructions out three times before he understood what they were for—and pressed it to the entry's first line.

The hall exhaled.

It was not wind. Wind came down the chimney or through the broken pane above the eastern stacks; Mirabel knew every breath the building took. This was different. This was the building itself drawing air, the way a diver draws it, deep and deliberate, and then the candle flame stood up straight and tall and went white, and the table beneath his hands became the surface of dark water, and Mirabel fell—not downward but *inward*—through the page.

* * *

He was standing in a corridor of white stone so highly polished that the floor showed him his own reflection walking beside him, upside down, like a companion he hadn't invited. Torchlight ran along the walls in ribbons of gold. Somewhere ahead, music—a string quartet playing something courtly and correct, each note placed exactly where etiquette demanded, and yet the whole of it sounded wrong to him, the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes follow you. He realized after a moment why: there were no rests in the music. No silence between phrases. It had been going on, he understood without knowing how he understood, for years. Perhaps since the coronation. A song that never allowed itself to end, because of what might listen in the pauses.

*The palace,* he thought. *I'm in the palace.*

No. He corrected himself, gripping the discipline the guild had drilled into him: *I am looking at the palace.* His body sat forty miles away at a reading table, and if he let himself believe otherwise the entry would close around him like a book snapping shut on a finger. Scrying was not travel. Scrying was remembering a place that wasn't yours.

He walked—or seemed to walk, the reflection below him matching him step for step—toward the great doors at the corridor's end, where two guards stood at parade rest. They did not look at him. Of course they didn't. But as he passed between them he saw their faces up close, and stopped, and felt the first cold thread of real fear go through him.

They were breathing in time. Not merely together—in *time*, to the quartet, chests rising on the downbeat, falling on the bar. Their eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, and they were beautiful faces, healthy faces, faces that should have held boredom or pride or the private misery of men standing watch through a long night. Instead they held nothing at all. They were like the bottled echo in the guild's collection: the shape of a sound with the sound taken out.

*Hollow eyes,* Mirabel thought, and did not know where the phrase came from, only that it arrived fully formed and true, the way some entries arrived, demanding to be written down.

Beyond the doors, the throne room opened up like the inside of a bell.

He had seen engravings of it, of course. Every child in the province had. The vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of Aldric the First, the banners of nine duchies, the throne itself—a slab of mountain granite that predated the kingdom and would outlast it. But no engraving conveyed the *crowd*. Court was in full session, hundreds of them, nobles and ministers and petitioners arranged in the strict geometry of precedence, and every single one of them swayed. Gently. Barely perceptibly. Like wheat. Like kelp in a slow current. In time with the music that never rested.

And on the throne, wearing the iron-and-gold crown of the Aldric line, sat King Aldric the Third.

Mirabel had expected—what? A monster? He had spent the day since Elodie's arrival trying not to imagine it, and everything he imagined had been monstrous: a thing of shadow, a beast in a man's shape, something with too many teeth. What he saw instead was worse, because it was almost nothing at all.

The king was young—younger than Corvane, younger than the war stories suggested. He sat straight-backed and still amid all that swaying, hands resting on the arms of the granite throne, and he looked, at first glance, like a king at his duties: composed, dignified, present. Then Mirabel watched for ten seconds more and understood what he was seeing, and his stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

The king was being *moved*.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. A tilt of the head here, acknowledging a minister's report. A slow lift of one hand there, granting a petition. Each motion arrived a half-second after it should have—the delay of a puppet whose strings are long, whose puppeteer is far away, or distracted, or savoring. And between motions the king went utterly, perfectly still, in a way living bodies are not built to be still. Living bodies fidget. They breathe unevenly. They swallow, shift their weight, blink against dryness. This body did none of it. It waited, the way furniture waits.

Then the eyes flickered.

It happened twice while Mirabel watched. The first time, it was brief—a flicker, gold passing over the brown of the king's iris like light over water, there and gone, and in that instant the face changed. The stillness became *attention*. The emptiness became appetite. For half a heartbeat Mirabel looked into something ancient, patient, and starving, something that had been hungry for longer than the kingdom had existed, and his whole body flinched back though there was nothing in the vision that could see him.

The second flicker lasted longer. And this time—this was the part he would carry into his dreams, he knew it already, the way you know which stones will fall on you before they do—this time the king's mouth moved. Not with the puppet's small administrative gestures. With words, silent ones, shaped carefully, desperately, by lips that had very little strength left to spend:

*Run.*

Mirabel's breath caught in his real throat, forty miles away.

*Run,* the king's mouth said again, and his eyes—for just that moment, just that sliver of a moment—were entirely his own. Brown. Young. Terrified. A man looking out of his own face through bars, watching a stranger in the crowd of his stolen life, and begging.

Then the gold came back like a tide, and the face closed, and the king turned his head with that terrible half-second lag to receive the next petitioner, and Mirabel stood in the scrying-vision shaking, his hand pressed flat against his own chest as if to confirm it still answered to him.

* * *

He made himself stay. That was the discipline, and he clung to it the way a drowning man clings to rope: the guild does not look away, the guild records, the guild bears witness even when witness costs. He moved along the edge of the throne room, insubstantial as smoke, cataloguing.

The courtiers swayed. The guards breathed in bar-time. The quartet played without pause. And near the throne, closer than protocol should have permitted, stood a woman in dove-grey with a chain of office that Mirabel did not recognize from any heraldry he'd studied—which was all of it; heraldry had been his refuge as a child, a language of certainty in a house that had none left. She was the only person in the room who did not sway. She stood planted as a post, and her eyes were not hollow. Her eyes were busy.

She carried a covered dish. Silver, domed, the kind used to keep food warm between kitchen and table. There was no reason to bring such a thing to an audience. There was no smell of food coming from under the lid, either—there was a smell of something else, faint and mineral, like the air inside the quarry caves, like struck flint, like the moment before lightning.

At the changing of some invisible hour, she ascended the steps to the throne. The guards did not stop her. The king's puppet-body turned toward her with its lagging grace, and she lifted the lid, and what lay inside was—

Mirabel couldn't hold it in focus. The vision resisted him, the way a horse resists a bit, and he understood dimly that whatever was in the dish was paying for the resistance, spending something to stay unseen even here, even in a memory recorded a century ago. He caught only impressions: a warmth that was not heat. A color that was not color, somewhere between the gold of the king's flickering eyes and the red of raw meat. A sense of *plenty*. Of harvest. Of a granary full to bursting in a year of famine.

The woman in dove-grey tipped the dish toward the crown.

And the crown drank.

There was no other word. The iron and gold did not move, and yet Mirabel watched the offering leave the dish—not poured, not eaten, but drawn upward in a slow spiral, thin as breath in winter, and vanish into the circlet above the king's brow. The king's body shuddered once, all over, like a dog shaking off rain. The gold flickered bright behind his eyes, brighter than before, and stayed a moment longer, and when it receded the hunger in that ancient gaze was a shade less sharp. Fed. Temporarily fed.

The woman replaced the lid and descended the steps, and as she passed through her place among the courtiers, Mirabel saw her smile. It was a small smile, private, satisfied, the smile of a gardener inspecting a bed that had come in well. And she hummed—three notes, soft, under her breath, a little descending figure that made the hair rise on Mirabel's arms, because Elodie had hummed something like it in the guild hall the night before, and where Elodie's humming had made the candles lean toward her like flowers toward sun, this made the candle-flames of the vision bend *away*, flattening themselves as if in wind.

Someone at court was feeding it. Someone at court knew precisely what slept—or half-slept, or woke by degrees—in the crown, and brought it supper on a silver dish, and smiled about it.

The entry shuddered. Mirabel felt the vision beginning to close, the palace graying at its edges, the quartet winding down at last as the memory reached the end of what it had been written to hold. He fought to keep his eyes on the throne, on the king, on that trapped and pleading face—but the last thing he saw clearly was neither. It was the woman in dove-grey, pausing at the doors, glancing back at the throne with her busy, unhollow eyes—and then, impossibly, tilting her head as if she had heard a footstep that wasn't there, and looking directly at him. Through him. Into the century between them.

Her lips moved. He heard nothing. But he read the shapes of the words as cleanly as if they were inked on the page he sat above:

*The ledger boy sees.*

* * *

The reading table came back up to meet him. The candle had burned down two inches; outside the shuttered window, the moon had crossed a full handspan of sky. His thumb was still pressed to the entry, and the ink beneath it was moving—rearranging itself, settling, closing like a wound. Before his eyes, the final line of the old entry rewrote itself in the true old hand:

*Observation noted. The watcher has been observed.*

Mirabel snatched his hand back and sat very still for a long time, listening to his own heart hammer, to the settling creaks of the dying guild hall around him, to the ordinary honest silence of a building that contained nothing hungrier than mice.

*The ledger boy.* Not *the apprentice*. Not *the keeper*. The woman had known what he was—or what he would be—or what he was already becoming, a hundred years before he was born. Which meant the entry had never been a record of the past at all. It had been waiting for a reader. It had been bait on a hook, or a message in a bottle, or a trap, and he had wet his thumb and pressed it open like a child opening someone else's letter.

He thought of the king's mouth shaping *run*, and his chest ached with a pity so fierce it frightened him. A man alive inside his own body, watching his hands grant petitions, watching his voice praise ministers, watching a silver dish come up the steps week after week, year after year—and unable to do anything but beg strangers in visions to run. How long had he been shouting into the dark? How many readers had the entry had before Mirabel, and how many had looked away?

The guild does not look away.

He got up on unsteady legs, lit a second candle from the first, and pulled the current-year ledger toward him across the table. If someone at court was feeding the crown, the feeding would cost something. Everything cost something; that was the first law of the guild, the law beneath all the others. Thirty years of a farmer's life bought a border crossing. A bottled echo cost a village its memory of one dead woman's laugh. So what did a century of offerings buy, and who was paying, and where was the receipt?

He began, with hands that gradually steadied, to search.

He found the answer an hour before dawn, in a ledger only six years old, in handwriting he knew better than his own—had mixed up with his own, more than once, filing pages in the archive. A correspondence entry, logged and cross-referenced by the guildmaster's own disciplined hand. Payments received. Information rendered. And at the bottom of the page, in Corvane's careful script, the seal of the spymaster's office pressed into the wax like a black eye.

Mirabel sat back. The candle flames leaned, though there was no draft.

Down the hall, behind the guildmaster's door, he heard the floorboards shift—an old man waking early, or an old man who had never slept at all. Footsteps. Pausing, perhaps, outside the archive door. Listening.

Mirabel closed the ledger softly, and did not move.
