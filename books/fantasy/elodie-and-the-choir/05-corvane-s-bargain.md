## 5. Corvane's Bargain

Elodie had learned the guild hall the way one learns a song—by listening for the places where it went wrong.

The Ledgerkeepers' hall was a body, and like any body it breathed. It inhaled through the high clerestory windows at dawn, when light fell in dusty columns across the counting floor. It exhaled at dusk through the cellar grates, carrying the smell of old vellum and lamp oil and the faint copper tang of trades not yet settled. And beneath the breath there was a pulse—a slow, patient thrum that lived in the walls themselves, in the shelves of ledgers that held everything the world had ever traded away and could never buy back.

Elodie heard it because she was made of hearing. She did not know precisely what she was made of—that question sat in her chest like an unstruck bell—but she knew this: sound told her truths that sight concealed. She could hear a lie the way other people heard a cracked glass, a hairline fracture singing under pressure.

And Corvane, Guildmaster of the Ledgerkeepers, was singing with fractures.

She had noticed it first on her second night beneath his roof, when he thought himself alone in his study above the counting floor. A murmur through the floorboards—not words, but rhythm. The rhythm of recitation. Ledgerkeepers, Mirabel had explained, memorized their entries as well as writing them; ink could burn, but a mind trained for sixty years held its ledger even in fire. So Elodie had assumed the old man was simply doing what old men of his craft did, walking the corridors of his memory before sleep.

But memory does not pause. Memory does not wait for a reply.

Three nights she had listened, lying on the narrow apprentice cot Mirabel had given up for her, staring at the ceiling beams while the murmur rose and fell below her like tide against a hull. Three nights, and each time, after the recitation, came a silence shaped exactly like waiting. Then footsteps. Then the scrape of a window latch. Then nothing—no return of footsteps, no latch closing, until the small hours, when Corvane climbed back into bed and lay awake long enough for Elodie to count his heartbeats and find them too fast for a sleeping man.

Tonight she would see where the window led.

She waited until the hall's pulse slowed to its deepest register—the dead hour past midnight, when even impossible things drowse—and she rose without sound. This was another thing she knew about herself: she moved quietly, as though sound itself were reluctant to betray her. Perhaps, she thought wryly, it considered her kin.

The stairs were stone and she took them along the outer edge, where the treads were worn shallowest. Below, the counting floor spread out in moonlight, its hundred desks bare, its great brass scales hanging silent from the ceiling beam like sleeping birds. Corvane's study door stood ajar.

She should have felt guilt. She was, after all, a guest—more than a guest; a claimant, a stranger who had walked into this dying guild and turned its last apprentice's world inside out with talk of prophecies and kings and thirteenth moons. She owed these people courtesy if nothing else.

But she had been made, whatever else she was, to bind things. And something in this hall was coming loose.

Through the gap in the door she watched Corvane stand at his window. He was smaller by night than by day, the way powerful men often were when no one watched them—a stooped silhouette wrapped in a dressing gown the color of wet ash. In his hands he held a book, and even in the dark Elodie recognized the shape of it: a ledger, but not one of the common ones shelved below. This one was bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its corners rounded like river stones, its spine cracked in three places from being opened too often in too much haste.

He opened it to a marked page and began to read aloud—quietly, but Elodie's ears were not quiet ears.

"The entry of the ninth of Harvestmonth, in the year the western wells failed. Trade recorded: one bottled echo, sold to a merchant of Vell for the price of his shadow's obedience. Witnessed by Underkeeper Sera Iversen. Sealed under the third seal."

He closed the book. He stood very still. And then he said, to the empty room, in a voice scraped raw:

"It's enough. It has to be enough."

The window opened. He passed the ledger out into the dark—to hands Elodie could not see, though she heard them take it, gloved hands, careful hands, the hands of someone accustomed to receiving contraband. A voice followed, low and clipped, pitched for secrecy but not pitched enough, not for her.

"Half now," the voice said. "The rest when the boy is delivered."

"He isn't yours to deliver." Corvane's whisper cracked on the word *yours*. "You said—you swore the guild would be left standing. That was the bargain. The secrets for the guild. Nothing in our bargain says anything about the boy."

"Nothing in *our* bargain matters anymore, old man. There is only His bargain now. His Majesty requires the ledgers entire. Every seal broken, every entry surrendered. And the boy who reads them—he comes to the palace, or the palace comes for all of you. Choose which grief you'd rather carry."

A pause. In the pause Elodie heard Corvane's heart, and it sounded like a drum struck in a collapsing house.

"And the girl?" he asked. "The one who arrived tonight?"

"What of her?"

"She is—" His breath caught. "There are entries about her. Old ones. Sealed ones. If His Majesty learns what she is—"

"Then His Majesty will be very pleased indeed. Goodnight, Guildmaster."

The window shut. The latch clicked. And Corvane stood alone in his study with his empty hands, and Elodie, pressed to the wall in the dark corridor, understood at last what she had been hearing for three nights.

Not recitation. *Inventory.* He was reading the guild's secrets out loud, entry by entry, seal by seal, feeding them through a window into the dark—selling every impossible thing the Ledgerkeepers had ever protected, one whispered page at a time.

Buying the guild's life with its soul.

She did not stay to watch him weep. She heard him do it anyway—it was not a sound one could refuse to hear, once begun—and she climbed back up the stairs with the knowledge sitting in her chest like swallowed ice.

*The spymaster.* She didn't know the man's name yet, but she knew the shape of him now: the crown's hand, reaching through a window into the heart of the last guild that kept the world honest. And behind the spymaster, coiled and patient, the thing in the crown that wore King Aldric like a glove.

It wanted the ledgers. It wanted Mirabel.

And the man Mirabel loved like a grandfather was selling them both, one page at a time, and calling it salvation.

* * *

She found Mirabel at dawn in the scriptorium, which was less a room than a habit—a corner of the counting floor where four desks had been pushed together over the years and covered in such strata of paper, ink pots, candle stubs, and biscuit crumbs that they had fused into a single archaeological site. He was asleep face-down in an open ledger, one arm curled around it the way children curl around pillows, and the prophecy book lay beside him, still open to the page that bore tonight's date and his name, written a century before either of them drew breath.

For a moment she only looked at him. He was so young. That was the thing that kept catching her off guard—she had come to this hall expecting a keeper of impossible things and found instead a boy with ink on his knuckles and pillow-creases on his cheek, guarding a dying craft with nothing but stubbornness and a beautiful, terrible memory. When he read aloud, his voice did something to the air that she felt in the roots of her teeth. She had not told him that. She was not sure she knew how.

"Mirabel." She said it softly, and when that failed, she touched his shoulder.

He surfaced all at once, blinking, one cheek printed with the mirror-image of a ledger line. "Is it—is it morning? Did I miss the—" He saw her face and stopped. Whatever he saw there made him sit up slowly, the sleep falling away from him like a dropped cloak. "What happened?"

"We need to talk somewhere the walls can't hear."

"They're just walls."

"No," Elodie said, "they really aren't. But your study will do. Yours has fewer ears than most."

He dressed and led her down the corridor in baffled silence, and she waited until the door was shut and she had pressed her palm flat against the wood—listening, always listening—before she spoke.

"Last night I couldn't sleep. I heard Corvane in his study. I thought he was reciting entries—Ledgerkeepers do that, you told me, to keep the ledgers in their memories. But he wasn't keeping anything. He was giving it away." She made herself say it plainly. Plainness was a mercy, even when it cut. "There was someone at his window, Mirabel. Someone wearing gloves. And Corvane handed him a ledger—one of the sealed ones, the grey-bound ones from the restricted shelf—and read him entries, and the man paid him, and called him *old man*, and said the rest would come when *the boy is delivered*."

Mirabel had gone very still. Not pale—not yet. Still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"That's not—" He laughed, one short syllable with nothing in it. "Elodie. You've been here two days. Corvane has run this guild for forty years. He buried my parents. He taught me my letters out of a ledger of debts because it was the only book he could bear to open after—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "You heard voices through a floor. People hear what they're afraid of. You came here telling me the crown wants me dead; of course your ear would find treachery in every creak of this place."

"I heard the transaction," Elodie said. "I heard the gloves take the book. I heard him say *the boy is delivered*. Those weren't creaks, Mirabel. They were words, and I don't mishear words. Words are—" She caught herself before she said *what I am*, and folded the sentence into something safer. "They're the one thing I have never gotten wrong."

"Then you misheard *him*." Mirabel was pacing now, three steps to the window, three steps back, his hands moving as though conducting an argument he was winning. "Someone came to the window, yes—maybe. Maybe someone blackmailed him, threatened him—but Corvane wouldn't *sell*—"

"He already has. More than one night. I've heard him three times. He reads entries, he waits, the window opens, the book goes out, it comes back before dawn. He's been doing it for weeks—I'd wager my own unmade soul on it. The restricted shelf is thinner than it should be. Isn't it? You've noticed. You catalogued it last month and told me half the grey bindings were *out for restoration*."

That stopped him. She watched the doubt land, watched it find the hollow she had aimed for—the hollow where he had filed his own small uneasiness and refused to look at it. For a heartbeat his certainty wavered, and she thought: *now. Now he'll believe me.*

Then he shook his head, hard, like a man shaking off water.

"Restoration. Yes. Because the bindings *were* failing—because this building is older than the kingdom and half the seals decay if you look at them wrong. He sends them to a binder in Lowmarket. I've carried the parcels myself." His voice was rising, and beneath the rise she heard the other thing, the true thing: fear. "Elodie, listen to me. Corvane is the reason there *is* a guild to save. He's spent his whole life keeping impossible things out of the crown's hands. When the purges came—when they killed everyone who wouldn't bend—he hid the ledgers in the walls and lied to inquisitors with a straight face for six days while they burned the east wing. Six days. He doesn't break."

"People break," Elodie said quietly. "Everyone breaks. I think maybe I was made to know that better than anyone alive. The question is never whether a person will break, Mirabel. It's what they'll trade to keep from breaking all the way."

"And what would you know about—"

He bit it off. She watched him bite it off, watched the apology arrive in his eyes a full second before his mouth caught up. But the shape of the sentence had already reached her, and it landed in the tender place, the place where Brother Oren's revelation had not yet scarred over—the place that whispered *you are more instrument than person, you are the choir's leftover, you are a song wearing a woman's shape.*

"You'd know about breaking," he finished, softer. "I'm sorry. That was cruel and I didn't mean it."

"Yes, you meant some of it," Elodie said. "And I forgive it, because you're not angry at me. You're angry at what I'm telling you, and since you can't burn the message, you'd like to shoot the messenger. I understand. I've been the messenger before." She had not, actually—she had no memories before the guild door, before the knocking, before the chord that made the candles lean—but the sentence felt true, and truth was what mattered now. "So don't believe me. Believe the evidence. Go to his study. Look at the windowsill—there's a scuff on the inner sill, fresh, where a ledger corner has dragged across the paint a dozen times or more. Look at the restricted shelf and count what's missing. Ask him where the binder in Lowmarket lives—ask for the binder's *name*, Mirabel. Ask him why he's never once let you make that delivery to the door."

"You want me to interrogate the only family I have left," Mirabel said, "based on a midnight eavesdropping."

"I want you to ask one question," Elodie said. "One. And watch his hands when you ask it. Liars guard their faces, because they've practiced their faces. But hands—" she spread her own, palms up, and saw him look at them despite himself, "—hands remember what they've done."

Silence stretched between them. Outside the study window, dawn was doing its slow work on the rooftops, turning slate to pewter to pale gold, and somewhere below them the guild hall exhaled its morning breath, and the pulse in the walls beat on, steady, trusting, unaware—or perhaps, Elodie thought with a cold new thought, not unaware at all. Perhaps the hall had known longer than she had. Perhaps that was why the pulse had sounded wrong to her from the first night. Not sick. *Grieving.*

"Corvane sold the guild's secrets," she said, "to spare the guild. I believe that part is almost noble, Mirabel. I believe he lies awake telling himself it's noble. But the bargain has changed. The man at the window doesn't work for the spymaster anymore—or rather, the spymaster doesn't work for the king. He works for the thing *in* the king. And it doesn't want to spare anything. It wants the ledgers entire, and it wants you delivered, and every page Corvane sells brings both closer. Your guildmaster isn't buying the guild's life, Mirabel. He's paying for its funeral in installments, and he's too heartbroken to read the contract."

Mirabel stood at the window with the light climbing his face, and for a long time he said nothing at all. She let him have the silence. Truth needed room to settle, like sediment in wine.

"If you're wrong," he said finally, and his voice was very quiet, "and I go to him with accusations—if I look at that old man and see a traitor because a stranger I've known two days heard ghosts through a floor—then I'll have broken the last good thing in this hall myself. Do you understand that? There's no coming back from it. Either way, I lose him. Either way, the guild loses him."

"Yes," Elodie said. "I know. I'm sorry. That's what truth does—it makes you pay before you've agreed to the trade." She stepped closer, close enough that he had to meet her eyes. "But there's a difference between losing him to a hard question and losing him to the crown's dungeon. One grief you choose. The other chooses you."

Something moved behind his eyes—she heard it, actually heard it, a small discordant note, the sound of a belief cracking along a seam. He turned toward the door. Stopped. Turned back.

"The binder in Lowmarket," he said slowly. "There isn't one. I've carried the parcels, yes—but I've never delivered them anywhere. I leave them at a drop-house on Tanner's Row and I've never once asked why, because he's my guildmaster and he asked, and that used to be enough." His laugh came out bent in the middle. "Gods. It's been under my nose for years and I called it trust."

"Mirabel—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, and it was not steady. "Don't say *I told you so*. I can't afford it yet. I need—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye, hard, as though he could hold his whole world in place by pressure alone. "I need to go down there and ask him. Right now. Before I lose my nerve, before I talk myself back into sleep. Will you come with me? Or—" he lowered his hand, and his eyes were wet and furious and younger than ever, "—or is this the part where you tell me you'd rather I faced it alone?"

"I came here to warn you," Elodie said. "I'm not going anywhere. But Mirabel—when you ask him, and when he answers, and when the answer is the one we both know is coming—remember that he broke because he loved something. Broken things that loved something can sometimes be mended. I intend to find out if I'm one of them."

He looked at her then, really looked, and nodded once.

Together they went down the stairs, past the sleeping counting floor, past the brass scales hanging silent as hanged men, toward the study at the end of the corridor where an old man sat—we could hear it, Elodie thought, we could both hear it now—with a grey-bound ledger open on his desk, and a window unlatched behind him, and the dawn coming up like a witness.

The door was ajar. Through the gap, Corvane's voice, low and ragged, mid-recitation:

"The entry of the third of Frostmonth. Trade recorded: one child's name, surrendered willingly, witnessed by—"

He stopped.

Because Mirabel had pushed the door open, and the light from the corridor fell across the desk, across the ledger, across the old man's face—and Elodie heard Corvane's heart stop for one full, terrible beat before it began again, faster, like something running.
