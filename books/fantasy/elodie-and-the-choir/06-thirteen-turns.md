## 6. Thirteen Turns

The prophecy book did not want to be read.

Mirabel had learned this in the three days since he'd smuggled it from Corvane's locked cabinet, wrapped in a dust cloth, hidden beneath the false bottom of his own writing desk. It was not that the book resisted him in any dramatic way—no snapping clasps, no pages that turned to smoke. It was subtler than that. The ink seemed to slide from comprehension, the way a word repeated too many times goes strange and meaningless on the tongue. He would read a line of it and feel the meaning drain out between one heartbeat and the next, like water through cupped hands.

So he had begun copying it. By hand. Letter by letter, in his own ink, on his own paper, forcing each character through the narrow gate of his attention before it could slip away.

It was slow work. It was also, Mirabel realized somewhere in the small hours of the third night, exactly what a Ledgerkeeper was trained to do: to hold an impossible thing steady by the act of recording it. The guild's oldest maxim ran *what is written cannot lie, though it may hide*. The prophecy was hiding. His transcription was the act of refusing to let it.

The hall around him was silent in the way only a dying building can be silent—not peaceful, but attentive, like an old dog listening for footsteps that no longer come. Three of them remained now. Corvane, who had not left his study in two days and whose voice through the door had gone flat and careful in a way Mirabel did not like. Old Hesk, who mostly slept. And Mirabel himself, youngest apprentice of a craft that had once filled every desk in this hall with scribes whose pens scratched like rain on a roof.

He missed that sound. He had never even heard it, and he missed it.

* * *

The arithmetic, when he finally cracked it, came not from the prophecy itself but from the margins.

Whoever had transcribed the original—a hand Mirabel now suspected belonged to the last Keeper of the previous century, a woman named Ilsa Vane whose name appeared in the guild's death-rolls dated ninety-nine years past—had been an accountant at heart. In the margins beside the verses about the moon, she had recorded dates. Small, neat notations: observations of the moon's phase on particular nights, matched against lines of the text.

Mirabel laid her notes beside his transcription and began, very quietly, to do what he did best.

The prophecy spoke of *thirteen turns*. Not thirteen months—the word used was older, *wends*, which the guild lexicon glossed as *the moon's full walking of its road*, one complete circuit from dark to dark. Thirteen circuits. And Ilsa Vane's marginalia established when the counting began: the first turn had opened on the night the binding first *creaked*, as the text put it, which Vane had marked with a date and a single underlined word—*audible*.

Mirabel cross-referenced that date against the guild's own weather ledgers, kept faithfully for two hundred years because, as the founding charter insisted, *even the sky keeps accounts*.

The night the binding first creaked had been ninety-one days ago.

His pen stopped moving.

Ninety-one days. A lunar turn ran twenty-eight days, twenty-eight nights, and a sliver besides. Thirteen turns was three hundred sixty-four days, give or take the sliver. Which meant—

He counted again. Then a third time, because a Ledgerkeeper never trusts a number until it has survived being checked by its own author twice.

Eighty-two days remaining. Two turns and change. Less than half the allotted time, already spent—no, worse than spent, *squandered*, because no one had known to count, because the prophecy had sat locked in a cabinet while the moon walked its road unwatched, month after month after month, and the thing in King Aldric's crown had been pulling at its cage with all the patience of something that had waited a century and could taste the lock rusting through.

Two turns. One waning, one waxing, and then the thirteenth moon would rise full over whatever remained of the kingdom.

Mirabel became aware, distantly, that his hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a kind of clinical interest, the way he might watch someone else's hands, and then he pressed them flat against the desk until they stilled.

*Fear is a debt,* his mother had told him once—he could not have been more than six; the memory arrived whole and smelling of tallow candles—*and debts are meant to be paid, not carried. Pay it and move on.*

He paid it. He let the fear move through him fully, coldly, honestly: the image of the thirteenth moon rising over a court of glassy-eyed courtiers, over a king who was no longer home inside his own body, over a kingdom that had forgotten how to want anything. He let himself feel how small he was—one apprentice, three voices if Elodie's choir-gathering went as hoped, against a hunger old enough to remember being worshipped.

Then he closed the account on that fear, drew the line beneath it in the manner of his craft, and turned the page.

* * *

Dawn found him in the Hall of Custody.

No one called it that anymore. It was simply the back room, the one with the iron-bound chests and the smell of cedar and cold iron, where the guild's true ledgers slept—the master volumes, the ones that predated the current charter, the ones Corvane had not permitted anyone to open since Mirabel's induction. There were eleven of them, arranged in chronological order on shelves cut directly into the stone, their spines labeled in a script so old it predated the kingdom's current alphabet.

Mirabel stood before them for a long moment, his breath fogging in the room's permanent chill.

There was a form for this. Of course there was a form for this; the guild had a form for everything, and the transfer of custody was among the most solemn. He had memorized it as a boy along with the catechism of entries and the seven ways to witness a signature. But the form required a Master of the Guild to preside, or failing that, a quorum of three members in good standing.

They were three. Whether any of them stood in good standing was another question entirely.

He went first to Hesk, who woke slowly, blinking at the daylight with the confusion of a man surfacing from deep water. When Mirabel explained what he intended, the old man was quiet for a long time.

"You're not asking my permission," Hesk said at last. It was not quite an accusation.

"No," Mirabel agreed. "I'm informing you. The form requires witnesses, and you're the only member of this guild who hasn't lied to me."

Something flickered across Hesk's face—hurt, maybe, or the recognition of a truth he'd been avoiding—and then, unexpectedly, he smiled. It transformed him, that smile, took twenty years off him and put a mischievous light in eyes gone milky at the edges.

"Corvane will rage."

"Corvane ordered me to burn the prophecy." Mirabel heard his own voice come out level, and was faintly surprised by it. "I've decided I'm done taking orders I can't respect."

Hesk pushed back his blankets and reached for his boots. "Then let's do this properly," he said. "I haven't witnessed a custody transfer since before you were born. I'd rather like to do it once more before I'm done."

* * *

Corvane they did not ask. They informed him too—or rather, they informed his closed door, because he did not answer Mirabel's knock, and when Mirabel announced through the wood what was about to occur, there was a long silence and then, muffled, in that flat careful voice: *"Do what you think you must."*

Not *don't*. Not *you're not ready*. Not even anger.

Mirabel stood a moment longer outside that door, waiting for some feeling to resolve in his chest—anger, grief, the ache of a boy whose teacher had become a stranger—and finding instead only a kind of hollow clarity. Whatever Corvane was carrying behind that door, whatever bargain he had struck or was striking, it had made him into a man who would say *do what you think you must* to the transfer of everything the guild had ever been.

Later, Mirabel would understand that sentence better. Later it would curdle and clarify. But standing there in the grey morning light, he filed it away as Ledgerkeepers file everything: accurately, without yet knowing what the entry means.

* * *

The ceremony itself took eleven minutes. Mirabel timed it, because ritual is made bearable by precision.

Hesk stood at his right hand as witness. For the second witness, to Mirabel's own surprise, he had asked Elodie—who had arrived the evening before, quiet as falling snow, and taken up residence in the guest cell without anyone offering it to her, as though the building had simply decided she belonged.

She stood now at his left, hands folded, watching him with those eyes that seemed always to be hearing something just beneath the world's surface.

"The form is simple," Mirabel said, and his voice echoed strangely in the cold room, as though the shelves themselves were leaning in. "The custodian places his hand upon the master ledger and speaks the entry aloud. From that moment, the guild's debts are his debts. Its contracts are his contracts. Its—" he hesitated "—its promises are his to keep or break, and either choice is written against his name forever."

"That's a heavy clause," Elodie said softly.

"It's meant to be. That's why no one's spoken it in forty years." He looked down at the first volume, *Foundations*, its cover scarred with candle burns and something that might have been a thumbprint set into the leather like a fossil. "Everyone always assumed there'd be time to train a proper successor. A dozen apprentices, years of preparation. Instead there's me."

"There's you," Elodie said, and there was something in the way she said it—not comfort, not flattery, but simple statement, the way she might name a note in a chord—that steadied him more than either would have.

Mirabel placed his palm flat on the cover. The leather was cold, then less cold, then—this he would swear to for the rest of his life, though no one ever believed him—warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, as though something beneath the record of two centuries had felt his hand and leaned into it.

"I, Mirabel Iversen," he said, and the old words rose in him complete, learned at nine years old and never once needed until now, "being of sound mind and full knowledge of the weight involved, do take into my custody the ledgers of the Guild of Ledgerkeepers, with all debts outstanding, all contracts binding, all entries open and closed, and all obligations unfulfilled. Let the record show that I claim them not as inheritance but as duty. Let the record show that I intend to honor them. Let the record show—"

He paused. The next line of the formula was *let the record show that my predecessors approved this transfer*, and his predecessors had done nothing of the kind; his predecessor was behind a closed door, saying *do what you think you must*.

Elodie touched his elbow. "Change the line," she murmured. "You're a Ledgerkeeper. Amend the entry."

Mirabel exhaled. And then, feeling the strangeness and rightness of it all at once, he spoke words that had never been spoken in that room:

"Let the record show that my predecessor abdicated by silence, and that I accept custody *despite* him, not *through* him. Let the record show that the guild's last apprentice is now its keeper entire. So entered, so witnessed, so bound."

The warmth beneath his palm pulsed once, like a heartbeat answering.

On the shelf, all eleven master ledgers shifted—imperceptibly, a settling of spines, a sigh of old paper—and Mirabel felt the weight of them settle onto him in earnest. Not crushing. Present. Like a yoke fitted by a patient carpenter, sized exactly to his shoulders, leaving him room to grow into it.

Hesk cleared his throat. "Well," he said, gruffly, blinking harder than the room's chill could account for. "That's that, then. First act of leadership I've seen in this hall in thirty years, and it took the youngest of us to manage it."

"It wasn't leadership," Mirabel said. "It was bookkeeping."

"Boy," Hesk said, "in this guild, those were always the same thing."

* * *

They gathered in the reading room afterward—Mirabel, Elodie, Hesk propped in the good chair with a blanket over his knees—and Mirabel laid out the arithmetic the way he laid out everything: plainly, in order, with sources cited.

"Thirteen turns from the first creak of the binding. Ninety-one days have passed. Eighty-two remain—two lunar turns and roughly three weeks. The prophecy names five voices needed for the unbinding, and we currently have two." He glanced at Elodie. "Perhaps two and a promise."

"The choir scattered when the binding was made," Elodie said. "Its voices went into hiding, or into exile, or into ordinary lives that forgot they'd ever been extraordinary. But a note doesn't die because the song stops. It waits. I can find them—if we're fast, and if the crown doesn't find us first."

"The crown has agents," Mirabel said. "And the crown has a spymaster, and the spymaster has been buying secrets from someone with access to guild knowledge. I don't know who. Yet." He let that sit in the room, unanswered, and saw Hesk's face go carefully still. "Which means our first moves must be quiet ones. We gather the notes. We stay ahead of the agents. And we learn everything the ledgers know about the binding, the chapel where it was sung, and the thing in the crown—because eighty-two days is not enough time to do anything clumsily."

"And if we fail?" Hesk asked. Not cruelly. The way a man asks about weather he can't control.

Mirabel looked down at his hands—steady now, he noticed, steady as they'd been since the moment the ledger warmed beneath his palm—and thought about the difference between fear and weight. Fear wanted him frozen. Weight only wanted him strong enough.

"If we fail," he said, "then the record will show that we tried, and *how* we tried, and that will have to be enough. But I don't intend to fail. I intend to keep excellent books about our success."

Elodie laughed—a real laugh, brief and bright, the first he'd heard from her—and the sound moved through the dusty reading room like a draft of spring air through a house shut for winter.

Outside the high windows, the morning was sharpening toward noon. Somewhere beyond the city walls, beyond the border wards and the flooded quarries and the mountain passes, four voices waited in their scattered lives, not knowing they were about to be summoned. Somewhere in a palace, a king who was not alone in his own skull turned his head at some whisper only he could hear. And somewhere behind a closed door, a guildmaster sat with a bargain he had not yet confessed to, counting his own private arithmetic of grief.

Eighty-two days.

Mirabel opened the master ledger to a fresh page, dipped his pen, and wrote the first entry of his custody in a hand that did not shake:

*Day 1 of 82. Custody assumed. Voices gathered: two. Debts inherited: all of them. Intention: to make the impossible possible, beginning immediately.*

He blotted the page, and was reaching to close the book when the ink stirred.

A second line was blooming up through the paper from nowhere—from the depths of the ledger itself, from whatever patient intelligence lived in two hundred years of recorded trades—curling into letters beneath his own:

*Day 1 of 82. Received: one keeper, willing. Entered in response: a summons. The first note hears you already. Go to the water where the singing drowned.*

Mirabel stared at it, heart hammering, and then looked up at Elodie, who had gone very still, her head tilted as though toward a sound only she could hear.

"The flooded quarry town," she said quietly. "Someone there is singing in their sleep."
