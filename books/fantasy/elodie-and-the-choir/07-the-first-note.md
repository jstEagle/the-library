## 7. The First Note

### Mirabel

The town of Greywash announced itself by smell before sight: wet stone and river silt and the green rot of things drowned too long. Then the road crested a hill of slag, and there it lay below—a town built in the bowl of an old quarry, its lower streets swallowed by water so still it looked like poured glass. Houses climbed the quarry walls in terraces, each row abandoned by the flood below and rebuilt above, so that the whole settlement resembled a tide mark frozen mid-retreat. Chimneys rose from the water itself, breathing nothing.

"Charming," Elodie said. Her voice carried strangely in the hollow, and Mirabel noticed that the puddles on the road shivered when she spoke. He was noticing that more and more. The world leaned toward her the way flowers lean toward light.

"How deep is it?" he asked, though the ledger already knew. He had read the entry three times on the road from the guild hall, by candlelight, while Elodie slept sitting up against the cart's rail: *Greywash Quarry, flooded in the year of the Long Rain. One voice remains below the waterline. The stone remembers her.*

"The old workings go down past the third terrace," Elodie said. "The choir sang here once. Before I—" She stopped, as she always stopped, at the edge of her own missing history. "Before."

They left the cart with a woman who kept goats on the upper rim and asked no questions, which Mirabel suspected meant she'd been paid to ask none. The descent into Greywash was a walk through drowned time. Doors along the second terrace were nailed shut with boards gone silver. On the first terrace, the water lapped at lintels, and fish moved through what had once been parlors, silver flickers behind windows furred with algae.

"She lives below," Elodie said quietly. "The one we're looking for. Sable Vane. She didn't leave when the flood came."

"Why not?"

"Because the flood came *for* her." Elodie's eyes were distant, fixed on the water. "Or she let it come for her. The choir's notes are strange things, Mirabel. Hers could crack stone. When you carry a gift like that, and the world has already decided what you are because of it..." She shook her head. "Some people stop running and simply sink."

Mirabel thought of the ledger under his arm, heavy as a sleeping child. He thought of the page he'd found, the one that had led them here—a trade recorded ninety years ago, in his predecessor's careful hand:

*Bought and held: the history of Sable Vane, soprano, in full. Seller: the impresario Orlan Fitch, who purchased her silence entire. Price paid: a theater that never burns.*

A theater that never burns. The guild had honored that contract for nine decades; somewhere, presumably, Fitch's theater still stood, fireproof as a tomb. And Sable Vane had walked out of the world with nothing—not even her own memory of who she had been. That was how the trades worked, the old ones. You couldn't buy a person's talent. But you could buy their history, and a person without a history soon forgot they'd ever had a voice at all.

"She'll hate us," Mirabel said. "When I read it to her. She'll hate knowing what was taken."

"Yes," Elodie said. "And then she'll hate it more that we're the only ones who ever bothered to bring it back." She smiled, and it was a sad, sharp smile. "Hate is just grief wearing armor. We can work with grief."

The way down was a stair cut into the quarry wall, slick with moss, descending past the last inhabited terrace into the drowned streets. Someone had strung oil lanterns along the route—old ones, black with soot, but maintained. Recent footprints marked the silt on the steps. Someone went down regularly. Someone lived where the water had won.

At the bottom, the street became a canal. Buildings stood waist-deep, their upper floors tilted like capsized boats. And at the end of the street, in what must have been the quarry's old counting house—stone-built, stubborn, its roofline just clearing the water—candlelight glowed through a window crusted with salt.

Elodie knocked. Once. Twice.

The door opened before the echo faded.

The woman standing there was perhaps sixty, though it was hard to say—her face had the weathered quality of driftwood, all grain and gray. Her hair was cropped short and iron-colored. She wore a man's coat over a singer's posture, spine straight as a plumb line, and her eyes went from Elodie to Mirabel to the ledger under his arm with the speed of someone who had spent decades learning exactly who knocked on doors like this.

"No," she said, and began to close the door.

"We haven't said anything yet," Elodie said.

"You don't have to. I can hear it." Sable Vane's gaze dropped to Mirabel's throat. "You're carrying something in your voice, girl. A chord that isn't yours alone. And the boy is carrying paper. Between the two of you, that's a sermon, and I stopped attending sermons when the water reached my knees."

"Please," Mirabel said. "Five minutes. And then if you want us gone, we're gone."

Sable's jaw worked. Water dripped somewhere in the dark behind her, patient as a clock. "Five minutes," she said, "because it's been eleven years since anyone stood on my step who wasn't a fish or a ghost." She stepped back. "Wipe your feet. The floor's older than your guild."

### Elodie

Inside, the counting house smelled of lamp oil and river and, beneath it, faintly, of rosin—the scent of bowstrings and stageboards that clung to some people long after the stages were gone. Elodie knew that smell intimately. It was, insofar as she could remember anything, the closest thing she had to a childhood.

Sable lit candles with hands that did not tremble, which told Elodie she had performed through worse than nerves. The room was spare but ordered: a cot, a stove, a table, and shelves lined with jars of preserved eel and river greens. On the wall hung a single framed playbill, blank—truly blank, bleached white, every word washed out. Elodie stared at it and understood.

"That's the night," she said. "The night they took it."

Sable's shoulders stiffened. "You know a lot for a stranger."

"I know what erased paper looks like. I've seen it before." In mirrors, in the empty rooms of herself. She sat across the table from the older woman and folded her hands. "I'm the last voice of the Choir of Binding. Or I was. Now I'm the choir, what's left of it, walking around in one body and trying to remember why."

Sable laughed—one short, harsh note—and the candle flames all bent sideways at once, toward her, like courtiers bowing. She didn't seem to notice. "The Choir of Binding. They taught us about you at the conservatory. Bedtime stories for sopranos. *Sing well enough and maybe the kings will need caging someday.*" She leaned forward. "There is no choir. There hasn't been for a hundred years. Whatever you are, you're alone, same as me."

"Not anymore," Elodie said. "That's rather the point."

Mirabel set the ledger on the table. His hands were steady, but Elodie saw the pulse jumping in his throat—he was afraid, properly afraid, and doing it anyway. She had watched him do that four times since the guild hall. She was beginning to suspect it was the truest thing about him.

"Sable Vane," Mirabel said. "Born in Vellmark, the daughter of a ferryman and a net-mender. You sang before you could talk—your mother wrote to her sister that you hummed harmonies with the river fog. At nine you were singing in the harbor taverns. At twelve, a traveling impresario named Orlan Fitch heard you and bought out your parents' debts. At fifteen you debuted at the Amberlight Opera House in Corvane's city, and the critics said—" he paused, reading the faded ink, and his voice changed, softened, became almost reverent— "*a voice like a bell rung inside a cathedral; when she holds a high note, the chandeliers ring in sympathy.*"

"Stop." Sable's word cracked like a whipcrack. One of the shelf jars buzzed against its neighbor.

"He hasn't even gotten to the part that matters," Elodie said gently.

"There is no part that matters. There's a flood and a blank frame on my wall and sixty years of remembering nothing before the water." But Sable hadn't moved to stop him again. Her hands were flat on the table now, pressing down as if the room might lift off its foundations.

"At twenty-three," Mirabel continued, quieter, "you sang the role of the Sea-Bride in Fitch's own production. On opening night, during the final aria, you held a note longer than any singer on record—and the load-bearing arch over the stage split clean through. Eleven people hurt. No one killed. Fitch didn't care about the people. He cared that his prima donna was a liability, and more than that—" Mirabel's jaw tightened— "he saw profit. He came to the Ledgerkeepers and made a trade. Your history, entire, sold to the guild's keeping. Every memory of your life up to that night. In exchange, the guild bound a contract: his theater would never burn. Not ever. Not by accident, not by arson, not by act of god."

The silence after was total. Even the dripping somewhere in the walls seemed to hold its breath.

"I don't remember any of that," Sable said at last. Her voice had gone very small, and Elodie thought: *there it is. The girl under the driftwood.* "I woke up in a charity ward with no name I could prove and a head full of fog. They called me Sable because of my coat. I assumed—I decided—that I'd done something shameful. Run from debts. Left someone. Why else would a woman arrive in the world with nothing?" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Sixty years I decided that about myself."

"You didn't run," Mirabel said. "You were *sold*. And the guild kept the purchase." He turned the ledger around so she could see the entry, the old ink, the seal. "Guild law is clear on one point, and my master never let me forget it: everything held in trust belongs, in the end, to the one it was taken from. I can't give you back the years. But I can give you back the record." He took a breath. "If you want it. It will hurt. Being remembered always does."

Sable stared at the ledger for a long moment. Outside, the water lapped the stones, and far overhead, through the quarry's throat, a slice of evening sky was turning the color of a bruise healing.

"Read it," she said finally. "All of it. Don't skip the bad reviews."

So Mirabel read.

He read for an hour while the candles burned down and the river-dark deepened outside the salt-crusted windows. He read her mother's letters, full of ferry schedules and pride. He read her debut, and the review that called her *insufferably young and insufferably right.* He read the aria, the split arch, the screams, the trial before the city magistrates where Fitch testified with a straight face that the girl was unstable, ungrateful, finished. He read the trade contract, clause by clause, and when he reached the line where her signature should have been, he stopped.

"There's no signature," he said slowly. "Because a signature requires a self to sign. Fitch signed for both parties. It wasn't lawful. It was never lawful. The guild accepted it anyway because—" he hesitated, and Elodie watched him choose honesty over comfort— "because we were dying even then, and we took what we were offered. I'm sorry. On behalf of the guild, such as it is. I'm sorry."

Sable Vane put her face in her hands.

She didn't sob. What happened was stranger and more terrible: she hummed. One low note, barely voiced, the sound a kettle makes just before boiling—and the candles guttered, and the water in the street outside pressed back from the walls as if flinching, and a hairline crack ran up the counting house's stone doorframe with a sound like a knuckle popping. Elodie felt the note in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the old scar-tissue of her soul where the choir had been broken apart. She knew that note. She had *been* that note, once, in another arrangement of herself.

*Re,* she thought. The second degree. The foundation. The note that everything else stands on.

Sable lifted her head. Her face was wrecked and open, sixty years of wrong conclusions collapsing all at once, and underneath the ruin she was laughing—or crying, or both, it made no difference.

"My name," she said. "My real name. Say it again."

"Sable Vane," Mirabel said. "Daughter of Maren and Odell. Singer of the Amberlight. The chandeliers rang."

"The chandeliers rang," she repeated, wonderingly. Then her expression shifted—hardened, but differently than before, the armor reforged around something worth protecting instead of something merely hidden. "Fitch is dead?"

"Forty years ago. His theater still stands. Fireproof."

"Good. Let it stand forever, empty, as a monument to what a bargain costs." She stood, crossed to the blank playbill, and took it down from the wall. She looked at it for a long moment—then set it face-down on the table, like closing a coffin. "You said five minutes. You've spent an hour. So talk fast, ledger boy. Why does the last voice of a dead choir climb down into a drowned town looking for a disgraced soprano?"

Elodie answered, because it was hers to answer. "Because the cage is failing. Because the king is drowning the way your town drowned, street by street, and the thing wearing his crown will be loose within thirteen turns of the moon. Because the binding needs its notes back, and you—" she met Sable's eyes— "you are the lowest stone in the arch. Without you, nothing we build will stand."

"And if I refuse? If I stay here with my eels and my quiet?"

"Then we fail," Elodie said simply. "And everyone else learns what you learned in the charity ward: what it is to wake up in a world with no proof you were ever in it."

Sable looked at the blank playbill. At the ledger. At the crack she'd put in her own doorframe without meaning to—the first crack in sixty years that was *hers*, earned, honest.

"I'll need ten minutes to pack," she said. "And someone to feed the eels. Mrs. Harrow on the top rim, the goat woman—she'll do it for nothing, she's been trying to get me to leave this hole for a decade." She was already moving, pulling a canvas bag from under the cot, and her voice came back over her shoulder with an edge that was almost, almost a smile. "And ledger boy? When this is over—if we live—you're taking me to that fireproof theater. I want to stand on Fitch's stage and sing the arch down myself."

"It would be my honor," Mirabel said solemnly, and Elodie laughed despite everything, despite the moon turning overhead toward its thirteenth face.

They were climbing back up the quarry stairs, three abreast now, when the first horn sounded from the rim of the quarry above—two notes, brass-bright, repeated. Sable froze, her bag half-slung.

"Crown riders," she said flatly. "I know that call. They used it hunting smugglers, back when the quarry still shipped stone." She looked at Elodie, then at Mirabel, and something in her face settled into grim recognition, the look of a woman whose new memories had just become immediately useful. "They're not here for the eels. They're here for the boy with the book, aren't they."

Below them, in the drowned street, the water began—very slightly—to tremble.

Above, the horns sounded again. Closer.
