## 8. Crown Agents at the Door

Sable had spent eleven years learning to hear trouble before it arrived.

It was a skill the opera house had never taught her—there they taught breath and pitch and how to hold a high C until the chandeliers wept—but the quarry town of Greymill had its own conservatory, and its curriculum was survival. Trouble had a sound. It came up the flooded road in boots that didn't splash right, too many men walking in step, or it came as a silence where dogs should be barking. On the morning the crown agents came for the ledger boy, Sable heard neither.

She heard a bell.

Not the chapel bell, which rang flat and cracked on the third stroke. This was a handbell, small and silver-toned, carried by old Maren the fishwife, who walked her route along the quarry rim every dawn ringing it once at each door—not for alarm, but because Maren believed a town that stopped greeting itself was already dead. Eleven years in Greymill, and Sable had learned every bell's voice the way other people learned faces.

This morning, Maren's bell rang twice at each door.

Sable sat up in her cot above the drowned boathouse, heart already quickening, and listened to the pattern travel down the lane. Twice. Twice. Twice. A rhythm with no name in any song she knew—which meant it was new, which meant someone had paid Maren to invent it, which meant *run*.

She was dressed before the thought finished forming, pulling on the grey coat that made her one more drab body in a town of drab bodies, and she was down the ladder and across the reeking shallows before the first boot touched the quarry road.

The ledger boy was asleep in the loft of the cooper's shed, curled around Mirabel's satchel of ledgers like a child around a warm loaf. Elodie slept sitting upright against the wall, which told Sable something about her—that she did not trust sleep enough to lie down for it—and when Sable's knock came, two soft raps, then two more, Elodie's eyes opened instantly, fully awake, no fog of waking at all.

"Agents," Sable breathed through the gap in the door. "On the road. Grey cloaks, six of them, maybe eight. They'll sweep from the water side."

Mirabel sat bolt upright, hair wild, clutching the satchel to his chest like a shield. "How do you know? How could you possibly—"

"Because I've heard this song before." She kept her voice low and even, the way she'd been trained to project calm into the back rows of a darkened hall. "Boy, I ran from the crown before you were weaned. Now listen. There's a way out under the cooperage—the old drainage tunnels run back into the flooded quarries. The water's chest-deep in places and black as sin, and there are things living in it that bite, but nothing in that water wears a crown's badge."

"The ledgers can't get wet," Mirabel said, and his voice cracked with genuine anguish, as if the books were children.

"Then hold them above your head and pray your arms are stronger than your questions." Sable was already moving, hauling aside the cooper's stack of staves to reveal the trapdoor beneath—a slab of oak gone soft with damp, banded in iron that had rusted to the color of dried blood. "Elodie. Help me with this."

Together they heaved it open. The smell rose up to meet them: cold stone, stagnant water, and underneath it something older and stranger, a mineral sweetness like the inside of a struck bell. The tunnel exhaled, and the candle by the door leaned toward the darkness as if bowing.

"Down," Sable said. "Now. And whatever you hear behind us—and you will hear things behind us—you do not stop moving. Do you understand me, boy?"

"I'm not a boy," Mirabel said, but he was already climbing down the rungs, satchel held high over his head like a man wading a river with his whole inheritance in his hands.

"You're whoever I say you are until we're past the third junction," Sable said, and pushed Elodie gently toward the hole. "Go."

* * *

The water took them at the bottom of the ladder, cold as a slap, rising to Sable's ribs. She heard Mirabel gasp—a small, strangled sound, quickly swallowed—and felt a flicker of respect for him. He didn't cry out again. Whatever else the boy was, he wasn't made of paper, whatever his guild believed.

"Left," she whispered. "Hand on the wall. The wall will tell you everything if you let it speak."

They moved through the dark by touch and by the faint phosphorescence that clung to the flooded stone—a ghost-light, greenish, the kind of glow that lived only in places the sun had abandoned. Sable had mapped these tunnels in her first year in Greymill, swimming them by night when sleep wouldn't come, because a woman who could crack stone with her voice needed to know exactly which walls were thin and which were thick, which passages would carry sound and which would swallow it. She had learned the acoustics of the underworld the way monks learned scripture.

Behind them, muffled by water and stone, came the sound she'd been waiting for: the trapdoor above the cooperage splintering inward. Boots on the ladder rungs. A voice, clipped and official, echoing strangely down the flooded throat of the tunnel:

*"Search the waterline. The ledger boy is here somewhere. Alive, if possible."*

*If possible.* Sable's mouth went dry despite the water around her waist. That phrasing meant orders from very high up. That phrasing meant someone wanted what was in the boy's head more than they wanted the boy.

"Deeper," she murmured. "Faster. There's a junction ahead where the tunnel splits three ways. We take the middle."

"Why the middle?" Mirabel whispered, teeth chattering.

"Because the left passage floods to the ceiling at high water and the right one is watched—they cut viewing grates into it twenty years ago when they still cared about smugglers. The middle goes nowhere useful, so nobody watches it." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Nowhere useful is the safest place in the world, boy. Remember that."

They reached the junction, a cathedral of dripping stone where their footsteps multiplied into a congregation. And it was there, standing hip-deep in black water beneath a ceiling lost in green-dark, that Sable heard the thing that turned her blood to ice water colder than the flood around them.

Singing.

Far off, thin as wire, coming down the left-hand passage—from the direction of the town, from the direction of the agents—one clear voice singing a scale. Rising. Falling. Testing.

"They're sounding the tunnels," Elodie said quietly. Beside Sable in the gloom, her face was pale as the phosphorescence. "Using resonance to find hollow spaces. To find *us*. That's choir technique." Her voice caught on the word. "That's *our* technique. Someone taught them."

"Someone teaches the crown everything eventually," Sable said grimly. "Money buys voices the way it buys everything else." But she was listening harder than she let show, because the singer was good—unnervingly good—and because the melody they were testing was not random. It was a search-pattern. An old one. One Sable had last heard sung in a marble courtroom eleven years ago, by a woman in grey robes who had pointed one white finger at Sable across the dock and said, *There. That is the voice that broke the amphitheater.*

"They're not just searching," Sable said slowly. "They're listening for a voice that answers theirs. Resonance finds resonance." She looked at Mirabel. "If either of us makes a sound in these tunnels, that singer will find the harmonic and follow it straight to us. So we go silent. Completely silent. No whispers. Hands only."

She pressed Mirabel's shoulder and pointed: forward, along the middle passage, following the wall. Then she began to move, and the three of them became three shadows wading through the belly of the earth, touching stone, feeling for the turnings she remembered—the low shelf where the ceiling dipped, the fallen pillar they'd have to climb over, the long stretch where the water deepened and they'd have to swim with the ledgers held aloft, Mirabel's arms shaking, his face a mask of desperate concentration in the ghost-light.

It was almost working. It was working beautifully, right up until Wren's whistle found them.

* * *

Later, Sable would learn how the child had done it—how a nine-year-old pickpocket had tracked three adults through a flooded labyrinth by listening at drain-grates, by reading the town's fear like sheet music—but in the moment, all Sable knew was that a sound came drifting down the middle passage from somewhere impossibly far ahead: four notes, high and sweet and looping, unmistakably deliberate. *Follow. Follow. Follow.*

Mirabel froze. Even in the dark, Sable could feel him straining toward the sound, wanting to answer it, and she clamped a hand over his mouth before the call could rise in his throat.

"No," she breathed against his ear. "Whatever that is, it isn't for us. Nothing in this town whistles like that except—" She stopped. Thought. The child. The thief girl with the sharp eyes who haunted the fish market, the one whose whistling could still the wind itself, the one Elodie had spoken of with that particular reverence she reserved for things that mattered. "Except her. The third note."

"She found us," Mirabel whispered against Sable's palm.

"She's leading us. There's a difference, and the difference is that she knows these tunnels better than the rats do." Sable lowered her hand. "We follow the whistle. Quietly."

They followed. Through a crawl-passage where the ceiling pressed down like a closing hand, through a chamber where the water fell away beneath them into some fathomless drowned shaft—Wren's whistle threading ahead of them always, patient, unhurried, a silver thread through the black—and it was in that chamber, with the abyss breathing cold against their legs, that the pursuit finally caught their scent.

The singer's voice changed.

No longer a testing scale. Now it was a sustained tone, low and grinding, pitched—Sable recognized it with a lurch—to the exact frequency of the middle passage's walls. The stone began to hum. Loose grit rattled down from the ceiling and pattered into the water around them like the first rain of a storm.

"She's trying to bring the roof down on our heads," Sable said. "Or seal the passage. Either way, we're about to lose our road." She looked back the way they'd come, at the long throat of tunnel between them and the singer, at the walls she knew the way she knew her own throat—every fissure, every fault line, every place where fifty years of quarry blasting had left the stone tired and ready to sleep.

And Sable made the decision she had sworn, eleven years ago, never to make again.

"Get behind me," she said. "Both of you. As far back as this chamber allows. Cover your ears. Open your mouths—yes, really, it equalizes the pressure—and whatever happens, whatever you see, do not try to stop me."

"What are you going to do?" Mirabel asked, and there was terror in his voice, and beneath the terror something worse: awe, arriving early, like an audience finding their seats before the overture.

"What I was made for," Sable said. "What they took my name for. Hold the ledgers high, boy. This next part gets loud."

She planted her feet on the submerged ledge, filled her lungs the way the opera masters had taught her—down into the belly, into the floor of herself—and she sang.

Not a note. Not a melody. She sang the *name of the stone*, the deep grinding syllable that lived at the bottom of every voice, the sound a mountain makes in the last moment before it decides to fall. She had discovered it by accident at nineteen, on a stage in the capital, when a high note had gone wrong in the most catastrophic way possible—when the amphitheater's great dome had cracked from crown to foundation like an egg, raining marble onto three hundred nobles, killing none but burying the career of Sable the soprano forever. They had called it a weapon afterward. They had tried to draft her, cage her, aim her. She had run. She had hidden in a dying quarry town among people who valued her only for her fish-cleaning hands and her decent singing voice on festival nights.

Eleven years of hiding ended in the space of one held breath.

The tunnel answered her. She felt it in her bones first—the walls shivering, the water trembling into rings—and then the fault lines she'd memorized woke all at once, and the passage behind them folded like a knee. Stone met stone with a boom that rolled through the flooded dark like the tolling of a drowned bell, and the green phosphorescence flared blinding-white and died, and dust and spray filled the chamber, and somewhere beyond the fresh-made wall of rubble the singer's tone broke off mid-phrase, severed, silenced.

Silence rushed in after, enormous, total.

Sable stood in it, chest heaving, ears ringing, and felt eleven years of careful smallness crumble away with the tunnel. Her voice echoed back to her from the new stone wall—her voice, the forbidden one, the broken thing—and for the first time since the amphitheater, it did not sound like a curse.

It sounded like a door, closing to protect the people behind it.

* * *

They came out of the tunnels an hour later, hauled up through a rusted grate by a pair of small strong hands, into the grey light of a birch wood on the far rim of the quarry country. Wren stood waiting among the trees, mud to her eyebrows, looking insufferably pleased with herself.

"Took you long enough," the child said. "I whistled myself hoarse."

"You led crown agents' prisoners through two miles of flooded tunnel," Sable said, hauling herself onto the bank, "on instinct, alone, at nine years old."

"Ten," Wren said with dignity. "And they weren't prisoners yet. I don't rescue people who are already caught. Bad economics." She squinted at Sable, and her expression shifted into something almost solemn. "The ground shook. The whole quarry shook. The fishwives are saying it was the old god under the hill."

"The fishwives," Sable said, wringing out her coat, "are welcome to their story. It keeps everyone safer than the truth would."

Mirabel sat on a root, ledgers hugged to his soaked chest, staring at her with an expression she recognized and dreaded—the look of someone rewriting everything they thought they knew about a person. "You collapsed a tunnel," he said. "With your voice. You *collapsed a tunnel*."

"Yes."

"And you've done it before. You've run from the crown before. You knew their search-songs, you knew their tactics, you—" He stopped, and being Mirabel, he arrived at the question gently, the way he seemed to arrive at most things. "What did they do to you, Sable?"

The birch leaves moved overhead. Somewhere far behind them, muffled by miles of stone and water, the agents would be regrouping, reporting, escalating. They would come again. They always came again. Sable had known that for eleven years; she had simply persuaded herself that when they came, she would be alone, and that being alone would make it bearable.

She looked at the strange little company gathered in the birch wood—the last apprentice of a dying guild clutching his books, the woman who might be a song wearing a person's shape, the mud-caked child who rescued people for the economics of it—and felt something shift in her chest, uncomfortable as a key turning in a lock that hadn't opened in a decade.

"They tried to make me a weapon," she said at last. "So I became a rumor instead. A woman who lives alone and sings only on feast days, quietly, where no one important can hear." She shouldered her pack and started up the slope, because if she stood still much longer she was going to say something unforgivably sincere. "Well. That woman is finished. If the crown wants a weapon, let them chase one that chooses her own targets."

"Where are we going?" Mirabel called after her.

"Away from here, tonight, before they tighten the net. South, then east." She glanced back at him, and despite everything—despite the cold and the fear and the eleven wasted years—she found she was smiling. "There's a border ward two days from here, boy, and I happen to know your guild holds a contract on the other side of it. A debt the Ledgerkeepers have owed for thirty years. You're the guild now, aren't you? Time you started paying what your guild promised."

Mirabel blinked. "How do you know about—"

"I clean fish for half the farms in this valley," Sable said. "People talk to the fish-cleaner. They tell her everything." She turned and walked on into the trees, and behind her she heard them gather—boots on leaves, the child's quick patter, the soft impossible harmony of two voices murmuring together—and she realized that the sound of it, this ragged little company moving through the birches, was the first music in eleven years that she had not been afraid to be part of.

Far to the south, past the ward and the mountains, a king sat on a throne wearing a crown that was eating him alive.

Sable flexed her throat, felt the mountain's name still sleeping in it, and walked faster.
