## 10. The Second Note

The mountain did not want us there.

I felt it in my teeth before we ever saw the monastery—a low resistance in the air itself, as though the whole mass of stone and pine were holding its breath against our arrival. The path climbed through cloud for the better part of a day, switchbacking up a shoulder of rock so steep that Sable twice had to catch Wren by the collar before the child tumbled into white nothing. Mirabel walked ahead with the ledger strapped to his chest like a second heart, and I watched him watch the mountain, and I understood that he felt it too. Things that trade in impossible things know when a place is refusing to be found.

"The ledgers say the monastery has stood here four hundred years," he said, breath fogging. "But there's no road. No supply line. Nothing written about how it feeds itself or who visits."

"Maybe it doesn't," Sable said. "Maybe it just endures. Some things don't need to be fed. They need only to be true."

Wren, perched on a boulder like a small dark bird, whistled a note that made the clouds shiver apart around us. "Then it's like her," she said, jerking her chin at me.

I didn't answer. I had been not answering that particular observation for six days now—since the quarry town, since Sable had looked at me singing the chord that bent the candle flames and said, quietly, *that isn't a voice, girl, that's an architecture*—and I intended to keep not answering it all the way up the mountain if the mountain would allow it.

It did not allow it. The mountain, I came to understand later, had sent for me.

* * *

The monastery appeared the way a thought appears: suddenly, completely, as though it had always been there and we had simply failed to notice. Grey stone grown out of grey rock, walls unbroken by gate or door, windows narrow as knife slits. No bell tower. That was the first wrongness I registered—the absence where a bell should be—and the second was the silence. Not quiet. Silence. The difference is that quiet is the absence of sound, but silence is the presence of something listening.

Mirabel raised his hand to knock on a wall that had no door, and before his knuckles touched stone, the wall opened.

Not swung open. Opened, the way a mouth opens, the stones parting along a seam I could not see even knowing it was there. Beyond lay a courtyard full of snow that had never been shoveled because no snow dared land in it, and beyond that, a cloister walk lined with columns, and beneath the columns, monks. A dozen of them, maybe more, robed in undyed wool, standing perfectly still.

None of them turned their heads. But every one of them was facing me.

"Brother Oren," Mirabel said to the nearest monk, and his voice came out smaller than he wanted it to. "We've come—we think you have something of ours. A note."

The monk did not speak. None of them spoke; I understood that now, watching their stillness, understanding it the way one understands falling. This was a community built around a silence the way other communities are built around a bell. They had taken a vow not merely of quiet but of *unmaking sound*, and they practiced it the way soldiers practice drill.

And then, from the center of them, from a broad-shouldered brother whose face was seamed like old bark, came a sound.

Not a voice. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only dignity left to me after what happened next. It was not a voice. It was a vibration that arrived in the body before the ear—a hum that rose up through the flagstones and into the bones of my feet, my ankles, my knees, until my whole skeleton rang like a struck glass. The note was low and round and absolutely steady, and it was the second note of the binding song. I knew it the way I knew my own heartbeat, which is to say: I did not know it at all, and yet it was mine.

Sable's hand closed on my arm. "That's him," she breathed. "That's the second note. Elodie—that's *your* harmony. Listen. He's singing the part that answers yours."

The note stopped. Brother Oren stepped forward, out of the line of his silent brothers, and looked at me—not at Mirabel, not at the ledger, at *me*—with eyes the color of wet slate. Then he raised one hand, palm out, and pressed it flat against the air between us.

And the courtyard sang back.

Every column, every flagstone, every flake of unmelted snow began to vibrate in sympathy, and the sound they made was *my* sound—the first note, the root of the binding, the chord I had carried in my throat since the night I knocked twice on the Ledgerkeepers' door without knowing why. The stone was holding my note the way a tuning fork holds pitch. It had been holding it, I realized, for a very long time.

Brother Oren lowered his hand. The resonance died slowly, reluctantly, like a guest who does not wish to leave.

"He knows the song," Mirabel whispered. "He's known it longer than we have. Elodie—how does he—"

Oren turned and walked into the cloister. It was not an invitation. It was simply the inevitable next thing, the way dawn follows dark, and we followed him because there was nothing else in the world to do.

* * *

They gave us a cell with a brazier and bread that tasted of juniper and cold air, and then Oren took me alone to the scriptorium.

I want to record what I felt walking there, because afterward I could not feel it anymore, and someone ought to remember. I felt curious. I felt wary in the ordinary way, the way one is wary of a stranger's kindness. I felt the pleasant ache of altitude in my lungs and the warmth of bread in my belly. I was, in the moments before the scriptorium door closed behind us, still a woman walking down a corridor.

The scriptorium held no books. That was the third wrongness, and by then I had learned to read wrongnesses the way Mirabel read ledgers. Instead of books there were instruments—bells without clappers, drums with heads of stretched river-ice, great hollow stones set into the floor like teeth—and instead of desks there were circles chalked on the boards, each circle occupied by a kneeling monk who was not writing but *humming*, pressing his palms flat to the wood so the table itself became the page and the vibration became the ink.

"They write in resonance," I said aloud, stupidly, because saying something ordinary was the last rope I had hold of.

Oren nodded. He sat across from me at the central table, laid both palms upon it, and the wood beneath my own hands began to murmur. And then—he was patient about it, gentle about it, I will grant him that forever—he showed me.

He showed me the choir.

Not told me. *Showed* me, the way the ledger shows Mirabel things, except his instrument was the table and his ink was frequency, and what he wrote into the wood was memory—old memory, guild-deep, choir-old. I felt it come up through my palms and into my wrists and I could not pull away because some part of me recognized it as a letter addressed to me alone, long delayed, finally delivered.

I felt a hundred voices singing in a vaulted chapel that smelled of incense and beeswax. I felt them bind a hunger into gold. I felt the crown pass from a willing king to an unwilling dynasty, the binding twisting like a rope hauled too tight around a load it was never shaped for. I felt the choir fracture—felt it the way a glass feels the first hairline crack, a high bright agony running through a hundred throats at once—

And then I felt the choir gather itself. Dying, broken, scattered into notes with no singers left to hold them, it did the only thing left to do. It composed. It took everything it was—all its harmonies, all its history, all its grief and purpose and terrible beauty—and it folded those hundred voices down, and down, and down, into a single shape small enough to survive.

A shape with two legs and a throat and no memory of a childhood, because it had never had one.

The table went still. My hands were shaking on its surface. Across from me, Brother Oren watched me with an expression I will spend the rest of my life trying to name—it was compassion, yes, but also recognition, the look of one instrument regarding another, and that was the worst of it, that was the thing that broke something in me that I am not sure has finished breaking.

"You're telling me," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me now, suspect, a thing I could no longer trust to be mine, "that I'm not the choir's last singer."

He shook his head slowly.

"I'm the choir." The words came out flat, factual, horrible. "Or what's left of it. I'm not a person who sings the binding. I'm the binding, wearing a person. I'm—" I laughed, and the laugh came out as a perfect minor third, and hearing it made me want to tear my own throat out. "*That.* Did you hear that? Even my laughing is composed. Even my grief is in key."

Oren reached across the table and took my hands. His palms were warm and rough and human, calloused from decades of pressing stone, and I hated how much comfort I took from it, because comfort taken from a man who had just unmade me felt like a lie I was telling myself.

He pressed my hands flat to the table and hummed again—low, brief, questioning. And the wood answered, and the answer it carried was not the choir's memory this time. It was his. A boy in a fishing village who lost his voice to fever at nine years old and screamed for a year in silence until the brothers came and taught him that a voice is not the throat. A young man who wept at his mother's funeral because he could not sing the mourning hymn and his brother had to sing it for him, badly, off-key, and the off-keyness was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard because it was *chosen*. A lifetime of learning to pour himself into surfaces—to become, deliberately, over forty years of discipline, an instrument of his own will.

His meaning came through my palms like heat through a door: *I chose this. That is the difference. An instrument that chooses is still a soul.*

"But I didn't choose," I whispered. "I was *made*. Made on purpose, made as a vessel, made to carry a song to whoever needed it. I'm not a woman who joined a quest. I'm a delivery. I'm a letter that learned to walk."

Oren's grip tightened. He shook his head once, hard, and pressed my hands down again, and this time what came through the table was not memory but argument—his whole life compressed into a single resonant insistence:

*A bell is cast in a mold. Is the bell therefore not real? The mold gives it shape. The striking gives it voice. What it means—what it says, whom it calls home—is decided after, by what answers.*

I pulled my hands away. I stood. I walked to the narrow window and looked out at the clouds boiling below the mountainside, and I said the thing I had been carrying since the quarry town, since before the quarry town, since the night I first understood that I remembered no mother, no scraped knee, no first snow:

"Mirabel thinks I'm a person. Sable thinks I'm a marvel. Wren thinks I'm a mystery worth following. And none of them know that when they look at me, they're looking at a cage wearing a chorus. If I tell them—if Mirabel knows that the voice he's been trusting is just the binding using me as a mouth—"

Oren came to stand beside me. He did not touch me this time. He simply stood, shoulder to shoulder, looking out at the same clouds, and let the silence do what his hands could not.

And in that silence, for the first time, I heard my own heart. Just a heart. Uneven, mortal, slightly too fast, keeping no time at all. Whatever else I was—composed remnant, folded choir, letter that learned to walk—this muscle beat without harmony, without design, without any composer's hand. It beat the way Brother Oren's brother had sung at the funeral: badly, off-key, chosen.

Perhaps that was the whole of the argument. Perhaps being made was only the mold, and everything after—the fear, the loyalty, the way Mirabel's face changed when I entered a room, the fierce tenderness I felt for a feral child and a disgraced diva—perhaps all of that was the striking, and the meaning was still mine to decide.

"Second note," I said at last. "You'll come with us. You'll sing the binding loose and free a king you've never met."

Oren inclined his head. Then he raised one finger—*wait*—and crossed to the central table, and laid his palms upon it one final time.

What rose through the wood was not for me. It was a message, addressed outward, meant to be carried down the mountain in my memory and delivered to a boy with a book strapped to his chest like a second heart. I listened, and my eyes filled, and I nodded.

When I opened the scriptorium door, Mirabel was waiting in the cloister with the others, and the question was already on his face—*is he with us, did he tell you anything, Elodie, are you all right*—and I opened my mouth to give him Oren's message.

But what came out instead, in a voice that shook, was my own confession: "Mirabel. Before we go anywhere. There's something you need to know about what I am. And I need you to hear all of it before you decide whether the fifth verse can really be sung by someone who was never a person at all."
