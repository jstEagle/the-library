## 11. Am I a Person or a Song?

The monastery bell did not ring. It hummed.

Elodie felt it in her teeth, in the hollows behind her eyes, in the cage of her ribs where a heart should have been beating and something else was instead—a chord, held, sustained, the way Brother Oren's silence held his song. The monks had given them cells for the night: narrow stone rooms with straw pallets and one candle each, windows facing east toward the mountains, where the moon was already rising fat and pale, nine turns gone from its fullness, four to go.

She could not sleep.

She had tried. She had lain down as she'd seen others lie down—knees first, then hip, then shoulder, folding herself onto the pallet like someone following instructions for a garment she'd never worn—and stared at the ceiling until the candle guttered low. Sleep was supposed to come the way it came for Mirabel, who was already breathing slow in the cell beside hers, or for Sable, whose snores rattled faintly through the wall with operatic grandeur even in unconsciousness. Wren slept curled like a comma. Oren, she suspected, slept the way bells rested between tolls—not absent but gathered, coiled around his own resonance.

But Elodie lay awake, and the question Oren had pressed into her palm that afternoon, written in charcoal on a scrap of vellum because he could not speak it, kept unfolding like a hand opening:

*You are not the choir's last voice. You are what the choir became when it broke.*

*More instrument than person. Ask yourself which.*

She sat up. The candle flame leaned toward her, the way all flames leaned toward her when she hummed, and she made herself stop humming—the small unconscious chord she carried always, the way other people carried a tune or a worry. When she silenced it, the silence was enormous. It filled her like water fills a dropped glove.

*I don't remember a childhood.*

She had said this to no one. She had barely said it to herself. But now, in the dark, with the mountain wind worrying at the shutters, she let the thought walk all the way to its end for the first time.

Other people had *firsts*. A first word. A first tooth. A first fall, a first fever, a first snow. Sable spoke of her girlhood in the flooded quarry town the way you speak of an old wound—with tenderness and flinching both. Wren remembered hunger, and cold doorways, and the specific gray color of dawn over a city of strangers. Even Mirabel, orphaned, purged-from, last-of-his-kind, had *that*: he had parents to have lost. He had a purge to survive. His grief had roots; it went down into soil that had once held him.

Elodie had nothing beneath her. She had arrived, fully formed, at the guild door on the night the prophecy named, humming a chord that made candles lean. Before that—

Before that there was nothing. Not darkness, not waiting, not a locked room. Nothing, the way there is nothing before your own birth, except she had never been born. She had been *composed*. Oren's hands had shaped the words in the air that afternoon, patient and terrible: the Choir of Binding had been twelve voices, and when the binding twisted and the choir shattered, the twelve notes had not died—they had *gathered*, the way water gathers, the way grief gathers, and condensed into a single walking shape wearing a woman's face.

Twelve notes. One throat.

And every memory she owned—every one, the smell of rain on hot stone, the taste of bread, the ache in her calves after a long road—all of it had begun the day she knocked twice on the Ledgerkeepers' door. Everything before that knock was a blank page. Not torn out. Never written.

*Am I a person,* she asked the dark, *or am I a song?*

The dark did not answer. The dark never did. That was the trouble with being made of music: silence was the one thing she could not bear, and the one thing she was most full of.

She rose. Bare feet on cold stone. She knew, without knowing how she knew, that Mirabel would be awake too—he was always awake on the nights that mattered, hunched over the ledgers like a man keeping vigil over a sick friend—and she found him exactly where she knew he would be: in the monastery's scriptorium, a single lamp burning, the great ledger open before him, his pen moving in small careful strokes.

He looked up when she entered, and something in her face must have shown, because he set down the pen at once. He did that. He always did that—abandoned whatever held him the moment a person needed holding. She had watched him do it for Sable's nightmares, for Wren's silences, for Oren's long stillnesses. She had told herself it meant nothing, that he was simply kind the way ledgers were simply accurate. Tonight she could not tell herself anything. Her voice, her own true voice, had gone somewhere she couldn't follow.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"I don't sleep." She heard how it sounded—flat, factual, a ledger entry. "I rest. There's a difference. Sleep is where people go to be someone else for a while. I have nowhere to go."

Mirabel closed the ledger gently, as if tucking it in. "That's a strange thing to say."

"It's a strange thing to be." She sat across from him. The lamp between them threw their shadows huge against the shelves of sleeping books. "Mirabel. I need to tell you something, and I need you to not be kind about it. Kindness right now would feel like pity, and pity would feel like being erased, and I—" Her voice cracked, and the lamp flame bent sideways, and somewhere down the corridor a sleeping monk murmured in sympathy without waking. "I need you to just *listen*."

"I'm listening."

So she told him. All of it, laid out in order like entries in a column: Oren's revelation by charcoal and gesture. The twelve voices. The breaking. The gathering. And then the confession underneath, the one that had been sitting in her chest since the quarry town, since the border ward, since the first night she'd watched him catalog impossible things with such lonely competence:

"I don't remember a childhood," she said, "because I never had one. I don't remember a mother because there wasn't one. My first memory is your door. Your knocker. Twice." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, a minor key where a major should be. "Do you know what that means? Everyone else in this company is running *from* something. Sable from disgrace, Wren from the streets, Oren from whatever broke his voice, you from Corvane and the crown and your own name in a prophecy. But I'm not running from anything. I'm running *as* nothing. I'm a song that learned to walk and put on a coat and started calling itself a woman, and some nights I can't feel the seams, and some nights—" her hands were shaking now, and she let them, "—some nights the seams are all I can feel. Am I a person, Mirabel? Or am I just the last note of something dead, wearing a body it borrowed?"

The lamp flickered. The wind pressed at the shutters. Somewhere far below, the monastery bell hummed its endless held tone, and Elodie hated it suddenly, hated the way it went on and on without ever needing to be anyone.

Mirabel was quiet for a long time. She braced for kindness. She braced for the soft terrible gentleness that would confirm everything.

Instead he opened the ledger again.

Not the main one—the great prophecy book stayed wrapped in oilcloth in his pack—but the working ledger, the daily one, thick and scarred and smelling of tallow and old ink. He turned pages. Past the bottled echo. Past the debt paid in years. Past trades so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. He turned past all the written things, all the recorded impossibilities, all the way to the back, where the guild's accountants had left empty pages for entries yet to come.

He turned the book around and pushed it across the table to her.

A blank page. Clean, unmarked, waiting. In the lamplight it glowed faintly, the way unwritten paper does when it belongs to a ledger that trades in impossible things—as if it were not empty but *holding its breath*.

"What's this?" she whispered.

"A page that hasn't happened yet." His voice was steady, but she saw his hands weren't—she saw that he was frightened too, frightened for her, frightened *with* her, and somehow that was better than calm. "You know what we do. What the guild does. We write things down and the writing makes them true. A debt paid in years. An echo in a bottle. Every entry in this book was once nothing—an idea, a hope, a thing that hadn't happened. Then someone wrote it, and reality honored the contract." He tapped the blank page with one finger. "This isn't emptiness, Elodie. This is potential. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"You're saying I could be written."

"I'm saying you already are being written. Every day since you knocked." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. Oren told you what you were made *from*. Fine. That's an origin, not a sentence. Sable was made from training and ruin. Wren was made from hunger and cleverness. I was made from a dead guild and dead parents and a prophecy I didn't ask for. None of us chose our materials. What we're doing with them—that's the part that's ours." He turned the page slightly, angling it toward her. "The choir broke, and you're what gathered. All right. But a river is what gathers when rain falls, and no one looks at a river and says it's only rain. It cuts canyons. It turns wheels. It has banks and a name and a direction. You've been cutting canyons since the day I met you."

"When have I cut canyons?"

"Sable." He said it simply. "You heard her sing the note that cracks stone, and you didn't flinch—you *listened*, like her ruin was music. She follows you now. Wren trusts exactly one adult on this earth, and it isn't me, and it isn't Sable. It's the woman who never tells her to stop whistling. Oren hasn't gestured this much in years—I can tell; monks keep records too. And me." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I was ready to burn the prophecy book, Elodie. Corvane told me to and I almost did, because I was afraid, and being afraid alone is how you die without noticing. Then you knocked twice, and I stopped being alone, and I stopped wanting to burn things and started wanting to finish them. If you're only a song, then you're the song that taught a ledger boy to be brave. Songs don't usually get credit for that. People do."

Elodie looked down at the blank page. Her vision blurred. When she blinked, a tear fell—one tear, the first she could ever remember shedding—and struck the paper, and did not bead and roll off as tears do. It *soaked in*. Spread. Left a faint mark, like a watermark, like a seal.

They both stared at it.

"The ledger accepted it," Mirabel breathed. "It—it registered you. As a—" He fumbled for the word, and she watched him find it, watched his face change as he did. "As an *author*. Elodie, the ledger doesn't take payment from instruments. It takes payment from people. It just—" He laughed, wet-eyed, disbelieving. "It just witnessed you grieving, and grief is a trade, and it paid you back in standing. You're on the books. You're *in the book*."

She touched the watermark. It was warm.

"I don't know what to write," she said. "If I write my history, I'd be inventing it. That feels like lying."

"Then don't write backward." Mirabel slid the pen across the table. "Write forward. Start tomorrow. 'Elodie woke in a monastery on the ninth turn of the waning moon, and she was afraid, and she sang anyway.' That's true. That's more true than most histories I've cataloged. Histories aren't what happened to you. They're what you carry forward. Write the carrying."

Her hand closed around the pen. It trembled. Twelve notes' worth of trembling, maybe—all the shaking the choir never got to do when it broke, saved up inside her single borrowed-and-earned body, asking to be spent.

"I might write myself wrong," she said.

"Then we'll amend the entry." He smiled, tired and crooked and real. "That's what ledgers are for. Nothing written in ink is final. That's the whole secret of the guild, the thing Corvane never understood when he sold us. The ledger isn't a record of what's fixed. It's a record of what's *changeable*. Every impossible thing we ever traded was just reality agreeing to revise itself." He tapped the watermark, her tear, her seal. "You're not a song pretending to be a person. You're a person the world hasn't finished writing. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Outside, the wind shifted. The bell's hum changed pitch—slightly, subtly, the way a held breath changes when the holder decides to keep going.

Elodie bent over the page. And in a hand that shook and steadied and shook again, she wrote:

*Elodie woke in a monastery on the ninth turn of the waning moon, and she was afraid, and she sang anyway.*

The ink took. The page seemed to exhale. And deep in the monastery, in the chapel where Brother Oren kept his silent vigil, a single bell that had not been rung in three hundred years gave off one clear, soft, involuntary note—like an instrument recognizing its player, or a choir recognizing its own.

Footsteps in the corridor. Fast ones. Wren's voice, sharp with the particular fear the child saved for emergencies: "Ledger boy! Ledger boy, wake up—there's a light coming up the mountain road, and it ain't a pilgrim's lantern. It's moving like soldiers march."

Mirabel was already on his feet, the ledger under his arm, his hand finding Elodie's shoulder—steadying her, or himself, or both.

"How many?" she asked, and was surprised to hear her voice come back whole.

"Too many," Wren called back. "And they're singing."

* * *

Down in the chapel, Brother Oren stood before the old mural of the broken choir, his palm flat against the painted mouths of the twelve lost singers. He could feel it through the stone: boots on the mountain road, many, and among them a sound he had hoped never to feel again—a voice trained in the crown's own conservatory, singing the counter-song. The unmaking melody. The one written to dissolve bindings.

Or, his hands told the dark as he turned toward the stairs, to dissolve *choirs*.

He rang the alarm bell with his fist. Once. Twice.

Like a knock.
