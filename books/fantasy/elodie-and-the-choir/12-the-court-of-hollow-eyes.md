## 12. The Court of Hollow Eyes

The capital of Veylmark had a smell, and the smell was ink and lilies.

Mirabel noticed it first from the crest of the eastern road, where the city unrolled below them like a ledger opened to its most expensive page—white walls, gilded spires, the palace rising at its heart like a paperweight pressed down on everything beneath it. Lilies, because the court planted them in every public bed this time of year, white as surrender. Ink, because the crown's assessors went door to door in every district, and each assessment was written twice: once in the assessor's book, once in the memory of the household being bled.

"You're certain these will pass?" Sable tugged at the gray collar of her assessor's coat as though it were strangling her. She wore disguise badly—not because the clothes fit poorly, but because her voice lived in her throat like a caged bird, and coats could not muffle eyes that had brought audiences to their feet in three countries.

"They'll pass," Mirabel said, "because they're real."

He turned one of the brass seals over in his palm. It had cost them nothing but a careful entry in the traveling ledger he now carried against his ribs—a trade of one forged commission for one forgotten debt owed to a customs clerk in the river towns. The guild's old magic, small and precise, like a lockpick made of language. The seal bore the true stamp of the Office of Assessment, wax still faintly warm when he'd pulled it from the page.

Elodie stood beside him at the roadside, hood up, watching the city with an expression Mirabel had learned to read the way sailors read weather. Her face did not move much—it never had—but there were tides in it.

"It hums," she said quietly.

"What does?"

"The city. Underneath." She pressed two fingers to her own collarbone, where, if you knew what she was, you might imagine strings. "A chord held too long. Do you hear how nobody on the road is talking? They walk. They don't speak unless spoken to. Even the birds are quiet."

Mirabel listened. She was right. The traffic into the city moved with the smooth, grainless flow of water through a pipe—carts, merchants, pilgrims, all of it orderly, all of it silent. A woman dropped an apple from her basket and did not stop for it. Another traveler stepped over it without looking down.

"Oren felt it too," Brother Oren signed, hands moving in the quick grammar only Elodie fully shared. He rode in the cart behind them, and Wren sat cross-legged atop the crates, whittling a stick into something that was either a whistle or a knife, depending on Wren's mood.

"Everyone feels it," Wren said around the stick. "That's the whole point of a capital. You feel small and keep walking." But her eyes flicked toward the walls, and her fingers had gone still on the knife.

Mirabel looked down at the city a moment longer. Somewhere in that white sprawl, King Aldric sat on a throne wearing a crown that ate him by inches. Somewhere in that sprawl, a spymaster read letters written in Corvane's careful hand. And somewhere—this was the thought that made his stomach tighten—somewhere the thing in the crown was counting the turns of the moon, patient as interest accruing.

"Assessors enter by the tax gate," he said. "We go in at midmorning, when the queues are longest. Nobody looks at your face when they're worried about their ledgers." He met Sable's eyes. "Nobody speaks above a whisper inside the Ministry. If we're separated, we regroup at the Lily Court fountain at dusk. If we're not separated—" he allowed himself the ghost of a smile, "—we drink bad wine and leave."

"And if someone looks too closely?" Sable asked.

"Then Wren whistles," Mirabel said, "and we all enjoy the wind."

* * *

The tax gate was exactly as boring as Mirabel had hoped, which was the greatest gift a lie could give. A bored guard waved their papers under his nose, grunted at the seals, and waved them through into a courtyard full of clerks and petitioners and the smell of hot wax. The Ministry of Assessment occupied four floors of a building that had once been a temple; you could still see the old saints' niches in the walls, filled now with filing cabinets.

What Mirabel had not expected was the silence inside.

Not quiet—silence. Petitioners stood in lines with their hats in their hands, and they did not shift their weight. Clerks wrote with pens that scratched in perfect unison, dozens of nibs rising and falling together like the legs of some enormous feeding insect. When a bell rang the hour, every head in the hall turned toward it at precisely the same angle, held for precisely the same breath, and then returned to work.

"Glassy," Elodie murmured at his shoulder. Her voice was very low. "Like windows after a storm. You can see through them, but nothing's home."

"The courtiers," Mirabel said. "This is what the plan said we'd find."

The plan—their plan, drafted in a quarry town by lamplight—had imagined corruption, fear, a court bought and bullied by a hungry crown. It had not imagined this: obedience so complete it had stopped looking like obedience and started looking like emptiness. A clerk handed Mirabel a form without being asked, her fingers cold, her eyes the color of a pond in November. "Assessment of assessments," she recited, "third floor, room nine. Present your seals. Wait to be acknowledged."

"Thank you," Mirabel said.

She did not respond. She had already turned away, her pen resuming its place in the great synchronized scratching, and Mirabel walked on with ice settling along his spine.

Room nine held a deputy assessor who examined their seals for a long, humid minute while sweat crawled between Mirabel's shoulders. Then the man stamped their commission, assigned them a route through the noble quarter—"Inventory of unregistered valuables, pursuant to the Crown's Edification"—and dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, as though brushing away ash.

They spent the afternoon doing exactly what real assessors would do: knocking on the doors of minor nobles, cataloging silverware and tapestries and one alarming collection of taxidermied owls, writing figures in columns with a fluency Mirabel hadn't known he possessed. It was good cover. It was also unbearable, because in every house the servants moved like sleepwalkers and the lords laughed a half-second too late at their own jokes, and more than once Mirabel caught a servant standing motionless in a doorway, staring at nothing, lips moving around words that made no sound.

Words that sounded, when he leaned close enough, almost like humming.

By dusk his hands shook, and not from fatigue.

* * *

The spymaster's office was not in the Ministry. That was the beauty of it, Corvane had once told him, back when Corvane's lessons had been the architecture of Mirabel's whole world: *the best-hidden room is the one everyone can see.* The Office of Correspondence occupied a modest suite on the second floor of the Chancery, directly across the Lily Court from the Ministry itself, its windows overlooking the fountain where they were supposed to regroup. Anyone could walk past it. No one ever had reason to enter.

Mirabel had a reason. He'd spent three days constructing it: an assessment discrepancy, a sealed query requiring the Chancery's records office, a forged routing slip that would deliver him—alone, briefly, plausibly—to the corridor outside the spymaster's door at the hour when clerks took their evening bread.

The others waited near the fountain. Elodie had argued, in her wordless way, to come with him; Oren had argued harder, signing until his hands blurred. But the corridor saw a hundred faces a day, and a hundred faces could hold one false assessor. Five would be a procession.

So Mirabel went alone, carrying his forged slip and his hammering heart, past the Lily Court where the fountains played and the flowers gave off their sweet, funereal scent, up the Chancery stairs, into the corridor that smelled of lamp oil and dust.

The records office was empty at the bread hour. So was the corridor. So, when he tried it—gently, with the little pressure-trick Corvane had taught him for doors that claimed to be locked but were merely shy—was the Office of Correspondence.

Inside, the room was neat as a hymnal. Desks in rows, ledgers squared to the desk edges, quills laid parallel like soldiers at rest. One window open to the evening air. And on the central desk, a stack of correspondence awaiting the spymaster's return from whatever shadow he was currently inhabiting.

Mirabel told himself he would look at only the top letter.

The top letter was in Corvane's hand.

He knew that hand the way he knew his own heartbeat—the slight forward lean of the numerals, the way the letter *r* ended in a hook like a question. He had watched it write out his apprenticeship contract. He had watched it sign the ledger entries that had defined his life. Now it wrote, in a careful, level script:

*The boy has left the hall. He travels with the singer and two others, identities uncertain. I have told them nothing of our arrangement. They believe me loyal to the guild.*

Below it, in another hand—sharp, economical, a hand that wrote the way a blade moves:

*And are you?*

And below that, Corvane again:

*I am loyal to what remains. Send no agents to the quarry town. I will manage the boy myself.*

Mirabel set the letter down with hands that had gone strangely calm. Cold, not calm—he knew the difference, and chose not to examine it. There were other letters beneath. He read them. He read all of them, because the part of him that had been raised among ledgers understood that a partial record was worse than none, and because some cold engine in his chest had taken over the business of feeling and would release it later, in installments, at moments of its own choosing.

Corvane had been writing to the spymaster for eleven months. Eleven months—back before the prophecy book had fallen open, back before Elodie had knocked twice, back when Mirabel had been cataloging bottled echoes and believing his guildmaster's sighs about the dying trade. Every movement of the guild's remaining members, itemized. The locations of the stored miracles. The names of clients who had traded in impossible things and might be leveraged. And once, in a postscript that made Mirabel's vision swim:

*You asked whether the Ledgerkeepers keep anything on the Choir of Binding. We do. I have not yet surrendered it. Ask me again when the guild is safe.*

*When the guild is safe.* As though safety were a thing the spymaster could grant. As though Corvane were negotiating with a man.

Mirabel reached the bottom letter and stopped.

It was not addressed to the spymaster. It was addressed *by* the spymaster—to someone or something called only **THE SEAT**—and the handwriting had changed. Not the style. The *substance*. Where the letters to Corvane were sharp and human, this was different: the strokes too regular, the spacing too perfect, each character identical to its siblings as if stamped rather than written. And the ink was wrong. It was not black. In the fading light from the window it gleamed a color Mirabel had seen only once before—in the scrying entry, in the ledgers, in the flicker behind King Aldric's eyes.

A gold that was not gold. A hunger given pigment.

*The thirteenth turn approaches,* the letter read. *The vessel weakens pleasingly. The choir-scatter is recalled: a cracked voice in the quarry country, a mute in the mountains, a thief-child in the capital's gutters. And the keeper of the book has begun to gather them. This was not foreseen. Correct this. The last note must not reach the chapel. Bring me the boy's hands and the girl's throat, and the remainder may scatter to the winds.*

*Bring me the boy's hands and the girl's throat.*

Mirabel read it three times. The cold engine in his chest processed the sentence, filed it, refused to let him feel it yet. His eyes moved instead to the signature at the bottom, and there he found the thing that finally, quietly, broke the world he'd been standing on.

There was no name. There was a seal, pressed into wax gone dark as dried blood—and the seal was a crown. Not any crown. *The* crown, rendered in miniature, and beneath it, where a signet should have borne a house or a title, the wax showed a single word repeated in a ring, around and around, like a prayer or a hunger with no bottom:

*MINE. MINE. MINE. MINE.*

The spymaster served no king. The spymaster served the thing that wore the king.

Every report Corvane had sent—every location, every secret, every careful negotiation for the guild's survival—had been delivered straight into the ear of the hunger in the crown. Corvane hadn't sold the guild to the crown's spies. He had sold it to the cage itself, and the cage had been using his letters to hunt the last notes of the song that could undo it. The quarry town. The monastery. Wren. *The choir-scatter is recalled.* The thing had known about them before they'd known about themselves.

And somewhere in those eleven months of correspondence, Corvane had withheld the choir's file—had stalled, had bargained, had *asked me again when the guild is safe*—and Mirabel understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever else his guildmaster was, he was also a man standing in a burning house trying to choose which room to save.

It didn't help. That was the terrible part. Understanding didn't help at all.

Mirabel copied the letter to THE SEAT into his traveling ledger, stroke for stroke, including the ring of *mine*s, because evidence belonged in ledgers and fury needed somewhere to live. Then he restored the stack to its original order, checked the room against his memory twice, and slipped out into the corridor, closing the shy door softly behind him.

* * *

The Lily Court at dusk was all long shadows and cooling stone. The fountain murmured. Sable stood at its edge in her assessor's grays, arms crossed, watching the gates. Oren sat on the fountain's rim, perfectly still, listening to the city with his whole body. Wren was upside-down on a bench, legs hooked over the backrest, and Elodie stood apart from all of them, facing the palace, whose windows were beginning to kindle with lamplight—hundreds of golden squares, and in one high tower, one window that burned a different gold entirely. A gold that was not gold.

Mirabel crossed the court slowly. They saw his face and came together without a word, the way they had learned to, five people becoming one shape.

"Well?" Sable said.

"He knows where we've been," Mirabel said. His voice came out flat and even, and he was distantly proud of it. "Quarry town. The monastery. He knows about Wren." Wren went rigid on her bench. "He's known since before we started gathering them. The spymaster isn't the crown's spy. He's the crown-thing's mouth. Everything Corvane sold him went straight to the thing in Aldric's crown, and it has been hunting the notes of the choir for months."

Silence, except the fountain.

"Corvane," Elodie said. Not a question. She had warned Mirabel once, in another life it seemed, and he had refused to believe her, and the refusal sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone.

"He thinks he's protecting what's left of us," Mirabel said. "He's been trading piece by piece, stalling, holding back the choir's file. He thinks he's negotiating." He heard his own voice crack on the last word and hated it. "He's feeding it. Every letter. Eleven months. He's been feeding it, and it let him believe, because belief makes better bait than chains."

Oren touched his arm—two fingers, light, a monk's benediction or a warning. *Grief later*, the touch said. *Walk first.*

"There's more," Mirabel said, and made himself say it plainly, because these people deserved plainness. "It wants my hands and Elodie's throat. Those were its words. The rest of us it's willing to let scatter." He looked around at them—at Sable's lifted chin, at Wren's knife suddenly very visible in her fist, at Oren's steady dark eyes, at Elodie, who was watching him with that tide-moving stillness. "I need you to understand what you're standing in. This isn't a court we're sneaking past anymore. It's a hunt, and we're the quarry, and the hunter never sleeps and never forgets and owns every wall in this city."

Wren spat over the edge of the bench. "Been hunted before. Didn't like the odds then either." She flipped upright. "So we hit the chapel fast. Before it can move."

"We have two turns of the moon," Mirabel said. "Maybe less, if it stops being patient. Which means we leave tonight, through the north gate, before the morning assessments put our faces in front of the wrong clerk." He paused. His hand found the ledger against his ribs, and the cold engine in his chest finally released one installment of what it had been holding, and he let it burn through him—Corvane's hooked *r*, the ring of *mine*s, the window in the tower burning its hungry gold—and when he spoke again his voice had changed, and everyone heard it.

"I came here to find proof of a betrayal. I found something worse. I found out we were never sneaking anywhere. Every step we've taken, it has been watching, and it has decided we matter." He looked up at the palace, at the one wrong window. "Good. Let it watch. Let it spend its strength reaching for us. Because it made one mistake in all those perfect little letters."

Elodie tilted her head. *What mistake*, the gesture said.

"It thinks we're scattered," Mirabel said. "Five notes it can pick off one at a time. It doesn't understand yet that we're not five notes." He put his hand flat on the ledger, on the cover that held every impossible trade the guild had ever recorded, and felt—or imagined he felt—a faint answering warmth, like a struck tuning fork remembering its pitch. "It doesn't know what a choir is."

Behind them, across the court, a door opened in the Chancery. Lamplight spilled onto the steps, and a figure descended them unhurriedly—a tall figure in a plain dark coat, moving with that too-perfect regularity Mirabel had seen in the handwriting, crossing the Lily Court directly toward the fountain where five fugitives stood in the last of the light.

The figure was smiling. It was the smile that was wrong: too symmetrical, arriving a half-second before the eyes warmed, like a mask hung slightly crooked.

"Assessors," it said pleasantly, in a voice like a well-tuned instrument playing a tune it had learned from hearing humans through a wall. "How diligent you are, working at this hour. Might I see your seals?"

Mirabel's hand closed around the ledger. Beside him, Wren's fingers rose, very quietly, toward her lips.
