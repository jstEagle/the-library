## 13. Corvane's Confession

The hall had never been quiet in Corvane's memory. Even in its dying, even after the last of the old keepers had gone into the ground or gone over the wall to safer trades, there had always been sound: the tick of ledgers settling on their shelves, the mutter of quills, the low argument between candle-flame and draft. A guild that traded in impossible things made a certain noise doing it, like a loom working in the dark.

Now the only sound was Mirabel's boots on the marble, and they were not the soft, apologetic steps of an apprentice. They were the steps of a keeper coming home.

Corvane heard them from the master's desk, where he sat with a single lamp and a ledger he was not reading. He had known this night would come. He had known it the way one knows weather—by the ache in old scars, by the way the candles had been guttering sideways for three days though no window stood open. The guild itself had been trying to tell him something. Guilds did that, at the end. They grew honest.

The doors at the far end of the hall swung inward without being touched. That was Mirabel too, now. The boy had taken custody of the ledgers, and the ledgers, apparently, had decided to open doors for him.

"You're back early," Corvane said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"Where is it?" Mirabel crossed the hall at a pace that ate the distance without hurrying. Candlelight caught him in pieces—a jaw set hard, ink-stained fingers, eyes that had been wide with wonder when Corvane first met him and were now narrow with arithmetic. "The correspondence. With the spymaster. I found the copies in Vessic's office in the capital. Your hand, Corvane. Every page your hand."

*Vessic.* So the spymaster kept copies. Of course he did. Men who served hunger kept receipts; hunger itself never did.

"Sit down, Mirabel."

"I've been sitting for twenty years." The boy stopped at the edge of the lamplight, and Corvane saw that his hands were shaking—not with fear. With the effort of holding still. "Twenty years of sitting while you told me the guild was dying of natural causes. While you told me the purge was an accident of politics. While you taught me every rule except the ones you were breaking." He lifted his fist, and something pale unrolled from it—the copied pages, crushed and smoothed and crushed again. "Explain these."

There were a hundred answers Corvane had prepared across the long months of his betrayal. Clever ones. Partial ones. He was a Ledgerkeeper of forty years' standing; he could balance any lie against any truth until the columns looked clean.

He found he had no appetite for bookkeeping tonight.

"The purge wasn't political," he said.

Mirabel went very still.

"It was aimed," Corvane said. "At us. At the guild. And I helped aim it."

* * *

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, in the archive loft, a shelf settled under the weight of its books, a small sigh of old paper, and Corvane thought absurdly of his own knees.

"Sit down," he said again, gentler this time. "This isn't a short entry."

Mirabel didn't sit. He dragged a stool to the edge of the light instead, close enough that Corvane could see every line of him, and perched on it like a bird deciding whether the hand offering seed might also close.

"I was thirty-one," Corvane began. "You weren't born. The guild had ninety-four members and a waiting list, because we were the only house in the world that could hold what couldn't be held. Bottled echoes. Debts paid in years. Griefs sold by the ounce to people who'd otherwise drown in them." He smiled, and hated how the smile felt—like a coin rubbed smooth of everything stamped on it. "You know the inventory better than I do now. You catalogued half of it alone."

"What does that have to do with—"

"I'm arriving there. Let an old man balance the account properly." He turned the lamp up a fraction, though neither of them needed more light to see each other; some conversations want darkness and get it whether or not the wick agrees. "Thirty years ago there was a king. Not Aldric—the father. Aldric's father, King Edmun, and before him his mother, and behind all of them, going back eleven generations, the same crown. We kept the ledgers on the crown, Mirabel. Do you understand? There was an entire sealed section—Crown Annex, we called it—that recorded every trade the throne ever attempted. Kings live long when they wear that thing. Too long. And long-lived kings make bargains, and bargains leave entries."

"And the entries said what?"

"They said the crown was hungry." The word came out of him flat and cold, a stone dropped down a well that never struck bottom. "Not evil—I need you to hear that, boy, because everything else follows from it. Hungry. It wanted to continue. It wanted to keep wearing heads and eating the will inside them, forever, and it was patient the way only a thing that has already outlived ten dynasties can be patient. And thirty-one years ago, it got tired of our ledgers existing."

Mirabel's voice was thin. "The purge."

"The purge. Soldiers in the hall at midnight—no warning, no trial, no charges anyone ever read aloud. They burned the west wing first. The Crown Annex." His hands remembered it whether he wanted them to or not; they curled on the desk around nothing, around smoke that was three decades cold. "Seventy-two keepers died that night. Some of them the finest minds the guild ever produced. Halden, who could weigh a soul to the grain. Old Maret, who'd taught me my letters in ledger-script. Ilse and Tam Iversen."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Mirabel did not move. But Corvane watched the color go out of his face in stages, the way dusk takes a hillside—from the top down—and when he finally spoke it was barely above the lamp-hiss.

"My parents."

"Ilse and Tam Iversen," Corvane said again, because some debts are only paid in full pronunciation. "Your father kept the memory-trades. Your mother ran the mercy desk—griefs bought, sorrows sold, all the work nobody thanks you for because the people she helped don't remember needing help. They were the best of us. They were—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "They were carrying you out through the cellar when the west wall came down."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. In the silence Corvane became aware of the hall listening—the way old buildings listen—with all its shelved impossibilities leaning in through the dark. A bottled echo on the third shelf stirred faintly inside its glass, as if the room's grief had reached it and it wished to join.

"You pulled me out," Mirabel said at last. "From the cellar. You're the one who—"

"I dug for two hours with my bare hands. You were three years old and you bit me." He showed the boy his left thumb, an old crescent scar he'd never once explained. "You wouldn't let go of your mother's ledger-book. We buried her without it because you wouldn't give it up. I let you keep it because taking it seemed like letting them die twice."

Mirabel's hand drifted to his coat, to the flat shape beneath the lining that he'd carried since before he could write his own name. All these years Corvane had assumed it was sentiment. He understood now—it had been an entry all along. An unfinished one.

"Why didn't you tell me?" The question cracked in the middle. "All those years. You raised me in this hall, you taught me the craft my parents died for, and you never once said their names."

"Because telling you was the second-worst thing I could do." Corvane rose, joints announcing themselves, and walked slowly to the tall window at the hall's end, where the moon was coming up over the rooftops—one turn shy of thirteen, fat and wrong-colored, like a coin about to be called counterfeit. "Because the worst thing I ever did, I did two years later. And if you knew their names, you'd ask how they died. And if you asked how they died, you'd eventually ask why I lived. And I couldn't answer that question without confessing everything, and I was a coward, and cowards defer their payments. We call it carrying a balance. It's the ugliest phrase in the craft."

"Corvane." Mirabel's voice had gone very level, which was worse than shouting. "What did you do?"

He pressed his forehead briefly against the cold glass. Beyond it the city lay in its usual sprawl of chimney-smoke and lamplight, and somewhere in the middle of it a palace with a crowned puppet in it, and somewhere beyond that a border ward he'd quietly ensured the agents could cross.

"After the purge, the guild rebuilt. Smaller. Sadder. Seventy-two empty desks and we filled maybe twenty. And I spent two years doing what you do when the wound is fresh—I studied the Annex remnants. Charred pages. Fragments. Trying to understand what we'd lost and why." He turned back. "And in the fragments I found something I should have burned. A residue. The crown-thing leaves a residue in anything it touches, and the Annex pages had been steeped in it for centuries. And it spoke to me off the page, Mirabel. Off the char. I was alone in the archive at night, grieving, exhausted, holding a piece of paper that had been hungry's neighbor for four hundred years, and it said my name."

"You could have destroyed it."

"Yes."

"You could have brought it to the surviving masters."

"Yes."

"You could have come to *me*, even then—you could have—"

"I know what I could have done!" It came out of him louder than he'd spoken in years, loud enough to wake the echo-bottles, and the whole hall seemed to flinch. Corvane closed his eyes. When he opened them, Mirabel had risen from the stool and stood gripping its back, knuckles white, waiting.

So Corvane paid it out, all of it, in order, like a man emptying a purse he'd been afraid to open for thirty years.

"It offered me a trade. That's all it ever does—it doesn't threaten, it *trades*. And what it offered—" His voice failed on the first attempt. He tried again. "It offered me them back. Ilse and Tam. All seventy-two. Restored. Not resurrected in some rotting parody—it showed me the mechanism, the entry, the exact terms. Their deaths unwritten. Returned to the world whole, remembering nothing, alive. A guild of the dead, standing up out of the ash, and all it asked in return was that I keep the new Crown Annex empty. That I feed it what the old one held. Names. Routes. Weaknesses. What the guild knew about it—which was everything, once."

"That's why the agents always find us. That's why they knew about the quarry town within days of Sable joining us. That's why—" Mirabel's breath caught. "Elodie. She warned me. Weeks ago. She said you'd traded secrets to the spymaster and I called her a liar. To her face."

"She wasn't lying."

"I know she wasn't lying." He said it like a man driving a nail. "That's what I can't forgive myself for. Not yet. Maybe not you either." He came around the stool, one slow step. "Did you believe it? The offer. Did you actually believe it would give them back?"

Here was the heart of it, the black center Corvane had orbited for three decades, and he found that having flown so far he might as well land.

"No," he said. "I want that written down somewhere, in whatever ledger you keep of me. I never believed it. A thing whose whole nature is hunger does not give; it only shows you the shape of giving, the way a lure shows a fish the shape of light. I knew, from the first night, that the dead would not stand up. And I signed anyway." He spread his hands—empty, old, ink-stained to the second knuckle. "Because the alternative was accepting they were gone. Truly gone, with no bargain pending, no appeal filed, no balance owed that someone might someday settle. Belief wasn't required, Mirabel. Only the refusal to stop hoping. Hope is the cruelest currency in existence precisely because it spends the same whether or not it's backed by anything."

"So you fed it the guild." Mirabel's voice shook now, anger and sorrow grinding against each other like millstones. "You fed it the people who survived. You nearly fed it *me*. And it never gave you anything."

"It gave me time," Corvane said simply. "Every year the guild limped on, every year you grew another year older learning the craft they died for, I told myself the debt was still open. That someday the thing would pay. That's the arithmetic of damnation, boy—it always balances, right up until it doesn't."

* * *

Mirabel walked past him to the window. For a while he looked out at the moon with his arms folded, and Corvane recognized the posture because it was Ilse's—she used to stand exactly that way at this same window, weighing some impossible trade, before turning around and saying the truest sentence in the room.

When Mirabel turned, his eyes were wet, but his voice had settled into something Corvane hadn't heard from him before. Not the apprentice. Not the angry orphan. Something with weight in it, like a key turning.

"Is it still promising you?"

The question went through Corvane like cold water through cloth.

"Every night," he admitted. "It's stopped pretending to be subtle. Lately it speaks in my parents' voices."

"Then here's your last entry." Mirabel stepped closer, and up close Corvane could see how much of his parents lived in his face—Tam's stubborn mouth, Ilse's grave gray eyes. "The choir is gathering. Four notes, soon five. In two turns we sing the binding loose, and when it goes, it won't restore the dead and it won't spare the guilty. It will just end. Everything it promised dies with it." He held out his hand—not for a shake; palm up, the gesture a keeper uses to receive a ledger. "Give me whatever you still hold. The rest of the correspondence. The terms you signed. Everything the spymaster knows about our route. Because in two turns none of your debt will be collectible—but between now and then, they'll come for us with everything you taught them."

Corvane looked at the open hand for a long moment.

Then he went to the master's desk, knelt, and pried up the third floorboard—the one that had hidden things since before either of their births—and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Correspondence. Terms. Thirty years of shame in a packet light enough to crush in one fist.

He laid it in Mirabel's palm and did not let go of it immediately.

"There's a name on the last page you should see before anyone else does," he said quietly. "The spymaster's superior. The one the crown-thing speaks through directly. You'll want to sit down for it, and you'll have questions, and I'll answer every one." He released the bundle at last. "But Mirabel—read it fast. Because the moment you do, you'll understand why the thirteenth moon can't come soon enough. And why we may already be later than we think."

Outside, the wrong-colored moon cleared the rooftops entirely, and its light fell across the open bundle, across the first line of the final letter—and Corvane watched the boy's face as he read it, and saw the blood leave it a second time.
