## 14. The Third Note

Sable had sung for kings, and she had sung for coin, and she had learned long ago that the two audiences were not so different. Both wanted to be moved without being changed. Both applauded and went home to their suppers.

The child was neither.

They found her in the city of Vess, in the market district, on a morning when the wind came off the harbor carrying salt and rot and the cries of gulls, and the wind stopped—stopped dead, all at once, mid-gust, like a held breath—whenever the girl in the ragged grey coat went past.

Sable felt it before she saw it. The stillness. It moved through the market like a ripple through water, and every hair on her arms stood up, because she had spent her whole life learning what voices could do, and she knew the sound of a note that could quiet the sky when she heard one.

"There," she said, and touched Elodie's arm.

The girl—Wren, the fishwife would later tell them, though nobody knew her true name—was perhaps eleven, small for it, with a sharp-boned face and eyes the colour of river slate. She moved through the crowd the way water moves through cracks: unremarkable, inevitable, gone before you noticed she'd passed. But the wind followed her like a dog follows its master's hand, leaning toward her, and when she paused at a fruit stall and pursed her lips—a whistle so thin and high that Sable nearly missed it—the gulls overhead went silent, and the awnings stopped their snapping, and for three full seconds the entire harbor held its breath.

Then the girl's fingers were in the fruit-seller's till, and she was gone.

"Oh, she's good," Mirabel breathed beside Sable, and there was something in his voice that Sable recognized with a small, unexpected ache: admiration. The pure delight of a craftsman watching craft. He'd been doing that all journey, she'd noticed—looking at Elodie's impossible voice, at Oren's humming bones, at Sable's own cracked flagstones in the quarry town—with the wonder of a boy who had spent his life among records of miracles and never once witnessed one.

"Good isn't the word," Sable said. "That was a stilling. Do you understand what that was? The choir's third note. The note of cessation. The pause between heartbeats." She watched the empty space where the girl had been. "I spent twenty years on opera stages learning to hold an audience in silence. That child can hold the *wind*."

Elodie had gone very still. Sable had learned to read her by now—the composed remnant of a broken choir, a woman made of music who was still learning she had hands—and stillness in Elodie meant recognition. Meant the song in her bones leaning toward something.

"She's frightened," Elodie said quietly. "The song in her. It's frightened. It's been alone a long time."

"Then we'd best not frighten her more," Sable said. "Mirabel. Your ledgers. Does the guild have anything on her?"

Mirabel's fingers were already moving, the ledger open against his forearm, pages whispering. He'd grown into the book these past weeks—carried it less like a burden and more like a limb. "Nothing. No entry, no trade, no debt." He looked up, troubled. "The guild has no claim on her. She's never been recorded at all."

"An unwritten thing," Elodie murmured, and Sable saw the two of them exchange a look that carried whole conversations in it. She was getting tired of those looks. She was getting tired, if she was honest, of being the third wheel on a cart that rolled between those two like it had its own axle.

"Then we do this the old way," Sable said, and rolled her shoulders, and felt the old ache in her throat where the stone-cracking note lived. "The way you convince anyone of anything. You make them an offer they can't refuse."

"And what," said Mirabel, "could a street child possibly want that we have?"

Sable smiled, and it was not a kind smile, because she had learned her smiles in theatres, where a smile was a weapon like anything else.

"Me," she said. "I'm going to give her me."

* * *

It took two days to find her again, because of course it did. You did not corner a creature like that; you let it come to you. Sable knew this from her years with the opera—singers who chased applause got none, and singers who made the audience lean forward got everything.

So she did not chase. She sat.

She sat in the market square on the third morning with a hat turned upside down before her and a single copper in it, and she sang. Not the quarry-note, not the stone-cracker. She sang the old arias, the ones from her first season, the ones she'd sung when her voice was young and whole and hers, and the market gathered the way markets do, slowly and then all at once, and coins began to ring into her hat like small bells.

And through it all, at the edge of the crowd, a small grey figure watched her with slate-coloured eyes.

Sable did not look at her. That was the whole art of it. She sang *past* the girl, sang *through* her, sang as though the girl were the only audience in the square and everyone else were furniture, and she chose her songs carefully—the ones about orphans, the ones about thieves, the ones the child would know were about her, because a child like that survived on knowing when a thing was about her.

On the second song, the wind stopped.

On the fourth, the crowd's murmuring stopped.

On the sixth, the girl was standing three feet away, close enough that Sable could see the grime in the creases of her knuckles and the way her coat had been mended with fishing line, and her slate eyes were narrow with the particular suspicion of someone who has been offered kindness before and learned what it cost.

Sable finished the aria. Held the last note until it frayed. Let the silence sit.

Then she said, to the girl alone, as though the crowd of forty were empty air: "You've been stealing from my performance all morning."

"I have not." The voice was flat, city-hard, older than eleven.

"Every time you stop the wind, you stop my resonance. Do you know what that does to a singer? It's like singing in a tomb." Sable gestured at the hat. "You owe me. Three songs' worth of stolen silence. I calculate it at—oh, let's say a conversation."

Something flickered in the slate eyes. Interest, maybe. Or the recognition of a game. "You're mad," the girl said.

"Thoroughly. It's a professional requirement." Sable gathered her hat and coins and stood, joints cracking, and looked down at the child who could still the sky. "There's a pie stall on the corner. You'll sit with me while I eat one, and you'll tell me why a girl with a voice like yours is picking pockets when she could be toppling governments. And then, if you still want me gone, I'll be gone, and you'll never see me again. That's the trade. I don't renege on trades. I knew a guild once that didn't either."

The girl's eyes flicked—just once, quick as a pickpocket's fingers—to Mirabel, standing at the square's edge with the ledger against his chest, and Sable saw her catalog him the way she'd catalog the crowd: threat, no threat, mark, not a mark.

"Ledgerkeeper," the girl said, and spat the word with a hatred so old and so complete that Sable's stomach dropped.

"You know the guild?" Mirabel asked, coming forward.

"I know what the guild *did*." And the girl was backing away now, all the game gone out of her, replaced by something raw. "My mother told me. Before the fever took her. The Ledgerkeepers came to our street when I was small, and they *wrote* people. Wrote them out. There was a man on our corner, a lamplighter, who argued with a keeper about a debt, and the next day nobody remembered him but her. Not his name, not his face. His lamp went dark and stayed dark." Her chin came up, defiant, and her eyes were shining in a way that had nothing to do with tears, because children like this did not cry where anyone could see. "So no. I don't sit with ledgerkeepers. I don't sit with anyone who carries a book that eats people."

She whistled—two notes, sharp—and the wind came up off the harbor like a wall, scattering the crowd, snuffing the pie stall's fire, and she was gone into it, and Sable stood in the sudden gale with her heart hammering and her throat aching, and thought: *there it is. The wound. Every note of the choir is carried by a wound. Mine was the stage. Oren's was his tongue. Hers is the book.*

* * *

"She's right," Mirabel said that night, in the rented room above the pie stall, and his voice was hollow. "What she described. That was a redaction. Keepers used to do them—for debts, for disputes, for the crown. Writing a man out of the record. Out of memory." He sat with the ledger on his knees and did not touch it. "I've read the entries. I never thought about the lamplighter's lamp."

"You were an apprentice," Sable said. "Apprentices learn the craft before they learn the cost. I sang for twenty years before I understood what my voice was taking out of the walls."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly the same, boy, and you know it, and feeling guilty about it is worth nothing to her. What's worth something is this:" Sable leaned forward. "She thinks the ledger only takes. So show her what it gives back. Show her what you showed me."

Mirabel went still. In the quarry town, he had read Sable's own erased history aloud—the career the crown had struck from record when she fled them, the reviews, the name of the teacher who had first called her voice *a force of nature*, restored to the page and therefore to the world. She had wept, though she'd told no one that. Some things a woman keeps.

"She won't let him near her," Elodie said from the window, where she kept her watch. The moon through the glass was waning, two turns left, maybe less. "And we don't have time to court her gently. The song needs five voices, Sable. We are four. Without her, the unbinding fails, and the thing in the crown comes off its leash with the whole court kneeling to it."

"Then we stop courting," Sable said, "and we trade."

She had been turning it over all day, the way you turn a stone in your hand, feeling its weight. She knew what the girl wanted. Not coin—coin was easy, and coin meant nothing to someone who could steal it with a whistle. Not kindness—kindness frightened children like that worse than cruelty, because cruelty they understood.

What she wanted was what they all wanted. What every one of them wanted.

*To be kept. To be remembered. To be, in someone's accounting, worth the ink.*

And Sable knew what she herself had that was worth more than coin, more than kindness, more than anything she'd carried out of her old life. She had a memory. One memory, out of all of them, that she kept like a coal in her cupped hands through every cold year since the quarry.

She told them at dawn.

"No," Mirabel said, flat and immediate.

"You don't get a vote, ledger-boy. It's mine to spend."

"It's your *mother*." His voice cracked on it. "Sable. The memory of her singing you to sleep. You told me it's the only thing from before the stage that you kept. The only thing the crown didn't touch."

"And it's the only coin I have that she'd believe." Sable stood at the window beside Elodie, watching the grey light come up over the harbor. "You think she'll trust a ledger that *writes*? After the lamplighter? She'll trust a ledger that *pays*. That gives something up. That bleeds a little, the way she's bled." She turned and met Mirabel's eyes. "You restored my history to me and I would walk into fire for you now, and you know it. That's the trade she'll understand. Not words. Price."

"And if she takes it and runs?" Elodie asked softly.

"Then I'll have paid a fair price for a fair try, and I'll have one less thing for the crown to take from me if they catch us." Sable smiled her theatre smile, and this time it hurt to wear it. "Besides. I've sung that lullaby ten thousand times in my head. The melody I keep. It's the sound of her voice I'd be trading. And voices—" she touched her own throat, where the stone-cracking note slept, "—voices are what I do. I'll make new ones."

* * *

They found her at noon on the rooftops above the fishmarket, because of course they did; a child who could still the wind could also call it, and she'd been leading them a merry chase across the city for a day, letting them see her and vanish, testing them. Sable went up alone. That was the terms she'd set, and the others—Mirabel grinding his teeth, Elodie with her terrible quiet eyes—had agreed.

The girl sat on a ridge of tiles with her back to a chimney, knees drawn up, watching Sable climb with the wariness of a gull watching a hand near its catch.

"You're persistent," Wren said.

"I'm an opera singer. Persistence is the entire profession." Sable settled onto the tiles across from her, tiles warm from the sun, the harbor spread out below all glitter and noise. "I've come to trade."

"I don't want your coin."

"I know. I'm not offering coin." Sable took a breath, and found—she was surprised to find—that it shook. "I'm going to tell you what I'm offering, and you're going to think I'm mad, and then I'm going to prove I'm not. There's a ledger. Not the guild's old one—the new one. Mirabel's. It can hold things. Keep things. Trade things. And it can take a memory out of a head and hold it, safe, the way you'd keep a jewel in a vault."

The slate eyes narrowed. "Why would anyone pay to lose something?"

"Because I'm paying it *to you*. As proof." Sable held her gaze. "You don't trust the book. You think it only eats. So I'm going to feed it my most precious thing, and you're going to hold the page, and you're going to keep it, and you're going to know—*know*, in your bones, the way you know the wind—that the ledger gave, and I gave, and neither of us took."

The girl was quiet a long moment. Below them, the fishmarket roared, and gulls wheeled, and the wind moved the harbor smells around like a hand stirring soup.

"What thing," Wren said at last, and her voice had lost its flatness. Curiosity, that oldest thief's instinct, was in it now.

"My mother's voice. Singing me to sleep." Sable swallowed. "I was four. She's been dead thirty years. It's the only memory I have of her that isn't a story I've told so often it's worn smooth. It's the real thing. Her actual voice. And I'm offering it to you to hold, because I want your note, Wren. The choir needs it. There's a king under a crown that's eating him, and there's a thing in that crown older than hunger, and we are the only four voices in the world that can sing it loose—and we need a fifth. We need the stillness. We need you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I've lost a memory and gained the knowledge that I offered the truest thing I had to a child who deserved the truth. That's not nothing." Sable reached into her coat and drew out the folded page Mirabel had prepared, and held it out across the tiles. "But you should know what you'd be holding. It's not a cage. It's a keeping. And keepings can be given back."

Wren did not take the page. She came closer instead—crawled across the warm tiles on her knees, close enough that Sable could see the wind bending gently toward her, the way it always did, like a plant toward light—and she looked into Sable's face with those slate eyes, searching for the lie. She was very good at it. Sable let her search. There was no lie to find.

"You'd really forget her voice," Wren said. "Forever? Even if I gave it back, you'd have to trust me to do it. You'd be giving a stranger on a roof the only—"

"Yes," Sable said. "That's what makes it a gift."

The wind stopped. All across the harbor, all across the city, the flags and the sails and the smoke and the sea-sounds—all of it stopped, one held breath, and in the stillness Sable heard the girl's heart, quick as a bird's, and heard beneath it something else: a single note, low and vast and patient, the note of cessation, the rest in the music of the world, waiting in a child's small body like a seed in frozen ground.

"Nobody's ever given me anything," Wren said, "that they didn't want back."

"I'm not giving it *to* you to keep forever. I'm giving it to show you what the book can be. What *we* can be." Sable reached out—slowly, the way you reach for a wild thing—and touched the girl's sharp little chin, and turned her face up. "You're an orphan. So am I, in every way that counts. So is Elodie, who was never a child at all. So is Oren, who gave his voice to a god that died. So is the choir itself, broken a hundred years and scattered like dropped coins. We are all of us orphans of the same song, Wren. And orphans—" her voice caught, and she let it, because this was the one performance of her life with no craft in it at all, "—orphans can make each other a family. If we choose it. If we *pay* for it. And I'm paying first."

Wren looked at the page a long time.

Then she took it, and held it in both hands like a bird, and said: "Show me how it works. And then—" her chin came up, and there were tears on her face now, and she was not hiding them, and Sable understood that this, too, was a payment, freely given, "—then you sing me something. So I know what I'm keeping you for."

* * *

The trade took less than a minute. Mirabel wrote; the ledger drank; and Sable felt the memory lift out of her like a thorn drawn from old flesh—no pain, only a sudden strange lightness, and a small blank place where the coal had been, and the melody of the lullaby still there, note-perfect, only the *sound* of her mother gone out of it, and she found she was crying without any grief at all, the way you cry at the end of a good performance.

Wren held the page to her chest and looked at Sable with something new in the slate eyes. Not suspicion. Not pity. Kinship.

"It's warm," she said wonderingly. "The page is warm. Like something alive."

"It is," said Elodie, from the rooftop door, where she and Mirabel and Oren had gathered without Sable noticing—orphans, all of them, drawn to the trade like the wind was drawn to the girl. "That's what it is, when it's done right. That's what we're going to be."

Wren looked at them all. Then she put two fingers to her lips, and whistled—and the wind came down off the harbor and circled the rooftop once, twice, gentle as a hand on a shoulder, and settled.

"The fifth note," she said. "Where are we going?"

And it was Mirabel who answered, and his voice was strange, and Sable turned to see him holding the ledger open to a page that had begun, all on its own, to bloom with fresh ink—ink that was not his hand, ink that was a century old, ink that spelled out words that made the blood run cold in the warm afternoon sun:

*The chapel. Before the thirteenth moon. All five, or none.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, still wet, as though someone were writing them at that very moment from somewhere very far away:

*They know you are coming.*
