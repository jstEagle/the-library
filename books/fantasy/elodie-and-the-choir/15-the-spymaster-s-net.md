## 15. The Spymaster's Net

They knew we were coming. The ink had told us so in letters a century old, and still we went—because the alternative was to sit in the dark and let the thirteenth moon rise over an unbound king, and I had already spent too much of my life sitting in dark rooms while impossible things happened without me.

We traveled by night and slept in ditches by day, which Wren declared "the most fun she'd had since the fire," and which Sable called, with the weary authority of a woman who had once sung for royalty, "a holiday compared to touring seasons." Oren walked without complaint, as he did everything, his bare feet finding purchase on wet stone where the rest of us slipped. Elodie walked beside me. She always seemed to find her way to my side without either of us deciding it, the way water finds the lowest ground.

The chapel lay three days east of the border ward, in a valley the maps had forgotten—or rather, in a valley the ledgers had *unremembered*, because when I checked our route against the guild's oldest atlas, the pages simply declined to show it. Blank parchment where hills should be. A cartographer's silence. The choir had been bound in a place that had been paid to disappear.

"They'll be waiting," Elodie said on the second night, as we crouched beneath a hedgerow watching the road below. She said it without fear, the way you might note that rain was coming.

"I know."

"You could turn back."

"Could I?" I looked at her. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, beautiful in the way of things not entirely made. "The prophecy says before the thirteenth moon. It doesn't say what happens if we're late. Prophecies never say, do they? They just let you imagine it."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "My imagination is very good. I wasn't given much else."

I reached out and found her hand in the dark. Her fingers were cool, and they closed around mine with a grip that was entirely, unmistakably alive.

* * *

The net closed on the morning of the third day, and it closed exactly as a spymaster's net should—not with soldiers bursting from trees, but with something far more elegant.

We had stopped at a roadside shrine to rest, one of the little stone alcoves that farmers maintained for luck. Wren was asleep against Sable's shoulder. Oren sat with his palms flat on the earth, listening to it, or perhaps comforting it. And I had the ledger open on my knees, checking the unremembered valley's coordinates one final time, when the ink on the page began to move.

Not blooming this time. *Draining.* The words I needed were pulling away from me like water down a plughole, letter by letter, until the page showed nothing but clean white paper—and then, in fresh black script that was most definitely not a century old:

*Good morning, apprentice.*

The air changed. I smelled it before anything else—cold iron and lamp oil and the particular staleness of men who have ridden hard through the night. Then the hedgerows moved, and they were simply *there*, a dozen of them in dun-colored cloaks, crossbows leveled, appearing out of the landscape the way fish appear when a pond goes still. No shouting. No commands. The silence itself was the ambush.

"Wren," Sable breathed, and her arm came around the girl like a gate swinging shut.

Oren rose. He did not raise his hands; he raised his *chest*, and I felt the vibration come off him like heat off a forge—that sub-audible hum that could crack stone, that had cracked stone in the flooded tunnels. The nearest agent took an involuntary step backward.

"Don't," I said quietly. "Please. There are too many, and some of them are just following orders, and—" I thought of the ledger's cold greeting. "And I think they want me alive."

A man stepped from behind the shrine. He wore no uniform at all, which was how I knew he was the one who mattered: a lean, gray gentleman with a scholar's stoop and a fencer's hands, carrying himself with the terrible mildness of people who have never once needed to raise their voice. He looked at the ledger in my lap the way Corvane used to look at the last bottle of bottled echo—with hunger wearing the mask of respect.

"Mirabel Iversen," he said. "Last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers. Do you know, I've read everything your guildmaster sold us, and do you know what struck me most? Not the miracles. Not the trades. The *marginalia*. All those neat little notes in a child's hand. *Entry verified. Balance confirmed. Paid in full.*" He smiled. "You've been keeping honest books your whole life, boy. That's going to make this very simple."

Crossbows did not waver. Wren woke, and Sable's hand covered her mouth before she could whistle, and Oren's hum died to nothing under the flat gray weight of the man's attention.

"The ledgers," the spymaster said. "All of them. And the woman who hums. And then everyone walks home."

* * *

They took the ledger first. I want to record—I need to record, even here, even now, in whatever corner of my mind remains mine—that I did not fight them. Four notes of the binding song stood in that shrine, and a dozen crossbows stood opposite, and mathematics is the first thing a Ledgerkeeper learns. I handed the book over with steady hands, and the spymaster received it with both of his, almost tenderly, the way a priest receives an offering.

Then they bound our wrists and hooded us, and put us in a wagon that rattled for hours, and when the hoods came off we were underground.

The cells beneath the spymaster's wing of the palace were not dungeon cells. That was the horror of them. They were *comfortable*—clean straw, wool blankets, a pitcher of water, a candle that burned without flickering. Someone had thought about what would break a person fastest and concluded it was not darkness but courtesy. Through the bars I could see the others: Sable pacing like a caged storm, Wren curled small and silent, Oren seated in perfect stillness with his eyes closed, listening for something in the stone. And Elodie, pressed to her bars, watching my cell with an expression I had never seen on her face before.

Fear. For me.

"They can't hold a song," she said softly. "Whatever they think they've caught, they haven't caught us. We're not five people, Mirabel. We're a chord. Chords don't fit in cells."

"Chords need throats," Sable said from across the corridor, "and throats need to not be full of crossbow bolts." But there was iron under her bitterness. She was already working the problem. They all were. Even Wren, feigning sleep, had her fingers moving in tiny patterns against the straw—counting, I realized later. Counting footsteps. Counting guards. Counting the intervals between the torch-passing. Even captured, we were rehearsing.

The spymaster came at what I judged to be midnight, alone, carrying two chairs and a small table, which he set up outside my cell with the unhurried care of a man arranging a dinner party. He poured wine into two cups. He slid one through the bars.

"I don't drink with crown agents," I said.

"I'm not offering it as a crown agent." He settled into his chair, folded his fencer's hands. "I'm offering it as a man who has read every secret your guild ever kept, and who has questions that keep him up at night. Sit, apprentice. Or stand. It changes nothing. What I have to say will change everything."

I stayed standing. He nodded as if I'd passed some test, and began.

"Do you know why the purge happened? Forty years ago. The one that killed your parents." My breath stopped in my throat, and he watched it stop, and continued gently, horribly gently. "Of course you don't. You were an infant. Corvane never told you—he never tells anyone anything true—but I have the reports. The crown didn't purge the Ledgerkeepers because they traded in impossibilities. The crown purged them because they *refused a trade*. One trade. A single contract, offered by a king on his deathbed, desperate beyond reason. And when the guild refused him, the thing that wears his descendant's crown made certain the refusal was punished."

"What trade?" My voice came out strange.

"He wanted to buy his death," the spymaster said. "He wanted the Ledgerkeepers to enter it in their books—a life, honestly ended, honestly paid for. Because he knew what waited inside his own crown. He had felt it waking. He wanted to die *before* it finished waking, while he was still the man holding the leash." He sipped his wine. "Your guildmaster's predecessors said no. Rules are rules. Death cannot be entered as a debt. And so the king died badly, and the thing got its teeth fully into the throne, and forty years of quiet obedience followed—including yours."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am about to offer you the trade your guild refused." He set down his cup. "The thing in the crown has power over what has ended. It cannot create life—it is only hunger—but it can *reach backward*. Into endings. Your parents, Mirabel. Ended for forty years, and yet not beyond the reach of something that has worn a dozen kings like gloves. Surrender the ledgers—all of them, every volume, every impossible thing your guild ever hoarded—and surrender the singer, and tomorrow night you will sit at a table with your mother and father. Breathing. Remembering you. Calling you by name."

The candle flame did not flicker. Nothing in that room flickered. Everything in me did.

I saw it—of course I saw it; I am a Ledgerkeeper, seeing the shape of a trade is the first thing we learn. I saw my mother's kitchen, which I did not remember but had reconstructed a thousand times from other people's descriptions of their mothers' kitchens. I saw my father's hands, ink-stained, turning a page. I heard my own name spoken in voices I had invented because no real ones survived. Forty years of orphanhood, undone. One signature. One book. One friend handed over to whatever the crown did with the last voice of the Choir of Binding.

"It's a lie," Elodie said through the bars. Her voice shook. "Mirabel, listen to me—the dead don't come back. Things that reach backward bring back *shapes*. It would wear them. Like it wears Aldric. You'd get your parents the way the court gets its king—hollow eyes and someone else's hunger looking out—"

"She may be right," the spymaster said, maddeningly calm. "Or she may be lying to keep you. That is the trouble with trades, isn't it? You never truly know the goods until payment clears." He leaned forward. "But consider, boy: I am not asking you to trust *me*. I am asking you to decide what you owe. To the dead who made you? Or to four strangers you've known a handful of weeks?"

And that was the question, laid bare and gleaming like a blade on a table. What did I owe?

I looked at Elodie. At her frightened, furious, *unfinished* face—at the woman who had no childhood because she had never been a child, who was more instrument than person and more person than anyone I had ever known, who had knocked twice on a dying guild's door because a century-old book told her to. Who had held my hand in the dark and whose grip had been the realest thing in my life.

I looked at Sable, who had traded her most precious memory for a thief child's trust, and who paced her cell now not in despair but in *calculation*, because Sable did not surrender, Sable performed.

I looked at Oren, silent as stone, whose stillness was not emptiness but a held note, sustained these many years, waiting for the rest of the chord.

I looked at Wren, small and fierce and counting footsteps in the straw, an orphan like the choir itself, who had chosen us in the space of a single collapsed tunnel.

My parents. My invented kitchen, my imagined hands, my dreamed voices. A family made of grief and guesswork, offered back to me by a thing that ate kings.

And here, now, behind these bars: a family made of choice.

"The guild refused that trade once," I said. My voice was steadier than I had any right to expect. "Forty years ago, with everything to lose, they looked at a dying king and said: some entries must remain blank. Everyone called it pride. I think it was mercy—they understood that a life bought back wrong is worse than a life honestly ended." I met the spymaster's eyes. "You're offering me my parents. But what you'd actually deliver is two hollow shapes wearing their faces, and I would spend whatever years I have left feeding them soup and pretending not to see the hunger looking out. I've seen the court. I know what your master's gifts look like when they arrive."

"Sentiment," the spymaster said softly. "The guild's fatal disease."

"No. *Ledgerkeeping.*" I stepped to the bars. "Every trade has a price, and every price must be entered honestly, and the honest entry on your offer reads thus: two lives returned, corrupted; one voice surrendered to destruction; one kingdom lost. That is not a bargain. That is a robbery with extra steps." I smiled, and I felt Elodie's startled breath catch across the corridor, because I had never smiled like that before, and neither had I. "You read our marginalia, sir. Did you notice what we write when a trade is refused?"

His eyes narrowed. "Tell me."

"*Declined. With thanks.*"

For a long moment the spymaster regarded me, and something moved behind his gray composure—something that might, in a better man, have been respect. Then he rose, gathered his chairs, and paused at the edge of the candlelight.

"A pity," he said. "The thirteenth moon rises in two nights. When it does, your friends will learn what obedience costs, and you will learn what refusal costs, and the difference between those lessons will be everything." He glanced back once. "Enjoy the blanket, apprentice. It's the last kind thing you'll receive from this office."

His footsteps faded up the stairs. The candle burned on, unwavering, unnatural.

In the silence, Elodie came to her bars, and Sable crossed to hers, and even Wren abandoned her pretense of sleep and pressed her face to the iron. Oren opened his eyes.

"Well," Sable said. "That was either the bravest or stupidest speech I've ever heard, and I performed at the Amber Court, so the competition was stiff."

"We're still in cells," Wren pointed out.

"We're still a chord," Elodie said. She was smiling now, fierce and bright in the candlelight. "And chords, Mirabel—what were you going to tell him? Finish it."

I looked down at my empty hands, at the place where the ledger's weight had lived against my palms for so long that I could still feel it, ghost-warm. And I smiled again, because for the first time since the ink had begun draining from the page that morning, I understood something the spymaster did not.

He had taken the book. He had not taken the keeper.

"When I refused him," I said slowly, working it out as I spoke, feeling it become true as the words left me, "he asked what we write when a trade is declined. Declined with thanks. But there's a second line, Elodie. There's always a second line. Every entry in every ledger ends the same way, in every hand, in every century of the guild."

Sable's eyes lit. "*Balance confirmed,*" she breathed.

"Paid in full," Wren whispered.

Across the corridor, Oren placed both palms flat against the stone floor, and deep beneath us, deeper than the cells, deeper than the foundations, the mountain itself gave the faintest answering hum—as though somewhere below, in the dark, the old chapel's stones had heard us coming, and had begun, patiently, to sing.

"And the balance," I said, "is about to shift."
