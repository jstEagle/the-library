## 16. Escape Written in Ink

Wren had been picking locks since she was six years old, which meant she'd been picking locks longer than most people had been alive to her. Not that anyone asked. That was the trouble with being small and quick and good with a bit of bent iron: people assumed the skill was a trick, or luck, or some adult's cleverness working through her fingers. Nobody assumed it was hers.

The lock on their cell was a Crown job, though. She could tell by the weight of it even before she got her hands on the door—thick brass, oiled, the tumblers cut by someone who took pride in their work. Spymaster's own cells, probably. The kind of lock that was built to insult you, all fat and confident, like a man who thought his money made him interesting.

Wren crouched in front of it and smiled at it the way you smile at an opponent across a dice table.

"All right," she whispered. "Let's see what you've got."

Behind her, in the dark of the cell, Mirabel was doing something much stranger than picking locks. He was writing.

She'd watched him do it once before, back at the quarry town, when he'd needed a door unbarred and there was no time to argue with the bar. He'd opened the ledger across his knees—the ledger, the one that weighed like a sleeping cat and hummed faintly when you touched it, like a hive of very polite bees—and he'd written three lines in ink that came out of the pen black and then dried silver, and the bar had simply lifted itself, courteous as a footman.

That was the part Wren couldn't stop thinking about. Not the magic of it. The manners of it.

Everything in her life had been taken by force or stolen by cunning. Doors were kicked in. Bread was snatched. Names were shouted after you down alleys. But the ledger didn't take anything. It *traded*. And watching Mirabel write in it was like watching someone haggle with the world itself—and win, because he knew what the world wanted.

"Tell me again what you're trading," Sable said from the corner, where she sat with her arms around her knees, keeping watch on the little barred window near the ceiling. Her voice was low, careful. In these cells, sound carried strangely; Wren had spent the first hour mapping every echo, learning where the guards' footsteps pooled and where they thinned. The corridor outside ran cold and damp, and twice each hour boots came down it, unhurried, bored. Bored guards were slow guards. Slow guards were late guards.

"The silence," Mirabel said. His pen scratched. "This place is quiet. Have you noticed? No rats. No drips. Even our voices don't echo right."

Wren had noticed. She noticed everything about rooms; it was how you stayed alive in them. The silence here wasn't empty silence. It was *kept* silence, like a purse kept under a floorboard.

"Someone's storing it," Elodie said softly. She sat closest to Mirabel, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and Wren saw how Mirabel wrote better with her there, steadier, the way a candle burns straighter out of a draft. "The spymaster hoards things. Silence would be worth a great deal to him."

"It's worth something to me," Mirabel said. "That's what matters. The ledger doesn't care who owns a thing. Only that it can be given." He paused, pen hovering. "It cares very much about fair trades. It's the only rule it has."

"And what are we buying?" Sable asked.

Mirabel turned and looked at them—all four of them, Wren crouched at the door with her picks, Brother Oren sitting perfectly still against the wall with his eyes closed and his palms flat on the stone (he did that a lot; Wren had figured out early that he was listening through the floor, feeling for vibrations the way a spider reads its web), Elodie with her hands folded, Sable coiled in the corner like a held breath.

"A way out," Mirabel said. "And time enough to reach the chapel."

*The chapel.* Everyone went quiet when the chapel came up, even Wren, and Wren didn't go quiet for anybody. But there was something about the word that made the air feel thin. Oren had shown them the murals' truth two nights ago, in the ruins above the river: the choir hadn't caged a demon. They'd caged a hunger—a god-king's terror of ending—and the cage had been twisted by theft and centuries into something crueler than either mercy or malice. Now the thing wore King Aldric like a coat worn inside out, and it was waking faster, and they had one turn of the moon left. Maybe less.

One turn. One song. Five voices.

Assuming five voices lived long enough to sing it.

The lock clicked its first tumbler, and Wren grinned.

"There you are," she murmured to it. "Thought you could hide that one, didn't you?"

* * *

Picking the spymaster's locks was not like picking a merchant's locks, and it was not even like picking the guild-hall locks, which had been old and fussy and full of personality, like cranky grandparents. These locks were *angry*. Every tumbler she lifted, another dropped behind it. She worked by touch alone, in the dark, with her ear pressed to the keyhole so she could hear the pins breathe.

"You're smiling," Elodie observed quietly. "Why are you smiling?"

"Because it thinks I'm going to rush," Wren said. "They always think that. Big fancy locks, they get smug. Smug locks make mistakes."

"You're nine years old."

"I'm ten next frostmonth," Wren said with dignity. "And I've been in more cells than you've sung notes, probably. Well. Maybe not. But close."

Elodie made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Wren had been cataloguing Elodie's almost-laughs for weeks now. There were four kinds. This was the startled kind, the one that escaped before Elodie remembered she wasn't sure yet whether she was allowed to find things funny.

Wren liked Elodie. She'd decided this on the second day, when Elodie had caught her stealing an apple from their shared supplies and, instead of scolding her, had simply handed her a second one without comment, as if abundance were obvious and hoarding was the strange behavior. Wren had spent her whole life among people who counted things. Elodie counted things too—you could see the arithmetic moving behind her eyes sometimes, the way she tilted her head—but she counted like someone who had once been a whole chorus and still remembered how it felt to have plenty of herself.

A boot fell somewhere far off. Wren froze, pick half-turned, and listened. Oren's hand rose from the floor—one finger, then flat again: *far, passing, gone.*

She went back to work.

The problem, she was discovering, was that the third tumbler was trapped. She could feel it: a false gate, a pin that would seat sweetly and then drop dead the moment she turned, snapping her pick and probably ringing some little bell somewhere that woke up twenty bored guards at once. Crown work. Spiteful as a tax collector.

"I need a minute," she announced to the room. "This one's got teeth."

"Then we give you your minute," Mirabel said, "and spend ours well."

He turned back to the ledger, open across his knees, and began to write, and Wren—between delicate, patient, murderous little movements of her pick—watched him over her shoulder.

He'd changed since she'd met him. That was another thing she'd been cataloguing, because cataloguing was how she loved things: quietly, precisely, without telling anyone. When she'd first seen him, in the quarry town, Mirabel Iversen had looked like a clerk pretending to be brave—shoulders hunched around the ledger like it was a shield, voice apologizing for itself. Now he sat in a cell in the spymaster's own dungeon with ink drying silver on his fingers, and when he wrote, he wrote the way Sable sang: like someone who knew exactly how much power he was holding and had decided, deliberately, to hold it gently.

"What does it feel like?" she asked him. She'd never dared ask before. Questions cost, usually. But they were all about to die together, probably, and dying people could afford generosity. "When you write in it. What does it feel like?"

Mirabel considered the question seriously, which was another thing about him she approved of. He never laughed off a real question.

"Like writing a letter to someone enormous," he said at last. "Who is reading over your shoulder. And who will absolutely hold you to every word."

"That sounds horrible."

"It sounds honest." He dipped the pen. "Everything else in my life lied politely. The ledger never lies. It just... waits to see if you mean it."

Wren turned that over while she worked the trapped pin. Mean it. That was the whole trick of everything, wasn't it? The merchants meant their ledgers. The crown agents meant their chains. The spymaster, whoever he was, whatever he served—he meant his cages. The world was full of people meaning terrible things, patiently, in neat handwriting.

Maybe that was why the ledger felt different. Same patience. Same neatness. Different heart.

The trapped pin gave—not to force but to finesse, a quarter-turn past where it wanted to sit, holding it there with the tension wrench balanced on a knife-edge of nothing. Sweat slid down Wren's spine. Somewhere below, Oren pressed both palms harder into the stone.

"Hold," he breathed, without opening his eyes. "Boots. Coming. Forty steps away."

"Can't hurry," Wren hissed. "I hurry, we all hang."

"We won't hang," Mirabel said, and there was something in his voice that made her look at him. He was standing now, ledger open on the cell's little shelf, pen moving in long sure strokes, and the silver ink was *glowing*, faintly, like moonlight soaked into paper. "Thirty seconds, Wren. Give me thirty seconds and the corridor will be too busy being silent to hear us."

"What's that supposed to—"

He finished the line. Drew a single stroke beneath it, the way a merchant closes a bargain, and spoke aloud, soft and clear:

"*Traded and witnessed.*"

The silence arrived.

Wren had no word for it. It wasn't that the dungeon went quiet—it had been quiet already. It was that the quiet became *heavy*, purposeful, like a held breath, like the moment between lightning and thunder stretched out and made to last. The forty-steps-away boots kept falling, but their sound fell into the new silence and sank, muffled, swallowed, gone. Even Wren's own heartbeat seemed to happen on sufferance.

And the lock, in front of her, sighed.

There was no other word for it. All its spite went out of it at once. The trapped pin settled home like a child finally agreeing to sleep, and the bolt drew back with a click so soft it was almost apologetic.

Wren stared at the open door.

"Okay," she said, into the enormous listening quiet. "Okay, that's the best trade I ever seen."

* * *

They went up through the dark like smoke finding a chimney.

Oren led. Of course he led; in the corridors of the spymaster's house, with its thick carpets and its guarded silences, the mute monk was king. He moved with his palms half-raised, reading the walls, and he signed his instructions in the finger-language he'd taught them all during the long road north—Wren was fastest at it, naturally, her hands being her best feature—and the others followed in the order they'd fallen into without discussing: Sable at the rear because she was the rearguard type and everyone knew it, Elodie in the middle where she could be protected, and Mirabel beside Elodie because nobody had ever needed to discuss that either.

Two guards stood at the stairhead. Oren raised a fist: *stop*. Then two fingers: *two men*. Then a slow tilt of his hand: *tired*.

Sable drifted forward. Wren watched her gather a note—not a song, barely a sound, a single low tone pitched so far down Wren felt it in her teeth rather than heard it—and lay it against the wall like a hand laid on a shoulder. The two guards swayed. Their eyelids drooped. One yawned enormously, and the other followed like an echo.

By the time they'd crossed the landing, both men were asleep standing up, gently propped against each other, dreaming of whatever tired guards dreamed of. Sable stepped past them without a glance.

"Show-off," Wren breathed.

"You're one to talk," Sable whispered back, and Wren glowed all the way up the stairs.

The house above them was the spymaster's town residence, all narrow windows and respectable brick, the kind of building that pretended hard to be nothing at all. They climbed through its bones—service stairs, linen passages, the dusty spine of the chimney stack—and everywhere Wren looked she saw the same thing: wealth arranged to look modest, power arranged to look like paperwork. Files behind false panels. A map of the capital with tiny glass beads pinned to it, glowing faintly, one bead for every person the spymaster watched. Wren counted eleven beads clustered in a ring around the old chapel district and felt her stomach drop through the floor.

"They know," she signed to Oren. "Look. Eleven watchers on the chapel."

Oren studied the map. His jaw tightened—the most expression she'd ever seen him spend at once. Then he tapped the beads, one by one, and frowned, and signed slowly: *These are old. Dust on the pins. He stopped watching the chapel recently.*

"Why would he stop?" Elodie whispered.

"Because he doesn't need to watch it anymore," Mirabel said grimly. "He knows we're coming. He let us be taken. This whole cell—" he gestured back down the way they'd come, "—this was a net, and we were meant to struggle in it until the twelfth turn passed."

Wren looked at him. "Then why'd the lock let me pick it?"

For a moment nobody answered. The silver-glow of the traded silence still hung in the air around them, thick and strange, and Wren realized they were all thinking the same thing: *because the ledger outranks him*. Because somewhere in the arithmetic of impossible things, a fair trade made in a dark cell outweighed a trap laid by a servant of a hungry god.

"Because hope cheats," Sable said finally, and pushed open the window onto the night.

* * *

The city at midnight was a country Wren owned outright.

She took point now, leading them across rooftops she'd mapped years ago in a former life, when rooftops had been her bedroom and her schoolhouse and her escape route all at once. The others followed her trustingly, which still astonished her. Grown-ups following her. A disgraced opera singer, a mute monk, a boy who wrote reality, and a woman who was somehow also a song—all of them stepping where she stepped, jumping where she jumped, trusting her small quick body to know the way.

She'd been the last of nothing her whole life. Last picked, last fed, last known. And now she was the *first*, the pathfinder, the one whose judgment the whole choir hung from, and she found she liked it so much it frightened her.

They crossed the river on the backs of the old bridge arches, dropping from stone to stone while the water muttered below. The moon rode high and thinned, one night shaved off full, and Wren caught Mirabel looking at it with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone counting down.

"One turn left," Elodie said quietly. She'd come up beside Wren on the arches, light on her feet for someone made of music. "You're not afraid of heights?"

"Wren doesn't have fear of heights," Wren said. "Fear of heights has Wren."

The almost-laugh again. The startled kind. Third time she'd earned it.

Below them, the chapel district spread out in its ruin: the old choir chapel where the binding had first been sung, sunk in weeds and memory, its tower broken like a snapped finger pointing accusingly at heaven. From this height Wren could see the streets around it, and she made herself look properly, because looking properly was her whole religion. Empty. Quiet. Too quiet, maybe—or maybe exactly as quiet as a place gets when the watchers have been called off because the prey is expected.

"He's waiting for us there," she said. "Isn't he. The spymaster. Or worse."

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"So we're running straight into it."

"Yes."

Wren chewed on that for the length of two rooftops. Then she shrugged. "Good. Hate waiting."

They descended into the chapel yard through the gap in the wall where the gate had rotted away, and the moment her feet touched consecrated ground, Wren felt it—a change in the air, like walking from a hot street into shade. Old singing soaked into the stones here. You couldn't hear it, exactly. You could only feel that the stones had once heard something enormous and had never quite gotten over it.

The chapel doors stood open.

Not broken. Not forced. *Open*, the way a host opens a door before the guest knocks.

Inside, in the moonlight that fell through the broken tower, something waited. Wren's hand went to her knife. Beside her she felt Sable inhale the deep breath that preceded cracking stone, felt Oren's palms turn outward, ready to shake the world apart.

And Mirabel stepped forward, ledger in his arms, and stopped dead.

On the altar, neat as an invitation, lay a single sheet of paper. And on the paper, in handwriting Wren recognized with a lurch—the cramped, careful, guilt-ridden hand she'd seen on letters in the guild hall—was written a single line:

*Mirabel. Come alone. I can still give them back to you. — C.*

The moon slid behind cloud. In the sudden dark, the paper seemed to glow, and somewhere far off—too far off to be real, too close to be denied—Wren heard the first thin note of a choir tuning itself, five voices where there should have been none, and the stones beneath her feet began, very softly, to remember how to sing.
