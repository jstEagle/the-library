## 17. The Broken Choir's History

Oren had not spoken in thirty-one years, and so he had learned to listen the way other men learned to fight—with his whole body, with everything he had.

The chapel spoke first.

It spoke through the soles of his feet as they crossed the threshold: a low, wounded hum rising out of the stone, out of the foundations, out of whatever lay beneath the ruined floor like a heart buried too deep to beat properly anymore. It spoke through the air, which tasted of old resin and colder things. It spoke through the dust that hung in the shafts of late-afternoon light, each mote trembling at a frequency Oren recognized the way a man recognizes his mother's voice.

*This place was built to hold a song,* the stone said. *And the song has been screaming for a hundred years.*

He stopped just inside the door and let the others pass him—Mirabel with the ledger clutched to his chest like a shield made of paper, Elodie moving as she always moved, as if the air parted for her out of old habit and old grief, Sable with her shoulders squared against a room she clearly did not trust, and Wren already up in the rafters somewhere, because Wren was always up in the rafters somewhere.

Oren remained where he stood. He pressed his palm flat against the doorpost.

And he listened.

* * *

They had come to the chapel because there was nowhere else to go. Two turns of the moon remained—Mirabel had done the arithmetic twice, then a third time, hoping the numbers would be kinder on repetition, and they were not—and every road forward ran through this ruin at the edge of the Weald, where the choir had been bound into being a century ago. Elodie had said it quietly, on the last night of travel, staring into the fire: *If I was made anywhere, I was made here.*

Oren had felt the truth of it before she finished speaking. Truth, in his experience, arrived in the body before it arrived in the mind—a tightening behind the sternum, a ringing in the teeth. His whole life in the monastery had been an education in listening to what the world said before it said anything at all.

Now the chapel was saying a great deal.

The nave stretched ahead of them, roof half-collapsed, stars already pricking through the gaps where slate had fallen. But the walls—the walls were whole, and the walls were covered.

Murals. Floor to vault, pillar to pillar, painted in pigments that had faded to the colors of old bruises: ochre gone brown, lapis gone grey, vermilion gone the color of dried blood. A hundred years of candle smoke had softened every edge until the figures seemed less painted than dreamed.

Elodie stopped in the middle of the nave and went very still.

"Oren," Mirabel whispered, though there was no need for whispering; the chapel swallowed sound the way sand swallowed rain. "Can you—do you understand these?"

Oren did not answer with his hands, as he usually would. Instead he walked to the nearest wall and laid both palms against the plaster, and let himself read.

Because that was the thing about murals no one remembered: they were written by people who needed to say something too large for words. And Oren had spent thirty-one years living in the country of the wordless. He could read a wall the way Mirabel read a ledger.

* * *

The first panel showed a king.

Not Aldric—an older king, broader, bearded, crowned with something simpler than the iron-and-gold monstrosity Oren had seen in Mirabel's scrying visions. This crown was thin, almost humble, a circlet of dark metal set with a single stone. The king stood at the center of the panel with his arms open, and around him the painter had rendered his court in exquisite detail: lords and ladies, soldiers, priests, children. Every face was turned toward him. Every face was weeping.

Above the king, where a lesser artist might have painted heaven, the muralist had painted hunger.

It was difficult to describe even in the privacy of Oren's own mind, and he had a great deal of privacy these days. It was not a shape so much as a *pulling*—a darkness rendered not as shadow but as absence, a place where the pigment itself seemed to have been drawn thin, as though the thing depicted had been drinking the paint. It reached down toward the king with tendrils that were not quite tendrils, and the king did not recoil from it.

The king leaned *toward* it.

Oren felt Sable come to stand beside him. She said nothing—she had learned, over their weeks together, that silence was not emptiness—but she exhaled slowly through her nose, the way she did when something hurt to look at.

"Is that it?" she murmured. "The thing in the crown?"

Oren shook his head once. Then he took her hand—not for comfort, but because Sable thought best with her fingers, tapping rhythms against her thigh when she was working through a problem—and pressed her palm to the wall beside his own, over the painted king's chest.

There, only visible under the touch, worn nearly smooth by some ancient devotee who must have traced it ten thousand times: a second figure inside the king's outline. Smaller. Curled. A man-shaped hollow, like the space left behind when a statue is removed from its mold.

Sable's breath caught. "That's the king," she said. "The real one. That's—" She looked again at the reaching darkness above. "Then what's *that?*"

Oren closed his eyes and listened harder.

* * *

The murals told it in order, and Oren walked the order slowly, and the others followed him because they had learned to follow him without being asked.

The second panel: the king older now, the hunger larger, wrapped around him not like a predator but like a shawl, like something cherished. The court had thinned. Some of the painted faces wore expressions Oren recognized from Mirabel's descriptions of the capital—the glassy calm, the pleasant hollowness. The hunger had begun to feed on more than its host.

The third panel: the king alone, kneeling in a chamber that Oren understood, from the architecture, was this very chapel when it was new. The crown lay on the floor before him. The hunger rose out of the crown like smoke from a snuffed candle, vast now, filling the upper reaches of the painted vault. And the king—

The king had his hands folded. His head bowed. His mouth open.

Begging.

"He asked for it." Mirabel's voice cracked across the nave, high and young and horrified. He had reached the same panel, ledger forgotten in his arms. "Look at him—he's *praying.* He came here and he *asked.*" He looked up at Oren, and his eyes were wet. "Everyone thinks the choir imprisoned a demon. The guild records call it a binding, a caging, a—I grew up on stories of the demon-king, the monster in the crown. But he *asked.*"

Oren nodded. And because nodding was not enough—not tonight, not for this—he raised one hand and pressed it flat against the stone floor, and let the chapel's own hum rise through his arm, through his ribs, through the great bell of his chest, and gave them the closest thing he had to speech: a single sustained tone, low and mournful, the note a cello makes when it remembers funerals.

The dust in the light-shafts trembled. Somewhere in the rafters, Wren went quiet.

The tone said what Oren could not: *I know why he begged. I have knelt in that chamber myself. Not this one—the one in my monastery, the one with the hard wooden bench, the night after they cut the words out of me, when I prayed to a god I no longer believed in to take away the thing I could not stop wanting.*

Every man kneels before his hunger eventually. The question the murals asked was only: what do you do when it will not die?

* * *

The fourth panel answered it.

The king, still kneeling, still begging—and descending upon him, robed and barefoot, seven figures. The choir. Painted at the height of their power, and Oren felt his breath go shallow, because he knew those robes. He wore their descendants every day. Grey wool, undyed, the hem embroidered with a single line of notation.

Seven singers. Seven notes. And between them, held like a net of light, the hunger—no longer vast, no longer filling the vault, but gathered, concentrated, *sung into smallness*, pressed down and down and down until it fit inside the crown's single dark stone.

"They didn't cage it," Elodie said. Her voice was strange—flat, careful, the voice of someone reading their own autopsy. She stood beneath the panel with her face tilted up, and the fading paint of the seven singers seemed to arrange itself around her, echoing her stance, her angle, her stillness. "They *compressed* it. They sang it small. They sang it—" She stopped. Started again. "They sang it into a shape that could be carried."

"And carried by whom?" Sable asked, though her tone said she already knew.

The fifth panel answered anyway. The king, freed, gaunt, weeping with what looked like gratitude, kneeling before the seven while one of them lifted the crown from the floor. And behind that panel, in the sixth, the succession: the king enthroned again, older, the crown returned to his brow—returned *willingly*, the painter had made that unmistakable, the king's own hands raising it, the hunger sealed in the stone and leashed by the song that lived in the court itself, in the acoustics of the throne room, in the seven voices kept close to the king for the rest of his reign.

A maintenance dose. Oren had administered enough of them in the monastery infirmary to recognize the shape of it. The binding was never meant to be permanent. It was meant to be *renewed*, nightly, weekly, for as long as the king lived—and then the crown should have been buried with him, sung shut, sunk in consecrated ground.

The seventh panel showed what happened instead.

The king dead on his bier. The seven voices scattered—fleeing, the painter showed, fleeing men with swords and torches bearing the sigil of a house Oren did not know but Mirabel clearly did, because beside him Mirabel made a small wounded sound and said, "Aldric's house. That's Aldric's founder. That's—" He swallowed. "His ancestor stole it. He stole the crown at the funeral."

And the eighth panel, the last, the largest, occupying the entire eastern wall above the shattered altar:

The theft. The crown passing from dead king's head to living thief's. And the hunger inside the stone, no longer held by seven voices, no longer renewed, no longer sung small—beginning, across a century of neglect, to grow back.

The painter had shown it honestly. Not as a demon. Not as evil. As a *thirst*. A thing shaped like want, expanding year by year in the dark of the stone, learning patience, learning the taste of courts, learning to reach through iron and gold and find the soft places in ambitious men. In the final corner of the mural, the thief-king's descendant sat enthroned with the crown upon his head, and his painted eyes were wide and wet and terrified, and his mouth was open in a scream the painter had dared to render truthfully, because the truth was:

*Aldric is in there. Aldric has always been in there. Watching through his own eyes while something else smiles with his mouth.*

* * *

They stood in the nave a long time while the light died and the stars thickened overhead through the broken roof. Wren came down from the rafters at last and stood before the seventh panel, arms crossed, studying the fleeing singers with the expression of a child memorizing a route.

"So the choir broke," she said, "because men with swords broke it."

"The choir broke," Elodie said quietly, "because the crown was stolen and the singing stopped mattering to anyone in power. Swords were just how it ended." She touched her own sternum, the gesture Oren had seen her make a dozen times without understanding, and now he understood it completely: she was touching the place where seven voices had been pressed together and poured into the shape of a woman. "I'm not the last singer of the choir. I'm the *residue*. What was left when the song was interrupted mid-note."

"You're the fifth part of the unbinding song," Sable said flatly. "Residue doesn't carry a fifth part."

"No," Elodie agreed. "Something else does that."

Oren watched Mirabel look at her, and look away, and look back, and say nothing, and understood that the boy had begun to suspect what the prophecy's arithmetic meant, and was not yet ready to speak it aloud. There would be time—or there would not. That was the nature of deadlines.

But Oren was not thinking about the fifth part. Oren was standing before the fourth panel, looking at the seven singers, and thinking about mercy.

Because here was the thing the murals said that no guild record had ever said, the thing the stories of the demon-king had buried under a century of comfortable horror: the seven had not hated the thing they bound. Look at their faces in the paint—look closely, past the fade, past the smoke. They were not grim. They were *grieving*. They sang the hunger small the way you sing a fever down, the way you sit with a dying man through the night not because you can save him but because no one should end alone in the dark.

The hunger was not a demon. It was a god-king's terror of ending, grown monstrous from ten thousand years of refusal. It was want, unaccompanied. It was loneliness with power.

And you do not kill loneliness. You cannot starve it or burn it or cut it out. Oren knew this better than any man alive. Thirty-one years of silence had taught him exactly what hunger does when you try to destroy it instead of answering it: it grows. It finds softer hosts. It learns to wear crowns.

You answer it. That was all. You answer it, or you spend eternity feeding it.

"Oren." Mirabel had come to stand beside him, following his gaze up to the grieving singers. "What are you seeing?"

Oren considered how to answer. Then he knelt, laid his palm against the cold floor, and struck the stone once with the heel of his hand—a sharp percussive crack that rang through the whole chapel, through the pillars, up into the vaults, and came back changed, came back carrying harmonics the chapel had been holding in its bones for a hundred years, waiting for someone to ask.

The hum resolved. Briefly—only briefly, only for those listening with their whole bodies—it became music. Fragments of the original binding song, stored in the architecture like breath held in lungs: seven interlocking lines, braided, each one incomplete without the others, each one *asking* something of the darkness it held. Not *stay down*. Not *be silent*.

Asking: *you are not alone in there. We are holding you. Endure.*

Sable heard it and her jaw tightened. Wren heard it and went pale. Mirabel heard it and pressed the ledger to his chest as though it had grown hot.

And Elodie heard it and began, silently, to weep—because it was her song, the song she was made of, and she had never known until this moment that it was not a weapon.

It was a lullaby.

* * *

Later—when the fire was lit in the shelter of the apse, when Wren slept curled against Sable's side, when Mirabel sat apart with the prophecy book open on his knees and his lips moving over its arithmetic like a man counting the steps down into deep water—Oren sat beside Elodie at the edge of the firelight, beneath the great eastern mural, and did what he could.

He rested his hand on the stone between them and sent a slow pulse through it, gentle as a heartbeat, and she leaned slightly until her shoulder touched his, and they watched the painted seven keep their vigil over the painted hunger.

"It begged," Elodie said at last, very softly. "The king begged, and they answered him with kindness, and a hundred years later his heir's heir wears the cage as jewelry and calls the prisoner a demon." She turned her head. Her eyes reflected the fire. "Oren. If the enemy is hunger—if it's just *wanting*, wanting so big it eats kingdoms—then killing it isn't the answer. And caging it was only ever a delay." Her voice dropped further. "So what *is* the answer?"

Oren looked at her for a long moment.

Then he took her hand, opened it, and pressed it flat against the chapel floor—and struck the stone once more, and let her feel what the walls had shown him: the binding song's true final verse, the one no record preserved, the one the seven had sung only among themselves. The verse that was not a command at all.

An invitation.

*Come out. Come out, and be held. You do not have to want forever.*

Elodie stared at him. Beyond the fire, Mirabel looked up sharply from the prophecy book, his finger frozen on a line of text, his face draining white in the firelight—as if the ledger had just shown him the price of the final verse, and whose name the price would be written in.

"Mirabel?" Elodie said. "What is it? What did you find?"

The boy did not answer. He only closed the book, very gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead—and outside, far to the east, over the towers of the capital, the moon began to rise: swollen, silver, and two turns from full.
