## 18. Rehearsal Under a Waning Moon

The chapel had no roof worth the name. What remained of it arched overhead like the ribs of some enormous drowned creature, and through the gaps the waning moon poured down in slanted silver bars, striping the broken flagstones with light and shadow in equal measure. Elodie stood at the center of the crossing—the place where the murals said five voices had once stood—and felt the stone beneath her feet humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums long after the sound has left the air.

Four notes. Four voices. Two turns of the moon.

She counted them again as she watched the others settle into their places among the rubble, because counting was what she did when fear came knocking. Sable stood near the shattered font, arms crossed, her ruined opera cloak drawn close around her shoulders despite the mild night. Brother Oren knelt on the worn steps of the altar, palms flat against the stone, reading its vibrations the way other men read letters. Wren perched atop a fallen column like a small dark bird, whittling something with a stolen knife, pretending not to care about any of this and fooling no one.

And Mirabel—Mirabel was at the far wall with the ledger open across his knees, tracing lines of ink by moonlight, his lips moving silently as he worked through the old notation for perhaps the hundredth time since they'd fled the spymaster's cells.

Elodie knew the song lived in her the way blood lives in a body. She didn't remember learning it; she *was* it, or a piece of it, the composed remnant Oren had named her in the mountain silence of his monastery. When she opened her mouth, the first note came out whole and perfect, and it frightened her every time, how easily it came, how little she had to do to earn it.

"Again," she said, and her voice carried through the broken chapel without effort. "From the third measure."

Sable sighed theatrically, but Elodie heard the truth underneath the sigh: the singer was afraid too. Her voice could crack stone, and the unbinding song asked her to hold that cracking power back, to keep it coiled inside a single sustained tone like lightning held in glass. The first two attempts tonight, she'd come within a hair's breadth of bringing the west wall down on all of them.

They took their places. Oren pressed his hands to the floor. Wren tucked the knife away and lifted the bone whistle to her lips. Sable drew a breath that seemed to pull the very air of the chapel toward her. And Elodie let the song rise out of her, the first note unfurling into the moonlight like a ribbon of silver smoke.

For eleven measures, it was beautiful.

Then the fifth part came due, and there was nothing there to sing it.

It happened the same way every time. The four notes braided together, climbed toward the place where the harmony demanded completion, strained against an absence shaped exactly like a voice—and collapsed. Sable's tone frayed into a snarl. Wren's whistle died in a squeak. Oren's hands flew up from the stones as if scalded. The silence afterward rang worse than any discord, a silence with a hole in the middle of it, and Elodie stood in the wreckage of it feeling the absence ache in her own chest, because the song was hers, and the song was incomplete, and she did not know why.

"That's it," Sable said flatly, rubbing her throat. "That's the fourth time. I'm not doing it again until someone tells me what we're missing, because I'll tell you plainly—I can feel where the note should go. It's like singing into a missing tooth. The whole chord wants to fall into that gap."

"It's written," Mirabel said quietly.

He hadn't looked up from the ledger. Elodie crossed the broken nave toward him, stepping over a fallen saint whose face the centuries had eaten away. "What's written?"

"The fifth part." He turned the ledger so the moonlight fell across the page. The old notation crawled there in brown-black ink, five staves stacked one above another, and four of them were dense with marks—but the fifth, the lowest, held only a single line of words beneath an empty staff, written in a hand steadier than any of the others. He read it aloud, though she could see he already had, many times, hoping the meaning might change:

*The fifth voice is the keeper of the book.*

The words sat there. The moon moved a fraction behind cloud. Somewhere out in the dark hills, a nightjar called twice and went quiet.

"No," Mirabel said.

"You haven't even—" Elodie began.

"I know what you're going to say." He closed the ledger, gently, the way he handled everything in it, as if the pages might bruise. "You're going to say it's obvious. You're going to say the prophecy named me, that I found the date, that I've been walking toward this since the night the book fell open on my table. I have read that line forty times, Elodie. I have checked the notation against three different cipher-keys in the guild archives. It doesn't mean a singer. It means a keeper. It means—" His voice caught, and he mastered it the way he mastered everything, with that terrible competent loneliness of his. "It means whoever holds the ledger when the song is sung has to be the fifth voice. And the only keeper left is me."

"And?" Wren said, from her column. "You can whistle, can't you? Everyone can whistle."

"Wren." Sable's voice was soft, which from Sable was more alarming than shouting.

"He's being dramatic," Wren said, but she'd stopped whittling. Her eyes, quick and dark, flicked between them. "He's been dramatic for days. Ever since the cells. Ever since he started reading ahead in the prophecy book instead of just the parts we need." She hopped down from the column, landed lightly, and stalked over to stand in front of him, a child barely taller than his elbow glaring up at a grown man. "What does the last page say, ledger boy?"

Mirabel's jaw tightened. For a moment Elodie thought he would refuse—he was very good at refusing, she had learned, especially when refusal was the kindest thing he knew how to do. But then his shoulders dropped, and he opened the ledger again, past the notation, past the murals' history transcribed in Oren's careful hand, to the final leaf of the prophecy itself.

He didn't read it aloud. He simply turned it toward them.

Elodie leaned close. The ink was old, a century old, and yet it seemed to shift in the moonlight the way water shifts, the way living things shift. Beneath the line about the thirteenth moon and the unbinding, a final passage waited in the same steady hand:

*And the keeper shall give his name to the book, and the book shall keep it, and the world shall forget him sang and unsung, and this is the price of the fifth voice: that it be paid entire.*

The nightjar called again. No one spoke.

"That's why the staff is empty," Mirabel said. His voice was very level, which Elodie had learned meant it was holding something terrible flat, the way you press down a lid. "A name sung into the ledger isn't a performance. It's a trade. That's what the guild always was—we didn't record impossible things, we *traded* them, and every trade costs. The song needs a fifth voice, and the fifth voice is a name given away forever. Whoever sings it stops existing. Not dies. *Stops having existed.* The guild records will show no apprentice named Mirabel Iversen. The purge that killed my parents will show no orphan left behind. You four will walk out of that court with a freed king and no memory of why you went, or who sent you, or—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Or who I am."

"Don't be stupid," Wren said immediately, but her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she looked away, furious at herself.

"It's the arithmetic," Mirabel said, gentler now, as if explaining to a child, which was the cruelest thing he could have done. "I've done the sums, Wren. The binding fails on the thirteenth turn. The song requires five. There are four of you. There is no version of the numbers where I don't—"

"There's no version of the numbers," Sable said, cutting across him with a voice that made dust sift from the rafters, "where you get to decide this alone. Sit down, apprentice."

To Elodie's astonishment, he sat. Perhaps it was the voice. Perhaps it was that Sable had once been famous enough that people obeyed her on instinct, and some fossil of that authority still lived in her throat.

* * *

They argued until the moon had crossed half the sky.

Sable argued that there must be another way—that the choir had bound a god-king's hunger with five voices once before, and bindings could be re-tied, re-cut, re-sung, that somewhere in the ledgers' million entries there was a loophole, a trade, a price that wasn't a whole human life. Oren could not speak, but he wrote, his finger tracing characters in the dust of the altar step, and what he wrote was this: *The original binding cost the choir everything. Five voices became none. One voice became Elodie. Do not mistake survival for mercy.* Wren argued that they should steal the crown off Aldric's head in the night and throw it in the sea, and when Sable pointed out that the hunger lived in the king, not the metal, Wren argued harder, and it was clear to everyone she was arguing because arguing was easier than crying.

And Elodie—Elodie stood apart, near the mural of the kneeling god-king with his open hands, and thought about what it was to be made of a song.

She remembered—no. That was the wrong word. She *had* the memory the way a book has a story printed in it. The choir breaking apart, the five voices pouring themselves into a single vessel rather than let the binding fail, the moment of composition that was the nearest thing to a birth she'd ever had. She had never been a girl. She had never been a child. She was a chord that learned to walk, a harmony wearing a woman's shape, and for a long time that knowledge had been a cold stone in her chest.

But Mirabel had shown her a blank page once, in the mountains, and told her unwritten things could still be written. And she had believed him. She was still believing him.

So when the argument wore itself down to exhausted embers, she walked to where he sat with the ledger in his lap like a shield, and she knelt in front of him, and she said, "Sing me your name."

He blinked. "What?"

"Not into the book. Just—to me. Right now. Out loud." She kept her voice steady. "You've heard all four of us. Sable's note, Oren's, Wren's, mine. We've never heard yours. Not truly. So sing it, Mirabel Iversen, and let us hear what it is we're fighting to keep."

"I can't sing. I'm a ledgerkeeper. I catalog things. I balance accounts. My voice is—" He gestured helplessly at his own throat.

"Your voice," Elodie said, "is the one the prophecy has been waiting a century to hear. Sing."

The others had gone still. Even Wren. The chapel held its breath, roofless under the waning moon, and Mirabel Iversen looked around at the four of them—at the disgraced singer and the mute monk and the thief-child and the composed remnant of a broken choir—and Elodie watched him understand, slowly and terribly, that he was not alone in this, that he had not been alone since the night a stranger knocked twice on the guild door, and that this was precisely what made the price unbearable.

He closed his eyes. He breathed the way Sable had taught them all to breathe, low and slow, from the floor of the self.

And he sang his name.

It was not a beautiful voice. It was thin and untrained and it wavered on the long vowels, and it cracked once in the middle, and none of that mattered at all, because the moment the sound left him, the chapel *answered*. The humming in the stones rose to meet it. The empty fifth staff on the ancient page seemed to darken, as if ink were blooming into it from beneath the parchment. The moonlight thickened. Elodie felt the song in her own chest lean toward him like a plant toward sun, the four completed notes straining toward the fifth, and for three heartbeats—three perfect, impossible heartbeats—the unbinding chord hung complete in the ruined air, whole, finished, *ready*, and it sounded like the first morning of the world.

Then he stopped, gasping, and the harmony folded away, and everyone was ordinary again in the silver-striped dark.

"Do you feel it?" Elodie whispered. "Do you feel what your voice does?"

"I feel terrified," Mirabel said.

"Good," said Sable, and there were tears standing bright on her cheeks, though her voice was iron. "Terrified is honest. Now listen to me, ledger boy, and listen well, because I've sung on stages where kings wept and I know what I'm talking about. A voice given freely to a song is worth ten taken by force. The prophecy says the price is your name. Fine. Then we change the terms. You taught us that yourself—you wrote our escape into a prison wall with a bribe reality couldn't refuse. So we don't sing your name *alone*. We rehearse until the five of us are one instrument, and when the thirteenth moon rises, we don't pay your name to the book." Her eyes swept them all, Sable and Oren and Wren and Elodie, gathering them like a conductor's baton gathers a chorus. "We pay it *together*. All five names, braided into one entry. Let the ledger try to erase that. Let it try to untangle a chord."

Oren's finger moved in the dust: *A shared trade. The guild's oldest law. No entry may take more than it is given—and if five give as one, the book must return as much.*

"A new guild," Wren said slowly, working it out, her knife forgotten in her hand. "Written while it's being sung."

Elodie reached out and took Mirabel's hand. It was trembling. So was hers. She laced her fingers through his anyway, and she felt the tremor pass between them and become, somehow, steadiness.

"You told me unwritten things can still be written," she said. "So write yourself a different ending. With us. In our handwriting, not the prophecy's."

Mirabel looked at their joined hands, at the four faces ringed around him in the moonlight, at the empty fifth staff waiting on its century-old page. Something in his expression broke—not the way stone breaks, but the way ice breaks on a river in spring, releasing the current underneath.

"All right," he said hoarsely. "All right. From the top, then. All five parts."

They took their places in the crossing of the ruined chapel, five points of a star the builders had carved into the floor a thousand years ago and no one had understood until now. Elodie raised her chin. Sable filled her lungs. Oren laid his palms on the singing stone. Wren lifted the whistle. And Mirabel, keeper of the book, opened the ledger to the empty staff and drew a breath.

Far to the east, beyond the hills, beyond the border wards, the capital lay sleeping under the same waning moon—and in the highest tower of the palace, something ancient and hungry felt five voices gather in the dark, and began, at last, to be afraid.
