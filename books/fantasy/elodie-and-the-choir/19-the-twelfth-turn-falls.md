## 19. The Twelfth Turn Falls

The moon had been dying for eleven nights, and we watched it die.

From the broken arch of the old choir chapel we could see it through the missing roof—a thinning silver coin, each night a little more gnawed away, as though the sky itself were being spent to pay for what was coming. Wren said it looked like a fingernail clipping. Sable told her that was the least romantic thing anyone had ever said about the moon, and Wren beamed as if she'd been given a medal.

I did not laugh. I had not laughed in three days.

The chapel sat in a fold of hills where the road forgot itself—blackened rafters open to the weather, walls of pale stone furred with moss, and everywhere, everywhere, the murals Oren had shown us: singers with their throats painted gold, a kneeling king offering up his crown like a wound, and above them all a vast face with no mouth, only hunger where the mouth should have been. The painters had understood something we were only beginning to. They had left the hungry face unfinished, because some things cannot be finished, only survived.

We rehearsed by candlelight among the fallen pews. Elodie's voice carried the ground of the song, low and patient as roots; Sable's note split the air above it like a blade of light; Oren hummed through his chest and the stones themselves answered, a vibration you felt in your teeth rather than heard; Wren whistled her stillness into the spaces between, and even the wind outside would pause to listen. And beneath them all, written for a fifth voice that had never yet been sung—the keeper's part. My part. A line of music that asked, at its final measure, for a name.

I had not told them how much it frightened me. I think they knew anyway. Elodie knew everything anyway; she was made of listening.

On the twelfth evening, the candles went out.

Not blown out. *Taken*—each flame plucked from its wick at once, the way a hand closes over a moth. The dark that rushed in behind them was not ordinary dark. It had texture. It had *appetite*.

"They know," Elodie breathed beside me. I felt her hand find my sleeve in the blackness. "Mirabel—they know where we are."

I already had the ledger out. My fingers found the page by touch, the way they always did now, the book warm as a sleeping animal against my palms. "How long?"

"The song needs the moon at its thinnest. Tomorrow night." Her voice was steady, but I could hear the chord beneath it trembling, the way a struck bell trembles after the sound seems gone. "They will come tonight."

She was right. She was always right, being made of the choir's last wisdom, and I hated her for it in the small, loving way one hates a truth.

Far down the valley, where the road bent toward the capital, lights kindled. Not torchlight—torchlight flickers, breathes, belongs to human hands. These lights burned white and unwavering, dozens of them, then hundreds, pouring along the road like milk spilled across black glass. The court of hollow eyes, coming for us. Every courtier whose gaze had gone smooth and glassy as river stones. Every servant of the thing that wore Aldric's crown, sent to snuff five voices before they could become a song.

"Wren," I said. "The back ways."

"Already moving." She was a shadow detaching from shadows, her whistle already shaping itself, soft as breath. "Oren, with me. There's a crypt door under the east wall—old, older than the chapel. We can hold them there a while."

"A while," Sable repeated. She rolled her shoulders like a woman preparing to lift something enormous, which in a sense she was. "Define *a while*, ledger boy."

"Until the moon sets," I said. "Then we sing at dawn's edge, when the thirteenth turn begins. One night. We need to give them one night of our lives."

Sable smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman whose voice could crack stone, aimed at an approaching army. "One night. I've sung longer encores."

* * *

They came at midnight exactly, because things that wear crowns love precision; it is the closest they come to reverence.

The first wave reached the chapel yard and stopped, all together, like puppets whose strings had snagged. In the moonless dark their eyes gave them away—hundreds of pairs, catching the starlight flat and dull, reflecting nothing. No fear in them. No anger. That was the worst part. Anger would have meant they were still people underneath. These were faces with the people scooped out, vessels walking, and every one of them had once been someone's daughter or son or friend before the crown drank them hollow.

"Do not kill them," Elodie said quietly. It was not a request. "They are not the enemy. They are the evidence."

"I know." I stood at the chapel door with the ledger open across my forearms, ink-stained fingers on a fresh page. "But knowing and stopping are different trades."

The first courtier crossed the threshold of the yard.

Wren whistled.

It was barely a sound—a thread of nothing, a held breath given shape—and the wind died across the whole valley. The courtiers' cloaks hung limp. The dust they raised fell straight back down. Even the stars seemed to hold still. And into that absolute stillness Sable sang.

Her note went through the yard like a hammer through ice. The front rank of the court folded—not broken, but *stopped*, knees buckling, bodies sitting down hard in the frost-killed grass as though their bones had briefly forgotten their duty. Oren's vibration followed, rolling up through the earth itself, and the chapel's ancient foundation stones answered with a groan that shook the yard. The second wave stumbled. The third hesitated.

And I wrote.

That is the truth of what I did that night, and I set it down here plainly: I opened the guild's ledger and I bargained with reality, trade by trade, line by line. *The chapel doors' rot for an hour of iron.* The doors hardened under my pen, black oak turning to metal grain by grain. *The silence inside these walls for the strength of the singers within.* I felt the price arrive immediately—a grayness creeping into my hearing, the world going slightly muffled, the sound of my own heartbeat dimming. The ledger always collected. Corvane had taught me that in my first year, tapping the margin of an entry with one long finger: *Nothing is recorded without cost, apprentice. Remember that the cost is also recorded.*

I wondered, writing in the dark while the court hammered at our doors, whether he had ever imagined his lesson used like this.

They broke the first door near two in the morning. It took forty of them throwing themselves against it in perfect unison, silent as tide, and when it gave, the sound of the splintering was the loudest thing I had heard in hours—which was not saying much, through the grayness the ledger had taken from me.

We fell back to the nave. The murals watched from the walls, gold-throated singers frozen mid-hymn, and I thought: *you bound him once with five voices, and here we are, four and a half, hiding behind your paint.*

Elodie stood in the center of the nave with her arms slightly out from her body, and she was singing—not the unbinding song, not yet, but a holding-chord, a single sustained tone that filled the ruined roofline like water fills a cup. Where it touched the courtiers they slowed. Some of them wept without knowing why, tears sliding down glassy faces, and I understood: somewhere deep beneath the hollowing, the choir's voice still lived in them, remembering. You cannot fully scoop out a person. Something always pools at the bottom of the vessel.

It bought us time. Time was the only coin we had left, and we were spending it like drunkards.

By the third hour of the morning they had taken the transept. By the fourth, the crypt door Wren had held lay in pieces, and she came scrambling up the stairs with Oren behind her, her lip split, her eyes furious and bright. "There's too many," she gasped. "There's *too many of them*, Mirabel, they don't get tired, they don't stop—"

"Then we sing early," Sable said grimly. "Half a moon. Maybe the song doesn't need to be perfect. Maybe close counts."

"It won't," Elodie said simply. "A binding half-sung is worse than none. It would tear him apart instead of freeing him. It would leave the hunger loose and the king dead." She looked at me, and in the guttering light of the last surviving candle her face was very calm, the calm of someone who has already grieved a thing and come out the other side. "We hold until the turn completes. However long that is. Whatever it costs."

And that was when the doors of the chapel opened from the *outside*—not broken this time, but pushed, gently, by a single pair of hands.

Corvane walked into the chapel of the broken choir like a man walking into his own funeral, which, as it turned out, was precisely what he was doing.

* * *

He looked terrible. He looked, in fact, like a man who had not slept since the night he betrayed us, which I later learned was true. His coat was travel-ruined, his silver hair unbound and wild, and he carried under one arm a case I knew better than my own heartbeat: the guild's miracle-case, black lacquer and brass fittings, the box in which the Ledgerkeepers stored the one working wonder we had never dared spend.

Behind him, the court of hollow eyes had stopped. All of them. Hundreds of bodies frozen mid-stride in the yard, staring at his back with their flat river-stone eyes—and not advancing. Waiting. Because the thing in the crown wanted something from him, and it was willing to pause its hunger to see what he would bring.

"Guildmaster," I said. The word came out of me like a stone dropped down a well. I heard it fall a long way before it landed.

"Apprentice." His voice was rough. He had never once called me anything else, not in nine years, and I realized suddenly that he had never called me *Mirabel* because names, in our guild, were entries—things written down, things that could be traded or erased. Perhaps he had been protecting me from that without my ever knowing. "You've grown into the ledgers well. Better than I taught you. Better than I deserved to teach anyone."

"You sold us." My hands were shaking around the ledger. "You sold the guild, you sold the secrets, you sold *me*—the spymaster has had my name since autumn, Corvane. Since *autumn*. People died getting us here. We nearly died in those cells—"

"Yes." He did not flinch. That was the thing about Corvane; he had never once flinched from a debt, only from feelings, and he was flinching now, visibly, standing in the doorway with his whole soul showing. "Every word true. And I have come to pay."

He set the miracle-case on the altar stone, and the sound it made rang through the nave like a bell tolling.

"The guild kept one miracle," he said. "One. Sealed three hundred years ago by the founders, for the day the craft itself was threatened. Every guildmaster swore an oath on it: *never for gold, never for power, only to save the Ledgerkeepers entire.*" He looked at me, and his eyes—heavy-lidded, ironic, forever half-amused eyes—were wet. "I have spent thirty years believing the guild was already dead. Dead men keep no oaths. But you—" his voice caught, actually caught, and I had never heard Corvane's voice catch in my life— "you walked into my hall with tonight's date in your eyes and you made the guild alive again just by refusing to let it die. So the oath wakes up. So the miracle gets spent. On you. On all of you."

"What does it do?" Wren whispered.

Corvane opened the case.

Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a single sheet of paper. Blank. But it was the most *present* blank I had ever seen—the way a held breath is blank, the way the instant before lightning strikes is blank. The founders had caught a piece of the world's own unwritten intention and pressed it flat between covers, and even sealed away it hummed against my senses like a plucked string that refused to fade.

"One wish," Corvane said. "Written, not spoken. The world must obey it—but the writer pays the full weight of the wish, body and years and memory, everything the change costs, all at once. The founders called it the Last Entry." He turned to face the yard, to face the hundreds of waiting, hollow-eyed servants of the crown. "I am going to write them asleep. All of them. A sleep like death, dreamless, until the sun stands high tomorrow. It will empty me. There will be nothing left of Corvane Voss to argue with afterward, and I find I am glad of that, because I have been arguing with myself for thirty years and losing."

"No." The word tore out of me before I knew I was going to say it. "No—you don't get to do this. You don't get to buy redemption with a grand gesture and leave me holding the bill. You don't get to *die well*, Corvane, you have to *stay* and be forgiven badly, day after day, that's how forgiveness works, that's the *trade*—"

He smiled. It was the smile he'd worn my first day in the guild hall, when I was fourteen and clutching my mother's last letter and he'd said, *well, boy, shall we teach you to weigh the impossible?*

"Mirabel." He said my name. For the first time in nine years, he said my name, and I understood what it cost him, and what it meant. "I sold your parents' memory to the crown. I sold the guild's soul in installments. There is no staying past this. There is no bad-day-after-bad-day forgiveness for what I did—there is only this, one clean entry, written at the end, and I take it gladly." He lifted the blank page from its case, and took up the founder's pen, and his hand did not shake. "Forgive me or don't. Both are honest trades. But let me make this one."

The court in the yard began to move again. The thing in the crown had finished waiting.

Corvane wrote.

I will carry the sight of it until whatever erases me erases this too: my guildmaster, my betrayer, my teacher, standing in the broken chapel with the founder's pen flying across the Last Entry, writing faster than any man should write, and as he wrote, he *dimmed*. Not faded—dimmed, the way a lamp dims when another flame takes its fire. His hair went gray-white, then colorless. The lines of his face deepened and smoothed at once, becoming generic, becoming *unwritten*. He was paying himself out onto the page, year by year, memory by memory, and he was smiling while he did it, and the last words his mouth formed—I read them, I will always have read them—were not a spell at all.

They were: *Well done, apprentice.*

The world obeyed.

Sleep went out across the yard like frost forming in fast motion. Hundreds of hollow-eyed courtiers folded down into the grass, gentle as leaves settling, and lay still, breathing slow and deep, dreaming nothing. The pressure that had been building against the chapel all night—the vast patient hunger pressing through every window and crack—recoiled, shrieking silently, cheated of its hands.

And Corvane Voss swayed on his feet, and set down the pen with great care, the way he had taught me to set down instruments, and looked at me one last time with eyes that no longer remembered my name but somehow, impossibly, were proud of me anyway.

"Keep the ledgers," he said—or perhaps he only moved his lips; the grayness had taken his voice along with everything else. Then he sat down on the altar steps, folding himself neatly, a man putting away his tools at the end of a long shift, and closed his eyes, and did not open them again.

* * *

I knelt beside him. I do not know for how long. Elodie knelt with me, and put her hand on my shoulder, and did not say anything, because she understood—she, who had been composed rather than born, who knew what it was to be made of other voices—that some silences are themselves a kind of song.

"He knew," I finally managed. "About the purge. About my parents. He wasn't buying them back from the crown, Elodie. He was trying to buy *me* back from grief, and he couldn't, so he—" My throat closed. "Thirty years. He carried it thirty years and paid it all in one night."

"He chose the ending," Elodie said softly. "Few of us are given that. Fewer still choose it for someone else's sake." She squeezed my shoulder. "Grieve him quickly, my keeper. The moon is almost gone, and dawn—the thirteenth turn—is coming to meet us."

Outside, the eastern sky had begun, very faintly, to bruise with color. The last sliver of the twelfth moon hung above the hills like a paring of bone. In the yard, hundreds of sleepers breathed in unison, and beyond the hills, far away in the capital, I felt—through the ledger, through the guild-sense Corvane had trained into me—a vast and desperate attention turning toward this valley. The thing in the crown had lost its court. It had lost its night. Which meant that when the thirteenth moon rose, it would come itself.

All of it. Everything it was. Hungry enough, at the end, to leave the king's body and pour its whole self into the world at once.

Let it come, I thought, and was surprised to find the thought was not brave but simply true, the way a settled account is true. We had four notes and a keeper. We had a song. We had a blank page in the ledger and, after tonight, I understood at last what it was for.

I rose. I wiped my face with my sleeve, the way Wren did, without dignity. Sable was warming her voice in the corner, low rolling scales like distant thunder deciding. Oren had climbed to the broken bell-tower and placed his palms flat against the stones. Wren stood in the shattered doorway, whistle ready, watching the road.

Elodie took my hand. Her fingers were cold and certain.

"The moon sets," she said. "Sing with me, Mirabel."

Above the eastern hills, thin as a knife's edge, the thirteenth moon began to rise.
