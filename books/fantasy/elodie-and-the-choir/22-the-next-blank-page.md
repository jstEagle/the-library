## 22. The Next Blank Page

The hall had learned to breathe again.

Elodie noticed it first in the mornings, in the way the light came through the high windows of the Ledgerkeepers' hall—not the thin, apologetic light of a dying place, but something warmer, gold-edged, that pooled on the floorboards like honey poured slow. Before, when she had first arrived with her chord that made the candles lean, the light had seemed afraid to enter. Now it came in boldly, and it found things worth touching: the new shelves, the smell of fresh-cut oak and binding glue, the sound of apprentices—*apprentices*, plural, a word that still made Mirabel go quiet whenever he said it—copying entries in their careful hands.

She stood at the window now, watching the town wake below. The bell in the market square rang the hour, and beneath its bronze voice she could hear, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, the other sounds the world had always carried for her: the hum of the river's current finding its note, the creak of cart wheels keeping time, a baker's boy whistling three floors down—a small, bright thing, not one of theirs, but music all the same.

Her own voice had changed since the thirteenth moon. She could feel it sitting differently in her chest, less like an instrument played from outside and more like a heart deciding, beat by beat, to keep going. Some mornings she woke and simply sang a single note into the dark of her room, just to hear it answer back, just to confirm what Oren had told her with his hands pressed flat against the floor, vibration rising through stone: *you are not the echo of a song. You are the singer.*

She was learning to believe him. It came slowly, the way dawn comes—not all at once but in gradations, each one a little harder to argue with than the last.

Behind her, pages turned.

That was the other change. The hall was never silent anymore. There was always someone writing, someone reading, someone arguing cheerfully about whether a bottled echo should be catalogued under Sound or Memory (Wren insisted Sound; Sable, with the authority of a woman who had once cracked a quarry wall with her voice, insisted Memory). The old ledgers had been carried up from the vaults and set out on open tables, no longer locked away like shame, because Mirabel had decreed—and Elodie had watched him say it, chin lifted, hands steady, the last apprentice becoming the first keeper—that a record hidden is a record half-erased, and they were done with erasure.

But there was one book that stayed shut.

It sat on its shelf in the inner room, where the prophecy books lived—the old ones, the ones written by keepers dead a century or more, their spines cracked like riverbed clay. This one was new. Or rather, it was *blank*: bound in grey leather with no title, no sigil, nothing but clean paper sleeping inside. It had appeared three days after the thirteenth moon, simply present on the shelf one morning, the way frost appears, the way grief appears. No one had put it there. Wren swore she hadn't touched it, and Wren, Elodie had learned, lied about many things but never about locks or books.

Mirabel had looked at it for a long time that first morning. Then he'd said, in the voice he used for ledger entries, "A guild that makes impossible things possible should expect the occasional consequence," and he'd left it alone.

They had all left it alone.

Until this morning.

Elodie heard it before she saw it—a whisper of paper, soft as a moth against glass. She turned from the window. In the inner room, through the open doorway, the grey book had shifted on its shelf. Not fallen. *Shifted*, tilted forward on its bottom edge, leaning toward the room the way a person leans toward a door when they've decided to leave.

She crossed the floor slowly. Her bare feet made no sound; she had never quite learned to make sound unless she meant to, a habit of being composed rather than born. Up close, she could see the book breathing—or something like breathing, the cover rising and falling by a fraction no honest book should manage.

"Mirabel," she called, and did not raise her voice. She didn't need to anymore. Her voice carried the way water carries, finding every low place in a house built of wood and memory.

He came quickly—she heard his step on the stairs, two at a time, the way he still climbed everything as if late for something, though these days what he was late for was usually breakfast. He appeared in the doorway with ink on his fingers and his hair doing what it always did, defying both comb and gravity, and his eyes went straight past her to the shelf.

"It's opening," he said.

"I know."

"Should we—" He stopped. She watched him do the thing she loved most about him, the thing that had been there even in the frightened boy who'd found his name inked in a century-old prophecy: he took a breath, and instead of fear, he chose attention. "No. We shouldn't stop it. That's the whole point of us now." He glanced at her, and something wry moved at the corner of his mouth. "Besides, last time a book opened itself, I burned three candles and half my nerve over it. I'd like to do better this time."

The cover lifted.

It moved the way a bird moves when it decides to fly—no hesitation, no mechanical creak, just a decision made and enacted. The grey leather swung back, and the pages inside stirred though there was no wind, riffling forward with a sound like rain beginning, until they settled on a page near the front.

Blank, Elodie thought.

Then the ink came.

It bloomed. That was the only word for it, and she knew blooming—she had spent weeks after the thirteenth moon learning what it was to grow rather than merely exist, and growth looked exactly like this: color arriving where there had been none, spreading outward from a center, inevitable and unhurried. Dark ink rose through the paper's fibers like a root finding water, drawing first a date.

Tomorrow's date.

She felt Mirabel go still beside her. Not rigid—she had known his rigidity, in the early days, the terror locked in his shoulders when the old prophecy book had shown him tonight's date and his own name. This was different. This was the stillness of a man watching weather come in and measuring, calmly, how much roof he had.

"Tomorrow," he said quietly.

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

Below the date, more ink was gathering, slower now, thoughtful, as if whatever wrote the book was considering its words. A line began to form. Elodie leaned closer, close enough to smell the iron-and-oak scent of fresh ink, and read aloud, because some things wanted to be heard and she had spent her whole strange existence learning which things those were:

"*When the thirteenth moon has waned, a debt unclaimed will seek its keeper.*"

The words finished themselves, the final letter curling into place like a hand closing gently around a pen. Then the page lay quiet. The book lay quiet. Somewhere below them, Sable's voice rose through the floorboards, running scales with a new apprentice whose tremolo needed work, and the ordinary morning went on around the extraordinary sentence the way a river goes on around a stone.

Elodie realized she was smiling.

She checked herself, surprised—but no, it was real, the smile sitting warm and certain on her face. A year ago—was it only a year?—a line like that would have been a death sentence. She remembered Mirabel telling her about it, later, when trust had grown between them like ivy over a ruined wall: how he had seen his name in a book written before his birth and had felt the future close around him like a fist. How Corvane had ordered the book burned. How he had disobeyed, hands shaking, because obedience suddenly cost more than defiance.

And look what defiance had built.

"Debt unclaimed," Mirabel murmured. He had taken off his spectacles—he wore them now while writing, a new affectation he pretended was necessity—and was cleaning them on his sleeve, which he did when thinking. "We don't have any unclaimed debts. I went through the ledgers myself, twice, after we reopened. Every contract honored, every payment returned, every—" He stopped.

Elodie watched the thought arrive in him. It arrived in his face first, in the way his eyebrows drew together, then in his hands, which went very carefully still.

"The farmer," he said. "Tomas. The thirty years."

"You returned them."

"I returned twenty-nine years, eleven months, and some odd days." His voice had gone flat, precise—the ledger voice, the voice that meant arithmetic was happening somewhere behind his eyes at terrible speed. "I paid a month of my memories for the transfer. But the contract was written in whole years. Thirty. And a year is not a fixed length—it depends on the turning of the moons. Thirteen moons in the old reckoning, and the old reckoning is what the guild used, and the thirteenth moon of this cycle was—" He looked up at her. "The thirteenth moon was the night we sang. The night everything changed. Time bent that night, Elodie. Every keeper knows the thirteenth moon doesn't fit inside an ordinary year. Which means the year we owed Tomas isn't finished. Which means—"

"There's a piece of it still outstanding," Elodie finished softly. "A debt unclaimed. Waiting for its keeper."

The ink on the page seemed to dry even as they spoke, settling into permanence, and Elodie understood suddenly why the book had chosen today to open, why it had waited three days and then breathed and stirred and bloomed. Prophecy, she was learning, was not a cage. It was a letter. And letters were only frightening when you believed you had no choice about answering them.

She had been a letter once herself. Written by a broken choir, sealed in flesh, delivered to a door where a lonely boy kept the last light of a dying guild. She had spent months believing she was only the message—composed, sent, spent. But messages could be answered. Letters could be replied to. Pages could be written on.

"Mirabel." She turned to face him fully. "Do you remember what you told me? On the mountain road, after Oren. When I couldn't stop shaking."

He did remember. She could see it in his eyes, the way the memory surfaced there gentle as a fish rising in still water. *Unwritten things can still be written.* He had shown her a blank page in the great ledger and said it plainly, without poetry, which somehow made it truer: that a life with no recorded past was not an empty life but an unwritten one, and unwritten was not the same as impossible. Nothing, he had told her, was impossible anymore. That was rather the point of them.

"I remember," he said.

"So." She gestured at the book, at the date, at the sentence sitting there like a knock at a door. "How do we answer?"

Mirabel looked at the prophecy for a long moment. Then he did something that would have been unthinkable in the old days, in the crumbling hall with its three remaining members and its locked vaults and its guildmaster trading secrets for survival: he picked up the book. He carried it, open, to the great table by the window where the morning light fell strongest, and he set it down among the working ledgers, among the ink pots and the sand jars and Wren's collection of stolen (returned, mostly) lockpicks, right in the middle of the living mess of their craft.

"We write back," he said.

From the shelf behind them, the old prophecy book—the first one, the century-old one that had started everything—gave a faint creak, like an elder clearing its throat, or perhaps laughing.

* * *

By midmorning, the whole choir had gathered.

They came the way they always came now, drawn by some frequency below hearing: Sable first, because Sable went wherever interesting trouble was, sweeping in with her opera-singer's stride and a pastry she had liberated from the kitchen; then Wren, dropping through the window rather than using the door on principle; then Brother Oren, who had taken rooms above the stable and communicated his arrival by pressing his palm flat to the doorframe so that the whole hall hummed a low, warm note, like a struck bell saying *here, here, here.*

They stood around the table and read the line together.

"A debt unclaimed," Sable repeated. She tapped the page with one lacquered fingernail. "Whose debt?"

"Tomas's," Mirabel said. He explained the arithmetic—the thirteen-moon year, the bent night, the fragment of thirty years still owed—and Elodie watched the others absorb it the way she had learned to watch people learn things: Wren fast and fidgeting, Sable skeptical until convinced and then ferociously committed, Oren simply nodding, because Oren had understood the shape of it before Mirabel finished speaking. Oren always did. Vibration carried further than words.

"So we pay it," Wren said, shrugging. "We find the farmer, we give him his extra bit of years, done by lunch. Why'd the book make such a fuss?"

"Because it won't be simple." Mirabel's hand rested on the page, careful, the way he touched everything the ledgers had taught him mattered. "Nothing the guild ever traded was simple. Thirty years of a man's life don't sit in a jar waiting. They've been woven into other payments, other contracts—I paid for Tomas's years out of my own memory, and my month of memory went into the founding entry, the one we all sang. Our names are in it. All of ours." He looked around the table, and Elodie saw him arrive at the edge of the thing and pause there, gathering courage the way he once gathered dread, except transformed now into something cleaner. "If a piece of that debt is still loose, it isn't just tied to me. It's tied to everyone who signed. To all of us."

Silence held the room for a heartbeat. Elodie felt it—the old fear, trying to rise, the fear of prophecy, of price, of names written in books by powers larger than yourself.

Then Sable laughed.

It was her stage laugh, full-throated, rolling, the laugh that had once preceded a note that cracked a quarry wall, and it broke the silence like sun breaking cloud. "Good," she said. "I was worried it would be boring."

Oren pressed two fingers to the tabletop. The wood hummed. Elodie translated for the room, as she'd learned to: *Whatever comes, we have sung against worse.*

"Wren?" Mirabel asked.

The girl had gone still, which was rare enough that everyone noticed. She stared at the page, at the date, and Elodie saw something flicker across her sharp young face—not fear, exactly. Recognition. Wren had been an orphan of the streets long enough to know that every gift came with a hook in it, that every open door might be a trap wearing welcome's clothes. But Wren had also, in the cells beneath the spymaster's tower, picked four locks in the dark while a boy wrote their freedom into reality, and she had learned something in that dark that was bigger than her caution.

"Tomorrow, huh?" Wren said at last. She grinned, quick and crooked. "Fine. But I get first pick of whatever we find."

Mirabel laughed—really laughed, head back, and Elodie drank in the sound, because a year ago she had met a boy who flinched at his own name in a book, and here he was now, treating prophecy like a work order.

He dipped a pen. He pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and above it he paused, glancing at Elodie across the table, and the look that passed between them needed no translation, no vibration, no song. It said: *we know what this costs, and we know what it's for, and we are not afraid.* Not fearless—they had earned their fear honestly, and it sat with them now like an old hound by the fire, useful, loyal, no longer master. But not afraid.

"Not fear this time," Elodie said aloud, because some things wanted to be heard.

"Readiness," Mirabel agreed. And he wrote.

Elodie watched the ink flow from his pen onto the blank page—*their* page now, the guild's page, the first entry of the answer they would send back through whatever channel connected keeper-books across the years. He wrote in the new charter hand, the one they had designed together, plain and strong: an acknowledgment of the debt, a declaration of intent to honor it, and at the bottom, where the old guild would have required a single name to bear the burden alone, he wrote five.

His own. Hers. Sable's. Oren's. Wren's.

Five names, side by side, sharing one line like voices sharing one chord.

When he finished, the prophecy book responded. Elodie felt it before she saw it—a shiver in the air, a tightening of the light—and on the page bearing tomorrow's date, beneath the warning line, new ink began to bloom in answer. Slowly, elegantly, as if pleased:

"*Received,*" the book wrote. "*Come.*"

And then, below that, in smaller script, almost sly, almost playful, one final line that made Wren whoop and Sable raise an eyebrow and Oren's low hum deepen with something that might have been amusement:

"*Bring the choir.*"

Elodie looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at her. Outside, the morning went on being golden; downstairs, an apprentice hit her scale cleanly for the first time, and Sable's distant cry of triumph rattled the windows; on the shelf, the old prophecy book closed itself with a soft sigh, its work finished, its story handed on.

Tomorrow, a debt. Tomorrow, a road, a farmer, a fragment of years, and whatever tangled wonder had grown around that fragment in the dark between the moons. Tomorrow, the next verse of a song that had begun with a boy reading a forbidden page and a woman knocking twice.

Elodie reached across the table and laid her hand over the five names, feeling the ink drying into permanence, feeling the future open rather than close.

"Well," she said, and her voice rang through the hall like the first note of something new. "Shall we rehearse?"
