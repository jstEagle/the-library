## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There are three rules to hiding a gift, and I have broken all of them at least once.

Rule one: never let anyone see you listening when there is nothing there to hear. Rule two: never write down what the dead tell you in ink that anyone else can read. Rule three — and this is the one I break most often, because I am a sentimental man pretending to be a practical one — never fall in love with someone whose secrets could get you hanged.

I was breaking rule three at roughly half past nine on a Tuesday morning, which is when Elodie Crane came down the guildhall stairs with her boots unlaced and her hair like a crow that had lost an argument with a windmill.

"Juniper," she said, "you're talking to the bone again."

"I'm assessing the grain," I said. "It's called craftsmanship."

"You're saying *mm* to it. You do this little *mm* sound, like it's told you something scandalous." She dropped into the chair across my workbench, stole an apple from my lunch, and bit it with the confidence of a woman who has never once been billed for anything. "What's it saying? Does the femur gossip?"

"The femur is discreet."

"Shame. I'd hoped for scandal before breakfast."

The Guild of Impossible Things occupies a building that should not stand, in a street that technically does not exist, in the city of Corvane, where everything worth trading has already been traded twice. We broker impossibilities: bottled Tuesdays, secondhand luck, the last word of a man who died mid-sentence (a bargain, if you can find it; most people die mid-*um*). My official title is Ledger-Keeper of Small Debts, which means I record trades too minor for the great ledger upstairs — a widow's borrowed courage, a cobbler's seven years of good weather, all the small collateral that keeps a city of dreamers solvent.

My unofficial talent is that I hear the dead.

Not ghosts, exactly. Not rattling chains or weeping in attics. Voices. The dead leave echoes in things they touched, debts they left unpaid, promises that outlived them, and I can hear those echoes the way a tuner hears a flat string. It is not a gift anyone has ever thanked me for. The dead are mostly worried, and mostly about money, and mostly about each other.

So I carve bone talismans — quiet work, honest work, work that gives me an excuse to hold a thing and listen without seeming to — and I keep my ledgers, and I pretend to be exactly as ordinary as a man with ink-stained fingers and no remarkable history ought to be.

Vess Crane, guildmaster, believes her ledger-keeper is merely meticulous. Her daughter believes I talk to bones because I'm strange. Only one of them is right, and she doesn't know it.

* * *

That Tuesday began with a trade so small it barely qualified as impossible: old Marta Voss wanted to sell the memory of her wedding day. Not the whole day — just the moment the rain started, which she claimed had ruined the photographs and improved the marriage.

"Rain memory," I said, writing it into the small-debt ledger in my careful hand. "One instance, non-transferable, seller retains the smell."

"She keeps the smell?"

"Marta insists the smell is the best part."

Elodie leaned over my shoulder to read the entry, which meant leaning close enough that I could count her breaths, which meant I stopped counting anything else. "You know what Mother will say. Weather-memories are volatile. They leak."

"They leak *gently*. There are worse things to leak than rain." I blotted the page. "Besides, the buyer is the tanner on Coil Street. He hasn't seen rain since the fire took his shop. He wants the sound of it on a roof."

Elodie went quiet in the way she did sometimes — all that sharpness folding away like a knife going back in its sheath. "That's not an impossible thing," she said finally. "That's just a kind thing wearing a coat."

"Don't tell the Guild. We'll lose our charter."

She laughed, and the dead in the room — there were always a few, drifting through the guildhall like dust motes with opinions — laughed with her, or near her, or in whatever way the dead laugh, which is less a sound than a settling, like a house cooling at night.

I looked back down at my ledger very quickly, because rule one exists for a reason.

* * *

At noon, Vess summoned me upstairs to the great ledger hall, where the Impossible Trades are recorded in a book the size of a door and the temper of a creditor. Vess Crane is sixty, iron-grey, and built like the concept of consequence. She has run the Guild for thirty years and has never once been seen to hurry, on the theory that time itself will wait for her out of professional courtesy.

"Crane," she said — she calls me by my surname, as though we might be related, which we are not, though I've privately decided to take it as a compliment — "the Hollow Market wants a talisman against drowning. Three of them. Bone."

"Drowning charms are grim work," I said. "Bone remembers water badly."

"Then carve it something better to remember." She slid a purse across the table. Then, because she is not actually made of iron: "You slept here again. I saw the blanket."

"I was reviewing entries."

"At three in the morning."

"Some debts don't keep office hours."

She studied me with those pale eyes that miss nothing except, mercifully, the one thing that matters. "You're a strange man, Juniper Crane. Loyal, punctual, strange. In thirty years you've never asked me for a raise, a holiday, or an explanation of what we really do here."

"Would you give one?"

"No."

"There's my answer." I took the purse. "Three drowning charms by Friday."

"And Crane—" I paused at the door. She almost smiled, which for Vess is a seismic event. "Whatever it is you're not telling me. Keep not telling me well."

* * *

The afternoon went wrong at four o'clock, in the way afternoons do: gradually, then all at once.

His name was Tobbe Salt, and he'd been a debtor of the Guild for eleven years — a lamplighter who'd once bought ten years of steady hands from us and paid it off honestly, coin by coin, until last winter when his lungs started filling with the soot of every lamp he'd ever climbed to. He lived now in a room above the chandler's on Wick Lane, and I brought him his receipts personally because he liked the company and because I liked hearing him complain about modern wicks.

When I came up the stairs that afternoon, I knew before I opened the door. The room was full of voices.

Not Tobbe's. Tobbe was asleep in his chair, grey as ash, breathing in the shallow, counted way of the nearly finished. But the room — the room was *crowded*. Every object he'd ever touched with intention hummed with echo. His lamp-trimmer sang a work song in a dead man's rhythm. His mother's ring, on a string around his neck, whispered a lullaby in a language neither of us spoke. And beneath it all, threading through like a bass note, the older voices — the ones that live in walls and floors and the bones of old cities — were saying something I'd never heard them say before.

They were counting.

"*...four hundred and six, four hundred and five...*" A slow descending count, patient as erosion, coming up through the floorboards, through the foundations, through whatever lies under cities.

"Tobbe," I said gently.

He opened his eyes. For a moment they were clear — clearer than they'd been in months — and he looked at me with the particular expression of a man who has been waiting for someone and is relieved it's me, even though he'd been hoping for someone else entirely.

"Ledger-keeper," he rasped. "Good. Good. I've a debt to file."

"You're paid up. Eleven years, clean as a whistle."

"Not that kind." He fumbled at his ring-string, and I helped him, my hands steadier than they felt. "Listen. You're the only one who'll — everyone else thinks I'm raving, but you *listen*, don't you? Always did. Looked at me like you heard more than I said."

My blood went cold and polite. "Tobbe—"

"Count the stars," he said, gripping my wrist with strength that shouldn't have been left in him. "Before they go. That's the message. That's what they're all saying, down under everything — the old ones, the deep ones. *Count the stars before they go.* I've been hearing it for a week. Thought it was the soot. It's not the soot, boy. It's a *countdown*."

"What countdown? Tobbe, slow down—"

"File it," he said. "In your ledger. Whatever kind of ledger you really keep." And he smiled, sly as a magpie, and I understood with a lurch that Tobbe Salt had known about me for years and had simply had the decency — rarest of virtues in this city — to say nothing. "Every debt gets collected eventually, eh? Even the sky's."

He died at sunset, quietly, mid-complaint about modern wicks, which I choose to believe was on purpose.

* * *

I walked back to the guildhall through streets that were lighting themselves lamp by lamp, and I caught myself doing it — counting the stars. All of them. Over and over, like a miser touching coins.

Four hundred and six. Four hundred and five.

The number Tobbe's floorboards had been reciting.

I stood in the middle of Lantern Row with my ledger under my arm and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. If the deep voices had been counting down from some vast number, and tonight they'd reached four hundred and six, and stars were what they were counting —

No. Stars don't go out. Stars are the one thing in the world that isn't collateral. Even the Guild doesn't trade in stars.

But the dead had never lied to me. That was the whole horror of the gift: the dead are incapable of it. They have nothing left to gain.

Inside the guildhall, Elodie was arguing cheerfully with the hearth about the proper way to bank coals, and Vess was somewhere above with the great ledger, and everything smelled of beeswax and apple peel and the ordinary evening of an ordinary guild, and I stood in the doorway like a man at a border crossing, deciding which country to live in.

"Juniper!" Elodie lit up. "You look terrible. Come argue with the fire, it's winning."

"Give me a moment," I said, and sat down at my bench, and opened the small-debt ledger, and wrote, in the private shorthand only I could read:

*Tobbe Salt, lamplighter, deceased. Final instruction received. Message: count the stars before they go. Status: unfiled with any authority. Action pending.*

And below it, because my hand apparently answers to something braver than my head:

*Current count: 406.*

Above the guildhall, through the high window, the first stars were coming out. I counted them anyway. Four hundred and six, if you knew where to look — and I realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, that I had never counted them before tonight, and yet the number fit perfectly, as if I'd always known it, as if it had been waiting in me like a debt waiting for its collector.

Outside, far off toward the mountains, something that was not thunder made the windows hum.

Elodie looked up from the hearth. "Juniper? What was that?"

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and "nothing," which was not.
