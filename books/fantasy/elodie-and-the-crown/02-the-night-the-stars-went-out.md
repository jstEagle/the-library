## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

I have kept ledgers long enough to know that the worst entries are never the ones written in grief. Grief, at least, is honest. The worst entries are the ones written in tidy, competent ink by a man who believed he was doing ordinary work.

That night began with ordinary work.

I was in my alcove at the guildhall, carving a debt-token from the knucklebone of a horse that had, according to its owner, been owed a kinder ending than it got. The lamplight was amber. The tea had gone cold in the way tea does when a carver forgets it exists. Somewhere above me, Elodie was arguing with her mother about the winter stores, and her voice came down through the floorboards like weather — bright, insistent, impossible to ignore and impossible to hold onto.

"Juniper!" she called through the floor, because she had learned years ago that the floorboards carried. "If you're eavesdropping, at least have the decency to laugh in the right places!"

"I'm carving," I called back.

"You're *listening*. You always listen. It's your most expensive habit."

She was right, of course. She usually was, which was the most irritating thing about her. I listened the way misers count coins — constantly, compulsively, and with a guilt I had long since stopped examining. Because the dead were talking again.

They had been talking all evening. A low murmur under the floorboards, under the foundations, under the frost-hardened earth itself. The dead of Marrowfield. The dead of the border villages. The dead who had no names left and only wanted to be counted. And underneath the murmur, one voice I had heard for three days now, thin as wire:

*Count the stars before they go.*

I told myself it was a dying man's poetry. Debtors' ghosts often spoke in poetry; it was the only currency they had left. I told myself the wards would hold, the way they had held for two hundred years, the way the guildmaster's grandmother had anchored them with — well. With whatever guildmasters anchored things with. I was a bone-carver. I carved talismans. I notarized impossible trades. I did not ask what held up the sky, because a man who pretends to be ordinary learns very quickly never to ask questions with large answers.

The moon rose at the ninth hour. I remember because I was checking it against the almanac, a habit from my mother — *always know where the moon is, Juniper, the moon is the only honest clock* — and because at the moment it cleared the ridge, the murmur of the dead stopped.

All of it. At once. The way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

Then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Went out* — one by one, from the north border inward, like candles pinched by an unseen hand moving down a long table. I stood in the doorway of the guildhall with my carving knife still in my hand and watched the sky close like a book.

And the dead began to scream.

I had heard the dead grieve before. I had heard them rage, and bargain, and recite their debts in voices like dry leaves. I had never heard them *panic*. Panic, it turns out, sounds the same in the dead as in the living — except that the dead have nothing left to lose but their names, and so that is what they screamed. *My name. My name. Who is holding my name.* A chorus of a thousand voices swelling up through the earth, through the stones, through my teeth, and I gripped the doorframe because if I let go I was fairly sure I would be swept out into the dark like a leaf.

"Juniper."

Vess Crane stood in the hall behind me. Guildmaster Vess, in her grey traveling cloak, which she had not worn in years, and her face was the face of a woman who has been waiting for a bill to come due for twenty years and has just heard the knock.

"You hear them," she said. It was not a question.

"Guildmaster, I—"

"Don't." She came and stood beside me in the doorway, looking up at the black sky with an expression I would later understand was not surprise. It was recognition. "Twenty-two years I've known you, Juniper. You flinch at graves. You leave coins on the windowsill at dusk. You once apologized to a chicken bone. I have known what you are since the week you arrived, and I have never said, because whatever you're hiding it from, I assumed you had your reasons."

The dead were still screaming. I could barely hear her. I could barely hear myself.

"What's happening to the sky?" I asked.

She was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had the flatness of a woman reading from a ledger she had hoped to burn.

"The border wards were never anchored by stone," she said. "Stone is only the shell. They were anchored by a soul. A soul given to the court beneath the mountain, at the founding, as surety — and the court holds it still. The wards run on that surety the way a lamp runs on oil." She looked at me then, and her eyes were terrible. "And three nights ago, the court called in its interest."

*Count the stars before they go.* I thought of the dying debtor's ghost, and my hands went cold.

"Whose soul?" I asked, though some part of me, the part that keeps ledgers, had already begun the arithmetic. A founding debt. A surety held for two hundred years. A guildmaster's family that had held the guild for five generations. A daughter who had been born in this hall, in the winter of a bad year, when the guild was nearly broke and desperate men sign desperate papers—

"Elodie's," Vess said. "Sold at her birth. By me. To pay the guild's founding arrears before the wards failed the first time."

The world did a slow, careful turn around me, the way a ledger page turns when you're looking for an entry you already know is there.

"You *sold* her."

"I signed a contract I did not fully read, in a winter when the alternative was the wards failing and every soul on the border unanchored. I told myself it was a formality. A token. That the court beneath the mountain would never collect." Her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "The dead are screaming because the wards are failing, Juniper, and the wards are failing because the court has begun to draw on its collateral. My daughter has been walking around for twenty-two years as the guarantor of the sky, and I let her believe she was only a guildmaster's daughter who was bad at arithmetic."

I should have said something. I know that. I have replayed that moment a hundred times since, and in every version I say something wise, or kind, or at minimum coherent. What I actually did was stand in the doorway with a carving knife in my hand and think, with a clarity that felt like a blade between my ribs:

*I notarized it.*

Three years ago. An old client of the guild, a pale gentleman with excellent manners and a courier's satchel, had brought me a sheaf of papers to witness and seal. Routine work. Impossible trades were my trade — I notarized them all the time, and I had long ago learned not to read them too closely, because a bone-carver who reads contracts he shouldn't tends to stop being a bone-carver. I remembered the seal. I remembered the weight of the wax. I remembered thinking the signature at the bottom was in a woman's hand, and the surety clause was on a page I had turned past.

I had turned *past* it. I had pressed my seal into the wax that made it binding, and I had gone home and slept soundly, and for three years Elodie had teased me through the floorboards while I held the pen that kept her sold.

"Juniper." Vess's hand was on my arm. "You've gone white as your own ivory."

"I notarized the sale," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Three years ago. A courier brought papers. I sealed them. I didn't read—" I stopped, because the excuse was obscene, and she saw it land, and to her credit she didn't pretend it hadn't.

"The court doesn't send couriers to the innocent," she said quietly. "It sends them to the convenient. That's how it has always worked. That is *how it got my signature*, if we're being honest."

But the dead were still screaming, and guilt is not a thing that listens to reason. Guilt is a ledger, and it balances itself.

Above us, the last stars on the southern rim guttered — and held. Barely. A handful of them, burning low and blue, like a fire left with too little fuel. The dead's chorus shifted, and for the first time I heard something in it besides panic. I heard *attention*. A thousand dead voices turning, all together, toward the guildhall. Toward the door where I stood.

*Carver,* they said. *Carver, carver.* The word moved through the chorus like wind through wheat. *You hear us. You count us. Count the stars before they go. Count them, carver, and find the debt.*

"Vess," I said. "The dead are talking to me."

"I know," she said. "They've been talking to you for years. That's why I hired you."

And that — that was the moment, I think, when the pretending stopped. Twenty-two years of being an ordinary bone-carver, of leaving coins on windowsills and never asking large questions, and it ended on a doorstep under a murdered sky, because a woman who had sold her daughter had hired a man who heard the dead and never told him why.

Inside the hall, I heard Elodie coming down the stairs. I heard her stop when she saw the open door, the black sky, her mother in the traveling cloak she hadn't worn in years. I heard her breath catch — that quick, bright mind of hers doing its arithmetic, arriving at the wrong answer first, as quick minds do, and then the right one.

"Mother," she said, in a voice I had never heard her use. "What did you do?"

Vess closed her eyes.

And I stood there with my knife in my hand and my seal's ghost on my conscience, and I understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading a debt he can never discharge, that I was going under the mountain. That I had been going there for three years, ever since I pressed wax to a lie. That the ledger had my name in it, and the dead were waiting to be counted, and there were perhaps a handful of stars left in the sky.

*Count the stars before they go,* the ghost had begged me.

I intended to. I intended to count every one of them back into the sky, even if I had to carve the ledger of heaven myself.

Even if the final entry was my name.

* * *

Later — much later, when the guildhall was dark and the surviving stars burned low over the ridge — I sat at my workbench and did not carve. I listened. The dead had quieted to a murmur, the way a crowd quiets when it has decided to wait. And beneath the murmur, faint and patient, from very far down, I heard something new.

Not the dead.

A voice like dry paper, like a page being turned. It said, in the tone of a clerk reading a line item:

*One soul of the Crane line, held in surety. Interest accrued. Collection to commence at the final star.*

And beneath it, so faint I might have invented it, a second voice. Young. Awake. Unafraid in the way that frightened me more than any scream.

*Juniper,* it said. *I know you're listening. Stop hiding.*

It was Elodie's voice. And it was coming from *below*.
