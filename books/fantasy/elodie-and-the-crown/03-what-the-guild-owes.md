## 3. What the Guild Owes

I had always known, in the way you know a house is cold before you admit the fire is out.

My mother kept me like she kept her ledgers: carefully, in a locked room, with the key worn smooth from handling. I was the guildmaster's daughter. I was the girl who teased Juniper Crane into smiling. I was the one who dusted the impossible things and never once asked why the guild's charter had my name inked into the margin of page one, in handwriting older than mine.

The night the stars went out, I stopped not asking.

Vess Crane sat in her study with every lamp lit and none of them helping. She looked small behind her desk, which was wrong—my mother is not a small woman; she is a woman who once stared down a debt-collector demon for six hours without blinking—but grief does that. It shrinks people down to their arithmetic.

"Say it," I told her. "Whatever it is. Say it plainly."

She turned the ledger toward me. The guild's founding ledger, the one with the margin note. And there, under the seal of the court beneath the mountain, in ink the color of dried blood:

*One daughter, born whole and healthy, held in trust against the maintenance of the border wards. Term: until the sky requires her.*

"You sold me," I said. It came out very calm. Calm is what fury looks like when it's still putting its coat on.

"I *traded* you," my mother said, and then flinched at her own words, because there is no version of that sentence that survives daylight. "Elodie. You were three days old. The wards were failing even then. The Mountain King's archivist came to the door and offered—the only offer anyone has ever made us. Your life, lived here, warm and fed and loved, in exchange for the sky staying stitched together above everyone else's heads. Every child born under those wards. Every harvest. Every winter that ended when it was supposed to end." Her hands were shaking on the ledger. "I have paid interest on that choice every single day since."

"Interest," I repeated. "You've been paying *interest* on *me*."

"You were never collateral to me."

"No," I said. "I was worse than collateral. Collateral just sits in a vault. I got to be *loved*. I got birthday cakes and a mother who taught me to read contracts before I could read stories, and all of it was—" My voice did something humiliating. I hated it. "All of it was a payment plan."

She reached across the desk. I stepped back. That, more than anything, seemed to break her.

"The stars went out because the wards are failing," she said quietly. "Because the term is up. Because the sky *requires* me now, doesn't it. That's what 'until the sky requires her' means."

"Yes."

"Then I'll go."

"*No.*"

"You don't get to say no. You spent my consent twenty-five years ago. You don't get to ration what's left of it now."

My mother closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet and furious and proud, which is the family expression, all four at once. "Then at least let me do this properly. Let me open the guild's books to you. If you're going under the mountain, you go as a member of the Guild of Impossible Things, with our full weight behind you—and Elodie, you will need every impossible thing we have."

* * *

Juniper was in the workshop when I found him, carving something small and white and trembling slightly, which meant he'd been listening to it argue with him. He didn't look up. He always knows it's me by my footsteps, which should have been my first clue about him, years ago.

"I'm going under the mountain," I said.

The knife stopped. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"The dead told me." He set the bone down and finally looked at me, and his face was doing that thing where he pretends to be ordinary and fails completely. "Elodie, I notarized your sale. Years ago. Before I understood what my gift was for. I heard the contract whispering and I sealed it anyway, because I thought it was just another trade, because I was a coward about being what I am." His jaw worked. "So either you go alone and I spend the rest of my life listening to ghosts tell me how it ended, or I come with you and we find out together whether a bad ledger can be rewritten."

"That's not an apology, that's an accounting error."

"I'm a bone-carver. We don't apologize. We amend."

Despite everything—the dying stars, the mother-shaped hole in my chest, the fact that I was apparently a line item—I laughed. It came out cracked but real. "Fine. Amend away. But Juniper: I'm not going down there to be traded back. Whatever the King wants, whatever deal he thinks he's owed, I'm not walking into that hall to be *an asset* again. If we go, we go to end it. All of it."

He studied me for a long moment, the way he studies bone before the first cut. "Then we'll need help," he said. "And I know exactly two people mad enough."

* * *

The first was waiting for us at the drowned river, though we hadn't asked him yet, which should have been impossible and therefore made him guild material.

Bram the ferryman stood at the edge of the black water with his pole planted and his boat rocking, and wrapped around him like a second cloak was a ghost—a thin, grey, patient shape, bound to him by a chain no one else could see. Bram had ferried souls across that river for forty years, and somewhere in the middle of it, death had decided he was too useful to release. So he worked with his own afterlife riding shotgun, and he charged no toll except memory, because memories were the only coin the river respected.

"You're going down," he said. Not a question.

"We're going down," I said.

"To the under-court." His ghost shifted against his shoulder, and Bram's face did something complicated. "I've been ferrying for forty years hoping someone would finally make that walk. Everyone who owes the dead ends up at that mountain eventually, girl. Nobody ever goes to collect them." He looked at Juniper. "Bone-carver. I've heard your name in the mouths of the drowned. They say you listen."

"I try."

"Then listen to this: I'll take you. But my toll is steep, and my ghost rides along, and if there's any chance at all that your business unbinds what binds me—" his voice caught, actual rust on it, "—then I'm coming the whole way down. Not for pay. For *chance*."

"Done," I said, and shook his hand, and his ghost leaned into the shake like a cat.

* * *

The hedge-witch was harder, because Maren Quill does not live anywhere so much as she *accrues* in places. We found her in a hedgerow outside town, sitting in a nest of drying herbs and bottled whispers, feeding crumbs to a crow that may have been a client.

Maren trades in last words. The final sentences of the dying, bottled and bartered—some people buy them for comfort, some for leverage, some because a last word is the truest currency there is and Maren accepts nothing false. She looked up at us with eyes like tea left too long and said, "Ah. The collateral and the coward. Sit down. This will take a while."

"I'm neither of those things," I said.

"You're both, dear, and that's fine, most people are. What matters is what you do about it." She patted the ground beside her. "The stars went out. I felt it in my bottles—every last word I own went dark at once, like candles pinched by the same hand. Do you know what that means? It means the dead can't finish their sentences anymore. Somewhere below us, the words of everyone who ever died under those wards are *shelved*, unfinished, and the silence is driving the living half-mad without knowing why." She fixed me with her stare. "You're going to fix that."

"It's not a request, is it."

"Nothing worth doing ever is." She began bundling herbs with brisk, vicious efficiency. "I'm coming. Not for you—for *them*. Forty years I've carried other people's endings, and I would like, just once, to deliver one where it belongs." She thrust a bottle into my hands. Inside, a single word glowed faintly: *stay.* "For the road. Everyone needs a last word before a first journey."

* * *

We gathered that night in the guildhall, the four of us plus one ghost, around a table my mother had cleared of everything except a map of the world's underside, which she had apparently owned all along, because of course she had.

Vess Crane stood at the head and did not apologize. I understood, looking at her, that she might never manage the word itself—that some debts are too large for language—but she had opened every book, unsealed every record, and put the entire guild's strange weight behind me, and that was its own kind of confession.

"The road goes through the drowned river," she said, tapping the map. "Then the bone corridors. Then the court. The King will demand a price. He always demands a price."

"Then we'll bring him one he doesn't expect," I said.

Juniper looked at me across the table, and Bram's ghost flickered hopefully, and Maren tucked another bottle into her coat, and I felt it settle over me—not the old weight of being owed, but something new and chosen and fierce.

A found family of debtors, going to collect on the oldest debt of all.

At moonrise, we walked out through the guild doors into a starless dark, and the first snow of the long winter began to fall—too early, too fine, too much like ash—and Bram said, "River's high this year," and we went down toward the water.
