## 4. The Ferryman's Toll

The world's edge is not what the songs promise. There is no cliff, no waterfall pouring into nothing, no dramatic wind to whip your cloak about while you gaze into eternity. There is a road that stops being a road, a fence with no land on either side of it, and then a river so wide you cannot see the far bank — a river that has drowned whatever edge it was supposed to mark, running flat and grey as hammered pewter under a sky with no stars left in it.

"It's smaller than I imagined," said Sable, our hedge-witch, who traded in last words and had therefore imagined everything at least once.

"Everything is," said Bram.

Bram was already there, of course. That was the trouble with ferrymen: they were always already there, the way grief is always already there when you finally arrive at it. He stood beside his boat — a long black thing like a coffin that had learned ambition — and he was not entirely alive himself. A ghost clung to him the way a burr clings to a coat, translucent and patient, a woman's shape folded into his shadow. He did not introduce her. We did not ask. Some debts are private even among debtors.

We had come down from the guildhall three days ago: me, Elodie, Vess's blessing wrapped around us like a shroud she pretended was a scarf, Sable the hedge-witch, and old Hollis, who had once ferried souls for a living before his own ghost took up residence in his chest and made him a ferryman of a different sort. Four of us walking toward the court beneath the mountain, carrying between us one stolen daughter, one guilty notary, one witch with a jar of other people's final sentences, and a man whose heart beat out of rhythm because someone else's soul kept time inside it.

The Guild of Impossible Things had never attempted anything this impossible. I found that oddly comforting. If we failed, at least we'd fail within our charter.

* * *

"You know the toll," Bram said. Not a question. Ferrymen don't ask questions; questions imply uncertainty about payment, and Bram had never once been uncertain about payment in what I judged to be several centuries of practice.

"A memory," I said. "One per passenger."

"One per passenger," he agreed, "freely given, freely taken. No substitutions after boarding. No refunds past the first stroke of the pole." He looked at each of us in turn, and his eyes were the colour of the river, which is to say the colour of forgetting. "I'll have them now. Boarding's easier when everyone's already lighter."

Sable went first. She pressed her palm to the gunwale and I watched something leave her — a small bright thing, visible only because I have never in my life managed to be properly ordinary. It looked like a smell looks, if that makes sense. It looked like the memory of rain on a particular street. She swayed, blinked, and said, "Huh. I appear to have lost my grandmother's recipe for nettle soup. Good riddance. It was terrible." Her voice shook on *terrible*, which told me it hadn't been terrible at all.

Hollis paid next. He gave up something older — I felt the dead around us lean in when it left him, the way dogs lean toward a door. Whatever it was, he came away quieter, and the ghost in his chest hummed a note I hadn't heard from her before. Almost approving.

Then it was my turn.

Here is what nobody tells you about paying a toll in memories: you get to choose, but choosing is its own cruelty. Every memory is load-bearing. Take out the wrong one and the whole architecture of you sags. I stood at the water's edge doing the arithmetic of myself, and I will confess that for one shameful moment I considered giving up the taste of Elodie's terrible tea, or the sound of Vess calling me *Crane-boy* when she was feeling fond, small debts, cheap coin.

But cheap coin insults a ferryman. And there was one memory I had been guarding too carefully, all these years, the way you guard a wound by never touching it. My mother's voice. The way she sang the ledger-lullaby, the counting song every bone-carver's child learns before they learn letters: *one for the living, two for the dead, three for the debts that can't be said.* I had not heard an actual voice say those words since she died. I had only ever heard them in my own head, wearing her voice like a borrowed coat, and I had begun, lately, to suspect that the coat no longer fit — that I was losing the original and keeping only my forgery of it.

So I gave him that. Better to lose it cleanly, to a professional, than to lose it slowly, to time.

"I'll take the singing," I said. "My mother's voice. All of it."

Bram studied me for a long moment. "You're sure of the weight?"

"No," I said. "But I'm sure of the choice."

He held out his hand, and I pressed mine to his, and the memory left me the way a tooth leaves — with a horrible looseness first, a gap where solidity used to be. I tried, immediately, desperately, to hear the lullaby in my head, and what I found was the shape of a song with no sound in it. Rhythm without music. Words I knew were words but could not have pronounced. I knew my mother had sung to me. I knew it the way you know a fact in a ledger. The knowing sat in me like a coin in an empty purse, rattling.

"That's done, then," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Not quite," said Elodie.

* * *

She had been standing behind me, and I had been so busy conducting my private funeral that I hadn't noticed her step forward. Elodie Crane noticed things; it was the family trade, apparently, noticing things and not saying so until the worst possible moment.

"He doesn't lose it," she said to Bram. "Not all of it. I'm paying a second toll."

Bram's eyebrows performed a slow, ancient arithmetic. "One per passenger."

"I'm a passenger. This is my toll. I just happen to be spending it on him." She turned to me, and her chin was set in the way it set whenever Vess told her she couldn't do something, which meant she was about to do exactly that thing with witnesses. "Don't argue. You're terrible at arguing. You once lost a debate to a tax assessor."

"The tax assessor was right," I said weakly.

"He was *smug*. Sit down."

There was nowhere to sit except the gunwale, so I sat on the gunwale, and Elodie Crane put her hand over mine on the black wood, and gave away a memory of me.

I felt it go. That's the part I still can't write down without stopping — I felt it leave her, felt the specific gravity of it, and I knew which one it was because I remembered it too, from the inside of my own head where it still lived, untouched, returned to me like a letter marked *undeliverable, addressee kept the copy.*

It was the night of the frost fair, four years ago. I had carved her a bone whistle — a foolish gift, a nothing gift, made from an offcut I should have thrown out — and she had blown it wrong on purpose to make it shriek, and laughed, and said, "You're the only person in this whole guild who gives me things that aren't lessons." And then, quieter, watching the lanterns: "When I was little I thought everyone could hear the dead. I thought it was normal, like being able to whistle. Then I found out it was just you, and I decided if the universe gave the hearing to only one person, it must be because that person needed help carrying it. So I've been helping. You never noticed. That was the point."

She had never told me that. She was telling me now, in effect, by handing the moment across the gunwale into Bram's waiting dark, buying back for me the sound of my mother's voice at the price of her own keeping of it.

The memory sank into the boat. Bram struck his pole against the hull once — the first stroke, no refunds now — and said, in a voice that had gone strange, almost gentle, "Paid in full. Both of you. Board."

"Why?" I asked her, as the river took us. My mother's lullaby was back in my head, real, warm, off-key in the exact way I'd forgotten I'd forgotten. I was holding it carefully, like a bird with a broken wing. "Why would you spend your own toll on—"

"Because you were going to give it up alone," she said, "in secret, like you do everything, like you notarized my sale in secret, like you'll probably try to die in secret somewhere under this mountain if I don't keep an eye on you." She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the far shore, where a crack in the world glowed faintly gold — the way down, the way under. "I'm done letting people pay for things alone, Juniper. Starting with you."

I am a ledger-keeper. I know what it means when a debt appears in two columns at once, owed and owing, cancelled and uncancelled. What passed between us on that boat was not a transaction. Transactions end. This had the unmistakable feel of a thing that intended to continue.

"Elodie," I started.

"Don't," she said. But she was smiling at the far shore, and her hand was still near mine on the wet wood, not touching, not leaving either.

* * *

The crossing took a day and a night, or possibly a night and a day; the sky had stopped keeping order. The drowned river was full, as rivers are, of what had fallen into it. I heard them — of course I heard them, I always hear them — the settled dead beneath the water, not screaming like the chorus above, just murmuring, patient as sediment. They knew where we were going. Several of them, in the voices of drowned travellers, wished us luck. One asked Sable to deliver a last word she'd been holding for forty years, and Sable, to her credit, wrote it down on the spot.

Near the far shore, Bram spoke for the first time since boarding.

"The King will offer you a bargain," he said, eyes fixed ahead. "He always offers a bargain. It will be fair. That's the trap of him — he is scrupulously, perfectly fair, and fairness is not the same as mercy, and he has never learned the difference." The ghost in his shadow stirred. "I sold someone to him once. A long time ago. I've been ferrying his tolls ever since, hoping the work would balance the book." His jaw tightened. "It doesn't. Nothing balances. You just keep rowing."

"And if we break the contract?" Elodie asked quietly. "The one that bought me. What happens to the ones like her?"

Bram was silent a long moment. The ghost in his shadow lifted her head, and though she had no face I could read, I swear she was listening harder than any of us.

"Then," Bram said slowly, "for the first time in six hundred years, the ledger opens." He drove the pole home into the shallows, and the keel ground against the shore of the world's edge, and ahead of us the mountain rose black against a starless sky, its roots split by a door of gold light that breathed, slow and patient, like something asleep.

Or like something waiting to be woken.

"Off my boat," said Bram. "And whatever he offers you — remember that I told you he'd be fair."

The door breathed again, and this time, faintly, from deep beneath the mountain, I heard the shelved souls turn in their sleep — thousands of them, all at once, like pages.

They had heard us coming.
