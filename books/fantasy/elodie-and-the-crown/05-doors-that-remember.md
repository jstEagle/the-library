## 5. Doors That Remember

The mountain swallowed us politely.

That was the worst part, I decided somewhere around the third hour of descent. Not the cold, which had teeth. Not the dark, which Juniper's carved lanterns held at bay like patient dogs. It was the politeness of it—the way the passage widened to receive us, the way the air smelled faintly of ink and candlewax instead of stone and rot, as if we were expected. As if someone down here had already entered our names in a book and drawn a neat little line beneath them.

"Stop narrating," I told myself out loud, because my mother's habit of talking to herself had apparently been hereditary after all, along with the jaw and the terrible stubbornness.

Behind me, Bram shifted the ferry-pole he'd refused to leave at the river, though there was nothing left up there for him to pole across but fog and regret. His ghost—his brother Tam, bound to his shoulder like a second shadow—flickered in the lantern light. Wren the hedge-witch walked last, humming something old under her breath, the way she did when she was listening for last words that hadn't been said yet.

And Juniper walked beside me, close enough that our sleeves brushed, saying nothing, which from Juniper was practically a sonnet.

"You're staring," I said.

"I'm observing."

"You're staring *observantly*."

His mouth did the thing it did—not quite a smile, more a door opening a crack onto a warm room. "There are doors ahead," he said. "I thought you'd want to know before you walked into one."

I stopped. Ahead, the corridor opened into a gallery, and the gallery was lined—floor to vaulted ceiling—with doors. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They stood free-standing, unhinged, some leaning against the walls like furniture in an abandoned house, some floating a hand's width off the ground. They were every kind: oak and iron and birch and bone, palace doors with gilt handles, cottage doors with horseshoe nails, one that was clearly just a garden gate painted blue.

Each one was slightly ajar. And through each gap came light—not firelight, not starlight (there was no such thing anymore), but a warm amber glow like memory given a body.

"Oh," said Wren softly. "Oh, they're *remembering*."

She wasn't wrong. As I drew closer I could hear it, faintly, spilling through the gaps along with the light: laughter, a dog barking, rain on a roof, a woman singing badly and happily while she kneaded bread. Each door played back what had happened on its other side—or rather, what had happened to whoever had once passed through it. Doors that remembered. The mountain kept receipts.

"They replay everyone who enters," Juniper murmured. He'd gone pale in the lantern light, and his hand had drifted to the ledger at his belt without him seeming to notice. "It's how the court keeps its accounts. Every soul that comes down here leaves its memories behind like footprints."

"Wonderful," said Bram flatly. "A house that gossips."

"It's worse than gossip." Wren touched one doorframe with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "These aren't copies. Look at the wear on the thresholds. These doors have been *lived*. The mountain doesn't record memories—it keeps them. Feeds them light so they don't go stale." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "Your King isn't hoarding souls, girl. He's hoarding *days*."

I didn't answer, because just then I saw it: a door near the center of the gallery, white birch with a silver handle shaped like a crescent moon, glowing brighter than all the rest. And carved into its grain, small and precise, in handwriting I knew better than my own—

*Elodie Crane.*

My name. In my mother's hand.

* * *

"I wouldn't," Juniper started, and then stopped, because we both knew I would.

I put my hand on the handle. It was cold, then warm, then cold again, like a pulse deciding. Through the gap spilled a smell I hadn't smelled in twenty-two years: beeswax candles and lilies and the particular soap Vess Crane used only on feast days.

I opened the door.

Light poured over me like water, and I stepped through into a christening.

The guildhall, but younger—newer timber, brighter banners, and crowded with people half of whom were dead now and all of whom were loud. A priestess with smoke-grey robes held me, an infant, aloft, and I watched my own tiny face screw up and produce a wail that could strip paint. My mother stood below in dove-grey, laughing, crying, reaching up—and beside her, holding a candle he was clearly afraid of, a young man with ink-stained fingers and hair that stuck up like he'd lost an argument with a windstorm.

Juniper. Twenty years old and terrified of a taper.

"That's not—" he began behind me, strangled.

"It absolutely is," I said. "Look at your hair."

"Everyone's hair was like that in—"

"*Shh.*" Because the door-memory had turned, the way dreams turn, and now the priestess was speaking the blessing, and the blessing was not the standard one. I knew the standard one; I'd heard it at three guild christenings since. This one was different. This one had clauses.

*"…and let this child be a lamp against the long dark, her soul a hinge upon which the wards may swing…"*

The hall cheered. Everyone cheered. They toasted and danced and drank, and not one of them—not my mother, not the priestess, not the grinning guildsmen—heard what had actually been said. But I heard it. And I felt Juniper hear it beside me, felt him go rigid as a struck bell.

"A hinge," he whispered. "They blessed you into a *hinge*."

The memory folded itself shut around us like a book closing, and we stumbled back out into the gallery, into the cold, and I stood there shaking with a rage so clean and total it didn't even feel like mine.

"They knew," I said. "At my christening. They *blessed* it. They made it sound holy."

"Vess might not have known the exact terms—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't do that. Don't sand the edges off it for me. I love her, Juniper, and I will forgive her or I won't, but either way I'm going to look at every ugly inch of this first."

He nodded slowly. And then, because he was Juniper, he said quietly: "There's another door. Behind that one. It has your name too—but different handwriting."

Of course it did.

* * *

The second door was black oak, unadorned, with an iron handle worn smooth by many hands. My name was carved into it in letters that were not letters—a script like frost forming, like the Mountain King's own accounting hand. The light leaking through this gap was colder. Thinner.

I opened it alone. Some doors you walk through with company. Some you owe yourself.

Inside: a birthing room. Rain against shutters. My mother, young and grey-faced and soaked with sweat, holding a red, furious newborn—me—while a figure stood at the foot of the bed that made my skin try to crawl off my bones.

It was tall and thin and pale as paper, robed in grey, with a face like a page waiting to be written on. Its eyes were the color of ledger-ink. The archivist. I recognized it from Juniper's reluctant descriptions, and recognizing it was somehow worse than meeting it blind.

*"The price is agreed,"* it said, in a voice like a quill scratching. *"One soul, freely covenanted, to anchor the border wards for as long as the stars endure. In exchange: the Guild of Impossible Things continues to exist. The famine ends. The winter lifts."*

*"She's my daughter,"* my mother said, and her voice cracked down the middle. *"Not collateral."*

*"Everything is collateral,"* the archivist replied, without cruelty—which was the horrifying part. There was no malice in it at all. Only arithmetic. *"That is what a ledger is. But note the word, madam. Freely covenanted. The contract requires consent. Yours will serve until hers can be obtained."*

*"She'll never—"*

*"Then the wards will fail, the stars will go out, and the winter will return and not lift. Weigh it."*

My mother looked down at my infant face. I watched her weigh it. I watched her lose.

*"Take the debt,"* she whispered. *"But write it so she never knows. Let her have her life before she has her sentence."*

*"Compassion,"* said the archivist, dipping its quill, *"is simply deferred billing."*

The memory closed. I came out of the door with my heart slamming and my hands steady, which surprised me. Steady hands. Mother's gift after all.

Juniper caught my elbow. "Elodie—"

"He's not a monster," I heard myself say. My voice sounded far away. "The King. He's not a monster at all, is he? He's a *bookkeeper*. Like you. Someone who looked at a world full of unbearable debts and decided the kindest thing was to keep immaculate records. To make sure everything was paid. Everything balanced. Everything *fair*." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Fair. Stars above. He probably thinks he saved us. Famine ended, winter lifted, one child billed. Perfectly equitable. He confused accounting with mercy and built a kingdom out of the mistake."

Juniper was quiet a moment. Then: "I've done that."

"I know," I said. "So have I. That's what frightens me. I understand him, Juniper. Down to the ink-stained fingertips. And I think—" I looked down the endless gallery, thousands of doors glowing with other people's days, whole lives filed away like receipts. "I think that's why we're going to win. You can't beat a monster with mercy. But a bookkeeper? A bookkeeper can be *audited*."

Wren appeared at my shoulder, Bram and his ghost behind her, and for once nobody made a joke. Even Tam the ghost looked grave, flickering like a candle in a draft.

"There's one more door," Wren said gently. "Smaller. Off to the side. It's not got a name on it, but it's been following you since we entered the gallery. Doors do that, sometimes, when the memory inside belongs to someone who hasn't finished feeling it yet."

It stood apart from the others, plain pine, humble, the kind of door found on a pantry or a servant's stair. No carving. Just a smear of candle-smoke above the frame.

I opened it.

My mother's grief. That was all it was—that was the whole room. Vess Crane, alone, at night, year after year, sitting at her desk with my christening candle burning down, holding a copy of the contract she'd signed and reading it until the words blurred. Not weeping. Worse than weeping. *Reconciling accounts.* Marking the interest accruing on her daughter's soul. Calculating, every single night, whether there was any payment, any trade, any impossible thing the Guild of Impossible Things could produce to buy her back.

Every night for twenty-two years. And every morning, coming down to breakfast and teasing me about burnt toast, because compassion, it turns out, is also deferred grief.

I closed the door very carefully, the way you close a door on someone sleeping.

"Well," I said, and my voice only shook a little. "Now I'm angry at *everyone*, including myself, which feels appropriately thorough. Come on. If the King wants a debtor, he should know the Crane family pays its debts in person."

We went on down. And the corridor, polite as ever, widened to receive us—and at its end, impossibly far below, I began to hear it: a sound like ten thousand voices breathing in unison, and beneath it, faint and dry and patient, the scratch of a quill that had been writing for a thousand years, and was, even now, adding our names to the page.
