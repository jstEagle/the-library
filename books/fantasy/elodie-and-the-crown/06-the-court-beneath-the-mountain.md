## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The last door remembered us wrong, and I think that was a mercy.

It showed Elodie a christening in a snowbound chapel, her mother's face young and unbroken, and it showed me nothing at all—just a blank pane of bone, fogged like breath on glass. The doors had been replaying us for three days as we descended: Bram's drowning, Sable's last words collected like pressed flowers, my mother's kitchen. But this one gave Elodie back her beginning and left me a mystery even to the mountain. I chose to be flattered.

"Your ledger's blank," Sable said, peering at the fogged bone over my shoulder. She was wrapped in every scarf she owned plus two she'd traded for at the drowned river, and she still looked cold. Hedge-witches feel cold in their bones; it's how they know they're still trading. "That's not natural, bone-carver."

"It's private," I said.

"It's *empty*."

Bram said nothing. Bram hadn't said much since the river, since Elodie paid his toll with a memory that wasn't hers to give and made it look like arithmetic. He poled the air now the way he'd poled the water, out of habit, and his bound ghost drifted at his shoulder like a gull following a boat. The ghost had started humming. None of us asked what the tune was. Some questions you save for when you can afford the answer.

Then the corridor opened its mouth, and we walked into the throat of the world.

* * *

I have heard the dead my whole life. I want to be clear about that, because everything after this depends on scale. Hearing the dead is like hearing rain on a roof—you learn to sleep through it, to read by it, to find it almost companionable. A ghost is a small sound. A grievance, mostly. Sometimes a lullaby.

The under-court was not rain on a roof.

The hall was vast enough to have weather. Its ceiling was lost in a glow like overcast, and the glow came from shelves—rows upon rows of them, rising into the dark, carved from the same pale bone as the doors, and on every shelf sat a soul. Not metaphorically. Little vessels of stone and horn and knucklebone, each one lit from inside, each one burning with the steady amber of a lamp someone forgot to trim. Ten thousand lanterns. A hundred thousand. They went up until the eye surrendered, and every single one of them was *saying something.*

Not aloud. Not separately. In unison—the way a choir is unison, which is to say a thousand throats agreeing on one grief. It wasn't words. It was the shape words leave behind. It pressed against my skull like deep water, and I put my hand against the wall to keep my feet, and Elodie caught my elbow without looking, because she'd learned my tells somewhere along the road and never once mentioned it.

"They're all talking," I managed.

"You hear them." It wasn't a question. Elodie had stopped asking those around me about a week ago, when I'd answered one too many things nobody had said aloud. "What are they saying?"

"Nothing. Everything. They're saying *count us.*" My voice came out strange. "They've been counting themselves for years. Nobody's balanced them."

Sable had gone very still, the way she did when she smelled a bargain or a lie. "This isn't a court," she said softly. "It's a *warehouse.*"

"It's both," said a voice like a ledger closing. "The best institutions always are."

* * *

He came down the central aisle between the shelves, and I will describe him honestly, because honesty is the only currency I have left that hasn't been spent.

The Mountain King was not large. That was the first shock. Stories make him a giant, a troll, a thing of granite and hunger, and instead he was a spare, grey man in robes the colour of slate, with a face like a page written on so many times the words had gone soft. He moved carefully, the way old accountants move, as if the floor were a column of figures he didn't want to smudge. At his shoulder floated the archivist.

The archivist was the thing that had bought Elodie.

I knew it before anyone told me. I knew it the way you know a signature you notarized—it was in the shape of its hands, long and white and precise, hands built for turning pages and taking possession. Its face was smooth as an egg, featureless except for two dark seams where eyes should be, and when it turned those seams toward Elodie, every hair on my arms stood up and began reading the contract aloud.

"Vessel Crane," the King said. "Ferryman. Hedge-witch. And—" the soft face turned to Elodie, and something in it *leaned*, the way a hungry man leans toward bread, "—the collateral herself. You've saved me a retrieval fee."

"She's not collateral," I said. "She's a person."

"She's a balance," the King said, without malice, which was infinitely worse. "Come. Walk with me. Negotiation goes better in motion; standing still, men dig in like posts."

And because none of us had a better plan, we walked.

* * *

He led us down an aisle of souls, and I hated him for it, and I think he knew, and I think that was also part of the negotiation.

"Do you know why the wards above run on a soul?" he asked conversationally, gesturing at the glowing shelves with a hand like a clerk's. "Because wards are promises, and promises need surety. Your guildmasters, your kings, your wise women—they wanted a border that would hold forever. Forever is expensive. So they shopped for a price that could pay itself indefinitely." He glanced sideways at Elodie. "A life sold before it could consent pays beautifully. The consent is presumed. Presumed consent accrues interest. It's elegant."

"It's theft," Elodie said.

"It's *bookkeeping.*" For the first time something moved behind the soft face—not anger, but the flicker of a man who has been misunderstood for centuries and has stopped expecting better. "You think I hoard these?" He spread his arm at the ten thousand lamps. "Every soul here was sold, gambled, tithed, or abandoned. Someone above wrote them off. I keep them. I keep them *lit.* Do you know what happens to an unwritten soul? It frays. It forgets its own name and becomes weather, becomes noise, becomes the voices your bone-carver hears and cannot help. I am not their jailer. I am their *archivist.*"

"You're their pawnbroker," Sable said.

"I prefer custodian." He stopped walking. Above us, the unison swelled, and I felt it in my teeth: *count us, count us, count us.* "But you didn't come to argue taxonomy. You came to buy. So let us talk terms."

The archivist drifted forward and opened a case at its breast—a little door in its own chest, which I will be describing to no one ever—and drew out a scroll. Even from six feet away I recognized the hand on it. Mine. Younger mine. The notary's mark of a nineteen-year-old Juniper Crane who'd been told it was a standard ward-annuity and believed it, because believing is cheaper than asking.

"You notarized this," the King said, watching me. "Interesting. The instrument requires a witness with the gift. Only a soul-hearer can witness a soul-sale. Did you wonder why the Guild took you in, Vessel Crane? Bone-carvers with your particular ear are rare. You were hired to sign yourself."

The floor tilted. Or I did. Elodie's hand found mine, and I gripped it, and the unison roared once, approvingly, like a sea acknowledging a shipwreck.

"Terms," the King repeated, gently, horribly. "The girl returns to the surface. The wards receive a replacement soul of equal weight—weight being a technical term, measured in attachment, consequence, and love. Love weighs heavily. It always has." The seams of the archivist's eyes brightened. "One soul, freely offered, freely given, and the sky relights within the season. Refuse, and the collateral remains, and the stars stay dark, and the winter finishes what it started. Those are the only instruments on my desk. I regret the selection, but I did not write the market."

"And if we offer nothing," Bram said. His first full sentence in days, and it came out river-flat and final. "If we just take her and go."

"Then the wards fail entirely, and everything above dies slowly instead of quickly, and her soul stays shelved regardless, because the contract holds until paid or broken, and it cannot be broken." The King folded his hands. "Only paid."

The unison rose around us, ten thousand voices saying *count us,* and I understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he wasn't lying. That was the dread of it. He was telling the truth as his books recorded it, and his books were the world.

"One soul of equal weight," I said.

"One soul of equal weight," he agreed. "You have until the final star's last light. Above, they count nine remaining. I would budget accordingly."

Elodie's grip on my hand had gone tight enough to hurt. I didn't look at her. If I looked at her, she'd see the arithmetic already running behind my eyes—attachment, consequence, love—and she'd know exactly what I intended to spend.

The archivist closed its chest-door with a click like a period at the end of a sentence.

And in the unison of ten thousand shelved souls, clear as a bell through the roar, I heard one voice that was not in unison at all. One voice, close, amused, and terribly familiar, humming a tune I'd last heard drifting at Bram's shoulder.

The ferryman's ghost had been shelved here once. And it was trying to tell me something.
