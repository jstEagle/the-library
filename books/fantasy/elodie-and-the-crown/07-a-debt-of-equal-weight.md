## 7. A Debt of Equal Weight

There is a particular kind of silence in the under-court, the kind that comes after a negotiation has been adjourned rather than concluded. The Mountain King had dismissed us with the courtesy of a man who owns the room, the mountain, and everything breathing inside it. *One soul of equal weight,* he'd said, as though weighing turnips. *Bring me your answer by the last star's last light.* And then the archivist had folded itself back into the shadows between the soul-shelves like a ledger closing, and we were left standing in a hall lit by ten thousand stolen lives.

The guild's delegation—such as it was—retreated to a side gallery carved from pale stone, where Bram's ghost-bound ferryman instincts had already identified a bench that didn't creak. Sable the hedge-witch sat cross-legged on the floor, trading last words with a shelf-soul that glowed faintly amber near her knee. Elodie was pacing. Vess stood at the gallery's edge, staring down into the court below with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she despises.

And I was carving.

I always carve when I'm lying. It's a habit I picked up young: keep the hands busy, keep the face dull, and no one looks twice at the bone-carver. Bone is honest material—it remembers what it was—but a carver can be anyone. That's been my whole life's trick. Be dull. Be invisible. Be nobody worth weighing.

So while Elodie paced and Sable murmured to the dead and Bram polished a ferry-pole that hadn't touched water since the river drowned, I took a sliver of rib-bone from my kit and began to carve my own name into it.

Not my whole name. Just enough. Juniper Crane, bone-carver, third-rate guildsman, first-rate coward on the subject of his own importance. A soul-token. The Mountain King's archivist would recognize the craft; I'd notarized enough of its contracts over the years for it to know my hand. One soul of equal weight, the King had demanded. And here was the arithmetic I'd been doing since moonrise, quietly, in the corner of my skull where I keep the sums I don't say aloud:

Elodie Crane was sold at birth. Her soul anchors the wards that hold up the sky. She is the guildmaster's daughter, the only person alive who can make me laugh at my own jokes, the woman who once traded three impossible things for my umbrella because she said I looked ridiculous holding it. Her weight is considerable.

Juniper Crane hears dead people and tells no one. Juniper Crane notarized the sale of the girl he loves without knowing it, took his fee, and went home to supper. His weight is—

Well. His weight is whatever the scale says when nobody's looking.

I pressed the last stroke of the knife home and felt the token take: a small cold weight in my palm, humming with the particular resonance of a soul offered freely, which is the only kind the under-court honors. Freely given. That was the phrase that would make it legal. That was the phrase that would make it binding. I'd written enough contracts to know exactly which words to use, and I used all of them.

I meant to slip away during the next watch. I meant to walk down to the dais alone, set the token on the King's scales, and be done before anyone could do anything so foolish as object. It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the same plan every martyr in every story has ever had, and like all of them, I failed to account for one variable:

People who trade in debts are very hard to sneak past.

* * *

It was Sable who caught me. Of course it was. Hedge-witches deal in last words, and there is no last word more eloquent than a man saying goodbye to his own belongings without saying goodbye.

"You've packed," she said, appearing at my shoulder in the way she does, which is to say without any warning whatsoever, smelling of ash and rosemary. "Your kit's buckled. Your knife's wrapped. And you gave Bram your good coat an hour ago and told him you were warm."

"I am warm."

"You're never warm. You complain about the cold the way other men complain about their mothers." She held out her hand. "Show me what's in your fist."

"It's nothing."

"Juniper." She said my name the way she says the names of the dying—gently, and with the certainty that she'll be proven right. "I have heard the last words of eleven thousand people. Do you know how many of them said *it's nothing*? All of them. Every single one. It's nothing, it's fine, tell my wife—show me your fist."

I showed her my fist.

She looked at the token for a long moment. Then she did something I have never seen her do: she sat down heavily, right there on the cold floor, and laughed—not her dry cackle but something rawer underneath it.

"Oh, you *idiot*," she said. "Oh, you magnificent, self-important idiot. You were going to just—walk down there. Alone. In the night. Like a man leaving a tavern without paying his tab."

"Someone has to pay."

"Yes! Someone! Not *only* you!" She was on her feet now, and her voice carried, and that was how Bram came over, and that was how Elodie stopped pacing, and within the space of thirty heartbeats the entire gallery knew exactly what I'd intended, and the look on Elodie's face is something I will be carving out of my memory for the rest of however long I have to live.

"You were going to leave," she said. Quietly. Which is worse than loudly. Elodie loud is a storm; Elodie quiet is the thing the storm is afraid of. "You were going to stand in front of that King and offer yourself and not even—" Her voice caught. "You didn't even plan to say goodbye."

"Saying goodbye seemed inefficient."

"Don't you *dare* be funny about this."

"I'm not being funny. I'm being practical. You're worth more than—"

"If you finish that sentence," she said, "I will learn bone-carving purely so I can carve something unflattering onto your headstone."

Bram cleared his throat. He's a big man, the ferryman, big and slow-seeming, with river mud permanently under his nails and a bound ghost who rides his shoulders like a scarf made of regret. When he speaks, which is rarely, it lands like a stone in still water.

"You made us mourners," he said.

I blinked. "What?"

"Mourners." He pointed one thick finger at me, then at himself, then at Sable. "You decided, all on your own, that we would grieve you. That we would walk out of this mountain carrying your absence. You spent our grief without asking, like it was coin in your pocket." His jaw worked. "My ghost has been bound to me forty years because I wouldn't let him go. Forty years I've refused to pay that toll. And you think I'll stand aside while you pay yours cheap?"

"That's different—"

"It's the same," said Sable. "Worse, actually. He's offering himself up like the debt's his alone, and it isn't. We came down here as a guild. Impossible things, plural." She snatched the token from my hand before I could react, turned it over, and read the terms I'd carved into it with a professional's eye. Then she spat on the floor. "Freely given, it says. Well. Here's what's freely given, Juniper Crane: my opinion. And my opinion is that this token is worthless unless it carries all our names."

"You can't share a soul."

"No," said a voice from the gallery entrance. "But you can share a price."

The Mountain King stood in the doorway.

He does not enter rooms so much as arrive in them, the way winter arrives. He filled the gallery door entirely, crown of unlit star-iron catching what little light the shelved souls threw, and behind him drifted the archivist, pale and patient as paper. How long he had been listening, I couldn't say. Long enough. With bookkeepers, they always let you finish the entry before they audit it.

"Your Majesty," Vess began, stepping forward with guildmaster reflexes, "we were just—"

"Negotiating," said the King. "Badly. In secret. Against each other." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't expected: not anger. Interest. The interest of a man who has read the same ledger for a thousand years and found, at last, an unfamiliar column. "You offered yourself believing yourself worthless. They demand to share a price believing you priceless. These are opposite errors, bone-carver, and both of them are love." He extended one vast hand, and Sable—Sable, who fears nothing—placed my token in it with the deference of a hedge-witch handing over a particularly dangerous mushroom.

The King weighed it in his palm. Whatever he found there, it made him go quiet for a long moment.

"One soul of equal weight," he said finally. "That was my term. But I confess the scales beneath this mountain have grown... curious. Three souls, freely entwined, each insisting on the others' worth above their own—that is a weight I have not measured in an age." His eyes moved across us: me, Sable, Bram, and lingering longest of all on Elodie, whose chin was up and whose hands were shaking and whose eyes promised murder if anyone solved anything without her again. "Very well. Here is my revised term, and it is final."

The hall hushed. Even the shelved souls dimmed, leaning in.

"By the last star's last light—the final ember in the sky above, guttering even now—you will bring me either one soul of equal weight, or three souls bound together, each given for the others. Choose your arithmetic. The wards will drink deep either way, and the sky will hold." He closed his fist around my token, and I felt it go out, like a candle pinched. "Refuse, and the last star dies, and the long winter becomes the only season, forever. There is no fourth option. There is no clever clause. I have written every contract beneath this mountain myself, and none of them leak."

He turned to go. Then paused.

"For what it is worth," he said, without turning around, "the girl's mother sold her for less than you are all willing to pay. That has never happened before. I find I do not know what to do with it."

And then he was gone, and the archivist after him, and the four of us stood in the gallery with a deadline instead of a plan.

Elodie rounded on me first.

"One soul or three," she said. "You heard him. So here's what happens now: nobody decides anything alone. Nobody walks anywhere alone. Nobody *carves* anything alone." She held out her hand, palm up, and waited until—feeling like a man surrendering his knife to a customs officer—I put my bone-carving kit in it. She upended it onto the bench. Every sliver, every blank token, every tool. "We do the arithmetic together, Juniper. That's the whole point of a guild."

"The whole point of a guild," said Sable, retrieving her favorite knife from the pile, "is that when one member tries to die for the rest, the rest get to be insufferable about it forever."

Bram said nothing. He just put his enormous hand on the back of my neck, briefly, the way you'd steady something that might blow away, and then went back to his bench as if nothing had happened. His ghost stirred on his shoulders and, for the first time since I'd known it, seemed to be looking at me with something like sympathy.

Above us, beyond miles of stone, the sky's last stars were going out one by one. Somewhere up there, the final ember was already guttering, counting down our deadline with the patience of all things that burn.

And beneath my ribs, in the place where I keep the sums I don't say aloud, a new number had started forming. Because the King had said *no clever clause*, and I am a bone-carver who spent years hiding inside a guild of impossible things, and there is one thing I know about contracts written by lonely men who confuse accounting with mercy:

They always leak.

I just needed Elodie to help me find where.
