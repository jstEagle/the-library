## 8. Elodie Refuses

I learned about Juniper's offer the way one learns most terrible things: from someone who thought they were being helpful.

The hedge-witch, Sable, found me in the guest alcove the Mountain King had assigned us—*assigned*, as though we were visiting dignitaries and not hostages with better posture. She didn't knock. Hedge-witches never do; doors are a courtesy of the settled folk.

"He's done something stupid," she said.

"That's not news. That's weather."

"Elodie." She said my name flat, the way you'd set down a heavy thing you were tired of carrying. "He's offered his soul. Equal weight for yours. He signed it into the King's ledger an hour ago while you were sleeping."

The candle between us guttered, and I had the strange, detached thought that even fire down here was on a budget.

"An hour ago," I repeated.

"You were sleeping," Sable said again, as if that excused anything, as if sleep were a crime I'd committed against my own rescue. "Bram's furious. He wanted to share the price—wanted all three of us to pay together—and Juniper went behind our backs like a man slipping a coin into a beggar's cup so no one has to see him do it."

"And what did Bram do?"

"What ferryman do? He swore in three dead languages and started sharpening his pole."

I sat down on the cot. The stone was cold through my skirts, and I was grateful for it, because I needed something in that moment to be simply, honestly cold instead of complicated.

Here is what nobody understands about being collateral: you spend your whole life being *handled*. My mother sold me before I had a name. The wards held because I existed in the wrong place. Every kindness I was ever shown came with the faint asterisk of *and she can't leave anyway*. So when a man carves himself out of the world to buy me back, the world calls it love, and it is love, and I know it is love—but it is also just one more person deciding what happens to my life without asking me.

Juniper Crane had spent years pretending to be ordinary so he wouldn't have to be seen. And now he'd arranged the grandest possible way to be unseen forever.

"No," I said.

Sable blinked. "No?"

"No. I don't accept the trade." I stood, and my knees only shook a little, which I counted as a personal triumph. "Where does the King keep his contracts?"

"The archive. But Elodie—the archivist doesn't let anyone—"

"Sable." I smiled at her, and I felt it go crooked, the way my mother's smile used to go when she was about to do arithmetic that would ruin someone. "I have been someone's asset since before I could walk. You think I've never read the fine print of my own existence?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and produced a stub of black candle and a key made, apparently, of a bird's wishbone.

"I trade in last words," she said. "Yours are going to be interesting. I want them."

"Done," I said, and meant it as a joke, and then didn't, because down here everything you say becomes a contract eventually, and I was beginning to think that might be the whole point.

* * *

The archive beneath the court was not a room. It was a throat—a long descending passage lined with shelves, and on the shelves, souls. Not jars, not lanterns, nothing so kind. They sat in their thousands like pale fruit, each one humming its own small note, and together they made a sound like a choir holding one breath for a hundred years.

The archivist waited at the bottom.

I had seen it once before, in the replaying door that showed my christening—the pale, patient thing that had come up out of the dark to make my mother an offer she'd been too grief-mad to refuse. Up close it was worse and better. Worse because it had no face worth describing, only a suggestion of features the way fog suggests hills. Better because it did not look at me with hunger or pity or any of the expressions people wear when they look at collateral. It looked at me the way Juniper looked at a ledger: assessing where I fit.

"You are not permitted here," it said. Its voice was dry paper.

"I'm the subject of the primary document," I said. "That makes me more than permitted. That makes me *load-bearing*."

A pause. Somewhere above us, a shelved soul hummed a little louder, as if amused.

"The bone-carver has already tendered payment," the archivist said. "His soul, of equal weight. The transaction awaits only the final star's consent."

"Then show me the original contract. Mine. The sale."

"It is not customary—"

"Neither," I said, "is a customer reading her own deed, but I notice your shelves are full of people who weren't asked anything, so perhaps custom and I should get acquainted elsewhere." I stepped past it. It let me. I would think about that later—that it *let* me—and what it meant that a bookkeeper's deepest instinct is to show you the books when you demand them loudly enough.

The contract hung on a hook of black iron, and it was written, of course, on bone. Thin as vellum, curved like a shoulder blade, and covered in script so fine it might have been etched by a needle threaded with hair. My name was there—or rather, the space where my name would be, filled in later in different ink, different hand. *One daughter-child, born under the failing wards, given freely.*

*Given freely.*

I read it four times. I read it a fifth time slowly, the way Juniper had taught me once, back in the guildhall, when he'd thought he was teaching me bookkeeping and was really teaching me how to catch lies: *the lie is always in the part that goes too smoothly, Elodie. Truth is clumsy. Fraud is elegant.*

This document was very elegant.

"Do you know what consent is?" I asked the archivist.

"A formal yielding of will, witnessed and recorded."

"Recorded. Yes. And this—" I tapped the bone, and my finger left no mark, though I swear the shelf-souls around us dimmed slightly, leaning in, "—records a giving. A free giving. By my mother. At my birth."

"The transaction is valid."

"The transaction is *fraudulent*," I said, and my voice cracked across the archive like ice on a pond, and every soul on every shelf went silent at once, which is a sound I hope never to hear again and will never forget. "My mother was grieving. My father had just died holding the border line. She was offered a bargain by a creature from under a mountain in the worst night of her life, and she said yes because grief says yes to anything that promises the pain will stop. That is not a free giving. That is a drowning woman signing over the lifeboat. Your own law—" I jabbed the bone again, "—your own law, the law this entire court runs on, requires consent. Freely given. Witnessed. The wards drink from this contract. They have been drinking from stolen water for twenty-six years, and everyone above thinks the sky is failing because of some cosmic winter, and it isn't. It's failing because the foundation was never real."

The archivist did not move. But the fog of its face shifted, and I understood—I suddenly, terribly understood—that I had not shocked it. I had *interested* it.

"You sound," it said, "like the bookkeeper."

"Like Juniper?"

"Like the first one. The King." It drifted closer, and the shelf-souls shrank from it, or leaned toward it; it was hard to tell which. "He was a ledger-keeper once, above. When the sky first began to fail, ages ago, he tried to save it the only way a keeper knows: by balancing. Something owed, something paid. He has been paying ever since. Collecting. Shelving. He believes if the accounts balance perfectly, the sky will hold." A pause, dry as dust. "They have never balanced perfectly. There has always been a remainder. He calls it the rounding error. It is standing in front of you."

"Me."

"You. The soul that was never quite willingly given. The flaw in every column." The archivist tilted its fog-face. "Do you know why the King agreed to hear your companions at all? Why he bargains, and sets deadlines, and plays at diplomacy? Because for the first time in an age, someone came down here carrying a debt he did not understand. The bone-carver offered a soul of equal weight—and the scales would not settle. They tipped. Toward *you*. Toward the unconsented. The books themselves are trying to tell him something, and he cannot read it, because he only knows how to read balance."

I looked down at the contract in my hands—at the elegant, fraudulent curve of it—and I felt something shift in my chest that was not fear. It was colder and cleaner than fear. It was the feeling of a lock recognizing its key.

"He can't read it," I said. "But I can. I'm the flaw. I'm the entry that won't reconcile. Which means I'm not collateral at all, am I? I'm the *error message*."

The archivist said nothing, which from a bookkeeper is a confession.

"Take me to the King," I said. "Both of you. Him, and Juniper, and Bram, and Sable, and every shelved soul in this mountain. Before the last star goes out, I am going to show the great bookkeeper of the under-court how to read his own books." I lifted the contract overhead, and the thousand humming souls brightened toward it like moths toward a moon they'd been promised. "And then I'm going to teach him the difference between a debt and a gift—starting with voiding mine."

Somewhere far above us, faint as a dying ember, the final star began to go out.

And I began, at last, to be the author.
