## 1. The Keeper of the Shut Gate

The door at the heart of the Ember Gate had not opened in a hundred years, and Elodie Coldharbour intended to keep it that way for at least one more night.

He said this to himself every evening, in more or less those words, as he climbed the switchback path from Thornfell with his lantern swinging and his supper cooling in its cloth. It was not a grand vow. It was not even, strictly speaking, necessary — the door had shown no interest in opening on its own since before his grandmother's grandmother had been born. But rituals are like bread: they do their work quietly, and you only notice how much you needed them when they're gone.

Besides, saying it aloud made the mountain feel less like an employer and more like a companion. A very silent companion who never answered and occasionally dropped scree at him, but a companion nonetheless.

"Good evening," he told the mountain, because his grandmother had taught him manners before she'd taught him anything else, and the mountain had never once corrected him.

The path was familiar enough that he could have walked it blindfolded, which was fortunate, because by midwinter the fog came up the valley like something poured rather than blown, and a man could lose the way between one step and the next if he didn't respect it. Elodie respected the fog. He respected the cold, the dark, the loose stones, the particular hollow note the wind made in the pass above the gate — a note like a wineglass rubbed at its rim, thin and patient. He respected everything about this mountain, in the way that a man respects a sleeping dog whose dreams he cannot read.

Thirteen turns of the path. At the ninth there was a flat stone where he'd once, at age eleven, carved a lopsided sun with his belt knife, and his grandmother had made him sit beside it for an hour learning why keepers don't mark the stone. *Because the stone remembers longer than we do,* she'd said, which had seemed deeply unfair at the time, given that the carving was still there and so was he.

At the thirteenth turn, the gate.

It was not, whatever the ballads claimed, a great iron portal wreathed in fire. The ballads were written by people who had never seen it, which was most people, which was rather the point. In truth the Ember Gate was modest — a door of black wood banded in dull metal, set into the living rock of the mountainside as though the mountain had grown around it the way a tree grows around a nail someone forgot to pull out. No hinges showed. No keyhole. Only a seam, straight and true, running floor to lintel, and above it, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, a crest: a circle broken by a single vertical line, like a candle flame seen through a shuttered window.

Elodie set down his lantern, unwrapped his supper — barley bread, hard cheese, an apple with ambitions toward mealy — and ate half of it, because Rule Three held that a keeper checks the gate fed and rested, never hungry or hurried, *for want makes men careless and carelessness opens doors.* His grandmother had written the rules herself, or copied them from whoever kept the gate before her, in a small square hand that leaned slightly leftward, as though even her letters were leaning away from the door.

There were nine rules. He could recite them in order, asleep, drunk, or mid-sneeze, and had done all three.

One: approach at dusk, leave before full dark.
Two: touch the seam each visit, and note what it tells your hand.
Three: come fed and rested.
Four: speak nothing at the gate but greetings and goodnights.
Five: tell no one what the gate is.
Six: mend the wall stones before they ask mending.
Seven: keep no journal.
Eight: trust the mountain's silence more than any voice.
Nine: —

Rule Nine was blank. Had always been blank, in every copy of the rules Elodie had ever seen, including the one folded into oilcloth inside his grandmother's coat, which now hung on a peg by his own door in town. When he was small he'd asked her what Rule Nine was. She had looked at him for a long moment with an expression he'd spent thirty years failing to name, and then she'd said, "You'll know it when you need it," and had gone back to salting beans, and that had been the whole of the conversation.

He finished the apple, brushed crumbs from his knees, and approached the door.

The ritual of checking was simple and took less time than the walk up. He laid his palm flat against the seam, shoulder-height, where the wood met the metal banding. Cold, as always. Still, as always. The seam ran true under his fingers, unbroken, unwarmed, giving back nothing but the mountain's own patience.

"Good evening," he said. "Still shut?"

The door did not answer. It never had. But he asked anyway, every night, because Rule Four permitted greetings and goodnights and he had decided long ago that a question shaped like a greeting was close enough, and because — though he would have denied this under torture — some part of him had always hoped that one night the door might say *yes, thank you, and yourself?*

"Good," he said to the silence. "Me too."

* * *

Thornfell at night smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and money changing hands in ways that would take too long to explain to a tax collector.

Elodie came down the last stretch of path as the town's lamps were being lit, one by one, along the crooked main street — a street that began nowhere important and ended somewhere less important, which was precisely why the smugglers loved it. Thornfell sat in a fold of the Thornfell mountains like a coin dropped into a pocket: easy to carry, easy to forget. Officially it appeared on no map worth trusting. Unofficially, it appeared on several excellent maps that circulated among people who paid for accuracy, and on all of them the notation was the same: *good roads, better discretion.*

He nodded to Marta at the Lamb and Lantern, who nodded back without pausing in her eternal argument with a keg. He stepped around Old Fenwick's goat, which had opinions about sidewalks and expressed them by occupying them. He was, by long practice, a man built to be overlooked — medium height, medium build, brown hair going grey at the temples in a way that suggested weather rather than wisdom, and a face that barkeeps forgot and portraitists would have charged extra for. This was not an accident. Keepers, his grandmother had said, are like the mortar between bricks: essential, invisible, and only noticed when something goes wrong.

He let himself into his house at the end of Tanner's Row — two rooms and a lean-to, rented from a woman who believed he was a surveyor for the mining concern in the next valley, which was a lie so boring it had proven bulletproof for fifteen years. He banked the fire. He hung his coat. He took down the tin box from the high shelf, and — because Rule Seven forbade journals and he was, at heart, an obedient man with one small rebellion — he moved a pebble from the left side of the box to the right.

One pebble per check. Left pile: unchecked days. Right pile: days kept. The right pile had been growing for thirty years and now numbered — he counted, as he did every night, touching each stone like a miser counting coins — ten thousand, four hundred and six.

Ten thousand nights. He tried to imagine them stacked end to end and found he couldn't; the number just lay there, heavy and smooth, like something the sea had polished.

"That's a lot of evenings, old man," he told himself, and put the box away, and felt the peculiar ache he always felt at this hour — the ache of a thing done well and witnessed by no one. Comfortable loneliness, his grandmother had called it, though she'd said it like a diagnosis. *A keeper's life is comfortable loneliness, Elodie. Learn to tell the difference between alone and lonely, and you'll outlast us all.* He had learned. Mostly. There were evenings — and tonight was shaping into one — when the difference got blurry around the edges, like ink in damp.

He was halfway through heating water for tea when the knock came.

This was unusual. People did not knock on Elodie Coldharbour's door. People did not, generally, remember that Elodie Coldharbour had a door. In fifteen years he could count his visitors on one hand: the landlady, twice, about the roof; a lost peddler, once, who'd stayed for soup and remembered neither the soup nor the house; and a tax assessor from the lowlands who had looked at Elodie, looked at his ledger, looked at Elodie again, and written *no assets* with the relief of a man closing a door on a room full of nothing.

The knock came again. Three raps, brisk, the knock of someone accustomed to doors opening.

Elodie turned down his lamp before answering — habit, though he couldn't have said whose rule it broke — and opened the door onto a stranger.

She was perhaps forty, wiry, rain-beaded, with the kind of face that had been handsome in the way of weathered cliffs and had decided long ago to stop pretending otherwise. She wore a road-stained traveling coat with far too many pockets, and she looked at Elodie with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a horse.

"You're the surveyor," she said.

"I'm a surveyor," Elodie agreed, which was technically true in the sense that he surveyed things, chiefly a door.

"Mm." She smiled like a knife being sheathed. "Funny thing. I've been asking after the mining concern all evening, and nobody in this town has heard of it."

"Fog's thick tonight," Elodie said. "People hear poorly in fog."

"They do." She tilted her head. "They also lie poorly, but I suppose that's not my business. My name is Cass. I run cargo over the passes, and my mule threw a shoe, and I need a farrier, a bed, and a hot meal, in reverse order of importance. Your landlady says you keep to yourself and pay on time, which in my experience means either a criminal or a saint, and saints are rarer on these roads."

"Criminals are also rare here," Elodie said.

"So I'm discovering." She was still looking at him in that pricing way, and something in her gaze snagged — briefly, like a fishhook catching a sleeve — on the tin box's shelf, or the coat on its peg, or some other detail he'd have sworn wasn't visible from the doorway. Then it passed, and she was just a tired traveler again. "Well? Farrier?"

"Tallow Lane, third house past the well. Tell him Bram sent you; he'll charge you less out of confusion."

"Bram," Cass repeated, tasting the lie and finding it adequately seasoned. "Much obliged, surveyor." She turned to go, then paused, rain dripping off her chin, and glanced back up at the mountain — at the black shoulder of it rising behind the town, where the pass wound up toward a door she could not possibly know about.

"Odd country, this," she said. "My grandmother came through here once, years back. Said there was a door in these hills that nobody talks about. Said the whole valley leans away from it, like folk leaning from a draft." She shrugged. "Superstition, probably. Goodnight, surveyor."

"Goodnight," said Elodie, and closed the door, and stood in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird.

*Superstition,* he told himself. *Smugglers' talk. She'll be gone by morning, shoe mended, memory of this town already blurring.* That was how Thornfell worked. That was how it had always worked. People passed through, and the town passed through them, and nothing stuck.

But his hands, he noticed, were not steady as he lit the lamp again. And when he went to bed he lay listening to the wind make its thin wineglass note in the high pass, and he thought — for no reason he could defend, with no evidence but a stranger's raised eyebrow and a grandmother's ghost standing behind every thought he owned — that tomorrow night, when he laid his palm against the seam, he ought to listen more carefully than usual.

Down in the valley, faint through the walls, he could hear the Lamb and Lantern spilling music and argument into the street. Somewhere in that noise was a smuggler named Cass, drinking on his coin-adjacent goodwill, telling stories to strangers.

He fell asleep telling himself it meant nothing.

The mountain, meanwhile, said nothing at all, in the manner of mountains. But if a man had been standing at the thirteenth turn of the path that night — and no one was; Rule One saw to that — he might have noticed that the frost on the black door had formed in a pattern no frost should form: not feathers, not ferns, but lines. Straight ones. Running floor to lintel.

Like a seam, deciding whether to hold.
