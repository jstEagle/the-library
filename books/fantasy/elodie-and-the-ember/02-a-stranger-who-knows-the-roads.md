## 2. A Stranger Who Knows the Roads

Elodie Coldharbour had a system for strangers.

It wasn't a complicated system. It was, in fact, almost embarrassingly simple, which was probably why it had worked for thirty years without anyone noticing it existed. When an unfamiliar face came up the mountain road toward his cottage, he would begin counting their steps. If they stopped before two hundred steps, they were lost and wanted directions, and he would give them directions to anywhere except the gate. If they passed two hundred steps, they were looking for him specifically, and then things became interesting.

Nobody had passed two hundred steps since the winter his grandmother's letters stopped coming.

So when the woman came up the road on a grey morning in late autumn—dust on her boots, mud on her coat hem, a smile already deployed like a weapon—Elodie began counting as he always did. One. Two. Three. She walked fast, the way people walk when they know exactly where they're going, which was itself suspicious, because nobody knew where they were going in Thornfell. Thornfell was not a place you went to. Thornfell was a place you ended up, usually by accident, occasionally by bad luck, and once, memorably, by falling off a mule.

Forty-one. Forty-two. She didn't slow at the bend where travelers always slowed, because the bend offered the first proper view of the valley and its forty-three chimneys, all of them crooked, all of them smoking, all of them leaning slightly eastward like old men eavesdropping on each other. She didn't look at the view at all. She looked at him.

Eighty-eight. Eighty-nine.

"Morning," she said, before he had decided whether to speak first. "You'd be the gatekeeper."

That was not a question. Questions could be answered or ignored. Statements had to be dealt with.

"I'm the keeper of a door," Elodie said carefully, "which is mostly the same thing but sounds less important."

The woman laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that suggested she found the world funny more often than she found it frightening, which in Elodie's experience was either wisdom or early symptoms of something terminal. "Less important," she repeated. "Right. And what do you keep, exactly?"

"A door," said Elodie. "In the mountain."

"What kind of door?"

"The kind that stays shut."

She considered this. She had a way of considering things that made him feel like a specimen pinned under glass, though not unpleasantly—more like a curiosity in a cabinet she intended to be polite about. Her name, when she finally gave it, was Cass. Just Cass. No surname offered, no explanation, and when he asked—because he did ask, because rules were rules—she said, "The roads don't use surnames. They get heavy."

Ninety-six. Ninety-seven. She passed the two hundred mark sometime around the moment she sat down at his kitchen table without being invited, which Elodie supposed was one way of resolving the ambiguity of the count. He put the kettle on. It seemed wiser than asking her to leave, and besides, his grandmother had raised him with certain doctrines, and one of them was: *never let a stranger leave hungry, because hungry strangers ask sharper questions.*

His grandmother had been full of doctrines. Most of them, in retrospect, were about doors.

* * *

Cass was a smuggler. She admitted this within the first four minutes, somewhere between the second cup of tea and the discovery that Elodie owned exactly three books and all of them were about weather. Smugglers, she explained, were misunderstood. Everyone thought they moved contraband. Really, she said, they moved *certainty*—things people needed so badly they'd pay anything, and the only crime was the price.

"And what do you carry?" Elodie asked.

"Whatever pays." She said it lightly, but there was a ledger behind her eyes; you could see the arithmetic running even when she smiled. "Silk going south. Iron going north. Letters that are technically nothing at all, because paper isn't illegal until someone reads it aloud." She paused. "And once, a choir."

Elodie waited for elaboration. None came. He had learned, over the years, that the best response to an unanswered thing was to pretend you hadn't noticed it, and so he pretended very hard, and she watched him pretend, and both of them understood the game perfectly, and neither acknowledged it. It was, he reflected later, the most honest conversation he'd had in a decade.

Thornfell tolerated smugglers the way a body tolerates a splinter: with irritation, and a grudging admission that pulling it out hurts worse than leaving it in. The town lived on what Cass called the fold roads—the goat paths and switchbacks that threaded through the Thornfell mountains like veins through a leaf, too narrow for soldiers, too steep for wagons, just wide enough for a determined person with a good mule and a better memory. Cass had a good mule. Her memory, Elodie was beginning to suspect, was the actual cargo.

"You know these mountains well?" he asked, because it seemed safe.

"I know them the way you know your own hands," she said. "Which is to say: I know where they hurt."

He liked that answer more than he wanted to. There was a whole philosophy hidden inside it, some doctrine of attention that his grandmother would have approved of, and Elodie felt the familiar small ache of recognizing a kindred rule-keeper in someone who broke every rule he'd ever heard of.

"Why come to me, then?" he asked. "If you know the mountains better than I do."

"Because there's one part of them I don't know at all." She set down her cup. "There's a door in your mountain. Everyone in Thornfell knows there's a door in your mountain. Do you know what everyone in Thornfell also knows?"

"That nobody asks about it."

"Nobody asks about it," she agreed. "Which means everybody wonders about it. Curiosity doesn't die in a town like this. It just goes underground. Like everything else worth keeping alive." She looked at him with an expression he couldn't quite file—sympathetic, appraising, faintly amused. "I've been running these roads for eleven years, gatekeeper. You want to know how many times someone's asked me about your door?"

"How many?"

"None." She held up a finger. "Until today."

* * *

Here is what Elodie knew about the Ember Gate, and he wanted it noted, for the record of his own conscience, that he knew it completely:

It was a door in the eastern face of the mountain, iron-banded, older than the town, older than the road, older than any name anyone currently used for anything. It had a crest worked into the lintel—a flame inside a circle, and around the circle, letters in a script that no one in Thornfell could read, including him, though he had spent more hours than he cared to admit trying. The door did not open. He checked it every night. He had checked it every night for thirty years, first with his grandmother and then alone, and in thirty years it had opened zero times, which he considered an excellent professional record.

He did not know who had built it. He did not know why. His grandmother had told him only this much: *it must never open, and you are the reason it won't.* Everything else—the who, the what, the terrible beautiful somewhere on the other side—had gone into the ground with her, or into the fire she'd burned her papers in, or into whatever silence lives between a child's questions and an old woman's refusals.

The crest, though. The crest he knew the way you know a scar. Flame in a circle. He had fallen asleep against that lintel as a boy, more than once, tracing the letters with his finger while his grandmother counted frost-flowers on the hinges. If you woke him at midnight and pressed a drawing of it into his hands, he would know it blind, drunk, grieving, or dead.

This is relevant, because of what happened next.

* * *

"So," Cass said, on her third cup of tea, having established that Elodie was polite, lonely, and possessed of a kettle older than most governments, "cargo."

"Cargo," Elodie repeated, warily.

"I picked up a run out of the lowlands last month. Ordinary freight—wool, lamp oil, a crate of nails, the usual boring bones of civilization. But the merchant who hired me was odd. Not strange-odd. Odd-odd. He paid half up front, which merchants don't do, and he paid in coin I'd never seen, and I have seen—" she gestured grandly at the concept of her own experience "—a lot of coin."

"Counterfeit?"

"If it were counterfeit, it'd be a good story and none of my business. It's not counterfeit. It's worse." She reached into her coat—an act that made Elodie's hand drift unconsciously toward the poker—and drew out a small leather purse, and from the purse, a single coin, which she placed on the table between them with the care of a surgeon laying out an instrument.

It was an old coin. Silver, worn nearly smooth, but heavy—heavier than it looked, the way certain stones are heavier than they look, the way certain silences are. And stamped into its face, fine and clear beneath a century of thumb-rubbing, was a crest.

A flame inside a circle.

Elodie looked at it for exactly as long as it took to recognize it, which was no time at all, and then he looked away from it for exactly as long as it took to decide what to do about that, which was considerably longer, during which time his tea went cold and his heart went somewhere he couldn't account for.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he had never told a living soul, the thing he kept the way misers keep their one gold piece, the thing that was not secret exactly but was *unshared*, which is different, which is lonelier: he knew that crest. He had always known that crest. It was on his door. It was the only ornament on his door, the only clue, the only message left by whoever built the thing, and in thirty years of nightly rounds he had touched it ten thousand times and never once known what it meant, and now here it was, sitting on his kitchen table, in a stranger's palm-print, wearing a stranger's dirt.

"It's pretty," he said.

Cass raised an eyebrow. "It's pretty."

"Old coins often are."

"This one's not just old." She turned it over with one finger. On the reverse: a mountain, and beneath the mountain, a word in that same unreadable script, the same letters as the ones above his door—or rather, the same *shapes*, arranged differently, like a song he almost recognized played in the wrong key. "This coin comes from a country that isn't on any map. I checked. I'm thorough about maps; it's a professional hazard. There is no country anywhere that stamps its money with a burning circle. Except apparently there was."

"Fascinating," said Elodie, and his voice did something complicated on the word *fascinating*, a small structural failure, a hairline crack running through the middle of it, and he heard the crack himself and hated it, because he had spent thirty years building a voice with no cracks in it.

Cass heard it too. Of course she heard it too. She was a smuggler; hearing cracks was her profession. People lied about cargo, about routes, about names, about everything, and the lie always cracked somewhere, and you learned to listen with the part of your ear that wasn't listening.

"Gatekeeper," she said gently, "you've seen this crest before."

"No," said Elodie.

"You're lying."

"Yes," said Elodie.

There was a moment—not long, maybe the length of a kettle cooling—when they simply sat with that, the honesty of it, the absurd mutual courtesy of a lie confessed the instant it was told. Elodie would think about that moment later, many times, usually at night, usually mid-round, usually with his hand on the cold iron of the door. Because it was the first crack. The first crack in thirty years of invisibility. He had been invisible so long he'd begun to mistake it for peace, the way you mistake numbness for comfort, and now here was this woman with dust on her boots and a silver coin and an ear for lies, sitting at his table, seeing him.

He did not enjoy being seen. He wanted to be clear about that. Being seen was dangerous. Being seen meant being *known*, and being known meant being *findable*, and everything his grandmother had ever taught him folded down, in the end, into a single instruction: *be findable to no one.* The gate must stay shut. The keeper must stay unseen. A visible keeper is a door with a handle.

But she had seen him anyway. And the strangest part—the part he turned over like the coin, again and again, that night and the next and the next—was that being seen hadn't hurt. Much. It had felt, in a way he had no vocabulary for and distrusted accordingly, almost like warmth.

* * *

"Why does it matter?" he asked eventually, because eventually is when he asked all his questions; haste was for other people. "A coin from nowhere. There are lots of coins from nowhere. Every dead kingdom leaves money behind. Numismatists weep over them. Museums hoard them. What makes this one worth carrying up two hundred steps to a doorkeeper's table?"

"Quantity," said Cass.

He waited.

"There aren't just one or two of these turning up," she said. "They're coming up the roads in handfuls. Purses of them. A farmer near Lowmarsh paid his tax bill in them and the tax collector bit one—old habit, testing silver—and swore it was true metal, right ore, right weight, mint-struck clean, and then couldn't tell him where it was struck *from*. My merchant had forty of them. I've seen sixty more change hands since. That's a hundred coins from a country with no name, no borders, no mint, no history anyone can point to—and they're behaving like currency. People are spending them. Shopkeepers are accepting them. Which means somewhere, someone is treating them as real."

"Someone's making them."

"Or," said Cass, and here she leaned forward, and the light caught her face in a way that made the humor drain out of it entirely, leaving something older and more careful underneath, "or somewhere, something is *remembering* them."

Elodie's hand, resting flat on the table, went cold. He noticed this the way you notice weather changing—in the joints first.

"Remembering," he repeated.

"Coins are memories you can hold," she said. "That's all money ever was. A promise that outlives the promiser. So if coins from a dead country are walking around acting alive—" she shrugged, and the shrug was somehow the most frightening thing in the room, more frightening than the coin, more frightening than the crack in his voice, because it was casual, because she said it the way you'd say *rain coming*— "then either there's a very committed forger with a very good press and no profit motive, or the country isn't as dead as the maps say."

"And you came to me because…?"

"Because you're the only man in these mountains whose door has that crest on it." She slid the coin across the table toward him, an inch, two inches, until it rested at the edge of his side, close enough to touch, far enough that touching it would be a decision. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I don't think you minted them; you strike me as a man who can barely mint tea. But you knew the crest the moment you saw it—I watched your face do a thing faces only do when they meet something they've met before. So I'm asking, gatekeeper, plainly, the way I ask everyone, because plain questions get plainer answers: what is that crest?"

And Elodie Coldharbour, keeper of the shut gate, sworn to silence, raised on doctrines, invisible for thirty years by design and by devotion, looked at the coin, and looked at the woman, and lied.

"It's a guild mark," he said. "Old stonemasons' guild. Local. Died out before my grandfather's time. Half the buildings in Thornfell have it somewhere if you know where to look. The mountain's full of their work."

It was a good lie. He was almost proud of it. It had texture, plausibility, a verifiable surface—you *could* find the mark on old buildings if you looked, because he had spent one feverish childhood summer finding it everywhere, or imagining he did, which amounted to the same thing in a town that preferred not to check. It was the kind of lie that survives questioning.

Cass didn't question it.

She just looked at him for a long, quiet moment, with that expression again—the cabinet-of-curiosities expression, the one that saw him—and then she nodded slowly, twice, and said, "Mm. Stonemasons' guild. That tracks." And then, softly, in a tone that was not quite accusation and not quite kindness and occupied some undiscovered country between them: "Funny, though. Guilds usually put their mark on their work. Your door's the only thing up here I've ever seen wear it."

"Their masterpiece, presumably," said Elodie, hating himself a little, admiring himself a little, mostly just tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix.

"Their masterpiece," Cass agreed pleasantly. "Behind which—" she tapped the table once, lightly, conversationally, the tap landing on his heart like a knock "—there's presumably nothing at all."

"Presumably," said Elodie.

* * *

She stayed until the light went amber and then grey, and then she left, because smugglers keep schedules the way monks keep vigils, and her schedule said the fold road east was passable for another hour and suicide after dark. Before she went she bought three of his candles—overpaid for them, insultingly, deliberately, a smuggler's courtesy, establishing credit—and told him she'd be back through Thornfell within the fortnight, because her route looped, because routes always looped, because the mountains were a knot and every road through a knot returns.

At the two hundredth step she turned around and looked back at the cottage, and Elodie, watching from his doorway per the ancient protocol of watching people leave, realized she was checking whether he was watching. He was. She noted it. Somewhere in the ledger behind her eyes, an entry was made.

He spent that evening's round in a state he had no name for. The gate was where the gate always was. The frost-flowers were blooming on the hinges, as they did every October, in patterns so consistent he could have dated the season by them alone. The crest gleamed under his lantern—flame in a circle, the letters he couldn't read, the letters he had lied about knowing—and for the first time in thirty years, standing in front of it, hand resting on the iron as it did every night, he felt something he had never felt.

Not fear. Fear was furniture; he'd lived with fear so long it had stopped registering, the way you stop hearing a clock.

It was *recognition*. The coin on his table that morning had looked at him—coins can't look, he knew that, he was a rational man, mostly—but something in that worn silver face had looked at him, and now the crest over the door was looking back, and the two of them, coin and lintel, stranger and keeper, were having a conversation above his head in a language made entirely of things he refused to know.

*A country that isn't on any map,* Cass had said. *Something is remembering.*

He finished the round. He checked the bands, the hinges, the frost, the seal of wax he renewed monthly in a ritual so private he'd never described it even to himself in words. All sound. All shut. Thirty years, zero openings, excellent record intact.

Then he went home, and he did not sleep.

He lay in bed doing arithmetic instead, the anxious kind, the kind that keeps a man awake not because the numbers are hard but because they're *true*. Sixty coins here. Forty there. A hundred walking around acting alive. Coins don't mint themselves. Someone was striking them, or something was, and either way, someone *knew*—knew the crest, knew the script, knew the country the maps had eaten—and that someone had sent a smuggler with a good mule and a better memory up two hundred steps to sit at his table and ask him a plain question.

And he had lied. He had lied well, and she had known, and she had let it stand, and he could not decide which of those facts frightened him most.

Somewhere out there, past the fold roads, past the lowlands, past the edge of everything he allowed himself to think about, there was a person or a power that remembered what he had spent his life guarding the memory *of*. And it was looking for him. Not urgently—not yet. Casually. The way you look for a house you half remember, checking door after door, knocking politely, asking gentle questions, noting which doors answer and which ones lie.

His door had lied.

He fell asleep near dawn with the coin's image pressed behind his eyes like a brand, and he dreamed—not of the gate, he never dreamed of the gate, that was another rule he'd kept without ever deciding to—but of a kitchen table, and a woman's hand sliding something silver toward him, and in the dream, unlike the waking, he did not look away.

* * *

Nine days later, as promised, as routed, as inevitable as a loop, Cass came back up the road.

Elodie counted her steps out of habit. Two hundred and six. She was getting faster at finding him, or he was getting slower at mattering less, and he genuinely could not tell which possibility disturbed him more.

She came in, put the kettle on herself this time—which he chose to interpret as familiarity rather than presumption, though the interpretation cost him something—and sat at the same seat at the same table, and produced, from the same coat pocket, not a coin this time but a folded page, ragged at the edges, water-stained, handled by what looked like a great many hands.

"I bought a book," she said. "Well. Bought is generous. I liberated it from a merchant who didn't know what he had, which is the purest form of purchase. It's old. It's strange. And there's a page in it I can't read, because it's written partly in that script of yours—the guild script, the mason's mark script, the one that's only on your door and nowhere else in the entire world, which, incidentally, gatekeeper, I checked, because I told you I'm thorough about maps, and it turns out I'm thorough about scripts too."

She unfolded the page on the table between them, smoothing it flat with both palms, and turned it to face him.

"And there's a name on it," she said. "Written in ordinary letters, in ordinary ink, in a hand that looks hurried. Right beside a date. And the date—" she paused, and looked up at him, and the humor drained out of her face entirely, completely, the way it had done once before, over a coin, "—the date is nine days from tonight."

Elodie looked at the page. At the script he had spent thirty years failing to read. At the name written in ordinary letters beside it.

The name was his.

*Coldharbour.*
