## 3. The Book That Waits

Wren Ashvale had been expelled from the Royal Archives of Callowmere with such bureaucratic gentleness that it had taken her three full days to realize she'd been thrown out at all.

There had been no shouting. No guards. Just a letter on cream-colored paper, delivered by a junior clerk who couldn't meet her eyes, informing her that her research privileges were "suspended pending review," that her office had been "reassigned to more current scholarship," and that the Archivist-General wished her "every success in future endeavors." It was signed with a flourish so elaborate it looked like the signature was trying to escape off the page.

She had stood in the corridor outside what had been her office for nineteen years, holding the letter, watching a young man named Pell carry her books out in armfuls and stack them on a trolley with the careful indifference of someone moving furniture that belonged to a dead relative.

"Where are they taking them?" she'd asked.

"Storage," said Pell. "Sub-level four."

"Sub-level four floods."

"Yes," said Pell, and kept stacking.

That had been eleven months ago. Eleven months of petitions, appeals, letters to colleagues who suddenly wrote back with suspicious brevity — *Dear Wren, how lovely to hear from you, I'm afraid I'm very busy this season* — and one final, humiliating audience with the Archivist-General himself, a soft-voiced man named Corvel who had listened to her entire argument about the Vaeli materials with his hands folded and his expression arranged into something like sympathy, and who had then said, gently:

"Scholarship is a garden, Ashvale. Sometimes we must prune what grows in the wrong direction."

"And what direction is Vael growing in?" she'd demanded.

"None," he had said. "That is rather the point."

* * *

Now she sat in the common room of the Drowned Lantern, the only inn in Thornfell that would take her coin without asking questions, and unwrapped the book for the fourth time that day.

It traveled wrapped in oilcloth, then in linen, then in a wool scarf she told herself was for warmth. Beneath all three layers it was a modest thing: a quarto volume bound in grey leather gone soft as chamois, its spine cracked in three places, its brass clasps long since pried away by some previous owner who had presumably needed two bits of brass more than he needed to keep a book shut. The title, if it had ever been gilded there, had worn to nothing. She had named it herself, privately, the way you name a stray: *the Thornfell Prophecy*, though prophecy was perhaps too grand a word for what it mostly contained.

Mostly it contained weather.

That was the joke she made when she needed to stop being afraid of it. A century-old almanac, probably. Some Vaeli farmer's notebook, recording frosts and harvests and the price of wool, annotated in three different hands, half of it in the elegant old script of Vael itself and half in the trade-tongue of the border towns. She had spent four years of her archival life cataloguing the scattered leavings of Vael — the country that wasn't — and she knew better than most how much of history was just weather with delusions of importance.

Except that farmers' almanacs did not usually predict things.

And this one did. Small things, mostly, in the early pages. *The millrace will freeze mid-turn, and the miller will blame his wife.* *A red-haired child will be born in the lower town, and the bells will ring wrong that day.* Little domestic auguries, the kind any clever observer could have written after the fact and backdated — except that Wren had checked the paper, twice, with two different methods, and the paper was old. Genuinely, verifiably, boringly old. Whatever hand had written those lines had written them a hundred years ago, and a hundred years ago they had been nonsense, and then they had come true, one after another after another, in small uncanny ways that nobody had bothered to notice because nobody had been looking.

Nobody looked at Vael anymore. That was the whole trouble. A hundred years ago the country had simply — stopped. Not conquered, not annexed, not destroyed in any war anyone could name. The histories of the period went strange and vague around it, like a sentence with a word missing. Maps from before showed it plainly: a green wedge of valleys tucked into the eastern shoulder of the Thornfell mountains, its capital drawn with a little star, its name printed in confident black letters. Maps from after showed mountain, mountain, mountain, and a note in the cartographer's margin that read, in every atlas she'd ever found, the same baffling phrase: *here be nothing particular.*

A whole country, and the maps couldn't even be bothered to lie about it. They just forgot.

Wren had built her career on that forgetting. Fourteen published papers. Two monographs. One scandal, when she'd presented her findings on the Vaeli succession to the Historical Congress and been interrupted, mid-sentence, by the Archivist-General rising to suggest a recess that never ended. She had spent nineteen years assembling the country back together out of tax ledgers and love letters and a single surviving recipe book, page by patient page, a nation rebuilt in footnotes.

And then, eight months ago, a dealer in Callowmere's shadier market had sold her this book for almost nothing, because he couldn't read half of it and didn't like the feel of the other half.

* * *

The Drowned Lantern's common room was nearly empty at this hour — the smugglers and mule-drivers who passed through Thornfell kept later hours than a respectable historian, and Wren had learned to claim the corner table by the peat fire as hers through the simple strategy of sitting in it at every available moment until the innkeeper's wife began setting aside her usual stew. Marda, the wife, was a broad, unimpressed woman who had decided within a day that Wren was harmless and possibly simple, and treated her accordingly, with a kindness that Wren found more restorative than she could say.

"You're doing the book again," Marda observed, setting down the stew.

"I'm consulting the book."

"You're hunched over it like a hen on one egg, is what you're doing." Marda wiped her hands on her apron and squinted at the oilcloth bundle. "You know what my mother used to say about books you have to wrap up special?"

"What?"

"She said if a book needs hiding, ask yourself who it's hiding from. And if the answer's 'everyone,' get rid of it."

"Your mother sounds like the Archivist-General."

"Flatterer," said Marda, and went off to argue with a delivery man about cheese.

Wren smiled, and unwrapped the book, and the smile faded the way it always did, layer by layer, with each fold of cloth. Oilcloth. Linen. Scarf. And there it was in the firelight: grey leather, soft corners, the faint smell of cold stone and lamp-oil that clung to everything that had survived the last hundred years of Vael. She had handled thousands of old books. They smelled of dust and glue and other people's hands. This one smelled of somewhere.

She opened it to the ribbon she kept as a marker — an extravagance, using a ribbon, but the book deserved one — and began, as she did every evening, to read.

The weather first. It steadied her. *The millrace will freeze mid-turn.* True, according to parish records. *The red-haired child.* True, according to a baptismal ledger she'd once walked four days through rain to consult. Onward, through decades of small fulfilled nonsense, the predictions growing slowly larger as the pages turned, the way a stream grows toward a river. *Two brothers will quarrel over a bridge, and only one will cross.* *The winter the larks sing in the mines, the mountain will remember its own name.*

She had underlined that last one, years of evenings ago, in pencil so faint it was nearly an apology. *The mountain will remember its own name.* At the time she'd read it as poetry. Almanacs drifted into poetry; that was their nature, the way rivers drifted into mud.

Then came the last quarter of the book, and the handwriting changed.

The early hands had been various — three, maybe four different writers across the decades, passing the book along like a relay baton. But the final section was one hand alone: a strong, slanted, hurried script, the writing of someone who knew exactly how much time they had and resented every second spent shaping a letter. Wren had spent months on that hand. She had traced it, magnified it, dreamed about it. It wrote differently from everything else in the book — less like prediction, more like report. As if the writer weren't guessing at the future but relaying it, badly, from somewhere the signal kept breaking up.

And it was that hand that had written, near the end, the passage she had copied out forty times and burned twice:

*When the frost walks inward and the door learns to beat, the keeper's line will fail or flower, and the choice will stand at the gate on the night the book names. The book cannot name it. Only the door remembers. Ask the door.*

Beneath it, in the same hurried hand but in different ink — newer ink, ink that should not have been possible in a century-old binding — a single line:

*Coldharbour.*

No first name. No explanation. Just the surname, sitting on the page like a stone dropped in a well, and beneath it a date.

Wren took out her notebook — her eleventh since the expulsion; she bought them cheap and filled them fast — and turned to the copy she'd made of that page six weeks ago, in a caravan wagon outside Dunmark, when the book had let her look at it for a full hour without doing anything strange.

Six weeks ago, the date beneath *Coldharbour* had read the ninth of Frostfall.

Tonight, in the firelight of the Drowned Lantern, it read the eighteenth.

Tonight.

* * *

She sat very still for a long moment, the way she'd trained herself to sit when a source contradicted itself, because panic was just excitement wearing a scholar's coat, and excitement made sloppy notes.

Then she checked the original again. Then her copy again. Then she held the book up to the firelight and tilted it, looking for the trick — a palimpsest, a scraped leaf, anything. The parchment gave her nothing. No ghost of the ninth of Frostfall underneath. The eighteenth hadn't been written over the old date. It had replaced it, the way morning replaces night: completely, and without apology.

"Well," said Wren, to the book, in the tone she used on misbehaving first-year students. "That's not how time works."

The book, being a book, declined to argue.

But her hands were not steady, and she noticed them the way you notice weather — from a slight distance, with disapproval. Because here was the thing she had not written in any of her eleven notebooks, the thing she barely admitted in the privacy of her own skull: the dates had been moving for weeks now. Not randomly. *Toward.* Each time she checked, the appointed night stood closer than arithmetic allowed, as if the book were walking to meet her. Six weeks ago it had named a night two months distant. A month ago, five weeks. Last week, twelve days. She had told herself it was her own error, transcription drift, fatigue. She had measured the intervals between checks and found them shrinking faster than the dates themselves, which meant either that the book was accelerating or that she was checking more often, and both possibilities felt like the same confession.

She had come to Thornfell chasing a footnote. That was the story she told Marda, and the customs clerk, and herself. A reference in a Vaeli trade manifest to a toll-house at "the Ember Gate," a gate that appeared on no map and in no survey, mentioned exactly once in all the literature she'd assembled, in a ledger entry that read, in full: *paid the keeper, as always.* One keeper. One gate. A hundred years of *as always,* implied.

Footnotes were how she loved the world. Safely. At a distance. With citations.

But the book had wanted to come here. She understood that now, and had understood it for some time, in the way you understand a thing you refuse to say aloud. It grew heavier in her satchel the farther south she stayed. Its pages stiffened when she planned a route east. And on the third night after she'd crossed into the high passes, she had woken in an inn loft to find the book open on the floor beside her pack — she had wrapped it, she always wrapped it, triple-wrapped it — lying open to the page with *Coldharbour* on it, facing the window, facing north, like a dog pointing.

So she had followed it to Thornfell, telling herself footnote stories all the way, and now she sat in the corner of a smugglers' inn with a book that edited itself, staring at a date that meant *tonight,* and the obsessive hope that had carried her out of the capital — *I will be the one who proves Vael existed, I will be the one who finds the gate, I will be right, finally, publicly, gloriously right* — curdled slowly in her chest into something with much colder feet.

Because hope had been abstract. Hope was a country on a missing map, a name in a ledger, a career resurrected. But *Coldharbour* was a person. A living person, presumably, currently going about their evening somewhere in this town — eating supper, arguing about cheese, entirely unaware that a century-old book had appointed them to something. And the keeper's line, said the book, would *fail or flower,* and the choice would stand at the gate, and the book could not name the night, only the door could, and —

"Ask the door," Wren murmured. "Yes, thank you, very helpful. I'll be sure to locate the impossible gate and have a chat."

Marda paused on her way past with a tray. "Talking to your supper again?"

"To the book."

"Same thing, the way you carry on." Marda glanced down at the open pages, and something crossed her broad, sensible face — not fear, exactly. Recognition's shy cousin. "Old writing, is it?"

"Vaeli," said Wren. "Do you know it?"

"No one knows it." Marda said it lightly, but she said it quickly. "My gran used to sing a bit of something she claimed was from over the mountains. Said her gran taught her. We thought she was making it up." She shrugged the moment away like a shawl slipping. "Stew's getting cold, professor."

She moved off, and Wren watched her go, and made a note — *innkeeper's wife; family song, claimed Vaeli origin; follow up* — in handwriting already shaking too much to be useful.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned low and the last mule-driver had thumped up to bed, Wren did the thing she had been not-doing all evening.

She turned to the final page.

Every other page of the book she had read a dozen times, transcribed, indexed, cross-referenced. The final page she had read exactly once, eight months ago, in her rooms in Callowmere, on the night the book first did something impossible. She had closed it afterward and not turned to it again, and she told herself this was scholarly discipline — work systematically, don't skip ahead — but discipline was a flattering word for it. The truth was simpler. The final page frightened her, and Wren Ashvale had built her whole life on the principle that if you were frightened of a fact, you hadn't yet found the right footnote for it.

There was no right footnote for the final page.

It held only a few lines, in the hurried hand, pressed so hard the stylus had torn through in one place:

*If you are reading this, the book has reached the town. Good. It was always going to. Books are doors too, smaller ones, and this one has been walking toward Thornfell for a hundred years.*

*I could not stay to finish. Someone must hold the far side, and I am the only one left who belongs there. Do not grieve the keeper who goes through. Grieve, if you must, the one who stays — theirs is the harder country.*

*Tell them: a gate is only shut from the side that stays.*

*And tell them the book lies about one thing. It will show them a date, and the date will move, and they will think the door chooses the night. It does not. The door is only waiting. The night is chosen by whoever is brave enough, or foolish enough —*

The page ended there. Torn. Not aged-torn, not damaged-torn. Torn cleanly, deliberately, sometime in the last hundred years, by whoever had written these words and decided the rest was either too dangerous or too hopeful to leave where a book could walk it into a smugglers' town.

Wren stared at the ragged edge until her eyes stung.

*A gate is only shut from the side that stays.*

She had read that line once before, eight months ago, and filed it under *poetry,* and slept poorly, and told no one. Tonight it read less like poetry and more like instructions. Tonight, with the date beneath *Coldharbour* burning quietly on the page behind her like a lit fuse, it sounded like something a dying woman had shouted through a closing door.

Somewhere above her, floorboards creaked. Voices — low, urgent, a man's and a woman's, in the room at the top of the stairs that Marda rented to travelers who paid extra not to be asked about their cargo. Wren barely registered them. Her whole attention had narrowed to the book, to the torn page, to the terrible arithmetic assembling itself in her chest: a keeper, a gate, a name, a night, and a stranger in a border town who happened to be the only person alive who could read the writing on the wall.

*Someone must hold the far side, and I am the only one left who belongs there.*

"Who are you?" Wren whispered to the torn page. "And who is Coldharbour?"

The book lay open under her hands, warm as a sleeping animal, and did not answer — but as she watched, in the space of one heartbeat, the ink beneath the name stirred, darkened, deepened, as though the page had been waiting all evening for the inn to empty and the fire to burn low and the historian to finally, finally stop pretending she hadn't noticed.

And upstairs, directly overhead, someone began to shout.
