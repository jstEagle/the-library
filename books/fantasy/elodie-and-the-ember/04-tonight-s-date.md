## 4. Tonight's Date

Elodie had three rules for dealing with smugglers, learned over eleven years of living among them in Thornfell: never buy the first thing they show you, never drink anything they pour you, and never let them see that you're interested.

He was breaking all three rules at once, sitting in the back room of the Drowned Lantern with his tea going cold in front of him while Cass laid a leather satchel on the table between them like it was nothing. Like she hadn't spent the whole walk from the docks being charming and evasive by turns, asking questions about the mountain, about the gate, about *him*, with the practiced lightness of someone who'd been paid to ask.

"You said you had cargo," Elodie said. "I'm not a fence. I'm not a buyer of anything. I sweep a door."

"Mm." Cass flipped open the satchel. "Funny thing about that door."

"Cass—"

"Humor me. It's been a long crossing." She drew out a small cloth bundle and unwrapped it on the tabletop with the care of a woman unveiling something either precious or cursed, and from her expression she hadn't decided which yet. Coins spilled out. Maybe thirty of them, old ones, their edges worn soft as river stones.

Elodie looked at them the way you look at a snake in your boot.

They were beautiful coins. That was the first thing. Stamped with a crest he knew better than his own face — because it *was* his own face's history, his family's charge, the emblem carved into the lintel above the Ember Gate where he touched it every night to check the wards: a tower with an open door, and above the door, a flame that did not consume what it burned. The Vaeli crest. The seal of a country that had not existed for a hundred years.

The second thing was that the coins were *warm*. He could feel it from across the table, faint as sunlight through cloth, the way he could always feel the gate when he stood too close to it. Which was impossible, because these coins had come off a ship from somewhere else entirely.

"Where did you get these," Elodie said. His voice came out flat. He'd meant it to come out bored.

"Now that," Cass said, "is the interesting part." She was a compact woman, quick-eyed, with a scar through one eyebrow that she'd told him four different stories about and clearly enjoyed every telling. Right now she wasn't smiling. "Picked them up in a lot of salvage out of Merrow's Reach. Mixed in with cutlery and a cracked mirror. Seller wanted almost nothing for them. And here's the thing, gatekeeper — I've been doing this since I was fourteen, and I know every coin that moves through the southern ports. Every kingdom, every dead mint, every counterfeiter's mark from here to the Salt Coast." She tapped one of the coins with one finger. "I don't know this one. Nobody knows this one. So I asked around, quiet-like, and do you know what happened?"

"No."

"Three separate people told me the same thing, in three different towns, without ever meeting each other or me." She leaned forward. "They said, *take it to the keeper in Thornfell.*"

"I don't know anything about coins."

"They didn't say coins." Cass's eyes were very steady. "They said, *take it to the man who keeps the shut door.*"

* * *

Later — much later, when he would lie awake going over every word — Elodie would understand that this was the moment he should have stood up, thanked her for her time, finished his cold tea, and walked home. That was the move. That was the move his grandmother would have made, if his grandmother had been the sort of person who sat in taverns with smugglers instead of the sort who vanished through doors in the dark.

Instead he heard himself say, "Show me the crest again."

Cass turned a coin over under the lamp. The flame above the tower caught the light and seemed, for a half-second, to flicker. Elodie told himself it was the lamp. The lamp flickered all the time; it was a cheap lamp in a cheap tavern and the draft came under the door like a cat looking for scraps.

"It's a forgery," he said.

"Is it?"

"Obviously. Somebody's idea of a joke. There are always people making up old countries — it's a whole trade. Fake antiquities, fake seals, tourists love it." He was talking too fast and he knew it. "Whoever struck these got the design close but look at the—" he reached out and stopped himself, hand hovering, "-the die work. Sloppy. Amateur."

Cass picked up the coin and pressed it into his palm before he could decide whether to take it.

It was warm. Not lamp-warm, not pocket-warm. Warm the way the gate's iron hinges were warm on the coldest nights of the year, warm against every law of weather and sense, warm like something alive and patient underneath. Elodie closed his fingers around it before he could think better of that too, and felt the crest bite into his skin, and for one dizzy moment he was nine years old again with his grandmother's hand over his on the lintel, saying *this is the flame that remembers, Elodie, and someday you'll tell me why that frightens you.*

He put the coin down. Carefully. The way you put down something sleeping.

"Sloppy amateur work," Cass agreed mildly. "That's why I brought it to an expert."

"I'm not an expert."

"You knew what it was before I unwrapped it. I watched your face. You went white as milk." She gathered the coins back into the cloth, unhurried. "And then there's this."

"There's more?"

"There's more." From inside her coat she produced a book.

Not a grand book. A plain one, water-stained, its spine held together with thread that had gone gray with age. But Elodie's stomach dropped anyway, because he recognized the binding style — everyone raised anywhere near Thornfell's older families would — the way Vaeli keepers bound their records, spine-stitched with seven knots, one for each generation of the founding line. His grandmother had kept exactly such a book. He still had it. He still couldn't bring himself to open it.

"Where did you get that?"

"Bought it off a dying archivist in Lowmark who swore it was worthless. Paid him double just to get him to stop apologizing." Cass set it down but kept her hand flat on the cover. "Before I show you what's in it, I want you to hear me properly. I'm not threatening you. I'm not selling you anything. I've got no stake in this except that it's strange, and I don't like strange things I can't price." A ghost of her usual grin. "You're the only person in three hundred miles who might make sense of it, and if you tell me to burn both the coins and the book, I will, and we'll drink our tea and never speak of it."

"That sounds like an excellent arrangement," Elodie said. "Do that."

She opened the book.

* * *

The page was vellum, browned at the edges, written in a hand that slanted leftward like it was in a hurry. Wren Ashvale's name was inked neatly at the top corner — though Elodie didn't know that name yet, wouldn't for another day — along with a string of archive numbers that meant nothing to him and would eventually mean everything.

What he saw first was the date.

Tonight's date. Written in ink that had dried a century ago, in a book that had supposedly sat untouched in a dead man's cellar: the day, the month, the year. Tonight.

His eyes slid off it twice. That was the only way he could describe it afterward — his eyes simply refused, skittering to the margins, to the watermark, to a stain shaped like a bird, anywhere but those three small clusters of numerals. He had to force himself down onto them the way you force yourself to look into a bright sky.

Beside the date, in the same hurried hand, two words:

*Coldharbour. Ember.*

His name. His family's name. His gate.

The room got very quiet. The Drowned Lantern's noise — dice, argument, a fiddle badly mistreated — receded as if someone had drawn a curtain across it. Elodie became aware of his own heartbeat, which was ridiculous, and of the coin lying wrapped in its cloth six inches from his hand, which was worse.

"No," he said.

Cass waited.

"No, this is—" He laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and thin. "This is a good one, actually. Who wrote this? Did you write this? You found my name somehow, you—"

"How would I find your name? You're nobody. No offense."

"None taken," Elodie said automatically, and then, with more feeling than he intended: "*None taken.* That's the entire point of me. I'm the least findable man on this coast. I pay cash. I have no family listed anywhere. I've lived in the same rented room for eleven years and my landlady thinks my surname is Marsh."

"Marsh," Cass repeated, savoring it.

"It's a good name. It's a forgettable name. That's what it's *for.*" He was gripping the edge of the table now. He made himself let go, finger by finger. "So explain to me how a hundred-year-old book has my name in it next to tonight."

"I can't. That's rather why I'm here."

"And the date? Coincidence. Dates repeat. Any date you pick, some prophecy somewhere says something happens on it. This is how fortune-tellers work, it's called the Barnum effect, my grandmother—"

He stopped.

Cass, to her credit, didn't pounce. She just watched him with those quick harbor-rat eyes, patient as tide.

"My grandmother," Elodie said carefully, "kept bees. She had opinions about everything. It doesn't mean anything."

"Your grandmother kept bees," Cass said, "and also, I'd wager, taught you to read a script most scholars can't, and made you swear oaths in front of a door in a mountain, and then disappeared. Because that's the other thing people whisper about the keeper of Thornfell. They say the last keeper vanished the same night her grandson took the oath. They say the boy stayed on. Alone. For years. Sweeping a door that never opens." She tilted her head. "People say a lot of things. Most of it's rubbish. But you flinched just now, so some of it isn't."

Elodie stood up.

It wasn't dramatic. He didn't knock over his chair or raise his voice. He simply stood, the way a man stands when the floor has quietly become the sea, and said, "Thank you for the tea. Burn the book. Keep the coins away from open flame and don't sleep in the same room as them, and if you have any sense at all, drop the lot off the pier at low tide."

"The coins too?"

"*Especially* the coins."

"And if I don't have any sense?" Cass asked. "It's been remarked upon."

"Then you'll do whatever you were already paid to do, and I hope whoever paid you in that counterfeit money chokes on it." He was at the door. He had his hand on the latch. He was leaving, he was leaving right now, this was fine, this was contained—

"Elodie."

Nobody used his name. That was the rule above all his rules. Marsh, friend, keeper, *oi, you* — but never Elodie, not in public, not in a voice that gentle. He stopped with his back to her and hated himself for stopping.

"Your hands," Cass said. "You've been shaking since you read the page. Forgers don't shake. Liars don't shake. Only people who recognize the truth shake like that."

He looked down. She was right. His hands trembled against the door-latch, fine and constant, like the hum he sometimes felt through the gate's iron on windless nights — the hum he had spent eleven years telling himself was the mountain settling.

"I swept that door last night," he said, mostly to himself. "It was shut. It's always shut. Whatever this is—" he gestured vaguely at the book, the coins, the whole impossible evening, "—it isn't mine. My life is small and boring and I chose it, and I am very tired, and tomorrow I have to re-lime the threshold."

"Tomorrow," Cass said, "you should come and look at the gate."

He turned. "What?"

"Not tonight. Tomorrow. Early, before the fish carts." She was repacking her satchel, calm as a woman stowing groceries. "Because here's the last strange thing, and then I'll let you go be nobody in peace. On the crossing, three nights ago, my ward woke up screaming. Pip. Eight years old. Sweetest child on any water. And she said—" Cass's voice did something careful, the way voices do when they're carrying something fragile, "—she said she dreamed the door in the mountain was humming. Singing, really. A song she couldn't stop humming after. She's been humming it for three days. Neither of us knows it. And I've asked, discreetly, in four port towns, and neither does anyone else."

The trembling in Elodie's hands climbed up into his chest.

"But you would know it," Cass said quietly. "Wouldn't you. If anyone alive would."

The lamp guttered. Under the door, the draft came in off the harbor, cold and smelling of salt and far-off pine, and beneath it — so faint he could have imagined it, so faint he would spend the whole walk home insisting he had imagined it — Elodie Coldharbour heard, threaded through the tavern noise and the gulls and the ordinary world, the first three notes of a lullaby his grandmother used to sing.

He left without answering. But he did not go home.

He went up. Toward the mountain. Toward the gate — walking fast through the dark with his lantern unlit, telling himself the whole way that he was only checking the wards early, that this was routine, that everything was fine, that the shaking in his hands was just the cold.

Behind him, in the Drowned Lantern, Cass watched him go and murmured to herself, "Well. That went well," and reached for the prophecy book to see what it said about tomorrow.
