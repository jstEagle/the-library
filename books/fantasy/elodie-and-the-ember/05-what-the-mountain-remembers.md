## 5. What the Mountain Remembers

The trouble with a nightly ritual, Elodie Coldharbour had long ago decided, was that it gave you nothing to blame when things went wrong.

If he'd been the sort of man who drank, he could have blamed the drink. If he'd been the sort of man who prayed, he could have blamed an inattentive god. But Elodie was the sort of man who counted his lantern's paces up the switchback trail — four hundred and twelve on a dry night, four hundred and nine when frost made the stones slick — and so when the mountain did something impossible, there was no one to blame but the mountain, and mountains were notoriously bad about apologies.

It began with the frost.

Frost was normal. Frost was, in fact, the only reliable companion of his evenings: it silvered the scrub pine, stiffened the moss, turned his breath into small ghosts that trailed him up the trail like a congregation of very quiet mourners. For thirty years the frost had behaved itself. It formed *outside* things. That was the whole arrangement of frost, as far as Elodie understood the world — it sat on the surface of things like a shy guest, touching nothing it wasn't invited to touch.

So when he rounded the last bend and saw frost blooming on the *inside* of the Ember Gate, he stopped counting paces altogether.

* * *

The Ember Gate was not, despite its name, made of ember or fire or anything dramatic at all. It was a door of grey stone set into greyer rock, tall as three men and narrow as a coffin stood on end, carved with a crest that no herald in Vael's successor kingdoms had ever been able to name: a circle broken by a single vertical line, like an eye half-closed. Around its edges ran a border of interlocking keys, hundreds of them, each key different, each key shut. His grandmother had taught him the names of the first twelve before she taught him his letters. She had never gotten past the twelfth.

Elodie set his lantern down on its iron hook — the hook his grandmother's grandmother had hammered into the rock, because a keeper's hands should always be free — and looked at the frost.

It was growing inward. Not on the stone face of the door, but from the seams around it, pushing out of the cracks the way steam pushes from under a kettle lid, except cold instead of hot, and patterned instead of formless. And the patterns weren't random. He crouched, close enough that his breath fogged the air between himself and the door, and saw that the frost was forming shapes. Small ones. A bird with a forked tail. A wheat sheaf tied with a ribbon. A child's mitten, absurdly, complete with a thumb.

He knew these shapes. Not from books — Wren Ashvale would have given her remaining teeth for what he was looking at — but from the carvings along the gate's lower border, worn nearly smooth by a century of weather. The frost was drawing the gate's own forgotten decorations back onto it, in ice, from the inside.

"That's not supposed to happen," Elodie told the door conversationally. Talking to the gate was another ritual; his grandmother had done it, and her mother before her, though neither had ever claimed the door talked back. "In case you're wondering. In case you've been wondering. Nothing in the keeper's book says anything about frost doing *embroidery*."

The keeper's book was a slim, water-stained volume locked in a box beneath his bed in Thornfell, containing mostly lists. Which oils to use on the hinges (there were no hinges). Which prayers to say on which nights (he said them all badly, in a language whose grammar had been deliberately broken by whoever wrote the book down, so that the words couldn't be used as a summons). What to do if the door hummed.

He had read that entry perhaps two hundred times over the years, mostly as a curiosity. *If the door hums, do not hum back.* That was the whole instruction. No explanation, no remedy, just that one line in a cramped hand, underlined twice, the ink gone brown with age or fear.

The door was humming.

* * *

It was not a loud sound. If he hadn't been crouched with his ear a hand's width from the stone, he might have mistaken it for wind moving through the pass, or the groan of the mountain settling its old bones against the cold. But wind didn't keep time. Wind didn't have a rhythm like —

Like a heartbeat. Slow, deep, patient. *Whum. Whum.* Two beats, then a rest, then two beats again, as though something enormous behind the stone had fallen asleep mid-sentence and was dreaming in pulses.

Elodie sat back on his heels. His knees cracked, which felt appropriate; everything else in the world seemed to be making noises it shouldn't.

"Right," he said. "Right. Let's be sensible about this."

Being sensible, for Elodie, meant running through the checklist. He did it aloud, because his voice steadied him and because, in thirty years of solitary rounds, he had developed the habit of narrating his own competence to an audience of nobody.

"One: I am not asleep. I checked this morning; I slept poorly, which is different from not sleeping, and I remember breakfast." He held up a second finger. "Two: I have not been drinking. Cass offered me plum brandy last week and I declined, which she called 'a crime against hospitality,' but there it is. Three: the frost is real." He touched it. It was real. It melted under his fingertip and left a wet crescent on the stone, and where it melted, the humming seemed — he pressed his ear closer — the humming seemed to lean toward the warmth. Toward *him.*

He snatched his hand back.

The heartbeat quickened. Just slightly. Just enough. Like a sleeper stirring when someone says their name.

"No," Elodie said firmly. "No, we are not doing that. You don't know me. I don't know you. We are going to maintain a professional distance, you and I, the way we have for thirty years, and tomorrow I'm going to write to — " He stopped. Write to whom? There was no ministry for gates. There was no one in Thornfell who would believe him, except possibly Cass, who believed in everything, which amounted to the same thing as believing in nothing. Wren Ashvale, maybe. The disgraced historian who had arrived three weeks ago asking questions about Vael with the hungry look of a woman who had been told *no* so many times it had become a vitamin.

He thought of the coins Cass had shown him. Vaeli coins, stamped with the half-closed eye. He had lied about recognizing the crest. He was still lying about it. The lie sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone, and the gate's slow pulse beat against it from the other side, patient as tide.

"Tomorrow," he said again, weaker.

That was when the music started.

* * *

It came through the stone the way light comes through a curtain — not a sound exactly, more a suggestion of sound, the memory of a melody rather than the melody itself. Strings, he thought. Or voices. Something high and sweet and a little bit sad, the way songs are sad when everyone who danced to them is dead.

Elodie stood up so fast he knocked the lantern, and it swung on its hook, throwing the gate's shadow huge and swaying across the snow. For a moment the shadow looked less like a door than like a mouth, opening.

He did not run. He wanted to run. Every animal instinct he owned was standing at the back of his skull screaming the obvious: *door humming, door singing, door growing frost mittens, RUN.* But he was a Coldharbour, and Coldharbours did not run from the gate. His grandmother had drilled that into him alongside the key-names and the broken prayers. *A keeper's legs belong to the door, Elly. Whatever it does, you watch. Watching is the work.*

"Watching is the work," he repeated aloud, and stayed.

The music resolved, slowly, the way a face resolves out of fog. It was a lullaby. He was almost sure it was a lullaby — it had that rocking, hushing quality, the tune circling back on itself the way lullabies do, promising sleep and meaning something else underneath. He didn't know it. He had never heard it. And yet some part of him, some part older than his knowing, kept leaning forward at the turns of the phrase, arriving at each note a half-breath before it sounded, the way you know the stairs of your own house in the dark.

"I don't know that song," he whispered.

The music paused. Politely. The way a stranger pauses at a doorway.

Then it began again, softer, and this time — and this was the moment Elodie would lie awake examining for years afterward, turning it over like a coin with two wrong sides — this time it waited for him. At the end of the first phrase it hung in the air, incomplete, unresolved, a question mark made of almost-sound. And every hair on his arms stood up, because he understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with thinking, that the song was waiting for him to answer it. That somewhere on the other side of a hundred years of stone, something was singing a call, and the call had words, and the words were probably *come home,* and it expected him to know the response because of course he should know the response, everyone used to know the response, everyone except the last man in the world who didn't.

"I don't know it," he said again, louder, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Do you hear me? Nobody knows it. Nobody's known anything for a hundred years. That was the whole *point.*"

The music stopped entirely.

The silence that followed was worse. It was not the silence of absence but the silence of listening — the specific, terrible silence of a large room going quiet when someone new walks in. The frost crept another inch along the seams. The heartbeat resumed, faster now, quicker and quicker, until it wasn't a sleeping pulse at all but something eager, something that had caught a scent after a hundred years of hunger.

And beneath the frost, beneath the humming, faint as breath on a window, Elodie heard — or imagined he heard, he would never afterward be able to swear to the difference — a voice. Not singing now. Speaking. One word, drawn out long and glad and ruined with longing, the way you say a name when the person it belongs to has come back from the war, or the sea, or the dead:

"*Elly.*"

Only his grandmother had ever called him that. His grandmother, who had walked up this same trail thirty years ago with his small hand in hers, who had made him swear the oath at the foot of this same door, who had kissed his forehead and told him to count his paces home — and who had never come back down.

Elodie took one step backward. Then another. Then he did the thing no Coldharbour had ever done, the thing that would have gotten him a scolding sharp enough to sting across decades: he picked up his lantern and he left the gate unwatched, and he went down the mountain in the dark at a pace that was not quite running, four hundred and twelve paces taken at double-time, while behind and above him the frost bloomed outward in patterns of birds and wheat and children's mittens, and the door hummed its hungry lullaby to the empty snow.

* * *

He did not go home. Home was the keeper's cottage below the tree line, one room and a stove and a bed that would feel, tonight, like lying in the mouth of something. Instead he went to the one lit window in Thornfell that belonged to a person who owed him nothing and therefore, he reasoned with the crooked logic of the badly frightened, was the safest person to be ridiculous in front of.

Cass opened the door on the second knock, dressed for bed, a knife already in her hand out of pure habit, and lowered both the knife and an eyebrow when she saw him.

"Well," she said. "Either the mountain's on fire or you've finally come about those coins."

"The mountain isn't on fire."

"Pity. Fire I could help with." She stepped back to let him in, and her eyes did the quick smuggler's arithmetic — his face, his hands, the lantern still burning, the frost melting off his cloak — and whatever sum she reached, she reached it kindly. "Sit down, gatekeeper. You look like a man who's heard something."

"I haven't," Elodie said, taking the chair she pushed him into. "That's the problem. I've heard something I don't know. A song. Through the gate." He wrapped his hands around the tea she pressed into them without being asked, mostly to stop them shaking, and found they shook anyway, rattling the cup in a small nervous rhythm. "It was a lullaby, I think. And it stopped and waited for me, Cass. Songs don't wait. And then—" He stopped. Saying the word *Elly* out loud felt like handing someone a knife handle-first: trusting, and stupid, and irreversible.

"And then?" Cass prompted gently.

"And then it knew my name. My *other* name. The one exactly one person ever used, who's been dead or worse for thirty years." He looked up at her, and was surprised to find his eyes stinging. Thirty years of comfortable loneliness, of routine like a wall around a wound, and it had taken one word through a stone door to crack him open. "Something in there remembers me, Cass. Something in there is remembering hard enough to make frost sing. And I don't know what happens to a door that starts wanting to open."

Cass crouched in front of him, forearms on her knees, and studied him with the frank brown gaze that had unnerved him since the day she'd arrived in Thornfell with cargo that shouldn't exist. Behind her, from the loft above the shop, came a thin thread of sound — a child humming in her sleep, some wandering little tune, circling back on itself the way lullabies do.

Elodie's head came up slowly.

"Cass," he said. "What is Pip singing?"

Cass frowned upward. "Nothing. She does that. Some nonsense she made up, she hums it every night—"

But Elodie was already standing, tea abandoned, because he knew the tune. He had heard it for the first time twenty minutes ago through a hundred years of stone, and here it was again, falling out of a sleeping child in a smuggler's attic in a town that wasn't even on the right maps — the same rocking, hushing, circling melody, the same unanswered question hanging at the end of every phrase.

The song hadn't been calling him alone. It had been calling anyone who could still half-hear it.

"Cass," Elodie said, and his voice had gone very quiet, the way voices go near doors. "I think we need to talk about where your ward came from."
