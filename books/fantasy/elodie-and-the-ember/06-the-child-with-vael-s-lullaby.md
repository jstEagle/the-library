## 6. The Child with Vael's Lullaby

Cass Marrow had three rules for running cargo through the Thornfell passes, and she'd learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything worth knowing. One: never carry what you can't name. Two: never sleep in a room with only one exit. Three: never, ever let Pip sing in front of strangers.

The first two rules kept her alive. The third kept her *sane*, mostly, because there was something about Pip's singing that made Cass feel like a woman standing on a frozen lake, listening to cracks spread under her boots.

It was evening at the Drowned Lantern, and the tavern was doing its usual business of pretending it wasn't full of smugglers. Smoke hung low over the tallow lamps. A dice game had gone quiet in the corner because Old Bramm had thrown four sixes in a row and everyone was deciding whether to be impressed or suspicious. Rain ticked against the shutters like a creditor with patience.

And Pip, who was supposed to be asleep in the chair by the fire where Cass had deposited her an hour ago with a bowl of stew and strict instructions to *stay put*, was humming.

Cass heard it before she understood what she was hearing. It came drifting across the room on that particular frequency children have — the one that slides under doors and into your chest without asking permission. Six notes, then a rise, then a fall like something setting itself down gently. Then the six notes again, patient as water wearing away stone.

Pip sat cross-legged on the floor by the hearth, braiding straw into a ring for no reason anyone could determine, and hummed like the song belonged to her the way her own heartbeat did.

"Where'd you get that?" Cass asked, crossing the room. Keep it casual. Casual was her native language.

Pip looked up with those enormous grey eyes that had made grown men hand over their rations on the road just so she'd stop looking at them like that. "Get what?"

"The song."

"I didn't get it anywhere." She said it the way you'd correct someone who asked where you got your elbows. "It's just the going-home song."

The words landed in Cass's stomach like a dropped coin. "What did you call it?"

"The going-home song." Pip went back to her braiding. "You know it. Everybody knows it. They just forget they do until I sing it, and then they look sad." She glanced up again, matter-of-fact as a tax collector. "You're looking sad now. See?"

"I'm not sad, I'm—"

But Wren Ashvale had gone white as flour across the table, and her hands were flat on the wood, and she was staring at the child like the child was a page burning in reverse.

* * *

Cass had known Wren Ashvale for exactly two days, which in smuggler time was long enough to know someone's weight, their tells, and roughly how much trouble followed them around. Wren traveled light but carried books like other people carried grudges — close to the body, always. She had a scholar's stoop and a fugitive's eyes, and she hadn't stopped unpacking and repacking that satchel of hers since she'd arrived, as though the right arrangement of papers might rearrange the world into one where the capital hadn't expelled her.

She was also, Cass had privately concluded, the most dangerous kind of person to travel with: someone who wanted something too much.

Now Wren slid off her bench and crouched down in front of Pip, slow, the way you approach a horse that might spook.

"What's your name, little one?"

"You know my name," Pip said, unimpressed. "It's Pip."

"Pip." Wren's voice had gone strange and careful, like glass held up to light. "Would you sing that song again? The whole thing?"

"It's not very long." Pip considered this. "That's why it's good. Long songs are for people who don't want to go home."

And she sang.

Not hummed — sang, in a small clear voice that shouldn't have filled a tavern and somehow did. Six notes rising, a fall, a turn Cass couldn't follow even trying, and words underneath, words in no language Cass knew, soft and round, vowels that opened like doorways:

*"Aelun vetha, aelun vae —
dommar illeth, dommar dae.
Sorren kalla, sorren kee,
amma illa vaelen free."*

The dice game stopped entirely. Old Bramm set his cup down without drinking from it. Somewhere behind the bar, the innkeeper's boy froze mid-pour, ale foaming over his thumb unnoticed.

Cass felt the hairs on her arms lift. Not because the song was frightening. Because it wasn't. It was the gentlest thing she'd ever heard — a lullaby's bones with something older inside it, something that made her think, absurdly, of bread baking, of lamplight in windows seen from a road at night, of being *expected* somewhere. It made her homesick for a place she'd never been, and Cass Marrow had spent thirty years making sure she was never homesick about anything.

When Pip finished, the silence had a texture to it, like the air after lightning.

"That's Vaeli," Wren whispered.

"No it isn't," said Cass. "There's no such language. There's no such—" She caught herself. Kept her voice down, leaned in. "Vael's a story. A ghost story historians tell each other so they can feel clever at parties."

"Then explain her." Wren's finger pointed at Pip, steady despite everything else about her shaking. "Explain how a seven-year-old girl born in a smuggling town sings the anthem of Vael — word-perfect, note-perfect — an anthem that hasn't been sung aloud in a hundred years. An anthem whose last singers were unwritten so thoroughly that I spent nine years in the capital archives and found *four lines* of it, four, in the margin of a customs ledger, and I wept over four lines like a fool, and this child just gave me the whole of it like she was handing me an apple."

Pip looked between them with the expression of a child realizing adults are being silly again. "It's not an anthem," she said patiently. "Anthems are for countries. This is for going home."

"It *was* the anthem," Wren said. "And then it was a lullaby, after. Mothers sang it to their children when—" She stopped herself, jaw tight. When what, Cass wondered, and didn't ask. "Where did you learn it, Pip? Think carefully. Someone taught it to you. Your mother, perhaps, before—"

"I told you." Pip's chin came up a fraction, stubborn, and Cass recognized the look because she wore it herself in mirrors. "Nobody taught me. It was already in me. Like the dream house."

"The dream house," Cass said carefully.

"My house. With the blue door." Pip resumed braiding her straw ring, calmer now, the song having settled something in her. "I go there when I sleep. There's a garden and a table and somebody's voice calling from inside, and the song is what she's singing while she works. I only borrowed it." A pause. Her brow creased. "Except lately I wake up and I still hear it. Even awake. Do you hear it too?"

"No," Cass said, too fast.

Pip studied her with terrible gentleness. "You will."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something breaking quietly, and pressed both hands over her mouth.

* * *

Later, when Pip had finally — mercifully — been steered upstairs to bed, Cass poured two fingers of plum brandy into three cups out of habit, remembered Wren barely drank, drank Wren's share herself, and sat down across from the historian at the corner table furthest from the stairs.

"Talk," Cass said. "And don't dress it up. I deal in goods, not mysteries."

Wren turned her cup slowly on the scarred wood. Up close, Cass could see the exhaustion banked in her face like coals — the specific exhaustion of someone who has been right about something for years and hated every minute of it.

"When the Concord ended the Border Wars," Wren said, "there were eleven nations at the table. The treaties name ten. Every map printed since shows ten. But there are songs, fragments, ledger entries, a bridge in the eastern hills built to a standard no surviving kingdom used, toll receipts for roads that go nowhere. A hundred years ago something was subtracted from the world so completely that even the subtraction was erased. And everyone left standing agreed — agreed without agreeing, if you take my meaning — not to notice."

"And the song?"

"Was theirs." Wren's voice dropped. "The Vaeli anthem. Sung at coronations, at funerals, at the turning of their year. After the Concord, mothers were forbidden to teach it. Not forbidden by law — nothing so tidy. It simply became the kind of song that slips out of memory, the way a name does when nobody says it. Within two generations it should have been gone. Extinct. Dust." She looked toward the ceiling, toward the room above where a child slept. "And tonight a girl who was never taught it sang every verse without taking a breath."

"Maybe she heard it somewhere. Travellers, drunks, some old woman in a market—"

"Cass." Wren leaned forward. "In nine years of searching, I found four lines. Four. If any living throat carried that melody, any archive, any scrap, I would have found it, because finding it was the only thing keeping me upright. It doesn't exist. Except apparently it exists *in her*."

Cass turned her cup too. Two cups turning on wet wood; the whole tavern seemed to lean in around them.

"There's something else," she said finally, because fair was fair and Wren had shown her cards first. "The coins."

Wren went very still. "What coins?"

"Nothing. Forget it."

"You just watched a child resurrect a dead nation's anthem in a tavern full of criminals. Whatever coins you're sitting on, they've officially joined the conversation."

Cass weighed it. Rule one: never carry what you can't name. Well. She was carrying quite a lot she couldn't name these days — a disgraced archivist, a prophecy book she'd peeked at and pretended she hadn't, a ward who sang extinct songs — and here came more.

She fished the purse from inside her coat and tipped one coin onto the table between them.

It was old gold, worn smooth, but the stamp was crisp as yesterday: a gate beneath a star, and letters in that same round, doorway-opened alphabet. The same crest Elodie Coldharbour had gone pale over two nights ago before lying about it with all the grace of a man falling down stairs.

Wren picked it up with both hands, the way you hold a bird.

"This is the Vaeli seal," she breathed. "This is *minted Vaeli currency*. There are perhaps a dozen authenticated examples in the world and every one of them is locked behind archival glass, and you have a *purse* of them, and—" She looked up sharply. "Where. Where did you get these."

"A client. Payment for a job I haven't done yet." Cass kept her voice level. "Which brings me to the part of the evening I like least."

"Which is?"

"Somebody wants me to bring the gatekeeper up the high pass. Paid in advance. In *these*. And wouldn't say who they were, and paid too much, and paid in money from a country that doesn't exist." She drained her cup. "I've been telling myself it's collectors. Eccentric ones. Rich madmen happen to history the way weather happens to farmers."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting here watching a seven-year-old sing a hundred-year-old anthem in her sleep-voice, and I'm thinking maybe the madmen aren't paying for the gatekeeper." Cass met Wren's eyes. "Maybe they're paying because the country's waking up, and it knows exactly which door it comes back through."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, so faint Cass could almost believe she imagined it — and chose, for one more night, to believe it —

Pip was humming in her sleep. The going-home song. Over and over, patient as water, sweet as bread baking somewhere behind a blue door, and underneath the tune, if you listened the way Cass was learning to listen, something else: a sound like a great stone door, far away in the mountain dark, beginning — very slowly — to remember how to open.

Wren was already pulling the prophecy book from her satchel.
