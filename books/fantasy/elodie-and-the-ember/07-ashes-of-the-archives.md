## 7. Ashes of the Archives

The letter that summoned Wren Ashvale back to the capital was written in her own hand.

She sat in the corner booth of the Drowned Lantern, a tavern in Thornfell where the tea tasted of iron and the floorboards complained about everyone equally, and she read the letter four times. It was dated eleven days from now. The ink was slightly too blue. The signature — *Wren A. Ashvale, Senior Archivist (dismissed)* — included the parenthetical, which she had certainly never added to anything herself, because even in her lowest hours she had standards about parentheses.

"Someone's having fun," said the barkeep, refilling her cup unasked. He was a broad, kind-faced man named Odo who had learned over three weeks that Wren tipped better when he pretended not to notice how many books she kept on her table at all times.

"It's either a forgery or an omen," Wren said. "And I've been forged before, so I know what my handwriting looks like when someone's guessing at it. This isn't guessing."

"What does it say?"

Wren hesitated. She had shown exactly one person the contents of this letter, and that person was currently upstairs sleeping off a smuggling run, which told you something about the quality of Wren's judgment lately.

"It says: *Come back to the archives. Third floor, east wing, alcove nine. Bring nothing.*"

Odo frowned at it as though punctuation might be contagious. "Bring nothing?"

"That's the part that frightens me," Wren admitted. "An archivist is someone who brings things. That's the whole job. You bring things together so they can be found again. 'Bring nothing' is like telling a fish to come ashore dry."

She should have burned the letter. She knew that even then, in some small stubborn room of her mind where the sensible archivist still lived, dusting shelves and muttering. But the letter was dated eleven days ahead, and Wren Ashvale had spent six years being told by the capital's College of Records that her theories were unsound, her citations nonexistent, and her obsession with a country that did not exist a symptom of grief rather than scholarship. If something out there could write letters in her hand and date them to the future, then somewhere there was ink, and paper, and intent — and intent meant traces, and traces were the only currency Wren had ever cared about.

So she packed her satchel with everything, because she was constitutionally incapable of bringing nothing, and she bought passage on the morning coach to Corivenne.

* * *

The capital in autumn smelled of woodsmoke, wet stone, and the particular ambition of people hurrying somewhere important. Wren had forgotten how much she'd hated it, and also how much she'd missed hating it. There is a specific loneliness to being expelled from the place you love best, and a specific comfort to returning to it even as a stranger. She walked the Processional with her collar up, past the College of Records — a vast grey building shaped like a filing cabinet that had been granted a cathedral's budget — and felt her heart do something complicated and embarrassing.

Alcove nine, third floor, east wing. She remembered it instantly: the alcove where, as a junior archivist twenty years ago, she had first found a shipping manifest listing a port called Vael's Landing. The manifest had been re-catalogued by the next morning, the entry gone, the ledger rewritten in a clerk's tidy hand. Everyone told her she'd misread it. She had spent six years since then learning that nobody misreads anything; they are only taught to forget.

The east wing door was unlocked. That was the first wrongness. The east wing had been locked since the War of Succession, which had ended before anyone currently employed there was born.

The second wrongness was the smell.

Smoke. Faint, old, soaked into stone — the smell of a fire that had happened days ago and been very thoroughly mopped up after. Wren stood in the doorway of the third-floor corridor and breathed it in the way other people breathe sea air, and her stomach dropped through six floors of archive and into the cellar.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, which was how most of her conversations went these days.

She walked the corridor fast, boots ringing on flagstone, past alcoves one through eight, each one intact, each one dusty, each one ordinary. Alcove nine was gone.

Not empty. Gone. Where the alcove had been — the carved shelf-niches, the reading ledge worn smooth by two centuries of elbows — there was simply wall. Unbroken plaster, painted the same institutional grey-green as the rest, without so much as a seam. Wren put both palms flat against it. Cool. Solid. Old-feeling, though she knew — she *knew*, with the certainty of twenty years of touching old walls — that plaster aged differently than this, and that no wall in this wing had been replastered in her lifetime.

"Excuse me." A young man in a clerk's coat had come up behind her, carrying a stack of ledgers and the apologetic posture of someone whose day has already gone wrong once. "Are you meant to be here? The east wing's closed for — " he paused, visibly reaching, "— maintenance."

"Since when?" Wren asked.

"Always," he said, and then blinked, because the word had come out wrong and he seemed to know it. He looked at the wall, and at her, and something flickered across his face like a candle guttering. "I mean — I don't actually — why am I holding these ledgers?"

He looked down at his stack with genuine bewilderment, as though he'd woken mid-task in someone else's life. Then the moment passed, whatever it was, and he straightened. "Maintenance," he repeated firmly, and walked away, and Wren stood alone with her palms against a wall where her life's work had used to begin.

She did what any scholar would do under such circumstances. She sat down on the floor and cried for approximately four minutes, checked her pocket watch, noted the duration in her notebook because data was data even when it was humiliating, and got back to work.

* * *

The fire marshal's office was a narrow room behind the constabulary, presided over by Marshal Hedda Brannock, a woman built along the lines of a well-used anvil, who received Wren's request with the expression of someone who had heard every possible version of trouble and expected hers to be worse than advertised.

"A fire report," Brannock repeated. "For the College of Records."

"The east wing, third floor. Night before last, I believe, though I wasn't present. I want the incident report, the response log, the damage assessment, and the names of any witnesses."

Brannock opened a drawer, ran one thick finger down an index, closed the drawer, and looked at Wren for a long time.

"There was no fire," she said.

"There's smoke damage throughout the third floor east corridor. There's a section of wall freshly plastered over what used to be an alcove. Someone responded to something."

"Madam, I have held this office nineteen years. There has never been a fire at the College of Records." Brannock said it evenly, but her hand — Wren watched hands; hands told truths faces were trained out of — her hand stayed resting on the drawer handle, and her thumb moved against the brass, back and forth, back and forth.

"Then let me see your index," Wren said gently. "Show me the page. Show me the blank space where the entry would be, if there had been one."

Something shifted in the marshal's face. People expect anger or pleading from petitioners; gentleness undoes them. Slowly, grudgingly, Brannock reopened the drawer and turned the index book around.

The page was there. Wren could see the faint ghost of ruled lines, the column headings in the marshal's own careful print — *date, location, nature of incident, disposition* — and beneath them, nothing. Not crossed out. Not torn away. Nothing, the way a page is nothing when it has never been written on, except that the ruling itself had faded unevenly, as if ink had once lain heavy across those lines and been lifted out again, root and branch.

"You see it too," Wren said quietly.

"I see a book with a hole in it that isn't a hole." Brannock shut the drawer hard enough to rattle the lamp. "I've been dreaming about smoke for three nights. My wife says I've been talking about ladders in my sleep. I don't dream. I'm fifty-eight years old and I don't have time to dream." She leaned forward. "Who are you, really? Because you walked in here asking questions like someone who already knows the answers, and I've spent all morning feeling like a man who's lost something on the tip of his tongue."

"My name is Wren Ashvale. I was a senior archivist until I was dismissed for documenting the existence of a country called Vael." She laid one of her copied pages on the desk — the manifest fragment, the customs roll, a scrap of treaty text with Vaeli seals — her whole portable evidence of a nation. "Six years ago I proved, to my own satisfaction, that Vael existed. Not long ago. Within living memory. And every record of it is vanishing faster than I can copy it, and last night, apparently, it vanished from a wall."

Brannock stared at the pages for a long moment. Then, without a word, she took them, locked them in a strongbox under her desk, and said: "You were never here, and neither were those. Now get out of my office before I remember you."

It was, Wren reflected later, the kindest thing anyone official had ever done for her.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the thing she was worst at: asking for help. The College library's night staff owed her favors; she had spent years correcting their cataloguing errors with a courtesy that had bordered on flirtation, in the way that only pedants can flirt. One of them, a nearsighted boy named Tallow, met her in the stacking-galleries after dark and pressed a bundle into her hands.

"Borrowed slips," he whispered. "Everything filed under V in the last hundred years. Don't ask how I got the key to the slip-drawer. And Mistress Ashvale — " he glanced over both shoulders, which in the stacking-galleries was mostly owls, "— there's a thing you should know. The senior cataloguers started a new rule last month. Anything that comes in mentioning a place nobody's heard of gets set aside for review. Set aside means destroyed. They call it 'deduplication.'"

"How many slips?"

"That's the thing. The review pile was this big." He spread his arms wide, nearly knocking over a cart. "Yesterday morning it was this big." He brought his hands together until his fingers touched. "Nobody remembers removing anything. The pile just — settled. Like snow going to ice."

Wren thanked him, gave him the only coin she could spare and half her dinner, and took the borrowed slips to a cheap room above a chandlery, where she read by candlelight until her eyes swam.

Most of it was nothing — V-shaped valleys, a merchant named Vasseur, a hymn "Vaunting the Dawn." But buried among the chaff: a land-grant referencing the Thornfell pass, dated ninety-one years ago, granting "custody of the Ember Gate" to a family whose surname had been scraped away with a knife, carefully, by someone who wanted the grant legible and the name gone. A coroner's roll from the same decade listing forty deaths in one week, cause of death recorded uniformly as *forgetting*. And a single sheet of children's copying exercises — the kind schoolmasters made pupils trace to learn their letters — in which every sentence began with the same word:

*Vael is north of the river.*
*Vael has seven bridges.*
*Vael sings at dusk.*

Over and over, a child's careful hand, and then, on the final line, the pencil pressing hard enough to tear the paper:

*I WILL NOT FORGET VAEL. I WILL NOT FORGET VAEL.*

And below that, in an adult hand, in red ink: *See corrected exercise attached.* No attachment had survived. Or perhaps no attachment had ever been permitted to survive.

Wren sat back. The candle had burned down to a stub. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog was barking at nothing, in the committed, professional way of dogs who have noticed the world is wrong and intend to file a complaint.

"They're not just erasing records," she said to the empty room. Her voice sounded thin. "They're erasing the *wanting to remember*. The correction isn't deletion. It's obedience. Someone taught a whole country to punish its own memory, and the punishment is still running, ninety years later, all by itself."

Which raised the question she had been circling for six years and could no longer avoid: erasure on this scale required either an army or a bargain. Armies left graves. Bargains left terms.

Somewhere, there were terms.

* * *

She was packing to leave at dawn — Thornfell again; everything pointed back to Thornfell now, to the gate, to the keeper with the sad steady eyes who lied beautifully about coins — when the light came.

It came up through the floorboards. Orange, flickering, silent. Wren stood very still in the middle of the cheap room, satchel half-packed, and watched smoke curl up between the boards of the chandlery below her, and heard — nothing. No shouts. No bells. No crackle. The smoke rose politely, parted around her candle flame without disturbing it, and threaded up through the ceiling and was gone.

She ran downstairs. The chandler's shop was intact. The chandler was asleep. Outside, the street was asleep. Above the College of Records, a mile distant, a pillar of fire stood up into the night sky, roaring silently, lighting the clouds from within like a lantern behind cloth, and not one bell in Corivenne rang, and not one watchman turned his head.

Only Wren watched it burn. Only Wren, it seemed, was permitted to.

She stood in the cold street with her arms wrapped around herself and understood, finally, the shape of the message. The letter in her own hand. *Come back. Bring nothing.* It hadn't been a summons. It had been a warning, sent by whatever small resistant piece of the world still fought — the ghost of the index page, the marshal's dreams, the child with the torn exercise paper. *Look,* it was saying. *This is what happens to places that hold us. We are burning, and we can only show it to you, because you are the only one left looking.*

The fire collapsed inward, drew itself down like breath, and was gone. In the morning there would be no scorch marks. She was sure of it. There would be no report. There would be a wall where alcove nine had been, and a marshal with smoke in her sleep, and a city that had dreamed of nothing.

Wren walked back to her room, finished packing with hands that shook only a little, and wrote in her notebook, under the date, in letters pressed deep enough to tear:

*They burned the archives tonight. Tomorrow no one will know but me. Therefore: I am the archive now. Everything I carry must be copied twice, hidden twice, taught twice. Find the keeper. The keeper's family held the Gate for a century — the land-grant proves the line survived. If Vael is trying to come back through anything, it will come through him. And if the Blank Page wants him stopped —*

She paused. Below her window, on the empty street, a figure in a plain grey coat stood perfectly still, face tilted up toward her window, though the angle of the lamplight showed her nothing above the chin — just a mouth, patient and polite, almost smiling.

The figure raised one hand. Not a threat. A wave. The way you wave to a colleague across a reading room.

Then it walked on, unhurried, and the dark folded around it, and Wren blew out her candle and sat in the black, listening to her own heart, counting the hours until the coach back to Thornfell.

There was a smuggler there, she remembered. Cass, with the quick eyes and the quicker prices, who carried cargo that shouldn't exist and knew every road through the mountains.

Wren had a feeling she was going to need to afford her.
