## 8. The Smuggler's Price

Cass had three rules, and she'd broken all of them by the time the kettle boiled.

Rule one: never take a job you can't name the client of. Rule two: never take a job paid in coin you can't spend. Rule three — and this was the one that stung, because it was the newest rule, barely a week old, written in the invisible ink of her own conscience — never lie to the gatekeeper.

She'd done all three in a single transaction, and now she had to sit across from Elodie Coldharbour at her own kitchen table, watch him butter his bread like a man who trusted her, and decide how to un-do it.

Pip sat between them, sawing away at her toast with the concentration of a surgeon. The girl had appointed herself referee of breakfast portions, which meant Cass's plate was currently lighter than it ought to be and Elodie's heavier, because Pip had decided the gatekeeper looked "hungry-shaped." Cass hadn't argued. It was hard to argue with anyone when your pockets were full of a dead country's money.

"So," Cass said.

"So," said Elodie.

"You slept well?"

"Like a stone."

"Liar," said Pip, without looking up.

"I did sleep," Elodie protested. "Mostly. There was some — turning over."

"That's not sleeping, that's *lying down with ambitions*," said Pip, and Elodie laughed, actually laughed, and Cass felt the betrayal settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. Because he trusted them. He'd slept under her roof, this man whose whole life was one long held breath behind a door, and he'd laughed at a nine-year-old's joke about lying down, and tomorrow night — if she kept the appointment, if she took the coin's price — she might be handing him to whoever had paid her in money that shouldn't exist.

The coins were upstairs. Wrapped in oilcloth, in the false bottom of her strongbox, where she kept things she didn't want to look at: her mother's wedding ring, a letter she'd never answered, and now forty-one pieces of Vaeli silver, each stamped with a crest she'd seen only once before — burned into the lintel of a door in a mountain that must never open.

*Never take a job you can't name the client of.*

The client had come to her eleven days ago, in the back room of the Drowned Lantern in Lowmark, through an intermediary who smelled of lamp oil and spoke like a man reading someone else's words. No name. No face she could describe later without inventing details. Just the offer: bring the keeper of Thornfell to the high pass above the old shepherd's cairn on the night of the new moon. Payment half in advance.

And then the man had opened his purse, and the light had come out of it wrong — warm, gold-tinged, like lamplight through honey — and Cass had known, before she'd even seen the crest, that she was being paid in a country's ghost.

She should have walked out. Any sensible smuggler would have. But the sum had been triple her usual rate, and — she could admit this to herself now, in the privacy of her own guilt — the coins had been *beautiful*. And there had been something else, something she hadn't let herself examine: the intermediary had said, just before leaving, *"He'll want to know. Whatever he says, whatever he does — he'll want to know."*

And that was the worst part. It was true.

* * *

She waited until Pip went out to feed the gulls. This was a fiction; the gulls of Thornfell had been fed twice already and followed Pip around town like a small feathered debt collection agency. But it got her out of the room for ten minutes, and ten minutes was what Cass needed to rehearse.

Elodie washed dishes while he waited. Of course he did. The man couldn't sit still in someone else's house; he cleaned things, straightened things, fixed the wobbly leg on the third chair with a folded scrap of leather he produced from his pocket like it was a normal thing to carry. A week ago Cass would have called it fussiness. Now she recognized it for what it was: a lifetime of keeping things in order so that nothing else could go wrong. You don't guard a door for twenty years without learning to tighten every screw in sight.

"Pip says you're going to tell me something," Elodie said, stacking plates, "and that I'm going to make a face."

"Pip talks too much."

"She also said you've been practicing sentences under your breath since dawn."

That child was going to be a terror at fifteen. Cass pulled out the chair opposite him and sat, and didn't sit right — sat forward, elbows on the table, the way she sat when a deal was going sideways.

"Eleven days ago," she said, "a man hired me."

Elodie's hands went still over the drying cloth. He didn't turn around. "A man."

"A middleman. No name. He wanted me to bring you somewhere. High pass, above the old cairn past Shepherd's Rest. New moon." She watched his shoulders. They didn't move, but they changed somehow — the way a door changes when it's been locked from the other side. "Tomorrow night."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because I haven't decided yet whether I'm going."

Now he turned. His face did exactly what Pip had predicted. "You *took* the job?"

"I took the advance."

"Cass."

"It's a good rate!"

"It's—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "You took money to deliver me. To someone unknown. To a place where no one would hear anything."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"How else would you like it said?"

"In verse, ideally. With a sympathetic chorus." She dragged a hand through her hair. "Look. I'm telling you instead of doing it. That has to count for something. In the smuggling trade we call that 'an act of extraordinary good faith.'"

"What do you call it in the friendship trade?"

Ouch. Fair. She winced. "All right. Yes. I lied to you. Rule three, broken. I'm sorry." And she was, which surprised her; apology had always come out of her like a tooth, slow and painful and usually too late. "But before you decide how angry to be, you need to see what he paid me with."

She went upstairs and came back with the oilcloth bundle and laid it on the table between them, unwrapping it the way you'd unwrap something venomous.

Forty-one coins caught the morning light and gave it back wrong. Not brighter — *warmer*. The light seemed to remember something. Each coin bore the same crest: a gate beneath a mountain peak, and above the gate, a single star.

Elodie didn't touch them. He stood over them the way a man stands over a grave.

"Where," he said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used at the gate, "did these come from?"

"Told you. A middleman. Lamp-oil smell, borrowed words. I couldn't pick him out of a crowd if the crowd was one man."

"No. Where did they *come from*. Vaeli coin hasn't circulated in a hundred years. Every piece was recalled or melted when—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. When Vael was unwritten. When the maps forgot. He'd almost said it aloud, and Cass watched him decide not to, watched him fold the sentence away like laundry. "These are real," he said instead. "The weight's right. The strike's right. This is a first-mint piece, this one — see the double ridge? They stopped making those within five years of the founding of the peace."

"You can date them?"

"My grandmother taught me coinage." He said it simply, and it landed on Cass like a blow, because everything about this man's grandmother landed like a blow lately. "Why? Why would someone pay you in these? Who has these?"

"Somebody who wants you at the top of a mountain tomorrow night."

"And you were going to take me there."

"I was going to *consider* taking you there."

"For triple rate."

"The rate reflects the danger!" She heard herself getting loud and made herself stop, made her voice drop low, because Pip was due back any minute and some conversations shouldn't have witnesses. "Elodie. Sit down. Please. I'm not finished, and the part I'm not finished with is the part that matters."

He sat. He picked up one coin — finally — and turned it over, and she saw his thumb find the gate-and-star and rest there, the way you'd touch a scar.

"The middleman said something," Cass said. "At the end. Something I've been chewing on for eleven days like gristle." She took a breath. "He said: *he'll want to know. Whatever he says, whatever he does — he'll want to know.*"

The coin stopped moving under Elodie's thumb.

"He knew you," Cass said. "Not your name. Your *shape*. Whoever sent this knows what you are, knows what you've spent your life not-knowing, and they're betting that the wanting-to-know is bigger than the fear. And I hate to say it, gatekeeper, but I think they're right, because I've watched you for two weeks now, and every time someone mentions Vael you go still as a hare, and hares don't run toward things unless—"

"Don't." Quiet. Not angry. Worse than angry. "Don't tell me what I want."

"Fine. Then tell me what you want, and I'll believe you, and we'll both feel better."

He set the coin down very gently, as though it might wake. For a long moment he looked at the window, at the grey line of the mountains beyond the rooftops, at the place where a certain door was.

"I want my rounds to stay boring," he said. "I want the frost on the north face to melt in spring like it always does. I want Pip to stop humming that song, because every time she hums it the gate hums back, and I want—" His voice caught, and he covered it with a cough. "I want the life I had two weeks ago. That's what I want."

"All right," Cass said softly. "And what do you *want* to want?"

The silence stretched. Down in the street, Pip was scolding a gull with tremendous authority.

"There was music," Elodie said at last. "Two nights ago. At the gate. Faint. Like someone playing in the next room through a wall." He looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "My grandmother used to hum it. I thought I'd forgotten it. I haven't forgotten it. I know every note." He pushed back from the table. "So yes. Maybe they're right. Maybe I want to know. And that is *exactly* why I'm not going."

Cass blinked. "Come again?"

"Whoever paid you knows that about me. They're not guessing — they're *counting* on it. Which means the trap, if it is a trap, isn't built around my body. It's built around my wanting." He stood, and there was something in his stance she'd never seen before, something that had probably lived behind his mildness all along, the way a keeper lives behind a door. "You don't walk into a snare just because the bait smells like home. My grandmother taught me that too."

"Your grandmother sounds like she taught you everything except how to have fun."

"She tried. There was an incident involving a festival and a stolen pie. We don't speak of the incident." But the corner of his mouth moved, briefly, and then settled again into seriousness. "Cass. Thank you for telling me. Truly. I mean that. But I need you to promise me something, and I need you to actually keep it this time."

"Name it."

"Don't go either. If I refuse, the meeting doesn't happen — fine. But if you show up alone, they'll know I refused, and they'll know why, and they'll know everything you know about me. About Pip. About Wren." He shook his head. "Whatever's waiting up there, it doesn't get to learn our shapes."

It was a reasonable request. It was, in fact, the obviously correct request, the kind of request any responsible adult would agree to instantly.

"I promise," said Cass, and meant it, and hated that some small traitorous corner of her mind was already asking: *but what if they have more answers? What if the next offer comes with a name attached?*

Because here was the thing she hadn't told him, the thing she'd buried under the oilcloth with the coins: the middleman hadn't just paid her. He'd left a second purse. *For expenses*, he'd said. And inside it, wrapped around the coin stack, had been a strip of paper with a single line in a careful hand:

*Ask him what his grandmother's name was. If he tells you, he already knows more than he admits.*

And Cass had asked Wren Ashvale, casually, over cards, what records said about the last keeper of Thornfell. And Wren, who never noticed anything about people and noticed everything about paper, had said: *the keeper's line ran through the mother's side, unusual, the last woman recorded was named Sable Coldharbour, died — well, no death recorded, interestingly, just a line through the entry, as if someone started writing and stopped.*

Sable. Cass had known that name for six days. She had carried it around like a hot coal in her pocket, and she had told no one, because telling Elodie would open the door, and opening doors was the one thing everyone agreed the world couldn't survive right now.

The front door banged. Pip came in trailing feathers and indignation — "The big white one STOLE my crust, I want it noted in the record" — and the moment broke apart into toast crumbs and gull politics, and Elodie smiled his careful smile, and the conversation was over.

But that night, after the lamps were out, Cass lay awake listening to the wind come off the pass, and the coal in her pocket burned, and the second purse sat in her strongbox whispering its question.

*New moon tomorrow,* said the wind. *Clear skies forecast. Good walking weather.*

Cass put her pillow over her head.

It didn't help. Underneath the pillow, underneath the dark, underneath forty-one impossible coins, the mountains were waiting — and so, she suspected with a sinking feeling she'd deny under oath, was she.
