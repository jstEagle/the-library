## 9. Doors That Should Stay Shut

Elodie did not sleep that night.

He tried. He lay in his narrow bed above the gatehouse with the blanket pulled to his chin, staring at the ceiling beam where he'd counted the knots as a boy—forty-one knots, one for each year his family had kept the Ember Gate—and sleep would not come near him. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the prophecy page: *Coldharbour*, written in a dead language, beside tonight's date, in ink that had dried a hundred years before he was born.

So instead of sleeping, he did what Coldharbours had always done when the night grew teeth. He lit the lamp, took up his keys, and went down to sit with the door.

* * *

The Ember Gate at midnight was a different creature than the Ember Gate at dusk. By day it was only stone—a slab of black rock set into the mountain's flank, banded with iron gone orange with rust, carved over with symbols that scholars had spent decades failing to read. But at midnight, with the lamp held close, the carvings seemed to breathe. The shadows in them deepened. And if you were a Coldharbour—if you had been raised on the mountain's milk and its silences—you could almost hear it thinking.

"None of that," Elodie told it, the way his grandmother used to. "You're a door. Doors don't think."

The door, as always, declined to answer.

He sat on the keeper's stool—the same stool, re-caned four times, older than the town below—and wrapped his hands around a mug of tea he wouldn't drink. It was for the ritual, not the drinking. Grandmother Maren had been very clear about that. *The tea is an offering, love. You offer it, you don't gulp it like a smuggler at closing time.* He'd been nine years old when she said that, sitting exactly where he sat now, and she had smelled of woodsmoke and juniper and something underneath both that he had never found a name for. Something old. Something like the inside of the mountain.

Nine years old. That was the age of the oath.

He hadn't thought about that night in years—not properly, not all the way through. He kept it the way he kept everything: shut, orderly, visited only on schedule. But tonight the prophecy had rattled every lock in him, and now the memory came pouring out like water finding cracks, and there was nothing to do but let it rise.

* * *

It had been midwinter. He remembered that because his toes had been cold in his boots, and Grandmother Maren had knelt down in the snow to warm them between her hands without ever stopping what she was saying, which was: "Elodie. Look at me, not the door."

He had looked at her. She had been sixty then, though he'd have guessed a hundred; she had one of those faces that seemed to have been finished long ago, like a carving someone had decided not to touch again. Grey hair braided tight. Eyes the color of the iron on the gate. Hands that were always warm no matter the weather, which he had accepted as simply a fact about grandmothers, the way you accepted that bread rose and rivers ran downhill.

"You're going to swear an oath tonight," she said. "And I want you to understand it before you swear it, because oaths are like doors. Once they're shut, they're very hard to open again."

"What kind of oath?"

"The important kind." She'd smiled, but it was a strange smile, tilted, like a picture hung slightly wrong. "Do you know why we live here? Just us, up on this mountain, while everyone else lives down in Thornfell with their taverns and their arguments?"

"Because the gate needs keeping."

"That's the *what*. I'm asking the *why*." She tapped his chest, right over the sternum. "Behind this door is a country. A whole country, Elodie, with cities and songs and kings and kitchens, and it isn't there anymore. It was taken out of the world. Not burned, not conquered—*taken out*. Like a word crossed out of a sentence so carefully you can't even see the pen mark. And the world agreed to forget it. Everyone shook hands and forgot, and the peace has held for a hundred years because of that forgetting." Her thumb traced the line of his collarbone, gentle. "But forgetting is a hungry thing. It doesn't like to be reminded. So the world put a door over the wound, and it gave the key to our family, and our family swore to keep the door shut until the end of time or the end of us, whichever came first."

Elodie, who was nine and mostly thinking about whether there would be honey cake after, said: "What happens if we don't keep it shut?"

Her face did something he had never seen before and never saw again. It went *afraid*. Not startled-afraid, not thunderstorm-afraid, but a deep settled fear, like water that had been standing in a cellar so long it had become part of the house.

"Then everything comes back," she said quietly. "Everything at once. And the world that was built on top of the forgetting—" she spread her hands, taking in the snow, the mountain, the distant lights of Thornfell, the whole patient machinery of ordinary life— "doesn't survive it. Do you understand? It's not that Vael was bad. Vael was *lovely*. That's the tragedy of it. Lovely things, when they come back wrong, are worse than terrible things."

"I understand," he said, because nine-year-olds always say they understand.

She studied him for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off. Then she nodded, once, as if confirming a price.

"Kneel," she said.

He knelt in the snow. She put her hands on either side of his face, and her palms were warm as fresh bread, and she spoke the oath slowly, line by line, making him repeat each one back to her:

*I am the door's keeper and the door is my charge.*

*I will open it for no voice, no knock, no crying, no song.*

*I will not ask what is behind it.*

*I will not wish for what is behind it.*

*If the door calls me by name, I will not answer.*

*If the door calls me by name—*

He stumbled there. Nine-year-old Elodie, kneeling in the snow with his grandmother's warm hands on his face, heard the next words coming and balked at them the way you balk at a high fence.

"Say it, love."

"If the door calls me by name," he whispered, "*I will not answer.*"

"Even if it sounds like someone you love."

"Even if it sounds like someone I love."

"Even if it sounds like *me*."

And here was the thing—the thing he had carried for twenty years without ever setting it down and looking at directly: even at nine, even cold and confused and wanting his honey cake, some animal part of him had understood what she was doing. She wasn't teaching him an oath. She was teaching him how to survive her.

"Say the last part," she said, and her voice cracked on it, just barely, a hairline fracture in the finished carving.

"*A gate is only shut from the side that stays,*" he recited.

"There's my boy." She kissed his forehead. Her lips were warm too. Everything about her was warm except her eyes, which had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere he couldn't follow. "Now. Honey cake?"

"Honey cake," he agreed, with the tremendous dignity of the recently sworn.

They ate it by the fire in the gatehouse, and she told him jokes, bad ones, the kind she always told, and he laughed, and it was—this was the unbearable part, the part grief had never managed to file away—it was a *good* night. One of the best. The fire crackled. The storm stayed outside. His grandmother ruffled his hair and called him her little doorwarden and promised that tomorrow they'd walk the high trail and look for ibex.

He went to bed happy.

* * *

He woke to lamplight moving through the house.

It was past midnight—he could tell by the quality of the dark—and the lamp was going down the stairs, and Grandmother Maren was carrying it, dressed for outdoors in her long grey coat with the torn pocket she always meant to mend, and Elodie, nine years old and sworn, sat up in bed and knew.

Not understood. *Knew.* The way you know a storm is coming before the first drop lands. Some knowledge doesn't travel through the mind at all; it arrives through the floorboards, through the air, through the sudden absence of a sound you didn't know you'd been hearing your whole life.

He followed her.

He was in his nightshirt and bare feet and the snow bit him like a hundred small dogs and he didn't care. She was already at the gate, and the lamp was set on the ground beside her, and she was standing in front of the black stone with her hand flat against it, and—

The door was open.

Only a crack. A hand's width of darkness where there should have been stone, and from that darkness came warmth. Actual warmth, spilling out into the winter night like breath, and a smell—juniper and woodsmoke and that other thing, that old thing, the thing under everything—and a sound. Faint. Far off. Music, maybe, or wind through streets, or ten thousand people breathing in their sleep.

"Grandmother." His voice came out tiny. "You said—I'm not supposed to—"

"I know what I said." She turned, and her face in the lamplight was calm, terribly calm, the calm of someone who has finally stopped holding a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. "I said the door calls you by name and you mustn't answer. But Elodie—" she crouched to his level, and her eyes were wet and bright, "—it hasn't called you yet. It's calling *me*. It's been calling me since before you were born. Tonight it finally used the right words."

"What words?"

She smiled. The tilted smile. The picture-hung-wrong smile.

"It said *come home*."

"But home is *here*." His voice broke on it, nine years old and furious and terrified. "Home is the gatehouse and the honey cake and the ibex trail, you *promised*—"

"Oh, my darling." She pulled him in and held him so hard his ribs ached, and her coat smelled of juniper and woodsmoke and the old thing, and he clutched the torn pocket like if he held it closed she couldn't leave through it. "Listen to me. Listen. This is the last lesson and it's the hardest one. A gate is only shut from the side that stays. Do you remember? Say it."

"A gate is only shut from the side that stays," he sobbed into her coat.

"And I have stayed forty years, and the door is still hungry, and it will not stop being hungry until someone goes through and holds it from the other side. That's the work, love. That was always the work. Your grandfather did it, and his mother before him, and someday—" her hand pressed the back of his head, gentle, "—someday, if the door is still hungry when you're old, it'll be yours. But not tonight. Tonight it's mine. Tonight I get to go home."

"It's not home if it's not with you!"

She laughed at that—laughed, wet and broken and real—and pushed him back to arm's length and looked at him the way you look at something you are memorizing on purpose.

"You magnificent boy," she said. "You stay. You grow up. You keep the rounds and drink the tea you don't drink and count the knots on the beam. And if the door starts whispering—and it will, it whispers to all of us—you come and sit with it and you *don't answer*, do you hear me? Don't answer. Not for anyone. Not for anything. Not even for me."

"Especially for you," he said. "That's the oath."

"And you'll keep it?" Her hands framed his face one more time, warm as bread. "Even now? Even watching?"

He wanted to say no. He wanted it so badly his whole body was made of wanting. But he was a Coldharbour, and he had sworn in the snow not three hours ago, and some doors, once opened, cannot be shut again.

"Yes," he said.

"Then turn around."

"No."

"Elodie."

"*No.*"

She sighed, and it was the sigh of a woman conceding a point in an argument she'd been having with the universe for forty years. "All right. Watch, then. But only watch. Promise me you'll only watch."

"I promise."

She stood. She picked up the lamp. And she stepped through the gap in the world as easily as stepping through a doorway into another room—as easily as that, no drama, no thunder, just a woman in a grey coat with a torn pocket walking out of existence—and the door swung shut behind her with a sound like a book closing, soft and final, and the frost bloomed across the stone in patterns like ferns, and the warmth was gone, and the music was gone, and the mountain was silent.

Elodie stood in the snow in his bare feet until dawn.

He never told anyone. What was there to tell? *My grandmother walked into a locked door and vanished*—the town already thought the Coldharbours were strange enough. He buried it. He built his life on top of it the way the world had built its peace on top of Vael: carefully, completely, refusing to dig.

For twenty years it had worked.

* * *

Now, on the keeper's stool, with the untouched tea cooling in his hands, Elodie finally set the memory down and looked at it whole—and saw, for the first time, its shape.

It wasn't the shape of abandonment. He had always thought it was; he had nursed that quiet grievance for two decades, the orphan's grievance, *she chose the door over me*. But seen whole, in the light of a prophecy naming him and tonight, the shape was different.

It was the shape of a lesson.

*A gate is only shut from the side that stays.* She had taught him the oath hours before she broke her side of it—or fulfilled it, depending on how you read the words—and she had made him swear never to follow her, and then she had left him the one inheritance that mattered: not the keys, not the stool, not the ritual of the tea, but the *understanding* that keeping a door shut is not the same as pretending it isn't there.

His grandmother hadn't abandoned him. She had gone where the hunger was, so it wouldn't come looking for him.

And now, according to a book written a century ago, the hunger had finished eating her and turned around and started looking anyway.

Elodie looked at the door. Black stone, rusted iron, breathing carvings. Somewhere behind it, if Sable was real, if the remnants were real, if any of this impossible week was real, his grandmother's coat was walking around on a woman who had never been born, waiting for him in a high pass.

*Come to the rendezvous,* Cass had said. *Midnight tomorrow, the north road, past the third cairn. Or don't. But you'll wonder your whole life, gatekeeper, and wondering's heavier than knowing.*

He had refused. Flatly, firmly, in the voice he used on smugglers who tried to bribe him—which was to say, unconvincingly, and Cass had grinned like she'd heard the yes underneath the no.

He looked at the door a long time.

"Don't call me by name," he told it quietly. "I'm warning you. I've had practice."

The door said nothing. But the frost on its surface, he noticed—told himself he noticed—had bloomed overnight into a pattern he'd never seen before: ferns, yes, but arranged in rows, like streets. Like a map of a town seen from above, drawn in ice on the skin of the world.

Tomorrow was midnight. Tomorrow was the north road, past the third cairn.

Elodie drank the tea after all—all of it, gulped like a smuggler at closing time—and went inside to pack a bag.
