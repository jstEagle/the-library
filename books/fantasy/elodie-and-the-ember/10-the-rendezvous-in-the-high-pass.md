## 10. The Rendezvous in the High Pass

Elodie told himself, all the way up the switchbacks, that he was not following Cass.

He was simply walking in the same direction she was walking, at a distance of forty paces, because the high pass was the only route north and because the night was cold and because a man was allowed to take a walk. The fact that he had taken this walk at the exact hour she'd slipped out of the Saltcellar's back door, wearing her hood up and her boots oiled for silence, was a coincidence he intended to defend to his grave.

"You're a terrible liar to yourself," he muttered, and the mountain, in the way of mountains, agreed with him by echoing nothing at all.

The truth was simpler and worse: he hadn't slept. He had lain in his room above the Saltcellar with the prophecy page's words printed on the inside of his eyelids — *Coldharbour, the Ember Gate, the ninth night of the Frost Moon* — and he had listened to the mountain hum. It had started humming three nights ago, low and patient, like a kettle deciding. His grandmother's voice had started arriving with it, uninvited, saying things she'd never said in life. *Count the hinges, Elodie. Mind the left-hand frost. A gate is only shut from the side that stays.*

He had spent thirty years being the only person in Thornfell who thought about the Ember Gate. Now everyone seemed to think about it for him. It was, he reflected, a little like being a lighthouse keeper who discovers the ships have opinions.

Ahead, Cass moved like smoke with a grudge. She didn't look back once, which he took as either proof she hadn't noticed him or proof she'd noticed him and found him beneath comment. With Cass, both were equally likely.

The pass narrowed where the old road cut through the shoulder of the mountain, and the wind came funneling down it smelling of snow and, faintly, absurdly, of woodsmoke and baking bread. That was wrong. Nothing baked bread at this altitude. Nothing baked bread in that direction at all, unless you counted a country that had been erased from every map in Vael's world for a hundred years.

Elodie's hands, tucked in his armpits, had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

He kept walking.

* * *

Cass had counted her shadows all evening and there had been exactly one, and it had been Elodie, and he was the worst tail she had ever had the misfortune to be tailed by.

She'd spotted him before she cleared the Saltcellar's yard. He'd waited a full ten minutes after she left — she'd heard him counting to himself through the window, which was somehow the most Elodie thing imaginable — and then he'd come out in his good coat, the one with the brass gatekeeper's buttons he polished for a job nobody in town officially acknowledged, and set off after her at a pace he clearly believed was stealthy. He walked like a man trying not to wake a sleeping house. Cass had once watched him sneak up on his own cat, and the cat had filed a formal complaint.

She should have lost him. She could have lost him inside of two streets, and had, in fact, briefly considered it, because the rendezvous in the high pass was the one job she'd taken that made her want to check the coin for poison. But she'd stopped at the corner of the tannery lane and stood there in the dark doing arithmetic she didn't like.

If she lost him, he'd follow anyway. She knew that now. Three weeks of knowing him had taught her that Elodie Coldharbour's stubbornness was not the loud kind, the kind that argued and slammed doors. It was the quiet kind, the kind that counted hinges and kept oaths and showed up at the pass at midnight pretending to be on a stroll. You couldn't argue with that kind. You could only decide whether to walk beside it.

And there was the other thing, the thing she'd been chewing on since the night she'd told him about the coin and the rendezvous and watched his face go the color of old paper. The person who'd paid her — the hooded voice at the crossroads shrine, the Vaeli coins counted out one by one like rosary beads — had said, *bring me the keeper, and no one else, and tell no one, especially not him.* Cass had agreed to all of it, because Cass agreed to most things that paid in old gold.

She had not agreed to bring him to something that might eat him.

So she'd let him follow. She'd even slowed down at the ford, pretending to check her bootlaces she didn't have, so he wouldn't lose her in the dark. If Wren ever found out, Wren would say something insufferable like *that's the first selfless act I've witnessed from you and I intend to have it framed.* Cass planned to deny everything.

The pass opened ahead of her, a gray bowl of rock and old snow under a sky the color of a scoured pan. The rendezvous point was the cairn at its center, a stack of stones older than the town, older probably than the road, and someone had been there already. A lantern hung from the cairn's topmost stone. It burned with a steady yellow light and did not flicker in the wind, which Cass's professional instincts flagged immediately as *deeply wrong* and her newer, more reluctant instincts flagged as *Vael.*

She stopped. She listened. Behind her, forty paces back, Elodie stopped too, loudly.

"Come on, then," she said, without turning around. "You've been on this walk with me for two hours. Least you can do is walk the last hundred steps in company."

A pause. Then Elodie's voice, dignified and doomed: "I was stretching my legs."

"At midnight. In the high pass. Toward the same cairn."

"The pass is a public road."

"It's a smuggler's road. I own it." She turned and looked at him, and whatever she'd meant to say next — some cutting thing, some *go home, gatekeeper* — didn't make it out, because his face in the lantern light stopped her. He wasn't afraid, exactly. He was hungry and afraid in equal measure, the way you look at a door you've been told never to open, and she thought, with a lurch, *oh no, I've brought the moth to the lamp.*

"Rules," she said, holding up fingers. "One: you stand behind me. Two: you don't give them your name. Three: if I say the word *mackerel*, we run, and you don't ask why until Thornfell."

"Why mackerel?"

"Because no one's ever run from anything called mackerel. It disarms them." She was already moving toward the cairn. "Come on, keeper. Let's go meet your ghost."

* * *

Wren Ashvale had not been invited, which was, in her long experience of being excluded from things, merely an administrative error she was entitled to correct.

She had followed Cass and Elodie out of Thornfell at a discreet interval of twenty minutes, having anticipated the whole expedition the moment Cass had asked her, over dinner, in a voice of elaborate casualness, *how does one tell if a lantern is haunted.* Wren had answered with a forty-minute lecture on spectral illumination in pre-erasure Vaeli folk practice, at the end of which Cass had looked like a woman regretting every choice that led her to this tavern, and Wren had known: tonight was the night. The rendezvous. The high pass. The coin.

She'd told herself she was coming for the scholarship. A living source connected to the erasure, possibly a remnant, possibly — her heart did its traitorous leap — an actual survivor, someone who remembered Vael from inside it. The archives of the capital had given her nothing but a burned room and a fire marshal's report that listed no fire. The prophecy book had given her a date that was now three days past and a name that was currently walking up a mountain in a coat with brass buttons. She was owed a source. She had been owed sources for twenty years.

But if she was honest — and she tried to be honest at least once a decade, as a discipline — she was also coming because she could not bear to let Elodie walk into this alone. He was the kind of man who would apologize to whatever haunted him. Someone had to take notes.

She reached the lip of the pass's bowl and dropped flat behind a spur of rock, notebook out, pencil out, heart conducting an unscheduled overture. Below her: the cairn. The lantern, steady as a painted flame. Cass, arms crossed, radiating the specific menace of a woman being patient. Elodie beside her, half a step behind as instructed, his face turned up toward the cairn like a plant toward a bad idea.

And then the figure came out from behind the stones, and Wren forgot how to hold a pencil.

It walked like an old woman. It was wrapped in a long coat of gray wool, and even at this distance, even in bad light, Wren's scholar's eye catalogued the coat's details and her scholar's heart broke over them: the Vaeli cut, the collar embroidered in a running pattern of small flames, the mended left elbow. She had seen that coat in exactly one place. A portrait. A plate in a banned book she'd once copied out by hand and then, when the Order came to her rooms, eaten the copy page by page in a soup, which remained the single most undignified act of her career and the one she was proudest of.

The coat had belonged to the last Keeper of the Ember Gate. The woman in the portrait had been labeled, in the caption Wren could still recite in her sleep: *name withheld by order.*

Below, she heard Elodie make a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound a word makes when it dies on the way out.

* * *

Elodie knew the coat.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, that grief had a wardrobe. He knew the coat the way he knew his own hands: the gray wool gone soft at the cuffs, the collar with its little stitched flames, the elbow his grandmother had darned with thread the wrong color because, she said, *a mend should be honest.* He had been nine years old, kneeling on a cold hearthstone with her hands on his shoulders, swearing an oath he only half understood, and she had been wearing that coat, and she had been wearing it the next morning when she walked up to the gate and did not come back.

Thirty years. He had built a whole life out of not thinking about that coat, the way you build a house around a hole.

Now it was standing forty feet away, hanging off a figure whose face was lost in a hood, and the lantern light lay on the wool like it remembered her.

"Elodie." Cass's voice, low. Her hand found his sleeve. "Mackerel?"

He couldn't answer. His mouth wouldn't take dictation. Every rule he had — and he had so many rules, rules were the walls of his whole life — every one of them said *turn around, walk down, bar your door, count your hinges.* And underneath the rules, older and quieter, something nine years old stood up inside him and said *grandmother* in a voice that cracked in the middle.

The figure raised one hand. The gesture was small, almost shy. It was the gesture she'd used to call him in from the gate at dusk — two fingers, a little curl, *come along, then, supper's getting cold.*

Elodie walked.

He heard Cass swear behind him, heard her boots follow, heard — faintly, absurdly, from the rocks above — the scratch of a pencil, which meant Wren had followed too, which meant that tomorrow there would be a conversation about this, and some part of him that still worked took comfort in that. But mostly there was the coat, and the hood, and the forty feet shrinking to thirty to twenty, and the wind dying as if the pass itself were holding its breath.

The figure spoke, and the voice was an old woman's voice, worn smooth as a stair.

"You got tall," it said. "I always wondered. I used to try to guess, at the end. Whether you'd be tall."

Elodie stopped, close enough now to see under the hood's edge: a chin, a mouth, lines like his own memory. Close enough to smell the wool, and under the wool something else — cold stone, and lamp oil, and, impossibly, baking bread.

"You're dead," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, the way a floor surprises you by holding. "You've been dead for thirty years. I stood at the gate. I waited. I know what dead is. I've kept its door my whole life."

"I know," the figure said gently. "I'm so sorry about this part."

"Who paid you?" Cass demanded, stepping up on his left, her hand somewhere inside her coat where knives lived. "Who gave you the coin? Who told you about the keeper?"

The hood turned toward her, and Elodie saw the profile, and the world tilted, because he had that same profile, or would have, in forty years, if he lived that long and if faces could be inherited by a country that didn't exist.

"Someone who wants the door open," the figure said. "And someone who wants it shut. They've both been paying, dear. That's the trouble with being a remnant — you take work from whoever remembers you."

"What's a remnant?" Elodie asked.

The figure looked back at him then, and pushed the hood back, and it was her face, it was her face exactly, down to the scar through the left eyebrow that she'd got, she'd always said, from a gate that bit — and her eyes were wrong. Not cruel. Just not finished, the way a sketch is not finished, the eyes of someone painted by a person who remembered her description but had never seen her blink.

"I don't know how to tell you this kindly," the remnant said, "so I'll tell it to you the way she would have. Straight on, and then tea." She reached into the coat and drew out an envelope, old and soft-cornered, sealed with a stamp of a small flame. "She left this for you. The real her. The night she went through. I was told to give it to you only when you asked the right question."

Elodie's hands, when he took the envelope, were shaking too hard to break the seal.

"What's the right question?" he whispered.

The remnant smiled, and the smile was all wrong and all hers, and above them on the ridge Wren's pencil stopped dead, and somewhere far below in the mountain the Ember Gate's hum rose a whole octave, like a held breath finally let go.

"You just asked it," she said. "Read it fast, love. The mountain's waking up, and it isn't only waking for you."
