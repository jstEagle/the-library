## 11. The Woman Who Was Never Born

The coat reached me before the woman did.

That's not poetry, though it sounds like it. I mean it literally: the wind came down the pass carrying the smell of that coat—woodsmoke and dried lavender and the particular ghost of bread baked too long ago to be real—and my body knew it before my mind could object. I had spent twenty years telling myself I'd forgotten that smell. My hands disagreed. They were shaking so badly I had to sit down on a rock like an old man, which, at thirty-one, felt premature.

Cass crouched beside me, chewing on a strip of dried apple she'd produced from somewhere in her coat with the casual magic of a woman who has never once been hungry by accident. "You all right, gatekeeper?"

"No."

"Good. Honest people make better company." She offered me the apple. I took it mostly so my mouth would have something to do besides say something stupid.

We had climbed since before dawn. The pass above Thornfell wasn't on any map Cass owned, and Cass owned maps of places that didn't exist yet, which was its own kind of unsettling. She navigated instead by a series of cairns that Wren swore had been catalogued in a Vaeli survey from ninety-eight years ago—the same survey Wren had been expelled for refusing to stop cataloguing. The mountains had been strange all morning. Roads appeared under our feet where there should have been scree: old paving stones, fitted close and smooth, running in straight lines toward nothing. Twice we crossed a bridge over a stream that wasn't there, and both times Pip walked across it without hesitation, as if bridges over dry air were the most ordinary thing in the world.

Pip. Seven years old, small enough to fit inside a flour sack, and currently marching at the head of our little procession humming a song that had been extinct longer than her entire bloodline. Every time I heard it, something behind my ribs turned over like a sleeper.

"Elodie." Wren caught up with me, breathless, notebooks clutched to her chest the way other people clutch children or money. She was a round, rumpled woman with ink permanently staining three fingers and the eyes of someone who has read everything and believed most of it. "The stones are Vaeli workmanship. I'm certain of it. The jointing pattern matches the archive drawings of the King's Road, the one that ran from—" She stopped herself, the way she always did when she remembered that the thing she was describing had been unwritten out of the world, and that talking about it too loudly made people look at her the way you look at someone who insists their imaginary friend is real.

"From where?" I asked quietly.

Wren looked at me with something terrible and tender in her face. "From your gate," she said. "It ran from the capital to your gate, Elodie. You've been living at the end of a road they cut off at both ends."

I hadn't answered her. I still hadn't, an hour later, when the cairns stopped and the pass opened up into a bowl of grey stone and thin grass, and the woman was standing in the middle of it.

She wore my grandmother's coat.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is what keepers have instead of courage. It was a long coat of undyed wool, patched at both elbows, with a tear on the left cuff that Maren Coldharbour had mended with red thread because she said if a wound must show it should show honestly. I knew the tear. I knew the patch. I had watched her sew it when I was six, sitting on her feet under the table while she worked, and she had told me that a good mend should be visible, because pretending things hadn't broken was how countries got into trouble.

The woman wearing it stood very still, the way you stand when you've been waiting a long time and you're afraid that moving will end the waiting.

"Maren?" I said. And then, because I am my grandmother's grandson and lying is a tool, not a comfort: "No. No, you're not. She's dead. She went through the gate twenty-two years ago and she's dead, or worse than dead, and you're not her."

The woman flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck her, and pressed one hand flat against her chest as though holding something in.

"Elodie," she said.

My name in her voice. That was the worst part. Not the coat, not the face—which was my grandmother's face, or nearly, the way a portrait is nearly a person, every feature correct and assembled slightly wrong, like a sentence translated twice. It was the voice. Low and roughened, with the particular way she'd always said my name, the *lo* softened into almost nothing, as if the word itself were a small animal she was careful not to startle.

"You know my name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"I know everything about you." She took a step forward. Cass's hand moved to her knife; Wren made a sound like a kettle beginning to think about boiling; Pip just watched, wide-eyed and unafraid, which frightened me more than either of them. "I know you were born in a house with a blue door in the fold below Harrow Fell. I know you broke your arm falling off the woodshed when you were five and cried not because of the arm but because you'd dropped the eggs. I know you hate the smell of tallow candles because your grandmother used them during the Long Dark and you associate them with being afraid, and I know you pretend to hate honey cake but you ate four pieces at Midwinter every year of your childhood and blamed the dog."

"We didn't have a dog," I whispered.

"You did," she said gently. "His name was Bracken. He died the winter you were eight. You buried him behind the gatehouse and told no one, and you left a stone on his grave every week for a year, and then the year ended and you thought you'd stopped, but you didn't, Elodie. You still leave stones. You leave them on the cairn by the north trail. You've done it your whole life without knowing why."

My hand, entirely without my permission, drifted toward my pocket, where there was, at that moment, a small grey stone I had picked up that morning from the cairn by the north trail.

Cass looked at my hand. Then at the stone-shaped bulge in my pocket. Then at me, and raised one eyebrow with the delicacy of a woman who has seen every kind of fool and ranks them by entertainment value.

"All right," Cass said. "Either this is your gran, or somebody's done their homework. Which is it, gatekeeper?"

"I don't know," I said. And it was the truest thing I'd said in years.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the rocks, because whatever else she was, the woman was standing in a cold pass in a dead woman's coat and her lips had gone blue around the edges, and I discovered that I could not watch that. Whatever she was, my hands wanted to build her a fire. I resented my hands enormously.

Wren sat as close to her as politeness allowed, notebook open, quill hovering, vibrating with the specific agony of a scholar who wants to interrogate a miracle but knows miracles don't answer well to interrogation. Cass kept Pip occupied on the far side of the fire with a game involving pebbles and cheating. And I sat across from the woman who wore my grandmother's coat and asked her questions.

"What's your name?"

"Sable," she said. Then, immediately, with a small frown, as if the word had arrived late: "Sable. Yes. That's—it came with me. Like the coat."

"It came with you?"

"Everything came with me." She spread her hands, and I saw that they were my grandmother's hands, the knuckles swollen the way hands get after decades of work, except that the work they described hadn't happened to these hands, couldn't have; they were too new underneath the age, like a stage set built to look ruined. "The memories came first. Then the voice. Then the face, last, which was strange—I remember assembling, Elodie. I remember it the way you remember a dream of walking. One moment I was nothing but knowing, and the next I was knowing *wearing* something. And the something was her, because she is what the country remembers best, near the gate. Near you."

"And what is the country?" I asked, though I dreaded the answer, and dread it I did, because she looked at me with my grandmother's patience and said:

"Vael. What's left of it. What's coming back." She leaned toward the fire, and the light moved on her face and for one horrible half-second the assembly slipped and I saw that her eyes were wrong—not wrong-colored, wrong-*aged*, young eyes in an old face, eyes that had never once needed spectacles—and then it settled again and she was Maren Coldharbour to the last grey hair. "A hundred years ago they unwrote us, Elodie. Do you understand what that means? Not conquered. Not destroyed. *Unwritten.* There was a war, and the war was ending badly for everyone, and there was a bargain, and the bargain was that Vael would be taken out—not killed, taken out—like a splinter. Removed whole. And everyone who remembered us agreed to forget, and everyone who was us agreed to be forgotten, and it worked, it worked perfectly, for a hundred years the world got along fine without us and didn't even feel the shape of the hole." Her voice cracked on the last words, and it was such a human crack, such a grief-worn, lived-in crack, that I forgot to be afraid. "But forgetting isn't killing. We went *somewhere*. And somewhere is getting crowded, and lonely, and it has started knocking."

"The music," I said. "At the gate. I heard music."

"The lullaby," Sable said, and glanced across the fire at Pip, who had fallen asleep against Cass's shoulder mid-cheat, one fist full of stolen pebbles. "Yes. It travels. Memory travels through whoever will hold it. Children hold it best—they haven't learned to put things down."

"You believe that's what she is? A—a vessel?"

"I believe that's what *you* are," Sable said, not unkindly, and the words went through me like cold water through cloth. "The last citizen of Vael, keeper. Born behind the gate, sworn to the gate, named in the book. The country doesn't reach for strangers. It reaches for its own."

"I'm not Vaeli. I'm from Thornfell. I'm a gatekeeper. I keep a door shut, that's the whole of my biography—"

"Your grandmother was born in Bellharrow," Sable said. "Her mother sold apples on the King's Road. Your great-uncle played second fiddle at the Harvest Court and was famous for it in three valleys that no longer exist. You have cousins, Elodie. Dozens of them. Most of them are dust now, but some of them are *scraps*, the way I am scraps, drifting in the memory of a country, waiting for it to wake up enough to wear them again." She smiled, and it was my grandmother's smile, crooked on the left side, and I had to look away. "You can renounce a country. But you can't renounce being made of one."

* * *

Later, when Wren had filled nine pages and finally slept sitting up with her quill still in her hand, leaving a line of ink across her cheek like a scar, and Cass had carried Pip to the tent muttering "one job, kid, the one job was to stay awake on watch," the woman and I sat alone by the coals.

"You're not her," I said. It wasn't an accusation anymore. Somewhere in the building of the fire and the answering of questions it had become something else—almost an apology, almost a prayer. "Are you."

"No." She said it simply, and I loved her a little for its simplicity. "Maren Coldharbour went through your gate twenty-two years ago and whatever became of her, I am not it. I'm what the country could rebuild of her from what it holds. Her coat. Her voice. Her love for you—that came through clearest of everything, do you know that? Of all the pieces, that one needed no repair. Everything else I am is approximate. The love is exact." She looked into the fire. "Sometimes I catch myself reaching for memories that aren't hers. A husband I half-have. A kitchen I can almost smell. The country gave me scaffolding to fill the gaps, and the scaffolding is made of other people's leftovers, and some mornings I don't know which of my feelings are mine and which were issued to me." She laughed, short and startled, a sound with no grandmother in it at all. "I woke up three weeks ago under a rowan tree knowing your name and not knowing whether I liked cheese. It turns out I do. Enormously. I would fight someone over good cheese. Is that mine? Did she love cheese? Or did the country just need me to want something?"

"She loved cheese," I said, and my throat closed. "She kept it wrapped in cloth in the cold box and lied about how much was left."

"There," Sable said softly. "You see? Some things survive the copying." She drew the coat tighter around herself. "I know what I am, Elodie. I'm a letter the country wrote in a dead woman's handwriting. And a letter isn't the person who wrote it—but it can carry what they meant. That has to count. I've decided it counts."

I sat with that for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us, wind moved through stone with a sound almost like breathing.

"When you called me Elodie," I said finally, "in the pass. For a moment I was nine years old again and everything was simple and my grandmother was going to come home and make honey cake and tell me the oath didn't matter after all." I stared at my hands. "Then I remembered she's the one who made me swear it. And I hated her a little, for the first time ever. And then I saw your coat and I stopped hating her and started hating myself, and I've been doing all three at once ever since, and I don't know how people live like this."

"People live like this constantly," Sable said. "They just hide it better than you do." She reached across the fire—hesitated, asking permission with the pause—and I nodded, and she took my hand. Her grip was warm and strong and slightly wrong, the calluses in the wrong places, a blacksmith's calluses on a baker's hands, and I found, to my astonishment, that I didn't care. "I'm not her. But I was made from her, and she loved you with the biggest piece of herself, so that piece came through intact. When I look at you, Elodie, something in me does exactly what she would have done. I can't help it. It's the truest thing in me." Her thumb moved once across my knuckles. "So let me say what she would say, and you decide whether to hear it as hers or mine or nobody's: you were never meant to be the wall, child. You were meant to be the doorkeeper. Those are different jobs. Walls don't choose. Doors do."

"The prophecy says tonight," I said. "Or it said a date, and Wren thinks—"

"Wren is right. Tonight." Sable released my hand and looked east, where the sky over the peaks had gone the deep bruised violet that comes before the stars commit. "The gate is waking. You've heard it. In a few hours it won't hum. It will *call*. And everything the country has managed to gather—every scrap, every remnant, every child who dreams of blue doors—will feel the pull, and so will the ones who unmade us, because they'll feel it too, and they will come with their blank pages and their polite questions and they will try to finish the job." She stood, and the coat settled around her, and in the dying firelight she was, for one full unbroken second, entirely and unmistakably Maren Coldharbour, and I ached so badly I could hardly breathe. "I was sent to bring you here, Elodie. That part is true. Whether I was sent to guide you or to *use* you—" her voice wavered, honest as a wound, "—I don't know. I genuinely don't know. The country made me and the country has wants, and I can't always tell where its wanting ends and mine begins. All I can offer you is this: I will never lie to you about what I am. That's the only real thing I own."

Down the slope, Cass emerged from the tent, saw us sitting close by the embers, and stopped. She looked at Sable. She looked at me. She looked at the coat—the tear, the red thread, the honest mend—and something flickered across her smuggler's face that I'd never seen there before: the expression of a woman recalculating a route she'd already committed to.

"Well," Cass said, arms crossing. "If we're doing impossible relatives, somebody should probably tell the historian that the mountain just moved the road. There's a new track heading down from the pass that wasn't there an hour ago, it's lit with lanterns, and there are people walking up it." She paused. "Lots of people. And none of them are casting shadows."

Sable rose slowly to her feet, and her face, in the dark, was full of a longing so vast it looked like terror.

"They're coming home," she whispered. "Elodie—they're all coming home."
