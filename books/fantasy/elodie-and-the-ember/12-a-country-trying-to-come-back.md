## 12. A Country Trying to Come Back

Wren Ashvale had spent eleven years being wrong in public, and she had developed a professional relationship with the sensation. It sat in her chest like a stone she'd learned to carry — a small, familiar weight that said *you missed something, you always miss something, go back and look again.*

Tonight, for the first time in her life, the stone was gone.

She sat at the corner table of the Drowned Lantern with the prophecy book open before her, four candles burning down around it like a shrinking fence, and she was not wrong. She was almost certainly not wrong. She was, in fact, so right that her hands wouldn't stop shaking, which was inconvenient, because decipherment required steady hands.

"Stop that," she muttered at them.

"You're talking to your hands again," said Cass, dropping into the chair across from her with a mug of something that smelled like it could strip barnacles. "The innkeep's starting to give us looks."

"The innkeep has been giving us looks since we arrived. We are four strangers, one of whom is a child who hums extinct anthems, one of whom is a woman assembled from a country's daydreams, and one of whom—" Wren gestured vaguely toward the stairs where Elodie had gone to pace, "—is the answer to a question I have spent my entire adult life asking."

"And what's the question?"

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"That's the thing," she said quietly. "I thought the question was *what happened to Vael.* That's what I told the archivists. That's what I wrote in my petition. That's what I said when they expelled me." She laughed, a short, unlovely sound. "But it was never the question. The question was always *why does everyone remember it wrong in exactly the same way?*"

Cass frowned into her mug. "Meaning?"

"Meaning every source I ever found about Vael's destruction disagrees on everything except the feeling of it. The fire, the flood, the plague, the war — no two accounts agree on how Vael ended. But they all agree it *ended*, completely, and they all have this same... seam. This place where the story doesn't fit together, like a garment sewn by someone in a hurry." She turned the book toward Cass. "Because it wasn't a story. It was a cover. Someone tore Vael out of the world and then stitched a hasty explanation over the hole, and everyone who's ever looked at the hole has felt the stitches itch without knowing why."

Downstairs, the common room murmured. Through the window, far up the mountainside, Wren could see the lantern-light of the road that hadn't existed an hour ago, and the slow procession moving along it — dozens of figures now, maybe more, walking with the unhurried certainty of people going somewhere they had been a thousand times.

None of them cast shadows. She'd checked. Of course she'd checked; checking things was the only prayer she knew.

"Read it to me again," Cass said. "The part you figured out. Slowly. I'm a smuggler, not a scholar. Use small words and don't be smug about it."

"I am never smug."

"You once corrected a man's pronunciation of his own hometown."

"He pronounced it wrong," Wren said with dignity, and bent over the book.

* * *

The prophecy book had defeated her for three days.

It was written in Old Vaeli, which Wren read fluently, but the text kept *sliding*. She would parse a line perfectly at midnight and find it gibberish by dawn. Words rearranged themselves between readings. Marginal notes appeared that she was certain — *certain* — hadn't been there before, in three different hands, as if the book couldn't decide who had written it or when.

She had assumed this was madness. Eleven years of exile gave a person a rich imagination for their own deterioration.

It wasn't madness. It was the book trying to be read by someone who didn't exist yet. Or rather — she understood now, with the terrible clarity of the nearly-finished puzzle — the book was written *in* a language that had been unwritten, and it could only hold still for a reader who belonged to the thing it described.

Elodie had held it last night. For him, apparently, it lay flat and obedient as a hymnal.

So Wren had done what she always did when the front door was locked: she went around the back. She stopped reading the Vaeli and started reading the structure — the rhythm of the lines, the acrostics, the way certain syllables recurred at line-ends like a heartbeat under a floorboard. And there, hidden in the pattern the way a watermark hides in paper, she found the second text.

Not a translation. A confession.

"It's a bargain," she said now, tracing the lines with one finger. "Listen. *'When the ninth year burns and the tenth year drowns, let the blankness take what we cannot hold.'*" She looked up. "There was a war. Not Vael against someone — a war that had eaten everyone, gone on so long that whole generations knew nothing else. And at the end of it, the surviving powers made a bargain. They couldn't defeat each other, so they took away the prize. They took away the *reason*. There was a country sitting at the center of everything everyone wanted — fertile, defensible, rich, positioned on every trade route worth naming — and instead of letting the war continue over its corpse forever, they agreed to remove the corpse."

"Remove it how?" Cass asked. But her voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they already suspect.

"Not burned. Not flooded. Those were the cover stories, and sloppy ones. *Unwritten.* Every record amended, every memory adjusted, every map redrawn, every grave reassigned to some other war. The Order of the Blank Page didn't spring up after — they *were* the bargain. Their whole purpose was enforcement. Making sure nobody remembered hard enough to undo it." Wren's finger trembled on the page. "And it worked. It worked so well that a hundred years later, the peace everyone celebrates — the peace that ended the Long War, the peace that all those treaties and monuments are built on — exists *because* Vael doesn't. Pull out that one stone and—"

"—the wall comes down," Cass finished. All the humor had drained out of her face. "All those people. All those years of nobody fighting."

"Every treaty assumes the territory question is settled. It's only settled because the territory was deleted. If Vael comes back — truly comes back, borders and all — then every claim the war was fought over comes back with it. Every debt. Every grudge. The descendants of every army that starved outside its walls." Wren closed her eyes briefly. "That's why the Order is here. That's why the inquisitor offered Elodie forgetting. He wasn't lying. From where he stands, he's protecting millions of lives from a resurrection."

Cass was quiet for a long moment. Upstairs, faintly, came the sound of Elodie's pacing — floorboard, pause, floorboard, pause, like a man wearing a groove into his own life.

"But the book," Cass said finally. "The prophecy. It wants him to open the gate. So either the book doesn't know what it's asking, or..."

"Or it knows exactly." Wren turned pages until she reached the passage she'd decoded at dawn, the one that had made her sit down on the tavern floor with her back against a barrel and breathe until the shaking stopped. "Here. *'The door remembers both directions. What was shut from without must open from within. One citizen, living, breathing, belonging — the land will know its own, and through that hand the country returns.'*"

"One citizen," Cass repeated.

"Vael can't come back on its own. The unwriting was total — the country itself has no purchase on the world anymore. That's why the remnants are half-real. That's why Sable is made of scraps. The memory is trying to rebuild itself out of whatever raw material it can reach — dreams, songs, a child's night terrors — but it's like smoke trying to knit itself a body." Wren spread her hands. "Except for one loophole. One thread that was never cut. A living citizen. Someone the unwriting missed. Someone who carries citizenship the way a key carries the shape of its lock — not because anyone planned it, but because nobody thought to check the mountain hermit whose family had guarded the door since before the bargain."

"And the gatekeeper's line," Cass said slowly, "has been keeping the door shut from the *outside* for a hundred years. Which means the country's been waiting a hundred years for someone to open it from the inside."

"Yes."

"And Elodie's the last one left."

Wren looked at the lantern-road, at the shadowless procession climbing patiently through the dark, and felt the full weight of her own sentence settle onto her shoulders like wet wool.

"He may be the only one there ever was," she said. "After the unwriting, the keeper's oath might be the only legal continuity Vael has. His grandmother walked through that gate — I think she understood some of this, even if she never had the words for it. She went in to hold the door. And the door has been waiting ever since for the next Coldharbour to come along and do the opposite."

* * *

Pip appeared at Wren's elbow sometime past midnight, barefoot, dragging a blanket, trailing sleepiness and the faint smell of woodsmoke. Wren had given up being startled by the child. Pip moved through rooms the way cats did: silently, suddenly, and with an air of having always been entitled to be there.

"Can't sleep," Pip announced. She climbed onto the bench beside Wren and peered at the book upside down. "Is that the words-that-move book?"

"It is."

"They only move for you 'cause you're looking at them sideways." Pip said it the way children say obvious things, with mild pity for the adult's slowness. "You gotta look at 'em straight-on. Like the house."

Wren went very still. "What house, Pip?"

"The blue-door house. In my dream. You can't get in if you look at it from the road. You have to already be standing at the door, and then it's just... there." Pip yawned enormously. "Sable says that's how Vael works. Things aren't hiding. They're just waiting for you to belong to them first."

"Sable says that," Wren repeated carefully.

"Mm-hm." Pip laid her head on Wren's shoulder, boneless with tiredness, and Wren — who had never in her life been comfortable with children, who had once fled a christening on the grounds that babies were "loud archives with poor indexing" — found herself holding still so as not to wake her.

"Pip," she said softly. "When you dream of the house. Do you dream of anything else? Streets? People?"

"Streets with lamps shaped like lilies. A fountain where the water goes up instead of down. My mum singing." A pause. "I don't got a mum. Cass says I got her, and she's better than a mum anyway, 'cause mums make you eat things. But the lady in the dream sings, and I know all the words even though I don't know what they mean."

*They aren't nonsense,* Wren thought, her throat tight. *They're Vaeli. You've been singing Vaeli your whole life and no one around you could hear it.*

The country wasn't just reaching for Elodie. It was reaching through everyone it could touch — pressing its shape against the world like a face against glass. Pip's dreams. Sable's impossible body. The roads folding. The procession climbing. A century of erased homesickness, leaking through every crack the unwriting had left.

And at the center of it, one exhausted man pacing a floor above them, who had wanted nothing in his life but to be left alone, and who was the single hinge on which a nation swung.

* * *

He came down the stairs near two in the morning, and Wren knew before he spoke that Cass had already told him. Some things traveled faster than words — the set of his shoulders, the way he carried the candle too carefully, as if the flame were a patient he was transporting.

"Wren." He sat down across from her. Up close he looked wrung out, gray-faced, older than his years and younger than his fears. "Cass says you translated it. All of it. The bargain, the war, the—" He stopped. Started again. "Say it isn't true."

Wren had rehearsed gentleness on the stairs. She abandoned it immediately; he deserved better than softness that lied.

"It's true," she said. "I've checked it six ways. The acrostic, the marginalia, the watermarks in the paper — the book was made in Vael, in the last months before the unwriting, by people who knew what was coming and wanted a record to survive it. They hid it inside a prophecy because a prophecy is the one kind of book the Order can't burn without proving it true." She slid the volume across the table. "Look at it. It'll hold still for you."

He looked. And she watched his face as the sliding stopped — watched him read, for the first time, the plain confession of his own country's murder, and understand that the door he had guarded his whole life was not a barrier against danger.

It was a wound. Deliberate. Surgical. Inflicted on the world by people who had decided one country's existence cost too much, and who had hired eternity to enforce the decision.

"My grandmother," Elodie said at last. His voice was very level, which meant it was taking everything he had. "Did she know?"

"I think she worked it out the way I did — backwards, from the seams. I think that's why she went through. She couldn't stop the unwriting, so she became the part of it that stayed human. A keeper on the inside." Wren hesitated, then gave him the rest, because hoarding truth was how institutions rotted and she had sworn off institutions forever. "And I think the book is addressed to her, Elodie. Or to whoever came after her. *'To the hand that remains.'* That's the dedication, hidden in the first letters of the opening verse. Whoever wrote this expected a Coldharbour to read it eventually."

"A hand that remains," he echoed. He laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound. "I'm a gatekeeper. I sweep the threshold. I oil the hinges. I write the weather in a ledger. I am not—" He gestured at the book, at the window, at the lantern-lit road beyond it where a nation was climbing home. "I'm not a *hand of prophecy.*"

"No," Wren agreed. "You're something rarer. Prophecies are cheap — every archive I've ever looted was full of them, most of them wrong, all of them vague. But a living citizen of an unwritten country? Do you know how many scholars have died without finding one? How many were ruined for suggesting such a person could exist?" Her voice caught, and she let it. "I was expelled for writing a footnote, Elodie. A *footnote*, suggesting that Vael's records showed signs of deliberate falsification. They took my credentials, my rooms, my access to forty years of my own research. And tonight I'm sitting in a smugglers' tavern in the Thornfells, and the footnote just stood up, poured itself tea, and asked me to say it isn't true."

Silence. The candles guttered. Somewhere upstairs, Sable was singing — very low, wordless, a tune that Pip stirred in her sleep and hummed along to without waking.

"What happens," Elodie said slowly, "if I never touch the gate again? If I walk south tomorrow and keep walking?"

"Then the memory keeps leaking. The roads keep folding. The remnants keep gathering, half-real, unable to live and unable to die. Pip keeps dreaming of a mother she never had. And the Order keeps burning towns off the hillsides to hold the line." Wren folded her hands. "The unwriting was never finished, Elodie. That's what nobody understood — not the Order, not the bargain-makers. You can amend every record and adjust every mind, but longing isn't a record. It doesn't live in archives. It lives in *people*, and people keep being born, and some of them keep dreaming of a blue door. Vael isn't trying to conquer the world. It's trying to come *home*. And it cannot stop, because stopping would mean accepting that it never existed — and it did exist. I have proof. I have spent my whole life becoming proof."

Elodie stared at the book. At the dedication only he could read steadily. At his own reflection, ghosted faint in the page beside the words *one citizen, living, breathing, belonging.*

"And if I do touch it," he said. "If I open it from the inside. Then what? The country comes back, and the peace dies, and the inquisitor's list of names gets longer?"

Wren opened her mouth to answer — and discovered, with a lurch, that she didn't have one. She had the beginning of the prophecy and the middle of the prophecy. The ending was obscured, damaged, a section of pages that had resisted every technique she knew.

"I don't know," she admitted. It tasted like ash. Eleven years of exile, and the honest answer was *I don't know.* "The final verses won't resolve. Either they're damaged, or they're not written yet, or—" she glanced at Pip, asleep against her shoulder, "—or they're waiting for someone who belongs to read them straight-on."

Outside, the lanterns on the impossible road had stopped moving. The procession stood still on the mountainside, hundreds of shadowless figures, all facing the town. Facing, Wren realized with a chill that was not entirely unpleasant, the tavern. Facing the table where their last citizen sat over a book that had waited a century for him.

Waiting, she thought. Not demanding. The way a house waits. The way a door waits.

The way a grandmother waits.

Elodie rose, took the candle, and stood a long moment looking at the window. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that only she and Cass heard it.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I want to learn the rest of the verses. Whatever it costs you. Whatever it takes." A breath. "If I'm the answer to a hundred-year-old question, somebody had better tell me what the question was *for*."

He climbed the stairs. Wren watched him go, then pulled the book back toward her, dipped her pen, and began, in a fair hand she hadn't used since the archives took it from her, to copy out everything she knew.

Above the mountains, the first gray hint of dawn touched the peaks — and on the lantern road, the shadowless procession took one step closer.
