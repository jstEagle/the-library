## 13. Pip's Dream of Home

Cass had smuggled a lot of things over the years. Brandy in false-bottomed barrels. Silk that had never seen a customs stamp. A fugitive countess, once, folded into a crate of pickled herring, which the countess still brought up at dinner parties and which Cass still regretted.

She had never before tried to smuggle sleep into a child.

It was past midnight in the back room of the Drowned Lantern, and Pip was awake again. Cass could hear her through the thin wall—not crying, nothing so simple as crying. Humming. The same tune she always hummed on these nights, soft and looping, like water finding its way downhill. Cass lay in her own bed and listened to it and felt the particular helplessness of a woman who could talk her way past any border guard in three kingdoms but couldn't talk a six-year-old out of a dream.

*Third night this week,* she thought, and got up.

Pip was sitting up in the little trundle bed with her knees drawn to her chest, eyes open but not entirely *here*, humming. Her hair was a catastrophe. There was dirt under her fingernails—actual dirt, dark and fine and smelling of turned earth—and Cass had checked the floor, the windowsill, the cracks in the boards. There was no dirt anywhere else in the room. There never was.

"Hey, sprout," Cass said, sitting on the edge of the bed. "You dreaming about the house again?"

Pip nodded without stopping the humming. Then she stopped, mid-phrase, the way you'd stop walking when you realized you'd been talking to yourself.

"The blue door," she said. "Mama was singing."

Cass kept her voice level, because she'd learned that level was what Pip needed. "What was she singing?"

"You know it." Pip said this with the serene confidence of a child who has never once considered that other people might not share the furniture of her head. "Everybody knows it."

"I don't, actually."

Pip looked at her with genuine, unguarded pity—the look of someone discovering an adult who couldn't tie her own boots. Then she hummed four notes of it, clear as a bell, and waited for Cass to catch up.

Cass didn't catch up. She knew every shanty, every drinking song, every bawdy verse ever composed within fifty miles of a port or a mountain pass, and those four notes belonged to none of them. They were strange. Not unpleasant—strange the way a word from a language you almost know is strange. Familiar-shaped and empty-centered.

"Where did you hear that?" Cass asked.

"From Mama." As if the answer were so obvious it barely deserved voicing. "In the garden."

"Pip. You've never told me your mama's name."

The pity deepened. "I haven't got one yet. She's in the dream."

And there it was, the thing about Pip that Cass had stopped trying to explain to herself sometime around the second month of keeping her: the girl spoke about the dream-house the way other children spoke about last summer. Not *I dreamed of* a mother. She had a mother, located precisely behind sleep, visited nightly, remembered fondly, missed honestly. A homesickness for somewhere she'd never been. For nowhere.

For a place that, as far as anyone in Thornfell knew, did not exist.

* * *

Cass got her settled—watered, tucked, the blanket with the crooked sheep pulled up to her chin—and sat by the bed until the humming wound down and Pip's breathing went long and even. She watched the girl's small hands curl against the pillow. Dirt under the nails. She'd have to scrub them in the morning, and they'd be clean by noon, and dirty again tomorrow after the dream-garden, whatever it was, wherever it was, whoever was tending it.

She should have found it frightening. She supposed some part of her did. But mostly what she felt, sitting in the dark of the Drowned Lantern's cheapest room, was something she hadn't let herself feel in years: the ache of being responsible for something she couldn't steal, bribe, or outrun.

Cass Vane had built her whole life on the principle that everything has a price and every problem can be carried across a border. This was not a problem that could be carried anywhere. It lived in a child's head and bloomed nightly, and no amount of coin would make it stay on the far side of anything.

"Who are you?" she murmured to the sleeping girl, not really expecting an answer, and got none, and stayed anyway until her own legs went numb.

* * *

In the morning she took Pip down early, before the tavern filled, and fed her porridge with the honey she saved for bad days, and Pip ate it happily and showed Cass her hands.

"Look. Clean."

"So they are."

"It comes off in the morning," Pip explained gravely. "But it comes back at night. Because of the digging."

"The digging."

"For the seeds." Pip frowned at Cass's incomprehension. "We're planting turnips. Mama says the soil wants turning before the frost. I don't know what turning is but my hands do."

*Your hands do.* Cass looked at the small fingers wrapped around the spoon and thought, not for the first time, that Pip had come to her two winters ago out of nowhere worth naming—a bundle left on a smuggler's sledge between one village and the next, no note, no mark, just a child asleep under a good blanket that no one in three valleys could identify. Cass had asked questions for a month. Then she'd stopped asking, because the answers kept coming back *nothing*, and a smuggler learns quickly that *nothing* is sometimes the truest answer there is.

Now the nothing was growing turnips.

"Wren's coming today," Cass said, mostly to change the subject in her own head. "The book lady. Maybe she'll want to hear your song."

"Is she the sad one?"

"She's—" Cass considered. Wren Ashvale, disgraced historian, currently living out of two trunks and a leather satchel, possessed of the specific haunted look of a person who has read too much and been believed too little. "She's the one with all the papers, sprout."

"The sad one with the papers," Pip confirmed, and finished her porridge.

* * *

Wren arrived mid-morning, shaking mountain damp off her coat, and Cass watched her cross the common room with the careful steps of a woman carrying something breakable inside her chest. Which she was. Cass had seen the prophecy book now—had held it, briefly, feeling like a pickpocket handling a relic—and she knew what tonight's date meant, and whose name was written beside it, and how close the keeper of the Ember Gate had come to throwing them both out of his tower when she'd shown him.

But that was Elodie's trouble, and this morning's trouble was smaller and wore pigtails.

"Wren," Cass said, "I need your particular kind of strange expertise."

"That's a worrying sentence."

"Aye, most of mine are. Sit. Tea?"

"Please." Wren sat, arranged her satchel on her knees like a shield, and waited.

Cass lowered her voice, though Pip was outside chasing the tavern cat with the single-minded brutality of the very young. "The girl dreams. Every night, same dream. House, blue door, garden, a mother singing. And here's the part that's got the hair on my neck standing up—she wakes with dirt under her nails. Real dirt. Garden dirt. And she hums a song nobody taught her, and I don't know it, and I know every song there is."

Wren had gone very still. That was the thing about Wren—she listened the way other people prayed. "Hum it. If you can."

"I can't. I've heard it forty times and it won't stick in me, like water off wax. But she'll sing it if you ask. She thinks everyone knows it."

So they waited for the cat to escape, which took four minutes, and then Pip came in flushed and victorious and was lifted onto the bench beside Wren, and Cass said, "Sprout, will you sing the lady your mama's song? The one from the garden?"

Pip beamed. Being asked to perform knowledge was her favorite thing after honey.

She sang it through, twice, once slow and once at proper speed, hands clasped, eyes half-closed, swaying slightly like a very small woman at a very old funeral. It was a simple melody. Rising, falling, rising again, ending on a note that hung in the air like smoke refusing to leave a room.

Cass watched Wren instead of listening, and what she saw frightened her more than the dirt under the nails ever had.

Wren Ashvale put both hands flat on the table. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a person steadies themselves against a ship's rail in weather. Her lips moved, shaping words along with the melody—words, actual words, in no language Cass knew—and when Pip finished, Wren's eyes were wet and her voice came out scraped thin.

"That's the Vaeli anthem."

The room got quieter. Not silent—the Drowned Lantern was never silent; there was always someone arguing about tariffs—but quieter, the way a room gets when it senses it should be.

"The what?" Cass said.

"Vael." Wren's hands were trembling. She pressed them flatter. "The country that isn't anymore. Erased a hundred years ago—wiped from the maps, struck from the treaties, the archives sealed, the name itself made contraband in the capital. I wrote my thesis on it. It's why they expelled me." She laughed, a small broken sound. "They expelled me for studying a country that officially never existed, which I maintain is either the pettiest act of censorship in history or proof that it existed, and either way—" She stopped. Breathed. "That song. That exact melody. I found a fragment of it once, eight bars, in a merchant's ledger from before the Unwriting. Someone had written the tune in the margin next to a list of wine prices, and there was a note: *the anthem, as sung at the autumn festival.* Eight bars. I hummed it to myself for a year trying to imagine the rest."

"And?" Cass asked, though she already knew.

"And she just sang me the rest." Wren looked at Pip the way you'd look at a locked door that had opened on its own. "All of it. The whole song. Perfectly. A hundred years extinct, and she sings it like she learned it at her mother's knee."

"Well," said Pip, with devastating simplicity, "I did learn it at my mother's knee. In the garden. By the blue door."

Wren made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite a sob and covered her mouth with both hands.

Cass poured the tea. It seemed like the thing to do. Her hands were steadier than she'd expected them to be, which was its own kind of information—Cass Vane's body always knew things before Cass Vane did, and its current message appeared to be: *this is real, and you are going to have to deal with it.*

"Ask her about the house," Cass said quietly.

So Wren asked, gently, the way you'd question a witness you were afraid of breaking, and Pip answered everything with the patience of a child explaining rain to a grown-up. The house was white with a blue door. The door stuck in winter and you had to lift the handle while you pushed. The garden had turnips and a plum tree and a stone wall with moss on the north side. The kitchen smelled of bread on baking days. Her mother's voice was low and warm and she sang the anthem while she worked, but only the first verses, because the later ones were for festivals. There was a hill behind the house, and from the top of the hill you could see—

Pip paused. Frowned. The first crack in all that certainty.

"You can see something from the top of the hill," she said. "I always forget what. It's important, though. Mama looks at it when she thinks I'm not watching, and she goes quiet."

"A town?" Wren asked. "A gate?"

"I don't know those words in the dream." Pip said it apologetically, as if the vocabulary gap were her fault. "It's bright. Like a lamp. Far away."

Cass felt the back of her neck prickle. She thought of a door in a mountain that must never open. She thought of a tall, gray-eyed man who lied badly about crests on coins and guarded that door alone, night after night, year after year, in a silence he called routine.

"Where is this house, sprout?" she asked. "Which way?"

Pip pointed. Without hesitating. Without looking around the room first, without checking the sun through the window, without any of the things a child does when guessing. She pointed north and a little east, arm steady, finger certain, straight through the wall of the Drowned Lantern, through the streets of Thornfell, through the foothills, into the high country where the mountains closed ranks around a pass that led exactly nowhere on any map Cass had ever run cargo across.

"That way," Pip said. "Home's that way. It takes three sleeps."

Three sleeps. Cass did the arithmetic without wanting to. Three days' walk north-northeast put you—well. It put you in the middle of rock and ice and old roads that weren't on maps. Or it put you somewhere else. Somewhere that used to be on maps, a hundred years ago, before the maps were cleaned.

"Cass." Wren's voice was very quiet. "The anthem. The house. The direction. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand you're telling me something."

"The child remembers Vael." Wren's hands were flat on the table again, pressing down, as if the truth might otherwise float away. "Not a story of Vael. Not a scholar's reconstruction. She remembers it the way you remember your childhood—specifically, uselessly, perfectly. The sticky door. The moss on the wall. Nobody invents moss on the north side of a wall. That's memory, Cass. That's somebody's actual memory, living in a six-year-old who was born a hundred years after the country died."

Outside, Pip had gone back to terrorizing the cat. Through the window Cass could see her running, laughing, alive and ordinary, pigtailed and gap-toothed, a child like any child except for the country singing in her head.

"What do we do?" Cass asked. It was not a question she asked often. Asking it felt like handing someone her knife.

"We don't frighten her," Wren said immediately. "Whatever this is, she's happy in it. Homesick, but happy. You don't take that away from a child on a historian's say-so." She hesitated, and Cass watched the hope and the dread fight openly across her face, neither winning. "But we tell Elodie. Today. Because if the gate is waking—if Vael is trying to come back—then it isn't only reaching through books and coins and old roads. It's reaching through *her*. And I need to know whether that's a miracle or a warning."

"And if it's both?"

Wren looked at her. "Then we'd better hurry."

* * *

That evening Cass climbed the tower path with Pip on her shoulders, because the girl had refused to be left behind—"the tall sad man has a door, I want to see his door"—and because Cass had learned that some battles weren't worth the cost of winning them.

Elodie met them at the top, lantern in hand, looking the way he always looked: like a man who had been interrupted mid-silence and resented it and was too polite to say so. His eyes went to Pip first. Something crossed his face—quick, unreadable, gone.

"This is Pip," Cass said. "My ward. She wanted to see the famous door."

"There's nothing to see," Elodie said. "It's a door. It's shut."

"It hums," said Pip.

Elodie went very still.

"At night," Pip continued, with the breezy authority of a tour guide, "it hums like Mama's kettle. And it knows the song. Everybody knows the song."

The wind moved along the ridge. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself. Cass watched Elodie Coldharbour stand in front of his shut gate and look down at a small girl with dirt under her fingernails, and she saw the exact moment the loneliness he'd built his whole life inside of developed its first crack.

"What song?" he asked.

Pip smiled, took a breath, and sang.
