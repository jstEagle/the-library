## 14. The Order of the Blank Page

Cass Marrow had a rule about trouble: it always arrived wearing good boots.

Smugglers came in bad ones—cracked leather, salt-stained, resoled twice with whatever was at hand. Priests came in soft ones. Soldiers came in heavy ones that announced them from the top of the pass. But trouble—the kind that didn't shout, didn't swagger, just *arrived*—trouble wore polished black boots and walked like it had never once been in a hurry because everything, eventually, came to it.

So when two figures stepped off the mountain road on a grey morning three days after the lullaby, and Cass saw the boots first, she was already moving.

"Elodie," she said, without turning her head, in the low carrying voice she used for cargo negotiations gone sour. "Back room. Now."

He looked up from mending a lantern wick at her table, all long limbs and keeper's calm, and opened his mouth to ask why.

"*Now,* Elodie."

He went. That was one thing she'd learned about him in these strange weeks of prophecy books and humming gates and a child who sang dead anthems: he took instruction well. It would have been easier if he'd argued. Arguing men were predictable. Obedient men did alarming things like walk toward high passes when you told them not to, which, she noted, he hadn't done yet, but the week was young.

The bell over the door of the Salt Cellar tavern chimed—Cass had stolen it from a chapel in Keswick and it still rang faintly of hymns—and the two figures entered.

They were unremarkable in the way that only very dangerous people managed. Grey cloaks, travel-worn but clean. A man and a woman, middle-aged, pleasant-faced, the sort you'd ask for directions. The woman carried a small ledger strapped to her hip like a midwife's kit. The man carried nothing at all, which was somehow worse.

"Cass Marrow?" the woman asked, smiling. "Proprietor?"

"Depends who's asking and what they're selling."

"We're not selling." The woman produced a folded paper, smooth as a love letter. "We're asking. May we sit? We've come a long way down a very cold road."

Cass weighed it. Refusing would be memorable. Memorable was death. She jerked her chin at the table by the window—the one farthest from the back room—and said, "Tea's a copper. Stew's three. Conversation's free, but I charge for lies."

"How refreshing," said the man, and his smile was warm as a banked fire. "Then we'll be honest with you. We're from the Order of the Blank Page."

*Well,* thought Cass. *At least they don't waste time.*

She'd heard the name maybe four times in her life, always whispered, always by people who then changed the subject as though the words might stain. Enforcers of the Accord, Wren called them. The bargain's broom, she called them, less charitably. The thing that swept up after the war nobody remembered.

"Never heard of it," said Cass.

The woman's smile didn't move a muscle. "No," she agreed pleasantly. "Most people haven't. That's rather the point of us."

* * *

Their names, offered with bureaucratic gentleness, were Inquisitor Hale and Sister Verity. They ordered tea. They paid. They complimented the stew, which they did not order, and the weather, which deserved no compliments at all.

"We're looking for a man," Verity said, opening her ledger with the care of someone handling scripture. "A gatekeeper. Elodie Coldharbour. He keeps a door in the Thornfell mountains—a door most maps mark as nothing at all. Does that sound familiar?"

"Nope," said Cass.

"You have flour on your sleeve," said Hale mildly, "from baking for four this morning. Your woodpile has grown by half since the frost. And your tavern, according to the toll-keeper at the pass, has been feeding a historian, a smuggler's ward, and a gatekeeper for going on three weeks." He sipped his tea. "We're not accusing you of anything, Mistress Marrow. We're simply noting that 'nope' is an economy of truth."

Cass kept her face doing what it did best, which was nothing. Inside, she was running calculations at smuggler speed. *How much do they know. How much do they think they know. What's the exit.*

"I bake for whoever pays," she said. "Historians pay badly, mind. All questions and no coin."

"And the gatekeeper?"

"What gatekeeper? There's a door up there everyone says is a door. Man tends it. Boring as bread. If he's your fellow, take him, honestly—he drinks my tea and fixes things I didn't ask him to fix. Fixed my shutter Tuesday. Didn't even break it first."

It was, she reflected later, a terrible lie. Not because it was untrue—every word of it was true—but because she delivered it too fast and too fully, the way liars do, painting a whole portrait where an honest person would have shrugged. Hale's eyes flickered, just once, like a man reading a page and finding a smudged line.

"The door," he said. "Has it been behaving strangely lately? Sounds. Lights. Frost where there shouldn't be frost."

"Frost? In Thornfell? In autumn?" Cass laughed, and hated how loud it came out. "Everything here behaves strangely. Last month Old Tam swore his goat recited debtors' names. You want strange, talk to Tam."

Verity wrote something in the ledger. Cass couldn't see what. She had a strong suspicion it wasn't about goats.

"We'll stay the night," Verity said, closing the book. "If you have rooms."

"I've got one room and it's got rats."

"We've slept in worse company."

*That makes one of us,* thought Cass, and showed them upstairs, and spent the whole climb praying to every god she'd ever swindled that Elodie had the sense to stay behind the false wall in the storeroom, and knowing, with the particular dread of a woman who had hidden contraband for twenty years, that he almost certainly did not.

* * *

He didn't.

Not immediately—credit where due. But an hour into their confinement, while Cass dealt cards with the inquisitors downstairs and lost gracefully on purpose (losing gracefully was tradecraft; winning made people curious), Pip pushed open the storeroom door, marched past the false wall, and informed Elodie in a stage whisper audible from the docks that "the scary polite lady wrote your name in her book and I saw it."

Elodie, being Elodie, did the worst possible thing: he worried. And when Elodie worried, he moved quietly and purposefully toward the problem, because twenty years alone with a door will teach a man that problems left alone grow teeth.

Cass found him at dusk in the alley behind the tavern, wrapped in one of her old smuggling cloaks, peering through the kitchen window at the Order's table like a man watching wolves around a campfire.

"You absolute turnip," she breathed, hauling him back by the collar. "There are two of the most dangerous people in Vael—*in the former Vael*—in the former everything—sitting in my taproom drinking my tea, and you're *window shopping.*"

"They wrote my name down."

"Yes! That's what they do! It's a whole—" she gestured wildly, "—*vocation.*"

"If they have my name, they have the gate. If they have the gate—" His jaw set in that way it did, the stubborn keeper's set, the one that made Cass want to shake him and, occasionally, in moments she refused to examine, something adjacent to shaking him. "I should talk to them. Reasonably. I'm reasonable."

"You are the least reasonable reasonable man I have ever met. You are a man who has argued with a *door* for twenty years and considers that a social life."

"That was one time."

"It was last week, Elodie. Wren heard you."

His ears went red. Cass allowed herself one second of satisfaction, then grabbed his sleeve again, because the satisfaction was evaporating fast under the weight of the actual situation.

"Listen to me." She lowered her voice until it was barely breath. "You don't talk to them. Not tonight, not tomorrow, not ever, if I can arrange it. People who talk to the Blank Page come out *lighter*, understand? Lighter in the head. Wren told me—they don't threaten you, they *offer* you things. Kind things. Sensible things. And you're—" she stopped herself before saying *lonely*, because some truths were knives you didn't hand a man in an alley, "—you're exactly the kind of person they're good at talking to."

Something moved behind his eyes. He knew. Of course he knew; he'd lived his whole life pressed against the shape of something missing.

"So what's your plan, smuggler?" he asked quietly. "Hide me forever?"

"Tonight? Tonight my plan is soup."

* * *

The soup plan lasted until midnight.

Cass had put the inquisitors in the rat room, brought them extra blankets she didn't mean, and retired to the kitchen to sit with a knife she was sharpening purely for emotional support. Wren sat across from her, ink-stained and vibrating, whispering fragments of Accord law like prayers. Pip was supposed to be asleep in the loft. Pip was, instead, sitting on the stairs in her nightshirt, listening with the shameless ear of the very young.

"They knocked on the blue door," Pip said suddenly.

Cass's knife stopped. "What?"

"In my dream. Two nice people knocked on the blue door and Mama wouldn't answer, and they waited so *patiently*, and then the house got embarrassed and turned into a hill." Pip hugged her knees. "I don't like when it turns into a hill. Mama's still inside."

Wren made a small wounded sound and reached for her notebook. Cass reached first and closed it gently. "Not tonight, scholar."

"But the corroboration—"

"Not. Tonight."

It was near two in the morning when the floorboard creaked—the third one from the cellar door, the one Cass had been meaning to fix since the reign of the previous toll-keeper—and Cass was moving before her brain finished the thought, knife flat along her forearm, rounding into the taproom ready to introduce someone to the concept of regret.

Elodie stood in the dark, dressed, booted, the false wall hanging open behind him.

"Don't," said Cass.

"They're awake," he said. "Both of them. They've been awake for hours—I can hear them breathing through the floor. They know I'm here, Cass. They've known since they walked in. This whole day has been them being *polite about it.*" His voice cracked, quiet as it was. "I'm tired of hiding in walls. I spent my life behind a door. It's the same wall. It's just smaller."

And there it was—the thing she'd feared, arriving right on schedule, wearing its good boots. Not the Order. Him. The moment when sheltering someone stops being a favor and starts being a fight against their own nature.

"Elodie." She set the knife down on the bar, deliberately, where he could see it, a gesture of surrender or solidarity, she wasn't sure which. "You want to know the cost of me lying for you today? I lied *badly*. I'm a professional. Twenty years. I lied like a drunk telling time. Do you know why?"

He said nothing.

"Because when Hale asked if I knew you, my chest did a thing. Right here." She thumped herself sternum-ward. "Some stupid loyal clench, like my ribs had opinions. I haven't lied badly since I was nineteen and in love with a customs officer. Lying badly gets people hanged, Elodie. Lying badly is a *luxury* of people who have something to lose." She exhaled. "Congratulations. You're something to lose. I hate it. Go to bed."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and the lamplight caught the exhaustion and the fear and something else underneath, something that looked horribly like gratitude, which was worse than fear because fear you could manage.

Then the stairs creaked, and Pip's voice floated down, sleep-thick and matter-of-fact:

"He's already in bed. I told them he was in bed. When they knocked on my door and asked where the keeper was, I said *he's in bed, he's always in bed, he's boring.*" A pause. "Was that lying? Because it felt *great.*"

From above, faintly, came the sound of a door opening, and Inquisitor Hale's voice, warm as banked coals, drifting down through the floorboards:

"Mistress Marrow? Might we trouble you for one more cup of tea? Sister Verity and I would so like to meet your other guest."

Cass closed her eyes.

Somewhere in the dark, Elodie's hand found her shoulder—not asking permission, not quite defying her either, just *there*, steady as a doorframe.

"Well," she said to the empty taproom, to the gods she'd swindled, to the century-old country pressing its ear against the world, "the soup plan's dead."

She picked up the teapot.
