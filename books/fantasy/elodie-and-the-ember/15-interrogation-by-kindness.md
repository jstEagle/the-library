## 15. Interrogation by Kindness

They came for him on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Even catastrophes, in Elodie's experience, ought to have the decency to arrive on a dramatic night — rain, thunder, a battering ram. Instead there was a knock at Wren's rented room above the tannery, polite as a tax collector, and Cass opened the door with her hand already inside her coat before she'd finished turning the latch.

The man on the landing was nobody. That was the first thing Elodie noticed, and the thing that frightened him most. Grey coat, grey hair, a face like a door left slightly ajar — you looked at him and your mind slid off, unable to say afterward whether he'd been tall or short, young or old. He held his hat in both hands. He smelled faintly of soap.

"Master Coldharbour?" he said. "My name is Brother Anselm. I'm with the Order of the Blank Page." He said it the way another man might have said *I'm with the postal service*. "May I come in? I promise I'm alone, and I promise I've no warrant. Only questions."

"You've got questions," Cass said flatly, not moving from the doorway. "I've got answers. Let's trade in the hall."

"Miss Cassian." He smiled at her, and it was a warm smile, unhurried, and somehow that was worse than any snarl would have been. "You lied to my colleagues in Thornfell three days ago. Badly, I might add. You told them you'd never heard of a gatekeeper."

"I lie to everyone. It's how I stay charming."

"It is," he agreed pleasantly. "And yet here we all are. May I?"

Elodie found himself saying, "Yes," before he could stop himself, and heard Cass make a small furious sound beside him. But the room was small, and Pip was asleep behind the curtain in the alcove, and something in him wanted — badly, stupidly — to hear what this man had to say. He had spent two weeks being chased by shadows and rumors and burning archives. A polite man with questions felt almost like rest.

Brother Anselm stepped inside, looked around the room with genuine interest — Wren's notebooks stacked like firewood, Cass's maps pinned over the water stains, the cold stove — and sat down on the one chair without being asked twice, folding his hands on his knee like a grandfather at a christening.

"Thank you," he said. "Now. Before anything else: tea?"

"I haven't got any," Elodie said.

"A pity. I'd have liked some." He said it so sincerely that Elodie almost believed he meant it, and then wondered why that mattered, and then realized that was exactly the trick of the man. "Well. Questions, then."

* * *

He began, of all things, with an apology.

"I want you to understand that I am not your enemy, Master Coldharbour. I know what my Order's name sounds like. The Blank Page. It sounds like erasure, doesn't it? Like burning." He shook his head slowly. "But a blank page is also where every book begins. We are not the people who burn stories. We are the people who keep the world legible. There is a difference, though I grant you it's a fine one, and fine lines are where all the blood gets spilled."

"That's very pretty," Cass said from her post by the window, arms crossed. "Did they teach you that at inquisitor school, or do you write your own material?"

"They taught me arithmetic," Anselm said mildly. "The rest I learned from ledgers." He turned back to Elodie, and his eyes were kind, and Elodie hated how much he wanted to believe them. "May I tell you what's actually written in those ledgers? Not doctrine. Numbers."

Elodie nodded. His mouth had gone dry.

"One hundred and four years ago," Anselm said, "there was a war. You'll know more of it than I do — you were raised on its edges, on its oaths, on its ghosts. It had gone on for eleven years. Eleven years, Master Coldharbour. Whole generations grown up knowing nothing else. And it ended — do you know how it ended? Not with victory. Not with a treaty anyone signed in good faith. It ended with a bargain. One country consented to be unwritten. Its name, its borders, its history, its dead — folded away, sealed behind a door, removed from the memory of the world. And in exchange, the war stopped. Instantly. Completely. Men who had been killing each other that morning put down their arms that evening because they could no longer remember why."

He paused, letting that settle.

"The peace has held for a hundred years. Do you know what that means, in real terms? I can tell you, because I've read the ledgers. It means roughly four million people who did not die in wars that would otherwise have followed. Four million. Farmers who grew old. Children who grew up. Grandmothers. Lovers. All of them alive because one country agreed to stop existing." He spread his hands, palms up, an honest man showing empty pockets. "That is the bargain your gate holds shut. That is what's on the other side of your nightly rounds, Master Coldharbour. Not a nation waiting to come home. A debt waiting to be called in."

"And if it's called in?" Elodie asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

Anselm's kindness deepened, if anything. "Then everything that replaced Vael comes undone. The borders redraw themselves. The grievances wake up. The four million become a number on the other side of the ledger. And Vael returns — beautiful, lamplit, whole — built out of the lives of everyone who has lived since." He shook his head. "A country cannot be resurrected for free. Nothing is free. Someone pays, and the someone is always people who never signed the contract."

"You don't know that," Cass said sharply. "You're guessing. Your whole Order is guessing. Nobody's ever seen it happen."

"We've seen pieces of it," Anselm said quietly. "Bellharrow, for instance."

Cass went still. So did Elodie. They had passed through Bellharrow eleven days ago; they had run from it nine days ago, when the Order had burned it back into blankness and the remnant-people had scattered like ash on wind. Elodie still dreamed about the woman at the well who hadn't been able to remember her own children's faces but remembered the well.

"Bellharrow was a fold," Anselm said. "A place where Vael's returning memory pooled, the way water pools in a low spot. Two hundred souls, half-real, living on scraps. When the memory strengthened, they strengthened. And when we blanked it—" he didn't flinch from the word, Elodie noticed; he used it like a surgeon uses a knife, cleanly, without apology, "—they faded. Some of them simply stopped. Mid-sentence, mid-step. Because they were never really alive, you see. They were echoes wearing coats. And the echo was taking up room that living people needed."

"That's a lovely way to describe murder," Cass said.

"It's a way to describe triage," Anselm said, without heat. "I've done it eleven times now. I remember every town. I keep a list of the remnants we've unmade, in case anyone should ever ask me to account for it. Two hundred and six names from Bellharrow alone." He looked at Elodei steadily. "I am not a monster, Master Coldharbour. That's rather the whole point of me. Monsters are easy. Monsters you can fight. What do you do with a reasonable man holding a ledger full of numbers?"

* * *

Elodie didn't answer right away, because the terrible truth was that part of him was doing arithmetic too.

He thought of Wren, hunched over the prophecy book until dawn, translating by candle stub, weeping over the second half — *whoever opens it from inside cannot return.* He thought of Sable, standing in the high pass in his grandmother's coat, admitting she didn't know whether she was a guide or a trap. He thought of Pip, humming a lullaby no one had taught her, waking with dirt under her nails from a garden that didn't exist, asking Cass when they could go home to the blue door.

Home. Everyone kept using that word around him as though it were simple. As though home were a place you walked back into, and not a wound with a roof on it.

"Say I believe you," Elodie said slowly. "Say the ledger's true. Say every number is honest. What is it you want from me?"

"Nothing," said Brother Anselm, "that you wouldn't be glad to give."

He reached into his coat, unhurried, letting them both see his hands, and drew out a slim volume bound in grey leather — the same grey as his coat, the same grey, Elodie suspected, as everything else about him. He laid it on the table between them but did not open it.

"This is what we offer the remnants, sometimes, when we find them early enough. Those poor souls in Bellharrow — some of them, near the end, were in agony. Half-remembering is worse than not remembering at all. Ask your historian; she wrote it down herself, I believe, in one of the notebooks I have unfortunately read." He smiled faintly at Cass. "You're not as careful with your papers as you think, Miss Cassian."

"Get to it," Cass said.

"The offer is forgetting," Anselm said. "Cleanly. Completely. Kindly." He turned to Elodie, and his voice softened into something almost tender. "Not just the memories of Vael, Master Coldharbour. The ache of them. The oath. The gate. The grandmother who walked through it and left a nine-year-old boy alone with a door. The prophecy with your name in it. The weight you've been carrying since before you knew it was a weight." He tapped the grey book once, gently. "We can take it all. Every trace. You would wake tomorrow in Thornfell, keeper of nothing, son of no vanished country, a man with a quiet life and no reason at all to be afraid. You would sleep well. Truly well, perhaps for the first time in your life."

"And the others?" Elodie asked. "Cass, Wren, Pip?"

"Free to go, once you agree. Their memories of recent weeks — smoothed over, nothing cruel. Miss Cassian will remember a profitable season and a ward she's fond of. The historian will remember a research trip that disappointed her. The child will remember a journey." He shrugged, apologetic. "It isn't punishment. It's mercy. For all of you."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the tannery vats sloshed. Behind the curtain, Elodie could hear Pip breathing in her sleep, quick and light, the way she always breathed, as though even asleep she expected to be running soon.

"And Vael?" he said. "If I forget — if the last citizen forgets — what happens to the country?"

Anselm folded his hands again. "It goes back to sleep. The memory loses its anchor. The roads stop rearranging. The remnants fade gently instead of violently. The gate seals itself, in time, the way a scar does. No one burns it. No one fights. It simply... ends, the way a song ends when the singer stops." He tilted his head. "Is that so terrible? A country that has been dead a hundred years, allowed to finish dying in peace, instead of clawing its way back through the bodies of the living?"

Elodie looked down at the grey book. His hands were shaking. He put them in his lap, where he hoped no one could see, and they kept shaking anyway.

Because here was the thing he couldn't say out loud, the thing that sat in his chest like a stone dropped into a well: *he wanted it.*

He wanted it so badly his teeth ached. He was tired. God, he was tired. Tired of mountains that moved, tired of prophecies with his name in them, tired of being the answer to a question a hundred years old, tired of loving people he was fairly sure he'd get killed. He was twenty-nine years old and he had spent twenty of them walking the same corridor to the same door every night, checking a lock on a country that wasn't there, and he had told himself it was duty, and some nights it even was, but most nights it was just loneliness wearing a uniform. And here was a kind man offering to set the loneliness down. Offering to let him be nobody. Nobody was safe. Nobody got to rest.

"You'd take the gate too," he said. "From my memory. I wouldn't know it existed."

"You'd know it as a story," Anselm said. "A mountain with an odd shape. Nothing more."

"And my grandmother."

The inquisitor was silent a moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentler than ever, and that gentleness was the cruelest thing in the room. "There would be no hole where she was. That's the artistry of it. You wouldn't grieve her, because you would never have known there was anything to grieve. She'd be a stranger's name in a stranger's story. You'd hear 'Vael' someday and feel nothing at all." He leaned forward slightly. "Think of it, Master Coldharbour. Nothing at all. Do you know how many people would kill for nothing at all?"

"I know how many people died for something," Elodie said, and heard how thin it sounded, and hated himself for how much he meant it anyway.

Cass made a noise that might have been a laugh or a warning. "Tell him the rest, brother. Tell him what happens to the ones who refuse."

Anselm sighed, and for a moment — just a moment — the pleasantness thinned, and Elodie saw underneath it: a tired man, middle-aged in a way his face refused to confirm, who had blanked eleven towns and counted four million saved and slept, he suspected, about as well as Elodie did. Which was to say, not at all.

"The ones who refuse," Anselm said, "we grieve. And then we proceed. If you will not lay the burden down, Master Coldharbour, then the burden stays yours — and so does everything that comes with it. The Order will continue its work. Bellharrow will not be the last town. And when the gate finally opens — yours or someone else's, now or in fifty years — the ledger opens with it." He rose, smoothing his coat, and picked up his hat. "I'm not threatening you. I want that understood. I'm telling you the weather."

He paused by the curtain — the curtain behind which Pip slept — and for a heartbeat his hand hovered near it, and Elodie's heart stopped, and Cass's knife was somehow already in her hand, though neither of them had seen her move.

Anselm withdrew his hand and smiled.

"She sings in her sleep," he said softly. "The old anthem. Word-perfect. Isn't that remarkable? A child born eighty years after the country died, singing its heart-song in the dark." He settled the hat on his head at last, and became nobody again, grey and unmemorable, a man shaped like a closed door. "Consider the offer, Keeper. The page stays blank until it's written. But ink dries faster than you'd think."

He let himself out. On the landing, his footsteps made no sound at all.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Cass crossed the room in three strides and shot the bolt, and stood with her forehead against the door, breathing, and Elodie sat down heavily on the floor with his back against Wren's stack of notebooks, because his legs had decided the chair was too far away.

"He offered to let me forget her," he said. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "All of it. The whole — the oath, the gate, her. Just — wipe it clean, like frost off glass."

"I know."

"He was *kind* about it, Cass. That's the worst part. He wasn't cruel, he didn't threaten, he sat there with his ledger full of four million saved and he made it make sense, and I sat there wanting it. Wanting it so badly I could taste it. A life where I'm nobody. Where nothing hurts." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know what I thought about, while he talked? Sleep. Actual sleep. A night where I didn't dream about a door."

Cass slid down the wall opposite him until they were sitting level, knees nearly touching, and for once she didn't have a joke ready. She looked at him for a long moment with those sharp smuggler's eyes that missed nothing, and whatever she saw in his face made her jaw tighten.

"How close?" she asked quietly. "How close did he get?"

Elodie didn't answer. He reached instead for the grey book, still lying on the table where the inquisitor had left it, and turned it over in his hands. It was lighter than it looked. Blank, when he opened it — of course it was blank; it was the Order's whole theology in binding. A page waiting. His page, maybe. His name wasn't in it yet.

His thumb moved across the empty paper, back and forth, back and forth, and somewhere under the exhaustion and the vertigo and the terrible sweet pull of *nothing at all*, a single sentence surfaced in him, his grandmother's voice, worn smooth as river stone from twenty years of repeating it to himself in the dark:

*A gate is only shut from the side that stays.*

He closed the book.

"Cass," he said, and his voice was steadier than he had any right to expect, "how fast can you get us out of Thornfell?"
