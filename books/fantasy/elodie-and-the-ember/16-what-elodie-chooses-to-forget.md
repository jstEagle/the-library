## 16. What Elodie Chooses to Forget

The inquisitor did not reach for the book when Elodie closed it. That was the first strange thing. Elodie had expected—had braced himself for—some sudden violence, the polite mask dropping to reveal whatever lived underneath all that gentleness. Instead Brother Halven simply folded his hands on the table between them, in the back room of the Drowned Lantern where they'd talked for three hours, and looked at Elodie with an expression that was not anger, and was not disappointment, and was somehow worse than both.

"You understand," Halven said, "that I cannot offer this twice."

"I understand you offered it once," Elodie said. His voice sounded like someone else's—someone braver, or perhaps just more tired. "That's already once too many."

"The peace held for a hundred years because people chose to forget. Not villains. Not cowards. People who loved their children and wanted those children to grow up in a world that wasn't at war." Halven's voice stayed level, warm even, the voice of a man explaining weather. "You would be one of them. You would be *safe*, Keeper Coldharbour. You would wake tomorrow and remember only that you keep a door, and that the door is shut, and that you have always been a little lonely, and that loneliness is no one's fault."

"And Pip?"

"Pip would be raised in comfort. Educated. Loved." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "She would stop dreaming of a house with a blue door. Isn't that a kindness? To let a child sleep?"

Elodie's hands were flat on the table. He watched them as if they belonged to a stranger, because in a way they did—they belonged to the man he was about to become, the man with the clean erasure, the man who would never again hear music through stone.

And here is the thing Elodie would carry, afterward, the way a man carries a scar he got doing something foolish: for one long moment—one breath, two, three—he had wanted it. Not in the abstract, not as a temptation he could look at from a safe distance. He had wanted it the way a drowning man wants air, whole-bodied, thoughtless. He had felt the shape of the forgetting already settling over him like snowfall, soft and total, and some animal part of him had leaned into it, had *thanked* it.

He had even said yes. Or nearly. His mouth had opened around the word and—

*You'll feel nothing,* Halven had promised. *No loss. How can you mourn what you won't know existed?*

—and then, underneath the promise, under the snowfall-softness, his grandmother's voice, worn smooth as river stone:

*A gate is only shut from the side that stays.*

He had stood up so fast the chair went over backward, and the crash of it had been the most honest sound in the room.

Now, in the present, Halven rose too, unhurried, and straightened his grey coat. "Then I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, which was the horror of him. "Truly. I've done this work twenty years, Keeper, and I will tell you the one thing I've learned: everyone who refuses me believes they're choosing memory. What they're actually choosing is being the last one holding a rope tied to a falling stone." He inclined his head toward the door. "Your friends are waiting out front. I'd hurry, if I were you. My colleagues are less patient than I am, and there are four of them, and they arrived by the north road an hour ago."

Elodie stared at him. "You're letting us go?"

"I'm telling you which road not to take." The ghost of a smile. "Consider it professional courtesy between men who both serve doors."

---

Cass was exactly where she'd sworn she wouldn't be—front table of the Drowned Lantern, hood up, a cup of something steaming going cold in front of her, and beside her Wren Ashvale with six notebooks open and Pip asleep across two chairs with her head in Wren's lap. When Elodie came through the door at a half-run, Cass read his face in the space of a heartbeat, the way she read weather and customs patrols and the exact moment a mark was about to bolt.

"North road?" she said.

"Four of them. An hour ahead of us."

"South then, through the tannery flats, over the weir—" She was already moving, shaking Pip awake with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about her hands. "Wren. Books away. We're leaving."

"I've barely begun cross-referencing the—"

"*Leaving.*"

What followed was not, Elodie thought later, the dignified flight of prophecy. It was four people and a satchel of contraband history stumbling through the reeking alleys behind the tanneries while Thornfell slept off its market day, Cass in front calling turns in a smuggler's shorthand Elodie didn't pretend to follow, Wren clutching her notebooks to her chest like a life vest, Pip running with her eyes half-shut and her hand fisted in Elodie's coat. Twice they froze in doorways while lantern-light swung past the alley mouth. Once a dog followed them for a hundred yards, deeply invested, until Cass flipped it a strip of dried fish with the weary accuracy of long practice.

At the weir—a low stone spillway where the mountain stream went fat and loud before dropping into the millrace—Cass stopped, breathing hard, and looked back at the town.

Thornfell at night was a scatter of orange windows under a black shoulder of mountain. Somewhere up there, invisible, was the Ember Gate. Somewhere behind them, four grey coats were knocking politely on doors, asking after a gatekeeper, and being told nothing, because Thornfell kept its own kind of silence—the silence of people who traded in things that shouldn't exist and had learned that questions were cargo best left undelivered.

"Coldharbour." Cass didn't turn around. "What did he offer you?"

"Forgetting. Clean, painless. And a life for Pip."

"And you said—"

"No."

"Why?"

It wasn't an accusation. That was the strange mercy of Cass; she asked questions the way she checked a lock, to see what gave. Elodie looked at the water roaring white over the stones and found he couldn't answer quickly, and that the slowness itself was the answer.

"Because I tried to say yes," he said finally. "I want that written down somewhere honest. I sat in that room and I *wanted* it, the way you want sleep after a night watch. And then I heard my grandmother tell me—for the ten-thousandth time—that a gate is only shut from the side that stays. And I understood that forgetting wouldn't be me walking away from the gate. It would be the gate walking away from me. There'd be nobody left on this side at all. Just a door in a mountain, humming, with no one listening."

Cass was quiet a moment. Then: "Huh," she said, which from Cass was a eulogy, a medal, and a marriage proposal all at once. "Right. Weir crossing. Mind the third stone, it lies."

They crossed single file in the dark, boots finding wet granite, the spray cold enough to bite. On the far bank the land climbed away from town into pine and shadow, and Thornfell shrank behind them to its handful of stubborn lights, and Elodie did not look back at the mountain, because looking back at the mountain felt like a question he wasn't ready to ask it.

Pip, riding on Cass's shoulders now, half-asleep, mumbled into her hair: "Are we going home?"

"Not yet, sprout," Cass said.

"But we're going *toward* it," Pip said—not a question, and she was asleep again before anyone could decide what to do with that.

---

They walked until the sky went the color of dishwater, and then Cass called a halt in a hollow where a rock overhang made something like a roof and old charcoal-burners' ash made something like a floor. Wren built a small fire with the guilty efficiency of a woman who had been expelled from an archive but not from competence. They ate hard bread and harder cheese. Pip slept the instant, total sleep of the young and the pursued.

And Elodie sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, and took stock of what he had chosen, because he was a keeper, and keeping was mostly inventory.

He had chosen to keep the ache. That was the plain truth of it. Brother Halven had offered him a life without the ache—the nightly walk to the gate without the dread, the mornings without the counting of days since the music started, the whole gray ledger of vigilance—and Elodie had said no, and now the ache was his again, all of it, plus interest. He tested it the way you press a bruise. The music through the stone. The frost blooming inward. His name in a dead woman's book. His grandmother's empty coat on a stranger's shoulders in the high pass. All of it, kept.

And underneath the ache, quieter, something he hadn't expected: relief. Not happiness—he wasn't sure he'd know happiness if it bit him—but the specific lightness of a man who has set down a weight he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for his spine. For twenty years he had kept the gate alone and told no one, and the secrecy had been its own second gate, and tonight, without meaning to, he'd opened it. Cass knew. Wren knew. Even Pip, in her dreaming way, knew. He was still the last citizen of a country that didn't exist, but he was no longer the only person who knew it, and it turned out that those were different things entirely.

"You're doing the face," Cass said, dropping down beside him with a canteen. "The inventory face."

"Do I have an inventory face?"

"You have a whole shelf of faces, Coldharbour, and every one of them is a locked storeroom. Drink." She pushed the canteen at him. "So. The Order's man. Twenty years doing this work, he says. Which means somewhere south there's a filing cabinet with your name in it, and probably your grandmother's, and probably a list of every keeper back to whoever first hung a lock on that mountain. Which means Wren's right, which means the prophecy's real, which means—" she waved her hand at the darkening pines, the indifferent stars, the whole rearranging world— "*that*. So my question is: where are we actually going? Because 'away' is a direction, not a plan, and I've run enough jobs to know the difference."

Elodie drank. The water tasted of iron and pine needles and, faintly, of the future.

"There's a place," he said slowly, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be lost. "My grandmother talked about it once, near the end. Before she—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a farmhouse. In a fold of the mountains. Blue door. She said if I ever needed to find the truth of Vael, I shouldn't look in archives or prophecies. I should look there." He glanced back toward where Pip slept, small and fierce even unconscious, dirt still under her fingernails from a garden that had never been planted. "I think Pip's been dreaming about it."

Cass followed his glance. Something moved across her face that she would have denied under torture.

"A house on no map," she said, "in mountains that move, leading to a country that doesn't exist, following the dreams of a seven-year-old." She took the canteen back and drained it. "Best plan I've heard all year, and I once smuggled a piano."

Across the fire, Wren looked up from her notebooks, eyes red-rimmed and burning. "If such a structure survived the Unwriting—if any *physical* record of Vael persists outside the Order's control—it would be the single most important historical discovery in a century. Possibly forever." Her voice cracked on *forever*. "When do we leave?"

"At first light," Elodie said.

But first light found them already standing, because that was when Wren, packing her books, went very still, and said, in a small voice, "Elodie. Come look at this."

She had the prophecy open on her knee, the page with his name and the date that had already come and gone. Below the passage they'd all read a dozen times, in the margin, in ink that had not been there yesterday—ink that glistened faintly, as if freshly spilled—new words had surfaced, curling up through the paper like frost through a windowpane.

Elodie read them, and the morning went cold around him, and somewhere far behind them, back toward Thornfell, he could have sworn he felt the mountain shift its weight.

The margin said:

*The keeper runs. The mountain lets him. Both roads lead to the blue door.*

"They know where we're going," Wren whispered.

"No," Elodie said, staring at the words in his grandmother's language, in handwriting he almost recognized. "Worse. *Vael* knows."
