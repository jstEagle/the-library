## 17. The Company of Strays

Cass had smuggled a lot of things in her life. Contraband brandy. Stolen relics. A live snow-lynx once, which she did not recommend. But she had never smuggled a person before, and it turned out that people were the worst cargo imaginable, because they talked.

"Are we lost?" Wren asked for the fourth time in an hour.

"We're not lost," Cass said. "We're taking the scenic route."

"The scenic route goes through a bramble thicket."

"That's the scenic part."

Behind them, Elodie said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than complaining. He walked like a man carrying something breakable inside his chest and trying not to jostle it. He'd been like that since the inquisitor's visit, since he'd come out of that polite little room where they'd offered to scoop his memories out like the pit of a fruit, since he'd refused and run. Cass didn't know what exactly had happened in there. She knew what it looked like from outside: a man who had been handed an easy way out and chosen the hard one, and now had to live with both facts.

Pip rode on Cass's shoulders because Pip's legs were six years old and the pass was steep, and because Cass liked having her up there, small and warm, humming that song. The Vaeli anthem, Wren called it. A dead country's lullaby coming out of a living child's mouth. Cass tried not to think about that too hard. Thinking about things too hard was how you ended up like Wren, hauling forty pounds of notebooks through the mountains.

They went north by the smugglers' roads — the ones that weren't on any map worth trusting, the ones Cass had learned from a man named Ferro who'd learned them from a woman whose name nobody remembered, which was its own kind of prophecy if you asked Cass, though nobody ever did. The roads wound through pine shadow and scree, under overhangs where travelers a hundred years gone had scratched their initials, past shrines to gods so minor even the priests had forgotten them. Good roads. Quiet roads. Roads that kept secrets.

She just wished her passengers would do the same.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a hollow beneath a leaning shelf of rock, with a view down into a valley full of dark pines and one distant farmhouse light, burning yellow and ordinary and very far away. Cass got a fire going small and smokeless, the way you did when someone might be looking, and started dividing out the food.

Which was when she discovered the flaw in her plan.

"You're giving him the bigger share," Wren said.

"He carried the packs all afternoon."

"I carried the books."

"Your books weigh more than he does."

"That is slander and also true." Wren adjusted her spectacles and accepted the smaller portion with the air of a woman filing a grievance for later. She'd set up camp the way she did everything: precisely, thoroughly, and slightly wrong. Her bedroll was folded into a shape no human spine could love. Her boots were lined up side by side, toes pointing at the fire, like tiny sentries. Cass had watched her spend ten minutes arranging a stone as a bookrest before realizing they were eating dinner, not holding a lecture.

Elodie sat apart, as he'd sat apart all day, turning his ration tin slowly in his hands without eating.

Cass pointed her spoon at him. "Eat."

"Not hungry."

"Eat anyway. Rule of the road. You eat when there's food, because tomorrow there might not be, and hunger makes people stupid, and stupid gets people caught."

He looked up at her then, and there was something raw and startled in his face, like she'd spoken a language he half remembered. "My grandmother used to say almost exactly that."

"Well, your grandmother sounds like she was smarter than you, because you're still not eating." She shoved the second tin across the fire at him. It scraped against stone and came to rest against his boot. "Go on. I don't carry extra weight for my health."

He ate. Slowly, but he ate. Cass counted that as a victory and pretended she hadn't been watching for it.

Pip, meanwhile, had appointed herself in charge of firewood, which meant she was collecting sticks roughly the size of her thumb and presenting them to the fire one at a time with great ceremony. The fire received each offering with the same indifferent crackle. Pip did not seem discouraged.

"That one's a good stick," she announced, holding up a crooked piece of pine.

"It's a terrible stick," Cass said.

"It's a good stick. It's curly."

Wren leaned forward, squinting. "That's a root, Pip."

"It's a curly stick."

"It's a root," Wren said again, with the gentle firmness of a scholar correcting a misdated manuscript, and Pip considered this, and decided, "It can be both," and put it on the fire, and Wren opened her mouth, thought better of it, and closed it again. Cass grinned into her tin. There were maybe four people alive who could win an argument against Wren Ashvale, and the smallest of them was currently six years old and covered in dirt.

The light went down. The stars came out the way they only did in the high country, thick and close, spilling across the sky like somebody had knocked over a bag of salt. The farmhouse light in the valley winked out. Somewhere far off, a night bird said something mournful and meant nothing by it.

And the four of them sat around a small illegal fire, strangers, more or less, and the silence got comfortable, which surprised Cass so much she nearly said so out loud.

* * *

It was Wren who broke it, because of course it was. Wren couldn't stand a silence any more than she could stand an unattributed quotation.

"Do you know," she said, to the fire generally, "I spent eleven years in the archives, and in all that time I never once ate a meal outdoors. There was a courtyard. We were permitted in the courtyard. But we took our meals in the refectory, at assigned seats, and conversation was limited to matters of scholarship." She paused. "I used to think that was discipline. Now I think it was fear wearing discipline's coat."

"Fear of what?" Pip asked.

"Of each other, probably. Of what people say when they're not being supervised." Wren turned her tin in her hands, mirroring Elodie's earlier gesture without knowing it. "They expelled me for unlicensed inquiry. Do you know what that means? It means I asked questions about Vael. Not dangerous questions. Just questions. Whether anyone had written down the words to the harvest songs. Whether the coinage reform of the last decade had finished or been interrupted. Small questions. And every answer I got was a locked door, and behind every locked door was another locked door, and eventually I understood that the doors weren't there to protect the answers." She looked up. "They were there to protect us from the answers."

Nobody said anything. The fire popped. Pip fed it a twig.

"I'm glad you got expelled," Pip said finally.

Wren blinked. "You are?"

"Mm-hm. Because if you hadn't, you wouldn't be here, and you're the only one who knows the song's name." Pip said this with the devastating simplicity of a child stating arithmetic. "Somebody has to know its name."

Cass saw Wren's face do something complicated — the look of a woman who had spent eleven years being told her work was worthless and had just been told, by a six-year-old, that it was load-bearing. Wren pushed her spectacles up and studied the fire very hard and said, "Well. Someone has to," in a voice that wobbled exactly once.

Cass passed her the tea flask without comment. That was the other rule of the road: you passed the flask when a person needed it, and you never mentioned it after.

* * *

Later, when Pip had gone down like a dropped puppet, asleep between one sentence and the next with her head on Cass's knee and one hand still curled around a curly stick, Elodie spoke for the first time in hours.

"Why did you come back for me?"

Cass looked over. He was sitting with his arms around his knees, watching the coals. "What?"

"At Thornfell. When the Order came. You hid me in the cellar under the fish barrels, and you lied to the inquisitor, and you lie badly, Cass, I heard you through the floor, you told him I was your cousin twice removed—"

"Cousins twice removed is a real thing."

"—and then when they left, you didn't take the payment and walk away. You could have. Everyone always does." He wasn't accusing her. That was the strange part. He sounded genuinely puzzled, like a man reading a map of a country he'd never visited. "Why?"

Cass took her time answering. She fed the fire a proper piece of wood, not a curly stick, and watched it catch.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I ran cargo with a man named Ferro. Good smuggler. Better liar. He taught me everything — the roads, the signals, how to spot an informer by how he holds his cup. And one night we got stopped at a checkpoint, and Ferro looked at me, and he gave me the package, and he walked toward the soldiers whistling, loud, happy, drawing every eye, and I ran the other way with the goods and got clean away." She turned the flask in her hands. "Took me three days to work out that he hadn't planned it as a lesson. He'd planned it as a trade. His freedom for mine. Except he never told me that was the deal, so I never got the chance to say no."

Elodie was quiet. "Did they catch him?"

"No. He talked his way out. He was good at that." She shrugged, which cost her some effort; the shrug came out crooked. "But I've been paying that debt for fifteen years, kid, in installments, to strangers. Every time I see somebody about to get taken and I've got a cellar handy, that's an installment." She met his eyes across the fire. "So don't make it a big thing. It's not a big thing. It's a debt."

"It doesn't feel like a small thing from in here," Elodie said quietly.

"Well." Cass cleared her throat and looked back at the fire, because some conversations you could only have while looking at a fire. "Feelings aren't cargo. Nobody weighs them."

From the darkness beyond the firelight, Wren's voice drifted over, dry as old paper: "That's the most dishonest thing I've ever heard a smuggler say."

"Go to sleep, historian."

"I'm cataloguing today's findings."

"You're eavesdropping."

"In scholarship we call that primary research."

Cass threw a pinecone at her. She heard it bounce off something notebook-shaped, followed by a dignified huff, and she lay back on her bedroll grinning at the stars, Pip's weight warm on her legs, the fire ticking down to orange.

A company of strays, she thought. A gatekeeper with no gate. A historian with no archive. A child with a country in her throat. And a smuggler with a debt she'd never finish paying, keeping them all moving north.

It was, objectively, the worst crew she'd ever run.

She fell asleep faster than she had in years.

* * *

She woke before dawn, the way she always did, to a sound that didn't belong.

Not footsteps. Not voices. Something subtler — the particular quality of silence that meant the mountains had changed shape overnight. Cass lay still and listened, and listened again, and then carefully extracted herself from under Pip and stood.

The valley below was wrong.

She'd looked at it last night. She'd looked at it properly, the way you do when you're running from the Order — landmarks, exits, sightlines. There had been a stream cutting the valley's throat, and a game trail along the ridge, and the farmhouse light in the middle distance.

The stream was still there. But the ridge trail was gone, smoothed over with trees that had no business growing that fast, and where the farmhouse had been there was now a road. An old road, sunken and mossy, running straight as a ruled line up the valley toward the north — a road built by people who expected carts and kings, not goats and smugglers. Stone-edged. Deliberate. Ancient.

Cass knew these mountains like she knew her own hands, and that road had never been there.

Behind her, a match scraped. Elodie stood at the dying fire, staring down into the valley too, his face lit orange and pale by turns. He didn't look surprised. That was the frightening part. He looked like a man recognizing something.

"Keeper's roads," he said softly. "My grandmother's maps had them. I thought they were stories." He glanced at Cass, and for the first time since Thornfell there was something in his eyes besides grief — something sharp and awake. "The mountain's remembering itself. And it's building the way north ahead of us."

Cass looked at the impossible road, and then at the sleeping child, and then at the road again.

"Right," she said. "New rule. Nobody walks anywhere until I've had tea."

Far off down the valley, faint on the morning wind, someone — or something — was singing.
