## 18. Roads That Fold

The trouble began on the third morning north, when the road behind them stopped being the road they had walked.

Elodie noticed it first, because noticing was his trade. A keeper's life was mostly noticing: the width of a crack in the lintel, the temperature of the stone at dusk, the way frost sat on the gate's hinges versus the way it sat on ordinary rock. He had spent thirty years learning to see what other people's eyes slid past. So when he turned around at the crest of a hill and saw that the switchback below them had moved—had *moved*, three feet to the east, its retaining wall now built of a different stone entirely—he did not shout or drop his pack or do any of the things Cass later claimed he'd done. He simply stood very still, the way one stands when a sleeping animal stirs beside you.

"Cass," he said quietly. "How long have we been walking?"

"Since the river. Why?"

"And we crossed the stream with the split boulder twice?"

Cass looked back. Her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding whether to lie: her jaw set half a beat too late. "Once," she said. "Obviously once."

"It's twice now," Elodie said. "It was twice an hour ago. The second time, the boulder was on the wrong side."

Pip, riding on Wren's shoulders with her chin hooked over the historian's head, said, "The mountain is doing the thing."

"What thing?" Wren asked, craning up at her.

"The folding thing." Pip made a gesture with both hands, like someone turning a page. "In my dream the roads do it all the time. You walk straight and you end up somewhere you remember instead of somewhere you're going."

Nobody answered that. Nobody ever quite knew how to answer Pip, which Elodie suspected was exactly how she liked it.

They stood in the cold morning light and watched, for the better part of an hour, as the valley rearranged itself around them—not dramatically, not with grinding stone and thunder, but with the slow confidence of a housekeeper refolding linens. A shepherd's path they had followed yesterday surfaced briefly through the turf like a scar through skin: old paving, fitted without mortar, each stone worn concave by centuries of feet. Then it sank again. Then a stretch of modern cart-road bulged and settled, and where it settled there were wheel-ruts cut into living rock, ruts too narrow for any cart built in the last hundred years.

"The Vaeli roads," Wren breathed. She had her notebook out before anyone could stop her, which was how most of her injuries happened. "Under the new ones. They're not being destroyed—they're being *remembered underneath*. Oh, this is stratigraphy happening in real time, this is—" She caught Elodie's expression and lowered the notebook. "Sorry. I know it's terrifying. It's also the single most important historical event of the century, and I would appreciate it if we could be terrified and taking notes simultaneously."

"That's generally how I live," Cass said. "Terrified and taking notes. Mostly inventory notes."

* * *

By noon it was worse, and by worse Elodie meant that the map stopped working.

Wren had drawn their route from Thornfell herself, in her precise archivist's hand, marking wells and waystations and the two passes that led north toward the high country. By noon the second pass was gone. Not blocked—gone. Where the map showed a notch between two peaks there was instead a solid shoulder of mountain, unbroken, wearing its rock like a man wearing a coat buttoned to the chin. And where no pass had been, according to every survey Wren had memorized, a gap now opened between different peaks altogether, breathing out a wind that smelled faintly of woodsmoke and something flowering.

"We can't go through there," Cass said flatly. "That's not a pass. That's a throat."

"It's a Vaeli pass," Elodie heard himself say.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. But the shape of the gap was in his bones, in the drills his grandmother had run him through until his knees bled—the blindfolded walks, the recitations, *name the passes, name them in order, name them as they were*. He had thought for thirty years that those lessons were a mad old woman's ritual, grief dressed up as education. Now the knowledge rose in him like water finding its level.

"That's the Coldwether Gate," he said, pointing. "Or it was. The Vaeli called it An-Sereth, the Breath Between. It runs above the snowline for six miles and then drops into a hanging valley. There's a shelter-station at the top carved into the rock—round, with a chimney, so the smoke tells travelers the fire's lit." His voice came out strange to him, steady and certain, a voice he hadn't used since he was nine years old. "There are cairns every quarter mile, but you don't navigate by the cairns. You navigate by the stones under your feet. The Vaeli paved their high roads with a pattern—chevrons pointing the way home. When the weather closes in, you walk by feel."

"You've never walked it," Cass said. It wasn't quite a question.

"No. I've only spent my whole life being taught to." He looked at his own hands as if they belonged to someone more interesting. "My grandmother used to say the mountains of Vael were a text, and the roads were the handwriting. She made me learn to read them with my eyes shut."

Wren had gone very still, in the way she went still around primary sources. "And you didn't think," she said carefully, "at any point in the last thirty years, to mention that you had a complete oral map of a nation erased from every archive in existence?"

"I mentioned it constantly," Elodie said. "You all assumed I was talking about hiking."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a ridge that had, according to Wren's map, been a lake until roughly Tuesday. Cass built the fire with her back pointedly toward the valley, as if refusing to watch it change. Pip fell asleep mid-sentence, as she did, her head in Wren's lap, one hand curled around a smooth grey stone she'd picked up that morning and refused to surrender.

"She says the stone is a doorstop," Wren murmured. "For a door she hasn't found yet."

"Everything with that child is doors," Cass said. She fed the fire a branch and watched it catch. "Elodie. Walk with me."

They walked along the ridge in the blue dark, far enough that the fire was an orange coin behind them. Cass waited until they were out of earshot, which told Elodie everything about how serious this was going to be; Cass did not believe in private conversations unless she was about to say something expensive.

"The Order's behind us," she said. "Two days, maybe less. Grey cloaks, traveling light, asking questions in that polite voice of theirs. I bought us the lead with a false trail south, but false trails are rent, not purchase. We need to move fast and we need to move *right*, and I don't mean right-and-left. I mean correct. And here's the problem." She stopped and turned to face him. In the starlight her face was all angles and honesty, the two things she usually kept separate. "I've smuggled through these mountains my whole life. Every pass, every fold, every game trail. I know them the way you know your own hands. Except as of this morning, I don't. The mountains aren't holding still long enough to be known. Which means the only person in this company who knows where we are"—she jabbed a finger at his chest—"is the person who learned a dead country's roads from a dead woman's mouth."

"I might not be right either," Elodie said. "The roads are changing. My grandmother's maps are a hundred years old."

"They're not changing," Cass said. "They're *returning*. There's a difference, and I think you know it, and I think it scares you worse than being lost." She let that sit. She was good at letting things sit; it was how she got people to pay debts they didn't owe. "Here's what I'm asking. Not as the one who's paid to get you places—I'm returning that coin, by the way, first town with a decent fence. As the one who doesn't want to die in a valley that wasn't there yesterday. Lead us. Your way. Blindfold drills and chevron stones and all of it."

Elodie looked out at the dark valley. Even now, even at night, he could feel it shifting beneath them—the faint muscular patience of it, a country rolling over in its sleep and pulling its old roads up around itself like blankets. Somewhere down there, a road his grandmother had walked was surfacing through a century of forgetting, stone by patient stone.

"If I'm wrong," he said, "we end up somewhere none of us can leave."

"If you're right," Cass said, "we end up somewhere the Order can't follow. They don't know these roads, Elodie. Nobody alive knows these roads." She smiled, thin and crooked. "Except you. Congratulations. You're the map."

He laughed despite himself—a short, startled sound, like a hiccup of joy. *You're the map.* Thirty years of being nobody, of keeping a door and being kept by it, of a loneliness so comfortable he'd mistaken it for peace. And now four strangers and a child needed the exact shape of what was inside him, the useless inheritance, the blindfolded recitations, the names of passes in a language no one spoke. It was the strangest feeling of his life, and he recognized it only after a moment, because he'd felt it so rarely: he was *useful*. Not dutiful. Useful. The difference was the difference between carrying a weight and carrying a gift.

"All right," he said. "But we travel by the old rules. Dawn starts, no fires on the high road, and everyone does exactly what I say even when it looks like madness. Especially then. Especially you."

"Especially me," Cass agreed, looking delighted about it in a way that promised future problems.

* * *

The Coldwether Pass took them two days, and it was like walking through a book that was being written as they read it.

The chevron-paved road was there, mostly—surfacing and sinking, sometimes whole miles of it, sometimes only glimpses, like a fish showing its spine. Elodie walked at the front with his boots bare for the first day, because his grandmother had insisted a keeper learned the stones through his soles, and because he had discovered, to his horror and delight, that she had been right. Through leather he felt nothing. Through bare skin he felt everything: the direction of the cut, the age of the wear, the faint warmth of certain stones that should have been freezing and weren't, as if the road remembered summer.

"Left fork," he said at a place where three ways braided together, and took the one that climbed, though every instinct of a modern mountaineer—and every line of Cass's body language—screamed for the low route. "The low road floods in spring melt. There's a washout at the narrows."

"There's no spring melt," Cass said. "It's autumn."

"In Vael it's always almost spring," Elodie said, and had no idea why he knew that, and hated how much he loved knowing it.

At the shelter-station near the summit—the round chamber with the chimney, exactly as he'd described it, down to the iron bracket where a lamp had hung—he found marks scratched inside the doorframe. Tallies. Dates in the old Vaeli script, which he could read and Wren, furiously copying, could not. Travelers' records. The last date was a hundred years and some months old.

Then, beneath it, fresh: a single word, newly cut, the stone dust still pale in the grooves.

*Coming.*

Wren stared at it for a long time. "Is that Vaeli?" she asked.

"Yes."

"What does it say?"

Elodie touched the letters. They were well-cut, confident, recent. Someone had stood exactly here, within days, within hours perhaps, and chiseled a promise into a doorway of a country that officially did not exist.

"It says 'coming,'" he said. "As in—we're not the only ones being summoned."

Behind them, far down the pass they'd climbed, a light flickered in the dark: small, steady, deliberate. Not a torch. A lantern, carried at walking pace, following the chevrons.

Cass blew out their own lamp without being told. "New rule," she whispered. "We stop being the ones who follow."

Elodie stood in the round shelter with his hand on the fresh-cut word, listening to the mountain breathe, and understood that the roads were folding for more travelers than one—and that whatever waited ahead in the folds was already expecting company.
