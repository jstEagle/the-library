## 19. The Town That Isn't There

Wren Ashvale had spent eleven years of her life being told, in increasingly creative ways, that she was wrong. Her thesis supervisor had said it with a fountain pen. The Archivists' Council had said it with a formal expulsion. Her mother said it every letter, in the margins, in a hand so neat it felt like violence.

So when the road bent around a shoulder of grey stone and Wren saw, in the last copper light of evening, a town that did not exist—she had the peculiar, private joy of a woman finally vindicated by reality itself.

"Bellharrow," she whispered.

"There's no Bellharrow," Cass said flatly. She had her map out on her knee without dismounting, which was a thing Cass did the way other people breathed. "I've got four maps and I've drawn a fifth myself, and none of them has a Bellharrow."

"Then your maps are incomplete."

"My maps are *mine*. They're the only honest things I own."

The town sat in a fold of the mountains like a coin at the bottom of a pocket: slate roofs, crooked chimneys, lamplight already blooming gold in windows, a church tower with no church attached to it—the tower simply ended, as if whoever remembered it had run out of memory before they ran out of tower. Smoke rose from chimneys and, Wren noticed with her scholar's heart doing something complicated in her chest, rose *wrong*—drifting upward for six feet and then simply stopping, hanging in the air like thoughts someone hadn't finished.

Pip stood up in the saddle to look. "It's like the drawings I do," she said. "When you can't remember what goes on the left side."

Elodie had gone very still. He did that; Wren was learning his silences the way she'd once learned footnotes. "The gate hums," he said quietly. "It's been humming since noon. Louder here."

"Then we go carefully," Cass said, "and we don't tell anyone our real names, and if anyone offers us pie we say thank you and eat it anyway, because I'm not rude even to ghosts."

---

They were not ghosts. That was the first thing Wren understood, and it undid her more thoroughly than ghosts would have.

The innkeeper of the inn with no name—it had a signboard, but the signboard was blank, painted over with a rectangle of nothing where words should be—was a broad woman named Marta who wiped down tables that were only half there. Wren watched her rag pass through the corner of one table as though the table were made of fog, and Marta didn't flinch or falter. She just wiped the parts that existed, the way you'd sweep around a chair leg.

"You'll be wanting rooms," Marta said. "We've got three that hold their shape all night. The fourth one leaks."

"Leaks?" Elodie asked.

"Forgets," Marta said, as though this were an ordinary word for an ordinary problem. "You wake up in the yard sometimes. No harm in it. Breakfast's included."

They took the rooms that held. And then, while Cass negotiated the price down out of pure principle and Pip investigated the hearth with the seriousness of a small general surveying terrain, Wren sat down at a half-real table across from a half-real man and opened her notebook.

His name was Aldous. He was a cobbler. He knew he was a cobbler the way you know your own name in a dream—completely, and with a strange hollowness underneath. His shop had lasted forty years, he said, and also he could not quite recall whether he had ever sold a single shoe.

"I make them," he said. He turned his hands over, palms up, as if presenting evidence. "Beautiful ones. Or—I believe I do. There's a pair in my window. Come look at them tomorrow, if the window's there."

"And is it? There, tomorrow?"

Aldous smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Wren had seen since the archives burned. "Things are here when somebody remembers them hard enough. Some nights the whole street remembers itself and it's the finest town in the mountains. Some nights it's just me and Marta and the dog, sitting in the dark, waiting for morning to bring the rest of us back."

"The dog?"

"Oh, the dog's always real." This with evident pride. "Best-remembered creature in Bellharrow. Children used to—" He stopped. Frowned. "Children used to. I think. There were children, weren't there?"

Wren wrote until her hand cramped. She filled nine pages before supper and twelve after, and when Cass came to drag her to bed she was interviewing a woman named Sef who remembered only her wedding day—not the groom, not the years after, just one golden afternoon of fiddle music and apple cake, which she lived inside the way you live inside a favorite coat.

"You're crying," Cass observed, not unkindly.

"I'm *working*."

"You're crying onto your work. It's going to smear."

"It's going to *preserve*," Wren snapped, and then heard herself, and laughed—a wet, cracked laugh—and Cass, who never touched anyone she hadn't decided to touch, put a hand briefly on her shoulder and said, "Come on, professor. Even books need sleep."

---

The trouble with Bellharrow, Wren thought later, lying awake in a room that held its shape by what felt like sheer civic effort, was that everyone in the capital would have called these people tragedies. Pitiable. Half-things. The Order of the Blank Page certainly would have—the Order, whose whole theology was that a thing half-remembered was a wound in the world, best cauterized.

But tragedy was the wrong word. Tragedy was what happened to people who lost everything. These people had *kept* something. Kept it against impossible odds, kept it the way a candle keeps flame in wind—cupped, stubborn, passed from hand to hand. Aldous kept his shoes. Sef kept her wedding afternoon. The dog kept being a dog, magnificently, for all of them.

There was dignity in it. There was more dignity in it than in anything Wren had ever catalogued behind three locks in the capital's archive, where truth was preserved the way preserves are—in jars, in the dark, nobody eating it.

Here, the truth walked around. Bought bread. Argued about the weather, which in Bellharrow was whatever the mountain remembered last.

She got up, lit her stub of candle, and went back downstairs to write more.

---

In the morning the town was fuller, and Wren understood why within the hour: word had gone round that a historian had come. A *real* one. With notebooks.

They lined up outside the blank-signboard inn like it was a clinic. A man who remembered only his hands—they had been a stonemason's hands, he was sure, though he couldn't find his arms. A pair of sisters who each remembered the other completely but themselves only vaguely, and who found this arrangement entirely satisfactory. An old soldier who remembered a war that Wren realized, with a chill that started in her spine and spread outward like frost, had been the war. *The* war. The one Vael had ended by ceasing.

"We held the pass at Cindermarch," he told her, eyes bright and far away. "Eleven days. Nobody held Cindermarch eleven days. But we did, because—" He frowned, groping. "Because we had a reason. I can't remember the reason. But oh, we *had* one."

Wren wrote it down. Her tears fell on the page and she let them, blotting them gently with her sleeve like pressing flowers.

"What was Vael like?" asked a girl of perhaps fifteen, who remembered a kitchen and a song and nothing else. "Everyone says there was never any such place. Was there?"

Wren looked at her—at her patient, hopeful, half-real face—and felt the full weight of her profession settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

"Yes," Wren said. "There was. And I am going to write down every single thing you can remember, and everything everyone else can too, and I will not stop until there's so much of it written down that no one—not the Order, not the maps, not a hundred years of forgetting—can ever say there wasn't."

The girl smiled, and for a moment—Wren would swear this to the end of her life—for a moment the whole street sharpened. The smoke finished rising. The church tower grew a roof. Somewhere a bell that had been silent rang once, experimentally, like a throat cleared.

Then it faded, and the girl blinked, and said, "That was strange. It felt like being *looked at*."

"You were," Wren said. "By me. By history. Hold still, I'm not done with you yet."

---

Cass found her at midday surrounded by notebooks, five deep now, and wearing the expression of a woman who has found her life's work and intends to die at it.

"We're leaving tomorrow," Cass said. "Elodie says the gate-hum's getting louder, and Sable's been standing at the edge of town since dawn staring at the church tower like it owes her money. Also the Order's trail is two days behind us and closing. So. Tomorrow."

"Cass." Wren grabbed her sleeve. Cass tolerated it, which was its own small miracle. "Do you understand what this place *is*? It's not a ruin. It's not a haunting. It's a *township of survivors*. Every person here is a primary source. Do you know how many primary sources I had for Vael before this week? Three. Three fragments and a forged tax ledger. Now I have a *cobbler*."

"A cobbler who might evaporate."

"Who might *evaporate*," Wren agreed fiercely, "which is precisely why we can't waste time sleeping. Get me more candles. And bread. And that dog, if it will sit still long enough to be described."

Cass looked at her for a long moment. Then she sighed the sigh of a smuggler who has accidentally acquired a company of strays, and went to steal candles.

---

Evening again. Wren sat in the inn's common room transcribing her field notes into fair copy, because a historian's first duty was redundancy, when Elodie sat down across from her. He had the particular careful quiet of a man about to ask something he'd been carrying all day.

"Wren. The people here. When the town forgets them at night—do they feel it?"

She set down her pen. "Aldous says it's like falling asleep without the dreaming part. You're just... paused. He says the worst isn't the fading. The worst is that some mornings he wakes up and can't remember why he minds."

Elodie looked into the fire for a long time. "My grandmother used to say a country doesn't live in its borders. It lives in whoever carries it." He rubbed his chest, absently, over his heart—the gesture he made whenever the gate-hum got loud. "I used to think that was just something old people said."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was giving me instructions."

Before Wren could answer, the door banged open and Pip came pelting in, breathless, dirt on her knees—which was normal; Pip collected dirt the way magpies collected silver—but her eyes wide with something new.

"There's a lady at the edge of town," she panted. "The one in the big coat. She's talking to the church tower. And Wren—" Pip's voice dropped, awed and frightened at once, "—the tower answered her. In *words*. And I know the words. They're from my dream. The house with the blue door. The lady said 'he's almost ready,' and the tower said—"

Pip stopped. Swallowed.

"The tower said, '*then take him home.*'"

Wren's pen had stopped moving. Around them the common room flickered—lamps guttering, the fire bending sideways as though listening—and for one clear moment Wren felt the whole town lean toward them, half-real and wholly attentive, a hundred unfinished lives holding their breath.

Sable appeared in the doorway behind Pip, framed against the dark, her grandmother's coat settling around her shoulders like something that had finally decided to fit.

"We need to talk," Sable said. "All of us. And Wren—" her voice caught, and steadied, "—you'll want your notebook for this one. Because I know exactly what I am. And it isn't what you think."
