## 20. Sable's Confession

The fire had burned down to that hour when even the coals seemed to be listening.

Sable sat apart from the others, as she always did—not far enough to seem unfriendly, not close enough to be asked anything. She had become very good at distances in her short, impossible life. The company slept in a loose ring around the embers: Wren with her notebooks stacked like a pillow she refused to use, Cass with one hand curled near her knife even in sleep, and Pip sprawled starfish-wide across both of them, as children do when they have decided a place is safe and intend to test it thoroughly.

Elodie was on watch. He sat at the edge of the light with his grandmother's coat—the coat, *her* coat—draped over his shoulders, though it hung wrong on him, too broad through the shoulders, smelling of cedar and cold stone. He wore it the way people wear grief: carefully, so no one notices.

Sable watched him and felt the thing in her chest that she had decided to call a heart do what it always did, which was ache in a way she could not source.

Because here was the truth she carried like a stone behind her ribs: the coat fit her perfectly.

* * *

She had woken—or begun, or assembled, or arrived; she genuinely did not know which word was honest—eleven days before she found Elodie at the high pass. She knew this because she had counted, the way a person counts when counting is the only proof they exist.

She had woken in a meadow above Bellharrow with snow on her eyelashes and a complete set of memories that were not hers, wearing a coat that fit her like a second skin, knowing things she had no right to know: the way Vaeli bread was scored with three slashes for the three rivers; the words to songs whose composers had been unwritten; the exact weight of a small boy's head against her shoulder on a night forty years gone, a boy who had fallen asleep crying because he'd been made to swear an oath too big for him.

That last memory was the cruelest gift. It came with warmth attached. It came with love attached. And it was not hers.

She had spent those eleven days testing herself the way Wren tested documents—holding each memory up to some inner light, looking for watermarks, looking for forgery. And every single one failed the test, because every single one felt true. That was the horror of it. A lie you can catch. But these were not lies. They were *borrowings*. They were the real memories of a real woman named Maren Coldharbour, kept alive in a country's mouth like a song hummed by someone who never met the composer—and then poured into a shape that walked and spoke and loved and lied.

Because Sable had lied. She knew that too. She had stood in the high pass wearing that coat and let Elodie look at her face—at a face built from his oldest grief—and she had said nothing while hope broke over him like a wave. She had let him call her *Grandmother* in the smallest voice, a voice he clearly hadn't used since he was nine years old. She had held his hands with hands made from the memory of holding them.

She was, she had concluded somewhere around day six, either the kindest thing Vael had ever made or the cruellest, and the truly unbearable part was that she could not tell which from the inside. There was no inside. There was only the coat, fitting perfectly.

Tonight, watching Elodie sit his watch with her—*Maren's*—coat around his shoulders, she understood that she could not go one more mile carrying it quietly. Whatever he decided to do with the truth, he deserved to hold it. It was the only thing she had that was actually hers: the decision to stop lying.

She rose. Her knees made no sound. Of course they didn't. She wasn't old. She only remembered being old.

* * *

"You'll want to sit," she said, when she reached him. "Or stand. Whichever. I've rehearsed this eleven ways and none of them survive contact."

Elodie looked up. In the ember-light his face was all Coldharbour angles—she knew because she was wearing most of them. "Rehearsed what?"

"The confession." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I've been putting it off since Bellharrow. Before that, honestly. Since the pass. There's a version where I tell you gently, over tea, with context. There's a version where Wren helps, because she likes footnotes and I thought maybe footnotes would soften it." She huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. "But you're a gatekeeper's grandson, and gatekeepers don't get gentle versions. They get doors opened straight onto the drop. So."

"Grandmother—" he started.

"That's the first thing." Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected, which felt like cheating. "I'm not her. Sit down, Elodie. Please. I need you sitting for this, because I'm going to say it fast, like pulling a splinter, and I'd rather you fall down on purpose than by accident."

He sat. Slowly, the way people sit when the ground has already moved once beneath them and they've learned suspicion of solid things.

Sable stayed standing. If she sat she might reach for his hand, and if she reached for his hand with these hands she would never forgive herself, and forgiveness—she was learning—was a currency she hadn't earned.

"I woke eleven days before I found you," she said. "In a meadow above Bellharrow. Alone. No past, no future, just—" she gestured vaguely at her own self, the whole improbable arrangement of her, "—this. Fully formed. With her memories. *Your* grandmother's memories. Every one of them, pressed into me like flowers in a book. The oath night. The bread. Your grandfather's terrible singing. The weight of your head on my shoulder." She saw him flinch and made herself keep going; mercy now would only make it worse later. "I didn't lose those memories, Elodie. I never had them. They were given to me. Assembled. I am—" and here was the sentence she had turned over eleven hundred times, looking for a softer shape and finding none, "—I am something Vael made out of her. Out of what it remembers of her. A remnant, like the people in Bellharrow, except they're half-real and I'm—" she laughed, and it came out wrong, "—apparently I'm very well-made. Vael wanted a guide it could trust. So it built one out of the person you trusted most."

The fire popped. Down in the ring of sleepers, Pip murmured something about a garden and rolled over.

Elodie said nothing for a long time. Sable let the silence stand, because she owed him that much at least—a silence he could fill at his own speed.

When he finally spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man checking whether floorboards would hold. "The pass. When I found you. You let me believe—"

"Yes."

"You said—" His jaw worked. "You said you'd been waiting for me. You said *you kept the lamp lit.*"

"I said what she would have said." Sable closed her eyes. "That's the worst part, and I want you to understand it fully, because you deserve the full ugliness of it. I didn't choose those words. I didn't craft them. They simply *came*, the way breathing comes, because Vael remembers her saying them—it must remember her saying them, standing at some door, waiting—and so when I needed words, its memory supplied them, and they were perfect, and perfect is exactly how you trap a man who has spent his whole life starving for one person." She opened her eyes. "I could have corrected you. In the first minute. In the first breath. I knew within an hour of waking what I was—I'm not stupid, whatever else I am—and I chose the coat and the lamp and the name because you looked at me like the sun coming up, and I was eleven days old, Elodie, and nobody had ever looked at me like anything, and I was selfish for exactly as long as it took you to love me."

"Don't," he said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't call it selfish." He was staring into the embers. "You're describing being born lonely and handed a family. That's not a crime. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know what I did after you vanished? After the gate? I went back up that mountain every night for a year and knocked. Politely. Like the door was a neighbor who'd stepped out. I was nine, and I knocked on a mountain for a year." His laugh had no humor in it at all. "And then a woman walks out of nowhere wearing her coat, and I didn't question it. Do you understand? I *wanted* not to question it. Whatever you did at that pass, I did worse, because at least you were new. I had forty years of practice telling myself the truth and I threw it away in four seconds."

They sat with that. Somewhere downslope an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which struck Sable as the most honest creature present.

"There's more," she said. "The part I like least."

He looked up. Braced. She was learning to read him—another borrowed skill, or maybe not borrowed, maybe earned, she couldn't tell anymore and had stopped trying—and she saw him brace.

"Vael sent me. I told you that part, or implied it. What I haven't said is that I don't know what I was sent *for*. And I mean that literally, not dramatically. There are two possibilities, and I carry both of them everywhere like twins." She held up one hand. "Possibility one: Vael loves what's left of itself, and it made me to guide you safely to the gate, to help you choose well, to protect you. A country's tenderness, given legs." She held up the other. "Possibility two: Vael is hungry. It has been shut behind a door for a hundred years, remembering itself harder and harder, going mad with wanting to exist, and it made me because it learned—from her, from Maren, from the woman it watched walk in and stay—that the surest way to move Elodie Coldharbour is to give him back the person he lost. Not to guide you. To *use* you. To walk you to that door the way you'd walk a key to a lock, and open you, and pour itself through, and hang the world's peace on the wall like a coat it doesn't need anymore."

The wind moved through the pines above them. Elodie's face had gone very still—the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want finished.

"And you don't know which?" he asked.

"I don't know which." She said it flatly, because dressing it up would be another kind of lie. "There's no little voice giving me orders. No instructions stitched into my lining. Just—impulses. Urges. A pull toward the gate that gets stronger every day, like tide. And I can't tell if the pull is mine or its. That's the truth of me, Elodie. I am a letter that doesn't know if it's a love letter or a summons. I've read myself a hundred times and the handwriting is beautiful and the meaning won't resolve."

"Then how do we find out?"

"We may not." She crouched down now, finally, level with him, keeping her hands folded tight so they couldn't commit treason. "But I'll tell you what I *do* know. I know that eleven days ago I was nothing, and everything I have become since, I became by watching you. Your stubbornness. Your awful jokes at bad moments—you get those from her, by the way, she'd have wanted you to know that. Your habit of feeding people before you feed yourself. I know that when I imagine the gate opening and Vael pouring out, some part of me is glad, and when I imagine *you* hurt by it, every part of me revolts. Is that loyalty? Is it programming? Is there a difference?" She shook her head. "I can't answer that. Nobody can. But I can offer you the only thing I own outright."

"Which is?"

"This. Right now. The truth, unlovely, unsoftened, delivered badly." She smiled, and it hurt her face in a way that felt human, which she chose to trust. "Wren collects documents. Cass collects debts. Pip collects rocks, apparently, I found seven in her boot. Me—I collect honesty. It's the only asset I have that Vael didn't mint. So here is my entire fortune, spent in one place: I am not your grandmother. I am made of her. I may be a guide or a lure and I cannot tell you which. And from this moment forward I will never again let you believe a comfortable thing about me that isn't true. That's my confession. That's all of it. Judge me however you need to."

* * *

Elodie was quiet for so long that Sable began composing her exile in her head—which valley, which road, whether she'd leave the coat (no; the coat was his by every right that mattered).

What he said instead was: "Say the third part again."

"Third part?"

"'Made of her.' You said you're not her, but you're made of her. Say it again."

She frowned. "I'm not your grandmother. I am made of her. Of what Vael remembers of—"

"No, that's—" He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he was almost laughing, in the unhinged way of a man whose night has gotten away from him entirely. "Do you realize what you've just described? You're describing *keeping*. That's the whole job, Sable. That's the entire oath. A keeper isn't the thing behind the door. A keeper is what the door is made *of*—memory, packed and shaped and given a duty. My grandmother packed herself behind that gate to hold it shut. You're her memory, packed and shaped and given a duty. You're not a forgery of her. You're her *next post*. The line didn't break when she walked through. It changed material."

Sable stared at him. Something in her chest—the thing she called a heart, the thing she had no right to—turned over like soil.

"That's either very wise," she managed, "or you're so tired you've started making sense by accident."

"Cass says the second one happens to me a lot." He looked at her then, really looked, and she braced for the grief in his face—for the moment he'd see the seams in her, the places where Vael's needlework showed. Instead he said, quietly: "Does it hurt? Being made of someone? Carrying their whole life and none of your own?"

And that—that was the question nobody had asked her. Eleven days of existence and the first person to ask about the *wearing* of it rather than the danger of it.

"Yes," she said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, and she let it. "It hurts like wearing a coat that fits perfectly and isn't yours. Everything fits. Nothing belongs."

"Then here's what we'll do." He stood, and held out his hand—her grandmother's gesture, offering a hand up off cold ground, and the irony of it nearly buckled her. "We can't untangle you tonight. Maybe we can't ever. But you don't sit watches alone anymore, and you don't confess alone, and when we get to the gate—if we get to the gate—we find out together what you were sent for. Guide or lure. And if it turns out you're a lure, Sable, then I'll do what Coldharbours do with traps."

"What's that?"

"Spring them early, and take the teeth out, and turn them into something useful." He shrugged. "Grandmother caught rabbits that way. Said a sprung snare makes the best pot-hook."

She took his hand. Her knees made no sound as she rose. His were louder, and she found she liked that—liked the evidence of him, the wear and weight of an actual lived body, the creak of a real man next to her borrowed perfection.

Behind them, from the depths of the sleeping ring, Pip sat bolt upright, hair like a startled hedge, and announced to no one in particular: "The garden's blooming. It wasn't yesterday. Somebody's *almost home*."

Cass, without opening her eyes: "Pip. Boots off the bedroll."

"They're not boots, they're rocks."

"*Pip.*"

Sable caught Elodie's eye, and for the first time in her eleven-day life she laughed at something that was hers—wholly, provably hers, grown from this moment and no other.

Then, over the ridge to the north, where the mountains had been rearranging themselves for days like furniture in a dream, a light appeared. Small. Steady. Lamplight, where no road ran.

It had not been there an hour ago.

Neither of them said anything. But Sable felt the pull in her chest lean toward it like a compass finding north, and this time—just this once—she thought she felt Elodie's hand tighten on hers, asking her silently:

*Guide, or lure?*

*Walk with me and we'll find out.*
