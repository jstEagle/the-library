## 21. The Order Closes In

Wren Ashvale had interviewed forty-one remnants in three days, and she had wept after every single one of them, which was ruining her notebooks.

The tears warped the pages. The ink ran. She had taken to writing in pencil, pressing hard, the way you press when you are afraid the words themselves might be unwritten if you don't leave a groove deep enough to find with your fingertips.

Bellharrow sat in a fold of the mountains that no cartographer had ever sinned against, because no cartographer had ever found it. The town had a mill without a river and a church without a god's name over the door—the nameplate above the altar was blank, polished smooth by a century of hands touching it, trying to remember. The people were kind and slightly translucent at the edges, like figures seen through frosted glass. A baker named Ollin who could not recall what his bread tasted of but baked it perfectly every morning anyway. A woman called Maren who had been waiting sixty years for a husband whose face she described as "sort of brownish, I think, or possibly a hat."

Wren filled notebook after notebook. She wrote down Ollin's recipe. She wrote down Maren's wedding song, hummed in a key that made her teeth ache. She wrote down the names the town still half-held—*Aldous, Brenna, Cott, Damaris*—an alphabet of ghosts marching toward some letter she never reached, because there were always more names than pages.

"You'll write us down proper?" Ollin asked her on the third evening, dusting flour off hands that left no flour anywhere. "So somebody knows we were?"

"Somebody will know," Wren said. "I promise you. Somebody will know everything."

She believed it. That was the terrible thing about Wren Ashvale: she believed her own promises, even the ones the world was actively arranging to break.

* * *

Cass knew trouble the way sailors know weather—not from books, but from a cold feeling at the base of the spine and a sudden interest in leaving.

She felt it on the fourth morning, standing in Bellharrow's market square, haggling for apples from a vendor who kept forgetting how many apples he'd sold and therefore always arrived at a number that benefited Cass enormously. She'd gotten six apples for the price of one and was feeling smug about it when the cold feeling arrived, followed by horses.

Not smuggler horses. Smuggler horses came up the roads tired and complaining, like their riders. These horses came up the road *quiet*, in step, ridden by men and women in grey coats with white buttons, and every one of them wore a blank white page pinned over their heart.

"The Order," Elodie said beside her. He'd gone very still, the way he went still at the gate when something moved on the other side of it. "That's them. That's the polite ones."

"Polite is worse," Cass said. "Rude ones want something from you. Polite ones have already decided."

There were twelve of them. They dismounted in the square with the unhurried grace of people who had never once been chased, and their leader—a woman with kind eyes and a voice like a lullaby read aloud from a warrant—addressed the market at large.

"Good people of Bellharrow." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "I regret to inform you that this settlement does not exist."

Ollin dropped his tray. The bread scattered and, Cass would swear to her dying day, began to fade before it hit the ground, going grey and then going nothing, and the smell of it—warm, real, the only honest thing about him—went with it.

"We're here," Maren said, stepping forward, chin up, translucent hands balled into fists that cast almost no shadow. "We've been here sixty years. My husband is due back any day."

"I'm so sorry," the Order woman said, and meant it, which was the worst part. "But you were a mistake in the accounting. And mistakes must be corrected."

Then they brought out the lanterns.

They weren't fire-lanterns. They were blankness-lanterns, if such a thing can be described, and Cass would spend years failing to describe them: light that unlit things. Wherever the glow touched, color went first, then shape, then the memory of shape. A fence became a suggestion of a fence became a straight line of pale air. A dog began to bark and forgot, mid-bark, why.

"Burn it back to blank," the woman said gently. "All of it. We'll be done by supper."

Cass grabbed Elodie's sleeve. "Back door. Every town has one. Even towns that don't exist *especially* have one."

But Elodie wasn't moving. He was watching an old man by the well—an old man who was looking at his own hands as they went faint, turning them over and over like a man checking whether he'd already died—and Elodie's face did something Cass had never seen it do in all their weeks of roads and firesides. It stopped being careful.

"Wren!" he shouted across the square. "WREN! Get Pip and *run!*"

* * *

Wren ran. It was not dignified. Wren Ashvale had spent thirty years in archives and her running style reflected it—elbows in, satchel clutched to chest, notebooks first, body a distant second consideration. She scooped Pip off the church steps where the girl had been drawing the blank nameplate ("it says something," Pip insisted, "it just says it quietly"), and ran.

Behind them, Bellharrow burned with white fire.

It didn't scream. That was what Wren couldn't stop thinking later, what she'd write and cross out and write again: *it didn't scream.* The town simply grew quieter, street by street, like snowfall in reverse. The mill went. The church went. Ollin stood in his doorway as the whiteness took the bakery and he waved—at her, she was sure, at *her*, the one person who'd promised—and then he was a smudge, and then a shimmer, and then a street corner where nobody had ever stood.

Maren lasted longest. She stood in the middle of the square facing the Order woman, fading from the feet up, and she was still arguing.

"He'll come home," Wren heard her say, thin now, like a voice through a wall. "He'll come home and there'll be no wife. Whose mistake is *that*, madam? Whose ledger balances *then?*"

"I'm sorry," the woman said again, and raised her lantern, and Maren was gone, and the square was just a square, and the mountains behind it were just mountains, and there was no town on any map because there had never been a town on any map, and soon there would be no Wren Ashvale who remembered otherwise except—

Except the notebooks. Except the grooves pressed deep enough to find with your fingertips.

Wren ran harder.

* * *

They regrouped—or rather, Cass herded them—in a ravine north of where Bellharrow had been, which was now just a ravine north of nowhere. Sable was already there, having vanished an hour before the Order arrived with the unnerving punctuality of someone who heard trouble coming the way cats hear weather. She had Pip by the hand and was scanning the ridgeline with eyes that saw too much.

"Elodie," Cass said, counting heads. "Where's Elodie?"

The silence answered wrong. Cass turned.

Elodie was walking back down toward the smoke-that-wasn't-smoke.

"Oh, you absolute—" Cass took two strides after him, stopped, because chasing a keeper into an active erasure was how you became a footnote, and shouted instead, "ELODIE COLDHARBOUR, IF YOU DIE DOING SOMETHING STUPID AND NOBLE I WILL BRING YOU BACK SPECIFICALLY TO KILL YOU."

He either didn't hear or pretended not to. Both were plausible. He disappeared over the rise.

"That boy," Cass said, to no one, to everyone, to the sky, "was raised by a mountain and it shows."

* * *

Elodie found three of them.

They were remnants from the town's edge, slower than the rest, caught in the white glow's outer reach. An old woman. A young man. A child—a girl of perhaps seven, sitting very still on a stone wall that was forgetting to be a wall, watching her own fingers go transparent with the terrible patience of someone who has been told all her life that she isn't quite real and has finally received confirmation.

Elodie knelt in front of her.

"I'm Elodie," he said. "I'm a keeper. I keep doors shut. But today—" and his voice cracked, and he let it, "today I'm opening one. Can you walk?"

"I'm Nettie," the girl said. Her voice was already thin. "Am I going somewhere?"

"Yes," Elodie said, and took her hand, and it was like holding a handful of cold smoke that gripped back. "You're coming with us. Hold on tight. Don't let go, no matter what."

The old woman was past saving; she smiled at him as she thinned, and said, "Tell someone I liked honey," and was gone between one word and the next. The young man made it ten steps before the whiteness took his legs out from under his existence, and he looked back at Elodie with an expression of apology, as though fading were a discourtesy he was committing personally, and then he was a warmth in the air and then not even that.

Nettie held on.

Whatever she was made of—scraps, echoes, a country's longing shaped small and stubborn—she held on to Elodie's hand with everything in her, and the whiteness washed over them both and broke around them like water around a stone, because Elodie Coldharbour was the last citizen of Vael and erasure, whatever else it was, apparently had rules, and he was exempt.

He walked out of Bellharrow carrying a child who weighed almost nothing and everything, while behind him the last lamplit window of a town that had never existed went dark forever.

* * *

By dusk they were miles north, camped in a hollow under a shelf of rock, with no fire because Cass had opinions about fires when twelve grey-coated riders were within a day's ride.

Pip sat with Nettie wrapped in Cass's spare cloak, holding her hand, and had not let go since the ravine. Not to eat. Not when Cass had checked her boots for stones. Not once.

"She'll fade if I let go," Pip explained, with the flat certainty of a six-year-old stating physics. "You can feel it. Like when you let go of a balloon."

"Pip, love, you need to sleep sometime."

"Then she sleeps too." Pip lay down, still gripping, and pulled Nettie down beside her, and within minutes both girls were asleep, one breathing loud and real and snoring slightly, the other breathing like an echo of breathing, and Cass looked at them for a long time and then looked away fast, because her eyes were doing something embarrassing.

Wren sat apart with her notebooks, writing by moonlight, pressing hard. Elodie settled beside her.

"How many did we lose?" he asked quietly.

"Forty-one I spoke to." Her pencil paused. "Plus everyone I didn't. Plus the town itself. The streets, the bells—I never learned if they had bells. Now nobody ever will." She stared at the page. "Twelve riders and two lanterns, Elodie. That's all it takes. Two lanterns and a polite voice, and a hundred years of *people* becomes a paragraph in a report that says 'no anomalies found.'"

"They'll come for us next."

"They'll come for *you* next. You're the anomaly now. You and—" she glanced at the sleeping girls, "—and apparently anyone you hold hands with."

Elodie watched Nettie breathe her echo-breath. "She held on," he said. "Everything else let go. Everything else just... agreed to stop. But she held on."

"Children do that," Wren said. "Adults call it stubbornness. Children call it being alive."

Sable appeared at the edge of the hollow—she moved like that, arriving without footsteps, a woman assembled from borrowed memories—and crouched by the sleeping girls. For a long moment she only looked at Nettie, and her face was unreadable, and then readable, and what it read was grief.

"She's newer than me," Sable said softly. "Made recently. Vael is getting... desperate. It's reaching further, making more of us, faster, cruder." She touched the girl's hair without quite touching it. "When I was made, I had a whole grandmother in my pieces. This little one got maybe a kitchen and a bedtime story and a name."

"Can she last?" Elodie asked. "Out here, away from—away from wherever she came from?"

Sable was quiet long enough that the question answered itself.

"There's a place she can last," Sable said at last, and her eyes went north, toward the high pass, toward a gate Elodie had spent his whole life keeping shut. "But you know that. You've known it since Bellharrow started burning. Everything Vael makes lasts only near Vael." She rose. "Every road you run down, keeper, ends at that door. It's starting to look less like a choice and more like an address."

She drifted off to take first watch, leaving Elodie staring north.

* * *

Cass took second watch, which meant she sat awake pretending to take second watch while actually inventorying her life and finding it catastrophically overstocked with people she cared about. A historian who cried onto primary sources. A gatekeeper who ran into burning nonexistence to grab children made of leftover country. A remnant woman who might be a grandmother or might be a trap with good posture. And two sleeping girls sharing one cloak, one heartbeat loud, one heartbeat like a rumor.

"This wasn't the job," she informed the night. "The job was coins and lies and never sleeping in the same valley twice."

The night declined to respond. The night was good at boundaries; Cass respected that.

Near dawn, Wren woke with a gasp and scrabbled for her satchel, pulling out the prophecy book—the book that had started everything, tonight's date written beside a name. She'd been translating its obscured final pages for weeks, working by scraps of firelight, and now she stared at the page as though it had bitten her.

"What?" Elodiе was up instantly. "What is it?"

Wren didn't answer right away. Her lips moved through the old Vaeli script one more time, checking, hoping she was wrong, and Wren Ashvale had spent her whole career hoping she was wrong exactly once per lifetime and this was shaping up to be it.

"The ending," she said hoarsely. "The part that was smudged—the part I couldn't read. I thought it was damage. It's not damage, Elodie. It's a warning." She looked up, and in the pre-dawn grey her face was the face of a woman holding a map to a cliff edge. "The gate opens both ways. And whoever opens it from inside..."

She stopped. Behind her, unnoticed, the first light touched the mountains, and far to the north—so far it should have been invisible, and yet every one of them saw it clearly, the way you see things a country wants you to see—a door-shaped seam in the rock glowed faintly gold, like an ember deciding whether to become a fire.

"...cannot return," Wren finished. "The prophecy never said Vael wins. It only ever said someone chooses."

Nobody spoke. Then Nettie stirred in her sleep, tightened her grip on Pip's hand, and murmured, in a voice like a kitchen and a bedtime story and a name:

"Don't let go."
