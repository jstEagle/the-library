## 22. The Prophecy's Second Half

Wren Ashvale had spent eleven years learning to read what wasn't there.

It was a peculiar specialty, and it had cost her everything—her post, her reputation, her rooms in the capital with the good light for manuscripts. The Archivists' Guild had a phrase for scholars like her: *chasing ghosts*. They said it the way you'd say *drinking before noon*. But chasing ghosts was precisely the work, because Vael had not been destroyed. It had been *unwritten*, and unwriting left seams. A country erased by bargain rather than by fire leaves fingerprints in the margins of other countries' books. You only had to know where to look, and be willing to look like a fool while doing it.

So Wren looked. She looked in tax ledgers where a line of revenue simply stopped mid-sentence, the clerk's hand trailing off into an ink blot as though he'd fallen asleep or been made to forget what he was writing. She looked in border treaties that referenced "the three parties" when there were plainly only two signatories. She looked in a cookbook from the eastern valleys that called for "Vaeli salt" in a recipe for honeyed lamb, and no editor had ever struck it out, because who edits salt?

And she looked, now, at the prophecy book spread open on the camp table between two tin plates of Cass's frankly criminal stew.

The book itself was a stubborn thing. It had been bound in grey leather gone soft as old bread, and its pages smelled of cold stone and something faintly sweet, like apples left too long in a cellar. The text was in Old Vaeli, which Wren read the way some people read music—fluently enough to perform, slowly enough to sweat over the difficult passages. Most of it she had translated weeks ago. The opening verses were straightforward prophecy boilerplate: a gate, a keeper, a date. The middle section named Coldharbour with the terrible specificity of a debt collector.

But the ending—the ending was obscured. Not damaged. *Obscured.* The final page bore a block of text that shimmered when you looked at it directly, letters sliding against each other like fish in a net. Wren had tried candlelight, moonlight, firelight, reading it upside down, reading it backward, reading it aloud until Pip begged her to stop because the words "made her teeth itch."

Tonight, something had changed.

She'd noticed it at supper. The page had been restless all day—she could feel it through her satchel, a faint vibration like a trapped moth—and when she finally laid it bare under the lantern, the shimmering had slowed. The letters still moved, but sluggishly now, wearily, like fish in a net that someone had carried a very long way.

Because Vael was stronger now. Because the roads were folding. Because Bellharrow had existed, and then hadn't, and they had run from the burning blankness of it with a remnant child clutching Pip's hand. Because Elodie had stood at the head of a funeral procession of the unwritten, white-faced and upright, walking toward a gate he'd spent his whole life keeping shut.

The book was tired of hiding. Or the country behind it was running out of time to be subtle.

Wren dipped her pen. Around her, the camp settled into its evening sounds: Cass re-tarring a seam on one of her packs with the focused profanity of a woman who trusted rope more than people; Pip teaching the remnant child—Ilsa, Ilsa who remembered a garden—to fold paper boats from old invoices; Elodie sitting apart, as he always did now, turning his grandmother's coat button over and over in his fingers like a man counting a rosary with only one bead.

Nobody noticed when Wren began to work. That was fine. Scholars were furniture until they shouted.

* * *

The first line came free within the hour, and it nearly stopped her heart.

*The gate opens both ways.*

Four words. Simple as a knife. Wren sat back and stared at them, because every account she'd ever found—the Order's own histories, the peace treaties, the smug little commemorative pamphlets printed after the Unwriting—described the Ember Gate as a door that had been *sealed*. Shut. Bolted from outside by the victors, locked by the keepers, closed forever. A wall. An ending.

But this said otherwise. This said the gate had never been a wall at all. It said the gate opened both ways, had always opened both ways, and that the sealing was not a locking but a *balancing*—something held shut from both sides at once, like two hands pressed palm to palm through a window.

Which meant the keeper's line wasn't a lock. It was half of a handshake.

Her pen trembled. She steadied it against the table and kept working, translating in small careful increments, checking each phrase three times against her lexicon because she had been wrong before—gloriously, publicly wrong, once, in front of the entire Guild conclave, and she would not be wrong about this.

*What is shut from without must be held from within.*

*The keeper keeps, but the kept must also keep.*

She read those lines four times. On the fifth, she understood, and understanding felt like the floor of the world quietly being removed beneath her chair.

Elodie's grandmother hadn't vanished through the gate. She had *gone to hold it*. From the inside. All these years. And whatever waited beyond the Ember Gate—the lamplit streets, the whole and hungry country—they had described in the letters, the home that wanted Elodie back—it was not merely waiting. It was *holding up its end*. A hundred years of a dead nation pressing its palms against the far side of a door so that the peace above it wouldn't collapse.

And if Elodie opened it—

Wren's mouth went dry. She reached for her tea, found it cold, drank it anyway.

*Whoever opens the door from within cannot return.*

There it was. Plain as a toll notice. No poetry, no ambiguity, none of prophecy's usual coy hedging. Whoever opened the gate from the inside—from Vael's side—would walk out into the world and never walk back. The door would take their return as payment. Or perhaps it was simpler than that, crueler: perhaps a person who crossed out of an unwritten country became the last thread holding it together, and a thread, once pulled out, could not be pushed back in.

Either way, the meaning was identical. If Elodie went through—if he answered the summons, if he gave Vael what it asked—he would not come home again. Not to Thornfell. Not to Cass's terrible stew and Pip's paper boats and the small warm circle of lamplight they had become without anyone agreeing to it.

He would trade one exile for another, forever.

Wren set down her pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations.

* * *

"You've got your shouting face on," Cass said, dropping onto the bench across from her. "I can tell from here. You've got the same look you had in Bellharrow when you found the census records, right before you cried into the notebook."

"I do not cry into my notebooks."

"You cried into two notebooks. Simultaneously. It was impressive, actually, the way you managed the pages."

"Cass." Wren lowered her voice and glanced toward Elodie, who was still apart, still turning the button. "I've finished the last page."

Cass went still in the particular way she did when cargo turned out to be more dangerous than declared. Her eyes flicked to Elodie, then back. "And?"

"And I need to think about how to say it."

"That's never once worked for you. You have the subtlety of a dropped anvil. Remember the innkeeper in Harrow's Ford? 'Madam, your establishment is built on the ruins of a massacre'? He threw a chair."

"The chair was thrown *at* me, which proves nothing except his poor character."

"Wren." Cass leaned forward, and beneath the smuggler's flippancy her voice went quiet and serious, which was rarer and more alarming than any threat. "Whatever it says, he has to hear it. He's walking into that gate tomorrow. If there's something on the last page that changes what he's walking into, and he finds out after—that you knew, and let him walk in sweet and ignorant—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

Wren looked down at the book. The final lines sat there in her careful transcription, ink still shining wet at the edges, patient as a grave marker.

"I know," she said. "That's why my hands won't stop shaking. It's easier to love the truth when it costs nothing. I've built a whole career on truths that cost other people their comfort. This one's mine to carry for five minutes, and already I'd give anything to put it down."

Cass studied her for a long moment. Then she stood, walked over to where Elodie sat, and said, without ceremony, "Wren's finished the translation. She needs to tell you something, and you're going to want to sit down, and she's going to talk too fast, and you're going to want to interrupt, and you're going to let her finish anyway. House rules."

* * *

They gathered around the lantern—Cass standing with her arms folded, Pip curled sleepily against Ilsa on the far bench, both children pretending with great dedication not to listen, which meant they were listening to every word.

Wren laid the transcription flat and put her finger on the first line, because if she didn't start immediately she never would.

"The gate opens both ways," she said. "It always has. The sealing wasn't a locking—it was a holding. Your grandmother didn't disappear through the gate, Elodie. She took up her post. From the other side. She's been holding the door shut from within for a hundred years, the way you've held it from without. Two hands, pressed together through the wood."

Elodie's face did something complicated. Hope arrived first—she watched it come, watched him reach for it—and she hated herself for what she had to do next.

"There's more."

His hand stilled on the button. "Tell me."

"*Whoever opens the door from within cannot return.*" Wren read it straight off the page, because she had learned long ago that the worst sentences were best delivered verbatim, without the mercy of paraphrase. "If you go through, Elodie—if you answer it, if you open yourself to it, if you become the citizen it needs—you don't come back. Not tomorrow. Not ever. The prophecy doesn't say Vael wins. It doesn't say Vael returns. It never promised restoration at all. Do you understand? It only ever promised a *choice*. Someone will choose. That's the whole of it. A hundred years of prophecy, and the grand conclusion is that somebody, someday, will be asked to decide."

The silence afterward had weight, like snowfall.

Pip's paper boat lay unfinished in her hands. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"So," Cass said at last, into the quiet, with the grim brightness of a woman hauling a heavy load uphill by joking at it, "the mysterious ancient prophecy, the one that's been dragging us across half the mountains, through burning towns and past polite men with warrants—the big secret ending is *someone has to make a hard decision*? I could have written that. I write that every time I price a shipment."

"It's worse than that," Wren said softly. "Don't you see? Every version of this ends in loss. If Elodie stays away, the gate fails and Vael floods back unchosen—unwriting the peace, erasing thousands of lives that were built on Vael's absence. If the Order has its way, Vael is burned out entirely, and everyone we met in Bellharrow, everyone like Sable, finishes fading—erasure answering erasure. And if Elodie goes through..." She looked at him. "Then he saves the world from both of those, and pays with ever coming home. There is no version where nobody grieves. The prophecy isn't a promise, Cass. It's an invoice."

"Aye," Cass said quietly. "Those are usually accurate."

Elodie hadn't spoken. He sat with the button in his palm, and Wren watched him do the thing she had seen him do at the gate, at Bellharrow, at every terrible threshold since: gather himself around the grief instead of fleeing it. When he finally looked up, his eyes were wet, but his voice was level.

"My grandmother's letters said the keeper's line was meant to end with a choice, not an oath." He turned the button over once more, then closed his fist around it. "I thought that meant I'd get to choose whether to go. I didn't understand it also meant choosing what it *costs*." He exhaled slowly. "Thank you, Wren. For finishing it. For telling me straight, even though—" he gestured vaguely at her hands, which were, embarrassingly, still trembling, "—even though it clearly cost you."

"Truth should cost the teller something," Wren said. "Otherwise it's just gossip."

That startled a small, cracked laugh out of him, and Pip lifted her head at the sound, and for one moment the camp was almost warm again.

But Wren couldn't leave it there. She was a scholar; she was constitutionally incapable of leaving things there. She turned back to the final page, to the last lines she had translated, the ones she had saved—not hidden, she told herself, just *saved*—and her breath caught, because under the lantern light, fresh ink had begun to rise on the old vellum. New text, seeping up through a century of blankness like water through chalk, in a hand she recognized from the letters.

The grandmother's hand.

"Oh," Wren whispered. "Oh, that's not possible."

"What?" Cass's hand went to her knife on principle. "What's not possible?"

Wren slid the book to the center of the table and turned it so they could all see. The new lines gleamed wet and black, still forming, letter by letter, as though someone on the far side of a very long distance were writing through the page:

*A choice made alone is a door half-opened. Come to the gate, all of you. What my grandson decides, he must not decide unheld.*

Below it, smaller, almost shy, as if the writer knew exactly how impossible it was:

*I can see your fire. Bring the child. She sings my lullaby slightly flat, and I should like to correct her before the end.*

Pip sat bolt upright, wide awake, her paper boat forgotten. "That's her," she breathed. "That's the voice from my dream. The blue door lady. She says my singing is *flat*?"

Cass looked at Wren. Wren looked at Elodie. Elodie stood, slowly, the button clenched in his fist, staring at the page where his grandmother—who had held a door for a hundred years, who could not return, who was watching them still—had just invited them all to the end of the world.

"She knows we're coming," he said quietly. "She's known all along."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the dark valley, faint and patient, the mountains hummed like a struck bell.

"We leave at first light," Elodie said.
