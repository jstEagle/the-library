## 23. Cass Runs

Cass Marrow had one rule, and she'd kept it for twenty years without a single exception: never promise anyone anything.

Not to clients, not to friends, not to the mule she'd owned for eleven years and loved more than most people. Promises were debts, and debts were chains, and chains were how smugglers ended up dead in ditches or worse—honest. She'd watched good people ruin themselves with a single word given at the wrong moment. *I'll come back. I'll pay you. I'll take care of you.* Every one of those words was a hook, and hooks caught fish, and fish flopped around on decks gasping until someone gutted them.

So when Elodie Coldharbour stood in front of her three nights ago, hands shaking, saying he didn't know what he was anymore, and Wren Ashvale wept into her fourth cup of wine about archives that burned themselves, and Pip asked—for the hundredth time—if they were going home soon, Cass had said nothing. Nothing at all. She'd topped up their cups, banked the fire, and gone to sleep with her coin purse under her head like always.

That was the whole trick of it. You could help people. You could shelter them, feed them, lie to Order inquisitors on their behalf, hide them in your cellar among crates of untaxed brandy. All of that was fine. None of it cost you anything permanent. But the moment you promised—*I will not leave you*—you'd signed something no court would ever enforce and no god would ever forgive you for breaking.

She lay awake that night doing arithmetic.

The arithmetic went like this: the Order of the Blank Page had agents in Thornfell now, polite men with soft voices and hard ledgers, asking after a gatekeeper. The gatekeeper was sleeping under her roof. Every day he stayed, the odds of her cellar being searched went up. Every day Pip stayed, the odds of Pip being taken went up faster, because the Order had developed an unsettling interest in children who remembered things they shouldn't. And every day Wren stayed, the odds of all of them dying together in some dramatic last stand over a prophecy book went up fastest of all, because historians did not know how to run. Historians only knew how to stay and be right about it.

The arithmetic came out bad. It came out bad every night this week, and every night she'd rolled over and told herself tomorrow, tomorrow she'd figure it out.

But tonight, lying there with her purse under her pillow and the mountain wind rattling the shutters, Cass finally admitted what she'd been avoiding: there was no figuring it out. There was only staying or going. And going was the thing she knew how to do. Going was the one skill she'd mastered so completely that she could do it asleep, drunk, wounded, or heartbroken. Going was safe.

Staying was a promise. Staying was a word she'd have to say out loud eventually, and then she'd be hooked.

* * *

She got up two hours before dawn, which was not unusual; smugglers kept the hours of owls and thieves. What was unusual was that she got up quietly, dressed by feel in the dark, and did not put coffee on.

That last part hurt. She loved her coffee. She loved the ritual of it—the little copper pot, the way the smell filled the whole house and woke Pip like a bell tolling. Skipping it felt like a small betrayal of herself, and she noticed that, and she noticed herself noticing, and she pushed both observations down deep where she kept everything else she didn't want to look at.

She counted out her coin. Not all of it. That was the compromise she made with herself somewhere between the third stair and the fifth: she'd leave half. Half the Vaeli coins, the strange ones stamped with the crest nobody could read anymore, those she'd leave in the flour jar where Elodie would find them when he went to bake bread, because he baked bread when he was frightened, everyone knew that, he'd been baking bread since the night the frost bloomed inward and the gate started humming. Bread was his version of running. He just couldn't leave the kitchen while he did it.

Half the coin. That wasn't abandonment, she told herself. That was logistics. A person couldn't run without money, and Elodie had never run anywhere in his life, and Wren hadn't either, and Pip—

Pip.

Her hand stopped on the seventh stair.

It stopped the way it always stopped lately, without her permission, as if some part of her had already decided things her mouth hadn't agreed to yet. She stood there in the dark hallway, one foot on the landing, listening to the house breathe. Pip slept in the small room at the back, the one with the crooked window that whistled in high winds. Elodie had fixed the window frame twice and it still whistled, and Pip said the whistle sounded like her mother singing, and nobody had the heart to fix it properly after that.

Cass had found Pip four years ago on the low road out of Marsh Hollow, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke, holding a wooden bird with one wing. No note. No name. Just the bird and a child who looked at Cass like she'd been waiting for her specifically, which should have been alarming and instead had felt, in a way Cass still refused to examine too closely, like being recognized.

She'd never promised to keep her. She'd simply never left. Twenty years of never promising, and here was the loophole she'd built without knowing it: you don't need promises if you just don't go.

Except tonight she was going.

She stood on the stairs and did the arithmetic again, but this time she included the numbers she usually left out. The number of times Pip had laughed this month, which was more than the previous six months combined. The number of times Elodie had said something dry and funny without noticing he'd done it, which used to be zero and was now roughly daily. The number of times Wren had looked up from her books and actually seen the people in the room instead of the past in the pages, which was fewer than the other two but growing.

Those weren't numbers that fit in any ledger she'd ever kept. They didn't convert to coin. They didn't weigh anything. But they were accumulating at a rate that scared her more than the Order did, because the Order could only kill her, and this—whatever this was—could change her.

She went down the rest of the stairs anyway. She was Cass Marrow. She'd made her decision on the fifth step and she stuck to decisions; that was the other rule, the one under the first one. Rules were how you survived alone, and she had been alone, successfully, for twenty years, and success was its own argument.

* * *

Outside, Thornfell was blue-dark and cold enough to bite. The town clung to the mountainside like lichen on a rock, all crooked roofs and smoking chimneys, and above it the Thornfell peaks stood against the last stars, and somewhere up there, invisible from here but present in her mind the way a toothache is present, the Ember Gate hummed its low heartbeat hum.

She'd heard it once. Up close, three weeks ago, when she'd led them all up through the switchbacks on the pretense of checking her old smuggling caches and really just wanting to see whether Elodie would flinch near the door. He hadn't flinched. He'd stood in front of it like a man greeting an old dog, and the door had hummed, and frost had crawled toward his boots like it wanted to hold his hand, and Cass had stood ten feet back thinking: *this is the strangest cargo I've ever guarded, and I once moved a caged chimera across three borders.*

She crossed the yard to the stable where Juniper waited, her mule, gray-muzzled and judgmental. Juniper took one look at the packed saddlebags and pinned her ears.

"Don't," Cass told her. "Don't start."

Juniper started. She sidled, she sighed theatrically, she stepped on Cass's foot with the precision of a creature who had been stepping on that exact foot for eleven years and knew precisely how much pressure constituted a complaint versus an injury. Cass loaded her anyway, muttering, and gave her the dried apple she kept bribes in, and Juniper ate it and continued complaining, because principles mattered even when apples were involved.

They went out the back way, the smugglers' way, the path that dropped off the ridge behind the tannery and joined the low road a mile south of town where nobody respectable ever looked. Cass knew forty ways out of Thornfell. This was the fastest and the ugliest, which suited her fine. Ugly roads were honest roads. Beautiful roads had tolls.

The low road ran along the river, black water sliding fast over stones, and Cass walked it with her hood up and her breath steaming and her mind, despite everything, remarkably quiet. This was the part she was good at. The leaving itself. The clean mechanics of it: saddlebags balanced, route chosen, alibi pre-built (*gone to Vellmark for the brandy order, back in two weeks, tell no one, especially tell no one with a ledger*). She'd left towns before. She'd left lovers, once or twice, though she'd deny it under torture. She'd left a whole life in the capital when she was nineteen, and she'd never once gone back to check what remained.

Leaving was easy. Leaving was the one muscle that never atrophied.

So why did her chest feel like this? Like someone had packed it with wet wool?

She blamed the altitude. She blamed the coffee she hadn't drunk. She blamed Juniper, whose reproachful ears were somehow audible from thirty feet behind. She blamed everything except the obvious candidate, because the obvious candidate was a child asleep in a crooked-windowed room, humming a song extinct for a hundred years, dreaming nightly of a blue door, waking with dirt under her fingernails from a garden that existed nowhere on any map Cass had ever smuggled anything across.

Two miles down the low road, at the bend where the river widened and the willows started, Cass stopped.

She didn't decide to stop. Her feet decided, the same way her hand had stopped on the seventh stair, the same way parts of her kept making decisions ahead of schedule and informing her later. She stood at the bend and looked back the way she'd come, at the thread of road unspooling behind her toward Thornfell, and she saw nothing—no pursuit, no lanterns, no Order riders—and that nothing was supposed to feel like freedom.

It felt like a door closing.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out somewhere in the last mile, walking, letting the rhythm of it shake the truth loose the way walking always did: she wasn't running from the Order. The Order was a problem, and problems were her native weather. She'd outrun tax collectors, border wardens, a husband, a war, and one genuinely memorable avalanche. The Order was just another item on a long list.

She was running from the promise. From the moment when staying would stop being a habit and become a word. Because habits could break quietly, without witnesses, but words—words were different. If she stayed much longer, someday soon someone was going to ask her, straight out, *will you stay*, and she was going to have to answer, and every possible answer terrified her. Yes meant chains. No meant becoming the kind of person who says no to a child with dirt under her nails, and Cass had met that kind of person, and she'd spent her whole life making sure she wasn't one.

Running now, before anyone asked—that was the coward's elegant solution. Leave while the question was still hypothetical. Keep the rule intact. Never promise, never break, never be either the chained woman or the cruel one. Stay perfectly, immaculately free.

Free. She turned the word over. Twenty years she'd polished it like a coin. And standing at the river bend in the dark, she finally asked the question she'd been dodging since the seventh stair:

Free for what?

* * *

It was somewhere in the willows, fumbling at her coat pocket for the map case, that she found it.

Pip's drawing.

Cass didn't know when the child had put it there. That was the unnerving part—Pip had a way of distributing objects through Cass's belongings like a benevolent poltergeist: a feather in her boot, a smooth stone in her saddlebag, a button in the flour jar. Cass had assumed it was play. Standing in the dark with the paper in her gloved fingers, she understood it might have been something else entirely. Might have been a girl quietly, patiently, in the only language she had, answering a question Cass hadn't asked yet.

The drawing was charcoal on the back of a piece of Wren's scrap paper, the kind with failed translations on the reverse. It showed five figures around a campfire. Cass recognized the scene instantly—it was the fireside night from the north road, the first one, the awkward warm one, the night Wren had burned the stew and Elodie had eaten it anyway out of politeness and then lied about liking it and Wren had caught him lying and he'd confessed that he'd never learned to taste food honestly because he'd eaten alone for thirty years, and everyone had laughed, even him, especially him, and Cass had sat slightly outside the circle of light the way she always did and pretended not to be part of anything.

In the drawing, she was in the circle.

Charcoal Cass, sitting between Juniper and the fire, laughing—actually laughing, mouth open, the way she almost never let anyone see—and Pip had drawn the laughter coming out of her in little curling lines, like music, like smoke, like the lullaby.

And underneath, in Pip's careful, wobbling letters, the ones she was still learning, pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn it, a caption. Pip labeled everything; Wren said it was a historian's instinct arriving early, and Wren said it with tears in her eyes, because everything about Pip made Wren cry these days.

The caption said: **THE NIGHT NOBODY LEFT.**

Cass stood in the willows and read it eleven times.

The night nobody left. Past tense. As if it had already happened. As if Pip had drawn not the memory of a night but the record of a fact—a fact established, witnessed, entered into evidence. Children did that, Cass knew. Children wrote history in crayon and charcoal and believed it into being. Pip had decided, at some point, with the terrifying authority of a seven-year-old, that the company around that fire was a fixed thing, a settled matter, a thing that had already survived its own ending.

And Cass had snuck out in the dark like a thief to prove the child wrong.

She thought about the flour jar. About the half-coin she'd left, the tidy compromise, the logistics-not-abandonment. She thought about how Elodie would find it and bake bread, and how Wren would find it and write a footnote about it, and how Pip would find it and understand exactly what it meant, because children always understood exactly what things meant, that was their curse and their genius. Pip would understand that the woman in the drawing, the laughing one inside the circle, had read her own caption and disagreed.

Cass Marrow had been many things. Smuggler, liar, runner, coward in at least three flavors. But she had never once, in twenty years of immaculate non-promising, made a child wrong about something that mattered.

She wasn't going to start at a river bend at four in the morning.

"Turn around, Juniper," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, which surprised her more than the drawing had. "We're going back."

Juniper turned around with the smugness of a mule who had known from the first pinned ear exactly how this night would end. Mules, Cass reflected, were better historians than historians. They remembered everything and forgave nothing and they never pretended otherwise.

* * *

She came back up the ugly road in the gray hour before dawn, moving fast, hating every step and loving every step, the two feelings sharing her body like passengers who refused to acknowledge each other. By the time Thornfell's roofs rose out of the river mist, the sky had gone the color of dishwater and the first chimney smoke was standing up straight in the still air.

She came in the back way, unsaddled Juniper, fed her the second dried apple as damages, and stood in the yard for a long moment looking at her own dark windows.

Then she went inside, and she put the coffee on.

The smell climbed the stairs like a rescuer. Within minutes, footsteps overhead—small bare feet first, thumping down with the urgency of the deeply caffeinated-by-proxy, then heavier feet, then the slow deliberate tread of a historian who had clearly been awake all night again, because Wren's eyes at dawn had the bloodshot shine of a woman who'd fought a book and lost.

Pip arrived in the kitchen first, hair like a startled owl, wearing yesterday's dress inside out, and went straight to the stove and stood next to Cass the way she always did, close enough to touch, not touching, watching the copper pot with the patience of a scientist awaiting results.

"You came back," Pip said.

It wasn't a question. That was the thing about Pip—she stated outcomes rather than asking about intentions. *You came back.* Flat, certain, already filed away in whatever inner archive the child kept, the one with the blue door in it.

"I went out," Cass said. "To check the road."

"The road was fine," Pip said. "You checked it last week."

"The road changes."

"So do you," Pip said, and reached up and put the wooden bird with one wing on the table beside the salt, which was Pip's way of saying something serious without saying it, and Cass looked at the bird and understood that the child had known. Had known last night, probably. Had known when she slipped the drawing into the pocket. Children always know. That was the whole horror and glory of them.

Elodie came down next, flour already dusting his forearms because apparently he'd started the bread before the coffee smell reached him, which meant he'd woken frightened and gone to the kitchen without breaking stride, and Cass looked at him—at this hollow-eyed keeper who had inherited a country's worth of grief and carried it like a sack of grain, politely, without complaint—and she felt the promise forming in her throat like a stone working loose.

She said nothing. Not yet. She wasn't ready. The rule was cracking but it wasn't broken, and a cracked rule, she'd learned, could hold a surprising amount if you didn't lean on it.

Wren arrived last, carrying the prophecy book like a wounded animal, and announced that she'd translated another fragment during the night and it was worse than the last one, and everyone groaned, and Cass poured her wine instead of coffee because it was technically still night somewhere, and Wren accepted this reasoning with scholarly grace.

And then they were all four around the table, plus the bird, plus the bread rising under its cloth, plus the coffee going around, and the light came up slow and gray-gold through the crooked window, and the whistle in the frame sang its thin note, and Pip said it sounded like her mother, and nobody fixed the window, and nobody left.

Later, when the others drifted to their tasks—Wren to her fragments, Elodie to his loaves, Pip to terrorizing Juniper—Cass sat alone at the table with the drawing spread out in front of her, smoothing the creases flat with her thumb, reading the caption one more time.

THE NIGHT NOBODY LEFT.

She got out her ledger—the real one, the coin ledger, the one with twenty years of entries in her tight crabbed hand—and on the first blank page, in the same tight crabbed hand, she wrote a single line. Not a promise. She wouldn't call it that. A line. An entry. Ledger-keepers didn't make promises; they recorded facts.

She wrote: *Stayed.*

And under it, because the pen kept going the way pens do when the hand behind them has finally stopped arguing: *Reasons pending.*

She was still staring at the word when the knock came at the front door—three soft raps, courteous, unhurried, the knock of someone who had never in their life needed to hurry—and through the shutter slats she saw, standing in the mist on her doorstep, two figures in travel-gray cloaks, and one of them held a ledger of his own.

"Cass," Elodie called quietly from the kitchen, flour to his elbows, voice steady as a hanging scale. "Someone's at the door."

"I see it," Cass said, and put her hand over the word *Stayed*, and reached for the crowbar she kept behind the salt barrel, and hoped—really hoped, for the first time in twenty years hoping for something—that the polite men outside had come about brandy.
