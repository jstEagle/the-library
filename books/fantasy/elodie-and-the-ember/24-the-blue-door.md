## 24. The Blue Door

Pip had been dreaming the house for eleven nights, and by now she knew it better than her own boots.

She knew the gate at the bottom of the garden leaned to the left because its hinge was rusted on one side only. She knew there was a stone shaped like a sleeping cat by the well, and that if you stepped on it in a certain way it went *clonk*. She knew the kitchen had a crack in the plaster shaped like a river, and that if you traced it with your finger from mountain to sea it took exactly as long as one verse of the lullaby.

She had never seen any of these things awake. But dreams, Pip had decided, were just remembering with your eyes shut, and she was very good at both.

"You're doing the face," Cass said.

They were walking single file up a valley that Wren's maps said did not exist and Elodie's face said should not exist and Cass's feet said *fine, whatever, feet have opinions too*. The morning was thin and silver. Somewhere above them a bird sang four notes and then, as if embarrassed, stopped.

"What face?" said Pip.

"The face you make when you're about to be right about something and everyone else is about to be wrong."

"I don't make a face."

"You absolutely make a face. It's your worst quality."

"It's my best quality," said Pip. "Wren says it's called *certainty* and it's what historians are made of."

"Wren also says sleep is for people without footnotes," Cass muttered, but she took Pip's hand anyway, which was how Cass said sorry without ever once saying sorry.

Elodie came back down the line to walk beside them. He had been quiet all morning — quieter than his usual quiet, which was already a famously deep quiet, like a well with a coin at the bottom. His eyes kept going to the mountains on their left, then away again, as if the mountains were a conversation he wasn't sure he'd been invited to.

"Pip," he said carefully. "Tell me about the door again."

"Blue," said Pip. "The blue of the sky when it's about to rain but hasn't decided yet. And there's a knocker shaped like an ember, but it's cold. And if you knock, nobody answers, but somebody's *glad* you knocked. That part's hard to explain."

"No," Elodie said softly. "That part's not hard to explain at all."

Wren caught up from the rear, where she had been walking backwards for a quarter mile taking notes on a road that kept trying to be older than it was. Her spectacles were fogged. She pushed them up and squinted ahead.

"The valley narrows past that ridge," she said. "According to every map I own, we should hit cliff face in half a mile. According to the road under our feet—" she stamped it, and the road gave a faint, satisfied thud, like a drum remembering a parade— "we won't."

"Roads don't lie," said Elodie.

"No," said Wren. "But they've started *remembering*, and I confess I don't know which is stranger."

Pip tugged Cass's hand. "It's past the ridge. Past the ridge and around the shoulder of the hill, and then you can't see it until you're nearly on top of it, because the hill keeps it like a secret." She frowned, surprised at herself. "Like Mama used to keep sweets behind the flour tin."

Cass stopped walking.

Everyone stopped, because when Cass stopped, the whole company stopped; she was rather like a cart horse that way. She looked down at Pip with an expression Pip had never seen on her before — something between fear and wonder, folded together like a letter.

"You've never told me about a mama," Cass said.

"You never asked," said Pip, who was seven and therefore honest the way weather is honest.

*

Past the ridge, the road did a thing that roads were not supposed to do: it bent *around* the hill instead of over it, sliding through a fold in the rock so narrow and so ordinary-looking that Cass walked straight past the entrance twice and had to be pulled back by the sleeve.

"In here," said Pip.

"That's a goat track."

"It's a road wearing a goat-track coat," said Wren, delighted, crouching to brush moss off a kerbstone. "Look — cut stone. Vaeli workmanship. It's been hiding under the moss the way the whole country has been hiding under everything." She looked up at Elodie, and her voice went careful. "Keeper. Is this—"

"I don't know," Elodie said. But he said it the way people say *I don't know* when they know perfectly well, and his hand had gone to the iron key at his throat, the one he'd worn since he was nine years old, turning it over and over like a worry-bead.

The fold swallowed them. For twenty paces there was rock overhead and dimness and the smell of cold stone, and then—

Then the world opened like a book.

A valley no bigger than a churchyard lay cupped in the mountains, green as a held breath. A stream ran through it, chattering. An orchard stood in rows, ancient and gnarled and — this was the part that made Wren sit down abruptly on a wall — *in blossom*. In late autumn, at altitude, in a fold of rock that no sun should reach properly: apple blossom, drifting down like slow snow.

And at the head of the valley, a farmhouse.

It was ruined. Of course it was ruined; a hundred years is a hundred years. Part of the roof had fallen in, and ivy had taken the eastern wall hostage, and one shutter hung by a single hinge, swinging gently in a wind that touched nothing else. The garden was a wilderness gone to seed, brambles over beds, a well with its bucket long rotted away.

But the door was blue.

Not faded blue. Not remembered blue. Blue as the sky when it couldn't decide about rain, bright and certain, as though someone had painted it yesterday and gone inside to put the kettle on.

Nobody spoke. Even the stream seemed to lower its voice.

Pip let go of Cass's hand and walked forward. Behind her she heard Cass start to say her name and then stop, because some things you cannot call a child back from, and this was one of them.

The stone by the well was shaped like a sleeping cat. Pip stepped on it exactly right.

*Clonk.*

She heard Elodie make a small sound behind her, like a man who has been punched very gently in the heart.

The gate at the bottom of the garden leaned left. Pip went through it. Brambles caught at her skirt and she forgave them, because in the dream they'd been roses, and maybe they would be again someday. The path to the door was three flagstones wide. The middle one rocked. She knew it would rock. She hopped it anyway, out of respect.

Then she stood in front of the blue door, and reached up — it was higher than she expected, doors in dreams are always the right height and real ones are rude about it — and laid her palm flat against the wood.

Warm. Just slightly warm, the way a chair is warm when someone has only just left it.

"Pip," Cass said from the gate, and her smuggler's voice had gone rough as sacking. "Sweetheart. Wait."

"It's all right," Pip said. "Somebody's glad we knocked."

She lifted the knocker — an ember shape, cold iron — and knocked three times, the way the lullaby's rhythm goes.

The sound didn't echo. Sound in a valley that size should have echoed. Instead the knock simply *arrived*, everywhere at once, soft as a hand laid on a shoulder, and the whole valley seemed to lean in.

Inside the house, dust stirred.

Pip heard it — a whisper of it, a hundred years of settled grey lifting all at once, like the house inhaling after holding its breath since before anyone she knew was born. The hanging shutter stilled. The stream ran backwards for exactly one heartbeat, which Wren would later insist upon writing down despite nobody asking her to.

And the door opened.

Not unlocked. Not pushed. It opened inward by the width of two fingers, the way a door opens when someone inside has unlatched it and stepped back to let you in, and through the gap came the smell of bread that had never been baked and woodsmoke that had never burned and, underneath it all, faint as memory, the first bar of the lullaby.

Pip looked back. Cass had both hands pressed over her mouth. Wren was crying without any dignity whatsoever, which Pip privately thought was the correct response. Elodie stood at the gate with the key at his throat and an expression on his face that Pip had no name for yet, though she would spend the rest of her life learning it: the look of a man coming home to a wound.

"Come on, then," Pip said, in the tone she used for people being silly about puddles. "It's rude to keep it waiting. It's waited *ages*."

*

Inside, the dust lay over everything like grey snow, except where it didn't.

That was the thing none of them could stop noticing, Wren least of all. The dust covered the floor, the broken chairs, the fallen beams. But the kitchen table — a big scarred oak table with one leg mended with a different wood, visible even under its shroud of grey — was set for four.

Four bowls. Four spoons. Four cups, and a jug, and a loaf-board with a knife beside it, and a fifth place at the end that was empty, and had perhaps always been empty, or perhaps was waiting, and nobody wanted to say which.

The dust on the table had settled *around* the dishes. Not on them. As if the table had been set last week and the century had happened politely elsewhere.

"A hundred years," Wren whispered, tracing the air above a bowl without touching it. "This house fell out of time. Or time fell out of this house. There are three accounts of Vaeli hearth-wards in the fragments — household protections, meant to hold a home *true* while its people were away. Nobody believed they worked." Her laugh cracked in the middle. "Nobody believed in Vael at all, that was rather the problem."

Elodie hadn't come past the doorway. He stood on the threshold with one hand on the blue frame, and Pip watched him look at the kitchen — at the crack in the plaster shaped like a river, mountain to sea — and watched him trace it with his eyes, slowly, and his lips moved, and she knew, she *knew*, he was timing it against the lullaby.

"There's a loose stone," he said at last, and his voice came out strange, young, a boy's voice wearing a man's. "Third from the hearth. Grandmother kept—" He stopped. Started again. "Someone kept dried apples under it. For children who came to the door hungry. You were supposed to take some and leave the rest for the next child." He looked at the floor as if he could see through a hundred years of dust and rot and time itself. "I don't remember living here. I *can't* remember living here. I was born in Thornfell." His hand closed around the key. "But I know where the apples are."

"Memory doesn't care whose it is," Wren said gently. "It cares where it's needed."

Cass had gone very still near the window, looking out at the orchard. When she spoke, it was to Pip, quietly, the way she asked questions she actually cared about the answer to. "The mama. In your dream. The one with the sweets behind the flour tin." She turned. "Was she here? In this house?"

Pip thought about it honestly, which took a moment.

"She was in the garden," she said. "Digging. She had dirt on her hands and she was singing the song wrong on purpose to make me laugh." Pip frowned. "Except I don't think she was my mama. I think she was everybody's mama. I think she was the house's mama." She looked up at Elodie. "I think she might be yours."

Something moved in Elodie's face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone.

He crossed the kitchen at last — stepping over the threshold like a man wading into cold water — and knelt by the hearth, and found the third stone, and it was loose, because of course it was, because the house had kept it loose for a hundred years against exactly this moment. Underneath: a hollow, and in the hollow, impossibly, a cloth bundle, dry as paper, tied with string.

He untied it with hands that shook. Dried apples, brown and wrinkled and old as sin. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of letters, bound in ribbon gone the colour of weak tea, and on the topmost letter, in a firm looping hand:

*For the boy who stayed.*

Elodie read those five words four times. Pip counted.

"Cass," he said finally, and his voice was steady as a hanging scale, which is how Pip knew it was the opposite of steady. "There's a light coming up the valley road."

And there was. Far off, down through the fold in the mountain, bobbing among the apple blossom: lanterns. Many lanterns. Moving fast, and in step, and altogether too orderly for anything in this story that had ever meant them kindness.

Cass was at the window in three strides, crowbar already out from behind her — she carried it everywhere now, ever since the polite men, and had begun referring to it as *the argument-settler*.

"How many?"

"Twelve," said Pip, who had excellent eyes and worse news. "Maybe thirteen. One of them's got a blank white banner."

Wren made a sound like a kettle giving up.

Elodie rose slowly, letters clutched to his chest, and looked around the ruined kitchen — at the table set for four, at the empty fifth place, at the blue door standing open behind them onto a valley that had waited a hundred years to be found.

"They can't burn this one," he said quietly. "I won't let them. Whatever happens next—" he looked down at the ribbon-bound letters, and his thumb moved over the ink, *for the boy who stayed*, once, like a blessing— "somebody stayed. Somebody left these for me. So this house gets to finish what it started."

Outside, the first lantern cleared the ridge, and the light it threw was very white, and very clean, and clean the way a page is clean before the words are wiped away.

"Right," said Cass, rolling her shoulders. "Everyone hide behind the historian. She's the only one of us who's ever been *paid* to argue."
