## 26. The Order's Offer Again

He came at dusk, the way honest men come to collect debts and dishonest ones to forgive them.

We had made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning cairn—Cass called it a smuggler's rest, which meant it was a place where you could see people coming long before they could see you, and where the firelight didn't reach the road. Wren had her notebooks spread in a half-moon around her like a scholar's version of a fortress wall. Pip was asleep against Cass's side, one hand still curled around a stick she'd been drawing in the dirt with. And I was on watch, because I had been on watch every night since Bellharrow burned, and because sleep had become a country I kept trying to cross and getting turned back at the border.

I saw him before Cass did. That was new. A month ago I'd have been the last to notice anything; now my eyes had learned the roads the way my grandmother's hands had learned the gate.

"Inquisitor," I said quietly. "On the ridge."

Cass was awake between one breath and the next, which is a smuggler's trick I've never managed to learn despite watching her do it forty times. She moved Pip behind her without seeming to move at all. Wren snatched up her notebooks with both arms, hugging them like a woman saving children from a flood.

The man came down the slope slowly, openly, his hands visible at his sides. No escort. No lantern. He stopped at the edge of our firelight as though it were a border of its own, and waited to be invited in.

"You're braver than your colleagues," Cass said. "Or stupider. In Thornfell we don't distinguish."

"Both, probably." His voice was the same as I remembered from that terrible afternoon in the tavern back room: gentle, unhurried, the voice of a man who has never once needed to raise it. "May I sit? I'll keep my hands where you can see them. I'm not armed. The Order doesn't arm its inquisitors."

"That's either very noble or very arrogant," Wren said.

"It's arithmetic," he said. "A sword stops one man. A warrant stops a hundred. And tonight I'm not using the warrant."

"Then what are you using?" I asked.

He looked at me then, and I saw what I hadn't let myself see the first time: he was tired. Not the theatrical weariness of a man performing virtue, but the deep, ground-down tiredness of someone who has carried something heavy for a very long way. There was dust on his grey coat. There was a bandage on his left hand, badly wrapped, the work of someone who had dressed it himself by firelight.

"Bellharrow," I said. "You were there."

"I was there." He said it simply. No defense. "I asked for the town to be catalogued instead of burned. My request was denied. I resigned my commission this morning." He held up the bandaged hand. "They took my seal. It cost a little skin. Less than it cost Bellharrow."

Wren made a small sound, halfway between a sob and a hiss. "You stood in that square. You watched them—"

"I watched them unmake four hundred people who only wanted to be half-alive somewhere quiet." His voice didn't waver, but something under it did, the way ice does before it goes. "Yes. I watched. And I have spent every night since doing sums, Mistress Ashvale, because sums are what I'm for. May I sit? What I have to show you works better sitting down."

Cass looked at me. I looked at Pip, asleep, breathing the small even breaths of a child who trusted the world enough to leave it for a while.

"Sit," I said. "But you'll say what you came to say to all of us. No private words with me. Last time you tried that, I nearly took your bargain, and I've regretted the nearly ever since."

"I know," he said. "That's why I came back."

* * *

He sat cross-legged by the fire like a traveler, which I suppose he was, and he set his satchel down between us with the care of a man laying an infant in a cradle.

"My name is Aldous Vey," he said. "You never asked before. I noticed. People who intend to refuse you don't ask your name—it makes refusing easier. So I'll give it freely, and you can throw it back at me whenever you like. Aldous Vey. Thirty-one years in the Order of the Blank Page. Twenty-two of them as an inquisitor. I have personally overseen the erasure of nine towns, two monasteries, and one river."

"A river," Cass repeated flatly.

"The Sorrel. It used to run through three duchies. There was a war over it once, before the bargain. Now there's a dry valley and no one remembers why the bridges are there." He said it without pride and without shame, the way a man reads a ledger. "I tell you this so you understand I am not a good man arriving late to goodness. I am the instrument itself, come to ask whether it should keep cutting."

"Get to the offer," Cass said. "And if it's the same offer, get to the leaving too."

"It isn't the same offer." He opened the satchel. "Last time I brought you a promise. This time I brought you a bill."

He drew out pages. Not Order parchment—those pale, featureless sheets they use, the kind that seem to drink ink—but ordinary paper, cheap paper, the kind taverns wrap fish in. Page after page after page, tied in bundles with string, and each bundle soft at the edges from handling.

"What is that?" Wren whispered, though I think she already knew. Historians always know; it's their blessing and their curse.

"Names," said Aldous Vey. "The names of the dead of the peace."

He untied the first bundle and laid the pages out in the firelight, face-up, and turned them over one by one as he spoke, so that we had to look at each name whether we wanted to or not.

"When Vael was unwritten, a hundred years ago, the war ended. You know this. Wren knows this better than any living soul. But here is what the histories skip, because histories are written by the survivors and survivors are sentimental about themselves." He turned a page. "Maren Ostwick, of Harrowgate. She died in the last winter of the war, aged six, of a fever that would have been treatable if not for the blockade. Her death was part of the price. The peace was purchased partly with lives like hers—lives that ended so that the unwriting could be justified, so that the grieving would agree that anything, *anything*, was better than another year of it."

Another page. "Tomas Bell, ferryman. Drowned holding the crossing open for refugees during the retreat. Another. Ellery Vance, who signed the bargain and was erased along with everything else because a signature cannot survive when the hand that wrote it belongs to no one." Page. Page. Page. "Four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and six names, Mistress Ashvale. I have been copying them for twenty-two years. Every inquisitor before me copied some. It's the Order's penance and its prison both—we are required to know exactly what we protect."

The fire cracked. Pip stirred and Cass smoothed her hair without looking away from him.

"Why?" I asked. My voice came out strange. "Why bring them here? Why show us?"

"Because last time I offered you forgetting, and I made it sound clean, and you were right to refuse a clean-sounding thing." He looked at me directly, and there was no gentleness in it anymore, only honesty, which is harder to bear. "So this time I want the choice to be dirty. I want you to choose with the whole weight of it in your hands. Because I believe—I have believed since Bellharrow—that the Order may be wrong. And if I'm going to help end the thing I've served my whole life, I need to know that whoever ends it has counted the cost. Not heard about it. Counted it."

"And if we haven't?" Wren asked. Her voice shook. "If we can't? Four hundred thousand names, Master Vey. No one can hold that."

"No," he agreed. "No one can. That's rather the point. A country is too heavy for one person. Which is why I find myself wondering—" and here his voice did something I hadn't expected, something almost shy— "whether it might be light enough for five."

* * *

Silence, for a while. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Cass broke it, because Cass always breaks silences; it's how she tests them. "Let me make sure I have this straight. You've walked away from your Order. You've crossed the mountains alone, unarmed, with a bag full of dead people's names, to find the man you were sent to erase. And your grand proposal is—what? That we sit around the fire reading obituaries until we feel properly guilty?"

"No," said Vey. "My proposal is that you hear the other half of the arithmetic, which no one has given you yet, because the Order keeps it and Vael's remnants can't remember it." He drew out one more page, a single sheet, older than the others, brittle at the folds. "This is the casualty projection. If the Ember Gate opens fully and Vael returns—not as memory but as fact, as land, as law—the peace unwinds. The borders it drew dissolve. The treaties signed in the space Vael left become signatures on water. We estimate—conservatively—the deaths of sixty thousand people within five years. Famine, war, the collapse of three governments that exist only because Vael does not."

"Sixty thousand," Wren repeated.

"Against four hundred thousand who were already lost," Vey said. "That is the bargain the Order has protected for a century. Four hundred thousand dead, traded for however many millions lived after. I used to believe that trade was sacred. I still believe it was *made*. Those are different things, and I only learned they were different last week, in a burning square, watching a child fade out of her mother's arms." He paused. "But belief and arithmetic still have to live in the same house. Sixty thousand, gatekeeper. Some of them will have names like Maren Ostwick. Some of them will be six years old. If you open that door—and everything you've done tells me you mean to—you will be choosing those deaths. Not causing them, perhaps. Choosing them. There's a difference, and it matters, and it isn't enough."

I looked at the pages. Names in careful, cramped handwriting, thousands of them, each one written by some inquisitor's aching hand across a hundred years—a litany maintained by the very people who enforced the erasure, like a jailer keeping a portrait of everyone his prison had swallowed.

"I know about choosing," I said finally. "I've known since I was nine years old. My grandmother chose. She walked through the gate to hold it shut from inside, and she knew exactly what she was trading, because she'd read the same histories you have, Master Vey, and loved more of the people in them than you ever did." I picked up one of the bundles. It was heavier than paper should be. Everything true is heavier than it looks. "So here's what I'll give you, since you gave me your name. I won't take your forgetting. I won't take your safety. But I won't pretend your numbers aren't real, either. I've spent a month learning that pretending is just erasure with better manners."

Vey closed his eyes briefly, and I understood that this—this exact sentence—was what he had walked through the mountains hoping to hear. Not agreement. Acknowledgment.

"There's more," he said. "The part I hate most." He glanced at Pip, asleep, and lowered his voice anyway, as if the numbers could be kept from her. "The Order's standing instruction regarding the child remains. She is a remnant, born of Vael's longing, and when the gate closes—if it closes—she will likely fade with it. Weeks. Perhaps days. Unless she is anchored." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded document bearing the Blank Page's seal, and slid it across the stones to Cass. "A wardship. Under Order protection, in the capital. Schooling, physicians, a stipend, a family chosen for temperament. She would forget Vael entirely—forgetting is the anchor—and she would live. Comfortably. Long."

Cass picked up the document the way you pick up a snake you're not sure is dead.

"And if we say no?"

"Then she fades, and I will write her name in the book myself, and I will hate every letter." He said it steadily, but his bandaged hand had closed into a fist. "I told you I wanted the choice dirty. That's the dirtiest part. That's the part I'd bury if I were the man I was a month ago."

* * *

He rose to go, because he was wise enough to know that a wound needs air and an argument needs sleep, and because I think the firelight had gotten to be more than he could sit in politely.

At the edge of the dark he stopped.

"For what it's worth, Coldharbour—I read your grandmother's file. All of it. She was offered the same bargain I offered you, ninety years ago, when she was young and the Order still thought kindness was a tool it owned." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, worn thin as old paper. "She refused too. But she sent the inquisitor of her day a letter. One line. It's quoted in the training materials, and every inquisitor memorizes it, and most of us spend our careers trying to prove it wrong."

"What did it say?" I asked, though some cold, knowing part of me had already begun to guess.

"*'You count the dead,'*" he recited, "*'but you never once ask what the living are carrying. Count that sometime.'"*

He bowed—actually bowed, an old-fashioned gesture, courtly and defeated—and went up into the dark, and the mountain took him the way the mountain takes everything, quietly and without comment.

* * *

Nobody slept. Of course nobody slept.

Cass sat turning the wardship document over and over, not reading it anymore, just weighing it, the way she weighs coin she suspects is clipped. Wren had pulled one bundle of names into her lap and was reading them silently, her lips moving, tears running freely down her face and dripping onto the paper, and she didn't wipe them away because historians know that grief is also a record.

Pip woke near midnight, took in the scene with the unnerving clarity of children—who always wake directly into the middle of things—and climbed into my lap without asking, the way she does, as if I were furniture she'd personally commissioned.

"Was that the paper man again?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Did he bring the forgetting again?"

"He brought something worse this time," I said. "He brought the counting."

Pip considered this with the gravity she gives to all matters of state. Then she leaned her head against my chest and said, in the small clear voice that has undone me a dozen times since Bellharrow:

"When I fade, will it hurt?"

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through stone with a sound almost—but not quite—like music.

"No," I lied, gently, because some lies are load-bearing. "It's like falling asleep in a warm room while someone sings."

"Oh," she said. "Like the lullaby."

"Exactly like the lullaby."

She nodded, satisfied, and was asleep again within minutes, trusting me completely, which is the heaviest thing anyone has ever handed me.

Cass caught my eye across the fire. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. The question was sitting there among the embers and the names and the wardship papers, wearing all our faces at once:

*Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow we decide.*

And I found, looking at the sleeping child and the weeping historian and the smuggler who couldn't stop weighing, that I already knew what I wanted to say—and that wanting to say it terrified me more than the Order ever had.
