## 27. The Vote Around the Fire

Cass had smuggled gunpowder past three customs houses, a fugitive countess past a wedding, and once—her proudest work—a live bear past an entire regiment of men who swore afterward they'd only ever seen a very large dog. She knew how to read a camp. She could tell you from the crackle of a fire whether everyone in it was about to start shouting.

This fire was about to start shouting.

They'd made camp in the hollow beneath a shelf of grey stone, high enough that the Order's lanterns below were just another constellation, wrong-angled and patient. Wren had her notebooks out—all eleven of them now, swollen with Bellharrow's names—and she'd laid them in a fan on her bedroll like a hand of cards she intended to win with.

"Read it again," Wren said. "The last line. Out loud. I want them to hear it."

"I heard it," Cass said. "Everyone heard it. You've said it four times."

"Because it matters." Wren pushed her spectacles up her nose in the way she did when she was about to be insufferable and correct. "Whoever opens the gate from inside cannot come back. That is not decoration, Cassian. That is the load-bearing wall of the entire prophecy. A hundred years of scholars missed it because they stopped reading at the pretty part."

"There's no pretty part," Cass said. "It's a book about a door."

Elodie sat apart from the argument, as he always did, knees drawn up, hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. The firelight did something unkind to his face—showed all the places where the last weeks had worn grooves into him. He'd been quiet since Bellharrow. Quiet since the burning. Quiet in the way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish.

Pip was asleep against Cass's side, or pretending to be, which for Pip amounted to the same thing: ears open, breathing theatrical.

"So we're agreed," Wren pressed on. "Vael returns. The country comes back. The maps get rewritten, the archives reopened, every name in my notebooks gets to be a name again instead of ash. That's the outcome. Everything else is logistics."

"That's not what the last line says," Cass said.

"The last line says *someone must choose.*"

"It says someone must choose *and never come home.* Those aren't the same sentence, professor."

Wren's jaw tightened. Cass watched her do the thing she did when she was deciding whether to be honest or academic, and—for once, maybe because Bellharrow was still smoke behind them—she chose honest.

"No," Wren admitted. "They aren't."

* * *

Here was the trouble with fires, Cass thought, as she fed it another branch: they made people honest. She'd seen it a hundred times on the roads. Something about the small circle of light, the dark pressing in polite and patient, loosened tongues that should've stayed knotted. People told fires things they'd never tell each other directly, using the fire as a middleman.

Wren was telling the fire about Vael.

"You weren't there," Wren said, quieter now. "In the archives. Before I was expelled. There was a room—the deep cataloguing room—where they kept everything that had been *deaccessioned.* That's the polite word. Deaccessioned. As if a country were a chair nobody wanted anymore." She turned a page without looking at it. "Shelves and shelves of Vaeli things. Census rolls. Seed ledgers. Children's primers. A hundred years of a whole nation, boxed up in the dark because some treaty required it and everyone agreed forgetting was cheaper than grieving. And I stood in that room and I thought: this is the largest theft in history, and the stolen goods are *memories*, and the thieves all went home to dinner."

"We know," Cass said gently. "You've told us."

"And then Bellharrow burned." Wren's voice cracked down the middle, and she let it. "I interviewed forty-one remnants in that town. Forty-one people who were half-real, living on scraps of themselves, and every single one of them thanked me for writing their names down. Do you understand? They *thanked* me. Mrs. Aldery of Bellharrow, who wasn't sure she'd ever had a first name, held my hand and said, *at least somebody knows I was here.* And then the Order came with their lamps and their blankness, and now Mrs. Aldery is—" She stopped. Started again. "She isn't anything. She's a comma in my notebook. That's all that's left of her. A comma."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"So yes," Wren finished. "At any cost. I'll say it plainly so nobody can pretend I said it slyly. If the price of Vael coming back is one man's exile, I think history would call that a bargain. I think Mrs. Aldery would call it a bargain." She looked at Elodie across the flames, and her eyes were wet and unashamed. "And I will hate myself for saying so, but I'd rather hate myself than watch it burn twice."

* * *

Cass had known this moment was coming since the pass. Maybe since the coins. She'd been carrying her own speech around like contraband, hidden in the false bottom of herself, waiting for the checkpoint.

"Well," she said, "since we're being plain."

"Cass," Elodie said softly. It wasn't a warning. It was almost gratitude—as if he already knew what she was going to say and wanted her to know he'd carry it either way.

"No, let me. I'm better at this than you are; I've argued with customs men my whole life and I've never once lost, mostly because I cheat." She shifted, careful of Pip's head against her ribs. "Professor wants her country back at any cost. Fine. Here's mine. Not at any cost. Specifically not at the cost of that child right there."

Pip's breathing went very carefully still.

"She's a remnant," Cass said. "We all know it. Vael made her out of longing and spare parts, same as Sable, same as poor Mrs. Aldery. Which means if Vael comes back—if the gate opens wide and the country pours through—Vael doesn't get a citizen back. It gets its *material* back. It takes her apart to build itself with. I saw what happened to the Bellharrow children when the Order blanked them, and I am telling you now, in front of witnesses, over this fire, that I will put this child on a boat, on a mule, in a barrel if I have to, and I will row her to somewhere with no mountains and no doors before I let a whole resurrected nation use her for mortar."

"You don't know that's what would happen," Wren said.

"Don't I? Sable didn't know what she was until she was standing in it. Half those remnants couldn't tell you which memories were theirs and which were Vael's leaking through. Pip dreams a mother's voice every night, Wren. Every night. What happens to that voice when the real Vael shows up with a million real voices? You think the dream survives the actual?" Cass shook her head. "I've moved a lot of cargo, professor. Rule one: you don't stack the fragile under the heavy. Your country is heavy."

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. To her credit—and Cass would remember this later, when liking Wren Ashvale had become easy—to her credit, she didn't argue. She just looked at Pip, asleep and faking it, and something in her face folded.

"I keep forgetting she's eight," Wren said quietly. "She argues about etymology."

"She's precocious."

"She corrected my translation of the lullaby's third verse."

"She's insufferable," Cass agreed, "and she's mine, and that's my vote."

* * *

Which left, of course, the third vote. The smallest one. The one that had been awake the entire time and listening with the discipline of a professional eavesdropper, which, Cass reflected, was probably what eight-year-olds raised by smugglers became if they survived to adulthood: professionals.

Pip sat up.

"Pip," Cass began, "you should be—"

"I want to see the garden bloom."

Four words. They went through the camp like a cold wind through a keyhole. Even the fire seemed to lean back.

Pip rubbed her eyes with the backs of her hands, the way she did when she wanted to seem sleepier than she was, and looked at all of them in turn with that grave, ancient patience she sometimes wore like a coat too big for her.

"My blue door," she said. "There's a garden. In the dream. Nobody's tended it in forever and the roses have gone all woody and the wall's fallen down on the east side, and there's a bench with a broken slat, and I always sit on the broken slat because it's *mine*, I broke it, in the dream I broke it when I was little." Her chin wobbled, and she steadied it with visible effort, the way Elodie steadied his hands. "I want to see it bloom. For real. With real dirt under my nails instead of dream dirt. That's my vote. That's all."

"Oh, sweetheart," Cass said, and her voice came out rougher than she'd authorized.

"It's not fair," Pip said, still calm, which was somehow worse than crying. "The professor wants a whole country and you want me safe and both of you are fighting about Elodie like he's a door handle. But nobody's fighting about the garden. Nobody even asked if the garden matters." She looked at Elodie. "Does it matter? Is it real? Is my house your house?"

Elodie set down his untouched cup. When he spoke, his voice was the gentlest Cass had ever heard it, and she'd heard him talk to spooked mules and spooked historians and one genuinely haunted lock.

"It's my house," he said. "The blue door. The bench with the broken slat—I broke it too, when I was seven. Fell off the wall trying to reach the plum tree." He smiled, and it hurt to look at, the way sunrise hurts when you haven't slept. "The east wall fell the winter after I left. Or—before I left. Time doesn't sort itself cleanly in there. But the garden is real, Pip. It's as real as anything I remember, and I remember it better than I remember my own hands."

"Then can I see it bloom?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions, the one the whole night had been walking toward with its lantern held low. Cass watched Elodie take it, watched him hold it, watched the arithmetic behind his eyes finally, finally finish.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I know someone who might."

* * *

Later—after Pip had actually fallen asleep this time, mid-sentence, mid-argument about whether plums counted as a legitimate currency (they did, Cass ruled, in certain jurisdictions)—the two adults who weren't arguing sat on either side of the fire and failed to look at each other with great skill.

"She's right, you know," Wren said at last. "The child. We were both talking past her. Past you."

"We were talking about you," Cass corrected. "Like you're cargo."

"Aren't I?"

Cass threw a pinecone at her. Wren caught it, which was showing off, and put it in her pocket, which was worse.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," Cass said, when the silence had gone on long enough to be respectable. "Every job I ever ran, sooner or later there's a moment where everybody looks at everybody else and waits for someone to make the call. And the amateurs think the trick is finding the right person to make it. It's not. The trick is knowing which calls can't be made *for* a person." She poked the fire. Sparks went up and died young. "This is one of those. Professor, you can want Vael back until your notebooks catch fire. I can want Pip safe until I'm blue. Neither of us gets to decide. Because whoever walks through that gate doesn't come back, and you don't volunteer a man for that. Not even a man who's been volunteering his whole life without noticing."

"He took an oath at nine years old," Wren said. "His grandmother made him."

"His grandmother also left him a letter saying the oath was never the point. We found it, Wren. You translated half of it while crying into your sleeve. The choice was always supposed to be his. Freely. That's the whole inheritance—not the gate, not the name. The *choosing*."

Wren was quiet a long time. The owl asked its question again, and this time Wren answered it, though not in any language owls respected.

"All right," she said finally. "All right. Then we put it to him properly. No speeches. No prophecy held over his head like a warrant. He decides alone, at the gate, with nothing but himself for company—which is, I suspect, the only condition under which Elodie Coldharbour has ever been able to hear himself think."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said and it's about solitude."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them footnoted."

* * *

They woke Elodie properly, because this deserved a waking, not a mumbling assent extracted from a man half-dreaming. Cass shook his shoulder and he surfaced the way he always did—instantly, completely, a keeper's reflex—with his eyes already searching the dark for whatever had gone wrong.

"Nothing's wrong," Cass said. "Something's decided. Sit up."

He sat up. Pip burrowed closer to Cass and stayed asleep, genuinely this time, one fist closed around a plum pit she'd been saving for reasons known only to Pip.

Wren explained it, because Wren was better at formal language and this was, in its way, a ceremony. "Tomorrow we reach the Ember Gate. The road's clear—the Order's pulled back to lick its wounds, and the remnants are gathering on the far side, waiting, but they won't press. When we arrive, the decision is yours. Only yours. We'll say our pieces tonight, and then we'll stop saying them. Whatever you choose—open it, close it, find the third door Sable swears is hiding in there—we walk out of those mountains with you or without a country, but *with you*. That part's not on the table. That part we've already voted on, all four of us, unanimously, and Pip seconded it in her sleep."

Elodie looked at them across the fire. Cass watched him try to find the trap in it, because he was a man who'd spent thirty years expecting traps, and finding none, watched something in his shoulders come loose by a degree.

"You'd let me close it," he said. "Even now. After Bellharrow. After everything."

"I'd hate it," Wren said simply. "I'd grieve it for the rest of my life, and I'd write a very long, very angry book about it, and you'd be in the acknowledgments whether you liked it or not. But a truth I have to force isn't a truth, it's just a victory. And I didn't become a historian to win. I became one because somebody has to *remember how it actually happened.*" She adjusted her spectacles. "So it will happen however it happens. Honestly. That's the only archive worth keeping."

Cass said nothing, because her piece had been said, and because some things you communicate by handing a man the last of the tea instead. She poured it into his cup. He understood. He always understood the language of rations; it was the language of love he needed translating.

"One condition," Elodie said.

"Name it."

"If I decide tomorrow—" he paused, and looked at the sleeping weight of Pip against Cass's side, and his voice went soft and absolutely immovable, "—whatever I decide, Pip stays outside the gate. Behind the line. With you. Sworn?"

Cass looked at Wren. Wren looked at Cass. Between them, silently, the last vote of the night passed, unanimous.

"Sworn," Cass said. "Though I warn you, she's going to argue. She'll cite precedent. She'll bring up the plum tree."

"I know," Elodie said, and for the first time in days, he smiled like a man and not a door. "That's why I'm swearing it in front of witnesses."

The fire settled. The owl gave up. And high above the hollow, past the shelf of grey stone, the mountains went on rearranging themselves in the dark—patient, remembering, waiting for morning the way all old countries wait: without hurry, and without mercy, and without any idea at all of what a person might choose, given the whole night to choose it in.
