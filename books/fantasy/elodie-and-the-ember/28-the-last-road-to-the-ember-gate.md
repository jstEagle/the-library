## 28. The Last Road to the Ember Gate

They left the ashes of Bellharrow before the sun did.

Elodie had thought, in the abstract way one thinks about anything that hasn't happened yet, that the final march would begin with a speech. Wren had prepared one—he'd seen her scribbling on the back of a map during breakfast, lips moving, crossing things out. Cass had probably prepared one too, though hers would have been shorter and mostly swearing. Even Pip had the look of a girl rehearsing something important behind her teeth.

But when they shouldered their packs and turned north up the old drover's road, nobody said anything at all. The mountain simply began to move, and after that there was nothing left to say that walking wouldn't say better.

It started subtly, the way all Vael's returns had started. A switchback that Elodie remembered climbing yesterday was suddenly three switchbacks today. A stream they'd forded at dusk now ran on their left instead of their right. Cass stopped at midmorning and stared at a cairn she swore hadn't existed an hour ago, its stones stacked in the old Vaeli pattern—three tall, two crossed—that every shepherd in Thornfell would call bad luck and every ghost in Elodie's memory called *welcome*.

"It's doing it faster," Wren said quietly. She had her notebook out, sketching the road as it rearranged itself around them, her hand shaking only a little. "The folding. Yesterday it took hours between changes. This morning it's minutes."

"Is that good or bad?" Cass asked.

"Yes," said Wren.

Elodie put his hand flat against the nearest rock. Under his palm, faint as a pulse taken at the wrist, he felt it: the heartbeat he'd first heard at the gate a lifetime ago—two weeks ago, really, but some things measured themselves in lifetimes whether you liked it or not. The stone was warm. Stone in the Thornfells was never warm.

"It knows we're coming," he said. "Or it knows *I'm* coming. It's clearing the road."

"Roads don't clear themselves," Cass said.

"These do," said Elodie. "That's rather the whole problem."

He took the lead then, because there was no one else who could. This was the part his grandmother had trained into him without either of them naming it—the keeper's geometry, the way you read a mountain that couldn't be trusted to stay where you left it. You didn't navigate by landmarks; landmarks were liars here. You navigated by *inclination*. By which way the water wanted to run, by how the wind bent the grass, by the small stubborn logic of stone. His grandmother used to blindfold him on the gate path and make him find his way by touching the walls. He had hated it. He had thought it was cruelty dressed up as a lesson.

Now he understood she'd been teaching him to walk through a country that was trying to remember itself, and that she'd known, all along, exactly what kind of road he would one day have to walk.

"Left here," he said, at a fork that had not existed ten seconds earlier. "No—wait." He closed his eyes. Listened with his feet. "Left. Then down. The high road is going to try to take us over the shoulder of the mountain, and the shoulder isn't finished yet."

"Not finished," Pip repeated, delighted. "Like bread."

"Exactly like bread," Elodie said gravely, "and we are not going to walk on unbaked mountain."

Cass snorted. "I've smuggled through worse."

"You've smuggled through worse because I wasn't there to tell you what it was," Elodie said, and she laughed, and the laugh did something kind to the fear in his chest, loosened it by one notch. Not enough. But one.

* * *

The remnants began gathering at noon.

Elodie saw the first of them standing beside a ruined wall that, if you squinted—and everyone had learned to squint lately—might once have been a tollhouse. She was an old woman in a coat cut from cloth no mill had woven in a century, and she stood very straight, and she did not speak. When Elodie passed her, she bowed. Not deeply. Just enough. The way you bow to someone whose job it is to open a door.

By midafternoon there were dozens. By evening, hundreds.

They came out of folds in the hills like fish rising in a lake—men and women and children in clothes half-remembered, faces half-finished, some of them so newly assembled that their edges shimmered, as though the world hadn't quite decided where they stopped. They did not crowd him. They did not touch him. They fell into line behind the company and walked, silent as snowfall, and the road widened to hold them, and the mountains folded themselves flatter to make the walking easier, and Elodie understood with a horror that sat in his stomach like swallowed river ice:

This was a procession. This was a funeral march. And he was at the head of it.

"They think I'm going to open it," he said to Cass, under his breath, somewhere past the second hour of the silence behind them. He could hear them breathing—a sound like wind through a house's empty rooms. "They think I'm going to bring them back."

"Aren't you?" Cass asked.

"I don't know." It was the truest thing he'd said in days. "Cass, I've voted. We voted. They agreed to let me decide alone. But nobody asked *them*." He jerked his chin backward without turning around. "Nobody told them the prophecy never promised them anything. Nobody told them I might walk up to that gate and shut it forever and leave them—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Leave them the way they are."

"Half-here," Cass said.

"Half-here," Elodie agreed. "Standing in the cold at the edge of a country that can't decide whether it exists."

Cass walked a while without answering. When she spoke again, her voice had the roughness it got when she was being more honest than was comfortable. "My da used to say people don't follow you because you know the way. They follow you because you're the one walking. Doesn't matter where you're going. Matters that you don't stop."

"That's terrible leadership theory."

"He also sold counterfeit cheese, so, grain of salt."

Despite everything—despite the procession of the unwritten breathing behind him, despite the warm stone, despite the date written in a dead woman's book—Elodie laughed. It came out cracked and startled, a bird flushed from cover, and behind him he heard it pass down the line of remnants like a coin changing hands, a hundred soft echoes of a sound most of them had perhaps never made. Something in his chest broke a little, in the way that things break open rather than apart.

Pip appeared at his elbow, as she did whenever the world grew too quiet, carrying a stick she had apparently appointed official walking companion. "Why are they following us?" she asked—not rudely, just with the directness of a child who has noticed adults avoiding a question.

"Because they don't have anywhere else," Elodie said.

"Neither do we," said Pip. "We go together, then. That's how it works."

He looked down at her—at this small fierce person born of a country's longing, dirt still somehow under her fingernails from a garden that existed nowhere but in her sleep—and felt the full weight of what the vote had actually been. Not *let Elodie decide alone*. Rather: *let Elodie carry it alone, because love that decides for you is just another kind of erasure.* Wren had said that, around the fire, crying and furious and certain. He was beginning to understand she'd been right, and that understanding it didn't make the weight lighter, only worth lifting.

"Together," he agreed. "For now."

"For always," said Pip, with the terrifying confidence of the very young, and ran ahead to walk with Sable, who had appeared at the column's edge an hour ago and had not spoken since, her lamp unlit in the daylight, her face turned toward the peak that hid the Ember Gate.

* * *

They camped that night in a fold of the mountain that had not existed that morning—a shallow green hollow, sheltered, with a spring that tasted of minerals no Thornfell water carried. The remnants did not camp so much as settle, folding down onto the grass in rows, quiet, patient, some of them dimming slightly as the light went, like lamps running low.

Wren sat beside Elodie at the fire, notebook on her knees, writing by flame-light. After a long time she said, "Do you know what I keep thinking? In the archives, when I found the first reference to Vael—a shipping ledger, one line, 'wool, Vaeli, see attached'—there was no attachment. There never is. That's how they erased it. Not with fire. With *incompleteness*." She looked up at him, firelight moving in her eyes. "And tonight I'm sitting in the middle of the incompleteness, and it's the fullest place I've ever been."

"Wren—"

"I'm not asking you anything," she said quickly. "I promised. We all promised. I'm just—recording. Someone has to record. If you shut it, someone has to remember that they stood here. All of them. In rows. Patient as anything." Her voice caught. "They've waited a hundred years. They can wait one more night. But I want it written down that they waited *well*."

Elodie reached over and closed the notebook gently against her hands. "Then rest," he said. "Tomorrow needs you sharp. Tomorrow needs all of us sharp."

"And tomorrow needs you most of all," Wren said, "and I hate that, and I'm going to say it anyway, because I'm a historian and accuracy matters: whatever you choose at that gate, Elodie Coldharbour, it will be the truest thing anyone's done in a hundred years. Either way. Either choice. Do you understand me?"

"No," Elodie admitted.

"Good," said Wren. "Neither did any of them. That's what made it true."

She went to her bedroll. Elodie stayed by the fire a long time, watching the remnants in the dark, dimming and brightening with some tide he couldn't feel, and listening to the mountain breathe beneath him, faster now than it had been at dawn.

* * *

Near midnight, Sable came and sat across the fire from him.

She wore the coat—the real one, the heavy grey wool with the burnt-sweet smell of a century folded into its seams—and she sat the way his grandmother used to sit, spine straight, hands loose, facing the fire as if it were a person owed attention. For a while neither of them spoke. Behind them the remnant-column lay in the dark like a field of banked embers.

"You're terrified," Sable said at last. Not a question.

"Completely."

"Good." She nodded, satisfied, and Elodie almost laughed. "Your grandmother was terrified too. Every day of her life. Fear isn't the opposite of keeping, Elodie. Fear is the material keepers build with. Anyone can guard a door who feels nothing. It takes a frightened person to guard one properly—they check the hinges twice."

"She checked everything twice," Elodie said. His throat hurt. "Salt, locks, the tally-book. Twice, always twice."

"Because she knew," Sable said softly, "that someday the door would ask her grandson a question no oath could answer. And she wanted him careful. Careful, and frightened, and loved." She looked up, and in the firelight her face was Sable's face and Grandmother Maren's face and neither and both, and it did not frighten him anymore, or rather it frightened him the way deep water frightens you—respectfully, from a distance, knowing it can hold you. "Whatever you decide tomorrow, decide it as yourself. Not as the last citizen. Not as the keeper. Not as the name in the book. Those are all coats, and coats can be handed on. I know," she added, with a small crooked smile that was pure Thornfell smuggler's humor and entirely her own, "I'm wearing one."

"Sable—"

"Don't thank me yet," she said. "Thank me at the gate, if there's a gate to thank me at." She rose, brushed off the grey coat, and paused. "One more thing. The road tomorrow will try to arrive you early. Vael wants this meeting very badly. Whatever shortcut it offers—water that runs uphill, a bridge that wasn't there, a door standing alone in a hillside—refuse it. Arrive tired and honest or don't arrive at all."

"And if the mountain doesn't give me a choice?"

Sable smiled again, sadder. "You're a Coldharbour. Choice is the family business."

* * *

Dawn came up thin and gold, and the procession rose with it, silently, hundreds strong, and formed behind the four of them as naturally as water finding a channel. No orders. No speeches. Wren had abandoned hers finally, tearing the page out at first light and using it to start the cooking fire, which Cass declared the best use of rhetoric she'd ever witnessed.

They climbed.

The last road to the Ember Gate was not a road anymore—it was a remembering. Old paving surfaced beneath the drover's track like bones under skin, Vaeli-cut stones fitted without mortar, each one humming faintly underfoot. The air warmed as they rose, which was wrong, and sweetened, which was worse, carrying smells that did not belong to any living mountain: woodsmoke from chimneys a century cold, bread, lamp oil, rain on slate roofs. Pip hummed as she walked—the lullaby, the anthem, the song that was both—and behind Elodie, in the vast hush, other voices joined hers. Faint. Unfinished. Hundreds of remnants singing a country back into tune, note by note, and the notes landing like footsteps on the far side of a door.

Twice the mountain tried Sable's warned-of tricks. Once a stairway opened in the hillside, clean and inviting, promising the summit in twenty steps. Elodie refused it, and the stairway sighed shut like a held breath released. Once a bridge arched over a gorge that would have taken them a day to walk around, lamplit at both ends, homey, waiting. He stood before it a long moment, heart hammering, wanting it so badly his teeth ached.

"We walk around," he said hoarsely. "Tired and honest."

"Tired and honest," Cass echoed, and spat ceremonially over the edge, and led the detour, and behind them the whole procession followed without a murmur, patient as the written word.

It was near dusk when they crested the final ridge, and the wind dropped all at once, the way it does before something listens.

Below them, in a saddle of bare stone, the Ember Gate stood in its frame of black rock—and it was glowing. Not blazing. Glowing, the way a window glows at evening seen from a cold road: warm, domestic, unbearable. The frost that had bloomed inward for a hundred years was melting, running down the ancient doors in bright threads, and through the seam in the middle, thin as a knife's edge, came light. Lamplight. Street-colored light. The light of a country holding its breath.

And the road down to it, the last hundred yards, was lined on both sides with remnants who had gone ahead—or had always been there, Elodie could no longer tell—standing silent, bowing their heads as the company passed between them. A coronation's honor guard. A funeral's mourners. Both. Neither. The unwritten, honoring the one man alive who could write them or leave them blank, and loving him either way, and that was the worst of it, that was the thing that nearly brought him to his knees in front of all of them: they loved him either way.

Cass's hand landed on his shoulder. "Whatever happens," she said, "you don't walk down there alone. That's not how this outfit works."

Wren took his left arm. Pip, solemn as a magistrate, took his right hand in both of hers. Sable fell in behind, wearing the coat, carrying the lamp, and lit it now, in the failing light, and its flame burned the color of the light seeping through the gate.

Together they started down the last hundred yards, and the procession followed, and the humming swelled, and the doors—

The doors were already opening.

Not wide. A crack. A hand's width. Enough. Through the gap Elodie could see cobbles, wet with evening rain, reflecting lamplight. He could smell bread. Somewhere beyond, faint and clear, a bell was ringing in a tower he had kept shut his entire life, ringing the hour as if no hour had ever been missed.

Elodie stopped at the threshold's edge, his company around him, his country's ghosts at his back, his heart going like a trapped bird.

From inside—warm, familiar, impossible—a voice called out to him across a hundred years:

"Elodie? Love, is that you? Supper's getting cold."

His grandmother's voice.
