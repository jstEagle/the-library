## 29. The Gate Stands Open

The last road to the Ember Gate was not a road at all by the end of it. It was a memory of a road wearing the mountain like a coat two sizes too large.

Elodie walked at the head of the procession because no one had asked him to and everyone had arranged themselves behind him anyway. Cass ranged at his left shoulder with her hand resting on the knife she hadn't drawn in three days, which for Cass was practically a state of prayer. Wren clutched her notebooks against her chest like a breastplate. And Pip—Pip held Elodie's hand, swinging it gently back and forth as though they were going to market, which was either the most comforting or most heartbreaking thing that had happened to him all week, and he genuinely could not decide which.

Behind them, the remnants came. He tried not to count them. Counting made them real, and real things could be lost.

There were hundreds. Maybe more than hundreds. They had gathered out of the folds of the mountains over three days of walking—farmers in coats cut from fabric that hadn't been woven in a century, women with Vaeli braids coiled at their ears, an old man who whistled fragments of songs and apologized after every one as if he'd been caught stealing. They didn't speak much. They walked the way people walk behind a coffin: present, patient, certain that something important was happening but unsure whether they were mourners or the deceased.

"You're doing the thing," Cass said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face does arithmetic about how doomed you are." She glanced sideways at him. "Stop it. You're bad at sums."

"I'm actually quite good at sums."

"You once told me a crate of brandy weighed 'about a hundred pounds' when it weighed four hundred. The dockmaster cried."

"That was one time, and I stand by my estimate being *spiritually* correct."

Despite everything—a hooded remnant-woman walking behind him who might have been assembled from his grandmother's sewing circle, a mountain that kept rearranging its shoulders around them, the fact that he was marching toward a door he'd sworn on his grandmother's grave never to touch—Elodie felt the corner of his mouth twitch. That was Cass's particular gift. She could smuggle anything across any border, including laughter into a funeral procession.

Pip tugged his hand. "The road's humming again," she reported, with the gravity of a ship's captain noting weather.

She was right. The ground underfoot had taken up a faint vibration, deep and regular, like a note held on an instrument far underground. Elodie had spent thirty years listening to the Ember Gate's moods—the way a sailor listens to the sea—and this hum was new. Or rather, it was very old. It was the hum the gate had made before his time, the one his grandmother's journals called *the waking note*, and reading those words in her handwriting had not prepared him for hearing it through the soles of his boots.

"It knows we're coming," Wren said softly. She'd said little all day. Her eyes were red-rimmed and shining, and she kept touching her notebooks as though checking they still existed. "Or—it's been waiting so long that waiting has become a kind of knowing. Do you understand? A country that spends a hundred years holding its breath doesn't need to hear footsteps. It only needs to remember that someday, someone would come to let it exhale."

"That's either beautiful or terrifying, Wren."

"Yes," she agreed simply.

* * *

They crested the last ridge as the sun went down, and the whole company stopped at once, the way a single body stops.

The pass below was exactly as Elodie remembered it, and nothing like it at all. The scree slope he had climbed ten thousand times was still there—but threaded through it now ran a paved way, pale stones fitted together with the obsessive precision of a people who believed roads should outlive empires. Vaeli roadwork. He knew the pattern. His hands knew it. Somewhere under the new world, the old stones had risen like bones surfacing through skin.

And at the head of the pass, set into the black rock of Thornfell's deepest shoulder, stood the Ember Gate.

It was open.

Not wide—not flung, not broken—but ajar. A finger's width of darkness between the great bronze leaves that Elodie had sealed every night of his life with iron bars, salt, spoken oaths, and the particular stubbornness of a man who had nothing else to live for. Through that finger-width poured light. Not daylight. Lamplight. Warm, golden, domestic lamplight, spilling out of the crack in the mountain as if the rock itself had swallowed a city and found it too bright to keep.

"Oh," said Pip, in a small voice, and then, with the unerring instinct of children for the wrong moment: "It looks like our house did, before."

Before. Before what, Pip never said. Pip's befores were not anyone else's befores.

"Nobody move," Cass breathed. Her knife was out now; she didn't seem to have noticed drawing it.

But Elodie moved. He heard himself say, "Stay here," in his keeper's voice, the flat calm one he used for avalanches and other acts of God, and then his feet were carrying him down the pale stones toward the door he had guarded his whole life, and nobody stayed anywhere, because of course they didn't.

* * *

Up close, the gate was worse. Up close, he could see that the bronze leaves were breathing.

Not moving—breathing. A swell and settle beneath the metal, slow as sleep, and the seam of light between them widened and narrowed by a hair with each pulse. Frost had bloomed across the bronze in spirals, inward-facing spirals, curling toward the gap like ferns reaching for a window. And from inside came sounds. Cart wheels on cobbles. A dog barking, twice, lazily. A woman calling a name—not his name, some other name, some name that had gone out of the world with everything else—and underneath it all, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, music. Strings. A waltz, maybe, or the ancestor of a waltz, played in a square somewhere in a country that had been dead for a hundred years and appeared to be having a lovely evening.

Elodie had imagined this moment perhaps a thousand times. In none of those imaginings had his first feeling been homesickness. That was the horror of it—that the gate opened and what poured out of the crack was not dread, not doom, but the specific ache of smelling bread baking in a kitchen you can't quite place. His body recognized the lamplight. His bones recognized the music. Some cellar of him, older than his oaths, whispered *there you are*.

"That's enough of that," he told his own sternum, quietly.

"You're talking to yourself," Cass observed, arriving at his elbow.

"I'm talking to parts of myself I don't trust."

"Good policy. Carry on."

Wren came up on his other side, notebooks forgotten, hands pressed to her mouth. Tears ran freely down her face and she made no effort whatsoever to hide them. "I studied this door for twenty years," she managed. "From copies. From descriptions in banned marginalia. There was a woodcut in a heretical psalter—I copied it out eleven times—and it showed the leaves with sunbursts on them, and everyone said the artist was inventing, embellishing—" Her voice cracked. "You never told me it was beautiful."

"I never looked," Elodie said. "Looking felt like letting it look back."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's been looking at me for thirty years."

The seam of light pulsed. Widened. Another inch. The smell that came out was cut grass and lamp oil and rain on stone, and underneath it, faintly, impossibly, woodsmoke from a hearth he suddenly, violently remembered—blue door, low ceiling, his grandmother's kettle singing—

He put his palm flat against the bronze.

The gate sighed. There was no other word for it. A long, shuddering exhalation through a century of held breath, and the frost spirals bloomed outward from under his hand like flowers opening in fast-forward, and the music swelled, and somewhere beyond the door a crowd—no, not a crowd, a *town*, a whole town's worth of distant voices—seemed to lean toward him the way wheat leans after wind.

"Don't open it," Cass said. Not sharply. Almost gently. "Whatever it's offering, don't."

"I haven't touched the latch."

"You touched it with your heart. Same thing."

* * *

That was when they saw her.

She stood on the threshold itself—one foot in the light, one in the dark, as if she'd been caught forever mid-step. She wore the coat. His grandmother's coat, dove-gray wool, brass buttons worn smooth by sixty years of thumbs, a patch at the left elbow stitched with thread that didn't quite match because Maren Coldharbour had never met a color she couldn't improve upon. The coat that had vanished through this door twenty-one years ago along with the woman inside it.

Except the woman inside the coat was weeping, and his grandmother had never wept where anyone could see. That was the first thing Elodie noticed, before her face, before anything: the crying. Open, unguarded, both-hands-over-mouth crying, and some cold, careful part of him noted it like a customs officer noting a seal that didn't match the manifest.

"Sable," Wren whispered.

Sable lowered her hands. Her face in the lamplight was his grandmother's face and also not—slightly too symmetrical, slightly too young at the eyes, like a portrait painted by someone working from a beloved description instead of the living original. But her eyes were right. Gray-green, sharp, amused even in grief. Those eyes had watched him learn his letters and checked his knots and caught him stealing honey with a single raised eyebrow that had ended better thefts than any lock ever built.

"Elodie," she said. His grandmother's voice. Exactly. To the grain. "Oh—you grew up *tall*. She always said you would. You were such a small boy. Such a stubborn small boy. You used to hide in the flour bin when you were frightened and swear you were looking for biscuits."

He had hidden in the flour bin. Twice. No living soul knew that but him.

"That proves nothing," he heard himself say, and hated how young his voice sounded. "You could know that from—"

"From what?" Sable asked gently. "Where would you write down a flour bin, Elodie? Where in the world is there room for a flour bin, except in someone who was there?"

Behind him, the remnants had come down the pass in silence. Now, at the sight of her, they sank to their knees in the snow—not to her, he understood dimly, but toward the light behind her, the way sunflowers go, without any decision involved. An old man near the front began to sing, very softly, the anthem that no one had taught Pip, and one by one the others joined, and the song rose up the black rock of the mountain and poured into the crack in the world, and the gate pulsed wider, wider, drinking.

"She remembers us," Sable said. Her tears fell and steamed faintly on the warm threshold stone. "All this time, we thought she was gone, and she wasn't. She was *holding on*. Every one of us is a handhold. Every street, every song, every stupid flour bin—she gripped it all so hard she wore grooves in herself, and now—" She laughed through the crying, and the laugh was so precisely Maren Coldharbour's laugh, the one that meant mischief approved of, that Elodie's knees nearly went. "Now she has enough of us back to open her eyes. And do you know the first thing she wants?"

Elodie shook his head.

"To see her grandson," Sable said. "She's been setting the table since sundown."

* * *

"No," Cass said loudly, cutting across the anthem, across the music, across whatever spell was settling over the pass like fresh snowfall. "Nope. Everyone stop kneeling, get up, you'll catch your deaths. Wren, stop crying, you'll rust. Pip—"

But Pip was already walking forward.

She slipped past Cass the way only Pip could slip past anything, small and quick and utterly certain, and stood beside Elodie at the threshold, staring up at Sable with her head tilted, evaluating, the way she evaluated everything—with a child's terrible honesty.

"You're mostly true," Pip announced. "But not all the way. Like the dream houses. You're a dream house person."

Sable went very still. "What did you say?"

"You're somebody's remembering of somebody." Pip shrugged, matter-of-fact. "That's okay. I'm probably somebody's remembering too. We're all a bit borrowed." She reached up and took Sable's hanging hand. "Your hand's warm, though. Dream house people usually have cold hands."

For a moment Sable looked down at the child with an expression Elodie couldn't read—grief and wonder and something like terror, the terror of a mirror realizing it reflects. Then her composure, so carefully assembled, so painstakingly *remembered*, cracked straight down the middle.

"He can't stay out here deciding," she said, and her voice wavered between two registers now, grandmother and stranger, Maren and remnant. "The gate won't wait. It's been waiting a hundred years and it is done waiting, and if he turns away now it will start pulling instead of asking. Do you understand? What happens next happens *now*, and it happens to all of you." Her eyes found Elodie's, and in them the amusement flickered one last time, defiantly. "Come inside, keeper. Your country would like a word with you. It's waited rather a long time, and it has *so much* to show you—and I am afraid, my dear stubborn boy, that it has already set your place."

Beyond the widening seam of bronze, lamplit streets stretched away into a warm and impossible evening, and somewhere down among them, faint but unmistakable, a kettle began to sing.

Elodie's hand, without any instruction from him, rose toward the door.
