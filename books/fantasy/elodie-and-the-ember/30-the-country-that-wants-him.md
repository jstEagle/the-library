## 30. The Country That Wants Him

The threshold of the Ember Gate was a line drawn in lamplight, and Elodie's boots were standing on it.

He knew this because he could feel the difference under his feet—the way you can feel the edge of a hearthstone with your eyes shut, warm on one side and cold on the other. Behind him: Thornfell, the mountain wind, Cass's hand hovering near his shoulder without quite touching it, Wren whispering translations to herself like a woman praying in a language she'd only just learned. Ahead of him: streets.

Lamplit streets. Cobbled ones, with rain-slick stones that threw back the glow of lanterns hung from wrought-iron posts, and above them the gabled roofs of houses painted the colors of a sunset someone had decided to keep—ochre, rust, a blue so particular it made his chest hurt, because he knew that blue. He had never seen it. He had never seen any of this. And he knew it the way you know your own hands.

"Vael," Wren breathed behind him, and her voice cracked clean down the middle.

"It's not real," Elodie said, because somebody had to say it, and it was apparently going to be him, as usual. "It's memory. It's the gate showing us—"

"Elodie." Cass's voice was very quiet. "There's smoke coming out of chimneys."

He looked. There was. Thin ribbons of woodsmoke rising from a dozen chimneys into an evening sky, and somewhere down those streets a dog barked twice and gave up, the way dogs do when they've barked at nothing their whole lives, and a bakery smell drifted on the air—bread and cinnamon and something darker, like burnt sugar—and Elodie felt his knees go soft with a homesickness so violent and so absurd that he had to grip the doorframe to stay upright.

He had never lived here. He had never smelled this bread. His body disagreed. His body said *you're home, you're late, they're waiting.*

"Don't step through," Sable said.

She stood just inside the threshold, on the Vaeli side, weeping without any noise at all, tears running down a face that was and wasn't his grandmother's. She'd been crying since they arrived, he thought, or maybe since long before—maybe she'd been crying for a hundred years in whatever place half-made people wait.

"Why not?" he asked.

"Because it widens," she said. "Every step you take inside, the gate opens further. It isn't holding itself open, Elodie. It's holding itself open *for* you."

* * *

He stepped through anyway. Of course he did. He was beginning to understand that every important decision in his life was going to be made by the part of him that ignored good advice, and that part had apparently been waiting thirty years for tonight.

The cobblestones took his weight like they remembered it. That was the thing no one had warned him about—not Wren with her notebooks, not even Sable—that memory has a texture, and Vael's texture was *worn smooth*, like a stair that generations of feet have hollowed. The street knew him. The lamplight leaned toward him. Somewhere a window opened and a voice called something cheerful in Vaeli—a language he didn't speak, except that he did, except that the words arrived in his head already translated, the way a tune arrives already hummed.

*Supper's on. Don't let it go cold.*

"I don't know whose supper that is," he said aloud, to nobody, to the street.

"You do," said the street, in the voice of everyone he'd ever missed.

Behind him, the gate creaked. He turned. The Ember Gate—which had taken four men and a winch to shift an inch in all his years of keeping it—had swung wide enough to drive a cart through, and beyond it Cass, Wren, and Pip stood in a huddle, lit gold by the lamplight spilling past them into the mountain dark.

Pip waved at him. Small, solemn, encouraging, the way you wave at someone about to do something brave and possibly stupid.

"The gate," Wren called, and her voice shook. "Elodie, the prophecy—the widening—it's tied to your steps. Every pace you take into Vael, it—" She stopped, looked down at her notebook, looked up again, and he watched a scholar decide, in real time, whether to say the true thing or the kind one. "It wants you deep," she finished. "It wants you *in*."

"Then I'll take shallow steps," Elodie said, and heard Cass snort despite everything, and loved her a little for it.

He walked anyway. Shallow steps. One, two, three, down the lamplit street, and behind him the gate groaned wider, and ahead of him the country unrolled like a carpet being thrown down by invisible servants, eager, hospitable, hungry.

* * *

The house was exactly where he knew it would be, which was the worst part.

Third street off the square, past the fountain with the chipped mermaid, up the steps worn pale in their centers, to a door painted the particular blue—the blue of a summer sky at seven in the evening, the blue of a dress in a dream, the blue that Pip drew on every door in every picture she'd ever made, and he would think about that later, he absolutely would not think about that now.

The door was open. Doors in Vael were apparently always open for him.

Inside: a hallway smelling of beeswax and dried lavender, coats on pegs, one of them his grandmother's other coat, the everyday one, with the frayed cuff she'd always promised to mend. A table set for supper. Four places. Steam still rising off a tureen of stew, which meant the kitchen believed someone had just ladled it, which meant the kitchen was lying, which meant the lie was perfect.

And in the doorway to the parlor, wiping her hands on her apron, stood his grandmother.

Not Sable. Sable was still back at the threshold; he could feel her there, a held breath. This was the memory itself, Vael's own recollection of Maren Coldharbour, keeper of the gate, and it was flawless. The gray braid pinned up wrong, exactly the way she always pinned it wrong. The flour on her cheekbone. The squint she got when she was deciding whether to scold you or feed you first.

"Elodie," she said. "You're late."

"You're dead," he said. "Or worse. You're a memory wearing her face, and my grandmother went through that gate to hold it shut, and she told me—" His voice betrayed him, cracked like river ice. "She told me a gate is only shut from the side that stays."

"Yes," said the memory gently. "And look how well that's gone."

* * *

She sat him down at the table. Of course she did. Memory-grandmothers are constitutionally incapable of letting a boy stand when there's soup.

"This isn't real," he said, for the fourth time, mostly to keep count.

"The stew is real," she said. "I made it in 1189, the autumn before the Unwriting, and I have been making it every evening since, because evenings need making. Taste it and tell me it isn't real."

He tasted it. It was real. It was lamb and barley and too much thyme, the way she always made it, the way no cook in Thornfell had ever managed, and it tasted like being nine years old and safe, and he put down the spoon very carefully because if he didn't put it down carefully he was going to throw it across the room.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"Nothing terrible," said his grandmother—or the country speaking through her, he couldn't tell where one ended and the other began, and suspected that was rather the point. "That's what nobody understands, on either side of that door. The Order thinks we want conquest. Your historian thinks we want resurrection. We want Tuesday. We want the market on Saturday and rain on the roof and someone to teach the fiddle the old songs before the old songs forget themselves. We want what any home wants. Someone in it."

"The peace," Elodie said. "Wren says the peace that ended the war was built on your erasure. If you come back—"

"Then a hundred years of weddings and harvests and children who were born in the shape the world took after we left—all of it gets rewritten to make room." The memory said it plainly, without flinching, and somehow that was more frightening than denial. "Yes. We know. We have had a century to know. And we want it anyway, Elodie, because wanting is the only thing left to us. Would you refuse to come home because home costs too much? Or would you stand in the doorway and argue price?"

"That's exactly what I'm doing."

"I know." She smiled, and it was her smile, the crooked one, and it went through him like a splinter. "You get that from me."

The gate boomed softly behind him, another inch, another degree of open. He didn't turn around. Turning around felt like losing.

"Come inside," she said. "Properly. Not as a visitor, not as a keeper. As a citizen. The last one—we've saved your seat a hundred years, do you understand? Every empty chair in this country has been yours. Every nameless grave, every unlabeled street, every song missing its second verse. Come in, and it all fits together again, and you will never eat alone, never sleep in a cold watch-house, never spend another night listening to a door to make sure it stays shut. You'll be *from* somewhere. Everyone will know your name."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the gate closes," she said, "and we go back to being almost, and you go back to being the man who kept a door shut on his own family." She reached across the table and covered his hand with hers, and her hand was warm, and that was the cruelest detail yet. "We won't hate you. Homes don't hate. But oh, Elodie. Oh, love. We will miss you forever, and you will feel it every day of your life, like weather."

* * *

He got up and walked out of the house, because staying one more minute was going to kill him, and walking away was also going to kill him, but slower, and slow was how he preferred his deaths.

The street had filled while he was inside. Not with crowds—with presences. Figures at windows. Faces at the ends of lanes, half-lit, patient. A child's chalk drawing on the cobbles of a stick figure holding a key. An old man on a bench who nodded to him the way neighbors nod, familiar, unsurprised, *there you are, we knew you'd come round eventually.* The whole country leaning toward him like plants toward a window.

And the gate, behind him, wide now. Wide as a barn door. Wide as a church. He could hear Wren reading aloud from the prophecy, her voice carrying down the lamplit street, and Cass saying something sharp and low, and Pip—

Pip was laughing. Pip was laughing at something, delighted, and the sound came down the street of his erased homeland and landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

He turned around.

They stood in the great open mouth of the gate, the three of them plus Sable, small against the mountain dark, and the lamplight of Vael poured over them and made them golden and made them *belong here*, which was the problem, which was suddenly, horribly the problem, because Pip was pointing at the fountain with the chipped mermaid and saying something to Wren with the absolute authority of a person describing her own front garden.

"Pip," Elodie called. "Come here a moment."

She came skipping down the street toward him, past the bench, past the old man who nodded to her too—nodded to *her*, familiar, unsurprised—and the cold went through Elodie like a blade of frost.

Because the old man hadn't been surprised to see her either.

Pip stopped in front of him, beaming, dirt on her knees from a garden that didn't exist, and pointed back the way she'd come. "Elodie. Elodie, the fountain lady's missing her arm and I *know* where it is, it's in the schoolyard, they used it for a hopscotch—"

"Pip." He crouched down to her height. His mouth was dry. "How do you know that?"

She blinked at him, puzzled by the question, the way you're puzzled when someone asks how you know your own name.

"Because I live here," she said.

Behind her, at the end of the lamplit street, the whole country of Vael held its breath—and the gate, patient as gravity, swung another inch wider.
