## 31. Pip at the Threshold

Pip had been told to stay put.

She was very good at staying put. She could stay put for whole minutes at a time, sometimes longer if there was something interesting to look at. But Elodie had said *stay here* in that voice he used when he meant it, the one like a door closing, and then he had walked through the Ember Gate as if walking through a door were a thing a person just *did*, and Cass had gone after him shouting his name, and Wren had dropped her notebook, which Pip knew was the historian equivalent of screaming.

So really, Pip figured, staying put would have been the rude thing to do. Everyone she loved was on the other side of a magic door, and she was supposed to stand in the frost with her hands in her pockets like a coat rack.

"No," she told herself out loud, so it would count as a decision. "Absolutely not."

She went through.

* * *

Going through the gate felt like stepping into a bath drawn by someone who loved you. Warmth first, then a kind of holding, all over, gentle as being carried. The frost on the threshold melted under her boots and did not come back. And then—

Then there was Vael.

Pip had dreamed this place her whole life. She hadn't known it was her whole life; she'd thought everyone dreamed of cobbled streets and lamps lit gold behind blue glass, of a smell like bread and rain, of a mother's voice humming low in another room. She'd thought the house with the blue door was just a dream, the way Cass's dreams were mostly about coin and running.

But it wasn't a dream. It was here. It was *here*, laid out beyond the gate like a table set for supper: streets going down and down in gentle curves, lamplit windows, smoke rising sweet from chimneys, mountains folded around it all like cupped hands. Somewhere a bell rang, unhurried. Somewhere someone laughed.

"Oh," Pip said. It came out very small. "Oh, it's *real*."

And Vael — the whole country, the air and the stones and the lamplight — turned toward her the way a sunflower turns toward morning.

It knew her.

That was the strange part, the part that made her stomach go soft and scared at once. It knew her the way her own blanket knew her, the way Cass's shoulder knew the exact shape of Pip's head when Pip fell asleep against it on long rides. The warmth that had held her at the threshold didn't let go. It settled around her like a second coat, and it hummed, and the hum was almost — almost — the lullaby. Her lullaby. The one nobody taught her.

*Come home,* said the lamplight. *Come home,* said the bell. *We kept your room.*

"I don't have a room," Pip whispered, but even as she said it, she could see it: a little room above a kitchen, a bed with a quilt stitched in stars, a windowsill with three smooth stones on it because she liked stones, because she had always liked stones, because *of course she had*, they'd known that about her before she was born—

Her feet started moving without asking her permission.

* * *

The street she walked down was called Larkspur Row. She knew that the way you know your own name. The cobbles were worn smooth in the middle from a hundred years of feet, except they couldn't have been, because no feet had walked here in a hundred years, and Pip's head did a little somersault trying to hold both of those true things at once. She decided, in the end, that both things could be true if the country wanted them badly enough. Countries were big. They could probably manage it.

There were people. That was the other thing. People in doorways, people sweeping steps that didn't need sweeping, people waving. A baker leaned out of her window and called, "Evening, little miss! You've grown!"

"Have I?" Pip called back politely, because it seemed rude not to.

"You have!" the baker said, wiping floury hands on her apron, and her eyes were shining and slightly wrong, like paintings of eyes, like eyes remembered rather than seen. "You've grown so tall. Your mother will be pleased."

Pip stopped walking.

The word *mother* landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It took a long time coming, and when it came it wasn't pain exactly. It was longing, enormous and specific, shaped exactly like a kitchen and a humming voice and arms that smelled like bread.

"She's here?" Pip asked. Her voice cracked in the middle. "My mum's here?"

"Of course, sweetheart." The baker smiled. "Everyone's here."

Everyone's here. In a country that had been unwritten for a hundred years. In a country made of memory and wanting, where every person on every step was stitched together out of somebody's remembering — Pip knew this, some cold sensible corner of her knew it, the same corner that knew Cass's smuggling routes and which tavern keepers watered the beer. These people were real the way a drawing is real. Real and lovely and made of love.

But oh, they were *warm*. And oh, they were *here*. And Pip was eight years old, and she had never once in her life had a mother, only a series of smugglers who were bad at cooking and good at running, and the best of them, the only one who'd stayed, was somewhere ahead in these lamplit streets chasing a man who was chasing a ghost.

"Which way?" Pip asked the baker. "To my house? The one with the blue door?"

The baker pointed down Larkspur Row, toward the sound of the bell, and the whole street seemed to lean that way with her, lamplight bending like grass in wind, and the country hummed its almost-lullaby louder, and Pip's feet — traitorous, eager, hopeful feet — started moving again.

* * *

She found Elodie in a square with a fountain that flowed upward, which Pip thought was showing off but forgave immediately because it was beautiful.

He was standing very still in front of a woman made of lamplight and memory, a woman with his same grey eyes, and Pip understood without being told: grandmother. The real one, or the shape of her, or whatever a country keeps of the people it lost. Elodie's face was doing something complicated, like weather.

"Elodie," Pip said.

He turned. For half a heartbeat his face did something worse than complicated — it did *afraid* — and then he crossed the square in about four strides and crouched down and gathered her up like she weighed nothing, which she didn't, but most grown-ups still picked her up like she was made of eggs.

"Pip." His voice wobbled. "I told you to stay put."

"I stayed put for ages," Pip said into his shoulder. "You have no idea how long. There was a whole fountain."

"That's not — " He pulled back and looked at her, and Pip watched him do the thing he did, the keeper thing, the checking-things-over thing. His eyes went to her hands. Then they went wide.

"What?" Pip said. She looked at her own hands.

They were fading.

Not scary-fading, not yet. Not gone. But the edges of her fingers, where the lamplight touched them, were soft. Unfocused. Like her hands were deciding whether to be here or be remembered, and the lamplight was voting very hard for *remembered*.

"It's all right," said the woman with the grey eyes, gently, from behind him. "Nothing here hurts. Nothing here ever hurts."

"She's a remnant," Elodie said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for hard truths, the one he'd learned to use since the pass, since Sable, since everything. "Like Sable was. Like Bellharrow. Vael is — it's reaching for her. It's making her out of its own wanting." His hands closed around Pip's fading fingers, warm and shaking. "Pip. Look at me. Do you hear it calling you?"

Pip considered lying. She was, after all, Cass's ward, and lying was practically the family trade.

But Elodie was looking at her with his whole heart in his face, and besides, the country was humming her lullaby right then, right into her bones, and lying to Elodie while a whole nation sang to her felt like too much work.

"Yes," she said. "It knows my room. It knows I like stones." Her chin wobbled. "It says my mum kept my room."

Elodie closed his eyes. When he opened them they were wet, and furious, and clear as winter.

"It doesn't have a room for you," he said softly. "It has a *want* for you. There's a difference, Pip. It's a big difference. It's the biggest difference there is."

"But it *feels* the same."

"I know." He pressed his forehead to hers. "I know it does. That's what makes it dangerous."

Behind them, the fountain sang upward. Down Larkspur Row, doors opened one by one, and people stepped out into the lamplight — the baker, a lamplighter, a dog that was definitely more memory than dog — and they all looked at Pip with such terrible tenderness, such open, welcoming, hungry love, that Pip's chest hurt with how much she wanted them.

"Come inside, little miss," the baker called. "Supper's nearly ready. We've waited so long for supper."

"A hundred years," murmured the lamplighter.

"We're so hungry," said the dog, which was somehow the worst one.

"They're not hungry for supper," Elodie said quietly. He stood, keeping Pip's hand in his, and faced the street full of loving faces. "They're hungry for *her*. For anyone. Vael has been alone inside its own remembering for a century, and every remnant it makes, it makes because it can't stand the quiet." His grip tightened. "And it would keep her. It would keep her forever, and she'd be happy, and she wouldn't be *her* anymore. She'd be its dream of a daughter. Its dream of everyone's daughter. Every daughter Vael ever lost, all folded into one small girl who likes stones."

Pip looked down at her soft-edged fingers.

"Cass would be sad," she said. Her voice came out tiny.

"Yeah," Elodie said. "Cass would be sad."

"Cass would burn this whole country down," Pip corrected, with dawning horror. "Cass would find a way to burn down a *memory*, Elodie. She'd invent it. She'd invent fire that works on countries."

"She might," Elodie agreed gravely.

"And she'd feel awful after. She cries when she thinks nobody's watching. I've seen her. She thinks she hides it but she does this sniff." Pip demonstrated the sniff. It was devastating. Even the memory-dog looked away.

"So we should probably not make her cry," Elodie said.

"No," Pip agreed. "Probably not."

* * *

They walked back toward the gate together, hand in hand, and the whole country walked with them — not following, exactly, but leaning, the streets curving subtly to keep them in view, the lamplights stretching their glow like arms. The lullaby followed them, patient, unoffended. It wasn't angry. That was the horrible part. It wasn't angry at all. It simply believed, with the bottomless confidence of a thing that had wanted for a hundred years, that Pip would come back. That everyone came back eventually. That wanting was a door and all doors opened inward.

At the edge of the square, Pip stopped.

"Elodie," she said. "The lady. Your gran. Is she real?"

He was quiet for a moment. Ahead of them, the gate glowed, and Cass's voice was echoing through it, getting less polite by the syllable.

"She's as real as anything here," he said finally. "Maybe more. Maybe that's the problem."

"Because real things can leave," Pip said, "and remembered things can't."

Elodie looked down at her like she'd spoken in an old language. "Where did you learn that?"

Pip shrugged. "From being a remnant," she said, practical. "Sort of. A bit. I'm not all the way one. But I know how it feels from the inside, wanting to be somewhere so much you start becoming the want." She squeezed his hand with her soft, uncertain fingers. "That's what this country is, isn't it? One big want. It's not mean. It's just lonely. Like Cass before she came back for us."

Something moved through Elodie's face then — fast, like a fish under ice — and Pip, who was extremely good at noticing things (it was practically her job; nobody else noticed anything), filed it away. He'd thought of something. Something important. Something with a shape like a door.

"Pip," he said slowly, "when you dream of the blue door. The house. Does it only have a front door?"

"What?"

"The house in your dream. Does it have any other doors? Back door, cellar, garden gate, anything?"

Pip frowned and went looking through her dreams, which were kept, like everything she owned, in a state of cheerful disorder. The house with the blue door. The kitchen. The stairs. The little room with the quilt of stars and the three smooth stones on the sill. And —

"There's a little door," she said. "Off the kitchen. Low down, like for a cat, but bigger. Blue too, but a different blue. Darker. I always thought it went to the cellar, but it doesn't." She looked up. "It goes outside. To the garden. You can see the light under it."

Elodie had gone very still.

"Light under it," he repeated.

"Like daytime light. Even in the dream it's night, but under that little door it's day." Pip tilted her head. "Why? What's through it?"

"I don't know yet," Elodie said, and he was smiling now, a fierce, frightened, wonderful smile, the smile of a man standing in a trap who has just spotted the third exit. "But I think my grandmother spent her whole life looking for it. And I think an eight-year-old remnant just drew me a map."

Through the gate, Cass's voice arrived at maximum volume: "*IF YOU TWO HAVE GOTTEN EATEN BY A COUNTRY, I WANT IT IN WRITING—*"

"We should go," Pip said.

"We should go," Elodie agreed.

Hand in hand, two small soft-edged people, they ran for the gate while a whole lonely country sang them home — and behind them, far down Larkspur Row, in a kitchen that existed only because someone had loved it, a small dark-blue door, low to the ground, began, very quietly, to glow.
