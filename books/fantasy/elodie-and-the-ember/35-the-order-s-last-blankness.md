## 35. The Order's Last Blankness

### Cass

Cass had fought in exactly three battles in her life, and she would be the first to admit that none of them had gone well. There was the dockside brawl in Saltmere that cost her two teeth and a business partner. There was the ambush in the Kettle Pass, which she survived mostly by playing dead, a skill she'd refined since childhood. And there was the night she'd tried to steal from a witch in Lowmarsh, which she did not count as a battle so much as an education.

So when the Order of the Blank Page came up the mountain road at dawn—two hundred strong, in grey coats, carrying lanterns that burned white instead of gold—her first professional instinct was to assess the exits.

There were no exits. That was rather the point of gates.

"Right," she said, hefting the smuggler's cutlass she hadn't unsheathed in six years. It had a notch in it shaped like her old life. "Wren. You're behind me. If anything happens to me, you run for the pass."

"Nothing is going to happen to you," Wren said. She was holding a satchel of notebooks against her chest like a breastplate, which was very Wren, and also completely useless as armor.

"If something does happen to me," Cass said, "you run for the pass, and you tell Pip I died doing something extremely heroic and probably illegal."

"You're not going to die."

"I know. But if I do, I want the version where I'm heroic. I've earned one good story."

Wren laughed—a short, wet, terrified laugh—and then the first line of grey coats crested the ridge, and nobody was laughing anymore.

The remnants stood along the road in their hundreds now. They had come all through the night, out of folds in the mountains that shouldn't have held them: the half-remembered baker from Bellharrow with flour still ghosting his hands; the woman who was only a name on a gravestone and a smell of lavender; children who were more suggestion than substance, flickering at the edges like candle flames in a draft. They didn't fight. They simply stood, in the way of the Order's advance, the way fog stands in the way of a ship—not stopping it, only making it slower, and sadder, and harder to see what you're doing.

"They won't hurt them," Wren whispered. "They can't. The Order doesn't kill. They *blank*."

"That's worse," Cass said.

"Yes," Wren said. "That's much worse."

The Order's vanguard stopped twenty paces from the nearest remnant—a young man who might once have been a fiddler, because his fingers kept moving against his thigh, playing music no one could hear. An officer stepped forward. Cass recognized him: Inquisitor Hale, the gentle one, the one who offered tea and reasonable voices and clean forgettings. He raised one hand, and his men halted.

He looked at the fiddler for a long moment. Then he bowed. Actually bowed.

And then he opened his white lantern, and the fiddler went out like a snuffed candle—not screaming, not falling, just becoming less, and less, and then a shape of empty air where a man had been standing, and then nothing at all, not even the memory of where he'd been standing, because Cass found she couldn't remember his face, and neither, by the look on hers, could Wren.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, that's how it works. It takes the remembering too." She was crying without seeming to notice. "Cass. Cass, I need you to remember him for me. He had a—there was a song—he played at—"

"I've got him," Cass lied, because she had nothing, because there was nothing left to have. She put her hand on Wren's shoulder anyway. "I've got him. Now stay behind me and write down everyone else. Fast. While they're still here to write down."

And that was the battle plan, such as it was: Cass with a rusty sword and a talent for lying, Wren with a notebook and a grief the size of a country, and somewhere behind them, at the gate itself, Elodie Coldharbour opening a small blue door while the world tried to burn down around his ears.

It was, Cass reflected, cutting her first line into the snow and shouting the oldest smuggler's oath she knew—which was not technically a fighting cry but had the advantage of being extremely rude—the worst plan she'd ever been part of.

She'd been part of worse plans that worked.

### Wren

Wren Ashvale had spent eleven years being told that history was a dead thing, that archives were tombs, that the past was best left buried under the peace like a body under floorboards. She had been expelled for disagreeing. She had been mocked for it. She had watched her life's work burn in a room where no fire was ever officially recorded.

Now she stood on the threshold of the Ember Gate with ink freezing in her pen, writing down names as fast as her hand could move, while a hundred yards away the Order erased people in real time.

It was, she thought grimly, the most alive she had ever felt.

"Hale's flanking left!" Cass shouted over the clash—because there *was* clashing now; some of the Order's rank and file had grown impatient with the polite work of blanking and had drawn steel, and Cass met them like a landslide with opinions. Wren had never seen her fight before. It was horrifying. It was beautiful. It was, Wren admitted, a little bit attractive, which was absolutely not the time.

"Noted!" Wren shouted back, and ducked as a white lantern's light swept past overhead, close enough that she felt it pluck at her thoughts like fingers at a shelf of books, looking for Vael, looking for the gate, looking for the girl who wouldn't stop humming.

Pip was singing.

That was the thing Wren couldn't stop noticing, even in the middle of everything: Pip stood at the threshold itself, small and stubborn in Sable's oversized coat, singing the anthem of Vael in a clear child's voice while the world came apart around her. And every note she sang did something. Where the lullaby fell, the frost bloomed brighter. Where the anthem rose, the remnants steadied—flickering less, standing straighter, becoming briefly, gloriously *more*.

"She's anchoring them," Wren breathed. "Cass! She's anchoring them! The song—it gives them something to hold onto!"

"Then don't let anyone shut her up!" Cass called back, parrying a bayonet with a sound like a struck bell.

Wren turned to the notebooks. Names. She wrote names. The baker: Harl, first name lost, made bread with caraway, hummed while he worked. The lavender woman: Maren? Marren? She had been somebody's mother. The fiddler—she reached for the fiddler and found the page blank where she'd written him, the ink gone, the memory gone, and she sat down hard in the snow and sobbed once, furiously, and then got up and kept writing, because that was the whole of what a historian was: someone who kept writing after the room burned down.

An Order soldier broke through the line near her—young, frightened, his lantern shaking in his grip. He pointed it at her satchel.

"The documents," he said. "Please. I'm sorry. Please give me the documents."

"They're not documents," Wren said, standing, putting herself between him and a hundred years of a dead country. "They're people. Every page is a person. You're not burning paper, boy. You're burning *them*, again, and this time they don't even get to be ash."

His lantern wavered. His eyes were wet. He was maybe nineteen.

Behind him, Cass arrived like weather and put the flat of her blade against his throat. "Walk away," she said, almost kindly. "I've killed four men today and hated every minute, but I'll hate five minutes more if you touch her bag."

He walked away. Most of them, Wren noticed, walked away, given the chance. The Order recruited gentle people and taught them that gentleness was a kind of arithmetic: thousands of living peace weighed against one unwritten country. It was hard to hate them properly. That was the worst part. Elodie had tried to explain it once, sitting by the fire: *the enemy isn't cruel, that's what makes it terrible.* Cruelty you could fight. Arithmetic you could only survive.

A horn sounded from the ridge. The Order's main column began to advance in earnest, lanterns opening one by one, a tide of white light rolling down toward the gate, and Wren grabbed her satchel and ran for the threshold, because whatever happened next, someone had to be close enough to see it, and write it down, and make it true.

### Pip

Pip sang.

She didn't know all the words—some of them weren't words anymore, they were sounds that had worn smooth like sea glass—but the tune lived in her chest the way her heartbeat did, and when she sang it, the cold mountain felt less like a stranger.

The lady with the lavender smell took her hand. Pip squeezed it. The lady's hand was getting thinner, like paper held up to light, but it was still a hand, and it was still holding hers, and Pip decided that counted.

The grey men with the white lights were coming up the road, and Pip wasn't supposed to look at them, Cass had said, *don't you dare look at them, little bit,* but it was hard not to look at things that were eating your friends. Except they weren't eating them. They were *un*-eating them, which was somehow ruder.

So she sang louder.

Her mother used to sing this song. Not the remnant-mother from the dream-house with the blue door—well, yes, actually, her, the dream-mother, the one whose voice was warm bread and whose garden grew flowers Pip could never remember the names of except one, emberbloom, red as a coal. The dream-mother sang it every night in the dream, and every morning Pip woke up with dirt under her nails and the song stuck in her teeth like honey.

Now she understood why. Now she understood a lot of things she didn't have words for yet, the way you understand hot stoves and locked doors and the specific silence of adults deciding things about your life without you.

The song wasn't hers. She was the song's. Vael had sung itself into her before she was born, looking for somewhere soft to land, and Pip had landed here, in this company of strays, and she loved them with her whole entire self, and that was why she stood in the wind and the white light and sang until her throat went raw.

Because you don't let go of your family's song. Even if your family is a whole country. *Especially* then.

The white light washed over the lavender lady, and Pip felt her hand go light, lighter, gone—and Pip cried, big ugly crying, and did not stop singing, not for one single note, because Wren had told her once that songs are how countries remember themselves, and Pip was going to be the remembering if it killed her, which it might, but Cass always said if you're going to do something stupid, do it loud.

Down at the gate, Mr. Elodie put his hands on the blue door.

Pip sang the verse about the garden. The one with the emberblooms.

### Elodie

The blue door was smaller than he remembered, which was true of everything in Vael, because memory shrinks things to fit inside a person, and Elodie was about to become a very crowded house.

Beyond it was not a room. Beyond it was everything: the lamplit streets, the terraced orchards, the harbor town of Vellichor with its bell that rang in thirds, the mountain passes stitched with wildflowers, the library of Ashfall with its ten thousand uncatalogued dreams, the graves, the births, the recipes, the arguments, the jokes, the names—all of it folded into a space the size of a door, waiting for someone to carry it out.

"It will weigh nothing," Sable had told him, or the grandmother had, or both; they were the same voice now, at last, fully herself, standing beside him with her lamp. "And it will weigh everything. Both at once. That's how countries are."

"Just open it," Elodie said. His voice cracked on *just*. "Before I think better of it."

"Elodie." Sable caught his sleeve. Her eyes were wet and steady. "When you go through—when you take it all—look for the emberbloom seeds. Your grandmother planted them the spring before the Unwriting. They'll be in the kitchen jar. Take the jar. A country should get to keep growing somewhere."

He nodded. He couldn't speak. Outside, the white light was climbing the mountainside, and Cass was roaring something unrepeatable, and Pip was singing, God, Pip was singing through her tears, and Wren was writing, always writing, and the remnants stood in their silent hundreds between the Order and the door like a congregation that had finally, after a hundred years, been allowed to attend its own funeral.

Elodie put his hands flat against the blue door.

It was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been waiting a century for someone to knock from the other side, and here he was, the last citizen of Vael, the keeper who had spent his whole life keeping a door shut finally opening the one that mattered.

"A gate is only shut from the side that stays," he whispered—his grandmother's words, his inheritance, his answer. "I'm staying. Just... elsewhere."

He turned the handle.

The door swung inward onto morning.

Light poured out—not white, not blank, but *gold*, the gold of lamplight and harvest and hearth-fire, and with it came the smell of bread and rain-on-stone and woodsmoke, and the faint sound of a bell ringing in thirds, and Elodie felt the country begin to pour into him like water into a cup, street by street, song by song, grave by grave—

—and the Ember Gate behind them blazed like a coal, and the Order's white lanterns guttered, and somewhere in the chaos an inquisitor's voice cried out, not in triumph but in something like awe—

—and the door held, and Elodie held, and Vael held its breath.

One step. That was all it would take. One step, and a hundred years folded into a man, walking out of a country that no longer existed into a morning that did.

He looked back once—at Cass bleeding and grinning, at Wren clutching her notebooks like scripture, at Pip singing with dirt under her nails and tears on her face, at Sable lifting her lamp high, higher, lighting the threshold for him the way she must have lit it a hundred years ago for a boy who stayed.

"Go on, love," Sable said. "We've got the door."

Elodie Coldharbour, last citizen of Vael, took a breath, took the jar of emberbloom seeds from the kitchen that existed only in memory, and stepped through the blue door—carrying home with him, out of the dark, into the morning.

Behind him, the Ember Gate began, slowly, gently, like a long exhale a century overdue, to close.
