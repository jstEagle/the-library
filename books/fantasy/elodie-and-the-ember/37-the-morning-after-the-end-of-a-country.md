## 37. The Morning After the End of a Country

I woke up on the floor of my own taproom with a blanket over me that I hadn't put there, and for one long, merciful moment I didn't remember anything at all.

Then I did.

The mountain was quiet. That was the first thing. Thornfell sits in the lap of the Thornfell range the way a child sleeps against a mother who snores — you stop hearing it after a while, and then one night it stops snoring and you wake up wondering what's wrong. The Ember Gate had hummed under everything for weeks, a note too low to name, and now it was gone, and the silence had a shape to it, like a room after a guest has finally left.

I lay there a while, listening to nothing, and then Pip's feet hit the floorboards overhead — she slept in the loft when we let her, which was every night now, because none of us had the heart or the sense to make her go back to her own bed — and I heard her run to the window, and I heard her stop.

"Cass," she called down through the floor, in the voice children use when they've found something important, which is to say loudly. "Cass, the mountains are just mountains."

I got up. My back informed me that forty-one is not thirty-one, and I told it to file a complaint with someone who cared, and I went to the window.

She was right. That was the strange thing. You'd think a thing like that would be hard to see, but it wasn't; it was as plain as a swept floor. The peaks stood there in the early light, gray and white and ordinary, wearing their snow like they'd worn it for ten thousand years and intended to wear it for ten thousand more. No roads where roads shouldn't be. No valleys folded up like letters. Just stone, and morning fog pooling in the passes, and a bird — some small brown offended bird — yelling at the dawn from the eaves of the stable across the way.

The world had gone back to being only itself. And I found, to my considerable surprise, that this made me want to sit down.

* * *

Elodie was already in the kitchen when I came down, which meant he'd either not slept at all or had slept exactly enough, and with Elodie there was never any telling the difference. He had the kettle on and a notebook open on the table, and he was writing with the particular hunched ferocity of a man trying to get something down before it could change its mind about being true.

"Tea's on," he said, without looking up.

"You look terrible."

"I feel wonderful." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *the roof leaks*, and I believed him, which worried me more than if he'd looked grief-stricken. "It's all still here." He tapped his temple. "All of it. The streets. The songs. There's a woman named Ilvet who made candles on Tallow Row and I never met her and I know the smell of her shop. It's all just — *in* me. Like a coat I'm wearing inside out."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is." He finally looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "And it's mine. Cass, I carried it out so it would be mine. I keep having to remind myself that was the point."

Pip came clattering down the stairs then, hair like a crow's nest, and climbed straight into the chair beside him, because that was hers now, apparently, the way certain chairs become yours in houses that take you in. She looked at the notebook.

"Did you write the garden?"

"I wrote the garden."

"The blue door garden? With the beans?"

"With the beans. Chapter six."

"Read it to me after breakfast."

"After breakfast," he agreed, and she nodded with the gravity of a magistrate, and I turned away to fuss with the kettle because my eyes were doing something I didn't care to have witnessed before I'd had tea.

That was the morning, mostly. Tea, and bread, and Pip demanding chapters, and Wren arriving at half past eight with ink on her fingers and three notebooks under her arm and the expression of a woman who had slept two hours and considered it an extravagance. She sat down, looked at Elodie's notebook, looked at her own, and said, "We're going to need more paper," and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

The Order left the way a fever breaks — slowly, then all at once.

They'd been camped at the mouth of the pass for two days, gray tents, gray men, waiting on word from their inquisitor, and on the third morning I walked down to the market square and found them striking camp with the brisk, unbothered efficiency of people whose appointment has been cancelled.

He was standing by the well. The inquisitor — Serel, his name was, though he'd never quite come out and say so; I'd had to pry it out of him over two cups of tea and the worst hour of his life — and he looked, I thought, lighter. Not happy. Lighter. A man setting down luggage he'd carried so long he'd forgotten which shoulder it bruised.

He saw me coming and inclined his head. Polite to the last. That was always the unnerving thing about him; he'd have apologized beautifully while erasing your grandmother.

"Master Quill," he said. He'd taken to calling me that since I'd refused, twice, to give him my real surname, and I'd stopped correcting him because it saved time. "You're up early."

"Trade waits for no one. Even the morning after the end of a country." I leaned on the well beside him. "You're leaving, then."

"The warrant is retired." He said it simply, no ceremony. "There is nothing left to blank. The gate is sealed beyond any craft of ours — believe me, we tested. Corven spent four hours yesterday trying to find the pass road and couldn't." A pause. "The mountain declined to be found. I respect that in a mountain."

"And Vael?"

"Vael," he said, and looked up at the peaks, and for a moment something moved behind his face that I suspected he'd deny under oath — something like envy, maybe, or grief for a thing he'd spent his life being the enemy of. "Vael is wherever your keeper keeps it. Which is, I'm told, in a notebook. In a taproom. In a town that smells of fish and tar." He almost smiled. "The Order spent a century ensuring that countries live and die by what's written down. It seems fitting that the last one will live by it too."

"He'll write all of it," I said. "Every street. Every dead person. He remembers names I never heard and he's writing them down like he owes them money."

"Good." He said it quietly, and I believed him, and that was the strangest part of the whole strange season — that I believed him. "For what it's worth, Master Quill — and it isn't worth much — I'm glad it ended this way. We were prepared to burn a great deal. Burning is easy. This—" he gestured vaguely at the square, the gulls, the ordinary outrage of the morning, "—this required someone to carry it instead. Carrying is harder. The Order has never been good at it."

He pushed off the well and gathered his cloak, and then paused, and reached into his coat, and held out a small paper packet.

"What's that?"

"The names." His voice went careful. "Of the peace's dead. I brought them once before, as an argument. I find I no longer need them as one. But they should be written somewhere, with the rest. They belong to the same story now, whether anyone likes it or not."

I took the packet. It was heavier than paper has any right to be.

"He'll want to know your name," I said. "For the record. He's fussy about the record."

Serel considered that. Considered, I think, a hundred years of blank pages, and what it might mean to sign one instead.

"Tellerin," he said at last. "Inquisitor Tellerin. Formerly." He bowed, actually bowed, small and dry and sincere, and walked off down the pass road with his gray men falling in behind him, and Thornfell let him go, because Thornfell has always known the difference between an enemy and a man doing a job nobody else would, and besides, the fish weren't going to salt themselves.

* * *

The town felt different that day, and it took me most of it to work out how.

Lighter, obviously. The Gate had pressed on everyone — even folk who'd never known it existed had been sleeping badly, snapping at their children, salting their fish crooked. Now the pressure was gone, and people moved through the streets like travelers whose packs had been quietly lightened while they dozed.

But sadder too. That was the part I kept turning over. Old Marta at the chandlery cried into her tallow vats and couldn't tell you why. The ferryman played a tune on his pipe that he swore he'd never learned, and got it wrong in the same place every time, and looked like he might weep each time he missed the note. Half the town woke with the ghost-feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue, and the word wasn't there anymore, and wouldn't ever be again except in one notebook in my taproom.

They'd never known Vael. Not one of them. But something had brushed against them these past weeks — a country reaching for the world through a crack in a door — and now it had drawn back, and you can miss a thing you never had. Anyone who's buried someone knows that. Grief doesn't check its paperwork.

So I did what I do. I opened the taproom early, put out the good cups, and let them come.

They came all day. Fishermen and salt-wives and the harbormaster and two rival smugglers who normally wouldn't share a tide, sitting shoulder to shoulder drinking in a silence that wasn't uncomfortable so much as full. Nobody knew what they were mourning. Everybody mourned. Around noon old Marta raised her cup and said, "To whatever it was," and the whole room drank to whatever it was, and that was the finest toast I ever heard given in my establishment, and I include the night the harbor fleet came home safe in '19.

Wren sat in the corner taking it all down, naturally. She'd gotten that look — the one that used to curdle into obsession and now just glowed, steady as a banked fire. "A town mourning a country it never knew," she murmured, mostly to her notebook. "Do you know what this does to every theory of collective memory?"

"No," I said, "but I know what it does to my wine stocks, so drink accordingly."

* * *

Evening came down soft. Pip fell asleep at the table mid-sentence — she'd been explaining, at length, that when she grew up she was going to be a keeper, or possibly a smuggler, or possibly both, which Elodie said was a fine tradition in this company — and I carried her up to the loft, and she stirred just enough to ask, sleep-thick, whether the lady with the lamp was all right.

"She's keeping watch," I said. "Best watcher there is."

"Good," said Pip, and was gone again, dirt still faintly under her fingernails from the garden she'd helped Elodie mark out that afternoon, behind the rebuilt blue door, where next spring things would grow that hadn't grown anywhere in a hundred years.

I stood at the loft window a while after that. The mountains were black shapes against the stars, and they were only mountains, and the pass road wound down empty toward the lowlands, and somewhere below it all, behind stone that would never open again, a lamp burned in lamplit streets that lived now in one tired man's memory and one historian's notebooks and one child's lullaby.

Lighter and sadder. Both at once. I'd thought they cancelled out, but they don't; they stack.

Downstairs, Elodie was still writing. I could hear the scratch of the pen through the floor, patient as a heartbeat. Tomorrow he'd plant the garden properly, and Wren would start on the fair-copy, and the town would go back to fish and tar and forgetting to be sad, because that's what towns do — the world continuing, which is the bravest thing the world knows how to do.

I blew out my candle and went down for one last cup, and found Elodie at the table with his pen paused, looking at a fresh page.

"Any trouble?" I asked.

"No." He turned the notebook toward me. At the top, in his careful hand: *Chapter 38.* Underneath, a single line, and beneath the line, a question mark.

*I taught the town the lullaby today,* it read. *But someone in the back of the taproom was already humming it — and getting it right.*

I looked up at him. He looked back at me, and outside, far off in the dark, some small brown bird started singing a song no one had taught it.
