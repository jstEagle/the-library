## 40. Chapter 40

The tea was going cold again.

Elodie noticed this the way he noticed most things now — with a kind of fond exasperation, as though the cup were an old friend who refused to learn better habits. He set it on the windowsill of the rebuilt blue door's kitchen, where the morning sun could argue with it, and went back to the page in front of him.

*The Vaeli calendar had thirteen months,* he wrote. *The last one, Embermonth, belonged to no season. It existed so that every year would contain one month that belonged to no one — not to planting, not to harvest, not to war. A month the country kept for itself.*

He read it back and frowned. It wasn't wrong. But it wasn't right either, and after a year of writing, Elodie had learned the difference. Facts were easy. The archives' worth of knowledge folded inside him came out clean and orderly whenever he asked it to. What was hard was the smell of Embermonth — woodsmoke and wet wool, the way the whole country seemed to exhale at once, strangers nodding to each other in the street with the particular courtesy of people sharing a secret from the rest of time.

You couldn't write a smell. You could only write around it and hope the reader leaned close enough to feel the warmth.

"You're doing the face," said Pip from the doorway.

"What face?"

"The one where you're mad at the paper." She came in on a tide of cold air and garden mud, eleven years old now and taller by a scandalous amount, her hair tied back with a strip of blue ribbon that had once, a hundred years ago, been a curtain tie in a farmhouse that no longer stood anywhere but here. "Wren says when you make the face it means you're trying to put a mountain in a teacup."

"Wren says a great many accurate things."

"She also says breakfast is ready and if you let your tea go cold one more time she's going to start writing *that* down too."

Elodie looked at the cup on the sill. Steam still rose from it, faintly, stubbornly. He had no memory of refilling it. He glanced at Pip, whose expression was the picture of innocence, an expression she had been practicing for years and had never once successfully deployed.

"Thank you," he said gravely.

"You're welcome," she said, and fled before gratitude could turn into chores.

* * *

Breakfast at the blue door was a crowded affair, which was the whole point of it.

Cass sat at the head of the table because Cass always sat at the head of the table, a fact she had established in the first week through sheer force of personality and had defended since through the strategic placement of her boots. She was reading a manifest, or pretending to; her eyes flicked up every few seconds to count heads, an old smuggler's habit that had never left her and that everyone pretended not to notice. Wren occupied the corner nearest the fire with three notebooks open at once, eating with one hand and annotating with the other, occasionally stabbing her fork toward the ceiling to punctuate some private argument with a historian two centuries dead.

"Say it again," Wren demanded, without preamble, the moment Elodie sat down. "The thing about Embermonth."

"Good morning to you too."

"Morning, yes, the thing about Embermonth."

He gave it to her — the exhale, the woodsmoke, the courtesy of strangers — and watched her write it down in a shorthand only she could read, and felt the small familiar click of a memory finding its place outside himself. This was how it worked now. He carried Vael, and Vael leaked out of him daily into notebooks and conversations and songs, and every leak made him lighter instead of emptier. His grandmother had understood that, he thought. Her letters had tried to tell him. A gate is only shut from the side that stays — but a memory is only safe when it lives in more than one place.

Pip, mouth full of bread, said: "Tell the one about the ferryman."

"I've told that one four times."

"Tell it five."

So he told it five — old Brannoc who poled the river ferry at Vael's heart and charged no coin but a story, and how the story had to be new each crossing or he'd know, he always knew, and how children used to spend their whole Embermonths inventing lies elaborate enough to fool him. Pip laughed in all the right places even though she'd heard it four times, because Pip understood something about stories that Elodie had spent forty chapters of his life learning: that the telling was never the same twice, and neither were you.

Cass lowered her manifest. "Brannoc," she said. "Was he real? Actually real? Because I've been using him in negotiations. *I'll take the story-price, like old Brannoc.* Very effective. If he turns out to be invented I'm going to feel like a fraud."

"He was real. He overcharged."

"Then I'm not a fraud, I'm a scholar." She went back to her manifest, satisfied, and under the table her boot found Elodie's foot and rested there, briefly, the way she'd done since the night she'd come back before dawn with a child's drawing in her pocket and never once mentioned it. Some things in this house were never discussed and were therefore load-bearing.

* * *

Midmorning brought travelers.

They came up the Thornfell road in ones and twos now, more than they used to — mapmakers chasing rumors, pilgrims of no particular faith, a growing trickle of the simply curious who had heard there was a town at the edge of the mountains where a man would tell you about a country that wasn't there. The Order no longer stopped them. The Order, these days, mostly sent observers who took notes and left politely, and once — memorably — had sent the inquisitor himself, retired now, gray at the temples, who had stood in Elodie's garden for a long moment looking at the blue door and then said only, "It suits you," and paid for his tea with exact change and perfect courtesy.

Today's travelers were three: a young cartographer, an old woman with a walking stick, and a boy of perhaps fourteen who hung back the way boys do when they've been dragged somewhere by an adult who won't explain why.

Elodie poured the tea himself. He'd learned that the telling went better standing up, moving, hands busy — a keeper's instinct repurposed, the same body language he'd once used pacing the gate corridor at night.

"You're the keeper," the cartographer said. Not quite a question.

"I was a keeper. Now I'm more of a... librarian." He smiled. "Of one book. That I am also writing. It's a very efficient arrangement."

"And the country — Vael — it's really gone? Sealed? There's nothing behind the mountain?"

"There's mountain behind the mountain," Elodie said. "That's all there's ever been, for anyone who isn't me. The gate shut a year ago and it will stay shut. Whatever's on the other side now isn't a country anymore. It's a memory, and memories don't need roads."

The old woman with the stick spoke for the first time. Her voice was thin and careful, the voice of someone rationing words. "My grandmother used to hum a song. Nobody knew it. We thought she made it up." Her hands tightened on the stick. "Is it yours?"

Elodie's chest did the thing it always did — the brief, bright ache, the ember-flare. "Sing it," he said gently. "Or hum it. However much you have."

She hummed. Four notes, wavering, half-remembered, passed down through a century of grandmothers who didn't know what they were carrying.

It was the lullaby. The first phrase of it, anyway — the descending line, the one like a door easing closed.

Behind him, softly, Pip picked it up and completed the phrase, and then the second, and the old woman's eyes went wide and wet and she gripped her stick and laughed and cried at once, and the boy who'd been hanging back stepped forward despite himself, drawn, the way everyone was drawn, the way Elodie had been drawn to a humming door a lifetime ago.

"That's it," the old woman whispered. "That's it exactly. What is it?"

"It's a lullaby from Vael," Elodie said. "It means *the lamp stays lit until you're home.*"

"Will you teach me? All of it?"

"Pip," Elodie said, "the board."

And Pip — grinning, ceremonial, this being the best part of her week — fetched the slate from its hook by the door, the one where they wrote the teaching hours like a shop writes its prices. Under *Lullaby: mornings, free* she added today's date, and the old woman laughed again, and the boy asked his first question — *did they really have a month that belonged to nobody?* — and the telling began, the way it began every day, the embers passing hand to hand.

This was the third door. Not stone, not prophecy. Just this: a kitchen, a kettle, a slate, a song. A gate that opened both ways after all — inward, into the past, and outward, into whoever was willing to carry a little of it home.

* * *

Evening found him alone in the garden, which was rare enough to be savored.

The garden was coming in well, its second spring. He'd planted it from memory — Vaeli seeds that weren't seeds, that had come out of him the way everything else had, pressed into Thornfell soil on a hunch and a prayer. Half of it hadn't taken. The other half had come up strange and lovely: silver-leafed herbs that smelled of rain on hot stone, a climbing flower the color of dusk that the bees had adopted immediately and without paperwork. And in the corner, blooming for the first time this week, the ember-lilies — small, gold-hearted, the flowers his grandmother had grown, the ones in the letters, the ones she'd described so carefully across a hundred years precisely so that a boy who stayed might one day plant them.

He knelt by them. He didn't pray, exactly. He talked, the way you talk to someone who has become the place they loved.

"They're up," he said. "All six. Pip counted them twice a day like they might change their minds. Wren wants a cutting for pressing and I told her absolutely not, and then I gave her one, obviously. Cass says the garden proves I'm getting soft. Cass says this about everything I do that isn't smuggling." He paused. "Sable keeps the lamp lit. I can't prove it. But the frost never comes past the garden wall anymore, and I choose to believe that's her, minding the threshold, the way we agreed. The way keepers do."

The dusk-flower nodded in a wind he couldn't feel.

Footsteps on gravel — unhurried, familiar, deliberately audible, because Cass had learned years ago that sneaking up on a former gatekeeper was a good way to get a trowel thrown at you. She settled onto the wall beside the lilies, manifest-less for once, hands wrapped around a mug of the tea Pip kept resurrecting.

"Town meeting voted," she said. "They want to name the square."

"We have a square?"

"We have the bit in front of the tavern where everyone trips over the cart wheel. They want to name it. There's a shortlist." She produced a scrap of paper with relish. "*Coldharbour Square* is winning. Wren is campaigning hard for *Ashvale Archive Gardens*, which is not a square, is not gardens, and contains her own name, which Wren insists is scholarly objectivity."

"And Pip?"

"Pip proposed *Embermonth*. For the month that belongs to nobody." Cass looked out at the darkening mountains. "For the square that belongs to everybody. She's eleven and she out-argued the entire council. Took her nine minutes. I timed it."

Elodie laughed, and it turned, halfway, into something else — not grief, exactly. Grief had been given its shape long ago, in a high pass, in a ruined kitchen, in a doorway between worlds. This was the thing grief became when you carried it long enough and shared it widely enough. Gratitude with weight to it.

"Coldharbour Square," he said. "Imagine. A year ago I was a man whose name meant *keep your head down.*"

"A year ago you were a man whose tea was always cold."

"Some things are eternal."

She drank. The mountains stood against the last light, only mountains, finally and forever only mountains, and yet — he had learned this too — not empty. Nothing was empty anymore. Every fold of those hills held a street or a song or a name he'd given back to the world, and every traveler on the road below carried a piece away, and every piece grew.

"One more thing," Cass said, and now her voice had the particular casualness she reserved for things that mattered enormously. "There's a ship comes through next month. Smuggling run, legitimate-ish, long route. South along the coast, then east." She examined her mug. "Pip's been asking about the sea. Never seen it. Neither have I, properly, and I'll admit the professional curiosity is killing me." A shrug, elaborately indifferent. "Thought maybe the famous librarian could be persuaded to close his library for a few weeks. Take notes. On, say, whether anyone out there hums four notes of anything."

Elodie looked at her. Cass looked magnificently anywhere else.

"You're asking me to go on holiday."

"I'm asking you to conduct field research."

"With my family."

"If you insist on that word." But her ears had gone pink, and her boot had found his foot again under the fading light, load-bearing as ever.

He looked back at the mountains. At the garden. At the lit window of the blue door, where Wren's shadow gestured wildly at Pip's smaller shadow, mid-lesson, mid-argument, mid-life. Somewhere beyond the ridge, sealed and silent and safe, a lamp burned on a threshold, tended by a woman who had chosen to stay so that he could choose to leave — and he carried her with him the way he carried everything now, not as a weight but as a warmth.

"Yes," he said. "All right. But I'm bringing the notebook."

"You're always bringing the notebook."

"And the lily seeds. For the sea air. We'll see if they travel."

Cass grinned, and got up, and called toward the window that dinner was happening whether the scholars liked it or not, and the house answered with noise and motion and the clatter of a family assembling itself around a table set, at last, for exactly the number of people it held.

Elodie stayed a moment longer in the garden, in the dark, among the ember-lilies with their small gold hearts.

*Stay is a door too,* his grandmother had said. And *go*, he understood now, finishing the thought she'd left him, *is just another word for carrying it further.*

Tomorrow there would be travelers, and tea, and the telling. Tonight there was dinner.

He went inside, and the lamp stayed lit until everyone was home.
