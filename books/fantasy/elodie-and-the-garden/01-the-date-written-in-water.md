## 1. The Date Written in Water

The valley had no name worth keeping, and no magic either. Coral Underhill had been born into both absences and had never once thought of them as a pair until the night the scholar came.

He was sharpening a blade that wasn't his when the knock came—three soft raps against the shrine door, the kind a polite man makes when he already knows he's unwelcome. Coral set the whetstone down. The blade belonged to a farmer upriver who'd paid half in advance to have his brother-in-law discouraged from a second courtship. Small work. Valley work. The only kind there was.

"Closed," he said, though the shrine was never closed. It had been his grandmother's before it was his mother's, before it was his—a single stone room at the valley's throat where the god of oaths had once been heard and now was not. A bowl for offerings. A shelf for candles burned down to pools. A silence so old it had grown its own weight.

The door opened anyway. Scholars, Coral would learn, were like weather: they did not wait for permission.

She was young, or young the way ravens are young—sharp-eyed, dust-cloaked, with a satchel that clinked like it carried more books than coin. She looked past him immediately, the way people always did, taking inventory of the shrine: the cold hearth, the godless altar, the sword rack where his sworn blade hung wrapped in grey cloth.

"You're Underhill," she said. "Coral."

"That depends on who's asking."

"No one." She set her satchel on the altar without asking leave, which should have made him angry and instead made something in his chest go quiet and attentive, like a dog hearing a far-off bell. "I'm asking a book. May I?"

She drew it out before he answered. It was small, bound in leather gone the color of old tea, and heavy in the wrong way—heavy the way wet things are heavy. When she opened it on the altar, the pages breathed. Coral felt it against his face, a faint exhalation smelling of rain on stone, of the mountain air above the tree line, of places he had never been because magic could not follow him there.

"This is a century old," she said. "Older than your grandmother. Older than the valley's founding, if you read the marginalia right. It fell out of a peddler's cart two winters ago and I've spent every season since learning its grammar." Her finger moved down a page dense with script that shifted when Coral tried to look at it directly, letters swimming like fish under ice. "It's a book of prophecies. Most of them are nonsense. Dates and names and riddles about kings I've never heard of. But this one—"

She turned the book toward him.

The page had a date inked in water. Not written—in *inked*, the way frost ferns a window, the characters blooming pale blue across the parchment as though the paper itself remembered being a river. And beneath the date, in a hand steadier than any scholar's:

*The mercenary of the silent god descends on this day. Thirteen turning moons. He will steal what the court beneath keeps.*

Coral read it twice. The date was today.

"That's not possible," he said, and his voice came out flat and professional, the voice he used when men tried to cheat him at contract. "This valley has no magic. Nothing crosses the boundary. That's the whole point of the place. People come here to be *safe*."

"I know." The scholar's eyes were bright and pitiless. "I checked the boundary myself. Three times. Whatever's written in this book doesn't care about your valley's rules, Master Underhill, because your valley's rules were written by someone else entirely. Someone who owed a debt." She tapped the page. "Look at the last line again."

He looked. Beneath the prophecy, smaller, almost an afterthought, the water-ink had gathered into one more sentence:

*He was always meant to leave.*

Something turned over in Coral's chest—not fear, exactly. Fear he knew how to carry; it rode with him on every contract, sat quiet in the saddle, kept his hands steady. This was different. This was the feeling of a lock he'd never noticed finally admitting it had been a door.

"Get out," he said.

The scholar closed the book gently, reverently, and left it on the altar. "It won't stay shut now that you've read it," she said. "I'm sorry. Truly. I thought—I hoped it might be someone else's date. But the book knows its reader." She paused at the threshold. "For what it's worth, the grammar suggests the descent isn't a punishment. Prophecies like this are usually *rescues*. Someone down there is waiting to be stolen back."

Then she was gone, boots crunching down the shrine path, and Coral stood alone with a book that breathed and a date that was today and a silence from his god that suddenly sounded less like absence and more like holding its breath.

* * *

He did the sensible things first. He finished the farmer's job by dusk—a single conversation across a fence line, the brother-in-law going pale and agreeable, no blood spilled—and collected his fee in eggs and silver. He ate. He banked the shrine fire. He wrote no letter, because there was no one to write to; his mother had died believing the god's silence was a test, and his grandmother before her had died believing it was grief, and Coral had inherited both beliefs along with the shrine and the wrapped blade, and had never once asked himself which lie fit better.

That was the thing about the valley. It didn't just keep magic out. It kept questions out. You could live sixty years inside its borders and never wonder why the god of oaths had nothing to say to the people who kept his shrine most faithfully.

Now, with the book open on the altar and the water-ink glowing faintly in the dark like a vein of sky, Coral wondered everything at once.

"Why him?" he asked the silence. "Why me?"

The silence answered the way it always answered: with weight, not words. But tonight the weight had a shape. Tonight it felt like a hand pressed flat between his shoulder blades, patient as a landslide.

He unwrapped the sworn blade. Grey cloth, grey steel, an oath cut into the fuller in letters too worn to read—the same oath his grandmother had sworn, and her mother, service given to the silent god in exchange for the valley's protection. He'd held it at nine years old and been told it was an honor. He'd held it at nineteen and been told it was a duty. He had never been told what it cost, or who had paid, or why the god who took their service had gone voiceless before Coral was born.

The blade hummed under his fingers. Not magic—the valley forbade that—but memory. Steel remembers hands.

"All right," Coral said aloud, to the book, to the god, to whatever waited beneath a mountain he'd never seen. "Thirteen turning moons. I'll count them."

The candle flames leaned toward the altar, all together, like grass bending under wind.

* * *

Midnight came the way water comes through a cracked dam—at first a seep, then a sound, then everything.

Coral woke at the shrine desk, cheek on the prophecy book, having sworn he wouldn't sleep. The water-ink had spread. The date glowed like a lantern sunk in a pond, and the words beneath it had begun to move, crawling off the page in threads of pale blue light that wound around his wrists like a merciful man tying a prisoner's hands.

He should have fought it. Some part of him catalogued all the ways a professional would fight it—blade angles, exits, the window behind the altar—and dismissed each one, because the light didn't feel like an enemy. It felt like an appointment finally being honored. It felt like relief, and the relief frightened him more than dread ever had.

The boundary of the valley passed through him like cold water through cloth. He felt the moment magic touched him for the first time in thirty years of life: a shiver up the spine, a taste of iron and honey, the sudden unbearable loudness of a world that had been muffled all along. Somewhere far below, impossibly, a god cleared its throat—or began to—and said nothing still, but the *nothing* changed key.

The shrine dissolved around him. Stone became starlight became the smell of high mountain passes, and Coral Underhill fell upward into a dark full of turning moons, thirteen of them, counting themselves aloud in a language older than oaths.

The last thing he heard, as the valley let him go, was the book's final line whispering itself against his ear in a voice that was almost—but not quite—his own:

*He was always meant to leave.*

And beneath the mountain, in halls he could not yet see, something that had once been a gardener lifted her head.
