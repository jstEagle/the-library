## 2. Thirteen Turns Beneath

The valley let him go the way a fist opens—slowly, finger by finger, as if reluctant to admit it had been holding anything at all.

Coral fell through a seam in the night and landed on stone that was not valley stone. It was warm. It breathed. Above him, where the stars should have been, hung roots: pale, thick as rivers, descending into darkness with the patience of things that had never known weather.

He stood. His sword was still at his hip—the sworn blade, the one he'd knelt with at fourteen while his father's hand pressed his shoulder and the god's silence pressed everything else. The blade hummed faintly now. In the valley it had never made a sound. Here, in the world where magic lived, it sang like a struck bell that couldn't decide to stop.

*Thirteen turns,* the prophecy had said, inked in water that never dried. *Thirteen turning moons beneath, and what was sold shall be reclaimed.*

He began to walk down.

The mountain's halls were not carved; they were grown. Coral passed through corridors of living rock ribbed like throats, lit by veins of light that pulsed slow as sleeping hearts. Doors appeared when he needed them and were gone when he looked back. He counted his steps because counting was prayer, and prayer was all his god had left him.

On the first turn of the moon—a disc of white stone that rolled past an opening far above, vast and deliberate as an eye closing—he found the first memory.

It lay on the floor like spilled milk, silver and shallow, and when he stepped in it, he was elsewhere.

* * *

*A garden. Terraced rows going gold with late summer. A woman on her knees in the dirt, sleeves rolled, dark hair escaping its braid. She is talking to the tomatoes the way some people talk to horses—firmly, affectionately, as if they might argue.*

*Coral knows her. Of course he knows her. Elodie, who tended the Underhill land before he could hold a rake, who taught him which weeds were liars and which were only lonely. She looks up as if she hears him thinking, though this is memory and he is nothing but a ghost standing in it.*

*"You'll want to be brave about this," she says—to someone behind him, or to herself, or to the soil. "Bravery's just grief you've decided to use."*

*She stands. Brushes off her knees. Beyond the garden wall, the sky is the wrong color—the bruised violet of something approaching—and Coral understands, with a lurch, that he is seeing the year the sickness came. The year half the valley's children stopped growing. The year the bargains started.*

*"One soul," she says quietly. "For the whole valley's harvest. That's cheap. That's insultingly cheap." Her laugh cracks in the middle. "But then, they know I'll pay it. That's how they set the price."*

*The memory shivers and drains away between the stones, and Coral is alone again in the humming dark, his heart doing something ugly against his ribs.*

* * *

He walked for what felt like days. The moons turned overhead—second, third, fourth—white discs rolling their slow circuit through openings in the mountain's crown, and each turn brought another pool of memory, and every pool was hers.

He learned to dread them and to seek them out in the same breath.

In the fifth, Elodie stood before a door of black oak beneath this very mountain, negotiating with something that never showed its face. Its voice came from everywhere, courteous as frost: *The valley's magic must have a keeper outside it. The valley cannot hold both its dead and its dreams. Choose what we take, gardener, and we will spare the rest.*

*I choose myself,* she said. *My soul. Take it, keep it, feed on it—I don't care what you do with it. But the price comes out of your own hide too, court. You'll go quiet in that valley. Your voice, your reach, your little miracles—all of it, sealed behind the bargain. Nothing of yours touches that soil again. That's my term. That's the term.*

*A pause, long enough for Coral's ghost-heart to stop.*

*Agreed,* said the frost voice.

*And Elodie laughed—not bitterly, but with a kind of terrible delight, the laugh of a woman who has just won a game whose prize is losing.* "Then it's done," she said. "Seal it in oaths. I know how you love oaths."

* * *

On the seventh turn, Coral stopped walking and was sick against the living stone.

Because he understood now. He understood what the silence was.

His god had never abandoned the valley. His god had been *sealed out*—shut beyond the border of Elodie's bargain, locked on the far side of a door she'd closed to save everyone, paying for it with the only coin the court accepted. Every prayer Coral had ever whispered into that silence, every vigil he'd kept at the inherited shrine, every night he'd knelt on cold flagstones begging for a sign—none of it had ever been heard. Not because the god was distant.

Because Elodie had built the wall.

His loyalty wasn't faith. It was a debt. His whole life—the mercenary work, the shrine-tending, the blade he'd sworn on—it was all interest paid on a bargain struck before he was born, by a woman who'd traded her soul so that a valley full of strangers could keep dreaming.

And no one had told him. No one had told *anyone*. They'd let generations kneel to a silence and call it holiness.

"Elodie," he said aloud, and the halls drank the name and gave back nothing.

* * *

The memories changed as he descended. The early ones were bright—her hands in soil, her voice teaching children the names of herbs—but deeper down they grayed. In the ninth turn he watched her sit in a cell of soft dark, folding her hands, growing still in the way gardens grow still in winter. Not dying. *Waiting.* Making peace with a thing she should never have made peace with.

"They think I'm a saint," she said to the dark, in the eleventh memory, and her voice had worn thin as old cloth. "They'll build me a story with a happy ending. Stories are cheaper than truth. Let them."

"I don't want the story," Coral said to the empty hall, and his voice cracked on it. "I want you home."

The twelfth moon rose above the last opening, enormous, close enough that he could see the grain of the stone it was carved from, and below him the corridor opened at last into light.

The throne of oaths stood at the center of a cavern like the inside of a bell: walls curved and golden-dark, hung with thousands of chains, and from every chain hung a small glass vessel, and in every vessel burned a flame that was not fire. Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one labeled in script that moved like water.

And on the throne sat the court—or the shape the court wore for visitors: tall, robed in shadow, crowned with a ring of small turning moons.

"You've come far, mercenary," it said, in the courteous frost-voice he knew from her memory. "Thirteen turns is a long walk for a man with no magic."

"I've come for a soul." His hand found his blade. It was singing now, loud, urgent, a hymn with nowhere to go. "Elodie's. The gardener. Sold under false pretenses—she didn't know what your keeping would cost her."

"She knew exactly." The court leaned forward, and its crown-moons spun faster. "That is what makes her soul so nourishing. Peace, freely given, ripens beautifully."

"Then I'm here to spoil the harvest."

The court studied him, and something almost like amusement moved through its shadow. "Bold. Very well, mercenary. We will offer you the courtesy we offer few: a trade. Walk away, and we return to you what your valley lost. Your god's voice, unsealed. Every prayer answered. Your silence ended—your inheritance restored, whole and holy, for the rest of your life. All you must do is leave the gardener where she hangs."

The chains rustled. Somewhere among those thousand flames, one of them flickered—small, stubborn, green-gold, like late summer light on terraced rows.

Coral thought of kneeling on cold flagstones. Of a lifetime of answered prayers. Of everything he had been raised to want.

He drew the sworn blade.

"No," he said. "I have a different trade in mind."

And before the throne could speak, before the frost-voice could name its terms, Coral Underhill turned the blade in his hands—his inheritance, his oath, his father's hand on his shoulder, four generations of borrowed faith—and broke it across his knee.

The sound went through the cavern like the end of the world.

Every chain shivered. Every flame flared. And the court rose from its throne for the first time in a hundred years, its crown-moons spinning wild, its frost-voice finally, finally uncertain:

"What," it said, "*are you offering?*"
