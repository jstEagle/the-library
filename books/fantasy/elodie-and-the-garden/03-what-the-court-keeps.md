## 3. What the Court Keeps

The twelfth moon turned overhead like a coin someone had forgotten to spend, and Coral climbed the last stair of the mountain with his hands empty and his heart full of knives.

He had counted the turns the way his father had taught him to count watch shifts—by breath, by ache, by the small betrayals of his own body. Thirteen moons between the valley and the court beneath. Twelve behind him now. The thirteenth waited at the top of this stair, vast and pale as a buried skull, and around it the halls of the court opened like the inside of a shell: walls of nacre, floors that gave slightly underfoot, warm as skin. Everything here was alive in the way a mouth is alive.

*Elodie*, he thought, and the thought had weight now. Not a name from a prophecy. A woman he remembered without knowing he remembered—kneeling in his mother's garden when he was six, showing him how to tell a weed from a seedling by the sound it made coming out of the ground. *Listen. The weeds come up angry.*

He had spent eleven moons learning what she had bought for the valley with her anger and her patience and her soul. He had spent those same moons learning what his god's silence was made of.

It was made of her.

* * *

Elodie heard him coming the way she heard everything now—not through ears, but through the oaths. The court beneath the mountain ran on them the way a mill runs on water, and after a century of service she could feel every promise in the place like currents against her legs. Somewhere far above, a merchant swore he'd repay a debt by spring. Somewhere below, a lesser thing of the court renewed its fealty to the throne. And climbing the last stair, steady as a heartbeat: a man who had been sworn to silence since before his birth, carrying an oath so old it had fossilized into faith.

She knew his name. She had known it before the prophecy book did.

*Coral Underhill.* She had planted marigolds along his family's fence line. She had taught his mother to graft pears. She had held him once, briefly, when he was small enough to fit against her shoulder, and he had grabbed her braid with a fist like a burr, and she had laughed, and that laugh had been one of the last things she'd owned outright before she signed herself away.

They would offer him something. She knew that too. The court always offered the rescuers something—it was how the court fed. Hope was just an oath you hadn't signed yet.

She sat in the garden the court kept for her, because even a prison wants its prisoner comfortable, and tended the pale flowers that grew there without sun. They were beautiful. That was the cruelest thing about the court; everything down here was beautiful, and none of it was free. She had made peace with all of it decades ago—the bargain, the walls, the slow erosion of self that came from being *kept*. Peace was easier than hope. Peace didn't cost anything extra.

But her hands, tending the flowers, were not steady.

* * *

The throne room had no doors. Coral walked in through a gap in the wall that closed behind him like water.

The throne of oaths stood on a dais of fused promises—centuries of vows compressed until they shone like jet—and on it sat the court itself, which had no face but many voices, which wore its majesty the way a spider wears a web. Around the dais, the courtiers drifted: tall, courteous things with mouths full of other people's words.

"Coral Underhill," said the throne, in a voice assembled from a hundred borrowed throats. "You have come twelve moons for a soul."

"I've come thirteen," Coral said. "I'm not leaving without her."

"Thirteen is ours." The voice rippled with something like amusement. "You stand in our hall on our moon. Do you know what we keep here, mercenary? Not gold. Not flesh. We keep *certainty*. Every oath ever sworn beneath this mountain comes home to us eventually, and we keep them safe from their makers." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Including yours."

Something cold moved through Coral's chest. "My god doesn't swear oaths. My god is silent."

"Your god is silent," the throne agreed, "because your god *paid*. When the gardener sold herself to save your valley, someone had to hold the price. Your god took the debt into its own throat and has not spoken since. Every prayer you ever whispered went unanswered because your god was busy being collateral." The courtiers leaned closer, gentle as vultures. "We can give the voice back. One word from us, and your god speaks again. Bells will ring in your shrine. Prayers will be answered. All you must do is walk back up the stairs alone, and leave the gardener where she is. She made her peace long ago. Why should you unmake it?"

And there it was—the trap, dressed as mercy. Coral felt it settle over him like a familiar coat. His whole life had been this offer in miniature: stay loyal, stay silent, inherit the debt and call it devotion.

He thought of his father kneeling at the shrine. Of the scholar's finger on the prophecy, inked a century before his birth. Of Elodie's hand on a child's head, saying *listen, the weeds come up angry.*

"No," Coral said.

"You would refuse your god's voice?"

"I'd refuse *your* version of it." He drew his sworn blade—the blade he'd carried since he was seventeen, blessed in the godless valley, the one object in his life that had never lied to him about what it was. "You feed on oaths. So hear mine, and choke on it."

He raised the sword high, so the whole court could see.

"I, Coral Underhill, break my oath of silence and my oath of service, given to a god I did not choose, inherited from a lie. I trade—" and here his voice caught, because he understood the price even as he said it, "—I trade my name. My remembered name. Take it. Take every memory anyone holds of who I was. Give her soul back, and let me be no one."

The blade came down on the dais of fused oaths and shattered—not bending, not breaking clean, but *shattering*, into a thousand shards that sang as they fell, each fragment a vow unmade. The sound went through the court like fire through dry grass. Courtiers recoiled. The throne's many voices spoke at once, and for the first time in a century, they did not agree.

* * *

Elodie felt the breaking from across the halls—a crack running through every current of promise in the mountain, a hole torn in the fabric the court wove itself from. She dropped her watering can and ran, past the pale garden, past the courteous horrors, toward the throne room, feeling something return to her with every step: first her hunger, then her grief, then her name, then—

Then a strange, light absence where a boy named Coral should have been in the world's memory. Where *she* should have remembered him.

She reached the throne room in time to see a man she did not know standing among shards of silver light, nameless, looking at her with eyes that were trying very hard to remember why they were wet.

The throne, wounded, furious, bound by its own law—for the court keeps its oaths, even the ones that ruin it—released her. She felt the soul slide home into her chest like a key turning.

"Who are you?" she asked him, and hated the question, and asked it anyway.

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at his own hands as if checking whether they belonged to anyone.

"I don't know," he said. "But I think—I think I was coming to find you. I think I walked thirteen moons to find you." He looked up, and though the name was gone from him, the direction of his heart remained. "Will you walk them back with me?"

Above them, the thirteenth moon began to turn—and with it, somewhere far away, a valley's sealed borders began, gently, to open.
