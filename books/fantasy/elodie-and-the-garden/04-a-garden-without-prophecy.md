## 4. A Garden Without Prophecy

The first thing I feel is the weight leaving.

Not the mountain's weight—I had stopped feeling that years ago, the way you stop feeling a stone you've slept on long enough—but my own. The court beneath kept me in a dress of oaths, thread by thread, each one a promise I'd made in the dark when I was young and frightened and the valley was dying of thirst. *Take me instead. Let them keep their rain. Let them forget.* I had said it all, and the court had gathered every word like ripe fruit and worn them as jewelry for a hundred years.

Now the threads are burning off me one by one, and underneath them I am thin and shaking and *me*.

The throne room is full of light where there should be none. The thirteen moons have all turned; I can feel their absence like teeth pulled from a jaw. And standing before the shattered hilt of his own sworn blade is a man whose name I cannot remember.

That's the price. I understood it even through the mountain's fog: he traded his remembered name for my soul, and so the name is gone—not hidden, not changed, but *gone*, scooped out of the world like a seed from its husk. When I look at him I see his face, his scarred knuckles, the way he stands like a wall between a thing and what it wants to harm. But the word that was him has been paid into the court's coffers, and no ledger gives anything back.

"You shouldn't have," I say, because it's true, and because someone should say it out loud in this place at least once.

He looks at me. His mouth does something complicated—half a smile, half an apology—and then he shrugs, and the shrug says everything the missing name used to say: *I would do it again. Come on. It's morning somewhere.*

* * *

We walk home through a world that has forgotten how to be cruel to us.

I had forgotten what magic looks like from the outside. In the court it was architecture—pillars of spoken vows, floors tiled with forfeited promises. Out here it is weather. It comes up through the ground in slow silver veins, ferns uncurling with too many fronds, a stream running uphill for three cheerful feet before thinking better of it. Where we step, small white flowers open behind us like a sentence being written.

"Is that us?" he asks. He hasn't spoken much since the throne room. I think he is learning which words fit the shape of himself now, the way a man learns to walk on a leg that's been set wrong and reset right.

"It's the valley breathing out," I say. "It held its breath for a hundred years."

The Underhill valley opens below us at dusk, and I stop on the ridge because my legs stop working. It's smaller than memory. Greener than grief. The shrine on the hill—the one his family kept, the one with the silent god inside it—stands with its door flung wide, and light the color of honey is pouring out of it into the grass like something spilled and unashamed.

"He's talking," Coral says beside me. Then he frowns, correcting himself. "Someone is. Not words. More like—" He presses a hand flat over his sternum. "Like a bell after the ringing."

I know what he means. I can hear it too, now that the court isn't screaming over it: a low, patient hum under the world, the sound of a god who spent a century holding a debt he could not pay and has just been released.

We go down into the valley together.

* * *

My garden is a graveyard, and I plant it anyway.

A hundred years of neglect, and still the bones of it are mine: the raised beds I built from river stones, the trellis rotted to lace, the well I dug with my own hands the summer before I walked up the mountain. The soil is black and greedy and alive. Magic has gotten into it the way spring water gets into everything—gently, everywhere, without asking.

I kneel in the dirt of the first bed and put my palms flat against it, and I weep, briefly and without drama, the way you water something.

Coral works beside me without being asked. That, at least, survived the trade. He clears the trellis. He hauls water from the well, wincing at the cold. When I unfold my seed catalog—a foolscap book I kept in my apron pocket through a hundred years of captivity, its pages soft as cloth—he holds the lantern for me while I read aloud what might grow here now.

"Moonsweet," I say. "It needs a little magic in the soil. We never could grow it before." I look up at him. "What did your family call this corner?"

And there it is again—the flinch, small and quick as a bird. He doesn't know. There is no *his family's land* in his mouth anymore, no inherited rows, no names handed down like tools. The prophecy took his name and took the whole shelf of names around it.

"I don't remember," he says quietly. "I don't remember any of it. Just the work. The work feels like mine."

"Then the work is yours," I tell him. "Names are just what other people called you. The hands are what you called yourself."

He considers this the way he seems to consider everything—like a man checking a blade for flaws—and then nods once, and goes back to turning soil.

* * *

We build the garden over eleven days, and I will not pretend those days were simple.

There were nights he woke shouting a word that wasn't there anymore, reaching for a god who owed him an explanation and finding only silence. There were mornings I woke certain I was still beneath the mountain, that the dirt under my nails was a dream the court allowed me, that any moment the oaths would crawl back up my arms like ivy reclaiming a wall. On the fourth day I found him standing at the shrine door, not going in.

"She prayed there," he said. "My grandmother. To a god who never answered. Because answering her cost too much." His jaw worked. "I was proud of that silence. I thought it meant we were chosen to endure it."

"And now?"

"Now I think we were chosen to pay for it." He turned to me, and his eyes were very tired and very clear. "Your bargain bought the valley's rain. Their faith paid the interest. I was born into a debt and told it was a blessing."

"Yes," I said. Because lying to him seemed obscene, under the circumstances.

He looked at the shrine a long moment. Then he picked up his hoe and went back to the beds, and I understood that he had decided something, though I didn't learn what until the last day.

* * *

On the eleventh day, the garden is planted, and the god speaks.

Not to me. I am kneeling in the moonsweet row, pressing seeds into soil that glimmers faintly, when Coral goes still by the well—still the way the whole valley goes still, birdsong included, the air pulling tight as a drumhead. The honey-colored light from the shrine stretches across the field like a hand and touches him on the shoulder.

I watch him listen. I watch his face do grief and fury and something older than both, and then—slowly, like frost melting off glass—something like peace.

The light withdraws. The birdsong returns. The god says nothing else, and I understand with total certainty that it never will again, and that this is right. Some debts end with payment. This one ended with a thank-you, and a thank-you is enough when both parties finally agree on what was owed.

"What did it say?" I ask.

"One word." He crouches beside me in the dirt, takes a pinch of moonsweet seed from my palm, and studies it like it might study him back. "*Free.*"

"That's two syllables."

"It said it slowly." A ghost of a smile. "It had a hundred years of practice being quiet. It wanted to get it right."

* * *

The catalog falls open to the last page while we're cleaning up, and that's where he finds it.

A flower I'd marked decades ago and never grown—no use for it, no buyer, no magic in the soil to feed it. It grows only where something has ended honestly. The illustration shows a bloom like a struck bell, petals the color of dawn coming off night shift.

"This one," he says. "What's it called?"

I read the entry. Then I close the book and give it to him, cover to cover, the way you hand someone a deed.

"It doesn't have one yet," I say. "The scholar who wrote the catalog left the line blank. Said some things should be named by whoever plants them first."

He turns the blank line toward the light. Behind us the moonsweet is already up—impossibly, gloriously up, magic doing what magic does now that the valley lets it. Somewhere on the hill the shrine door stands open, empty, finished. Somewhere beneath the mountain, thirteen moons turn for someone else's story.

He dips a stick in the ink pot and holds it over the page, and I lean in close, and together—we'll argue later about whose handwriting it really is—we write the name of the flower that grows where an oath dies honestly.

Outside, it begins to rain. Gently. Like something remembering how.
