## 1. The Quiet Trade

The trouble with carving bones for a living, August Okonkwo had learned, was that bones talked.

Not the fresh ones — those were mostly rude and brief, all complaints about the cold. It was the old ones, the river-found ones, the ones he dug from the grey clay banks of the Vellwater at dawn when nobody respectable was watching. Old bones remembered things. And they were not shy about sharing.

"Left," murmured the femur he was working that morning, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "You always cut left-handed curves first. She did too. The one who wore me. She braided her hair on festival days and sang off-key and once she stole a peach from a magistrate's cart and never told anyone."

August set down his knife, flexed his fingers, and said nothing, because saying something was how men ended up in the Edict's ledgers.

Seven years he'd kept this rule. Seven years of nodding politely to the dead inside his head and answering them only with silence, which they found maddening, which was rather the point. A man who spoke to empty air in his own workshop was eccentric. A man who answered bones aloud was evidence.

So he carved, and he listened, and he sold what couldn't hang him.

* * *

His stall in Tallowmarket Row was the third from the corner, wedged between a chandler who smelled permanently of mutton fat and a dyer whose fingers were every color except her own. August liked the position. Third from the corner meant foot traffic without attention, customers without conversation, and — crucially — a roof beam overhead that caught most of the drips when it rained.

He laid out his wares each morning with the care of a man arranging alibis. Little charms of sheep-bone and deer-antler: luck-knots for fishermen, teething rings ground smooth as river stones, buttons, hairpins, whistles that produced a sound like a disappointed goose. Nothing enchanted. Nothing *true*. He'd sanded every scrap of meaning out of them before they ever saw daylight, bleached the whispers away until the bone was just bone, dumb and honest and safe.

Mostly.

"You've got a new one," said Marta the chandler, leaning over the stall partition with the shamelessness of a woman who'd known him since he'd been a skinny apprentice stealing candle ends. She tapped a finger on the little round charm at the end of his display row — a flat disc of antler, polished to the color of cream, with a spiral worked into its face.

"That's just stock," August said.

"It's facing the street."

"Bones face where I leave them."

"Mm." Marta squinted at him over her spectacles, which she wore for effect rather than need, being sharp-eyed as a magpie. "It's turned since yesterday. I'd swear it was facing the wall before."

August looked at the charm. The charm, insofar as a disc of antler could be said to look at anything, appeared to be gazing out at the market crowd with an air of quiet expectation.

"I'll turn it around," he said.

"Don't trouble yourself on my account." Marta bought two candles she didn't need, as she did most mornings, because buying candles was cheaper than admitting she came for the company, and shuffled back to her own stall trailing the smell of tallow.

The moment she'd gone, August picked up the disc. It was warm. Antler didn't hold heat like that, not in October, not under a sky the color of dishwater. He held it to his ear the way you'd hold a seashell, an old habit he pretended he didn't have, and heard — faintly, the way you hear rain through a roof —

*"She's coming."*

He put it down. He turned it to face the wall. His hands were steady, because seven years of practice will do that, but his stomach performed a slow, complicated maneuver that no amount of practice ever seemed to fix.

*She's coming.* Bones loved their riddles. They'd been full of portents lately, the whole lot of them, muttering about moons and doors and hunger in voices that frayed at the edges like old rope. He'd decided sometime in the summer to treat it the way one treats a neighbor who talks about the government: listen politely, agree with nothing, wait for it to pass.

Except it wasn't passing. If anything, the murmurings had grown more urgent as autumn deepened, and this morning the river-clay had practically hummed against his digging spade.

Business, at least, was ordinary. Ordinary was the whole architecture of his life, and he tended it like a garden.

A fisherman's wife bought three luck-knots, though she informed him they were for her sister, who was having a difficult year, and that she personally thought luck was nonsense, and would he take a penny less? He would. A boy of perhaps nine spent a long time deliberating between two whistles, blew both, chose the more disappointed-sounding goose, and left beaming. An elderly gentleman commissioned a walking-stick handle shaped like a hound's head, "with kind eyes," he specified, "my last one had mean eyes and I never trusted it."

August took the commission. Kind eyes were within his abilities. He'd gotten good, over the years, at putting harmless things into bone: comfort, humor, small kindnesses. It was, he sometimes thought, the closest he could come to an apology — filling the world with trinkets that would never whisper, never hunger, never remember anything at all.

The Edict Against Magic hung on the customs house wall in letters tall enough to read from across the square. NO CHARM, SPELL, OR SPEAKING ART SHALL BE PRACTICED, SOLD, OR SUFFERED WITHIN THE CITY OF VELL. Below it, in smaller letters added by some clerk with a sense of theater: THE PENALTY IS FORFEITURE OF GOODS AND PERSON.

Forfeiture of person. The Edict's drafters had been proud of that phrase. August read it every morning on his way to the stall, the way other men checked the weather, and every morning it said the same thing: *we are watching, we are patient, and we have lists.*

He was almost certainly on a list somewhere. Not a serious list — he'd made sure his work was dull enough that any inspector would yawn — but a name in a margin, perhaps, next to a note reading *bone-carver, unremarkable.* Unremarkable was the finest thing a man could be in Vell. He cultivated it the way farmers cultivated soil.

Which made it doubly strange that today, unremarkable refused to cooperate.

* * *

It began with the dog.

There was a dog — brindled, enormous, officially belonging to the butcher but functionally self-employed — who had patrolled Tallowmarket Row for as long as August had held his stall. The dog ignored everyone equally. It was famous for it. Children had tried for years to win it over with sausage and failed; the dog regarded sausage as a form of bribery and bribery as beneath its dignity.

At midday, the dog walked past August's stall, stopped, backed up, and stood there staring at him.

Not at the charms. At *him.*

"I haven't got anything," August told it.

The dog continued staring with the grave, troubled expression of a magistrate reviewing bad testimony. Then it whined — low, once — and pressed its nose briefly against the stall post nearest to him, and walked on.

Marta leaned over again. "That animal has never acknowledged your existence."

"I'm as surprised as you are."

"It looked at you like you owed it money."

"Everything looks at me like I owe it money. I'm a tradesman."

But his hands weren't quite steady when he arranged the afternoon stock, and the bones noticed. Of course they noticed. The dead missed nothing; it was one of the few advantages of being dead.

*"He felt it,"* whispered the rib-bone in his apron pocket, gleeful, a gossip at a wedding. *"Even the beast felt it. Oh, it's starting, carver, it's starting, the thin time—"*

"Hush," August said, barely moving his lips, barely breathing the word into the space behind his teeth.

*"Hush yourself,"* said the rib, with feeling. *"We've been quiet for your sake seven years. Quiet, quiet, quiet, while the door goes unwatched and the anchors go dark and the moon counts toward thirteen. We are allowed one morning of gloating."*

Anchors. Doors. Thirteen. The same words, over and over, all season, from bones pulled from different graves along fifty miles of riverbank. Dead women and dead soldiers and dead children who had never met in life, agreeing in death on a vocabulary none of them should have shared.

August had a theory about it, and the theory was that he was tired, and that autumn was melancholy, and that if he kept his knife moving and his mouth shut, winter would arrive and the bones would settle into ordinary reminiscence — peaches stolen from carts, songs sung off-key, the small domestic eternity of the remembered dead.

The alternative theory was worse, and he did not permit himself to think it. He was very good at not thinking things. It was, after all, his craft.

* * *

The storm came in off the salt flats at dusk, early for the season, dragging the curfew bell behind it like a bride's train. August heard it while packing the stall — the great bronze tongue of the Belltower booming four times, then the fifth that meant *off the streets*, then the sixth that meant *we mean it.*

By the time he'd shuttered his wares and thrown the oilcloth over the frame, the rain had gone from *weather* to *opinion.* It came down in slabs. The dyer next door swore cheerfully at the sky in three languages. Somewhere a cart overturned to general exclamations.

August walked home fast with his collar up, through streets emptying like basins, past the watchmen who stood in doorways looking miserable and official. One of them nodded to him — recognition, nothing more. *Bone-carver, unremarkable.* The nod said. *Nothing here.* August nodded back and gave the rain credit for his haste.

His house sat at the end of Chandler's Lane, narrow and crooked, leaning companionably against the fuller's shop next door as though the two buildings were old friends sharing a secret. He'd chosen it for the cellar — dry, deep, good clay soil beyond — and for the lane itself, which was the sort of place trouble forgot the address of.

He lit the lamp, hung his wet coat, fed the stove, and went through the evening ritual: kettle on, bread cut, knife honed, tomorrow's stock counted. Outside, the storm threw itself against the shutters like a creditor. Inside, the bones in his workshop basket murmured among themselves in the low, urgent way they'd had all week, and August, pouring his tea, told them firmly:

"Not tonight. Whatever it is — thirteenth moon, dark anchors, hungry doors — it can wait until market hours. I'm closed."

The bones, insulted, went quiet in the particular way that meant they hadn't gone quiet at all.

He was halfway through his tea and entirely through his bread when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child might make, or a very polite ghost, and August sat very still with his cup halfway to his mouth and listened to the rain hammering the lane and thought: *no one is out in this. Curfew's rung twice. No one is out in this unless they're lost, or mad, or—*

The knock came again. Three knocks, small, patient.

And beneath them — so faint he might have imagined it, except that he had spent seven years learning exactly which things he imagined and this was not one of them — came a sound from outside his door that stopped his heart the way a hand stops a clock.

A voice. Thin, young, half-drowned by the storm. Speaking words in no language spoken by any living mouth in the city of Vell.

Words that sounded, God help him, like the ones his bones used.

In his apron pocket, the rib-bone woke up and began, very distinctly, to sing.

August Okonkwo set down his tea, stood, and stood there a long moment, a man at the exact border of the life he had built and whatever was knocking on the far side of it.

Then he went to the door, and lifted the latch, and opened it onto the storm.
