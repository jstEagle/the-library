## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The storm came in off the salt flats an hour after curfew, the way storms in Vell always did—late, rude, and full of opinions.

August Okonkwo heard it coming through the bones.

That was the trouble with his particular affliction. Weather didn't whisper through stone or wood or glass, but bone carried everything. The little carved sparrows on his windowsill murmured of pressure and wind the way sailors' wives murmured of husbands at sea. The half-finished comb on his workbench fretted about the cold. Even the box of failed charms under his bed—the ones he'd carved too honestly, before he'd learned better—stirred in their sawdust bed and muttered nonsense in voices he hadn't heard in years.

"Hush," he told them, as he did every night. "You'll wake the neighbors."

The neighbors, of course, were the least of it. The Edict Against Magic had teeth, and the witch-hunters had the Edict, and August had spent seven years making himself so small, so harmless, so thoroughly dull that even Seraphine Voss's hounds would find nothing to sniff at. His shop sold trinkets. Pretty, pointless trinkets. Love-lottery tokens that meant nothing, birthing charms that were purely decorative, little bone fish that were, he assured customers with a straight face, "simply fish."

The fact that he could hear the dead murmuring through every one of them was a secret he kept the way other men kept their hearts: close, and quietly, and with a constant low-grade terror that someone might ask to see it.

The storm arrived and threw itself against his shutters like a creditor.

August was awake anyway. He was always awake in the small hours; the dead kept odd company hours, and lately they had been restless in a way he didn't like. For weeks now, the older bones—his oldest pieces, the ones he kept locked in a cedar box and never sold—had been whispering the same word over and over, soft as ash falling.

*Thirteenth. Thirteenth. Thirteenth.*

He'd told himself it meant nothing. Bones were dramatic. Bones had been buried under gossiping graveyards and drowned in sailors' wrecks and passed hand to hand through a hundred years of human foolishness; if anything had a right to be dramatic, it was bone.

Still, he had lit an extra candle tonight, and he told himself that was for the carving, and not because the whispering had made his hands unsteady.

The knock came at the worst possible moment, which August would later decide was the only moment it could possibly have come, because fate in Vell had a sense of timing like a bad comedian's.

Three sharp raps. Small ones. A fist the size of a plum.

August went very still.

Nobody knocked at his door after curfew. Nobody knocked at his door before dawn. The combination of the two was so far outside the ordinary run of things that for a long moment he simply sat there, knife poised over a piece of sheep femur, listening to the storm and to the sudden, electric silence of every bone in his shop.

They had gone quiet. All of them. The sparrows, the comb, the muttering box under the bed—silent as a held breath.

In seven years, his bones had never once gone quiet.

The knock came again. Three raps. Then, after a pause, a fourth, almost apologetic, as though whoever was knocking worried three had been rude.

August set down his knife. He picked up the poker from his cold hearth, considered it, and set it down again, because a man answering his door armed with a poker at this hour was a man who looked guilty of something, and August had worked very hard to look guilty of nothing at all.

He opened the door on the chain, the way a sensible man does in a city under Edict, and the storm shoved a child through the gap.

* * *

She was perhaps ten years old, though it was hard to say. Mud had gotten to her first and claimed most of her—the hem of a dress that had once been good wool, her bare feet, her elbows, a long smear across one cheek as if she'd tried to wipe her face and given up halfway. Her hair was the color of wet rope. Her eyes were the color of nothing August had a name for: pale, and deep, and fixed on him with the terrible seriousness of the very young and the very lost.

She stood on his doorstep in the driving rain and did not shiver, which was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was what she held. In both hands, the way other children held baby birds or stolen sweets, she carried a shard of white antler, longer than her forearm, forked at the top like a bare tree. It should have been dripping. It was not. The rain struck it and slid away, and the shard sat in her small hands dry as a sermon, and it *hummed*.

August felt the hum in his teeth. In the roots of his hair. In the cedar box under his bed, where his oldest bones woke all at once and began, very softly, to sing.

He knew that sound. He had heard it once, years ago, from behind a barricade of burning carts, in the last hour of the purges, when something beneath the streets of Vell had stirred in its sleep and every bone in the city had answered it like a struck bell.

He had spent seven years convincing himself he'd imagined it.

"Close the door," said the sensible part of August, which had kept him alive this long. "Bar it. Put the chair under the handle. In the morning this will be a strange dream you had, and you will carve something soothing, and you will never, ever open your door in a storm again."

August Okonkwo unlatched the chain.

He would spend a great deal of time in the coming weeks trying to understand why. He would settle, eventually, on the truth, which was this: the child looked at him the way the dead looked through the bones—like he was a door, and she had been walking a very long way in the rain to reach it.

He stepped back and let her in.

* * *

She stood in the middle of his shop, dripping on his floorboards, and looked at everything. At the sparrows on the sill. At the combs and tokens and little bone fish laid out on their velvet—velvet being a generous word for a blanket that had once been green. At the knife and the sheep femur on the workbench. At the candle, whose flame leaned toward her, gently, the way a flower leans toward light.

August closed the door. Barred it. Put the chair under the handle anyway, because if he was going to be damned he might as well be thorough.

"Right," he said, in the tone he used for customers who haggled over fish. "You can't stay. I'm sorry, but I have a reputation, and my reputation is that nothing interesting has ever happened here, and you—" he gestured at her, at the mud, at the impossible dry antler, at the general aura of catastrophe she carried like a scent, "—are the single most interesting thing to walk through that door since I opened this shop. So we're going to wait out the worst of the storm, and then you're going to tell me who sent you, and then you're going to leave, and tomorrow I'm going to convince myself this didn't happen. Agreed?"

The child looked at him.

And she spoke.

The words were nothing he knew. They were nothing *anyone* knew, he'd have sworn it—low and resonant and strange, syllables that seemed to arrive a half-second after her mouth made them, as though the sound had to travel up from somewhere very far below. They were not harsh. That was the worst part. They sounded like the space beneath roots. Like the inside of a hill. Like a word spoken in a room where no one had spoken for a hundred years, and the room was glad of it.

Every bone in August's shop answered her.

The sparrows on the sill turned—*turned*, on their little carved feet, though he had never hinged them—toward the sound. The combs hummed. The box under his bed sang out clear and sweet and terrified, a chord he felt in the floorboards, and the shard of antler in the child's hands flared white and rang like a bell struck at midnight, and the candle went out.

In the dark, the girl's voice went on, gentle and low, saying something that August's bones understood perfectly and August himself understood not at all.

And the bones, all of them, every piece he had ever carved, answered back in a language he had spent seven years pretending not to speak.

*Here. Here. Here, here, here.*

"No," August said aloud, to the child, to the bones, to the storm, to the general state of the world. "No, no. We don't do that. We are quiet. We are *nothing*. That is the entire arrangement—"

The girl stopped speaking. The dark held its breath.

August fumbled for flint and lit the candle again with hands that were, he noted with distant embarrassment, shaking badly enough to click the flint three times. When the light caught, she was still standing in the same spot, still holding the antler, watching him with those pale eyes. There was mud on her chin. There was a scratch healing along her jaw that had no business healing that fast.

She pointed at the workbench. At the cedar box, specifically, which was under the workbench, which she should not have been able to see.

She spoke again—two words this time, soft, almost a question.

And this time, though the sounds were foreign, though they belonged to no tongue of Vell or the flats or any port August had ever drunk in, something in him *understood* them. Not translated—understood, the way you understand a hand on your shoulder. The way you understand your own name called from a great distance.

*You hear us too.*

August sat down on his stool, because his legs had made an executive decision without consulting him.

"I don't," he said. "I hear nothing. I'm a trinket-seller. I carve fish. Fish, do you understand? Little decorative fish for people with mantelpieces. There is no hearing. There is no *us*. There is a curfew, and an Edict, and a woman named Seraphine Voss who would burn this whole street on a rumor, and you, child, are a rumor with mud on it."

The girl considered this speech at length, with the gravity of a magistrate.

Then she held out the shard of antler to him.

"No," said August immediately. "Absolutely not. I know what happens when I touch things like you. Things like you are how men end up in the Edict's ledgers."

She didn't withdraw it. She just held it there, patient as tide, and the antler hummed its low bell-note, and under the workbench the cedar box sang harmony, and August Okonkwo—seven years careful, seven years small, seven years of decorative fish—heard, beneath it all, from very far below the floorboards, below the cellar, below the foundations, below the whole worn and sensible city of Vell:

*Thirteenth. Thirteenth. Thirteenth.*

The whisper he had been telling himself was nothing for weeks. It was not nothing. It had never been nothing. It was a count, and it was nearly finished, and something beneath the world was turning in its sleep toward the sound of a child's voice.

August took the antler.

It was warm. That was the thing that undid him—not the humming, not the light, not the impossible dryness of it in a rainstorm, but the warmth, gentle as a hand, like something alive and glad to be held. The moment his fingers closed around it, every bone in the shop went silent again, all at once, the way a room goes silent when someone important enters.

The girl smiled. It was the first thing she'd done that looked like something a child would do, and it was so sudden and so unguarded that August felt something in his chest he had not felt since the purges, and had hoped never to feel again, because it was the feeling that made men do stupid, generous, ruinous things.

She pointed at the antler in his hand. Then at herself. Then at the floor—straight down, through the boards, through the earth, down.

She said one word.

He didn't understand it. Not this time. But the shape of it stayed in the room after the sound had gone, and the antler hummed it back to him, patient, over and over, like a bell that had been ringing a very long time and was in no hurry to stop.

Outside, the storm was passing. The sky over the rooftops was going the grey of early dawn, which meant the hunters would be stirring, which meant the streets would fill with people who had no idea that the most dangerous thing in Vell was currently dripping on a bone-carver's floor and smiling at him.

August looked at the child. The child looked at August.

"Well," he said, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting surprisingly pleasant. "I suppose you'd better tell me your name."

She opened her mouth. What came out was not a name he could have spelled, but the antler rang it sweet and clear, and under the floor, far below, something ancient and patient turned the sound over, and answered:

*Elodie. Elodie. Elodie.*

And then, softer, in the whisper that had been counting the moons:

*Thirteenth.*
