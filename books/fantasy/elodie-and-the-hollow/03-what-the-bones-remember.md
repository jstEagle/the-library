## 3. What the Bones Remember

August did not sleep.

He tried. He made a show of it, in fact—banked the fire, dragged his blanket over the cot in the corner, arranged himself with all the theatrical commitment of a man who very much intended to sleep and would be deeply offended if anyone suggested otherwise. Elodie watched him do it from her perch on the one stool by the hearth, knees drawn up, the antler shard cradled against her chest like a bird she was afraid would fly.

He lasted perhaps four minutes under the blanket before he gave up the pretense entirely.

The problem was the bones.

They were talking. Not whispering—the way they usually did, that low tide-murmur he'd spent seven years learning to ignore, the dead muttering their small grievances through the marrow of whatever he'd carved them into. No. Tonight they were *loud*. Every piece in the shop, from the humblest button to the great narwhal spiral on its shelf of honor, had woken up and found something to say, and what they were saying, all of them, in overlapping voices like a congregation that had lost its priest, was:

*She's here. She's here. She's here.*

And beneath them, deeper, older, the thing under the floor kept saying: *Thirteenth. Thirteenth. Thirteenth.* The way a clock says a hour. The way a debt says your name.

August sat up, sighed at the ceiling as though the ceiling had personally wronged him, and swung his feet to the cold boards.

"All right," he told the room. "All right."

Elodie tilted her head at him. She hadn't moved from the stool in an hour. He'd offered her the cot; she'd declined with a look of such polite horror that he'd almost laughed, and then felt bad about laughing, because there was nothing funny about a child who flinched when you pointed at furniture. There was mud dried in the creases of her knuckles and old salt stains down the front of her dress, the kind of staining you got from walking through ground that used to be sea, or sea that used to be ground. Her eyes were the grey of weather coming.

"You're not tired either," August observed. "Fine. Then we may as well be honest with each other, which will make a nice change, since I've been lying professionally since before you could walk."

He lit the lamp. He got out the tea things, mostly for something to do with his hands, and set two cups down before remembering she was mute and wondering if a mute child drank tea with the same enthusiasm as any other child. She did. She held the cup in both hands and blew across it with grave ceremony, and something in August's chest went soft and stupid about it, and he firmly told that something to mind its own business.

Then he went to the shelf.

* * *

The oldest carvings lived at the back, behind the trinkets, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and curiosity. August had not unwrapped them in three years. He took them down now the way a man takes down a letter he knows contains bad news—slowly, with unnecessary care, giving himself every opportunity to change his mind.

There were seven pieces. He'd made them in his first year in Vell, back when his hands still shook and his hearing of the dead was less a gift than a wound. He'd carved them to test himself, or to punish himself—he'd never decided which. Each one was cut from bone that had once belonged to someone who died badly, and each one, when he held it, spoke with a clarity that the newer work never managed. The dead who died badly remembered more. That was the cruelty of it. That was always the cruelty of it.

He laid them out on the workbench in a row, and Elodie slid off her stool and came to stand beside him, and every single bone began to hum.

Not murmur. *Hum.* A chord, low and resonant, like the inside of a bell.

August looked at the child. The child looked at the bones.

"Right," he said. "So that's new."

He picked up the first piece—a knuckle-bone of some river-drowned man, carved with the coiling wave-patterns of the old estuary folk. When he'd finished it seven years ago, the dead man inside had spoken exactly once, in a voice like water over stones: *Cold. So cold. Tell my wife the boat was sound.* August had wept, quietly, alone, and then wrapped the bone in oilcloth and put it where he wouldn't have to hear it again.

Now, as Elodie's fingers drifted near it—not touching, hovering—the bone spoke again. But not in the drowned man's voice.

In hers.

Or rather: she spoke, and the bone answered, and August could not have said which was which. The sounds she made were the same ones she'd made on his doorstep in the storm, those syllables that bent wrong in the mouth, vowels that seemed to come from slightly below the floor of the world. And the bone rang them back, harmonizing, the way a tuning fork answers its twin across a room.

Elodie said something short and urgent. The knuckle-bone shivered in August's palm. The drowned man's voice surfaced underneath, faint but unmistakable, and this time it didn't say *cold* or *wife* or *boat*.

It said: *Yes. Yes. She asks if you can hear us. Tell her yes.*

August set the bone down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be a bomb.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the child, to the dead, to God if He was listening and hadn't better things to do. "No, no, no."

Elodie frowned at him. She said another phrase—shorter, questioning—and the whole row of old carvings stirred on the bench like sleeping men disturbed by light.

"I don't know what you're asking," August told her. "That's rather the point. I've spent seven years making sure I don't know what anyone's asking. I carve little ducks. I carve buttons. I carve things so harmless that even the Edict's inspectors yawn when they see them. I am," he said, with feeling, "a boring man, and boring men do not get burned, and I would very much like to continue being one."

She watched this speech with the patience of someone who understood none of it and all of it, which, August was beginning to suspect, was the natural condition of children everywhere.

Then she reached past him, deliberately, and touched the second carving.

* * *

It was a rib, that one. Old, yellowed, cracked along one edge. It had belonged to a woman who had died in the purges—before the purges, really, in the ugly years when the Edict was being written in ash. August had bought it from a relic-monger in his first desperate month, telling himself it was research. It had been the first bone whose voice he'd ever clearly heard, and the voice had said only one thing, over and over, for as long as he'd owned it:

*They're coming down. They're coming down. Whatever you do, don't let them come down.*

He'd assumed she meant soldiers. Soldiers came down stairs, broke down cellar doors, dragged people up into the light to be questioned and then burned. It had made a horrible kind of sense.

But now, under Elodie's fingers, the rib woke, and the woman's voice came through changed—or maybe August was finally hearing it right—and she wasn't saying *they're coming down* the way you'd say soldiers are coming down the stairs.

She was saying it the way you'd say *the sky is falling*. The way you'd say *the end is coming*, except not *the* end. *Down.* They were coming *down*.

And Elodie, hand flat on the rib, said a word.

One word. Two syllables, the first rising, the second falling away like something going over an edge. And every bone on the bench rang it, and the drowned man said it, and the burned woman said it, and the thing beneath the floor said it, deep and slow and satisfied, the way a lock says *click*.

*Hollow. Hollow. Hollow.*

August sat down. His legs made the decision without consulting him, which was probably just as well, because the part of him doing the consulting had gone white as tallow.

He knew the word. Of course he knew the word. Everyone in Vell knew the word, the way everyone knows the name of the disease that killed their grandmother—it was in the Edict itself, buried in the preamble, in the old formal language nobody read anymore: *whereas certain traffickers with the realm beneath the world, called the Hollow...* Children dared each other to say it. Grandmothers spat after it. Seraphine Voss had built a career on it.

But knowing a word and hearing it were different things. Knowing a word was safe. Hearing it spoken in the First Tongue—the actual tongue, the living one, the one that had supposedly died with the last wardens two hundred years ago—by a mud-streaked child in his kitchen while his own carvings sang harmony—

"Where did you learn that?" he asked, uselessly, in Trade-Speech she couldn't understand. "Who taught you? Because there is no one left to teach it. That's the whole point. That's the entire point of the last two hundred years. We killed it. We salted the earth of it. There are no wardens, there are no speakers, there is a city full of honest frightened people carving honest frightened charms, and the Hollow is a story we tell children so they'll stay away from cellars—"

Elodie, watching him rant, reached out and patted his arm twice, gently, the way you'd calm a horse.

It was, somehow, the most humiliating moment of his adult life.

* * *

He made more tea. Neither of them drank it. It sat there steaming between them like a small domestic lie.

Because here was the thing, and August turned it over and over in his mind like a coin he was checking for clipping: the language fit. He'd heard enough of the dead in seven years to know the shape of old words when they passed through bone—the estuary dialects, the highland burr, the formal liturgical cadences of the temple-dead. This was none of them. This was *under* all of them. When Elodie spoke, the syllables didn't travel through the air to reach the bones. They traveled through the bones themselves, through the marrow, through whatever quiet country the dead inhabited, and the dead recognized it the way exiles recognize their mother tongue in a foreign street.

The First Tongue. The language of the Hollow. Spoken fluently, natively, *comfortably*, by a girl of perhaps nine years, standing in his shop with salt stains on her dress.

Which meant one of two things. Either the world had gone mad in the night—

—or the stories were true, and the wardens had not all died, and somewhere below or beyond or underneath, something had been keeping the language alive the way a bank keeps a flame alive under ash, waiting.

And the thing under his floor had been counting moons.

August got up, crossed to the window, and pulled back the shutter a finger's width. The storm had passed. The sky over Vell was swept clean and hard with stars, and above the eastern rooftops hung the moon—three-quarters fat, climbing, bright enough to throw shadows.

He counted without wanting to. He'd been counting for weeks anyway, the way you count when something itches at the edge of your hearing. Full moon past: twelve. The posters had started appearing around the eleventh, he realized now—the reward notices, the *tongued child*, the neat official lettering of the Office of the Edict. Someone else had been counting too. Someone else had known the schedule.

Behind him, Elodie spoke again. Just one phrase, soft, almost apologetic. The antler shard at her throat glowed faintly, and the bones translated, and August closed his eyes because he already knew what they'd say before they said it:

*Twelve are dark. One remains. The door is hungry.*

"Stop," he said. "Please. Just—stop. Give me a moment."

She stopped. She was good at stopping, he noticed. Good at silence. It occurred to him, with a small cold shock, that she must have been silent a long time—in every sense—to be that good at it.

He stood at the window with his back to her and did the arithmetic of his own cowardice, which was the only arithmetic he'd ever been quick at.

Option one: in the morning, he walked her to the Office of the Edict. Turned her in. Collected whatever they paid for a *tongued child*, which would be enough to close the shop and buy a cottage somewhere far from Vell, somewhere with no cellar and no bones and no whispers. Option one was sane. Option one was survival. Option one was everything he'd built these seven years out of silence and small harmless ducks.

Option one ended with a child in a fire, and the door below opening anyway, because a door held shut by thirteen anchors does not care what happens to the thirteenth anchor's body. It cares whether the anchor-*word* is spoken. And if the word died with her—

He thought of the burned woman in the rib. *Whatever you do, don't let them come down.* Seven years he'd thought she meant soldiers.

He turned around. Elodie was watching him with those grey weather-coming eyes, and she had, he saw, drawn something in the spilled tea-leaves on the bench with one careful fingertip: a circle, and thirteen marks around it, and one mark—no, two—still bright while the others were smudged dark.

She looked at the drawing. She looked at him. She said something quiet, and the bones carried it up to him like a message through water:

*You hear them. You have always heard them. Please don't pretend anymore. It makes them sad.*

August Okonkwo, bone-carver, seller of harmless ducks, survivor of seven careful years, stood in his own shop and felt the whole careful architecture of it—every silence, every pretense, every safely boring day—crack straight down the middle like river ice in spring.

"The thirteenth moon," he said hoarsely. "When is it?"

Elodie held up the antler shard. It hummed. Beneath the floor, patient as tide, the old voice gave its answer, and for the first time all night, August understood it without needing the bones to translate—understood it in his teeth, in his marrow, in the place where his own gift of hearing lived.

*Nine days.*

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring. Not the curfew bell. The watch-bell from the Edict garrison, ringing the pattern that meant a hunt had been declared.

And August thought, with a sinking feeling he recognized from seven years ago, from a rain-slick street and a crossbow bolt buried in the cobbles at his feet: *Seraphine.*

She was already looking for the child.

And now, thanks to a night of singing bones and a storm and one act of charity he should have known better than to commit, she would be looking at him.
