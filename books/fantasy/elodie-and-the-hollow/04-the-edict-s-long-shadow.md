## 4. The Edict's Long Shadow

The poster went up on the market square's notice board between the announcement of a new tax on lamp oil and a lost-dog notice that had been there so long the dog was probably dead, married, or running its own business by now.

August read it twice while pretending to adjust the awning of his stall, which needed no adjusting, and which he adjusted anyway because a man fiddling with his own awning attracted no attention whatsoever, whereas a man reading a poster with his spine going rigid attracted every kind.

**BY ORDER OF THE WARDENS OF VELL**, it said, in the crisp official hand that always reminded August of a knife laid neatly on clean linen. **REWARD OF FORTY CROWNS FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO THE RECOVERY OF A TONGUED CHILD, LAST SEEN NEAR THE RIVER GATE. APPROACH WITH CAUTION. DO NOT ENGAGE. REPORT TO COMMANDER VOSS.**

Forty crowns. That was a year of rent, a new set of carving knives, and a coat that didn't have a burn hole shaped like a small country in the left sleeve. Forty crowns for pointing at a mud-streaked girl who was currently, at this very moment, sitting in the back room of his shop eating the last of his bread and drawing pictures of doors on his floor with charcoal.

He had not reported her. Obviously. He had fed her, which was worse, in the eyes of any law worth its ink. Feeding implied intent. Feeding implied shelter. Feeding implied that August Okonkwo, bone-carver, licensed trader of trinkets, had made a choice — and choices were precisely what the Edict Against Magic existed to prevent people from making.

He lowered his gaze from the poster and went back to his stall, where a woman was waiting to buy a luck charm for her son's apprenticeship.

"Does it work?" she asked, as everyone asked, as they were required to ask, because asking *does it work* was how a citizen proved they believed charms were nonsense, which was itself a kind of loyalty oath under the Edict.

"Not at all," August said warmly. "It's a nice shape, though. Boys like nice shapes."

She laughed, paid her two coppers, and left holding the little carved fish like it might bite her, which was exactly the correct amount of reverence to show a piece of dead bone. The fish said nothing. The fish never said anything anymore, not out loud, not where anyone could hear. But as her fingers closed around it, August felt the faintest stir against the inside of his skull, like a sleeper turning over: *tell her the boy chews his nails, tell her he'll grow out of it, tell her—*

August set the fish down and thought very hard about inventory.

* * *

Seven years ago, there had been fire.

Not the dramatic kind — though there had been some of that, later, in the memory's retelling, the way memories add drums to quiet scenes. It had started as smoke. Smoke over the Tanner's Quarter at dawn, and bells, and men in grey-and-silver going door to door with iron rods that hummed when they came near anything touched by the Hollow's old craft.

August had been nineteen, living above a dyer's shop, carving in secret the way other young men drank in secret — badly, compulsively, certain he'd stop soon. He'd made the mistake of selling one true charm. Just one. A hearing-charm for a deaf grandmother on Cordwainer Lane, carved from the knucklebone of an ox that had once stood in a field where something old had died, and when the grandmother held it she'd heard her late husband humming in the garden again, and she'd wept, and she'd told her neighbor, and her neighbor had told a warden, because forty crowns was forty crowns even then.

They'd come for him at dusk. He'd heard them from three streets away — not with his ears but through the half-finished raven on his workbench, whose borrowed bones trembled at the approach of iron. He'd had time to run. He'd almost run. His bag was packed; his feet were at the window.

Then the door below opened, and a woman's voice said, "Third floor. Alone. Everyone else out."

Seraphine Voss had been thirty then, already commander, already famous for the purges in the Salt Districts, already carrying whatever it was she carried behind her eyes like a stone carried in a sock. She'd climbed his stairs alone, dismissed the wardens at the landing, and found him sitting at his bench with his hands flat on the wood, doing the arithmetic of a man deciding whether to leap.

"You're Okonkwo," she'd said. "The carver."

"I carve trinkets," he'd said. "Fish. Birds. A duck, once. Very harmless duck."

"The grandmother on Cordwainer Lane heard her dead husband." She'd picked up the half-finished raven and turned it in the lamplight, and August had watched her face for the flicker — the warden's tell, the moment the iron rod would start to hum. "This is good work," she'd said instead. "This is *very* good work. You could hear through this if you wanted to. Can you?"

And here was the moment his whole life had pivoted on, the hinge-creak he still heard sometimes when he couldn't sleep: he could have lied. Nineteen-year-olds lie beautifully. He'd opened his mouth to do it.

"Yes," he'd said.

Silence. Somewhere below, a warden coughed. Seraphine set the raven down with a care that frightened him more than violence would have.

"My mother sang to me," she'd said, to the window, to the smoke. "Before. I was six. I remember the tune and not her face. Do you know what I would give—" She stopped herself the way a person slams a door on a draft. Then she'd turned around and become Commander Voss again, all edges. "Here is what happens now. You will surrender your materials. You will take a license as a plain tradesman. You will carve ducks. And if I ever find evidence — *any* evidence — that you've worked true craft again, I will not come alone next time, and I will not climb these stairs myself, because I will not be able to trust what I'll do when I see your face. Do you understand me?"

"Why?" he'd asked. It was the stupidest question he'd ever asked, and he'd asked it anyway, because he was nineteen and alive and confused. "Why spare me?"

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice. "Because someone spared me once," she'd said, "and I intend to pay it back by being everything they weren't." She'd paused at the door. "Burn the raven yourself. It deserves better than the pyre."

He'd burned it. He'd cried while it burned, which he'd told no one, and then he'd taken the license, and rented the little shop on Bellows Row, and spent seven years becoming the most harmless man in Vell.

Or so he'd believed until four nights ago, when a knock before dawn had put a hole in all of it the size of a mute child who spoke the language underneath the world.

* * *

Presently, the bell over his shop door chimed, and August surfaced from the past to find a customer: old Master Hollis from the chandlery, who bought a charm every month whether he needed one or not, mostly, August suspected, for the conversation.

"Heard about the poster," Hollis said, picking up a carved owl and examining it with the gravity of a jeweler. "Tongued child. Nasty business."

"Is it?"

"Well, a child running loose speaking *that* talk. My grandfather used to say the First Tongue was how the Hollow got its hooks in you. You say a word down there and something down there says it back, and then you belong to it." He set the owl down. "Course, my grandfather also said turnips cured gout, so."

"So," August agreed.

"You seen anything? Down your end of the row? River Gate's close to you."

August felt the words line up in their usual neat formation — *no, nothing, quiet as a grave, quieter actually, graves are rather noisy where I'm concerned* — and for one absurd second he nearly said the last part out loud, just to feel what it was like to be honest after seven years.

"Quiet as ever," he said. "Though I did hear a strange knocking a few nights back. Storm, probably. Shutters."

"Mm." Hollis bought the owl, counted out his coppers one by one with the ceremony of a man paying a ransom, and paused at the door. "Voss herself is asking after the row, they say. Not sending her people. Going herself. Door to door." He tapped his nose, which in Hollis's personal sign language meant either *I know more than I'm saying* or *my nose itches*. "You want to sweep your step, lad. She notices steps."

Then he was gone, and August stood in his empty shop with his heart performing a slow, heavy drumroll, and went to the back room, where Elodie looked up from her charcoal drawing of a door — the same door, always the same door, cracked down the middle with something like smoke leaking out — and blinked at him with the patient, unblinking patience of a cat who has decided you are her problem now.

"We need to talk about your face," August told her. "Specifically, its total absence from wanted posters. This is a problem. People are being paid forty crowns to find it."

She tilted her head. She reached into the collar of her shirt and drew out the shard of white antler, which hummed — softly, constantly, a note just below hearing, a note August felt in his teeth rather than his ears — and held it up as if to say, *this is why.*

"Yes, yes, magic antler, chosen child, ancient covenant, I've read the same bedtime stories you have," August said, pacing. "But here's what the stories leave out: Seraphine Voss is walking Bellows Row personally, and I owe that woman a debt I can't describe without sounding like a bad novel, and if she finds you here, she won't arrest me. She'll be *disappointed* in me. And I have survived seven years on the fact that Seraphine Voss has never once been disappointed in me, and I would very much like to keep it that way."

Elodie considered him. Then, deliberately, she picked up the charcoal and wrote on the floorboards, in letters that made August's bones ache to look at — not with pain, but with recognition, the way a tuning fork aches near its twin:

**THE DEAD ARE LOUD TODAY.**

August stopped pacing.

"They are not," he said. "I haven't heard a thing all morning."

Which was a lie, and she knew it, and worse — the bones knew it. Because the truth was that since dawn the carvings in his back room had been murmuring like a crowd before a storm: fragments, warnings, the same phrase rising and falling through a dozen different voices, some of them voices he recognized from years of quiet companionship, all of them saying the thing he'd been refusing to translate since yesterday.

Elodie pointed at the shelf where his oldest work sat — the pieces he kept for no honest reason, the ones too well-made to sell, too *awake* to destroy. The raven's successor. A wolf carved from river-jaw bone. A small round stone-polish of elk horn that had been whispering the same sentence for three days.

She pointed at the elk horn, then at him, then folded her arms.

"I don't need to listen to it," August said. "Whatever it's saying, I can guess. Things are grim downstairs, the moon's behaving strangely, doom doom doom. The dead love a bit of doom. It's the only theater they get."

Elodie didn't move. The antler hummed. Outside, far off toward the river, a bell began to ring the curfew-warning hour, early, wrong, and both of them turned toward the sound.

And beneath the bell, beneath the wind, beneath seven years of practiced silence, the elk horn said clearly, patiently, in the voice of a grandmother who had been dead longer than August had been alive:

*She is coming up the row, boy. Sweep your step.*
