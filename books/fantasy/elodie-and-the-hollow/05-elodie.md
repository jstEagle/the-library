## 5. Elodie

The world above was too loud.

That was the first thing Elodie understood about it, lying awake on the bone-carver's floor with a blanket that smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil. Even in the deep hours, even under the storm, Vell made noise. The wind worried at the shutters. The house itself ticked and settled like something dreaming. And beneath it all—under everything, always—the man breathed in the next room. Slow. Steady. A sound like a tide that had forgotten how to leave.

She pressed her palms over her ears and found that this did not help, because the loudness she meant was not the kind ears caught.

In the Hollow's edge, where she had lived her whole life until the sinking, quiet had been a *thing*. You could hold it. You could carry it in cupped hands like water from a well, cool and clean, and pour it over your thoughts when they grew tangled. The wardens had taught the children how. *Cup the quiet, little fish,* old Warden Maren used to say, her fingers gnarled as driftwood. *The world below is hungry, but it is patient, and patience is made of quiet.*

Elodie had been good at holding quiet. Better than good. The wardens had watched her do it with a look she hadn't understood then—a look like hunger and grief sharing one face.

Now there was no quiet anywhere. There was only the grey tide behind her, and the loud city ahead of her, and a white shard of antler against her breastbone that hummed its one long note, day and night, like a bell that had been struck once at the beginning of the world and never allowed to stop.

*Broken,* the shard said, in the way it said all things, which was not words at all but pictures pressed against the inside of her eyes. *The door is broken.*

*I know,* Elodie answered, in the First Tongue, which was not a language of the mouth at all.

It was the language of the *underneath* of things.

---

Here is what Elodie knew about herself, arranged the way she arranged things: in rows, by weight, the way her mother had sorted seeds.

She was nine years old, or near enough. She had never spoken—not because her throat was broken, but because the first word ever offered to her had been a word of the First Tongue, whispered into her cradle by a warden with cold fingers, and after that no other word would fit. Her mouth could make the shapes of common speech. She had tried, alone, in the reed-beds, shaping *mama* and *bread* and *please* the way other children did. The sounds came out flat and wrong, like coins rubbed smooth of their stamping. Words in the common tongue slid off her the way rain slid off oiled cloth. But the First Tongue lived in her chest, in the space behind her navel, in the soles of her feet. When she spoke it, she did not move air. She moved *meaning*, and meaning went wherever it was sent.

Her village had been called Sennet-by-the-Edge, though the maps had stopped naming it years ago. It sat—or had sat—at the place where the world grew thin, where the ground was salt-grey and the wells tasted of iron and memory. The people there were not afraid of the Hollow. That was the difference between them and everyone else. Everyone else built walls and passed edicts and burned practitioners in the market square, as if the realm below were a wolf to be starved out. The people of Sennet-by-the-Edge knew better. The Hollow was not a wolf. It was a *stomach*, and it had been fed carefully for a thousand years, and the feeding was called the covenant, and the covenant was kept by anchors, and the anchors were kept by—

By whom? Elodie turned that question over many times on the bone-carver's floor, in the dark, and could never find the whole answer. She had been a child. Children are told what keeps them safe and little more.

But she remembered the last night. She remembered it in perfect pieces, the way you remember anything that ends the world.

---

* * *

The moon had been wrong for a long time before the end. Everyone said so. It hung fat and yellow and *close*, closer than any harvest moon, and it did not move the way moons should. Old Warden Ivo had stood in the square every evening counting on his fingers, and his face grew worse each night, and finally he stopped counting altogether and began building instead—building a small boat of woven reeds, which made no sense, because Sennet-by-the-Edge was nowhere near any water anyone could sail.

Except that the water came anyway. That was the thing no one had understood. The grey tide did not come from the sea.

It came from *below*.

Elodie woke on the last night because the humming in her chest—the shard's note, always soft before—had gone sharp as a needle. She climbed out of bed and stood at the window, and the fields beyond the village were glowing. Not burning. Glowing, a pale drowned light, as if the moonlight had sunk into the ground and decided to come back up through the soil instead.

And the ground was *breathing*.

Slow swells moved across the salt-fields, the way a cat's flank moves, and between the swells the light pooled and gathered, and Elodie felt—she would feel this forever, she was sure, even if she lived to be ancient—she felt the Hollow notice her. Not the village. Not the wardens. *Her.* The attention came up through the floorboards and into her feet and it was vast and slow and terribly, terribly empty, the way a winter field is empty, the way an unfed thing is empty.

*Cup the quiet, little fish.*

She cupped it. She held all the quiet she had ever practiced holding, every morning's stillness, every evening's calm, and she poured it down through her feet into the breathing ground, and the ground paused. The swells slowed. For one heartbeat—for three—the Hollow considered her offering, the way a great fish considers a small bright thing drifting past its jaw.

Then the wardens came for her.

There were four of them, moving fast through the dark, and Warden Maren caught her up under the arms like a sack of meal and ran. Behind them the village was making sounds Elodie had never heard houses make. Ahead of them, at the edge of the fields, stood the anchor-stone—one of thirteen, though Elodie didn't know that yet; she only knew it as the Grey Stone, the one children were forbidden to touch, the one that hummed the same note as the shard in her chest—and the stone was *cracking*. Lines of darkness spread across its face like frost across a window, and through the cracks leaked a smoke that was not smoke, a cold that was not weather.

Maren pressed something into her hands. The antler shard, white as a knucklebone, warm as a living thing.

*Carry it,* Maren said, in the First Tongue, and her sending was ragged with fear, which frightened Elodie more than the breathing ground, because wardens were never afraid, wardens *held* the fear for everyone else. *Carry it north. Find the door. There is a word that must reach the door, and you are the one who carries it now, little fish, do you understand, you are the—*

The rest of it was lost. The ground opened.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. The ground simply *sank*, gently, the way a sleeper turns over, and the houses of Sennet-by-the-Edge went down into the pale light like stones lowered into milk, without splashing, without screaming—or with screaming, Elodie supposed, but the grey tide swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything, and afterward the night was quieter than any night had ever been.

Maren's hands let go. Or the ground took them. Elodie never knew which.

She ran north carrying the shard, and the shard carried her, tugging in her grip like a compass needle finding its pole, and she walked for days on the edges of roads, sleeping in ditches, eating stolen bread crusts and unripe plums, until the salt gave way to grass and the grass gave way to the great sprawl of Vell, lit up at night like a hearth-fire with a thousand embers, and the shard pulled her toward one small dark house at the edge of the tanners' quarter, and she knocked.

And a man with tired eyes and bone-dust on his hands opened the door, and every carved thing in his house *answered* her.

---

* * *

Morning came grey through the shutters, and with it came the problem of the man.

He was kind. Elodie could see that the way she saw everything, in the underneath-of-things: kindness moved through him like blood through a limb, sometimes sluggish, sometimes blocked entirely, but always present. He had given her bread and honey the night before, and hot milk that he'd claimed was for himself, and he had not asked her a single question aloud—which was wise of him, since she could not have answered—but his eyes had asked forty questions, and she had answered none of them, because answering required trust, and trust was a bridge, and bridges take time to build.

So she began building.

She started with drawings, because drawing was the one tongue that belonged to everyone. The bone-carver had charcoal for his marking-work, and a scrap of smooth board, and while he pretended to busy himself at his bench—pretending badly, his eyes flicking to her every few breaths—she knelt on the floor and drew.

First she drew the village. Houses with steep roofs, a well, a crooked fence. Then she drew the same houses again, lower, half-sunk, with wavy lines over them, and she looked up at him to see if he understood.

His face did something complicated. Grief and recognition wrestling in the same features, like two dogs meeting on unfamiliar ground.

Encouraged, she drew the fields breathing—the swells, the pale light rising from the ground. She drew the anchor-stone cracking. She drew herself, small, running, with the shard drawn as a white line against her chest. She worked quickly, urgently, her charcoal snapping twice because she pressed too hard, and when she looked up again the man had gone very still, the way animals go still when they hear something in the brush.

Then, slowly, he crossed the room and knelt across the board from her, and he did something that undid her completely.

He pointed at her drawing of the village, and then at himself, and shook his head—*I don't understand*—and then he pointed at the drawing again, and at her, and raised his eyebrows, and *waited*. He waited with his whole body. His hands lay open on his knees. His breath slowed down to match hers. As if to say: *take whatever time you need; I am not going anywhere.*

No one had waited for Elodie in a very long time.

Her eyes stung, and she was furious at them for stinging, because crying was a luxury for girls who had not walked out of a sinking world, and she blinked the sting away and bent back over the board.

She drew the door.

This was the shard's doing. All morning it had been pressing its vision against the inside of her eyes, insistent as a headache: *the door, the door, the broken door.* So she drew it—a great round doorway set into a hillside, or a hillside shaped like a doorway, it was hard to say which, banded with thirteen stones around its rim. Twelve of the stones she shaded dark. One she left white.

The man made a low sound when he saw it. Not a word. Something older than a word.

And then, because loneliness is heavier than fear, Elodie did the bravest thing she had done since the sinking. She put down the charcoal, laid her hand flat on the board beside the drawing, and *spoke*—really spoke, opening the doors of the First Tongue wide, letting her meaning pour out of her chest and into the room, into the walls, into every piece of carved bone hanging from the rafters, into the man himself if he could catch even a thread of it:

*I am Elodie. My village has gone under. I carry a word for a broken door, and I do not know what the word is, and I am so tired, and I do not know where I am going, and please—*

The bones in the rafters shivered. All of them at once, a small dry rustle, like leaves remembering autumn. The man flinched as if the room had spoken against his ear.

And his face—his tired, guarded, bone-dusted face—did the thing that decided everything. It did not harden. It did not calculate. It *ached*.

He reached out, hesitated, and then touched two fingers gently to the white stone in her drawing. Then he pointed at her. Then, with excruciating care, he pointed at his own chest.

*You. Me. This.*

It wasn't understanding. It was something better than understanding. It was a promise to try.

---

* * *

They spent the day learning each other the way two people learn a country with no shared map.

He showed her his work, laying out the carvings on a cloth: birds, fish, small round charms worn smooth at the holes where cords passed through. Pretty things. Empty things—no, not empty, she corrected herself, feeling them with her fingertips. *Quiet* things. Someone had taken the voices out of these bones, or never let the voices in, and the workmanship was beautiful in the way that locked houses are beautiful.

But when she picked up a carving of a wren and held it, the bone warmed against her palm, and something far down inside it stirred—a murmur, faint as a sleeper turning over—and the man snatched it from her hand with a speed that told her everything.

*You can hear them,* she sent, not really meaning to send it, the thought slipping out of her the way thoughts slip out of tired children. *You can hear the dead, and you pretend you cannot, and you are so lonely it rings off you like a struck pot.*

He couldn't have received the words. She knew he couldn't. But he went white, and set the wren down very carefully, and looked at her for a long moment with an expression she would remember much later, in another life, when she understood what it had cost him: the look of a man who has hidden a treasure so long he has forgotten whether it is a treasure or a wound.

To cover the moment, she drew again. This time she drew the two of them—two stick figures, one tall with a smudge of beard, one small with wild hair—standing together before the round door. Then she added, after consideration, a third figure: a cat, because every household should have a cat, and because she wanted to see him smile.

He studied the drawing. He studied the cat. And then—this was the moment Elodie knew she would stay, whatever staying meant, whatever danger came with it—he picked up the charcoal and, with the grave concentration of a man defusing a trap, drew a fourth figure beside the cat.

A mouse. Enormous. Wearing what appeared to be a tiny hat.

Elodie laughed. It burst out of her before she could stop it—a real laugh, bright and shocking in the dim room, her first since the sinking—and the sound startled them both. The man's mouth twitched upward at one corner, cautiously, as if smiling were a skill he was relearning. The bones in the rafters rustled softly, and Elodie could have sworn they sounded pleased.

*All right,* she sent, to the room, to the man, to whatever was listening. *All right. I will trust you a little. A little is a start.*

Outside, the storm had passed, and the light through the shutters had gone from grey to gold. Somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour, and Elodie counted the strokes out of habit, and stopped at twelve, and felt the shard against her breastbone give its long cold note, and press against the inside of her eyes its oldest picture:

Thirteen stones around a broken door. Twelve dark.

And somewhere far beneath the world, patient as winter, something vast turned its attention northward—toward Vell, toward a small house that smelled of cedar and bone-dust, toward a girl who carried a word she could not yet read.

The man followed her gaze to the window, to the sky, where the moon hung pale and early above the rooftops.

He frowned. And Elodie, watching him frown, understood that he had begun—only begun—to grasp what she had known since the breathing fields: that the thirteenth turn was coming, and it was coming *here*.
