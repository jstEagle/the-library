## 6. The Bone-Carver's Bargain

August had a rule about trouble: it was like rain. You didn't argue with it, you didn't chase it off the roof, and you certainly didn't invite it inside to sit by your fire and eat your bread.

And yet here was trouble, sitting by his fire, eating his bread.

Elodie ate the way she did everything—quietly, completely, with an intensity that suggested bread might be a sacred rite if you took it seriously enough. She held each piece in both hands, studied it, then consumed it with the focus of a scholar deciphering scripture. Crumbs gathered on her chin like snow on a fencepost. She did not wipe them away. August suspected she hadn't noticed them, or possibly that she considered them part of the meal.

"You're going to eat all of it," he said. "Aren't you."

She looked up. Her eyes were grey—the grey of the salt flats at dusk, of old ice, of things that had been washed a long time by a lot of water—and they were fixed on him with an expectation he found deeply unfair. As though *he* were the one who'd shown up unannounced during a curfew storm speaking a language that made dead bones sit up and pay attention.

"I have maybe four days of food," August told her. "I planned for one mouth. One mouth that doesn't eat like it's making up for a famine."

The girl swallowed, set down the last crust with the gravity of a judge laying down a gavel, and said something in the First Tongue.

The words moved through the room like water through a cracked wall. August felt them in his teeth. On the shelf above his workbench, three of his carvings—a sparrow, a hare, a little fat fish he'd made on a slow Tuesday—trembled against each other with a sound like whispered conversation.

"Stop that," he told them. "You're supposed to be decorative."

They did not stop. They never stopped, not really; seven years of pretending his own work was inert had never once fooled the work itself. But tonight the murmuring was louder than usual, insistent, and when Elodie spoke again—pointing at herself, then at him, then out the window toward the storm-lashed street—the bones answered her in a rustle of small dry voices, and August understood, with the particular dread of a man watching his carefully constructed life develop a leak, that they were having a conversation without him.

"That's rude," he said. "Both of you."

* * *

He spent the rest of the evening doing what he always did when the world grew too large: he worked.

The workbench occupied the corner beneath the window, scarred and pale with bone dust, littered with knives finer than any surgeon's and coarser than any carpenter's. August sat down, pulled a shinbone from the bucket of soaked pieces—he preferred them half-cured, still soft enough to persuade—and began to carve without quite deciding what he was carving.

This was how it always started. His hands knew things before he did.

Behind him, Elodie had curled up in the chair nearest the hearth, knees drawn up, the shard of white antler clutched against her chest. She wasn't asleep. He could tell because the antler hummed faintly whenever she breathed deep, a note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and right now it was humming steadily enough to make his fillings ache.

*A translation charm,* he thought, and immediately hated himself for thinking it. *Because obviously. Because why wouldn't I. Why wouldn't I carve the one thing I've spent seven years not carving.*

The Edict Against Magic hung in every public square in Vell, printed in letters tall enough to read from a galloping horse: NO CHARM, NO CANT, NO CRAFT OF THE OLD ART. PENALTY: THE PYRE. It was a good Edict, as far as August's continued survival went. He had built his entire life inside its shadow, selling trinkets stripped so thoroughly of power that even the most paranoid witch-hunter's rod would find nothing but clean, honest bone. The whispers in his carvings were private. Unprovable. Harmless as memory.

But a translation charm—that was different. A translation charm was a working. A real one. The kind of thing Seraphine Voss could smell from across a market square.

His knife slipped. A thin curl of bone fell away, and with it came a whisper, close to his ear though no one stood there:

*The covenant starves.*

August closed his eyes. "Not now," he said.

*The covenant starves,* said the dead, in the voice of a woman who had been dead so long she'd forgotten her own name but remembered her opinions perfectly well. *Thirteen moons, carver. Thirteen moons turning, and nothing fed.*

"I don't know what that means," he said, which was true, and also a lie, because some low animal part of him had known what it meant since the moment the girl had spoken at his door and every bone in his house had turned toward her like flowers toward sun.

Behind him, the humming changed pitch. Elodie had gone rigid in the chair, the antler shard raised, its pale surface catching firelight. Her lips moved—not the First Tongue this time, or not only it—and her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely.

August put down the knife very slowly.

"Elodie."

She didn't answer. The shard flickered, and for half a heartbeat August saw what she saw: a door, vast and dark, split down the middle like a struck bell. Then it was gone, and the girl slumped forward, gasping, crumbs scattering from her chin.

August crossed the room in three strides and caught her before she slid off the chair. She was cold. Not storm-chill cold—cold like a cellar, like stone, like something that had been standing too near an open door into winter.

"Right," he said, mostly to himself, because there was no one else to say it to. "Right. Fine. This is fine."

It was not fine. But he had learned long ago that saying otherwise was a luxury for people with better options.

* * *

He made up a pallet for her by the hearth, using the blanket he'd been saving against a winter he kept telling himself would be milder than the last one. She lay down without being asked twice, which told him something about how long it had been since anyone had offered her anything. Within minutes she was asleep—truly asleep, the antler's hum fading to almost nothing—and August sat at his workbench in the small hours, carving by lamplight, listening to the dead argue about him.

*He'll do it,* said the nameless woman.

*He won't,* said a man with a river-smoothed voice, someone drowned, probably. Drowned men were always pessimists. *Seven years hiding under the Edict's skirts. You don't come out from under skirts like those.*

"He came out once," the woman said. "For the girl. You saw his hands. His hands decided before his head did."

August looked down at his hands. They had indeed decided. Somewhere between the first slip of the knife and now, the shapeless bone in his grip had become unmistakable: a charm in the old style, twin-faced, hinged at the top like a locket. One face bore the spiral script of the First Tongue—or rather, would, once he finished cutting it. The other face was blank, waiting for whatever language lived on the other side of a person's silence.

He knew the design the way he knew his own knuckles. He'd carved it exactly once before, seven years ago, in a cell beneath the witch-hunters' hall, as a demonstration for a woman with iron-grey hair and a voice like a drawn blade.

*Show me it can be harmless,* Seraphine had said. *Show me you can hold the old art in your hands and let none of it through. Do that, boy, and you live.*

So he had. And she had kept her word, which was more than could be said for most of her order, and he had owed her ever since—a debt with interest accruing daily, payable on demand.

Carving a translation charm for a hunted child seemed, on balance, like the sort of payment that would come due all at once.

*The covenant starves,* the dead said again, softer now, almost gentle. *Feed it, carver.*

"I don't even know what the covenant *is*," August muttered.

But he cut the spiral anyway. And as the lamplight filled the grooves, the whispering in the room changed—shifted from argument to attention, dozens of dry voices going quiet at once, the way a forest goes quiet when something large moves through it.

Elodie stirred in her sleep. Without waking, she turned toward the workbench, toward the half-made charm, and smiled.

August decided not to think about that either.

* * *

Morning came grey and rinsed-clean, the storm blown out to sea, curfew lifted. Vell's bells rang the all-clear in their flat municipal way, and carts began squeaking through the streets below, and the city resumed its great performance of being ordinary.

August performed along with it. He swept his step. He set out his tray of wares—harmless sparrows, harmless hares, one ambitious whale—and opened his shutters to the morning trade.

Elodie watched from behind the curtain, as instructed. He had explained the arrangement to her at length, using gestures, diagrams scratched in flour on the table, and increasingly desperate facial expressions. She had watched all of this with grave attention and then pointed at herself, then at the curtain, then nodded once, which he chose to interpret as understanding rather than mockery.

The morning's customers were the usual assortment. Mistress Corva from the dyers' row, buying a bone comb for her daughter's hair and complaining that everything cost more these days, including, apparently, complaints. Two dockhands wanting dice, honest ones—"no luck in 'em, carver, just bone"—which August promised solemnly while the dice themselves whispered *none in us, guv, promise* in voices that made keeping a straight face a professional challenge. An old soldier who bought a small carved dog every month and never said why, and August never asked, because the dog whispered the soldier's dead son's laugh every time he picked it up, and some trades were better left unexamined.

Business as usual. Invisibility as usual.

Then, near midday, a woman stopped at his tray. Plain dress, plain face, the deliberate plainness of someone trained to be overlooked. Her eyes, however, went over his wares with a thoroughness that had nothing to do with shopping.

"Fine work," she said.

"Thank you."

"They say bone-carvers used to put more than shapes in their work." She smiled. "Before the Edict. Old stories, of course."

"Old stories are the best kind," August said. "No one checks them for accuracy."

Her smile stayed exactly where it was. "There's a reward posted. For information regarding a child. Tongued, the notice says. Found speaking strange." She picked up the fat fish, turned it over, set it down. "Strange tongues travel strange distances, they say. Sometimes right past a carver's window in the night."

August kept his hands loose at his sides and his heart rate at something approximating retail calm. "I sleep like the dead," he said, and only he heard the dead snort at that.

"Mm." The woman set a coin on the tray—too much, deliberately—and took the fish. "Lovely day," she said, and walked off toward the square where the notices were posted.

August closed his shutters early.

* * *

"She knows," he told Elodie, back inside, curtain drawn, afternoon light coming through in dusty bars. "Or she guesses. Either way, we're on a clock now."

Elodie sat cross-legged on the floor, watching him with the antler shard in her lap. At the word *clock*—or perhaps at the tone—she tilted her head.

"So here's the bargain," August said, and heard himself continue in the brisk tone of a man negotiating with a supplier rather than surrendering to fate. "Temporary. Strictly temporary. Until the moon turns—thirteen nights, that's all. You stay inside, you don't touch the shutters, you don't sing, hum, whistle, or speak your... your language anywhere near a window. I finish this—" he held up the half-carved charm, "—and we can actually talk, and then we figure out where you go next. Someone. Some place. Not my problem permanently. Understood?"

Elodie considered this speech for a long moment. Then she reached out, took his hand—his carving hand, the one still holding the charm—and pressed it gently against the antler shard.

The hum ran up his arm like cold water. And through it, faint but unmistakable, came a word. Not in the First Tongue. Not in any tongue at all, really—just a meaning, arriving whole, the way meanings arrive in dreams:

*Stay.*

August withdrew his hand as if the shard had bitten him.

"That's cheating," he said. "That is—you can't just—you don't get to *do* that."

Elodie's expression did not change, but something in her eyes did—the smallest loosening, the look of someone who has been holding a rope for a very long time and has just felt it go slack.

"The covenant starves," August said aloud, because the dead had fallen silent and someone had to fill the room. "Whatever that means. Thirteen moons. Twelve nights left. And I am not—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm a trinket-seller. I carve things that do nothing. That's the whole trick of my life. Nothing."

The bones on the shelf murmured. The antler hummed. The girl waited, patient as stone, patient as the realm beneath the world.

August looked at the charm in his palm—twin-faced, half-finished, seven years of careful silence balanced against one word from a mute child.

"Thirteen nights," he said finally. "Then you're someone else's problem."

He was already reaching for his finest knife as he said it, and both of them knew it, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon and into the evening, and the charm came alive under his hands faster than any piece he'd ever made—as though the bone remembered the design from seven years ago and had only been waiting for permission. Spiral script on one face. On the other, he cut the shapes Elodie traced for him in the air, letter by letter, her fingers moving with the sureness of someone writing her own name.

By lamplight, with the moon rising thin and hungry over Vell's rooftops, he held the finished charm up between them.

"Now," he said. "We try. You say something. Anything. Small words. Start small."

Elodie took the charm in both hands, the way she'd taken the bread. She studied it. She looked at him. And then she spoke—one soft phrase in the First Tongue, and the charm warmed, and the sounds folded over on themselves like paper, and—

"—like bread," said a voice from the charm. Her voice, or nearly it, translated into Vellish with a slight accent of somewhere very far below. "*It works like bread.*"

August stared. "Did you just say it works like *bread*?"

"*Like bread,*" the charm repeated helpfully.

Elodie grinned—her first grin, wide and crooked and entirely human—and August felt something in his chest crack along a seam he'd thought was healed over years ago.

"Well," he managed. "First words in a new language. Most people start with *hello*."

But outside, far off across the city, a bell began to ring—not the curfew bell, not the all-clear, but the sharp triple stroke of the witch-hunters' summons, rolling through the streets like a dropped stone. And beneath it, so faint only August and every bone in the house could hear it, the dead began to whisper:

*The hunters ride. The covenant starves. Thirteen moons, carver. Twelve nights left.*

Elodie's grin faded. She looked at the window, then at him, and pressed the charm to her chest.

August blew out the lamp.
