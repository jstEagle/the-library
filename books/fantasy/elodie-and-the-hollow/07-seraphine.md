## 7. Seraphine

The interrogation room in the Vell garrison had been designed by someone who understood that cruelty was mostly a matter of furniture.

Seraphine Voss had inherited the room along with her command, and in nine years she had changed exactly one thing about it: she'd replaced the single chair opposite the desk with a second chair, padded, comfortable, positioned close enough that a prisoner and interrogator sat nearly knee to knee. Her lieutenants thought it was a trick. It was not a trick. It was simply that Seraphine had learned, over a long career of prying truth out of frightened people, that fear made liars of everyone, and that a man who was comfortable would talk just to keep the conversation going, because talking to someone warm and patient was the closest thing to kindness his life contained.

She sat now across from a smuggler named Doss Marlowe, who had been caught three nights ago at the Salt Gate with forty pounds of untaxed saffron hidden in barrels labeled as salted eel, and who had spent those three nights in a cell telling anyone who'd listen that he was a simple man, an honest man, a man who loved the Edict and everything it stood for.

"Doss," Seraphine said, pouring tea from the pot between them. "Do you know why you're really here?"

"Because I love the Edict," Doss said, "and the Edict loves me back."

"That's the spirit." She pushed the cup toward him. He looked at it the way a starving man looks at a trap. "Drink. It's chamomile. If I wanted to poison you I'd have done it before you woke up, and saved us both the tea."

He drank. She let him drink for a while, saying nothing, watching the steam curl. Outside the narrow window, Vell went about its morning — cart wheels on cobbles, a fishmonger arguing with a gull, the bells of the Foundry Quarter marking the hour with their usual enthusiasm, as if the hour were something to be proud of.

"The saffron is a footnote," Seraphine said at last. "You'll pay a fine. You'll pay it twice, once to me and once to the magistrate who pretends he isn't taking a cut, and then you'll go home to your wife, who I'm told makes excellent dumplings, and you will never smuggle anything again except perhaps your own opinions, which are worthless anyway. That's the saffron."

Doss's shoulders came down half an inch. "And the... other thing?"

"The other thing is a child." Seraphine folded her hands on the desk. She was aware of how her hands looked — scarred knuckles, a burn mark along the left thumb from a fire eleven years ago that had taken a safehouse and two colleagues and most of her eyebrows for a season. She did not hide them. Hands told prisoners what mouths wouldn't: *I have done hard things and I am still sitting here.* "A girl, eight or ten years old, mute in every tongue but one. She passed through the river villages in the last month. Three people saw her. Two of them are dead, and the third is the reason I'm pouring you tea instead of handing you to Magistrate Corren, who does not believe in chairs with cushions."

Doss set down the cup very carefully, as though it might go off.

"I didn't see any child," he said.

"No," Seraphine agreed pleasantly. "But you sold passage on your barge to a man named Halloway Fen, and Halloway Fen saw her, and Halloway Fen has been missing for nineteen days, which means either he ran off with someone's wife or something found him first. You're going to tell me which."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Corren gets you, and Corren believes the Edict permits him to ask questions until the answers stop mattering." She refilled the tea. "I don't enjoy threatening people, Doss. It's bad for the digestion. But I'll be honest with you, since honesty seems rare in your profession: I am more afraid of this child than I have been of anything in twenty years of hunting, and I do not frighten easily, and when I am afraid, I become extremely thorough. You want me thorough on your behalf or thorough on your case?"

Doss looked at the tea. Looked at her. Looked, briefly, out the window at the gull, which was winning its argument with the fishmonger.

"She came up the towpath," he said quietly. "Walking. Barefoot, in the frost month, walking barefoot like the ground was warm. My deckhand wanted to give her bread. Halloway said no. Halloway said—" Doss swallowed. "Halloway said she was talking to the water. Not near it. To it. And the water talked back, and my deckhand heard it too, and my deckhand hasn't slept right since. He hears humming. In the hull. At night."

"Does he."

"Seraphine." Doss leaned forward, and there was something in his face beyond fear now — something older and sadder, the look of a man remembering a story his grandmother told and never quite believed. "My grandmother used to say the Hollow wasn't empty. She said it was hungry, and that hunger doesn't stay below forever. She said that's why the old covenant existed, before the purges broke it. Keep the door fed, keep the door shut."

"Your grandmother also believed that eating eel on a Sunday gave you dreams of your future husband, and you've never married, so we can see how her prophecies run."

Doss almost smiled. Then he didn't. "Where is the girl, Commander? Because if she's coming to Vell, I'd rather know than wonder."

"So would I," Seraphine said. "That's the entire problem."

* * *

Afterward she stood at the window of her office for a long time, doing the thing her lieutenants called *the statue*, which was standing motionless while her mind walked through a city none of them could see.

The office was spare. A desk, a map of the city pinned with red thread where reports clustered, a shelf of casebooks going back thirty years, and on the wall behind the desk, in a plain frame, a charcoal drawing of a farmhouse with a plum tree beside it. The drawing was childish — she had made it herself, at fourteen, the year before everything — and she kept it not out of sentiment but out of discipline. It was good to remember exactly what you were fighting for. It was better to remember exactly what you had failed to save.

Her family had died in the autumn of the Grey Tide, the year the Hollow rose high enough to touch the lowland farms east of Vell. The official record — the record Seraphine herself had helped write, years later, as a young officer eager to make sense of senselessness — said *flood, unusual, cause unknown*. Forty-one souls. Her father. Her mother. Her little brother Tam, who was seven and afraid of geese and had been, the last time she saw him, attempting to befriend one through sheer stubbornness.

She had been in Vell that autumn, apprenticed already to the hunt, learning the shapes of wards she'd never need. She had come home to a farm that was simply *gone* — not burned, not broken, but sunk, the way a boat sinks, straight down into ground that had turned grey and fine as ash. The survivors spoke of a sound under the earth for three nights before. A sound like chewing.

The Edict Against Magic had been written in blood long before her family died. But Seraphine had rewritten it in her own, privately, in the language of grief: *never again.* Whatever lived beneath the world, whatever had chewed through the roots of her childhood, it would not rise again while she drew breath. Every purge she had led, every practitioner dragged into a courtroom, every charm confiscated and burned — all of it stood on that foundation, and the foundation held, because she never let herself look at it too closely. Looking closely was how doubt got in. Doubt was a splinter. Ignore it long enough and the flesh grew over it and you forgot it was there.

Mostly.

"Commander?" Her lieutenant, Bramwen Okafor, stood in the doorway with a folder. Bramwen was twenty-six, earnest, and possessed of the unshakable belief that paperwork could solve most evils if applied in sufficient quantity. "The river reports. And, ah. Something else."

"Give me the something else first. Bad news keeps better."

Bramwen hesitated. This was another thing about Bramwen: he felt guilty delivering bad news, as though the news were his personal failure. "A charm-seller in the Tannery Row market reported a customer yesterday. Bought bone dust, binding wax, and a length of silvered wire. The combination is..." He consulted the folder, unnecessarily; Seraphine knew the combination. "...consistent with translation work. Speech-bridging. Which is proscribed under articles four and nine."

"Who was the seller?"

"A woman named Petrick. Harmless. Sells luck-charms to sailors, ninety percent of which are painted buttons."

"And the buyer?"

Bramwen turned the folder around. On it was a sketch, done from Petrick's description by the garrison artist, who was better at horses than faces. Even so, Seraphine felt the floor tilt very slightly under her boots, the way it always did, the way it had for seven years whenever a certain name surfaced.

August Okonkwo.

* * *

Seven years ago, in the third year of her command, Seraphine had raided a cellar workshop off Cooper's Lane on a tip about unlicensed warding. What she'd found instead was a young man, barely twenty, sitting amid a hundred small carved bones — birds, fish, moons, little round beads — and every one of them, when she picked it up, had hummed against her palm like a struck bell.

She had known immediately what that meant. Bone-listening. The oldest gift, the rarest, the one the old texts called *the ear of the covenant*. The texts also said the gift had died out with the last of the wardens, decades ago. Apparently the texts hadn't checked Cooper's Lane.

By every article of the Edict, she should have taken him in. By every instinct of the woman she'd become, she should have taken him in. Instead she had stood in that cellar for a long moment, holding a carved wren that whispered something soft and sorrowful against her skin, and she had looked at the boy's face — terrified, exhausted, braced for the pyre — and she had seen, absurdly, her brother Tam. Not the resemblance. Just the age. Just the bare, undefended youth of him.

"You can hear them," she had said. Not a question.

"Yes," he'd whispered.

"Do they hurt you?"

"...No. They're lonely. Mostly they're lonely."

And that — that stupid, unbearable answer — was why she'd offered him the bargain instead of the cell. Renounce the craft. Carve only dead things into dead shapes. Never speak to the voices again, never work a living charm, and live. She had told herself it was mercy. She had told herself it was strategy: a bone-listener left alive and watched was worth more than one burned. Both things were true. Neither was the whole truth. The whole truth was that some nights, in the quiet of her rooms, Seraphine missed being spoken to by something that wasn't afraid of her, and she could not quite bring herself to extinguish the one person in Vell who might understand what that loneliness was like.

She had regretted the mercy perhaps six times. She was beginning to suspect she was about to regret it a seventh.

"Bramwen," she said. "Pull Okonkwo's file. All of it. And put eyes on Cooper's Lane — discreet ones. I want to know everyone who visits that shop, everyone who leaves it, and whether anyone small and barefoot has gone in or out."

"You think the child's with him?"

"I think," Seraphine said slowly, "that a man who spent seven years being perfectly, obediently silent has suddenly started buying translation components. Which means he has something he needs to say to someone who cannot hear him. Or—" she stopped.

"Or?" Bramwen prompted.

Or someone who cannot speak to anyone else. Someone whose only tongue was the one the Edict existed to bury. Someone who had come up a frozen towpath barefoot, talking to water, and left a wake of sleepless men and silence behind her.

"Or nothing," she said. "Get the file."

When he'd gone, she stood again at the window. Below, the city moved and murmured, ten thousand lives going about their business, unaware that beneath their cellars and their graves and their oldest stones, something waited with its mouth open. She thought of Tam and the goose. She thought of the sound like chewing. She thought of a carved wren whispering *lonely, lonely* against her palm, and how she had carried that word out of the cellar like a splinter of her own, working deeper every year.

*If the child reaches him,* she thought, *he'll choose her. People always choose the voice that calls them over the hand that spared them.*

She pressed her fingers briefly to the charcoal farmhouse on the wall — the plum tree, the crooked chimney, the small stubborn figure chasing a bird across the yard — and then she turned back to her desk and began, with the terrible patience of a woman who had never once stopped believing she was right, to write the warrant.

It was, she noticed distantly, the day the moon would begin its thirteenth turn.
