## 8. The Market of Whispers

The rain had stopped by the time August decided he could no longer avoid the inevitable, which was typical of Vell. The city always cleared its weather the moment you'd resigned yourself to drowning in it, as if to say: *there, now you have no excuse.*

He had excuses anyway. He stacked them up on his workbench like kindling and looked at each one honestly.

Excuse one: Elodie needed food that wasn't dried herring and the heel of a loaf going stale on the shelf. This was true, and also the smallest of his problems.

Excuse two: the girl had been with him four days now, and in those four days the dead in his carvings had not stopped murmuring. Not the usual drowsy complaints—*cold, cold, who carved me, put me down*—but something sharper. A word repeated over and over through every piece of bone in the shop, high and urgent as a smoke alarm made of teeth.

*Covenant. Covenant. Covenant.*

Excuse three, which was not an excuse at all but a confession he kept swallowing like a stone: he needed answers, and there was only one place left in Vell where a man might find them.

"You'll stay here," he told Elodie, crouching so they were eye to eye. She sat on his work stool with her legs folded under her, the white antler shard cradled in both hands like a bird she was afraid would startle. "Bolt the door behind me. Don't open it for anyone. If anyone knocks—" He hesitated. How did you explain witch-hunters to a child who spoke only a language older than hunting? "If anyone knocks, be very quiet and very small."

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. Then she set down the antler, picked up the stub of charcoal he'd given her, and drew on the floorboard: a stick figure with August's crooked shoulders, standing beside a door. From the door, wavy lines radiated outward—the First Tongue's mark for *waiting*, he'd learned, or possibly *patience*, or possibly *the smell of bread*. Context mattered enormously in a language built from the bones of meaning.

"Yes," August said. "Waiting."

She added a second figure outside the door. It had a hat. Then she gave it a large, exaggerated frown and crossed its arms.

"That's a bit unkind," August said. "I don't look like that."

She drew a third figure. Smaller. Herself, presumably, hiding under the workbench, drawn with such accurate self-pity that August felt something twist in his chest.

"No one will find you," he said, and hated how much he wanted it to be true. "I'll be back before the lamps are lit."

She watched him go with the antler shard held up to one eye, and just before he pulled the door shut, he heard—not with his ears, but in the old way, the way he heard everything—a single word drift off the shard like heat off a stove.

*Careful.*

The dead rarely bothered with warnings. They were mostly concerned with their own discomforts. That one word followed him down three flights of stairs and out into the wet evening streets of Vell like a hand between his shoulder blades.

* * *

The Market of Whispers did not exist, officially. Unofficially, it existed beneath the Tanner's Reach aqueduct, in a stretch of disused cistern that the city had abandoned two hundred years ago when the river changed its mind about geography. To get there, August walked past the tanneries with their reek of lime and ruin, counted eleven arches, and slipped through a gap in the wall that only looked impassable if you were the sort of person who believed walls meant what they said.

Inside, the dark smelled of candlewax, wet stone, and dozens of bodies trying very hard not to be noticed. Which was, in its way, its own kind of perfume. August had missed it more than he'd admitted.

Lanterns hung from iron hooks at intervals, hooded so their light fell in small private pools. Between the pools moved the market: a woman selling bottled breath—young breath, old breath, the last breath of a saint (allegedly); a man whose stall was nothing but mirrors turned to face the wall, sold by appointment only; a knot of hedge-witches arguing in whispers over a crate of smuggled rowan. Every voice here was pitched low. Not because loudness attracted the hunters—though it did—but because whispering was the market's whole theology. You came here to say quietly what could not be said at all above ground.

"August Okonkwo." The voice came from a shadow that resolved into Maren Quill, proprietress of the market's only tea stall, which served tea that was definitely just tea, and that was precisely why everyone trusted it. She was seventy if she was a day, round as a kettle, with eyes that had seen every kind of trouble and found most of it overpriced. "Seven years, and now you come crawling back to my cistern. Sit. You look like a man who's been carrying something heavy and pretending it's a coat."

"Maren." He took the offered stool. "Is it that obvious?"

"You've got the look. All my best customers get the look eventually." She poured without asking. "So. What is it? Love? Debt? Magic?"

"A child."

Maren's eyebrows performed a slow, eloquent ascent. "August. You know I love you like the nephew I never asked for. But if you tell me you've gotten a child, I'm going to need to sit down myself."

"Not like that." He lowered his voice until it was barely a shape in the air. "A girl. Eight years old, maybe nine. She turned up at my door four nights ago during the storm. She can't speak—not our tongue, any tongue. Except she *can*. She speaks the First Tongue, Maren. Fluently. Like it's the only language there ever was."

The teapot stopped mid-pour. A drop fell, hit the counter, and in the sudden silence August would have sworn the whole market leaned in to listen.

Maren set the pot down with exaggerated care. "Say that again. Quietly."

"The First Tongue. And she carries a piece of white antler that hums. And my bones—" He glanced around, then leaned closer. "Every carving I own has been saying one word since she arrived. *Covenant.* Over and over. I thought I'd misheard at first. I don't mishear."

"No," Maren said softly. "You never did. That was always your curse, dear boy." She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture August recognized as her version of rolling up sleeves. "Come with me. There's someone you need to hear this from, and it isn't me. I sell tea. Old Tobin sells the past."

* * *

Old Tobin's stall was at the far end of the cistern, past the mirror-man and the breath-bottles, in a alcove where the original Roman-era brickwork still showed through the plaster. Tobin himself was less a man than an arrangement of blankets, beard, and one ferociously bright eye. He had been old when August first came to the market at nineteen; whatever process was making him older appeared to have stalled out of sheer stubbornness somewhere around ninety.

"Tobin," Maren said, "the bone-carver's back. And he's brought a story worth more than he knows."

Tobin's bright eye fixed on August. "Bone-carver. The quiet one. Seraphine Voss's student." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Sit, boy. My stools are all knees and regret, but they hold."

August sat. "You know why I'm here?"

"I know why everyone's here. They're here because the world's gone wrong in a way they can feel but not name." Tobin drew a clay pipe from somewhere in the blankets, considered it, and put it away again—no smoking near the candles, some rule even Tobin obeyed. "Thirteen moons ago. Does that number mean anything to you?"

"It means a year."

"It means a year *and a warning*." Tobin held up one gnarled finger. "Thirteen moons ago, the moon rose full and stayed full a night too long. Ask any astronomer in Vell—they'll tell you it was atmospheric refraction, which is a word clever men use when they mean *we don't know and we'd rather not think about it*. Since then, things have been sliding. Quietly. The way ice slides before it cracks."

"What kind of things?"

"Charm-work failing. Wards thinning. Beasts gone strange at the edges of the map. And below—" Tobin tapped the floor with his staff, and the tap rang oddly, deeper than stone should sound, as though the floor were a drumhead stretched over something vast. "Below, boy. The Hollow. You know the stories."

Everyone knew the stories. The realm beneath the world, older than the city, older than the Edict, older probably than storytelling itself. The place the dead went when they weren't done being anywhere. The place the First Tongue belonged to. August had spent seven years pretending he didn't believe in it, which required a daily discipline of not listening to the dead in his bones, several of whom claimed firsthand experience.

"I know the stories," he said carefully. "What do they have to do with a thirteenth moon?"

Tobin and Maren exchanged a glance. It was Maren who answered, and her voice had lost its teasing entirely.

"There was a covenant," she said. "Not a story—a covenant. An agreement, older than the Edict, older than the purges. The Hollow is hungry, August. It's always been hungry—it's hunger, mostly, wearing the shape of a place. And once, long ago, someone made a bargain with it. Thirteen anchors, thirteen voices, holding the door between here and below. The Hollow agreed to stay down and stay quiet. In exchange, it was fed—not blood, nothing so dramatic. Fed with *speech*. With names spoken in the First Tongue. With remembrance. Hunger doesn't only want flesh, dear. Sometimes it wants to be acknowledged."

"And the anchors?"

"Were people. Living people, born to it, bound to it. Each anchor carried a word of the covenant—one syllable of a very long sentence, if you like—and as long as the anchors lived and remembered, the sentence held." Maren wrapped her hands around her own untouched cup. "Twelve moons ago, the first anchor died. Not naturally, we think. And each full moon since, another has gone dark. Eleven now. Maybe twelve. We can't count them anymore; the ones who kept the count are gone."

The dead in August's pocket—two small charms he carried everywhere, a sparrow's wishbone and a knucklebone of unknown origin—had gone silent. Not calm-silent. Listening-silent. Even the dead, apparently, wanted to hear this part.

"So the Hollow's getting hungry," August said. His mouth felt dry. "Hungrier each month."

"Hungrier each month," Tobin confirmed. "And here's the arithmetic that should keep you awake, bone-carver: thirteen minus twelve leaves one. One anchor left. One voice. And if that voice goes dark before the next turn of the moon—"

"The covenant fails. The door opens."

"The door doesn't open," Tobin said. "Doors opening implies something on the other side wants *in*, with intention. The Hollow doesn't want in. It wants *outward*. Like water finding cracks. Like cold finding gaps in a window. It won't invade, boy. It will simply... seep. And everything it seeps into goes grey and quiet and stops being quite itself." He looked at August with that one fierce eye. "Your village ever have a well that went bad? Everything downstream of a poisoned spring. Now imagine the spring is underneath everything."

August thought of Elodie's drawings. The village with the wavy lines rising through it. *Sank quiet,* she'd shown him, in pictures because she had no other words he understood. Houses dissolving into grey. People becoming outlines.

"She came from the edge of it," he said hoarsely. "Her village. Whatever happened there—it already started happening."

Maren reached over and gripped his wrist, her hand surprisingly strong. "Then you understand why the hunters want her."

"I understand why they think they want her." The words came out harder than he intended. "Seraphine thinks killing the last anchor ends magic forever. Cuts the Hollow off at the root. She thinks she's saving the world."

"She's half right," Tobin said. "That's the tragedy of zealots, boy. They're always half right, and the half they're wrong about is the half that kills everyone." He shifted among his blankets. "Kill the last anchor, and yes—the covenant dies. But a dying covenant doesn't seal a door. It *breaks* one. There's a difference between a lock and a snapped lock, and Seraphine Voss has spent thirty years confusing the two."

The cistern seemed colder suddenly. August became aware of the market around them—the whisper-voices, the hooded lanterns, the whole hidden congregation of people who had spent their lives pretending magic wasn't real while quietly keeping it alive—and how small it all was, how human, how utterly insufficient against a hunger beneath the world.

"How do I fix it?" he asked. "The covenant. If the anchors are dying—if there's only one left—"

"Why do you assume it's yours to fix?"

"Because she's sleeping in my shop. Because she drew my face in charcoal four days ago and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. Because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because every bone I own has been saying *covenant* since she walked in, and I'm starting to suspect they weren't talking to me. They were talking about her."

Old Tobin was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gentled into something almost kind.

"Covenants can be reforged. It's rare—it's maybe never—but the old texts say it can be done. A new anchor raised where the old ones failed. A new voice to carry the word." He paused. "But reforging isn't free, bone-carver. Nothing true is. The old wardens gave their *lives* to it, in the end, when the hunger pressed hard. And a new covenant needs something given freely, willingly, completely. Something the giver can't take back."

"Like what?"

Tobin looked at him—at his ears, specifically, in a way that made August's skin prickle.

"That depends," Tobin said, "on what the giver has that the Hollow wants. And the Hollow, boy, has always been hungry for exactly one thing above all others."

"*Speech*," August whispered. "Names. Remembrance. Voices."

"Now you're doing arithmetic." Tobin settled back into his blankets. "Go home. Feed your guest. Think about what you'd give, and what you couldn't. And August—" the bright eye caught the lantern light, "—if Seraphine Voss finds that child before you find your answer, none of the rest will matter. She's close. My listeners say she's pulled every informant in the Lower City. She's looking for a tongued child, and she's looking at bone-carvers."

"She wouldn't suspect me."

"Boy. You were her student. You're the only practitioner in Vell she trusts enough to suspect."

* * *

August walked home the long way, through streets gone soft with fog, and let the market's words settle over him like the damp.

*A new voice to carry the word. Something given freely, completely. Something the giver can't take back.*

His fingers found the charms in his pocket without his permission—the sparrow's wishbone, the nameless knucklebone. For seven years they had been his secret companions, his curse and his comfort both. The dead grumbling in his carvings had been the closest thing he'd had to family since Seraphine Voss spared him and exiled him in the same breath. The idea of silence—not imposed silence, the silence he'd built his life around, but a silence given away, emptied out, permanent—

He stopped under a lamp at the corner of Tanner's Reach and stood there longer than a sensible man would.

Then he went home, because a child was waiting behind a bolted door, drawing pictures of him on the floorboards, and whatever the arithmetic demanded, it did not demand it tonight.

The lamp above his own door was lit.

He had not left it lit.

August stood very still in the fog, heart hammering, and read the paper pinned to his door in a fine, severe hand he would have known anywhere—in any light, in any life, dead or alive:

**CARVER — WE SHOULD TALK. YOU KNOW WHERE. — S.V.**

From inside the shop, faint through the wood, came the sound of the antler shard humming—and beneath it, in the First Tongue, in a voice like a struck bell, Elodie speaking one word over and over, patient and grave, to whoever or whatever was listening on the other side of the door.

Later, when he understood the language better, he would learn what she'd been saying.

That night, all he knew was that it sounded like a warning.
