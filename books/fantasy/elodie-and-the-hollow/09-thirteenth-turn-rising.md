## 9. Thirteenth Turn Rising

Elodie dreamed of the door again.

She had stopped fighting the dreams somewhere around the fourth night in August's attic, because fighting them only made them louder, and because—though she would never have found words for this, even if she'd had any—the dreams were not unkind. They were heavy, the way a wool blanket is heavy. They pressed down on her and held her and showed her things.

The door was vast. That was always the first thing. Vast and old and made of something that was neither wood nor stone nor bone but all three at once, grown rather than built, its grain swirling like frozen smoke. And across its face, set into it like nails into a coffin lid—or like stars into a sky, she could never decide which—stood thirteen anchors.

Twelve of them were dark.

She knew they were dark the way you know a room is empty before you look inside it: by the quality of the silence. Each anchor had once burned with a light that wasn't light, a warmth that wasn't warmth, and each one had gone out, one by one, over thirteen turnings of the moon, and now only the thirteenth still glimmered, faint as an ember under ash, guttering.

And the door was leaning.

Not open. Not yet. But leaning, the way a tired man leans against a door he is too polite to push. Behind it, something breathed. Elodie had felt that breath on her face in the dream, cold and grey and patient, and it had smelled like her village did on the morning after it sank quiet—like wet stone and old snow and nothing at all.

In the dream, she walked along the row of anchors. Her bare feet made no sound, because there was no floor, only the idea of one. She touched the first dark anchor and it crumbled at her fingertip like bread left too long in milk. The second did the same. The third. She went down the line touching each one, and each one fell to dust, and behind her the door leaned a little further with every anchor lost, and the breathing grew a little deeper, a little closer, a little more *interested*.

Then she came to the last anchor—the thirteenth—and it was not a stone at all.

It was a face.

* * *

She woke in the attic with straw in her hair and her heart going like a rabbit's, and for a long while she lay still and listened to the house settle around her. Below, she could hear August moving through his workshop—the soft scrape of his knife on bone, the clink of a cup set down too hard because he'd forgotten he'd already set it down. He talked to himself when he worked. She'd noticed that. Little mutters, half-words, the way people talk when they think no one is listening. Once she'd heard him say *not tonight, Marta, I'm busy*, to no one, and had spent a happy quarter hour trying to work out who Marta was.

Elodie sat up in the dark and reached for the antler shard.

It hung around her neck on a cord August had given her, along with a stern lecture—in gestures and frowns, since their shared vocabulary consisted of about eleven hand-signs and a great deal of pointing—that she was never, ever to show it to anyone. She wrapped her fingers around it now and felt it wake against her palm, humming faintly, like a struck bell that had never quite finished ringing.

The visions came easier in the dark. She didn't know if that was true for everyone or only for her; there was no one to ask. But in the dark the shard warmed, and the warm spread up her arm and behind her eyes, and the attic fell away, and she saw.

The door. The thirteen anchors. Twelve dark, one dim.

But tonight the vision went further than it ever had before.

Tonight she saw what the anchors *were*. Not stones. Not nails. People. Or they had been, once—figures standing in a ring before the door, arms raised, mouths open, singing or speaking or holding some word so long and so large that their bodies had become the shape of it. Wardens. The word arrived in her mind whole, the way First Tongue words did, without passing through her mouth at all: *wardens*. Thirteen wardens, and twelve of them hollow now, empty coats of grey dust, and the thirteenth—

The thirteenth flickered.

And in the flicker, Elodie saw the vision do something it had never done before. It turned. It looked back at her.

Not the door. Not the breathing thing behind it. The *vision itself*, the shard's showing, tilted toward her like a curious bird, and in the last anchor's dying light she saw a figure begin to form—a small figure, child-sized, mud-streaked, with a cord around its neck and a white shard in its fist.

Herself.

Her breath caught and the vision shattered. She was back in the attic, gasping, the shard hot enough now that she had to let go of it. It swung against her collarbone, cooling, humming its unfinished note.

Downstairs, August's scraping had stopped.

* * *

He appeared at the top of the ladder a few minutes later, candle in hand, hair sticking up on one side where he'd clearly been resting his head on his fist and drifted off. He looked at her sitting bolt upright in the straw, took in her wide eyes and the antler shard still glowing faintly against her chest, and his shoulders did the thing they did when he was deciding between several bad options and had picked the least bad one.

"You're supposed to be asleep," he said, which was pointless, because she couldn't understand him, and they both knew it. He said things like that anyway. She had decided early on that he said them to comfort himself, and she found this so endearing that she'd begun keeping a mental list of his useless sentences. *You're supposed to be asleep. Don't touch that. We really need to work on your Vellish.* That last one he'd said while gesturing at her, then at himself, then at his own mouth, then throwing up his hands entirely.

She pointed at the shard, then at her own eyes, then made the sign they'd invented for *dream*—two fingers walking down through the air, like something sinking.

August's face changed. He came up the ladder properly and sat down across from her, cross-legged, the candle between them, and he held out his hand—not taking, asking. She gave him the shard and watched him hold it gingerly, the way other people held snakes.

"It's warmer," he murmured. "It's never been—" He stopped himself, glancing at her, and she realized he'd been about to say *it's never been like this in seven years of me having it near my bones and pretending I don't notice.* He'd told her that much, in his halting way, the second night: that he could hear the dead through carved bone, that he'd spent seven years carving the hearing out of everything he made, that silence was his trade and his hiding place. He'd confessed it like a man handing over contraband.

Now he turned the shard in the candlelight and frowned at it, and Elodie, watching him frown, felt the dream rise up in her again—the row of anchors, the crumbling dust, the small figure forming in the last light—and her stomach clenched.

Because she had recognized the figure. Of course she had. But that wasn't the part that frightened her.

The part that frightened her was that the figure hadn't looked afraid.

* * *

In the morning she asked him for paper.

This required some doing. Their language of signs covered *eat*, *sleep*, *danger*, *stay*, *go*, *water*, *thank you*, and a gesture August used constantly that seemed to mean roughly *gods give me strength*, though she suspected it was aimed at her more often than at the gods. *Paper* was not among the eleven. So she took his hand—he flinched, he always flinched first, then let her—and led him to the corner of the workshop where he kept his charcoal sticks for marking measurements on bone, and she held one up and looked at him with what she hoped was a sufficiently tragic expression.

"Charcoal," he said. "You want... you want to draw?"

She nodded so hard her neck hurt.

He found her the backs of six failed carvings—trinkets whose whispers he'd carved away until nothing was left but smooth blank bone—and a stub of charcoal, and he cleared a space on his workbench with the ceremony of a man preparing an operating table, and then he stood back and watched her with his arms folded and his mouth doing the tight worried thing.

Elodie drew the door.

She drew it big, spilling off the edges of the first piece of bone onto a second and a third, which August silently slid together for her. She drew the grain of it, the frozen-smoke swirls, the great leaning weight of it. Then she drew the thirteen anchors in a row across its face, and above each one she drew a little flame, and then she licked her thumb and smudged out twelve of the flames, pressing her thumbprint into the charcoal so hard the bone squeaked.

August had gone very still.

She drew the wardens next—thirteen figures in a ring, arms raised, mouths open—and then, because the vision had shown her and her hands knew it even if she didn't, she smudged twelve of those figures into grey ghosts too, leaving one standing, flickering, alone.

Then she paused.

Her charcoal hovered over the last warden. In the vision, the figure had been forming, resolving, becoming specific. And she understood—with the terrible clarity that visions leave behind, like footprints in snow—that if she drew what she had seen, she would be drawing a person. A real one. Someone who existed, here, in this city, above this realm, within reach of the grey tide and the witch-hunters and the moon that was growing fat and wrong in the night sky.

She almost drew a stranger. She honestly tried. She started a jawline that belonged to no one.

But her hand betrayed her, the way honest hands do. The jawline became familiar. The nose followed. The slightly crooked brow, the deep-set tired eyes, the hair that stuck up on one side where its owner rested his head on his fist.

When she finally put the charcoal down, August Okonkwo's face stared up from the last anchor of the Hollow's door, drawn in charcoal on a failed charm, with a little flame sketched above his head that she had not meant to make look so much like a candle.

The silence in the workshop lasted long enough for her to hear the house settle twice.

"That's me," August said finally, very quietly. "That's—why am I on your doom-door, Elodie?"

She shook her head. She wished, with a ferocity that surprised her, that she could answer. She pointed at the drawing, then at the smudged flames, then counted on her fingers—one, two, three—all the way to thirteen, and then pointed at the sky, and made the slow circle of the moon with her hands, and then, because she had no other way to say it, she pointed at the thirteenth anchor, at his face, and then at the flame above it, and then she closed her fist slowly, snuffing it.

His face went the color of old tallow.

"No," he said. "No, no, no. I'm not—I'm a *charm-seller*. I sell *buttons*, basically. I sell buttons with delusions." He was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the exact path worn into his floorboards by seven years of exactly this kind of pacing. "I hide. That's the whole job. Hiding. You don't put the hider on the—on the *door*—"

Elodie watched him wear his groove in the floor and felt something shift in her chest. It was fear, still—it was fear all the way through, fear like marrow—but threaded through it now was something else, something with structure, something almost like a spine. Because she had seen the door lean. She had felt the breath behind it. And she had seen herself standing in the last light, unafraid, and she had understood, in the wordless way of the First Tongue, that the vision had not come to frighten her.

It had come to *ask*.

* * *

That evening, while August pretended to eat supper and actually rearranged the same potato around his plate forty times, Elodie sat by the window with the antler shard in her lap and let it show her one more time. Just a peek. Just the edge of it.

The door. The anchors. The twelve dead wardens, and the thirteenth place waiting, shaped like a question.

And beneath the question, so faint she might have imagined it, the shard hummed a new note—a lower one, patient, almost gentle—and with the hum came a knowing that settled over her like the wool-blanket weight of her dreams:

*One voice must hold the word. One voice must stand in the light until the door remembers how to be shut.*

The moon rose over Vell, swollen and strange, one night shy of full.

Below her, in his workshop, August had stopped pacing. She could hear him talking to someone—his dead, his bones, his Marta, whoever they were—and though she couldn't make out the words, she could hear the shape of them, urgent and rising, and once, sharply, the sound of a carved trinket being thrown across a room.

Elodie closed her hand around the shard and made herself a promise, there in the dark, in the language she had and no one else did: whatever the thirteenth anchor needed, whoever it needed, she would find a way to say it.

Even if saying it cost her everything.

Even if the price was written, already, in charcoal, on a piece of failed bone downstairs, wearing a tired man's face.

The moon climbed. Somewhere far below the world, something ancient leaned against its door, and waited, and breathed.
