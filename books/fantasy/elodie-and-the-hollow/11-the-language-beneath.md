## 11. The Language Beneath

The charm failed eleven times before it worked.

August knew this because he had counted—not out of any particular discipline, but because counting was what his hands did when his mind was busy elsewhere. Eleven failures, each one a small bone disc carved with spirals meant to catch Elodie's words and turn them into something his ears could hold. Eleven discs that had warmed in her palm, flickered faintly with borrowed heat, and then gone quiet as river stones.

The twelfth was different.

He'd carved it from the knucklebone of an ox that had died old, in a warm barn, surrounded by daughters. August had chosen it for exactly that reason. The bones of contented creatures held their shape better; the dead who had died satisfied were less inclined to chatter over the living. He had etched into it not the market-spirals of his trinkets but the true patterns—the ones he hadn't touched in seven years, the ones Seraphine Voss would have flayed him for even sketching on a scrap of paper. Deep grooves. Listening-grooves. Grooves shaped like the inside of an ear, if an ear belonged to something patient and very old.

Elodie sat across the workbench from him, knees drawn up on her chair like a bird on a wire, watching his hands the way she watched everything: completely, hungrily, as though the world might be taken away mid-look.

"It's going to itch," he warned her. "Probably. I don't actually know. Nobody's ever—well." He turned the disc over in his fingers. "Nobody's ever done this."

She tilted her head. *Why?* said the tilt.

"Because it's mad, is why." He laughed, and the laugh came out rougher than he intended. "Charm-work is supposed to be small. A warmth here, a luck there. You don't build a bridge between two languages out of an ox's knuckle. That's—" He stopped himself before saying *that's magic*, which was ridiculous, because of course it was magic, and they both knew it, and pretending otherwise had been his whole life for seven years and was getting heavier every day, like carrying water in cupped hands up an endless stair.

Elodie reached out and tapped the bench twice. Then she pointed at him, then at herself, then pressed both palms flat over her heart.

*I know,* the gesture said. Or possibly *hurry up.* With Elodie it was often hard to tell urgency from tenderness.

"All right." He set the disc in her palm and closed her fingers over it. "Hold it against your throat when you speak."

* * *

It worked the way dawn works—not all at once, but in stages, grey to rose to gold, so gradual that neither of them could name the exact moment it happened.

First came the hum. The antler shard at Elodie's throat answered the ox-bone disc, two notes finding each other like tuning forks, and the air between them went taut and singing. Then Elodie opened her mouth and spoke—and the sounds that came out were still the First Tongue, still that layered, resonant speech that seemed to come from beneath her voice rather than through it—but this time, as the words crossed the space between them, they *changed*. They passed through the listening-grooves of the charm and arrived in August's ears as Vellish. Rough Vellish. Market-Vellish, the plain speech of fishwives and carters.

"—and then the goat bit me," Elodie was saying, "which I feel was uncalled for, since I was only trying to help it out of the floodwater, but goats have no gratitude, none at all—"

August dropped his carving knife.

Elodie stopped mid-sentence. Her eyes went wide, dark pools in the lamplight, and her hand flew to her mouth. Then to the charm. Then back to him.

"You can hear me," she said. The words came out careful, each one placed like a foot on uncertain ice. She was hearing her own voice translated too, he realized. Hearing herself for the first time in—who knew how long? Months? Years?

"I can hear you," August said, and his voice cracked on the second word like a boy's.

Elodie began to cry.

Not quietly. Not the dignified, single-tear crying of storybook heroines. She cried the way children cry when a fever breaks—great, gasping, ugly sobs of relief, her whole small body shaking with it, tears cutting clean tracks down the dust still on her face from the road. And she kept talking through it, words tumbling out in a torrent, years of them, everything that had backed up behind the dam of her silence:

"—I tried and tried to make people understand, in Thistlebrook first, and then on the road, and the priest in Marrowford hit me with his stick when I spoke because he thought I was possessed, and maybe I am, I don't know, but the *goat*, August, nobody listened about the goat either—"

"The goat," August managed, fishing for something solid to hold onto while his eyes stung traitorously, "bit you during a flood."

"During a *flood*." She wiped her nose on her sleeve with tremendous dignity. "And now you're laughing at me."

"I'm not laughing at you." He was absolutely laughing at her, but gently, the way you laugh at something precious. "I'm laughing because I spent six days carving listening-grooves into an ox knuckle and the first thing the bridge between the language of creation and the tongue of the deep earth carried across was a complaint about livestock."

That startled a wet, hiccupping laugh out of her. "You made this? From nothing? From a *knuckle*?"

"From a knuckle of a very contented ox. Craftsmanship matters." He retrieved his knife from the floor, mostly to give his hands something to do while his heart finished its business of breaking open. "Now. Before either of us loses our nerve. Tell me. All of it. Start wherever starting lives."

* * *

She started with the water.

"There was a spring under our village," she said, once she'd settled, once August had put bread in one of her hands and tea in the other because a story this size needed ballast. "A cold spring. We thought it was just a spring. But it was a door—or the edge of one. The Hollow doesn't always look like darkness, you know. Sometimes it looks like clear water over deep stone, and you drink from it your whole life without knowing you've been drinking from the underside of the world."

August's oldest carvings, hanging silent on their pegs by the window, had begun to murmur the moment Elodie started speaking. He heard them the way you hear wind in a chimney—a low unrest, the dead shifting in their sleep. *The thirteenth moon,* one whispered. *Ask her about the thirteenth moon.* He ignored it. For once, the living woman—girl—in front of him mattered more than any number of dead voices.

"A year ago," Elodie said, "the spring started whispering. At night. My grandmother said it was frogs. My grandmother lied beautifully; it's a skill. It wasn't frogs. It was the wardens."

"Wardens," August repeated.

"The keepers of the door. They live—lived—at the place where the Hollow touches the world. Old things. Older than the covenant, older than the Edict, older than your city, probably older than the idea of cities." She turned the antler shard over in her fingers, and its pale surface caught the lamplight like a sliver of frozen moon. "They were dying. Not killed—dying. Starving. The covenant that bound the Hollow shut runs on thirteen anchors, thirteen stones, thirteen... voices, I suppose you'd say, and twelve of them had already gone dark. The wardens were the last light keeping the door from opening, and they were burning out."

"And they chose you."

"They chose me." Her jaw tightened. "I didn't volunteer. I want that understood. There's a version of this story where I'm brave, and I'd like to state for the record that I mostly screamed."

"What did they do?"

"They gave me a word." She said it simply, but August felt the room change temperature around the sentence. "One word. The anchor-word. The final lock. They put it in my mouth the way you'd press a seed into soil and told me I had to carry it somewhere safe, somewhere the hunger couldn't reach, until the thirteenth turn of the moon. And then the village—" Her breath caught. She looked down at the bread in her hand as though surprised it was there.

"You don't have to tell me that part."

"I do, though." She lifted her chin, and August saw for the first time not a frightened child but someone doing hard labor with her whole soul. "If we're going to do whatever it is we're going to do, you should know what it costs. Everyone in stories skips the cost part, and I hate it. So." A breath. "The village sank quiet. Not flooded—sank *quiet*. The grey came up out of the spring like smoke made of water, and where it touched people they didn't die, they just... stopped. Stopped wanting things. Stopped moving much. My mother tucked me into a root cellar and told me to speak my word to no one and went out to buy us time, and the grey took her mid-stride, and she smiled while it happened, August. She *smiled*. That's the part I can't—I dream about the smiling."

August's hand found hers on the table. He didn't remember deciding to reach for it. The dead in his carvings had gone utterly silent, which never happened, which was somehow worse than their murmuring—as though even they knew to take off their hats.

"The wardens got me out," Elodie continued, quieter now. "What was left of them. They walked me to the edge of the Hollow and gave me the antler and pushed me up into the world, and the last thing the eldest one said to me was: *find the one who hears the dead, for the reforging needs two hands and a voice.*" She looked up at him. "I didn't know who that was. I asked everyone. Most people ran away. One man threw a boot."

"That tracks," August said. "Vell's a practical city."

"But then I found your stall. And I heard—" she pressed the antler shard briefly to her sternum, "—I heard the bones around you humming. All those little charms on your table, all those pretty useless trinkets, and every single one of them was *listening*. Like a hundred small ears pretending to be asleep. And I thought: oh. Oh, there you are."

* * *

August stood and paced. It was what he did instead of screaming, and he was aware, distantly, that Elodie was watching him pace with the expression of someone cataloguing a new animal's habits.

"Say the rest of it," he said finally, stopping at the window, not turning around. "The part the wardens told you about the reforging. Don't soften it. I'll know if you soften it. Softening has a sound."

Silence. Then, reluctantly:

"The covenant isn't a wall. It's a song. That's why anchors go dark—one by one, the voices holding the song fall silent, and the Hollow wakes a little more each time. To reforge it, you need a new voice for the thirteenth anchor. Someone willing to sing themselves into the stone." Her voice wavered and steadied. "Willingly. It has to be given. Taken, it won't hold. And giving means—" 

"Giving means losing whatever gift the singer carries into it." August turned around. His mouth was dry. "For you, the First Tongue. If you sing the anchor-word into the stone, you lose it forever. You'd be mute again. Ordinary again."

"No," Elodie said. "Not ordinary. Never ordinary." And then, gentler, watching his face with terrible perceptiveness: "But that's not the cost you're asking about, is it?"

August laughed—a short, unhappy sound. Seven years, and the child saw through him faster than his own mentor ever had.

"For me," he said, "hearing the dead isn't a gift I use. It's a gift I hide. But it's also—" He groped for the words, hating every one of them for being true. "I came to Vell with nothing. No family. No name worth saying. And every night, in a rented room above a tannery, I would lay out my carvings and listen to the dead talk. Farmers complaining about weather two centuries stale. A soldier telling jokes to no one. An old woman singing to a baby that grew up and died old itself, and her still singing anyway. They were—" His voice did something humiliating. He let it. "They were company. Bad company, rude company, endless company, but mine. Seven years of never being entirely alone in the world, even when I was alone in every other way. And you're telling me the price of saving everything is handing that over. Forever. Every voice. Silence, real silence, the kind I built my whole life to avoid."

"Yes," Elodie said. Because she was honest, and honest people are sometimes cruel without meaning to be.

August sat down heavily. On the pegs by the window, the carvings murmured back to life, and for the first time he listened to what they were saying instead of shutting it out.

*The thirteenth moon comes,* whispered the ox-knuckle's cousins, the old pieces, the true pieces. *The covenant starves. Choose, bone-carver. Choose.*

"Two hands and a voice," August murmured. "That's what the warden said. Two hands and a voice." He looked at his own hands—carver's hands, scarred, steady, good for exactly one thing in this world. "The voice is yours. The word lives in your mouth. But someone has to carve the anchor-song into the stone, and the stone will only accept the carving from hands that can hear the dead well enough to write it down." He met her eyes. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Elodie's breath caught. "Both of us pay."

"Both of us pay." He said it flatly, because if he gave the sentence any feeling at all it would collapse under its own weight. "You lose the First Tongue. I lose the dead. And the world gets to keep existing, which I'm told is a fair trade, though I notice the world isn't offering to chip in."

For a long moment, neither of them spoke. The lamp guttered. Outside, far off, the curfew bell rang the hour, and beneath it—so faint he might have imagined it—something vast and patient shifted beneath the floor of the world.

Then Elodie did something unexpected. She climbed off her chair, walked around the table, and hugged him. Fiercely, the way you hug someone at a departure, even though no one was going anywhere yet.

"We have until the thirteenth turn," she said into his shirt. "That's nine days. Nine days to decide, and practice, and be afraid properly, and eat too much bread, and complain about goats. That's not nothing, August Okonkwo. That's almost everything."

He put his hand on top of her head, carefully, as though she were one of his carvings—which, he supposed with a lurch, she rather was. Made by patient old hands. Carrying a word inside her.

"Nine days," he agreed. And then, because the dead had gone quiet again, all of them, listening: "We should start tomorrow. Tonight, we—"

The knock came at the street door below. Three slow blows. Deliberate. Unhurried. The knock of someone who had never in her life needed to hurry, because sooner or later everything she hunted came to her anyway.

August went cold from the scalp down.

Through the floorboards, muffled but unmistakable, came Seraphine Voss's voice, addressing his landlady in that iron-calm tone he knew better than his own heartbeat:

"No trouble tonight, mistress. I only need a word with your tenant. The bone-carver."
