## 12. What Loyalty Costs

The knock came at dusk, and August knew before he opened the door that it was her.

He knew because Elodie went still first. She had been sitting cross-legged on his workbench—*on* it, not beside it, a distinction she maintained with the stubbornness of a cat claiming territory—sorting bone scraps into piles only she understood. When the knock sounded, three slow raps with the flat of a knuckle rather than the fingertips, she turned her head toward the door like a deer catching wind of something on the far side of a hill.

Then August heard the second thing: nothing. No whisper from the bones in the back room, no murmur from the half-finished charms hanging by the window. Seven years of quiet company, and for the first time since he'd known them, the dead were holding their breath.

"Stay here," he said, which was absurd, because where would she go? He said it anyway. It was the sort of thing people said when they wanted to feel like the situation was still under their control.

Elodie looked at him with those grey eyes that seemed to hold weather in them, then deliberately picked up her antler shard and held it against her chest. *I'm not going anywhere,* the gesture said. *But I'm not hiding either.*

He appreciated that about her, he decided later. Most people would have hidden.

He opened the door.

Seraphine Voss stood on his step with rain beading on the shoulders of her grey coat, and she was alone. That was the detail that frightened him most. Seraphine did not go anywhere alone. Seraphine arrived with six riders and a warrant and the particular silence of a woman who had already decided how things would end. Alone, she was something else entirely—a memory wearing boots, standing on his doorstep with her hands empty and her jaw set like a drawn bowstring.

"August." His name in her mouth sounded the way it had seven years ago, when she'd pulled him out of a burning workshop and asked him one question: *Can you stop?*

"Sera." The old nickname slipped out before he could catch it. Her eyes flickered—something moved behind them and was gone, quick as a fish under ice. "You're wet."

"It's raining."

"It usually is, when you come to ruin my evening."

Something almost like a smile touched the corner of her mouth and thought better of it. "May I come in?"

Every sensible part of him said no. Every sensible part of him also said *run now, out the back, take the girl, take the antler, take your chances with the salt flats.* But sensible parts had never governed much of what August Okonkwo did, and besides—the dead were silent, and silence from the dead, he had learned this week, was its own kind of warning.

"You may," he said, stepping aside. "Wipe your feet. I just swept."

"You have never once swept in your life."

"I've grown as a person."

* * *

She sat in the chair by the cold hearth, and he made tea because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Elodie remained on the workbench, openly watching, and August watched Seraphine watch Elodie, and the whole room became a triangle with nowhere comfortable to stand.

"The child," Seraphine said at last. Not a question. A stone placed carefully on a table.

"A child," August agreed. "She's mute. Found her shivering outside the market two days back. Been feeding her soup."

"Soup."

"Barley. With a ham bone." He winced internally. *Bone.* Of all the words. "It's good soup," he added, with dignity.

"August." Seraphine set down her cup with a click that was very quiet and somehow very loud. "There is a poster on every post in Vell with a reward for a tongued child. There is a smuggler in my cells who saw a mud-streaked girl come off a barge two nights ago heading toward the carver's quarter. And there is a bone-carver in this room whom I taught to read charm-structure before he could properly read letters, and whose work I would know blindfolded, because I burned the last of it myself while he cried." She paused. "Do not insult me with barley soup."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, the curfew bell rang the hour, muffled by rain.

"I taught you better than to lie badly," she said, softer now. "Or perhaps worse. Perhaps I taught you to lie well enough that you believed it yourself. That's the danger of a gift like yours."

"And what gift is that?" August kept his voice level. "Carving trinkets? Selling lucky rabbits' feet to grandmothers?"

"You know what I mean."

"I know what you *think* I mean." He set his own cup down. "Sera. Why are you here alone? If you knew—if you suspected—you'd have brought the Wexley brothers and a writ. You always bring a writ. You love the writ. You have them printed special."

That landed. He saw it land, in the small tightening around her eyes, the way her hand drifted toward her belt and stopped. For a long moment she looked at the fire instead of at him, and August realized, with a strange lurch, that she was tired. Not the ordinary tiredness of a long ride, but the deep kind, the kind that lives in the bones—and he flinched at his own choice of word.

"When your parents died," she said quietly, "who found you?"

"You know who found me."

"Say it."

"You did. You carried me out of the smoke."

"Say the rest."

August's throat worked. "You carried me out of the smoke, and you didn't report me, even though the workshop was full of charms, even though the Edict said the penalty was fire, and you told me—" His voice caught on the old words, worn smooth by seven years of repeating them to himself in the dark. "You told me the Hollow takes everything we love, and the only mercy is to live in a world with no doors to it. You told me to carve pretty things and mean nothing by them. And I have. Seven years, Sera. Pretty things. Nothing by them."

"I know." She said it simply, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "And I have spent seven years telling myself I did right by you. So understand what it costs me to sit in your kitchen and ask you for the only debt I ever let you carry." She turned and looked at him fully, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Give me the child, August. Not to the pyre—not to the cells. To me. I'll take her north, past the border, somewhere the Edict has never reached. She can live. But she cannot stay here, because in four days the moon turns its thirteenth turn, and if what the old texts say is true, everything with a drop of the First Tongue in it will become a door. And I will burn whatever stands near that door. Even if it's you."

* * *

Later—he would think about this later, turning it over like a stone in his pocket, wondering when exactly he had decided—later, the deciding moment was not her threat. It was Elodie.

Because while Seraphine spoke, the girl had slid off the workbench and crossed the room, quiet as falling dust, and stood directly in front of the witch-hunter commander of Vell, close enough to touch, and simply *looked* at her. Not afraid. Not defiant. Looking at her the way you look at a locked door, searching for the shape of the keyhole.

And Seraphine—who had stared down smugglers, hedge-witches, and once, according to rumor, an entire coven armed with nothing but a writ and her own fury—Seraphine leaned back a fraction in her chair.

"She understands us," Seraphine said slowly.

"She listens," August said. "It's not the same thing."

"No." Seraphine hadn't taken her eyes off the girl. "It isn't." And there was something in her voice then that August had never heard in it before, or rather something he had heard only once, years ago, over the body of his mother: grief recognizing itself in a stranger. "What happened to your voice, little one?"

Elodie tilted her head. Then, deliberately, she raised the antler shard and pressed it briefly to her own throat, and shook her head—not *I can't speak*, but something more specific, something like *it isn't mine to use here.*

Seraphine went very still.

"What did she do?" August asked, because her face had changed.

"That shard." Seraphine's voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "Where did she get it?"

"I was hoping you'd tell me. You're the one with the old texts."

"The old texts say the wardens of the covenant carried antler-white tokens, cut from the Door itself, so that they could hear the Hollow's hunger coming." She finally tore her gaze away from Elodie and fixed it on August, and he watched the terrible arithmetic happen behind her eyes: the poster, the smuggler, the mute girl, the shard, the thirteenth moon, and the bone-carver who had been hers. "August. Tell me you didn't know."

"I didn't know," he said, and it was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and they both heard the gap between those things. "Not at first."

"*At first.*"

"Sera—"

"Don't." She stood. The chair scraped. Outside, the rain thickened against the shutters. "Don't *Sera* me. Don't stand in this kitchen and tell me you've been harboring a walking breach in the Edict for days while I rode the salt road looking for her, and expect the nickname to soften it." But her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and August saw the truth underneath the anger, plain as a bone under skin: she wasn't angry that he'd broken the law. She was angry because she loved him, and love, in Seraphine Voss's ledger, was the one currency she had never learned to spend safely.

"The texts," August said quickly, before his courage failed him. "Your old texts. What do they say about why the covenant exists?"

"To bind the Hollow. To keep it hungry and harmless below."

"And what do they say happens if it fails?"

"Nothing fails. That is the point of binding things." But she said it too fast, and they both knew it.

"They starve, Sera." He heard his own voice go low and urgent, the voice he used for the dead, though these words were for the living. "Thirteen anchors held the door. Twelve have gone dark, one for each moon since winter. Something beneath us has been getting hungrier for a year, and nobody noticed because the hunger leaks slow, like cold through a wall. And in four days, when the moon turns, either the last anchor holds—or everything your Edict ever tried to prevent comes up through the floor of the world at once."

Silence. Rain. The fire settling.

"You've been listening to them again," Seraphine whispered. It wasn't a question. It was the oldest wound between them, reopened. "The voices in the bones."

"There are no voices in trinkets, Sera. I made sure of that. I made *sure*." He laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "Seven years of making sure. Do you know what that's been like? Carving a hundred charms a month and hollowing every one of them out like gutting a fish, so that no whisper could ever live inside, so that I could pass your inspections with a clean conscience and dirty hands?" He stopped. Breathed. "They're not in the trinkets anymore. They don't need to be. They're everywhere now. In the walls. In the rain. Getting louder every night since she knocked on my door."

Elodie had not moved from her spot in front of Seraphine. Now she did something extraordinary: she reached out and took the hunter's hand. Small fingers, mud-scarred knuckles, closing around a gloved fist that could sign a death warrant.

Seraphine looked down at their joined hands as though she'd been struck.

"She's a child," August said gently. "Whatever else she is, whatever the covenant says she's meant to be—she got here walking, alone, out of a village that sank into the ground, and the first person she trusted enough to follow home was a man selling fake luck to grandmothers. That's the judgment she made, Sera. Out of everyone in Vell. Me."

"Because you hear them," Seraphine said. "The dead. She knew."

"Maybe. Or maybe I was just the only door with a light on."

For a long time, Seraphine said nothing. She stood in his kitchen with rainwater drying on her shoulders and a child holding her hand, and August watched thirty years of certainty fight seven years of doubt across her face, and he understood suddenly that he could not save her from this. Whatever she chose, she would have to choose it herself, the way he had chosen, the way Elodie had walked out of the dark on her own two feet.

"The debt," Seraphine said at last, and her voice was steady again, which meant she had decided something, though he couldn't yet see what. "You asked me once what would repay it. I told you: live quietly, and mean nothing by it. You've paid that debt a thousand times over, August. More than I ever asked." She drew a breath. "So here is my new price, and it is the last one. Four days. Give me four days to read the texts—all of them, the ones they keep locked under the Chapter House, the ones I was never permitted to question. If what you say is true, I need to know it from the source, not from a carver's kitchen and a mute girl's antler. And if it isn't true—" her eyes hardened, but her hand, he noticed, had not pulled free of Elodie's grip, "—then you will give her to me, and you will not make me take her."

"And if I won't wait?"

"Then I'll have to treat you as what the law says you are." She said it evenly. She even managed to sound like she meant it. But at the door, pausing with her hand on the latch, she looked back at both of them—at the carver and the child, the liar and the truth-teller, whichever was which—and said, quietly:

"For what it's worth, August. I hope you're lying. I have never wanted anything less than to find out you were right."

Then she was gone into the rain, and the door closed, and the bones in the back room began, very softly, to whisper again.

August stood listening to them for a long moment. Elodie tugged his sleeve and pointed at the window, where Seraphine's lantern was receding up the street—and then pointed east, toward the salt flats, toward the sinking village, toward the door below the world. She raised four fingers. Lowered them to three. Looked at him with those weather-grey eyes.

*Four days is too many,* her gesture said. *We leave tonight.*

August looked at the cold hearth, at the unfinished charms, at the small life he had spent seven years building out of silence and sawdust.

"Well," he said to no one in particular, and began packing his knives.
