## 13. Flight Across the Salt Flats

Elodie had never run so far in her life, and she had run from a great many things.

She ran because August ran. She ran because behind them, on the ridge above Vell's eastern gate, torches moved like a slow orange river finding its way downhill. She ran because the word August kept saying—*faster, faster, faster*—was a word that needed no translation in any tongue.

But mostly she ran because of what she carried against her chest: the shard of white antler, humming now like a struck bell that would not stop ringing.

*They are coming,* it said, in the way it said things—not words exactly, more like weather inside her ribs. *The one with the grey braid. The one who smells of ash and old grief.*

Elodie already knew. She had seen the woman in her dreams for three nights running: tall, straight-backed, with a crossbow slung across her shoulder the way other people wore shawls. In the dreams the woman stood at the edge of a broken door, and her face was full of such sorrow that Elodie had woken crying twice, which was strange, because you should not cry for the person hunting you.

August glanced back over his shoulder, his breath tearing out of him in white rags. He was older than her by a lot of years—he had lines around his eyes like cracks in dry clay—and he ran with a slight hitch in his left leg, as if the leg had once been broken and had never quite forgiven him.

"Salt flats," he gasped, pointing ahead to where the last fields gave out into a vast pale nothing. "Two days' crossing. If we can reach the far ridge before they—"

He stopped talking, because Elodie had stopped listening. Not rudely. It was just that the antler had gone suddenly cold against her skin, and cold meant *look*, and when the antler said *look*, you looked.

Behind them, down in the dark fold of land between the fields and the flats, something howled.

Not a wolf. Elodie knew wolves. Wolves sounded hungry, but their hunger was simple, an honest hunger, the kind you could respect. This sound was hungry the way a hole is hungry—the way the Hollow was hungry. It was a sound with too much emptiness in it.

August heard it too. She watched all the color leave his face, which was interesting, because she hadn't known faces could do that so quickly.

"That's a lanner-hound," he said. His voice had gone very quiet, the way voices go when they're trying not to be overheard by the world itself. "The hunters breed them. They're... they're not natural animals anymore. They've been fed on Hollow-touched things until—" He swallowed. "Until there isn't much animal left."

The antler pulsed against her chest. *It is coming. It is fast. You are slow.*

Thank you, Elodie thought at it sourly. Very helpful.

*You are welcome.*

That was another thing about the antler. It did not understand sarcasm. Or possibly it understood perfectly and simply refused to dignify it.

* * *

They reached the salt flats as the moon rose—fat and pale and only a few nights shy of full, hanging low over the white plain like an eye that had opened to watch them.

Elodie had never seen salt flats before. Her village had been green and close and crowded with growing things; the Hollow had taken it sideways, drowning it in grey tide rather than swallowing it whole. So the flats were new to her: miles and miles of cracked white earth stretching to the horizon, glittering faintly under the moon, so flat and so bright that walking across them felt like walking across the sky's floor.

It was beautiful. It was also completely exposed, with nowhere to hide, and somewhere behind them the lanner-hound was coming.

August walked fast, favoring his bad leg, checking the sky every few minutes the way other men checked their purses. He had bought—or perhaps stolen, Elodie wasn't sure, and had decided not to ask—a hooded lantern, two skins of water, and a bag of hard biscuits that tasted the way she imagined chalk would taste if chalk were trying its best.

"Here." He handed her a biscuit and sat down heavily on a salt ridge, rubbing his leg. "Eat. We walk through the night, rest at midday when the light makes us visible, then push for the ridge tomorrow night."

Elodie ate the biscuit. It was terrible. She ate another one anyway, because being hunted made her hungry in a way that frightened her a little.

August watched her eat, and something in his face softened. He did that sometimes—looked at her as though she were a puzzle he was afraid to solve. She wondered what he saw. A mud-streaked girl who couldn't say a single word he'd understand? Or the thing the posters said she was? *TONGUED CHILD. REWARD. REPORT TO THE ORDER OF THE VEIL.* She had seen one pasted to a wall in Vell, with a charcoal sketch of her own face beneath the words, and the sketch had made her look meaner than she felt.

"I wish I knew what you were thinking," August said quietly, to himself more than to her. "I've spent seven years learning to hear everything except the living."

Elodie looked at him. Then, deliberately, she set down her biscuit, picked up a stick, and drew in the salt-crusted earth between them.

She drew a hound. Long legs, narrow head, too many teeth. Then she drew a line from the hound to a stick-figure with a crossbow, and a second line to a smaller figure with wild hair, and then she drew a question mark as big as herself.

August studied it. "How long until the hound catches us?"

She nodded.

He looked out across the endless white. "At our pace? Tomorrow night. Maybe dawn after." He rubbed his jaw. "Maybe sooner. Lanner-hounds don't tire the way we do. They don't really tire at all."

Elodie considered this. Then she picked up the stick again and drew the hound again—but this time she drew it lying down, curled up, asleep. And beside it she drew herself, standing over it, with little marks coming out of her mouth.

August stared at the drawing for a long time.

"You want to *talk* to it," he said slowly.

She nodded.

"Elodie." He said her name carefully, the way he always did, tasting each syllable like a man testing whether a bridge would hold his weight. "Those things aren't animals. They're—" He stopped, choosing his words the way he chose his carvings, shaving away everything unnecessary. "They're wounds that learned to walk. The Order feeds them pieces of the Hollow until there's nothing left in them but hunger. There's nothing in there to talk *to*."

Elodie looked at him steadily. Then she touched the antler shard at her throat, and raised her eyebrows.

August's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"The First Tongue mended the beast on the road out of Vell," he admitted, quieter now. "For a moment. Before it shook itself free." He dragged a hand down his face. "And that was a *guard dog*. This is a lanner-hound. If you stand in front of one of those and speak to it and it doesn't work—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Elodie finished it for him, in the privacy of her own head, in the First Tongue, where all her truest thoughts lived: *then I will be eaten, and you will be caught, and the thirteenth anchor will go dark, and everything ends.*

So. No pressure, then.

* * *

The hound came at dusk the next day.

They heard it long before they saw it—that hollow howl rolling across the flats, bouncing off nothing, arriving from everywhere at once. The salt crystals around them seemed to shiver. Even the antler went still against Elodie's chest, the way small creatures go still when a hawk passes overhead.

August grabbed her arm. "Down. Behind the ridge. If it hasn't got our scent yet—"

But it had. Of course it had. Two days of sweat and fear and biscuit crumbs across open ground—it had probably known exactly where they were since yesterday, and had simply been enjoying itself.

It crested the salt ridge a hundred paces away, and Elodie's first thought was that August had been wrong to call it a wound that learned to walk. Wounds were red and raw and alive. This thing was grey. Grey as the tide that had taken her village, grey as old ice, grey as the space between stars. It had the shape of a hound stretched wrong—too long in the body, too high at the shoulder—and where its eyes should have been there were two places where eyes had clearly once been and had since been *unmade*, smooth grey skin grown over the sockets like water closing over a dropped stone.

It smelled them anyway. Its nostrils flared, black and wet.

"Elodie." August's hand found her shoulder and pulled her half behind him, which was absurd—he was a bone-carver with a bad leg and a carving knife, and she was the last anchor of an ancient covenant—but it was such a kind absurdity that she let him do it. "Get behind me. Whatever happens. Get *behind* me."

The hound took a step forward. The salt crunched beneath its paws with a sound like teeth.

And Elodie understood, suddenly and completely, that this was the moment. Not the door below, not the thirteenth turn, not whatever terrible ceremony waited at the end of all her visions. *This* was the first moment of the rest of everything. Either she stepped forward now, or she spent the rest of her short life stepping backward.

She stepped forward.

"Elodie—!"

She walked past August's outstretched hand. She walked toward the hound. Ten paces. Twenty. Close enough now to see that its grey hide was scarred with old brands—the Order's mark, burned into it again and again, a whole constellation of ownership. Close enough to feel what came off it in waves: not malice. Malice would have been almost a relief. What came off it was *hunger*, pure and bottomless and agonized, the hunger of a creature that had been emptied on purpose and then set loose to fill itself with anything it could catch.

It was starving. It had always been starving. It would starve forever, because the people who made it had scooped out the part of it that could ever be full.

Elodie stopped five paces away. The hound's head lowered. A sound came out of it—not a growl, something worse, a thin whining rasp, the sound of an empty belly the size of a cathedral.

And Elodie opened her mouth and spoke the First Tongue.

The words came up out of her like water from a well she hadn't known she owned. She didn't choose them; they chose themselves, arranging in her mouth the way the antler arranged visions. And she felt, for the first time, what August must have felt all these years hearing the dead through his bones—except inverted, reversed, *alive*: the language going out of her and *into* the world, and the world leaning in to listen.

*I see you,* she said. *Empty one. Scooped-out one. I know what they took from you.*

The hound froze. Its grey head tilted. Somewhere deep in its ruined skull, something turned over—she felt it the way you feel a fish move under ice.

*I cannot give you back what they carved out of you. I am sorry. I am so sorry. But listen—listen—hunger is not all you are. Hunger is only the shape of the hole. The hole is not you. Underneath the hole there is still a dog. There is still the thing that runs because running is joy, that sleeps in the sun, that belonged to someone once. Can you feel it? Underneath?*

The hound trembled. All over, head to tail, a fine continuous trembling, like a rope about to snap or a bell about to ring.

*Come underneath,* Elodie whispered. *Rest here. Just for a while. Just for tonight.*

And the strangest thing happened—which was not that the hound obeyed her, because it didn't, not exactly. It was that the hound *believed* her. She felt its belief settle over her like a blanket. Here, at last, was a voice that spoke to the part of it that remained, the buried part, the dog-shaped ghost inside the wound. It lay down. Slowly, joint by joint, folding its impossible length onto the salt, until its chin rested on the white earth and its breath came out long and ragged and—for the first time in what might have been years—even.

It did not sleep. Elodie didn't think it remembered how. But it rested, and its hunger pulled back from the edges of itself, and in the place where its eyes had been unmade, the grey skin twitched, once, as though something behind it had dreamed of light.

Behind her, August made a small sound. When Elodie turned, he was standing with both hands pressed over his mouth, staring at her as though she had parted the salt flats like a sea.

"It's resting," he whispered. "It's actually—you *spoke* to it, and it—" He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet. "Seven years I've believed the Hollow's language was only a leash. Something that binds. Something to be hunted out of the world." He laughed, a shaky, wondering laugh. "It *mends*. Doesn't it? It mends."

Elodie smiled at him. Then, because the moment seemed to call for it, she pointed at the enormous sleeping monster, then at August, then mimed petting it.

August went pale all over again. "*Absolutely* not."

* * *

They walked on through the night, and the hound followed.

It kept its distance—fifty paces, a hundred, drifting at the edge of vision like a grey cloud that had decided to be loyal—but it followed, and when the hunters' torches appeared far behind on the flats near midnight, the hound positioned itself between the torches and them and stayed there, a wall of emptied-out devotion.

August kept glancing back at it, then at Elodie, then at it again, wearing the expression of a man whose entire understanding of the universe had been rearranged without his permission.

"They'll recall it," he murmured. "Voss will whistle it home when she realizes. And then she'll know. She'll know what you can do, and she'll know it isn't what she thinks it is, and I honestly don't know whether that will make her stop or make her worse." He sighed. "Seraphine has never once in her life let a fact change her mind gently. Facts have to break down her door and set fire to the furniture."

Elodie filed this away. *Seraphine.* So that was the grey-braided woman's name. The name fit her somehow—sharp sounds, like a blade being drawn.

Dawn found them at the far edge of the flats, where the white earth began to darken and rise into low hills, and beyond the hills, according to August's map, lay the drowned country—her country—the village that had sunk quiet.

Elodie stopped at the crest of the first hill and looked back.

The salt flats blazed pink and gold in the rising sun, impossibly beautiful, and far out on all that brightness she could see them: tiny dark figures on horseback, fanned out in a wide arc, moving with terrible patience. And at their center, a figure on foot beside no horse at all, tall and straight-backed, with a crossbow slung like a shawl.

Even at this distance, even without the antler's whispering, Elodie knew her.

*Seraphine Voss,* the antler hummed against her heart. *She is not riding to catch you, little anchor. Look at her path. She is riding to cut you off. She has guessed where you are going.*

Elodie looked at the hunters' arc, and then at the hills ahead, and did the geometry in her head the way her father had taught her, back when her father had a head to teach things to.

August was right. Seraphine wasn't chasing them.

She was waiting for them—at the one place in all the world they could not avoid.

The village that sank quiet.

Elodie's hand closed around the antler shard, and far beneath her feet, deeper than salt, deeper than stone, something ancient stirred in its sleep and began, very softly, to sing.
