## 14. The Village That Sank Quiet

They smelled it before they saw it.

August had been trying to explain, through gesture and the half-working translation charm at his throat, that the salt flats would end soon — that the ground would remember how to be soil again. Elodie had been nodding along with the particular patience of a child humoring an adult, which stung a little, because he was fairly sure he was the one who knew things here. Then the wind shifted, and both of them stopped walking at the same moment.

It wasn't rot. He'd expected rot. Rot he could have handled; rot was honest work, the kind of smell you could shovel. This was something else — wet stone and cold iron and underneath it all a sweetness like fruit left too long in a cellar, the sweetness of something that had decided to stop being food and start being something else.

"Grey tide," Elodie said, in her own tongue, and the charm at his throat translated it into a pressure behind his eyes rather than words: *the quiet water. The patient water.*

"Cheerful," August muttered.

She looked up at him, mud-streaked and road-worn and utterly unimpressed by his tone, and for a moment — not for the first time on this terrible journey — he thought of Seraphine. The same flat look. The same refusal to be comforted by small jokes. He pushed the thought away the way he pushed everything about Seraphine away these days, which is to say badly, and often.

The flats ended the way a sentence ends when the speaker loses heart: gradually, then all at once. Salt gave way to grey silt, silt to a soft ashen mud that took their boots to the ankle with every step, reluctant to let go. And rising out of it, tilted at angles houses are not built to hold, were the roofs.

* * *

There had been perhaps forty homes here once, August judged. A village of fishers and farmers, or whatever passed for them at the edge of the world, where the land got thin and strange and people learned not to ask too many questions about what lived below the cellar floors. Now the houses stood sunk to their eaves in the grey tide, some of them only chimneys poking up like drowned men's fingers. Doors hung open at impossible angles. A well rope still dangled from its crossbeam, swaying gently in a wind that didn't reach the surface.

Elodie did not run to it. August had braced himself for that — for the child to break loose and wade screaming into the ruins of her old life — but she only stood at the edge of it, very still, her hand closed around the white antler shard until the knuckles went pale.

"It's lower," she said finally, and the charm translated: *the water has gone down. It ate enough.*

"Ate enough," August repeated. "Of what?"

She didn't answer. She started walking instead, picking her way along a lane that was now a channel of grey sludge, and August followed, because following children into haunted villages was apparently what his life had become. Seven years of careful, invisible, unremarkable bone-carving, and here he was ankle-deep in apocalypse, chasing a mute girl toward the realm beneath the world. If Seraphine could see him now. When Seraphine saw him now, he corrected himself, because she would, because she always did, because that woman had never once in her life failed to find the person she was hunting, and he had been fooling himself for three days if he thought three days of head start meant anything.

The first house they entered properly was Elodie's.

He knew it without being told. She stopped at the doorway the way you stop at a door you've opened ten thousand times, out of habit more than intention, and then she just stood there while August peered past her shoulder into the gloom.

Inside, the grey tide had risen and fallen and left its signature: a high-water mark of pale mineral crust across the walls, four feet up. Furniture sat where it had always sat, furred with silt. A table. Two chairs. A shelf of clay cups, each one filled to the brim with fine grey sediment, like little pots of ash. On the wall, someone had nailed a string of dried flowers that had long since given up being anything but shapes.

And everywhere — August felt it before he understood it — everywhere, the dead.

Not the muffled murmurs he'd spent seven years strangling down to a whisper. Not the half-heard complaints and lullabies and gossip of the bones he carved, voices like conversations through a wall. Here, in this village, the dead spoke *clearly*. It hit him like stepping out of a noisy tavern into open air after years indoors. One moment, murmur-murmur-murmur, the constant sea-sound of the departed he'd learned to live inside of. The next:

*—cold, we were so cold at the end, but it was quick, it was quick, tell my sister it was quick—*

*—the wardens said they would hold it, the wardens promised, the wardens—*

*—I hid in the root cellar and I heard my mother stop singing and I have been listening for her ever since—*

August put a hand against the doorframe. His knees informed him, politely but firmly, that they intended to buckle.

"August?" The charm rendered Elodie's word as a shape of concern. She was watching him with those too-old eyes, and he realized he must look exactly the way he felt: like a man hearing for the first time after a lifetime deaf, except inverted, except drowning in reverse.

"I'm fine," he lied. "It's just — the dead here. They're loud."

*Loud,* the dead agreed, from every direction. *Loud, loud, listen, listen, no one has listened in so long—*

"They've been waiting," Elodie said softly. She stepped past him into the house, into the room where her mother's cups sat full of ash, and touched the table with two fingers, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "Since the village sank quiet. They've been waiting for someone who can hear them."

"And you can't," August said. "Hear them, I mean. You speak their language but you can't hear the dead?"

She shook her head. Then, after a moment, she added something longer, and the charm worked hard to bring it through: *The First Tongue is for speaking to the Hollow. Your gift is for speaking to the ones who left. Different doors.*

"Different doors," August repeated. His voice came out strange. "Right. Because one set of doors wasn't enough for this world."

* * *

They made camp that night in the upper floor of the village's meeting house, which stood on slightly higher ground and had only sunk to its windowsills. August got a fire going in a hearth that had, remarkably, survived, using driftwood-soft planks pried from a collapsed shed. Elodie ate the last of the smoked fish they'd bought in the whisper-market and drew in the silt with a stick: the thirteen anchors, twelve dark, one — the thirteenth, the last, the one shaped like a shard of antler — glowing faintly under her fingertip.

August watched her draw it and tried very hard not to think about the fact that her drawings kept including him lately. A stick figure with his crooked shoulders, standing among the stones. He'd asked her about it twice. Twice she'd looked at him with an expression of genuine bafflement, as if he'd asked her why the sky insisted on being up.

The dead talked all night.

That was the thing he hadn't been prepared for. In Vell, the whispers came only through bone — through the charms he carved, the trinkets he sold, the little animals and rings and buttons that carried echoes of whoever had owned the skeleton before. He'd built his whole life on the principle that the dead needed a medium, a lens, a carved piece of femur to press their faces against. But here, in the village that sank quiet, they didn't need anything. They were simply *there*, in the air, in the mud, in the standing water pooled in the foundations, and they talked the way a fever talks: endlessly, urgently, overlapping.

*—it came up slow, that's the worst part, it came up slow like water finding level—*

*—the wardens went down to argue with it. Down the old stair. They said they'd renew the terms—*

*—they never came back up. But we heard them. Oh, we heard them, right until the end—*

"Stop," August whispered, around midnight, to no one. "Please."

They didn't. Why would they? He was the first listener they'd had in years. He pulled his coat tighter and stared at the fire and thought about sleep the way you think about a country you emigrated from.

It was near dawn when the wardens' names surfaced.

He hadn't known they were names, at first. Just sounds in the flood, syllables bobbing past like debris. But the dead kept circling back to them, the way mourners circle a grave, and slowly August understood that he was hearing a roll call. Nine names. Spoken over and over, in grief, in anger, in the flat recitation of the facts.

*Warden Maren, who kept the eastern stone. Warden Osk, who kept the deep stair. Warden Ilsette, who taught the children the courtesy-words—*

"Elodie." He shook her awake. Her eyes opened instantly, fully, the way they always did — no groggy surfacing for this child, just immediate and total presence, which he found either admirable or deeply unsettling depending on the hour. "The wardens. Tell me about the wardens."

Her face did something complicated. She sat up, hugged her knees, and spoke, and the charm labored: *Nine wardens kept the covenant here. The oldest anchor-stones need living keepers. Someone must tend them, feed them, speak to them. My grandmother was Warden Ilsette.*

"Your grandmother." August sat with that. Of course. Of course the last anchor-child came from warden stock. Nothing about this had ever been coincidence; he'd known that since the night she knocked on his door speaking a language that made his carvings sing. "What happened to them? The nine?"

And the dead answered before she could.

They rose all at once, every voice in the village converging, and for the first time since he'd discovered his gift as a boy — crouched behind his father's workshop, holding a chicken bone, hearing a stranger's grandmother complain about damp weather — August was afraid of what he could hear. Not uneasy. Not weary. Afraid, down in the animal dark, the way you're afraid of deep water.

*Consumed,* said the dead of the village that sank quiet, in a chorus like a landslide. *Eaten. Taken into the hunger. The covenant weakened and the wardens went to strengthen it and the Hollow drank them down — their voices, their keeping, their tending — and the stones went dark one by one, and the grey tide rose, and we sank quiet.*

"Not died," August said. His mouth was dry. "You're saying they didn't die."

*Dying is leaving,* said a voice near his ear, an old man's voice, patient as winter. *Dying is going somewhere else. The wardens didn't leave, carver. They were taken in. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth.*

Beside him, Elodie had gone very still. She was looking at the silt drawing, at the twelve dark anchors, and August watched her do the arithmetic he was doing — nine wardens here, but twelve dark stones across the whole border of the Hollow, whole villages like this one, whole lines of keepers, generations of them, drunk down whenever the covenant faltered and the stones needed feeding.

"That's what reforging costs," he said quietly. Not a question. He'd known it since the threshold vision, since the dead offered him the choice in his own head with voices like cold smoke. He'd simply been carrying it lightly, the way you carry a heavy pack by pretending it isn't there. "A keeper. A voice. Something living, poured into the stones. That's the price of the thirteenth anchor."

Elodie nodded. Once. No drama, no tears. She pointed at herself, then at the glowing thirteenth stone in the drawing, then — with the maddening calm of the truly doomed — she shrugged.

"No," August said. "Absolutely not. No shrugging. Shrugging is not an acceptable response to your own sacrificial death, I want you to know that. When you're older and someone asks how you reacted upon learning you were chosen to be devoured by the realm beneath the world, 'she shrugged' is not the answer anyone wants in the record."

Something happened to her face then that he hadn't seen before: the ghost of a smile. Small, crooked, gone in a heartbeat, but real. She tapped the charm at his throat and signed something quick, and the translation came through warm and dry as bread: *You sound like Warden Ilsette. She also scolded the inevitable.*

"Well," August said, "your grandmother sounds like a woman of excellent sense," and then he had to stop talking, because his voice cracked in the middle of the sentence, and there are only so many indignities a man can absorb before dawn.

* * *

He walked the village alone while Elodie slept — really slept, finally, curled in her coat with the antler shard tucked under her chin like a talisman, which, to be fair, it was.

The grey tide had receded enough in the center of the village to expose the old stair. It was exactly what the dead described: a stair going down, cut into the bedrock beneath the meeting house, wide steps worn concave by centuries of warden feet. It descended into darkness that wasn't ordinary darkness. Ordinary darkness is just an absence. This had texture. This had appetite. Cold breathed up out of it, slow and rhythmic, like something enormous asleep at the bottom of the world, dreaming of the surface.

Twelve stones, the visions said. Twelve dark. And at the center of the Hollow, a great door held shut by the covenant, cracked now, leaking hunger into the world like cold smoke through a bad seal.

August stood at the top of the stair for a long time.

He thought about Seraphine, who had found him at seventeen, half-starved, selling a bone whistle that actually worked to a smuggler who would have used it to call up worse than wind. She'd had her blade at his throat and the Edict's law in her mouth, and then she'd looked at him — really looked, the way she looked at everyone, as if reading a document — and she'd seen a frightened boy, not a witch. She'd broken the arrow she was going to pin him with, right there in the street, in front of her whole company. *You will carve nothing true again,* she'd said. *You will be dull and harmless and alive. That is the mercy I am buying you, boy, and it is expensive, and someday I may come to collect.*

Seven years of dullness. Seven years of harmless. Seven years of carving love-knots and luck-pigs out of bones that wanted to sing funeral dirges, and he'd paid it gladly, because she'd saved his life, and debts like that don't expire.

But she was coming. He could feel it the way he felt the dead — not through any gift, just through seven years of knowing her. She was coming with her hunters and her certainty and her grief worn smooth as river stone, and when she arrived she would find him standing at the mouth of the underworld beside the tongued child she'd sworn to burn, and one of them was going to learn what their loyalty had actually been made of all along.

*Carver.* The old man's voice again, close, kind. *You are doing the thing where you stand very still and pretend you are thinking when really you are hiding.*

"You don't know me," August said to the empty air, and then laughed, because obviously the dead knew him better than anyone living. They'd heard him muttering over his workbench for seven years. They'd heard every argument he'd ever lost with himself.

*The wardens were consumed because the covenant starved,* the old voice said, gentle now. *Understand this, carver, before you climb down or run away: the Hollow does not eat out of malice. It eats out of hunger, and the hunger grows because the door leaks, and the door leaks because the stones are dark. Feed the stones, and the hunger sleeps. Let the stones stay dark, and nothing above or below will be safe — not your hunter, not her Edict, not the child, not you. Everything ends up in the tide eventually. The only question is whether it goes down quiet or goes down screaming.*

"Comforting," August said. "You'd have done well in the charm trade."

Behind him, up in the meeting house, Elodie cried out in her sleep — a short, sharp sound, the first noise of fear he'd ever heard from her — and August turned and forgot the dead and the debt and the stair, because the antler shard in her hand had begun to glow.

Not faintly. Not the soft pulse he'd grown used to on the road. It burned white as a struck bell, throwing her shadow huge and shaking up the ruined wall, and through the charm, through the morning air, through the grey tide itself, August heard — clearly, unmistakably, in a voice that was not Elodie's and not the dead's and not anything that had spoken to him before —

*Thirteen nights,* it said. *Then the moon turns. Come down, little anchor. Or send the carver. Either will do.*

The light went out. Elodie woke gasping, clutching the shard, looking at August with an expression he'd never seen on her face before and hoped never to see again.

Because it was hope. Terrible, dawning hope — the look of a child who has just realized the monster in her story might accept a substitute.
