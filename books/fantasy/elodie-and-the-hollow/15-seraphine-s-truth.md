## 15. Seraphine's Truth

The trail led north, as trails of the desperate always did.

Seraphine Voss had been following desperate people for thirty years, and she had learned to read their leavings the way scholars read scripture. A snapped twig told you how fast they moved. A dropped crust told you whether they ate standing or running. The boot prints in the frost outside Vell's northern gate—small ones beside large ones—told her that August Okonkwo had not dragged the child. He had let her set her own pace.

That was the detail that kept snagging in her mind like a burr in wool. A kidnapper does not slow for small feet. A man who has stolen a child for dark purposes does not stop at a stream so she can wash her face—there was mud on the bank stones, two sets of handprints, one large and careful, one small and impatient. He had waited for her. He had perhaps even helped her, cupping water in his palms the way you do for someone you are fond of.

*He is deceiving the child,* she told herself, and then, because she was honest even when honesty hurt: *Or he is deceiving me.*

She rode on.

Her lieutenant, Brammel, rode half a length behind with the particular silence he adopted whenever he disagreed with her but lacked the courage—or the sense—to say so. She could hear his disagreement the way other people heard weather coming: in the creak of his saddle, in the too-careful rhythm of his breathing.

"Say it," she said, without turning.

"Say what, Commander?"

"Whatever it is you've been not-saying since the salt gate."

A long pause. Somewhere behind them, one of the outriders coughed.

"You knew him," Brammel said finally. "The carver. You trained him yourself, didn't you? Before my time."

"I did."

"And now you're hunting him across half the province." Another pause, longer. "Doesn't that sit strange on you?"

Seraphine considered the question the way she considered all questions about herself: briefly, and from a great height, where it looked very small.

"What sits strange on me, Lieutenant, is a child who speaks a dead language walking out of a village that sank into grey mud overnight, carrying an anchor-shard from a covenant we were told was myth. What sits strange is twelve wards gone dark in thirteen months while the Edict's enforcers burned hedge-witches who couldn't light a candle with anything but flint." She nudged her mare around a fallen branch. "August Okonkwo sitting strange on me is the least of it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They made the ruined market by midafternoon of the second day.

She had known about the underground market for years—the so-called Market of Whispers, tucked into the bones of an old quarry three days north of Vell—and she had left it alone, mostly, because raiding it produced nothing but frightened grandmothers and confiscated candle-stubs, and because some part of her, a part she did not examine, understood that fear needed somewhere to go. But August had passed through it. The quarry keeper, when persuaded, admitted as much with the enthusiasm of a man eager to be anywhere else.

So she went down into it.

The market was emptying as they descended—practitioners scattering like roaches before lamplight, stalls folding, charms and talismans vanishing into sleeves and bodices. By the time Seraphine reached the central gallery, a vast limestone chamber ribbed with old quarry arches, only a handful remained: the too-old, the too-slow, the too-proud to run.

And one woman who had not tried to hide at all.

She sat at a stall near the gallery's far wall, an ancient creature wrapped in shawls the color of pond water, sorting through a tray of river stones as though the world were not ending around her. When Seraphine stopped before her stall, the woman looked up with eyes gone milk-pale, and smiled.

"Well," she said. "The Commander herself. I'd have baked something."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know why you think you're here." The woman set down her stones. "You're here for the child. And the carver. And you think finding them will close some wound in you that's been open thirty years." She tilted her head. "How am I doing?"

"Name."

"Maren. Though most call me Grandmother, which is flattering and inaccurate." She gestured at the emptying gallery. "Sit, if you like. Everyone else has somewhere better to be, apparently. Cowards, all of them. It's a good stall. I've had it forty years."

Seraphine did not sit. "You spoke to Okonkwo."

"I speak to everyone. It's my curse." Maren's smile faded into something older and more careful. "He asked about the covenant. About the moons. I told him what I knew, which is more than your Edict ever taught you, Commander, and less than the truth, which nobody living has entire."

"The covenant is superstition."

"Mm." Maren picked up another stone, turned it over. "Is it. Then why are you chasing a mute girl across the salt flats, Commander? If it's all superstition, go home. Put your feet up. Burn someone harmless—it's nearly the season for it."

Brammel's hand moved toward his sword. Seraphine stilled him with two fingers, never taking her eyes off the old woman.

"Careful," she said quietly.

"Oh, I'm always careful. That's how I got to be old." Maren leaned forward, and her voice dropped, and something in it changed—not softer, exactly, but *truer*, the way a bell changes when struck properly. "I'm going to tell you something, witch-hunter, and you can arrest me after, I don't mind, I've been arrested by better than you. Your family. The farm outside Hollowmere. The fire, thirty-one years ago."

The chamber seemed to tilt. Seraphine kept her feet planted through sheer discipline. "What do you know about that?"

"I know everything about that. I was there." Maren folded her hands on the stall. "Not at the fire. At the aftermath. I was young then, younger than you are now, and I helped bury what was left to bury. Four graves. Father, mother, brother. You were away at your aunt's in the city—that's why you lived, though I expect you've spent thirty years wishing otherwise."

Every word landed like a stone dropped down a well. Seraphine heard them fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"The Edict's records say the Hollow took them," she said. Her voice came out flat, distant, someone else's voice. "Grey tide. The first incursion. It's why I—" She stopped. Started again. "It's in the record."

"The record was written by men who needed it written." Maren's pale eyes held hers without mercy and without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Commander. The first purge wasn't a response to the Hollow. It came *before*. There were zealots in those years—good Edict men, fire-and-brimstone men—who decided the realm below was an abomination and the folk who honored the old covenant were its servants. They went farm to farm. Hollowmere was a covenant village. Do you understand? Your family kept the old ways. Left offerings at the boundary stones. Sang the songs your grandmother taught them. And for that, one cold night in autumn, nine men with torches and Edict badges came down the lane, and they burned a farmhouse with four people inside it, and they wrote *the Hollow did this* in their report, because a lie written down becomes a truth, given enough years."

The silence that followed was very long.

Somewhere in the gallery, water dripped from the quarry ceiling, patient as a clock.

"You're lying," Seraphine said.

"I might be. Check." Maren reached beneath her stall and drew out a wooden box, old and warped, and opened it. Inside lay papers—yellowed, brittle, real. "The zealots kept a ledger. Prideful men always do; they wanted credit with their superiors. Nine names. Nine marks for nine farms. Hollowmere is the fourth line down. Read the hand. Compare it to the Edict report filed the same month. Same clerk wrote both, Commander. Same ink. One says the Hollow killed your family. The other says *cleansed, per directive.*"

Seraphine looked at the papers.

She did not touch them. Some part of her understood, with the cold clarity of a woman watching her own house burn, that once she touched them, everything would change and nothing could be unchanged.

Thirty years. Thirty years of the fire behind her eyes. Thirty years of hating the grey dark below the world, of sharpening herself into a blade against it, of becoming the thing the Edict needed her to be because grief needed somewhere to go and hatred was such a convenient place. Every crossbow bolt loosed, every pyre lit, every frightened hedge-witch dragged from her home—all of it built on a foundation she had never once thought to check, because who checks the foundation of the only thing holding you up?

"They sang songs," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word. She pushed through it. "My mother sang to us. My father carved little figures for the boundary stones. I thought—I was told—they were victims. Innocents."

"They were both," Maren said gently. "Innocent *and* faithful. That's rather the point. The Hollow didn't take your family, child. Men did. Men wearing the same badge you're wearing right now."

Seraphine's hand had drifted, without her noticing, to the badge at her shoulder. Silver, stamped with the closed eye of the Edict. She had polished it every morning for thirty years.

She took the ledger.

She told herself she took it as evidence, to be verified, to be disproven. She told herself a great many things in the next few minutes, and if any of them were true, she was in no state to know which.

* * *

They camped that night on the flats above the quarry, and Seraphine did not sleep.

She sat by the fire with the ledger open on her knees, reading the fourth line until the letters swam. *Hollowmere. Cleansed per directive. Nine souls assigned, eight returned, one lost to the burning.* Eight returned. Eight men had walked away from her family's farm, warmed their hands, eaten supper, slept soundly, and grown old.

One of them, according to the muster rolls she'd memorized long ago in her own training, had later risen to a regional command. Had signed commendations. Had, once, shaken a young recruit's hand and told her that her family's sacrifice would not be forgotten.

*Sacrifice.*

Brammel appeared at the edge of the firelight with two cups of something hot and terrible, and handed her one, and sat down uninvited, which he never did, which meant he was worried.

"Bad news?" he asked.

"My whole life, possibly."

"Ah." He drank. "Those are the heavy ones."

She almost laughed. Almost. It came out as something broken and airless instead, and Brammel had the grace to pretend he hadn't noticed.

"Commander," he said, after a while. "May I say something insubordinate?"

"It's practically required."

"When I joined the hunt, they told us about you. The orphan of Hollowmere. The one the Hollow left alive. They said you hunted harder than anyone because you remembered." He stared into the fire. "And I remember thinking—even then, green as I was—that it was strange. That they used your grief like a whetstone. Kept it sharp. Never let it heal over." He glanced at her. "Grief that heals makes soft soldiers. Grief that stays raw makes weapons. They needed a weapon, and there you were, all ready-made."

Seraphine looked at him. Thirty years of certainty lay in her lap in the form of brittle paper, and a lieutenant she'd underestimated was handing her the truth she'd been too wounded to see: they had not merely lied to her. They had *farmed* her. Planted her sorrow, watered it with ceremony, harvested it year after year in the form of a perfect, tireless hunter.

"I rode past Hollowmere once," she said. "Twenty years ago. On assignment. I didn't stop." Her thumb traced the ledger's fourth line. "There's nothing there. Just the foundation stones and the boundary markers my father carved. I told myself the Hollow had taken even the memory of the place." She swallowed. "It was men. It was always men. And I've spent my life burning grandmothers for it."

"Some of those grandmothers were probably guilty of something," Brammel offered. "Everyone is, a bit."

"Brammel."

"Yes, Commander?"

"Shut up."

"Yes, Commander."

But he was smiling faintly at the fire, and despite everything, so—briefly, painfully—was she.

* * *

Dawn found her saddled and ready before the others woke.

She had spent the dark hours doing what she always did with unbearable things: breaking them into pieces small enough to carry. Here was the piece that said the ledger was forged, planted by practitioners to turn the hunt aside. Here was the piece that said Maren spoke true, and the Edict had murdered her family and crowned the crime with her devotion. Here was the piece that said it didn't matter either way, because the child still spoke a language that should be dead, and the wards still failed, and something hungry still pressed against the door of the world regardless of whose hands had kindled her hatred.

Here was the piece she kept turning over longest: August Okonkwo, seventeen years old, kneeling in the ash of a purge she'd led, looking up at her with eyes that knew what she was. She had spared him. Everyone said it was mercy. She had told herself it was mercy. But she had begun to suspect, in the long grey hours before dawn, that it was something else entirely—that some part of her, even then, had recognized a fellow survivor of the Edict's appetite, and had saved him the way you save the one witness who understands what was done to you.

And he had hidden from her for seven years anyway. Because she was the thing he hid from.

The outriders stirred. Brammel emerged from his bedroll, saw her mounted, saw her face, and wisely asked no questions.

"North," she said. "To the Hollow's edge. Full gallop where the ground allows."

Brammel hesitated. "Commander—if the ledger's true, then the child, the carver... they're not what we were sent for. Maybe we should—"

"We ride north," Seraphine said again, and her voice was steady, and her hands on the reins were not.

Because here was the truth beneath the truth, the one she could not break into carryable pieces: she did not know how to stop. Thirty years of riding toward the fire had shaped her spine, her hands, her sleep. If she laid the hatred down, what was left holding her up? A woman of fifty with ash where her purpose used to be? She was not yet brave enough to find out. Perhaps she would never be. But she could ride. Riding she knew.

And besides—beneath the fracture, beneath the shame, one thing in her remained whole and certain, and it was this: whatever happened at the door below, whatever the covenant demanded, no one else was going to burn for her family's story. Not the child. Not the carver. Not one more soul on either side of that door.

The hunt would ride north. But it would ride, for the first time in thirty years, toward something instead of against it.

Whether that made her a traitor or simply late, she supposed she would learn at the threshold.

She put her heels to the mare, and the salt flats opened before them, white and endless, and far to the north—a smudge on the horizon, like a bruise under skin—the grey tide of the Hollow marked where the world's skin had worn thin.

Three days' hard ride, maybe four.

The moon would be full in three.

"Ride!" she called, and the company broke into a thunder, and Seraphine Voss rode north with a dead ledger against her heart and a live doubt in her chest, unable to stop, unwilling to arrive, and arriving anyway.
