## 17. Hunters at the Threshold

Seraphine Voss had led a hundred raids in her nineteen years with the Order of the Edict, and she had learned long ago that the last hour of any hunt was always the quietest.

Not silent—never silent. Her company moved behind her through the salt flats' broken country in a rustle of oiled leather and muffled buckles, thirty-two crossbows wrapped against the damp, boots bound in felt so they made no more sound than falling snow. But beneath all that discipline there was a hush, the particular hush of people who had stopped believing their own orders were simple.

She heard it in the way Lieutenant Corren checked his gear twice instead of once. She heard it in the silence of young Pell, who hadn't cracked a joke since the market raid three nights ago, when he'd found a charm-carved crib mobile hanging over an empty cradle and gone grey as ash. She heard it most clearly in herself, in the place where her certainty had lived for forty years like a hearth-fire that had never once gone out—and where now, since the sunken village, since the wardens' bones and the ledger written in a dead woman's hand, something hissed and spat and would not quite catch.

*Her family was killed not by the Hollow but by the first purge's zealots.*

She had read the words four times by lantern light and burned the page on the fifth, and still they sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Ahead, the land fell away into a bowl of black glass and grey stone, and at its center, lit by a moon one night shy of full, stood the door.

Seraphine had seen drawings of it in the oldest Edict archives, woodcuts so faded they looked like fever dreams. None of them had prepared her for the scale of it. The door was not built; it *had been grown*, or perhaps it had grown itself—a seam in the world's skin sixty feet tall, framed in pale stone that might have been antler on a god, its surface veined with hairline cracks that wept a slow, cold smoke. The smoke did not rise. It poured outward along the ground like fog with intent, and where it touched the salt, the salt went grey and soft as old flesh.

Twelve stones stood in a crescent before it, each taller than a man. Eleven of them were dark as grave-dirt.

The twelfth still held a faint light, the color of moonlight through milk, and beside it—small against all that vastness—stood two figures.

"There," Corren breathed at her shoulder. "The carver. And the child."

Seraphine raised a closed fist, and thirty-two hunters sank down into the rocks around the bowl's rim like water finding its level.

She looked at August Okonkwo through her spyglass and felt the old debt sit up inside her like a dog hearing its name. Seven years ago she had found him in a burning workshop, seventeen years old, bone dust on his hands and terror on his face, and she had looked at him—at the boy who'd once brought her wildflowers because he'd heard, through some dead thing he'd touched, that her mother's favorite flower had been crocuses—and she had let him live. On condition. One condition.

*Never again.*

And here he was at the door of the Hollow itself, with the tongued child beside him, on the thirteenth turn, when every wise soul in Vell knew the veil wore thin.

But that was the trouble, wasn't it. That was the coal sitting in her chest. Because the wise souls of Vell had also known, forty years ago, that a widow named Halloran in the fen districts practiced the old craft, and they had come for her in the night, and Seraphine's own mother—the widow Halloran's dearest friend—had died in that same fire, and the zealots had blamed the Hollow, and Seraphine had built her whole life on their blame like a house on a lie.

She lowered the spyglass. Her hands were steady. They were always steady. It was everything behind them that shook.

"Commander," Corren said quietly. "Orders?"

She watched the two small figures at the door. The girl was doing something with her hands—drawing shapes in the air, patient, deliberate, the way you'd speak to someone deaf. And Okonkwo was watching her mouth, and nodding, and Seraphine thought: *he understands her.* The bone-carver who gave up magic had learned to hear the First Tongue after all, or something near enough.

"Wait," she said. "On my word only."

Corren hesitated. "If they begin the rite—"

"They haven't begun anything. Look at them. They're talking." A pause. "We wait."

It was not what he wanted to hear. She could feel the company wanting it too, wanting the clean simplicity of bolts and fire, wanting an end to a march that had taken them further from Vell than any Edict force had gone in a generation. But Seraphine Voss had not survived nineteen years by giving her people what they wanted. She raised the spyglass again and waited, and hated herself a little for how much she was hoping to see nothing at all.

* * *

August had seen them first—or rather, the dead had.

He'd felt it as a change in the bones he carried, a sudden tension like strings drawn tight, and then Elodie had tugged his sleeve and pointed at the ridge without any need of language at all. Thirty-two dark shapes against the stars. He'd counted twice to be sure his heart wasn't lying to him.

Now he stood at the threshold of the Hollow with witch-hunters at his back and a cracking door before him, and he found—to his considerable surprise—that he was mostly just tired.

"She's come alone," he said to Elodie, low. "No horns, no torches. That's Seraphine. If she'd wanted us dead already, we'd be dead."

Elodie frowned up at him. She signed something quick—*the small angry one?*—and August huffed despite everything.

"The tall angry one. And yes. The very one."

They had perhaps an hour before the moon reached zenith. The translation charm hung at his throat, warm as a held hand, and through it Elodie's voice reached him—not sound exactly, but meaning arriving dressed in sound, a voice like wind through a reed bed. She had spent the climb telling him the shape of the rite, the anchor-word the dying wardens had pressed into her keeping, and every detail had made the cost clearer and heavier, until he'd stopped letting himself think about it at all and simply walked.

Now, watching the ridgeline, he let himself think about one thing only: Seraphine Voss, alone in the dark, holding a crossbow and a lifetime of grief, and whether either of those things could be put down.

"I'm going to talk to her," he said.

Elodie grabbed his arm. *Danger.*

"Probably." He managed a smile. "But if she fires, she fires at me first, and I've spent seven years being very hard to kill. Mostly by hiding. There's less hiding out here, admittedly, but I'm improvising."

Elodie did not smile back. Instead she took his face in both small hands, the way no one had done since he was a boy, and she spoke—one word, in the First Tongue, and the charm translated it into his chest rather than his ears:

*Stay.*

"Not yet," he said gently. "But soon. I promise you that."

Then he stepped away from the door, out onto the open salt, raised both empty hands high, and called out across the bowl in a voice cracked from cold and fear and seven years of disuse:

"Seraphine Voss! Your student requests a word!"

* * *

The voice carried impossibly well across the black glass, and Seraphine flinched as though struck.

*Your student.* He had no right. He had every right. Both things were true and she despised him for the second one.

"Commander?" Corren again, whisper-tight. "Shall we take him?"

"No." She stood, deliberately visible against the starlight, and walked down into the bowl alone, crossbow slung but hand near it, felt-wrapped boots silent on stone that hummed faintly underfoot like a plucked string that wouldn't finish its note. "Hold the line. If I raise my right hand, loose on the carver. Left hand, the child. Nothing else moves anyone."

"And if the door—"

"If the door opens, Corren, I suspect crossbows will be decorative. Hold the line."

She crossed the grey ground alone, the cold smoke curling around her ankles like cats greeting a returning owner, and stopped ten paces from August Okonkwo, who stood with his hands up and his breath steaming and his eyes—she noted this with a strange, unwelcome tenderness—red-rimmed with exhaustion.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look well," he answered. "Which is unfair, given the circumstances."

"What are you doing here, August?"

"What are *you* doing here?" He lowered his hands slowly, testing her. "Thirty-two hunters and a three-week march to the edge of the world. For a mute child. That's not an Edict operation, Seraphine. That's a pilgrimage."

"It's a duty."

"Is it?" His voice gentled in a way that was worse than anger. "I found papers in the sunken village. Warden records. Forty years old. Do you want to know what they say about the fire on Fenrow Street?"

The coal in her chest flared white. "Don't."

"They say the Hollow never touched it. They say—"

"*Don't.*" Her hand had gone to the crossbow without her permission. She made herself take it away again, finger by finger. "I didn't walk three weeks across salt to relitigate the founding. I came for the child. Hand her over, August. That's all. You know what happens at the thirteenth turn if the covenant breaks—you've heard the dead your whole life, you know better than any of us what's pressing against that door. The Edict ends magic. All of it. Every voice, every whisper, every crack between worlds. Sealed. Forever. No more Hollow. No more hunger. No more—" her voice caught, traitorously, "—no more fires."

"No more anything," August said quietly. "That's what the seal means, Seraphine. Not just the Hollow. The covenant isn't a wall—it's a *joint*. It's how the world bends without breaking. Kill it and you don't get quiet. You get shatter."

"Lies from the realm below."

"Lies from me. From your student. From the boy whose life you saved, who has lied to you exactly once in seven years, and it was every day of those seven years, because you asked him to." He spread his hands. "Ask me to lie to you one more time. Tell me the child is safe with you. Say it, and mean it, and I'll consider it."

Seraphine opened her mouth—

And the door screamed.

* * *

There was no other word for it. The crack down the door's center widened with a sound like a cathedral of ice calving, and the cold smoke surged, and every hunter on the ridge cried out at once as the ground beneath them shivered. The eleventh dark stone flickered. The twelfth—the living one—pulsed like a failing heart.

And Elodie stepped forward.

Later, Seraphine would try to describe what happened next to the inquiry board at Vell, and would fail, and would finally write in her report only: *You had to be there, and I hope to God none of you ever are.*

The child walked past August as though he weren't there. She walked to within twenty paces of the door, planted her bare feet on the grey ground, turned to face the hidden company on the ridge—and spoke.

The First Tongue did not sound like language. It sounded like the moment before language: the groan of a continent settling, the first word a mother says to a newborn, the note a bell holds after the striking is done. It came out of a body half August's size and filled the entire bowl of the world, and every hunter on that ridge heard it not with their ears but somewhere older and less defensible.

And the Hollow answered.

The smoke rose. All of it, every grey thread pouring from the cracks, lifted and gathered and shaped itself—and showed them things.

Seraphine saw her mother's kitchen. Saw the fire. Saw—saw *truly*, saw with the Hollow's terrible impartial sight—men in Edict grey moving through the smoke with torches, men carrying the seal of the Order on their shoulders, and her mother's friend Halloran dragged into the street, and the cry of *witchcraft* going up, and the blame being laid, neat as a folded letter, at the door of the realm below. She saw the lie passed down like an inheritance. She saw herself, a girl of nine, kneeling in ashes, being told *the Hollow did this*, and she saw the forty years of hunting built on that sentence like a tower on sand.

Around her, she heard her company breaking. Pell was weeping openly. Old Marta, who never wept, was on her knees. Corren stood rigid, staring at visions only he could see, and whatever they were, they had emptied his face of everything except awe.

Because the Hollow was not showing them horror. That was the unbearable part. It was showing them *hunger*, yes—the vast, patient, starving pressure of a realm whose keepers were consumed, whose anchors were dark, whose door was held shut by a mute child and a bone-carver and eleven dead stones—but beneath the hunger, woven through it, was everything else. Seeds turning in dark soil. Roots finding water. The slow patient work of the world's foundations, the deep machinery that the covenant fed and the covenant fed upon, balance like breathing, give and take, give and take.

*Seal us,* the vision said, in the child's ringing voice, in the voice of the realm itself, *and you do not seal hunger. You seal the roots. And everything above them dies.*

The smoke settled. The vision released them. Thirty-two hunters knelt or swayed or stood transformed on the ridge, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing Seraphine had ever heard.

She became aware that August had come to stand beside her. Neither of them looked at the other.

"Well," he said at last, hoarse. "That saved me the speech."

Seraphine's hand was on her crossbow. It had been there the whole time. She looked down at it as though seeing it for the first time—an instrument she had carried for nineteen years, oiled and true, aimed in her heart at everything that had taken her family from her.

Except it hadn't. Except it never had. Except the thing that took her family wore her own order's colors, and the realm she'd sworn to destroy was holding up the floor of the world.

The moon crested the ridge behind them, one turn from full, and its light fell across the twelfth anchor-stone, and the stone's faint milk-white glow guttered like a candle in a draft.

Elodie turned from the door. She looked at Seraphine—directly, steadily, a child looking at a woman—and she spoke one more time, softly now, and though Seraphine understood nothing of the First Tongue, the meaning arrived in her anyway, the way meaning arrives in dreams:

*Choose.*

Seraphine Voss stood at the edge of belief, on the threshold of the world below, with her crossbow in her hand and her whole life burning down behind her eyes.

And the moon began to rise toward its thirteenth turn.
