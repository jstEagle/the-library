## 18. The Thirteenth Anchor

The moon reached the top of the sky like a coin balanced on a needle's point, and August Okonkwo knelt at the threshold of the Hollow with a carving knife in his hand and his own forearm bared to the cold.

"You don't have to do it this way," Elodie said.

She said it in the First Tongue, of course—she had no other language yet—but the translation charm at his throat turned her words warm and small against his skin, like a bird settling on a wire. *You don't have to do it this way.* He had heard that sentence from her four times now, and each time he had answered the same way, because there was only one answer.

"There's no other way," he said. "The wardens gave their hearing to the stones. That was the price. Somebody has to pay it, and—" he gestured with the knife at the twelve dark anchor-stones ringing the cracked door, at the grey smoke seeping through the crack like breath through clenched teeth, "—nobody else is volunteering."

"I could do it."

"No." It came out harder than he meant. He softened it. "Elodie. You're the voice. The covenant needs a speaker on this side, someone who can carry the anchor-word forward, teach it, keep it alive. If you pour yourself into the stones too, there's nothing left but a locked door and no key. The Hollow starves quietly for a hundred years and then breaks out angry. I've read enough bones to know how hungry things behave when they finally get loose."

Behind them, thirty yards back up the slope, Seraphine Voss stood with her crossbow half-raised and her face lit silver by the thirteenth moon.

August could feel her the way he felt all living things near bone—not her thoughts, nothing so tidy, just her weight in the world, her grief and her certainty pressing against the night like a thumb against a bruise. She had not fired. She had not lowered the bow either. She stood in the space between, which was, August thought, the most honest place she had ever occupied.

"Seraphine," he called, without turning around. "You might as well come closer. You'll want to see this. And if you're going to shoot me, I'd rather you didn't do it while my hands are shaking."

* * *

The dead had told him what to do, in the hours since they'd fled across the salt flats. They had been almost gentle about it, which was worse than if they'd been cruel. Cruelty he could argue with. Gentleness left him nowhere to stand.

*The covenant is a carving,* said the voice of an old woman who had died before Vell had walls, speaking through the shard of white antler Elodie carried. *A carving made of living bone. The wardens wrote it into themselves, letter by letter, and when they died the letters held. But letters fade, carver. Twelve have faded to nothing. Yours must be cut deep.*

"Deep," August had repeated, looking at his own hands—the hands that had spent seven years making trinkets, harmless things, pretty lies. "You want me to carve the First Tongue into my own skeleton."

*We want you to choose it,* the old woman said. *That is the whole of the magic, carver. It has always been the whole of it. A gift given freely binds; a gift taken only festers. Why do you think the Edict never worked? Your witch-hunters have been trying to take magic away from people for a generation, and every seizure only makes the hunger below louder. You cannot confiscate a covenant. You can only keep it or break it.*

Now, kneeling at the threshold, he understood why the wardens had built their door here, at the edge of Elodie's drowned village, where the grey tide had swallowed the houses to their rooftops. The door wasn't stone. Up close, in the moonlight, August could see the grain of it—the long pale sweep of it—and his carver's eye knew material the way a shepherd knows sheep.

"It's antler," he said aloud. "The whole door. One piece."

"The first warden," Elodie said, and the charm carried her voice to him soft as snowfall. "She grew it herself. Over three hundred years. They say she sat at the edge of the Hollow and let it grow through her, and when it was done she stepped inside and became the lock."

August looked at the crack running down the door's center, thin as a hair, weeping cold smoke. Three hundred years of patience, and it had still cracked. Everything cracked. That was the first lesson of bone-work: bone was strong because it yielded. Bone that would not yield shattered.

He set the antler shard on the ground between himself and the door. It hummed its low struck-bell note, and the twelve dark anchor-stones answered it—one by one, a sound like a chord being remembered.

"All right," he said, mostly to himself. "Let's see what you've got."

* * *

The first cut was the hardest, and not because of the pain.

Pain, it turned out, was negotiable. The moment the knife touched his forearm and he began—shaking, sweating, whispering an apology to his own body—to trace the first character of the First Tongue into his skin, the dead came close. Not the voices in his charms, which were distant and secondhand, echoes in echo. These were the wardens themselves, or what remained of them, gathered at the threshold like family at a sickbed. Their presence was cool and enormous and kind, and it slid under the pain the way a sheet slides under a falling glass, catching it.

*Steady,* said the first warden, whose antler formed the door. Her voice was the creak of a very old tree in wind. *The line curves where the bone wants it to curve. Do not fight your own grain, carver. No one has ever improved anything by fighting their own grain.*

"That," August gritted, dragging the knife through the second stroke, "is the best advice anyone has ever given me, and I resent how much it costs."

Beside him, Elodie began to speak.

She spoke the anchor-word—the word the dying wardens had pressed into her, the word she had carried alone out of a sinking village, through the salt flats, across half of Vell, to the door of a stranger who sold fake charms. August had heard it before, in fragments, and it had always sounded like weather. Now, with the first characters open in his arm and the moon overhead, he heard it properly for the first time.

It was not one word. It was a hundred words folded into each other, the way a seed folds a tree. It meant *stay* and it meant *enough* and it meant *I see you and you are fed*. It was, he realized with a laugh that hurt his ribs, the exact opposite of everything the Edict stood for. The Edict said *no*. The covenant said *yes, but gently*.

His blood rose along the carved lines, silver in the moonlight, and sank into the characters, and the characters woke.

The nearest anchor-stone flickered—not alight, not yet, but the way coals flicker when breathed on. The grey smoke at the door's crack paused, tasting the air.

*Good,* said the first warden. *Forearm next. Then the wrist-bones. We will go slowly. There is no hurry except the moon, and the moon is patient tonight—it knows what it is for.*

* * *

He lost track of time. Carvers did. You started a line and the line became a road and the road became the only world. He carved the covenant into his forearms, then—when the skin was full and the dead showed him how to pass beneath it—into the long bones of his arms, feeling the knife find a resistance that was not quite flesh, guided by presences on either side of him like patient tutors. Elodie's voice never stopped. When it flagged, she drank from the waterskin and kept going, and once she laughed mid-syllable, and the laughter went into the covenant too, and somewhere far below something vast turned over and made a sound that was almost a purr.

It was during the third hour, working on the fine bones of his left hand, that August noticed the silence.

Not around him. Inside him. The constant murmur he had lived with for seven years—the dead whispering through every charm he touched, every bone he passed, a crowd he had never been able to dismiss—was quieting. Not gone. Quieting, the way a market quiets at dusk, stall by stall.

"Oh," he said softly.

*Yes,* said the old woman who had died before the walls. *We are going first, carver. The oldest leave first. It is only polite.*

"I didn't say goodbye properly."

*You are saying goodbye properly right now. This is what proper goodbyes look like—they are work, and they hurt, and someone holds the light.* A pause, and then, with dry humor he hadn't expected from someone so ancient: *You held the light for us for seven years, pretending you couldn't hear us. Did you think we didn't know? We knew. We talked about it constantly. Very rude of you, incidentally, pretending. My granddaughter thought you were simply hard of hearing.*

August laughed, and the laugh shook the knife, and the first warden steadied his hand with a pressure like cool water.

"Tell your granddaughter I'm sorry."

*I will tell her nothing. In a few minutes I will be part of a door. But she is here, carver. Look behind you.*

He looked. The slope above the threshold was full of the dead—not ghosts, nothing so theatrical, just a shimmering, the way air shimmers over summer stone. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Everyone the Hollow had ever taken, everyone whose village had sunk quiet, everyone whose name had been carved into some trinket and forgotten. They had come to watch. They had come, he understood suddenly, the way people come to a wedding: because something was being mended, and mending deserved witnesses.

And behind the dead, walking down the slope alone, crossbow lowered to hang from its strap, came Seraphine Voss.

* * *

She stopped ten feet away. Up close, in the moonlight, August could see everything the last seven years had written on her—the lines around her eyes, the grey coming in at her temples like frost claiming a field, the way her jaw was set in exactly the shape it had been set the day she'd found him, seventeen years old, kneeling over a bone charm that whispered, with her sword already drawn.

She had spared him then. He had never asked himself, until this moment, why.

"You're carving it into yourself," she said. Her voice was flat, but flatness, August knew from years of listening to what people didn't say, was just another kind of trembling.

"Somebody has to hold the door," he said. "Turns out the lock takes a living key. Turns out the key is a person willing to give up the thing they love most." He flexed his carved hand; the silver lines glowed faintly. "For me that was never the hearing, Seraphine. The hearing was just company. What I loved most was being left alone. And look at me now. Audience of thousands."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It might have been a smile trying to remember its shape.

"I came to arrest you," she said. "Both of you. I had a speech prepared."

"I'd like to hear it."

"It was very good. I worked on it for two days." She looked past him at the cracked door, at the grey smoke, at the twelve stones with thirteen waiting to be born. "Then I found the records. In the archive under the Chapterhouse. The purge ledgers, from the first year." Her voice did not tremble after all; it simply stopped being flat, and became human. "My family didn't die in the Hollow's famine, August. They died in the purges. Zealots burned the east farms because a child there had spoken a word in the First Tongue, and they needed a reason, and the reason they wrote down was *the realm below*. They blamed the Hollow. Everyone blamed the Hollow. It was easier than blaming ourselves."

The dead around them had gone very still, listening.

"So your crusade—"

"Was aimed at the wrong murderer. Yes." She laughed, short and terrible. "Twenty-five years. I have hunted the wrong grief for twenty-five years, and it nearly made me kill the only person who could fix what's actually broken." She looked at Elodie—at the mud-streaked, silent, unbreakable girl who had walked out of a drowned village carrying a word in her mouth—and something in Seraphine's face came apart and reassembled itself into something older and kinder. "I'm sorry," she said to Elodie, in accented, careful, learned-by-heart First Tongue. *I am sorry. I see you. You are fed.*

The anchor-stones flared.

All twelve of them, at once, and the crack in the antler door began, slowly, to knit.

*There,* said the first warden, wonder in her ancient voice. *Do you see, carver? An apology in the First Tongue, freely given, from a hunter's mouth. The covenant was never only anchors and words. It was always this. It was always people choosing, at the worst possible moment, to be gentle.* She gathered herself, and August felt her attention turn to him like a hand on his shoulder. *The last characters, carver. The ones that go deepest. Are you ready?*

August looked at his hands. His beautiful, ruined, ordinary hands, seven years of trinkets in them, and now the whole architecture of the world.

He thought about the voices. The old woman and her rude granddaughter. The soldier who hummed. The child who had taught him the word for *rain*. All the company he had kept in the dark, all the whispers that had made a lonely life bearable, and which he was about to give away forever, freely, to a door.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm ready. Elodie—keep singing."

She sang. Seraphine Voss knelt in the salt-grey grass beside them, laid her crossbow on the ground where everyone could see it, and took August's uncarved hand in both of hers, the way you hold someone's hand when they're doing the brave thing and you can't do it for them.

The moon reached its zenith. The knife went in.

And deep beneath the world, at the bottom of everything, the Hollow opened its eyes—and for the first time in three hundred years, it was not hungry.
