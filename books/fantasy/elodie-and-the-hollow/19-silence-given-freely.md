## 19. Silence Given Freely

The moon stood at the top of the sky like a coin someone had forgotten to take back.

August knelt at the threshold of the Hollow with a knife in his hand and twelve dark anchor-stones arranged before him in a broken ring. The thirteenth stone—the one that would be his—waited at the center, pale as a tooth, humming faintly against his knee. The crack in the great door behind him breathed cold smoke into the world, and every breath of it made his bones ache in a way he had no word for.

*You could still stop,* said the dead, all of them at once, the way they always did when they were frightened for him. *There is no shame in stopping.*

"There is," August said quietly. "I've just decided not to feel it."

Beside him, Elodie sat cross-legged on the grey salt-crusted ground, the white antler shard held to her chest with both hands. She was watching him with those enormous eyes that had learned, over these long weeks, to stop apologizing for what they saw. She reached out and touched his wrist—not to stop him, never that—and tapped three fingers against it. In the language she'd taught him with salt and charcoal and endless patient repetition, three taps meant: *I am here. You are not alone in this.*

"Sentimental," August said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word, which rather undermined him.

She smiled anyway. It was her whole face doing it, the way children smile, and it was so absurdly bright there at the edge of the end of everything that August had to look away and pretend to inspect his knife.

The knife was his oldest one. Whalebone handle, worn smooth by seven years of his grip. He'd carved ten thousand trinkets with it—little birds, little fish, little houses with crooked chimneys—and sold them in the market of Vell while the dead murmured inside them like tenants who never paid rent. He had spent seven years pretending not to hear them. He supposed it was fitting that the last thing they'd ever say to him, he would hear perfectly.

"All right," he said. "Let's do this before I get sensible."

He set the blade against his own forearm.

It did not hurt the way he expected. It hurt the way remembering hurts—a deep, old ache, familiar and unwelcome. He carved the first line of the covenant into his skin, and where the blood welled, it came up silver-grey, the color of the Hollow's tide, and the nearest anchor-stone shivered like a dog hearing its name.

*What are you doing,* whispered Marta-the-drowned, whose voice lived in the little bone fish he'd carved his second year in Vell. *August. What are you doing.*

"What it says on the tin," August muttered. "Hold still, all of you. This requires concentration, and you lot chatter worse than a fishwife."

Elodie began to sing.

That was the only word for it, though the First Tongue didn't really have singing—it had something older, something that was less sound than shape, a way of bending the air until it remembered what it was for. Her voice was small and clear and utterly unafraid, and the words wrapped around the cut in his arm like bandages. The silver blood rose off his skin in threads and drifted toward the thirteenth stone, and the stone drank them, and began, very slowly, to glow.

One down. The covenant needed more than light, though. It needed voices. That was the terrible arithmetic Seraphine's crossbow had interrupted an hour ago, standing in the smoke at the door: a covenant is a promise, and a promise needs someone to keep it, and keeping it meant giving the stones something living to hold onto forever.

The dead had explained it to him while Elodie slept. They had been very gentle about it, the way people are gentle when they're delivering news they know will break your heart but can't bear to soften, because softening it would be a lie.

*Your gift,* they'd said. *The hearing. Pour it into the stones, and the covenant holds. Refuse, and the door cracks wider, and the hunger comes through, and everything above drowns quiet like her village.* A pause, and then, because they were kind even now: *We are sorry, carver. We would have liked more years of you.*

August had lain awake the rest of that night listening to them breathe—which dead things don't do, but they pretended, for his sake, sometimes—and thinking about silence.

Here was the thing nobody told you about being haunted: you got used to it. Seven years of whispers in every carving, murmurs in the marrow, the dead commenting on his posture and his cooking and his taste in hats (they had opinions; the dead were insufferable about hats). It had been a curse once. Somewhere along the way, without his noticing, it had become company. The loneliest man in Vell had never actually been alone, and he had built his whole careful, hidden life around that secret companionship, the way a man builds a house around a hearth.

And now he was going to give the hearth away.

"It's fine," he said aloud, to nobody, to everybody. "I've always talked too much anyway. This way I'll finally win an argument."

Elodie's song faltered—she'd understood him, or understood enough—and she gave him a look of such fierce, offended tenderness that he laughed, and the laugh came out wet, and he decided not to be embarrassed about it. There was no one left to perform for. That was rather the point.

He carved the second line.

The threads of silver thickened. The twelfth stone flickered—just once, like a candle deciding whether to bother—and then caught, and a pulse went around the ring, stone to stone to stone, and the great door behind them groaned like a ship in weather.

And the voices began to leave.

Marta went first. He felt her go the way you feel a hand slip from yours in a crowd—one moment there, the next just the memory of warmth. *Goodbye, carver,* she said, from very far away already, from inside the stone now instead of beside him. *The fish were my favorites. Carve me another one someday, won't you? One that swims.*

"I'll carve you a whole shoal," August said, and his voice broke clean in half this time, and he let it. "Swim well, Marta."

The silence where she had been was enormous. It had texture. It had weight. He hadn't known a single absence could weigh so much.

Then old Tomas, who had lived in the rib-bone beads and complained for six years about the draft. *About time,* Tomas grumbled, going. *Seven years of your humming. Seven years. Do you know what it's like being dead and forced to listen to a man hum?* But under the complaint, soft as ash: *Thank you, boy. For listening. Nobody else ever did.*

"You complained better than anyone I ever knew," August told him. "Rest badly, you miserable old ghost. It's what you'd want."

Tomas laughed as he faded, and the laugh was the last of him, and then that space was empty too, and August knelt in the growing quiet and wept without any dignity whatsoever, and kept carving, because the work did not care about his dignity and neither, tonight, did he.

They went one by one. The woman who had taught him the market prices. The soldier who'd died on the salt flats and liked to narrate the weather. The child—gods, the child, the small voice in the sparrow charm who had never told August her name and had asked him, every night for four years, to leave the window open a crack. *Leave it open,* she whispered, going. *Even when I'm gone. Even when you can't hear why.*

"I will," August promised. "Every night. I swear it on the knife."

And the child said nothing more, because she was gone, and the open window would be his penance and his prayer both, forever, and he would never again know exactly why it mattered, only that it did.

The quiet grew and grew. It pressed on him like deep water. He had not been truly silent since he was nine years old, since the fever, since the first whisper in the chicken bone he'd been gnawing. Twenty years of voices, and now the silence came for him like a tide coming in, patient, total, filling every channel the voices had carved.

Elodie sang harder. Tears ran down her face and she did not stop singing, because she knew—if the song stopped, the silver would stop flowing, and everything they'd given would be for nothing. So she sang through her tears, and her small voice was the bravest thing August had ever heard, and he thought: *when I can't hear anything anymore, I'll remember this. Whatever else the silence takes, it doesn't get this.*

The eleventh stone lit. The twelfth.

His arm was a map of silver lines now, the covenant written in him, throbbing in time with his heart. The thirteenth stone blazed like a captive star. Behind them, the crack in the great door had narrowed to a seam, and the cold smoke drew back from it like a scolded animal.

Last of all, the ones who had been with him longest. The first whisperers. The chicken-bone voices, the ones from the fever, who had been a frightened child's only friends in a house that had stopped understanding him.

*Little carver,* they said, and their voices overlapped the way they always had, warm and tangled, impossible to separate. *You were never strange. You were never wrong. You were a door, and doors are not to blame for what walks through them.* A pause, and August felt them gathering themselves, felt them choosing their last words with the care of people packing a single suitcase. *Be ordinary, August Okonkwo. Be gloriously, boringly ordinary. Sleep through the night. Hear nothing. It is the finest gift we know how to imagine for you.*

"Go on, then," August whispered. "Get out of my head. It's filthy in there anyway."

They went laughing. That was the worst of it, and the best of it—they went laughing, and the laughter rang in the stones and became part of the covenant, sealed into the thirteenth anchor with everything else, and then—

Silence.

True silence. Total. The silence of a man alone in his own skull for the first time in twenty years.

August knelt in it and shook.

It wasn't pain. That surprised him most. It was like taking off a coat he'd worn so long he'd forgotten it was a coat—the sudden lightness, the strange naked air on every side. He could hear the wind. Just the wind. Just wind, and somewhere below, water moving in the dark, and his own ragged breathing, and nothing else, nothing else, nothing else, and it was the most terrible and beautiful thing he had ever experienced, and he could not tell, even then, whether he was grieving or free.

Both. He was both. He suspected he always would be.

A hand touched his shoulder. Small, chapped, certain.

He looked up. Elodie had stopped singing. The thirteen stones burned in a steady ring, and the great door behind them stood closed—truly closed, the seam gone, the hunger sealed behind it—and the grey tide that had crept up the flats had begun, almost imperceptibly, to recede, leaving the ground dark and glistening like a beach after a storm.

Elodie was looking at him with her whole heart in her face. And she spoke.

She spoke the First Tongue, softly, the words rising like smoke: gratitude, he somehow knew, though the sounds passed through him without catching anywhere, meaning nothing to his ears, arriving nowhere, touching nothing. His gift was gone. The words fell on him like rain on stone.

But he watched her mouth. He watched her hands, which had learned to speak alongside her voice these long weeks. He watched her eyes, which had never needed translation at all.

*Thank you,* she said, in a language he would never hear again. *You gave your silence so mine could mean something. I will carry both. I will carry them always.*

And August Okonkwo—who could no longer hear the dead, who would never again hear the dead, who was at this moment the emptiest-eared man in two worlds—understood every word.

He laughed. It came out cracked and sodden and helpless, and he pulled her into his arms and held on, and she held on back, fierce as a burr, and together they knelt in the ringing quiet while the moon began, at long last, to come down.

"Well," he said finally, into her hair, his voice hoarse and strange and entirely his own. "I suppose I'll have to learn to talk to the living now. Gods help us all."

Elodie snorted—a real, human, untranslatable snort—and punched him gently in the ribs, and pointed down the flats, where the tide had withdrawn far enough to show a path: a long dark road leading away from the door, back toward the world, back toward Vell, back toward whatever came next.

At the edge of the path, small and pale in the dying moonlight, lay a single bone. A little carved bird, dropped days ago on the flight across the salt. Ordinary whalebone. No whisper in it. No tenant, no murmur, no ghost.

Just a bird.

August picked it up, turned it over in his fingers, and found—to his vast and stupid relief—that it was still beautiful.

"Come on," he said, tucking it into his pocket, and took Elodie's hand. "Let's go home."

Behind them, beneath the earth, something ancient settled itself comfortably against its new-won silence—and, contented, began to sleep.
