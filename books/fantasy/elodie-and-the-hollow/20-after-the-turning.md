## 20. After the Turning

The grey tide went out the way a guest leaves who has finally understood they were never invited.

Elodie watched it from the roof of her grandmother's house—the half of it that still stood above the salt—her chin on her folded arms, her legs swinging behind her. Below, the village of Marrow's Rest breathed out its long held breath. Water that had swallowed doorways and gardens and the well where she'd once fallen in and been fished out screaming receded in slow silver sheets, leaving everything behind glistening and strange, like a world someone had washed and hung out to dry.

She could feel it going. Not hear it—she had never heard anything the way other people heard—but feel it, the way you feel a fever break. The Hollow beneath the world had turned over in its sleep, and the hunger that had leaked through the cracked door for thirteen moons had curled back down into its ancient bed like a dog called home.

*Sleep,* she said to it, in the First Tongue, without meaning to. The word rose out of her the way breath rises, unasked.

And far below, something enormous and patient sighed.

* * *

August found her on the roof an hour later, which was not difficult, because August always found her, and because she had left the ladder down on purpose. He stood at the bottom with his hands in his pockets and his face doing the thing it did when he was trying very hard to look casual and fooling no one, least of all a girl who had spent her whole life reading mouths instead of listening to them.

"You're going to fall," he said.

"I'm going to *not* fall," she said.

This was their new game, or one of them. The translation charm he had carved for her—a small white disc of bone on a cord, warm as a held hand against her throat—let her speak the ordinary tongue of Vell, the tongue everyone else used, the tongue she had never owned. It did not give her the words. She still had to learn them, one at a time, the way you learn stones across a river. But it carried what she knew up out of her mouth shaped correctly, so that when she thought *I am not going to fall* with all her stubbornness behind it, the charm translated her meaning into sound, and the sound came out in August's language, flat and clear and slightly amused.

He squinted up at her. "You said that wrong."

"I said it *rightly.*"

"Wrongly."

"*Rightly.*"

He gave up, which was his way of winning, and climbed halfway up the ladder to sit on the lowest rung, which was his way of staying. Elodie had learned this about him in the weeks since the turning: August Okonkwo expressed affection through proximity and complaint. He would grumble about the cold, and then build the fire higher. He would complain that she ate too much bread, and then buy more bread. He had once spent an entire afternoon telling her that teaching him the shapes of First Tongue words was a waste of time, while writing each one three times in his little notebook in his careful carver's hand.

Below them, the village went on drying out. A neighbor from two houses down—Marta, whose roof had survived and whose temper had also survived, unfortunately—was already sweeping grey silt off her doorstep as though the apocalypse were a kind of weather. Which, Elodie supposed, for Marrow's Rest, it had been.

"They're saying the water won't come back," August said. "The dead in my bones said so before they—" He stopped. Elodie watched him find his way around the sentence, the way he always did now, gently, like a man walking around a sleeping animal. "Before they quieted. They said the tide was hunger, and the hunger's fed now. Bound. Sleeping." He looked up at her. "They said you did that."

*We did that,* she signed, because some things the charm couldn't carry, and because her hands still spoke faster than her new mouth. *You and me. You gave your hearing. I gave my voice. We traded silences.*

"That's not how I'd put it."

"How would you put it?"

He considered. "I'd say," he said slowly, "that I spent seven years hiding in silence, and you spent your whole life trapped in it, and between us we built something loud enough to save the world. And now I'm deaf to the dead and you're learning to talk, and somehow we both ended up less alone than when we started. Which is either a miracle or a very good joke, and I've never been able to tell the difference."

Elodie laughed. She was still learning laughing too—not the feeling, which she'd always had, but the sound of it, which came out of her now rusty and surprised, like a door opening in a house long shut. August pretended not to notice every time, and every time his ears went slightly pink, and she pretended not to notice *that*, and this was also their way of loving each other, though neither of them had said the word yet.

*Come down,* she signed. *Marta is making soup. She will pretend she made too much by accident.*

"She does that every day."

*I know. It is her way of loving people. Like complaining is yours.*

August opened his mouth to object, discovered there was no objection available, and closed it again. Elodie grinned and swung down the ladder past him, quick as a squirrel, and did not fall, mostly.

* * *

The soup was fish, because everything in Marrow's Rest was fish now; the retreating tide had left the shallows fat with silver-bellied things that had never known a hook. Marta ladled it out and complained about the salt damage and the price of timber and the government in Vell, which had sent no one, helped no one, and according to Marta probably caused the whole business somehow. August agreed with everything she said in the tone of a man agreeing with weather. Elodie ate three bowls and let the charm rest against her throat, quiet, because some afternoons didn't need translating.

It was strange, learning to be a person with a voice. She had been the mute child for so long—the girl who pointed, who drew, who spoke only the language of the deep places—that having words come out of her felt like wearing someone else's coat. Some days she loved it. Some days she took the charm off and sat in her old silence like an old room, familiar and safe, and let her hands do the talking.

But the days she loved most were the ones like today, when she got to use her new voice on August.

"Marta says you should fix her gate," she said, as they walked back along the drying lane toward his cart.

"Marta says a lot of things."

"She says you are the best bone-carver in three villages and too proud to admit it."

"I am not proud. I am accurately modest."

"She says you fixed the Kessler boy's flute and refused payment and then complained about it for a week."

"That flute was badly made. Complaining about it was practically craftsmanship."

Elodie laughed again, easier this time. The lane was soft underfoot with wet salt-sand, and the evening light came low and gold across the flats, catching the roofs that had come back above the waterline—some broken, some whole, all of them steaming gently in the sun like the village itself was cooking. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere children shouted, real living children, playing in the shallows where a month ago nothing living would have gone.

Her chest did the tight thing it sometimes did. Because these had been her streets. This had been her village, before it sank quiet, before the wardens chose a seven-year-old girl and pressed the last anchor-word into her like a seal into wax, before she ran and ran and ran until she fell asleep in a curfew storm against a stranger's door in Vell.

There was almost nothing left to return to. Her grandmother's house stood, but empty. Her schoolmates were scattered or gone. The wardens who had chosen her were consumed, gone down into the dark they had guarded, and the First Tongue they had given her was now a language spoken by exactly one living person—and even she could only speak it now through August's charm, which translated it away into ordinary words the moment it left her lips.

Sometimes, at night, she said the anchor-word silently, just to feel it. It sat in her still, the way a scar sits. But it was a scar now, not an open wound. The covenant held without her carrying it alone. That was the whole point of what they'd done: no more one child holding shut a door beneath the world. Just thirteen stones, fed and full, and something old sleeping easy, and nobody sacrificed.

Well. Almost nobody sacrificed. August had given up the voices of the dead, and some evenings she caught him with his head tilted, listening to nothing, reaching for companions who were no longer there. On those evenings she sat next to him and leaned her shoulder against his arm and stayed until the reaching stopped. It was the only thing she could think to give him back.

"Marrow's Rest needs a carver," Marta had told him that afternoon, apropos of nothing, in the way of a woman laying a trap. "Half the doors need new hinges. There's a chapel roof. And the Kesslers want grave-markers for the ones the tide took, proper ones, cut nice."

"I live in Vell," August had said.

"Do you," said Marta, and had looked at Elodie, and had looked back at August, and had said nothing further, because Marta's silences were as eloquent as her complaints.

* * *

That night they camped outside the village, because August claimed the inn charged extortionate rates, and Elodie knew perfectly well he simply slept better under open sky since the voices went quiet—as if he were still getting used to a world with only one layer in it. They built a small fire. August carved. He always carved now, even with no whispers to strip out of the bone, even with no Edict to hide from. His knife moved the way it always moved, sure and slow, and the shavings curled off white as milk.

"What are you making?" Elodie asked.

"A moon."

She looked at the sky, where the true moon hung, round and pale and ordinary again, just a moon, thirteenth turn passed, nothing hungry in it. "Why?"

"Because a month ago the moon was going to kill us, and now it isn't, and I find I have feelings about that." He turned the little carving in his fingers. Bone-white, smooth, with a faint spiral worked into its face—the mark of the covenant, the shape she'd drawn in salt on his workbench a lifetime ago. "It's for you. Don't make a thing of it."

Too late. Elodie was already making a thing of it, hugging it to her chest with both hands, and August was already pretending to be deeply interested in the fire.

Then, because she was who she was, she asked the question she'd been carrying for three days, the one that had been sitting in her like a stone in a shoe.

"When we go back to Vell," she said carefully, "what happens?"

August's knife paused. "What do you mean?"

*I mean,* she signed, then caught herself and said it aloud, practicing, *I mean. Do I go to an orphanage? Do you go back to your stall? Do we—* she ran out of learned words and finished with her hands, *—do we stay together?*

The fire popped. August looked at it for a long moment.

"The orphanage in Vell," he said at last, "is run by a man named Brother Aldous who once reported a woman to the witch-hunters for selling love charms. I'm not sending you there. Over my dead body, and possibly over his."

*So?*

"So." He exhaled. Elodie watched him wrestle with the sentence, watched him lose, watched him say it anyway. "My stall has one chair. My rooms have one bed and a floor that creaks. I'm forty-one years old, I'm set in my ways, I complain constantly, and I don't know the first thing about raising a child." He looked over at her, firelight in his eyes, and his voice went rough. "But I've gotten used to you asking questions at breakfast. And I've gotten used to you beating me at cards, which shouldn't be possible, since you cheat. And when I imagine going back to that quiet room alone, I—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't much care for it."

Elodie's throat was doing something complicated. She made herself say it plainly, the way the wardens had taught her to say important things: plainly, completely, once.

"Then I'll stay," she said. "With you. If you'll have me."

"I'll have you," August said immediately, and then, mortified by his own speed, added, "someone has to keep the mice away, you're very good at that," which was a lie, because Elodie was terrified of mice, and they both knew it, and the lie was so transparent and so kind that she laughed until her ribs hurt, and eventually, helplessly, so did he.

Later, wrapped in blankets with the moon-white carving under her cheek, she listened to August's breathing even out into sleep, and she reached with her mind down, down, past the salt and the stone and the roots of the world, to the great door and the dark behind it.

*Goodnight,* she said in the First Tongue, silent, just for herself.

And the Hollow, dreaming its long dream, answered—not in words, but in warmth, a vast slow contentment rising through the ground like the memory of sun.

* * *

Three mornings later, a rider came up the coast road to Marrow's Rest: a lean woman on a grey horse, travel-worn, with a crossbow slung across her back and a face Elodie knew from visions and from nightmares and, lately, from something softer. Seraphine Voss reined in at the edge of the village square, where August was arguing with a hinge, and sat looking at him for a long moment.

"Voss," August said, not looking up. "You're a long way from Vell."

"I resigned," Seraphine said. Her voice was flat, but Elodie, watching her mouth, saw the tremor underneath it. "The Order didn't take it well." She dismounted slowly. "I've been riding for weeks. I kept telling myself I was checking on the coast settlements. Official business." She looked at the village, at the drying houses, at the children in the shallows, and something in her jaw worked. "It was never official business."

"No," August said quietly. "I don't expect it was."

Seraphine's eyes found Elodie then, standing in Marta's doorway with flour on her hands. For a moment the hunter and the anchor looked at each other across the square—the woman who had hunted her, the child who had saved the world she'd sworn to protect. Elodie felt the old fear flicker and fade, replaced by something more complicated. Pity, maybe. Recognition. Two people who had believed terrible things in order to survive terrible things.

"You look well," Seraphine said at last, awkwardly, to Elodie, as if reading it off a card.

*Thank you,* Elodie signed, and then, deliberately, aloud, letting the charm carry it clear across the square: "*You look tired. There is soup.*"

Seraphine stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she huffed—almost a laugh, worn rusty as an old lock. "Soup," she repeated, as though it were the strangest word in any language, First or otherwise. She tied her horse to the post. "I suppose I could stay for soup."

August straightened up from the hinge, and Elodie saw him weigh it—seven years of fear, one night of truth, a debt repaid in fire lowered instead of fired—and then step aside from the doorway, making room.

"There's plenty," he said. "Marta always makes too much."

And far below them all, in the dark that was no longer hungry, something ancient turned over, settled deeper, and slept.
