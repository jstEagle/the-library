## 21. The Charm-Seller's New Trade

The trouble with silence, August had learned, was that it took up more room than you'd think.

For seven years he had filled his shop with the dead's murmuring—a low tide of voices under every carving, so constant he'd stopped hearing it the way a man stops hearing a river outside his window. Now it was gone, and in its absence the shop had grown enormous. Every creak of the settling timber sounded like a proclamation. The scratch of his knife along a curl of birch wood was thunderous.

He didn't regret it. That was the strange part he kept turning over like a stone in his pocket, looking for the flaw. He had given away the only companionship he'd known for most of his adult life, and he could not find a single corner of himself that wanted it back.

Mostly.

There were moments—usually at dusk, when the light went amber through the shutters and the market noise below faded to a murmur of its own—when he caught himself tilting his head toward a finished piece on the shelf, listening for something that wasn't there. A habit of seven years. Like reaching for a stair that isn't where your foot remembers it.

"You're doing it again," Elodie said.

She sat cross-legged on his workbench—not beside it, *on* it, because chairs were apparently a form of tyranny she had rejected early and permanently—shelling sunflower seeds into a clay bowl and dropping the shells directly onto the floor, which she knew infuriated him, which was why she did it.

"Doing what?"

"Listening." She said it in Vellish now, her third language and the one she spoke with the careful, deliberate precision of someone who had learned to talk late and intended to do it properly. Her accent was still faintly musical, vowels held a half-beat too long, as though each word were being set down gently rather than spoken. "At the sparrow charm."

"I'm inspecting it."

"You're *listening* to it." She cracked a seed between her teeth. "It doesn't work that way anymore. You made sure."

"I know how I made sure."

"Then why are you looking at it like it owes you money?"

August put down his knife. "Where did you learn that expression?"

"Marta at the fruit stall. She says it about the tax man." Elodie swept the seed shells off the bench with her forearm, an act of pure provocation, and hopped down to gather them up before he could object—which was the thing about Elodie now, eleven months in the world above: she caused messes and then cleaned them up with such brisk competence that you couldn't even be angry properly. It was, he suspected, a strategy. "Marta also says you should charge more. She says beautiful things should cost ugly prices."

"Marta sells apples at three coppers the pound and calls it charity."

"She's right about this, though." Elodie picked up the sparrow charm from the shelf and turned it in her fingers. He'd carved it from a single piece of pale ash, wings folded, head cocked as if it had just heard something interesting. No whispers inside. No power. Just a bird, frozen mid-curiosity. "You used to sell these for two bits. Now people come from three districts over and pay silver."

"People are fools with coin."

"People are *hungry*," Elodie said, setting the sparrow down with exaggerated care. "You can see it when they hold them. Old woman last week, with the fish charm. She held it like—" she searched for the word, hands shaping the air— "like it was warm. Like it was a small animal that might wake up."

August said nothing, because she was right, and because he had watched that old woman too, and had felt something in his chest shift uncomfortably when she'd tucked the little wooden fish into her shawl like a secret worth keeping.

That was the new trade, then. He carved beauty now. Nothing else. And it turned out the city of Vell, starved for eleven years under the Edict—starved for color, for wonder, for anything made by human hands that carried no fear in it—was hungry as a winter wolf.

He hadn't planned it. For the first month after the Turning he hadn't carved at all. His hands had felt like borrowed tools. Then one gray morning Elodie had set a block of birch in front of him and said, in her blunt First-Tongue-turned-Vellish way: "Your hands are sad. Fix them."

So he had fixed them. And somewhere in the fixing, the shop had changed.

* * *

The bell over the door rang at midday, which was unusual; his customers came early or late, slipping in around work hours, and never in the bright honest middle of the day when anyone might see them.

Seraphine Voss stood in the doorway holding a loaf of bread.

She looked—different was too weak a word. Transformed was too strong. She looked like a woman who had taken off a heavy coat and not yet decided what to wear instead. Her hair was down, which August had seen perhaps four times in nine years, and her witch-hunter's coat was gone, replaced by a plain gray traveling cloak that hung on her like an apology.

"August." His name came out stiff, rehearsed. He suspected she had practiced the whole visit on the walk over and was already losing her place.

"Sera." He set down his knife. "You're blocking my light."

"I'm—" She stepped sideways, then seemed to realize she'd been dismissed from the doorway like a delivery boy, and something flickered across her face that was almost amusement. Almost. "I brought bread. From the baker on Corvin Street. The good one."

"The one who waters his dough?"

"The one whose wife is the baker. He just carries things."

Elodie, who had been pretending to sort charms at the back counter with the theatrical innocence of a child who intends to hear everything, said, "We like that one."

Seraphine's eyes found her, and August watched the moment happen—the tightening around her jaw, the old arithmetic running behind her eyes, *the tongued child, the anchor, the thing I hunted across salt flats*—and then watched her force it closed, deliberately, like shutting a ledger.

"Hello, Elodie," Seraphine said.

"Hello, Seraphine." Elodie's voice was polite and utterly without warmth, which was, August reflected, exactly what Seraphine deserved and exactly what Elodie would give. The girl held no grudges loudly. She held them quietly, permanently, like stones at the bottom of a river. "Your bread is probably stale. You walked past three fountains. Bread dries out near water."

"That's not—" Seraphine stopped. Looked at August. "Is she serious?"

"No idea," August said. "I've stopped trying to determine which of her rules are real. Last month she told me never to whistle indoors during a waning moon. I whistled. Nothing happened."

"Something happened," Elodie said darkly. "You just weren't paying attention."

Seraphine crossed the shop slowly, and August saw her cataloging it the way she would once have cataloged a crime scene—the shelves of carvings, the workbench with its dozen knives, the little clay bowl of sunflower seeds, the sweepings on the floor. Looking for evidence of what? He wondered. Magic? There wasn't any. That was the entire point. Just wood, and bone—he still worked bone, but only old, willing donations from the knacker's yard, and only into shapes: hares, moons, a running horse he was rather proud of—and a girl eating seeds.

"It's different in here," she said finally.

"You've been here before."

"Not since." She set the bread on the counter with both hands, carefully, as if it were an offering at a shrine and she wasn't sure the god accepted bread. "Before, it was a shop that hid something. Now it's..." She gestured vaguely. "A shop."

"Thank you," August said dryly. "I'll have that engraved."

"I mean it's—" She exhaled, frustrated with herself. August recognized the frustration; it was the same one that had lived in her for decades, the frustration of a woman whose entire life had been built on certainty and who had lately been robbed of it. "I don't know how to do this, August. I resigned from the Hunt eight weeks ago and I still wake at dawn reaching for my crossbow. I rode out here rehearsing a speech and I've forgotten all of it except the part where I say I'm sorry, and now I've said the bread part instead of the sorry part, and the bread is possibly stale."

"There's the sorry part," Elodie observed from the back. "Took eleven minutes."

"Elodie."

"What? I counted."

August picked up the bread. It was a good loaf, dense and dark, the kind with seeds baked into the crust. He tore off the end piece—the heel, the best part, which was a peace gesture in itself and they both knew it—and handed half to Elodie without looking, and she caught it out of the air the way she caught everything, silently, impossibly, still moving like someone the ordinary world hadn't fully convinced to obey its rules.

"Sit down, Sera."

"There's nowhere to sit."

"There's a stool behind the counter."

"That's your stool."

"My guest stool now." He nudged it out with his foot. "Sit."

* * *

They ate bread in a shop full of harmless carvings while afternoon light crawled across the floorboards, and it was, August thought, the strangest meal of his life, and he had once eaten dinner in the Hollow's antechamber while the dead sang through the walls.

Seraphine talked the way she did everything—precisely, in order, with the sense that a report was being filed. The Hunt was dissolved in all but name; the Edict remained on the books, because laws were like teeth, apparently, and no councilman wished to be the first toothless one, but enforcement had gone soft as old fruit. Captains who once burned workshops now issued warnings. A hedge-witch in Lowmarsh had reopened a tea-stall openly, selling charms of finding against pickpockets, and the constable had bought one.

"And the thirteenth moon?" August asked quietly.

Seraphine's hand paused on the bread. "Full tonight," she said. "And quiet. As they've all been since the Turning." She met his eyes. "I know what you did. Don't look like that—I've known for months. I was there at the door, August. I watched you carve the covenant into your own—" She stopped. Started again. "I lowered my crossbow. Do you imagine I've spent eight weeks not knowing exactly why?"

Elodie had gone very still on her windowsill perch, watching them both with the particular attention she gave to moments that mattered.

"I never thanked you," Seraphine said. "For lowering it, I mean. No. That's wrong. I never thanked you for making me lower it. There's a difference." She laughed, short and unsteady. "Seven years I told myself I spared you because you were salvageable. Because you'd renounced it. Because mercy, given once, buys loyalty forever. And then at the door I understood I'd spared you because I couldn't shoot you, and I'd built thirty years of hunting on top of a lie about my family, and the whole edifice came down in one night on a salt flat, and now I'm a retired woman who brings bread to a bone-carver and gets insulted by a child about fountain proximity."

"Bread dries near water," Elodie confirmed solemnly.

"It's still good bread," August said. It was. He ate another piece.

They sat with that for a while—the imperfect, lumpy quiet of three people who had hurt each other in various directions and were attempting, clumsily, to be in a room together anyway. Forgiveness, August thought, wasn't the clean transaction he'd imagined as a young man. Nobody signed anything. Nobody wept. Mostly it was bread, and insults about fountains, and the slow discovery that you could sit across from someone without the old wound opening.

"I keep waiting to feel punished," Seraphine said, looking at her hands. "Thirty years of believing the Hollow ate my family. Finding out it was our own zealots who did it—you'd think that would break something. Instead I mostly feel..." She searched. "Lighter. Which feels obscene. I should feel destroyed. I feel like I got away with something."

"You grieved the wrong murderer for thirty years," August said. "But you still grieved. It counts. It counts badly, but it counts."

"That's the worst comfort anyone has ever given me."

"I'm a charm-seller, not a priest."

Seraphine smiled then—a real one, brief and crooked, the smile he remembered from before, from the years when she'd been his teacher and the world had simpler shapes. Then her eyes drifted to the shelves, and she rose, and began to move among the carvings the way customers did: slow, touching nothing, looking hard.

She stopped at the hare. The running horse. The sparrow with its cocked head.

"These are good," she said, and there was something raw under it. "These are better than anything you made before. Before, even stripped of power, they always felt like—they felt like they were *hiding* something. Waiting. These are just themselves."

"That was rather the lesson," August said.

"How much for the horse?"

"For you? Nothing."

"Absolutely not. Marta told me about your pricing. Beautiful things cost ugly prices." She was already digging in her cloak purse. "I will not accept charity from a former criminal."

"Former," August noted. "Progress."

She paid him full price, counting coins out with officious precision, and he took them because refusing twice would have spoiled something fragile. At the door she paused, bread loaf abandoned on the counter—no, she pushed it toward him deliberately, *keep it*, the gesture said—and stood a moment in the doorway with the evening light coming in behind her.

"August. The council meets next month on the Edict. There's talk." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. "Talk of amendment. Registration instead of prohibition. Some of the old captains are furious. One of them said to me last week, 'If the law softens, what did all of it mean? What were we *for*?'"

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him the truth." She pulled her hood up. "I told him we were afraid, and we dressed it up as duty, and the kindest thing we can do now is stop pretending it was ever holy." A pause. "It won't pass cleanly. Nothing passes cleanly. But it'll pass."

She left. The bell rang. The shop settled around them like a dog lying down.

* * *

That night the thirteenth moon rose full, and August stood in the doorway and watched it come up over the rooftops of Vell, fat and white and utterly silent, and felt nothing underneath the world but the world.

Elodie appeared beside him with the clay bowl of seeds and offered it. He took some. They stood shoulder to shoulder, chewing, watching.

"Are you sad?" she asked eventually. "On the loud moons. When it used to sing."

He considered it honestly, because she could tell when he lied and considered it rude.

"A little," he said. "Like missing a house I moved out of. I know it's better that I left. I still sometimes want to go stand in the rooms."

Elodie nodded, satisfied with the shape of the answer. She cracked a seed. Then she said, in a voice too casual by half: "Under the floor last night. Something rolled over. Did you feel it?"

August went still.

"Feel it? I can't feel it, Elodie. I don't hear anymore. That was the price. That was the *point*."

"I know." She crunched. "I'm just saying. It sounded content."

"You heard it?" He turned. "Through the floor? With your ears?"

"With whatever I have instead of ears." She shrugged, maddening, inscrutable, a girl-shaped window into a country he could no longer visit. "It's been doing it more lately. Rolling over. Settling. Like a big dog getting comfortable." She looked up at him, moonlight in her eyes, and grinned. "Maybe it likes the new charms. Maybe it's taking up collecting."

Below their feet, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something ancient shifted its weight and sighed—or didn't sigh, because August heard nothing, absolutely nothing, and told himself firmly that the warmth spreading through the floorboards was just the day's stored heat escaping into the cool of the evening.

Just the day's heat.

Probably.

"Come inside," he said. "It's cold, and tomorrow Marta wants me to carve her a charm against bad debts, and I intend to overcharge her outrageously."

Elodie followed him in, and the door closed on the moonlit street, and beneath the city of Vell—patient, fed, and, if a certain girl was to be believed, contented—something ancient curled itself more comfortably around its anchor, and waited, and dreamed.
