## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley had three rules, and Ansel Thackeray had written none of them.

First: magic does not cross the border. It simply doesn't. A wizard could stand at the northern pass with a fireball the size of a cathedral cupped in her palms, take one step down into the valley, and find herself holding nothing but warm air and an inexplicable sense of embarrassment.

Second: memories thin here, like butter scraped over too much bread. Stay a week and you'd forget your mother's face. Stay a month and you'd forget you ever had one. The old-timers said it was the valley's mercy. Ansel thought that was generous of the valley, considering what it charged for everything else.

Third — and this was not so much a rule as a law of nature, like water running downhill or cats knocking things off tables — travelers will pay almost anything to get their own lives back.

Ansel had built a cottage, a trade, and something adjacent to a life on that third rule.

* * *

The morning the story properly begins, Ansel was up before dawn, which was not unusual, because the dawn in Hollowmere Valley came late and sullen, dragging itself over the eastern ridge like a cat being put in a bath. Ansel stood at the window of the cottage with a chipped mug of barley tea and watched the mist lie in the low fields like something sleeping.

"Good morning," Ansel said to the valley.

The valley did not answer. It never did. But there was a particular quality to its silence at this hour — a held-breath quality, a listening quality — and after eleven years, Ansel had learned to hear it as company. Which said rather a lot about how lonely a person could get without noticing.

The kettle ticked. The fire settled. Somewhere outside, a goat named Duchess was eating something she shouldn't, judging by the guilty quiet coming from her direction.

"You're a thief, Duchess," Ansel called through the door. "A common criminal."

Duchess bleated, unrepentant.

Ansel drank the tea. It tasted like hot water that had once been introduced to barley at a party. And then Ansel went to work, because today was a market day, and market days meant travelers, and travelers meant coin.

* * *

Here is how the trade worked, and it is important to understand that Ansel considered it honest work, or at least honest-adjacent:

When people crossed into the valley, they began to forget. Not all at once — the valley was cruel but patient — but steadily, the way a candle burns down. By the time they reached the far side, most travelers had lost days, sometimes weeks, sometimes whole reasons for making the journey. Merchants arrived at the southern border unable to remember whether they'd sold their wool or bought it. Pilgrims forgot which god they were walking toward. One poor fellow had once spent four days trying to remember if he was married, and had wept both when he concluded he wasn't and again when he remembered he was.

But here was the thing about forgotten things: they don't vanish. They pool. They settle into the stones and the streams and the dark corners of the valley like sediment, and certain people — people with a knack, a touch, a small crooked talent that magic couldn't explain because magic wasn't allowed in to check — could scoop them back up.

Ansel had the knack. Ansel's hands, when pressed flat against a traveler's temples, could lift the loose memories right out — gently, cleanly, the way you'd lift a splinter. And then, at the border house where every departing traveler had to stop anyway, Ansel would sell them back.

Fair prices, mostly. Sliding scale. Ansel even gave discounts to pilgrims, on the theory that gods probably appreciated the gesture even if no god had ever been able to enter the valley to say so.

It was, Ansel told anyone who asked (nobody asked), a public service.

* * *

The market square of Lowmark, the last town before the southern pass, smelled of wet wool, fried onions, and the particular desperation of people who were about to discover they'd lost something and couldn't name what.

Ansel set up the stall at midmorning: a folding table, two chairs, a striped awning that had been patched so many times it was more patch than awning, and a hand-painted sign that read:

**T. THACKERAY — MEMORY RESTORATION & CURIO**
*No refunds. No exceptions. Yes, even you.*

The "curio" part was a legal fiction. Ansel sold a few genuine trinkets — river-glass beads, carved spoons, a suspicious number of combs — to keep the sign from being a confession.

The first customer came before the onions finished frying. He was a wool merchant named Bost, broad as a barn door, with the ruddy complexion of a man who argued professionally.

"Thackeray." He dropped onto the chair across the table like a sack of turnips. "I've lost Tuesday."

"All of it?"

"The whole blasted day. I know I had it. I remember Monday night, I remember planning things for Tuesday, and then Wednesday I'm standing in a field outside Marrow Ford wondering why my boots are muddy and where my hat went."

"Do you have the hat?"

"I do not have the hat, Thackeray."

"Then we know what we're looking for." Ansel cracked knuckles theatrically, which served no purpose whatsoever but made customers feel they were getting their money's worth. "Hold still. This won't hurt. If it hurts, I'm doing it wrong, and you should tell me immediately, because I charge extra for pain."

Bost snorted. Ansel leaned forward and laid fingertips against his temples, and let the knack rise.

Lifting a memory felt, from the inside, like dipping a hand into cool still water and feeling around for smooth stones. Most minds were murky near the bottom — the valley saw to that — but the memories themselves stayed whole, round and self-contained, waiting. Ansel found Tuesday quickly. It was a good Tuesday, as Tuesdays went: rain, a card game in an inn, a joke involving a bishop and a donkey that Bost clearly treasured, a hat purchased in a moment of weakness from a peddler with shifty eyes.

Ansel drew it out and set it into Bost's mind the way you'd slide a book back onto its shelf.

The merchant blinked. His whole face changed — softened, brightened, reorganized itself around the returned day.

"The donkey!" he bellowed, delighted. "The bishop! Ha! I knew I'd had a good Tuesday. Nobody has a Tuesday like that and just loses it." He slapped coins on the table — too many, as usual; Bost tipped like a man compensating for something — and stood, adjusting his coat. "You're a marvel, Thackeray. A swindler, but a marvel."

"High praise. Mind the pass, Bost. And buy your hats closer to home."

He lumbered off, and Ansel pocketed the coins and felt — and this was the feeling Ansel never examined too closely, the way you don't press on a bruise to see if it still hurts — satisfied. Useful. Warm, in the way a banked fire is warm.

There were fourteen more customers before dusk. A pilgrim who'd lost her prayer. Two brothers who'd each forgotten the other existed and were reunited at Ansel's table in a scene so sentimental that Ansel charged them double out of self-defense. A young woman who wanted to sell a memory rather than buy one back — a grief, fresh and heavy — and Ansel took it, because that was also part of the trade, the other half of the ledger: some people paid to forget.

That was the part nobody thanked you for. That was the part that sat in the cache under the floorboards with all the others, in the dark, in the jars.

By evening Ansel's shoulders ached and the coins made the apron pockets hang lopsided, and the young woman's grief sat behind Ansel's ribs like a swallowed stone, cold and borrowed.

Time to go home.

* * *

The cottage sat a mile out from Lowmark, up a slope, beside a stream that forgot which way it was flowing twice a year and had to be politely redirected. It was small, well-built, and defended by a garden that produced vegetables of aggressive mediocrity and one pumpkin of frankly alarming ambition. Duchess lived in the lean-to and considered herself the true owner of the property, with Ansel as staff.

Ansel lit the lamp, fed the stove, fed the goat, fed the self, in that order of decreasing enthusiasm, and then sat at the table with the ledger.

The ledger was a habit from Corvane — write everything down, girl, ink remembers what heads can't — though Corvane hadn't come through the valley in over a year now, and the ledger had quietly become less a business record than a diary with columns. *Bost, wool merchant: 1 day, returned, tip excessive.* *Pilgrim, unnamed: 1 prayer, returned, no charge.* *Young woman, red cloak: 1 grief, received.*

Ansel looked at that last line for a long time.

Under the floorboards, in the cache, the jars waited. Each jar held a memory that someone had paid to be rid of. Ansel didn't open them often. There was no reason to. They weren't for selling — who would buy a stranger's grief? — and they weren't for keeping, exactly. They were just... kept. The way you keep a letter you'll never send.

Eleven years in the valley. Eleven years of Tuesdays and prayers and griefs, of tips and haggling and the small warm satisfaction of handing people back their own lives. It was a good trade. It was a good life. Ansel had a roof, a goat, a pumpkin, a ledger full of names, and enough coin buried in three locations that retirement was a genuinely plausible future plan.

Wary contentment, Corvane had called it once, watching Ansel count the till. *You've built yourself a life with the doors locked from the inside, girl. Comfortable as a coffin.*

Corvane talked like that. It was why he was fun at parties and unbearable at breakfast.

Ansel blew out the lamp and lay in bed listening to the stream and the wind working at the eaves, and told herself, as she did every night, that the silence of the valley was peaceful. That solitude was a choice. That a person could live an entire good life among strangers' memories and never once need a memory of their own.

Because here was the thing Ansel did not think about, the thing at the very bottom of the very darkest jar, the thing the valley had taken first and best:

Ansel Thackeray could not remember anything from before arriving at the border, eleven years ago, with sore feet, empty pockets, and a head that was — though Ansel had only realized this years later, lying awake in exactly this bed, in exactly this silence — strangely, terribly light.

No childhood. No mother's face. No first snow, no first theft, no first anything. Just a name, a knack, and a note in unfamiliar handwriting pinned inside a coat: *Head south. Sell what you find. Trust the fence called Corvane.*

Everyone assumed the valley had eaten those years. Everyone would be wrong, though no one alive knew it yet.

Ansel fell asleep. Outside, the wind turned, and turned colder, and began to carry the smell of a storm coming down off the mountains — a storm that would arrive tomorrow night, along with something else, something small and silent and speaking a language older than any kingdom, whose words would make the very stones of the valley sit up and hum.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight, the valley forgot, and Ansel slept, and the jars under the floorboards held their breath in the dark.
