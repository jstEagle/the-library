## 2. A Price in Small Coins

The trouble with stealing memories, Ansel had learned over eleven years of practice, was that nobody ever missed them right away.

A purse stolen from a belt was noticed within the hour. A horse from a stable, by morning. But take a man's memory of his mother's laugh and he would walk around for days with a small, unnameable ache behind his ribs, like a tooth that hadn't decided whether to hurt yet. He'd blame the weather. He'd blame the stew. By the time he worked out what was actually missing, he'd be three valleys away, and Ansel would have already weighed it, priced it, and sold it back to him at the border for exactly what the market would bear.

This was not, Ansel reminded themselves while setting out the morning's cups on the trestle table, a dishonest trade. It was a *service*. The valley had no magic in it—couldn't hold it, couldn't grow it, wouldn't so much as let a candle flicker blue—and so travelers came through carrying their wonders like wet wool, heavy and dripping, glad enough to set them down for a while. Ansel lifted the load. Kept it safe. Sold it back at the north border, where the world remembered how to be strange again, at a fair markup for storage and handling.

Fair markup. Storage and handling. Ansel repeated these phrases to themselves the way other people said prayers.

The morning's first customer was already trudging up the path: Master Hobb, a wool merchant built like a rain barrel, who had come through the valley four times now and each time left a little lighter and returned a little heavier. He settled onto the bench with a groan that suggested the bench had personally wronged him.

"Thackeray." He nodded at the cups. "Is that the good tea or the tea you give people you don't like?"

"The tea is identical," Ansel said, pouring. "It's the price that changes."

Hobb laughed, which was generous of him, because he was about to be robbed.

Ansel's hands did the work while their mouth did the talking—a trick Corvane had taught them in the first year, back when Ansel's fingers shook and their conscience shouted loud enough to wake the neighbors. *Talk about the weather,* Corvane had said, folding Ansel's wrist into the correct angle as easily as teaching a child to whistle. *Talk about anything. The hands know what they're doing even when the heart objects. And if the heart keeps objecting, feed it. That's what the ledger's for.*

So Ansel talked about the weather—the storm due up from the south, by the smell of it, two days out maybe three—while their fingertips rested, light as moths, against the back of Hobb's broad red hand. The lifting itself was nearly nothing. No flash, no shiver. Just a sensation like drawing a thread through cloth, and then a small weight in the palm, warm as a fresh egg.

Ansel glanced down at it, cupping it below the table's edge out of habit.

And stopped talking mid-sentence.

It was grief. Ansel knew grief's weight the way a fishmonger knows fish—by feel alone, no need to look. But this grief was *familiar*, the way a road is familiar, the way a particular creak in a particular stair is familiar. Ansel turned it over in their palm. Inside it, dimly, was the shape of a woman singing, off-key, in a kitchen that smelled of—

Of bread. Of burnt bread, specifically, because she always forgot the loaf until the smoke told her, and she'd pull it out laughing and say the same thing every time:

*Well. Now we know the oven still works.*

Ansel's stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the cellar, and kept going.

They knew this memory. Not its contents—they'd never dared open one of their own stock, not once in eleven years, because opening a thing meant knowing it, and knowing a thing meant wanting it, and wanting was how thieves became something worse. But they knew its *weight*. They knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark. This was the memory they had lifted from Hobb four years ago, on his first pass through the valley, and sold back to him twice since, each time taking it again at the far border because Hobb, like most travelers, found the valley easier to cross without it.

Four sales. Four markups. One dead woman singing about an oven.

"Thackeray?" Hobb was frowning at them. "You've gone pale as a turnip. Bad tea after all?"

"No," Ansel said, and heard their own voice arrive from somewhere very far away. "No, it's—it's strong, is all."

*Put it back.* The thought arrived whole and urgent, wearing the voice of whoever Ansel had been before Corvane got hold of them. *He's paid for it twice. It's his. Put it back and charge him for the tea.*

But here was the arithmetic, and Ansel had always been good at arithmetic: if they put it back now, unprompted, Hobb would ask why. And there was no honest answer to why. There was no answer at all that didn't end with *because I have been selling you your own dead mother four times over*, and Hobb was a large man with large hands, and the nearest constable was a two-day ride away and also imaginary, because the valley didn't believe in those either.

Better, whispered the older and quieter voice, the one that sounded disconcertingly like Corvane, to do nothing dramatic. To finish the transaction. To keep the ledger clean.

Ansel looked at the little warm weight in their palm. Somewhere inside it, a woman was pulling a ruined loaf out of an oven and laughing about it, and she had been laughing in there, in the dark, in Ansel's keeping, for four years.

*Storage and handling,* said the Corvane voice.

*Oh, shut up,* said Ansel, to both voices, and slipped the memory into their coat pocket instead of the scale-pan.

* * *

The rest of the day went the way days went, which is to say it went according to the ledger.

A pair of pilgrims came through heading south, and Ansel lifted their wonder at the shrine they were traveling toward—no point arriving awed, they agreed cheerfully, better to save the awe for the arrival—and sold them, in exchange, three hours of pleasant boredom, which was the cheapest thing in Ansel's stock and the most popular. A courier took a dose of courage, borrowed against his return trip. A young couple on their way to be married asked, blushing, whether Ansel could take the memory of an argument they'd had in the spring, and Ansel did, gently, and charged them half rate, and did not tell them that the argument was the truest thing either of them owned and that they would want it back someday, probably around their first hard winter.

That one Ansel put in the drawer marked *to return unsold*, which was not strictly a business practice but which Ansel maintained anyway, the way a miser might keep one coin too bent to spend.

By dusk the pocket with Hobb's memory in it felt like a stone sewn into the lining.

Ansel ate supper standing up, which they did when thinking, and thought, which they did rarely and badly. The cottage was small and well-ordered: one room, one bed, one shelf of cups, one iron stove that had never once done anything interesting. On the wall hung the ledger, bound in grey leather, its spine cracked from use. Eleven years of entries. Names, weights, prices, dates. Ansel had never gone hungry. Ansel had never gone cold. Ansel had, by any reasonable measure, made a life.

So why did the room feel smaller tonight? Why did the stove look less like a hearth and more like a box that held fire prisoner?

"You're being ridiculous," Ansel informed the kettle.

The kettle declined to comment. Things in the valley generally did. That was rather the point of the place.

Ansel took down the ledger instead, because if the hands are busy the heart has less to say, and began, without quite deciding to, at the beginning. Their own handwriting stared back at them, younger and loopier in the early pages. *First entry. Traveler, male, peddler. Took: fear of drowning. Gave: 2 silver.* Below it, in Corvane's hand, a note: *Good lift. Clean edges. You're a natural, chick. Don't let it go to your head or anywhere else soft.*

Chick. Corvane had called them chick for three years, until Ansel grew sharp enough to object and Corvane had switched to *Thackeray* with such elaborate formality that it became a joke between them, and the joke had become a friendship, or whatever Corvane had instead of friendships.

Ansel turned pages. Seasons of them. Weights and prices, weights and prices. And then, near the middle of the fourth year, an entry that stopped their thumb like a nail:

*Traveler, male, wool merchant (Hobb). Took: grief (mother). Gave: 4 silver.*

Below it, in Ansel's own younger writing, a line they had no memory of writing:

*Felt wrong. Ask C. if we can stop doing these.*

And below that, in Corvane's confident slant:

*C. says: wrongness fades. Coin doesn't. Also, you cried, which was embarrassing for us both. Drink something.*

Ansel sat down slowly on the stool.

They remembered the crying. Dimly. The way you remember weather from childhood—a fact more than a feeling. They remembered Corvane's hand on their shoulder that night, and the drink, and the lesson that came with it, delivered in Corvane's gentlest register, the one that could make cruelty sound like arithmetic: *Everything we take belongs to someone else, chick. That's what taken means. If you start weighing whose grief hurts more, you'll starve. Take the coin. Feed yourself. The world will keep making grief; it's the one crop that never fails.*

And Ansel had taken the coin. For seven more years.

The pocket pulled at them. Ansel reached in and drew out Hobb's memory, held it in the lamplight. From outside it looked like nothing much—a bead of amber warmth, ordinary as a drop of honey. Inside it, a woman sang off-key about an oven.

Here was the thing Ansel had never let themselves think, in eleven years of careful not-thinking: the valley took nothing from anyone. The valley only made room. Every traveler who walked in carrying their wonders and their wounds walked out lighter, and lighter, and lighter, until some of them—like Hobb—were crossing back and forth so hollowed-out that Ansel had begun to recognize them not by their faces but by their *emptinesses*, the specific shapes of what was missing. Hobb without his mother. The courier without his nerve. The young couple who would someday want their argument back and find the drawer empty, because Ansel had sold it after all, because coin doesn't fade, because the world keeps making grief and someone may as well harvest it.

"I'm a farmer," Ansel said to the lamp. "That's what I am. I run a farm where the crop is people."

The lamp, being a lamp in a magicless valley, offered no opinion.

But the memory in their hand was warm, and it was Hobb's, and tomorrow—or the day after, when the storm blew through and Hobb passed north again—there would be a chance to set it back where it belonged. Quietly. Without explanation. A thief's refund. Ansel wrapped it in a scrap of flannel and placed it on the shelf beside the bed, where they would see it first thing in the morning and last thing at night, a small amber accusation.

Then, because the alternative was lying awake listening to their own conscience learn to talk, Ansel banked the stove, blew out the lamp, and went to bed.

* * *

The storm arrived a day early, the way storms and debts do.

It came up from the south at dusk, shouldering the hills aside, and Ansel spent the evening doing the small necessary things: bringing in the firewood, latching the shutters, weighting down the ledger. Hobb was due at the north border by noon tomorrow, storm or no—he had wool contracts waiting and the patience of a rain barrel, which is to say none. There would be time in the morning. There would be a moment at the door, a handshake, a *safe travels, Master Hobb*, and the memory could slip back into his pocket like a returned wallet, and Ansel's account would be—well, not clean. Nothing was going to make it clean. But balanced. Balanced was something.

The wind rose. The chimney spoke in its low voice. Ansel sat by the stove with the flannel-wrapped memory in their lap, turning it over and over like a worry-stone, and tried to remember what their own mother's kitchen had smelled like, and could not, and understood—with a clarity that felt almost like the magic the valley forbade—that somewhere in the ledger's eleven years there was an entry with their own name in the *took* column, and that they would never find it, because the person who wrote it had made sure of that.

Somewhere out in the dark, thunder rolled, and beneath the thunder—so faint Ansel might have invented it—the wind carried a sound like stones humming.

Ansel sat up straight.

The valley did not hum. The valley was the one place in the world that had been scoured silent, that was the entire *point* of it, that was the founding principle of Ansel's whole comfortable, crooked, carefully balanced life. Stones did not hum. Wind did not sing in dead languages. And yet the sound came again, threading under the storm like a wire under a carpet, and it was coming closer, and it was coming up the path, small footsteps in it, quick and bare.

Someone was walking to Ansel's door through a night that no sensible creature would cross a yard in.

And the stones were humming for them like a welcome home.

Ansel stood, clutching a dead woman's laughter in one hand and a poker in the other, and watched the latch begin to move.
