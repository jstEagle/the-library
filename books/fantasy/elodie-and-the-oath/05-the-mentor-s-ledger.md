## 5. The Mentor's Ledger

The storm had passed by morning, leaving the valley rinsed and shining, the way a liar looks after a good confession. Ansel stood at the cottage door with two cups of barley tea—one for themself, one set carefully on the low stool where Wren sat cross-legged, watching the steam rise as though it might say something.

Three days now, and the child had not spoken again. Not in any tongue. Wren ate what was offered, slept by the hearth without being told to, and watched everything with the patience of a cat deciding whether the house was worth living in. The stones around the cottage had stopped humming—or perhaps they hadn't, and Ansel had simply stopped being able to hear anything else over the sound of their own thinking.

*You could still take the child to the border,* said the practical voice in Ansel's head, the one that sounded suspiciously like Corvane. *Hand them to the next caravan going south. Problem solved. No one in this valley remembers problems longer than a week anyway.*

But when Ansel imagined Wren's small hands on a stranger's cart rail, shrinking in the distance, something in their chest pulled tight, like a stitch catching.

"You're thinking too loudly," Ansel told the child. "It's very rude."

Wren blinked at them, solemn as an owl, and drank their tea.

Ansel was reaching for the kettle when they felt it—that prickle along the back of the neck that every thief in every kingdom knows, the one that says *someone is watching your door.* Not a traveler. Travelers came up the road with their memories jangling loose like coins in a torn pocket. This was something else. This was someone standing very still on purpose.

Ansel knew only one person who stood still on purpose.

They set down the kettle. "Wren. The back room. Now, please."

The child went—not running, which somehow made it worse—and Ansel took a breath, straightened their collar, and opened the front door wearing the expression of a person whose morning contained absolutely nothing unusual.

Corvane stood in the yard like a crow that had decided to become a man and had mostly succeeded. Tall, narrow, dressed in gray so fine it made Ansel's coat feel like sacking. His hair was iron-colored and swept back from a face built entirely of angles and patience. He held a ledger under one arm—the ledger, black leather gone soft as old skin, its corners worn round from forty years of thumbs.

"Ansel." His voice was warm the way a banked fire is warm: useful, controlled, and capable of burning your house down if you left it alone too long. "You look well. You look *settled.* I've always found settled people easier to talk to."

"Corvane." Ansel leaned against the doorframe, casual as anything, heart hammering like a traveler's knees at the border post. "Nobody told me you were coming. Nobody ever tells me you're coming. It's practically our tradition."

"Traditions matter." He smiled, thin and genuine at the edges. "May I come in? It's damp out here. Damp is bad for paper."

Everything was bad for paper except Corvane's own careful hands, which had taught Ansel how to hold a stolen memory like a soap bubble—gently, briefly, and never letting it touch your face.

Ansel stepped aside. What else could they do? Slamming the door on Corvane would be like slamming it on weather.

He entered the way he entered every room: as though it had been tidied in anticipation of him. His eyes did a slow circuit—the hearth, the tea things, the second cup, the small stool. Ansel watched him notice all of it and file it away behind those pale eyes, and thought, with distant despair, *I have already lost.*

"A child lives here now," Corvane said. Not a question. Corvane didn't ask questions; he announced facts and let you decide how much trouble they were.

"A traveler left them," Ansel said. "During the storm. Orphans turn up in valleys. It's practically a tradition too."

"Mm." He lowered himself into Ansel's good chair—the one with the cushion—and arranged his long legs, and set the ledger on his knee, and looked at Ansel with something that in a lesser man would have been called fondness. In Corvane it was called *assessment.* "Do you know why I came all this way, Ansel? Past the pass, past the border stones, into this charming little hole in the world where nothing works and nobody remembers?"

"The tea?" Ansel offered.

"I've had word," Corvane continued, unbothered, "from people south of here. People who deal in rarer goods than we do. They say a child walked out of Aldermont three weeks ago. A child who doesn't speak—at least, not anything anyone can use. And they say the crown is paying a great deal to have that child found before the season turns." He tapped the ledger once, softly, with one long finger. "A great deal, Ansel. Enough that a certain fence in the low country could retire. Enough that his favorite student could stop selling memories of first kisses to homesick wool merchants and buy an actual house, with actual windows, somewhere the fog doesn't come indoors."

Ansel kept their face pleasant and empty, the trade's oldest trick. "Sounds like a lot of walking for a man your age."

"My age has excellent knees." He smiled again. Then, gently, almost kindly: "Where is the child, Ansel?"

"In the back room," said Ansel, because lying outright to Corvane had never once worked, and because some stubborn thing in their chest had apparently taken over management of their mouth. "Asleep, probably. Or listening. They're very good at listening."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. Not anger. Something more like *disappointment*, which from Corvane was far worse.

"You've grown attached." He said it the way a physician says *you've grown a tumor.* "That's my fault. I taught you the trade but I never taught you the rule that comes after it. Do you remember the rule?"

"*Nothing you carry belongs to you,*" Ansel recited. First lesson, learned at fifteen with cold fingers and a stolen memory of somebody's wedding day warming their palm.

"No. The one after that."

Ansel hesitated.

"*Nothing that carries you belongs to itself,*" Corvane said quietly. "Attachment is just theft turned inward, girl. You steal a piece of someone by loving them. And then someday someone comes to collect, and you learn what all your hoarding was really for." He patted the ledger. "Speaking of which. Before we discuss the child further, there's a small matter of accounts."

* * *

Here is what Ansel should have done: refused. Told him the ledger could stay shut. Told him whatever was written in it, they'd take his word for it, the same as always.

Here is what Ansel actually did: watched, with a kind of fatal curiosity, as Corvane opened the black book across his knees.

The ledger was famous between them, or rather, infamous. Every debt, every favor, every memory advanced against future earnings, recorded in Corvane's small precise hand. Ansel had seen it a hundred times over fifteen years. It was the spine of their whole arrangement: *you owe me, I own you, isn't family wonderful.*

But today, for the first time, Corvane turned it toward the light and read aloud.

"Year one. Advance: lodging, winter coat, instruction. Against: services rendered. Year two. Advance:—" he read on, dry as dust, sums and seasons, and Ansel nodded along, half-listening, until—

"—year four. Received from A. Thackeray: one name, freely given. Consideration received: protection during the Hollow Night. Witnessed and sealed."

Ansel stopped nodding.

"What?"

"One name," Corvane repeated, patient as a schoolmaster. "Freely given. It's all here. Your own hand signed the margin—you had terrible handwriting then, I could barely read it myself."

"That's not—" Ansel crossed the room and took the ledger, and Corvane let them, which was its own kind of alarm bell, because Corvane never let anyone touch the ledger. "This isn't right. There's no such entry. I'd remember giving away a *name.*"

"Would you?" said Corvane, mildly. Too mildly.

Ansel stared at the page. The ink was brown-black, old, the handwriting unmistakably theirs—younger, loopier, but theirs. *Received from A. Thackeray: one name, freely given.* And below it, in the consideration column, in Corvane's hand: a single word, blotted, illegible.

Except—it wasn't quite illegible. It was as if Ansel's eyes kept sliding off it, the way eyes slide off a word in a dream. They tried three times to read it and three times arrived at the end of the line without having seen the middle.

"There are others," Corvane said, watching them with that terrible gentleness. "Look further. Year six. Year nine. Year twelve."

And there were. Small entries, scattered through the years like burrs in wool. *One afternoon, freely given. One grudge, surrendered. One promise, recalled and returned to sender.* Each in Ansel's hand. Each sealed. Each describing something Ansel had absolutely no memory of doing.

"Corvane." Their voice came out strange and flat. "What did you do to me?"

"Nothing." He spread his hands, all innocence, all angles. "I wrote down what you gave me. That's all a ledger is, Ansel—a memory that doesn't forget. That's the whole point of this valley, isn't it? Paper remembers so people don't have to." He rose, took the book back with two fingers, and closed it softly. "Though I confess even I find these entries curious. Names given and forgotten. Afternoons surrendered. Almost as though someone were..." he tilted his head, "...*thinning* you. Trimming you back, year by year, like a hedge. Keeping you neat."

"Keeping me *useful,*" Ansel said.

"I said what I said."

From the back room came a small sound—a floorboard, or a foot, or a breath. Corvane's head turned toward it with the smooth economy of a heron striking water, and Ansel moved without deciding to, stepping sideways into his line of sight, arms folded, smiling.

"The child stays," Ansel said. "Whatever the crown is paying. Whatever's in the book. They stay."

For a long moment Corvane simply looked at them, and Ansel felt the full weight of fifteen years of lessons settle onto their shoulders—every kindness, every coin advanced, every winter coat—all of it suddenly legible as *debt.*

"Well," Corvane said at last, and sighed, and snapped the ledger shut. "Then I suppose I'll stay a few days myself. See the valley. Visit old friends." He paused at the door, gray hat in gray hands, and gave Ansel a smile that was almost the old one. "You know, when I found you, you were nothing but hunger and cleverness. I made you a professional. Remember that, whatever else you remember—which, granted, may not be much."

He stepped out into the bright washed morning.

"Oh—and Ansel." He didn't turn around. "The entry from year four. The name you gave me. I'd think very hard about whether you want it back. Some things are priced the way they are for a reason."

Then he was gone up the road, a gray figure growing smaller between the humming stones, and Ansel stood in the doorway holding two cold cups of tea, hearing over and over the sound of that ledger closing like a mouth.

Behind them, small bare feet padded across the floor. Wren appeared at their elbow, silent as ever, and looked up—not at Ansel's face, but at the road, at the place where Corvane had vanished. Then the child reached out and touched the ledger-shaped absence in the air where the book had been, and made a sound.

Not a word. Not in any tongue Ansel knew.

A hum—the same note the valley's stones had sung the night of the storm—and Ansel felt it go through them like a key finding a lock they hadn't known they had, deep in the gap where year four used to be.
