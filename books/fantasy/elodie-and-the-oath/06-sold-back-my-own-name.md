## 6. Sold Back My Own Name

The child slept in the good chair.

Ansel had given up the good chair without ceremony, which surprised them more than anything else about the situation. The good chair was the one with the cushion that still held its stuffing, positioned to catch the morning light and the warmth of the hearth at once, a piece of furniture so comfortable that Ansel had once watched a traveling tinker sit down in it and immediately begin weeping for reasons he couldn't name. Ansel had sold him that feeling back later — the weeping, not the chair — at a modest markup.

Now the child occupied it like a small, solemn king, knees drawn up, chin on her knees, watching the fire with eyes that reflected it too faithfully. She hadn't spoken since the storm. She didn't need to. The stones of the cottage had done enough speaking for all three of them, humming their low buried note whenever she breathed near them, and Ansel had spent two days pretending not to notice that the foundation of their home was singing.

Pretending was a skill. Ansel was very good at skills.

"You'll ruin your eyes," Ansel said, because the child was staring into the fire the way other people stared into abysses, and because saying something ordinary felt necessary, the way a man might whistle while crossing a rope bridge.

The child looked up. Blinked. Went back to staring.

"Right," Ansel muttered. "The conversation thing. We're still working on that."

They turned instead to the shelf above the hearth — the one that looked, to any visitor, like a row of dusty jars full of river stones and old buttons. Visitors were meant to think that. Visitors were meant to think a great many things about Ansel Thackeray, most of them boring, all of them wrong in useful ways.

Each jar held a memory.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Actually, physically, in the way that a valley which magic cannot enter develops its own stubborn little magics out of pure spite — or perhaps out of grief; Ansel had never been able to decide which. A memory lifted from a traveler settled into glass like sediment into wine. Hold the jar to your ear — no, that was nonsense, you held it to your *temple* — and you could feel it: a weight, a temperature, a flavor of someone else's living.

Ansel ran a finger along the row. *The ferryman's first kiss. The widow Hale's wedding day. A soldier's last morning before the war took his hearing.* Forty years of trade, give or take, though "trade" was a generous word for what happened at the valley's border, where travelers arrived heavy with their lives and left lighter, paid fairly in coin for what they'd never miss until they did.

And then there were the ones Ansel didn't sell. The private stock. The bottom shelf, behind the false panel, where the oldest memories lived.

Ansel hadn't opened the false panel in years. There was no reason to. The oldest memories were capital, and capital was not for spending, and besides — some of them made the room feel cold when you held them, and Ansel had long ago decided that a person was entitled to avoid the things that made their own kitchen feel cold.

But the child had come through a storm speaking a dead language, and the stones had hummed, and Ansel had spent two sleepless nights listening to their own heartbeat and finding it suspiciously like a drumbeat before a battle. So perhaps it was time to take inventory. Perhaps it was time to count what they actually knew, as opposed to what they'd told themselves they knew.

Ansel moved the panel aside and took down the oldest jar.

It was small — smaller than the others, the glass gone faintly amber with age, the memory inside dark and slow-moving, like honey that had thought better of being sweet. No label. Ansel remembered labeling everything, eventually. This one had no label, which meant either that Ansel had taken it before establishing the system, or that Ansel had deliberately chosen not to know what it was.

Both possibilities were unsettling. Ansel set it on the table anyway, next to the teapot, and sat across from it like a merchant appraising goods from a customer who might be dangerous.

The child watched. Of course the child watched. The child watched everything with the patience of a stone, and Ansel was beginning to suspect that this was not a figure of speech.

"Don't judge me," Ansel said. "I'm allowed to look at my own inventory."

The child said nothing, judgmentally.

Ansel picked up the jar. It was heavier than it should have been. Memory-jars always weighed more than the glass and the sediment could account for — grief has mass, joy has mass, though joy weighs less, which Ansel had always found telling — but this one had a weight with a shape to it, a center of gravity that shifted when tilted, as if whatever was inside were leaning to see what Ansel would do.

"Right," Ansel said aloud, mostly for courage. "Here goes nothing. Or possibly here goes something. I've genuinely no idea."

They pressed the jar to their temple and let it open.

* * *

The trouble with lifting a memory is that you experience it whole. That's the craft of it, actually — anyone can crack a jar and get a flood; a thief gets the flood *shaped*. You step into someone's remembering the way you'd step into a river, and you learn very quickly which currents are safe to stand in and which will take your feet out from under you.

This one took Ansel's feet out from under them immediately.

Firelight. Not hearth-fire — too big, too orange, too much smoke in it. A hall, a great one, banners on the walls that Ansel's eye slid over without reading, the way dreams refuse to let you read signs. Stone floors worn smooth by centuries of feet. And voices — dozens of voices, chanting, but not in any tongue Ansel knew, and underneath the chanting a woman's voice, clear as a bell cracked down the middle, saying words that—

That stopped.

Not faded. Not blurred, the way old memories go soft at the edges. *Stopped*, clean as a cut rope, and where the rest should have been there was nothing — not darkness, not silence, but an absence with edges, a hole in the remembering shaped precisely like whatever had come next.

Ansel pulled out of the jar so fast they nearly knocked the teapot off the table.

Their heart was going like a smith's hammer. The cottage was quiet. The fire ticked. The child watched them with those too-faithful eyes, and somewhere beneath the floorboards, faint as a thought not quite finished, the stones hummed.

"That," Ansel said, when they could speak, "was not supposed to happen."

Memories didn't do that. Memories frayed. Memories went soft and golden and unreliable, that was half the charm of selling them back — people bought their own pasts at the border and found them sweeter than they'd left them, and nobody complained, because who complains about a kinder yesterday? But a memory didn't just *end*. A memory wasn't a road that stopped; it was a road that grew over. There was always something under the moss if you dug.

Unless someone had cut it. Unless someone had taken a knife to the remembering and removed a piece so cleanly that the wound had healed around the absence, smooth as river stone.

Ansel looked at the jar. The dark sediment swirled, slow and deliberate, and settled again.

"No," Ansel told it. "Absolutely not. I don't chase holes. Holes are how you lose fingers."

The child stood up from the good chair. Crossed the room without a sound. And put one small hand flat against the table beside the jar, and the stones beneath the floor began to hum — louder now, insistent, the note of a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"I said don't judge me," Ansel said weakly.

* * *

Here is what Ansel knew about their own history, in its entirety:

They had come to the valley young. How young, they couldn't say — the valley softened beginnings the way it softened everything, wore the sharp dates off events like water wearing letters off a gravestone. They had been taken in, or found, or collected — Corvane's word was *collected*, delivered with a smile that made the word worse — by Corvane, who taught them the trade: how to lift, how to hold, how to price, how to smile at travelers while picking their pockets of the only wealth they didn't guard.

Before that: nothing. A fog. The usual fog, Ansel had always assumed. Everyone's childhood went foggy at the start; that was nature's mercy, or nature's laziness, depending on your view.

Everyone's childhood went foggy. But everyone's childhood didn't have *edges*.

Ansel sat up most of the night with the amber jar and a candle, and did the thing thieves are best at, which is looking carefully at something everyone else walks past.

Because here was the thing about the hole in the memory. It wasn't empty. An absence is never truly empty — ask any thief; the space where the purse used to be tells you the purse's whole story. And the absence in that memory had a shape, and Ansel turned the shape over and over in their mind like a coin they couldn't read, until sometime past midnight, with the candle guttering and the child asleep in the good chair and the stones humming their low lullaby, the coin finally turned face-up.

The chanting had been coming from a circle of people. The circle had a gap in it — a gap in the *memory's* rendering of the circle, a place where the eye of whoever remembered had been fixed, fixed with terrible attention, on one particular point in the ring of robed figures. And the woman's voice had been addressing that point. Speaking toward it. Speaking, Ansel realized with a cold that started in the teeth and worked inward, speaking *to* —

To a child.

A small figure at the edge of the firelight. Small. Seven, maybe eight. Standing very straight the way children stand when they are frightened and have decided nobody will ever know.

The memory ended there. Cut. Clean. And the shape of the absence — the exact size and bearing of the small straight-backed figure the remembering had been fixed upon —

Ansel got up, walked to the small spotted mirror by the door, and looked at their own face for a long time.

"Oh," they said finally, to the tired, sharp-eyed, forty-some-year-old stranger in the glass. "Oh, that's not funny at all."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Ansel had laid out every jar from the false panel on the table in rows, forty-one of them, arranged by age — the amber one at the head, the newest at the foot — and spent the night doing what no memory-thief is ever supposed to do, which is auditing their own mind.

Because that was the real horror, once you saw it. Not the hole in the old memory. The holes everywhere else.

Ansel's own recollection of arriving in the valley: intact, vivid, detailed. Too detailed. Nobody's memory of a foggy, frightening arrival was that crisp unless someone had *built* it that way — and sure enough, when Ansel held it up to the light and examined the seams, there they were, fine as spider silk. A crafted memory. A gift. A replacement part installed where the original had been removed.

And the original had been removed by a professional. By, say, someone who had taught a child everything they knew about lifting and holding and pricing and smiling.

Ansel sat back from the table and laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh that woke the child, who sat up in the good chair and regarded the rows of jars and the wreck-faced thief behind them with an expression that managed, without a single word, to say *I could have told you this days ago.*

"Forty years," Ansel said to the room. "Forty years I've been the best thief in this valley, and I never once checked my own pockets." They rubbed their face with both hands. "Do you understand what that means? Someone robbed me before I even knew I had pockets. Someone robbed me and then — and this is the elegant part, this is the part I'd almost admire if it weren't *me* — someone taught me the trade so I'd spend my whole life handling other people's memories and never once think to weigh my own."

The child slid off the chair. Came to the table. Looked at the amber jar for a long moment, and then reached out and touched it, one finger, feather-light.

The stones under the floor sang.

Not the low hum this time. Something with structure to it — three notes, rising, like a question asked in a language made of geology. The jars on the table trembled in their rows, all forty-one, and inside the amber jar the dark sediment rose off the bottom and hung suspended, turning slowly, and for one heartbeat Ansel saw it clearly: not sediment. A figure. Small, straight-backed, standing at the edge of firelight.

Then it settled, and the humming died, and the child withdrew her hand and looked at Ansel with an expression of such plain, uncomplicated sorrow that Ansel had to look away.

"Yes, well," Ansel said briskly, because briskness was armor and armor was cheaper than tears. "Someone cut a piece out of me. Fine. I've cut pieces out of better people than whoever did it, probably. The question is *what* they cut." They tapped the amber jar. "There's a woman in here. A voice like a cracked bell. She was saying something important — you can tell, the whole memory leans toward her the way a crowd leans toward a hanging. And then it stops. Which means somebody very much did not want me to remember what she said."

The child's eyes had gone bright. Fixed on the jar. Her lips moved — no sound, just the shaping of silent syllables, over and over, like a girl tasting a word she'd been carrying a long way.

Ansel leaned forward. "You know it. Don't you. Whatever she was saying — you know it."

The child looked up. And shook her head — slowly, and then, with visible frustration, shook it again, harder, and pressed both hands flat over her own mouth, and Ansel understood with a lurch that it wasn't *won't*. It was *can't*. That whatever lived behind the child's silence was locked, and the lock was not hers.

Which meant the key was somewhere else. Which meant, Ansel thought, stomach dropping through the floor and into the valley's singing stone, that the woman in the memory — the voice like a cracked bell, the mother-shaped absence at the heart of Ansel's oldest theft — had hidden something in the very place the cutter had failed to check.

Inside the hole itself. Inside the gap. Because no thief searches the empty space. Every thief knows the empty space is worthless.

Every thief except, apparently, the one who'd been robbed first.

Ansel picked up the amber jar and held it to the gray morning light, and somewhere down the valley road — though Ansel could not yet have known it — a man with a ledger was walking toward the cottage, and the ledger had a new line written fresh in ink, and the line said *the child*, and beneath it, in an older hand, faded but legible, it said a name.

The name was not Ansel Thackeray.
