## 7. The Word Beneath the Tongue

The valley does not have a word for *morning*.

Wren knows this the way they know most things now—not by learning it but by feeling its absence, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. In the old language there were forty words for morning, one for each way light could arrive: light through smoke, light on water, light after grief, light that comes back like a dog that wandered off and thinks you won't notice. Here there is only *the sun is up*, which Ansel says while yawning, as if the sun has done something mildly inconvenient.

Wren sits on the cottage floor with their legs folded under them and their hands flat on the stones, because the stones are listening. The stones have been listening since the night Wren arrived. They hum when Wren dreams. They hum when Wren is hungry. Last night they hummed so persistently that Ansel had gotten up three times to check whether a cart was coming down the road, and finally had thrown a blanket over the hearthstone as if tucking in a restless child, muttering, "Settle down, we're all tired."

That had made something happen in Wren's chest—a small bright folding, like paper creased into the shape of a bird. They think the valley-word for it might be *laughter*, except laughter requires sound, and Wren's sounds died somewhere on the road south, along with everything else the palace took from them.

But the language did not die. That is the thing the crown-thing never understood, though it wore a king and ate a kingdom and called itself salvation. You can take a child's voice. You can take their name, their mother's face, seven years of their small life. But the old language does not live in the throat. It lives underneath—beneath the tongue, beneath the ribs, beneath the place where memory is kept—and it goes out through the hands, through the feet, through the soles pressed flat against patient stone.

This morning, Wren has decided, they will teach Ansel a word.

They have been deciding this for four days, which in Wren's private accounting is roughly a season. Teaching is dangerous. Every syllable that leaves them costs something—they have learned this too, learned it in the shrinking of their own remembering, the way the palace grows smaller and smaller behind their eyes until soon it will be no bigger than a painted miniature, and then no bigger than a coin, and then gone. Each word spent is a room closed off inside them. But Ansel needs the words. Ansel is stitched into this valley like a patch over a wound, and patches cannot see what they cover.

And besides—and here Wren allows themselves the smallest, most selfish thought—besides, it has been so long since anyone listened to them properly. Ansel listens with their whole body. When Wren gestures, Ansel leans forward like a plant toward a window. It is not nothing, being leaned toward.

Ansel comes in from the garden with dirt to the elbows and a basket of onions that look personally offended by the weather, and Wren pats the floor beside them. Twice. Firmly. The way one summons a large dog or a slow student.

"I'm coming, I'm coming." Ansel sets down the basket, wipes their hands on their trousers, which accomplishes nothing, and folds themselves down onto the stones. "You've got that look. The one where I end up doing something foolish."

Wren points at the hearthstone.

"The fire? It's summer, Wren, I'm not lighting—"

Wren shakes their head, takes Ansel's hand—it is calloused and warm and it flinches and then doesn't—and presses it flat against the stone. Then they press their own hand beside it, and they reach down, down beneath the tongue, and they give the stone the first syllable.

It is not a sound. That is the first thing Wren must make Ansel understand, and it is the hardest thing, because Ansel lives in a country made entirely of sound and sight and has forgotten—or perhaps never knew—that meaning can travel other roads. The syllable moves through Wren's palm like water through a cloth. It is shaped like a door opening. It tastes, if taste could be trusted here, of iron and honey.

The stone answers.

The hum rises out of the floor and up through both their joined hands, and Ansel makes a noise—not quite a word, more the sound a person makes when a stair isn't where their foot expected—and snatches their hand back and then, remarkably, puts it right back again.

"That's—" Ansel's eyes are very wide. "The stones have done that before. Since you came. I thought it was the river. Underground water, or—Wren, that was *you*?"

Wren nods, and then, because honesty matters between people who are about to commit crimes against silence, tilts their head side to side. *Me and the stone. Both.*

"What was it? What did you say?"

How to explain. Wren looks around the cottage for tools—the chalk stub by the hearth, the back of an old ledger Ansel uses for accounts. They take the chalk and draw, in careful strokes, a door. Closed. Then they draw the same door, open, with light spilling out of it.

"A door," Ansel says slowly.

Wren taps the open door. Taps the stone. Draws a line from one to the other.

"You... opened something."

Wren considers this. Closes enough. They take Ansel's hand again and press it to the stone, and this time they guide—slowly, patiently, the way you teach someone to float by holding them under the shoulders until they discover the water wants to hold them too. *Here,* they push, along with the syllable. *Feel the shape of it. Not the sound. The shape.*

The second time, Ansel does not flinch. Their brow furrows in fierce concentration, the look of a person doing arithmetic at speed. And then—faltering, lopsided, like a foal standing for the first time—Ansel gives the shape back.

The hearthstone rings like a bell struck underwater. Dust jumps from the mantel. Outside, Wren feels rather than hears every stone within fifty paces sit up and take notice, and somewhere down the road a loose cobblestone settles itself with a satisfied little click, tidying its own bed after thirty years of complaint.

Ansel stares at their own hand as if it has been replaced overnight with a better model.

"That came from *me*," they say. "I don't—I've lived here my whole life and nothing here has ever—" They stop. Wren watches them arrive at the edge of a thought and step carefully around it rather than over it, the way Ansel steps around all the holes in themselves. "The valley doesn't do this. Nothing here does this. That's the whole point of the valley."

Wren writes on the ledger-back: **THE VALLEY IS NOT EMPTY. IT IS QUIET.**

Ansel reads it twice. "Those aren't the same thing?"

Wren draws a jar with a lid on it. Then they draw the jar full of bees.

Ansel looks at the drawing for a long moment. "Huh," they say, in the voice of a person whose house has just grown one room larger without any carpentry.

* * *

The second syllable takes three days, and nearly costs them the roof.

It is the walking-word, the one that tells stone it may move aside, and Wren teaches it last of the day, when the light through the window goes long and gold, because it is heavier than the door-word and leaves them tired in a way sleep does not fix. They practice on the garden path, which is sensible, because the garden path is already crooked and cannot be blamed for getting crookeder.

Ansel's first attempt produces a ripple that travels the length of the path and gently elevates every cabbage two inches above its bed, where they hover, rotating slightly, before settling back with the dignity of vegetables pretending nothing happened.

"Right," says Ansel, staring. "So it's less *move* and more *please*."

Wren grins—which is to say, their whole face does the thing that would be a grin if it had a voice attached—and corrects them. The shape must be offered, not commanded. The old language has no commanding in it; that is why the crown-thing hates it, Wren suspects, and why it survives. You cannot force stone. You can only remind it that once, long ago, it agreed to be a road.

On the fourth evening Ansel gets it truly right. They crouch at the garden gate where the hinge-post has rotted and the gate drags, and they lay two fingers against the post, and they offer the word—and the wood sighs, and straightens, and grain that had split years ago knits closed like a scar fading, and the gate swings afterward with a sound like a contented sigh.

Ansel stands there touching the post over and over, and Wren sees something move across their face that has no name in any language Wren still owns. It looks like grief wearing hope's coat. Or hope wearing grief's. On Ansel the two are hard to tell apart.

"My mother," Ansel says quietly, to the post, to the dusk, maybe to Wren. "I keep almost-remembering her hands doing things like this. Little mends. Small magics that weren't magic, she'd say, just *attention*. I always thought I invented that memory. Sold memories get jumbled, you pick up bits of other people's mothers and—" They stop. Shake their head. "Doesn't matter."

Wren writes: **IT MATTERS.**

Ansel looks at the slate a long time. "You're a strange child," they say, and their voice cracks in the middle of *strange*, and they go inside to put the kettle on, which Wren has learned is what Ansel does instead of crying.

* * *

The third syllable Wren does not want to give.

Not because it is dangerous—though it is, it is the heaviest of the three, the one that costs the most—but because of what it shows. It is the listening-word, the one that lets a person hear what the quiet is hiding. And Wren has heard what this valley's quiet is hiding, on the bad nights, when the hum under everything turns over in its sleep and murmurs, and the murmuring is not the murmur of stones.

It is the murmur of names.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. A whole sediment of remembered people lying under the valley floor like coins dropped through a grate, and every one of them belonging to some traveler who walked in whole and walked out lighter, and none of them belonging to the travelers anymore. The valley is not empty. The valley is a purse turned wrong-side-out, and everything shaken from it is still *here*, sunk into the ground, humming.

If Ansel hears that—if Ansel hears what their trade has been sitting on all these years—

But Wren has watched Ansel mend a gate and hover cabbages and laugh—actually laugh, a silent hunched shaking of shoulders—at a floating turnip, and has watched the loneliness recede in them like a tide going out, and Wren is seven-and-something years old and has already lost everyone once, and is selfish in the way of children who have learned that people leave.

Still. The crown-thing is looking for them. Somewhere south of the hills a man named Corvane is sharpening his patience, and Wren remembers him—remembers him the way you remember cold, as a fact about the world rather than a scene—and Ansel cannot fight a fence and his hunters with hovering cabbages. Ansel needs every word Wren can afford.

So on the sixth night, with rain starting against the roof in a rhythm the valley almost remembers how to love, Wren takes Ansel's hand, presses it to the floor, and gives them the listening-word.

Ansel goes very still.

Then their face changes in stages, like a sky. First wonder—the ordinary wonder of hearing rain properly for the first time, each drop distinct, the roof a drum, the world suddenly enormous in the ear. Then puzzlement, as beneath the rain another sound surfaces, low and layered, rising. Then recognition, arriving like cold water.

"Those are—" Ansel's whisper barely clears the rain. "Wren. Those are voices. Those are *names*." Their hand presses harder against the floorboards. "There are so many. There are—these are travelers' names. People who came through. People whose memories I—" The sentence falls off a cliff. Wren watches it go.

For a long time neither of them moves. The rain fills the silence, and beneath the rain the names go on murmuring, patient as sediment, and Wren keeps their hand on the boards beside Ansel's because some things should not be heard alone.

"They're not gone," Ansel says at last, and their voice is strange—stripped and rebuilt in the same breath. "All this time I thought the trade emptied people out and the emptiness went... nowhere. Into me, into the selling, into Corvane's ledgers. But it didn't go anywhere. It's *here*. Under the floor. Under the road." A wet, unsteady almost-laugh. "I've been sleeping on top of a hundred stolen lives and calling the humming the river."

Wren squeezes Ansel's hand. Writes, slowly, choosing each stroke: **NOT STOLEN. KEPT. THERE IS A DIFFERENCE.**

"Is there?" Ansel asks, but softly, genuinely asking, and Wren thinks: yes, actually, there is, and you of all people are going to learn it, because a keeper holds a thing so it can be given back, and a thief holds a thing so it can't.

Then Ansel looks up, and the lamplight catches their eyes, and the fear in them has changed shape—no longer the fear of the strange child at the door, but the fear of a person who has just discovered the floor of their life is thinner than advertised.

"The man who taught me the trade," Ansel says. "Corvane. He'll have felt this. Whatever it is you're waking up—he has a ledger for everything, and he'll have felt the valley changing, and he will come." They rub their thumb across the floorboards, over the hidden murmuring names, absently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "And I need to decide, before he gets here, which side of the ledger I'm standing on."

Outside, far down the dark road, a stone clicks—settling, or turning over in its sleep, or perhaps announcing a footstep that is still miles away. Wren feels it through the soles of their feet, and holds Ansel's hand tighter, and begins, silently, to rehearse the next word.
