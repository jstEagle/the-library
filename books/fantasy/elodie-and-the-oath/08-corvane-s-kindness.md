## 8. Corvane's Kindness

Corvane arrived the way bad weather arrives: first a change in the air, then the smell of him—oiled leather and dried herbs and something underneath like old coins—and finally the man himself, standing in Ansel's doorway as though he had grown there overnight.

Ansel had known he was coming for two days. The birds had gone quiet along the southern ridge, which meant someone with a heavy tread was walking softly, and only one person Ansel knew walked softly out of courtesy rather than caution.

"You look terrible," Corvane said, by way of greeting. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he trimmed himself and therefore trimmed badly, and eyes the color of tea that had been steeped too long. "Have you been eating?"

"I've been eating."

"Sleeping?"

"Corvane."

"Answer the question, Thackeray."

"I've been sleeping *fine*," Ansel lied, and Corvane's eyebrows rose in a way that suggested he had heard the lie, weighed it, found it wanting, and decided to be generous about it anyway. That was Corvane's whole manner, really: generosity with a ledger behind it. Every kindness came stamped with an invisible price, and you never saw the price until the debt collector arrived.

He stepped past Ansel into the cottage without being invited, which was also very Corvane. He set down his pack on the table—the good pack, the one with the brass buckles that sang when they moved—and began unpacking it with the unhurried confidence of a man who intended to stay.

Bread, dark and dense, still warm from some oven miles away. A wheel of cheese wrapped in waxed cloth. Two jars of honey. Dried apples. A twist of paper that, when Ansel sniffed it, turned out to be pepper, actual pepper, worth more per pinch than a traveler's best memory.

"You're bribing me," Ansel said.

"I'm *feeding* you," Corvane said. "There's a difference. Bribes are for people who haven't decided yet. I'm feeding a friend who's forgotten how to take care of themselves." He glanced around the cottage—at the unwashed cups, at the blanket dragged to the floor by the hearth where a small person had clearly been sleeping, at the little wooden bird Ansel had whittled badly and left on the windowsill. His gaze lingered on none of these things longer than it should have, which meant he was cataloguing all of them. "Though I'll admit the feeding comes with a conversation attached."

"There it is."

"There it is," Corvane agreed pleasantly, and sat down, and poured himself tea from Ansel's kettle without asking, because Corvane had never once in fifteen years asked permission for anything smaller than a kingdom.

Wren was not in the cottage. Ansel had made sure of that before opening the door—the child was up the hill with the widow Marta, ostensibly helping with the goats, actually learning goat-words in the valley's plain tongue with the fierce concentration Wren brought to everything. Still, Ansel stood between Corvane and the window that faced the hill, and Corvane noticed Ansel standing there, and Corvane noticed Ansel noticing, and nobody said anything about any of it, because that was how conversations with Corvane worked: the real one happened underneath, in the pauses.

"Eat something," Corvane said, pushing the bread across the table. "You're thin. You were always thin, but this is a new thin. This is a *worried* thin."

Ansel tore off a piece of bread mostly to have something to do with their hands. It was excellent bread. That was the trouble with Corvane—he never brought anything but the best, because he understood that generosity was most effective when it hurt to refuse.

"The child," Corvane said.

"Here we go."

"The child," Corvane repeated, with the patience of a man laying out cards one at a time. "The mute one who isn't mute. The one who speaks a language that makes my teeth ache from fifty paces. The one you've been hiding up the hill with Marta, who, incidentally, tells me everything, because I sell her back her late husband every spring and she thinks I hung the moon." He sipped his tea. "You've had the child what, three weeks now?"

"Two and a half."

"And in two and a half weeks, how much work have you done? How many travelers come through? How many memories lifted, sold, returned?" He didn't wait for an answer. "I'll tell you. Four. Four travelers, and two of those you let pass without so much as a skim, because you didn't want to leave the cottage long enough to do the work. Your larder is empty, Thackeray. Your coin box has dust in it. And meanwhile there is a creature in your keeping that half the people I know would pay a fortune to see, and the other half would pay more to make sure no one ever sees again."

"They're a child," Ansel said. "Not a creature."

"A child that hums stones." Corvane set down his cup. His voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "Ansel. Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"*Sit down.*"

And here was the thing about Corvane, the thing Ansel kept forgetting and then remembering with a lurch: the voice could change. Not loudly, not cruelly—there was never anything so honest as cruelty in Corvane—but the warmth would drain out of it like water through sand, and what remained was the thing that had taught a starving teenager to slide a stranger's grief out of their head without leaving a scar. What remained was the fence. The trader. The man whose ledger had no blank pages.

Ansel sat.

* * *

"Let me tell you a story," Corvane said, once Ansel was seated and the bread was torn and the tea was poured, because Corvane believed all negotiations went better with props. "Fifteen years ago, give or take, a child came stumbling down the south road into this valley. Half-starved. Feverish. Couldn't say where they'd come from, because—" he tapped his own temple— "well. You know why. The valley takes what you bring in, if you bring too much of it. That child had nothing. No name they could hold onto, no family, no trade, no coin. Nothing except a talent. A lightness of touch, we called it. The child could slip into a head like slipping into a house with the door left open, and slip out again carrying things."

"I know the story," Ansel said quietly.

"Of course you know the story. It's your story. But listen to how it goes, because you always skip the middle part." Corvane leaned forward. "In the middle part, there was a man—a fence, a trader, a reasonably decent sort given the profession—who found that child and took them in. Fed them. Taught them. Gave them a name, because they had none. Gave them a trade, because they had nothing. Gave them a *cottage*, Ansel, and a border to work, and a system, and a life. Fifteen years of protection. Do you know what that costs? Do you know what I spent keeping the guilds off you, the memory-brokers east of the pass, the ones who'd have snapped up a talent like yours and worn it down to the nub inside five years? Do you know what I paid the year the fever came through and you nearly died and I rode three days through snow to get the herb-woman from Coldbrook?"

"No," Ansel said. "I don't know. Because I don't remember any of it."

The words came out flatter than Ansel intended, and Corvane's face did something complicated—hurt flickering under the composure, quickly smoothed over, but not quickly enough.

"No," Corvane said. "You don't. Because the fever took it, or the valley took it, or you sold it yourself in a lean winter—I honestly don't know which, and neither do you. That's the bargain we all make with this place. It keeps magic out and it keeps our yesterdays loose." He spread his hands. "But I remember, Ansel. I remember all of it. And I'm telling you: you owe me. Not in coin. In something better than coin. In trust."

"That's a strange kind of debt," Ansel said. "One only you can count."

"It's the oldest kind." Corvane smiled, and it almost reached his eyes. "Every parent runs one."

"I'm not your child."

"No," Corvane agreed, after a pause a half-beat too long. "You're something better. You're my best work."

* * *

He laid it out then, the offer, the way he laid out everything: neatly, in order, each piece touching the next.

Protection. The people asking after the child—Corvane named three names, and Ansel recognized two of them, brokers from the eastern passes with reputations like knife wounds—would be turned away, bought off, misdirected. Corvane knew how to do this. Corvane had been doing it for Ansel for years, apparently, invisibly, a shield held up by hands Ansel had never seen.

Food. He gestured at the pack, at the jars and wheels and twists of paper. Monthly deliveries. Real food, valley food being what it was—goats and stubborn greens and whatever the river gave up.

A future. This was the part he saved for last, and he saved it because it was the blade inside the bread. "The valley can't hold you forever," he said. "You know that. You've felt it. One day the trade dries up, or the road moves, or you get old and slow, and then what? But there are places—south, past the salt marshes—where a person with your talents could live like a lord. I have contacts. I have arrangements. I could set you up by midsummer. A house. A garden, if you want a garden, you always struck me as a garden person." He paused. "All of it. Everything. For one thing."

"Don't," Ansel said.

"The child comes with me."

"Corvane—"

"Hear me out before you start building the argument you've been building since I walked in." He raised both hands, palms out, the picture of reasonableness. "I'm not going to hurt the child. Whatever you think I am, I'm not that. There are buyers who'd hurt the child, yes, and worse than buyers, there are *scholars*, and worst of all there are the king's people, eventually, because word travels and kings collect oddities. If the child stays here, with you, alone, poor, and getting poorer—what happens? Sooner or later someone comes up that road who doesn't want to buy. Someone comes up that road with iron. And you—" his voice gentled again, horribly— "you'd stand in front of them. I know you would. You'd stand in front of them with a whittling knife and your light touch, and they'd go through you like weather, and then they'd take the child anyway, and everything you sacrificed would have bought nothing at all."

Ansel's hands had gone cold around the teacup. "And if the child goes with you?"

"Then the child goes somewhere safe. Somewhere I choose, where people are paid to be discreet and paid well enough to stay that way. The child eats. The child learns. The child grows up." A shrug, expansive and false. "Maybe someday, when it's safe, you even visit."

"You're lying," Ansel said. "Or you're not lying, and you believe it, which is worse. Either way, you're not taking a child anywhere. You're taking a *commodity*. I've seen your ledgers, Corvane. I learned bookkeeping from you. I know what a line item looks like."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind Corvane's tea-colored eyes, something old and tired and hungry, and then it was gone, packed away, and the reasonable man returned.

"You think I don't care about them," he said. "That's fair. You've earned the right to think the worst of me; I taught you that too. But ask yourself this, Thackeray, and answer honestly, because you're the only witness either of us trusts: what exactly is your plan? Keep the child hidden in a valley that eats memories, teaching them goat words, until when? Until they're grown? On what money? Against what's coming?" He shook his head slowly. "Kindness isn't hiding someone in a hole and calling it love. Kindness is doing the hard arithmetic. And the hard arithmetic says: one child, handed over gently, against one thief kept alive and whole. That's the kindest sum available. I've done this math my whole life. I've never once enjoyed it. But I've never once gotten it wrong."

* * *

Afterward—after the pack was repacked, after the goodbyes that weren't goodbyes, after Corvane paused in the doorway and said, "Three days, Ansel. Bring the child to the crossroads inn by the third day, or I come back with the two names I mentioned and we do this the ugly way"—after all that, Ansel sat at the table among the crumbs of the excellent bread and did not move for a long time.

The terrible thing, the thing that sat in Ansel's chest like a swallowed stone, was that the arithmetic made sense. That was Corvane's genius, and maybe his tragedy: he was almost always *right*, in the narrow way that ledgers are right. The larder was empty. The hunters were real. Ansel had no plan, no coin, no allies beyond a widow with goats and a border full of strangers' borrowed feelings. Handing Wren over might well be the safest thing—for everyone except Wren.

And underneath all of it, coiling tighter with every hour, was the other thing. The debts. Fifteen years of debts Ansel couldn't verify, couldn't weigh, couldn't dispute—because the proof lived in Corvane's memory and nowhere else. A person could build a cage out of gratitude just as surely as out of iron, and Corvane had built this one patiently, bar by bar, kindness by kindness, over a decade and a half. *You owe me. You owe me. You owe me.* And Ansel, who remembered nothing of the account's beginning, had no way to check the sum.

Unless.

Ansel looked up at the shelf above the hearth, at the row of small stoppered vials that no traveler ever saw—the private collection, the hoard, the memories too precious or too strange to sell. And at the far end, wrapped in black cloth and older than everything else there, the one vial Ansel had never opened. The oldest theft. The first. Bought back from a dying peddler years ago for a price that had bankrupted a season, labeled in Corvane's own handwriting: *YOURS. DO NOT.*

Ansel had never dared. Opening it meant learning what the valley had taken, or what Corvane had, and either answer might unmake the person who opened it.

But Corvane wanted Wren delivered in three days. And Wren had been teaching Ansel words that burned holes in the silence, and the first of those words, spoken softly over the oldest vial, might show whether the ledger was true.

Ansel stood, took down the black-wrapped vial, and felt it hum faintly against their palm—like a stone remembering footsteps.

Outside, up the hill, Wren was singing to the goats in a language older than kingdoms, and the evening light bent toward the sound like it was listening.
