## 9. Night of the Humming Road

The road out of the valley began at a place Ansel had never once looked at directly.

That was not an accident. In thirty years of living in the valley of no magic, Ansel Thackeray had learned that some things you did not look at too hard, the way you did not count your coins in front of strangers or name your hens if you ever meant to eat them. The northern road was one of those things. It ran up out of the valley between two hills shaped like sleeping shoulders, and everyone in the valley knew it was there and nobody used it, because leaving was a thing you decided against so many times that eventually the deciding became part of you, load-bearing, structural.

Ansel stood at the mouth of it now with a satchel of stolen memories, a child's hand gripping two of their fingers, and the distinct sensation of having pulled a thread that was going to unravel rather more than a sweater.

"We're going to die," Ansel said conversationally. "I want to be clear about that before we start."

Wren looked up at them. The child's face, in the last grey light, wore the particular expression of someone who has heard this speech before and found it unconvincing both times.

"Fine," Ansel said. "But when we die, I'm telling Corvane it was your idea."

It was not Wren's idea. It was entirely, catastrophically Ansel's idea, born somewhere between Corvane's ledger full of names Ansel didn't remember writing and the hole in Ansel's oldest memory shaped exactly like themselves. But blame was a comfort, and Ansel had always been generous with it.

They stepped onto the road.

And the road *sang*.

Ansel stopped dead. Under their boots — under the packed earth and old gravel of a road that had carried nothing but traders' carts and forgetting for as long as anyone could remember — something hummed. Not wind. Not water under stone. A low, patient note, like a finger run around the rim of a glass, and it rose through the soles of their feet and settled somewhere behind the breastbone.

Wren's grip tightened. The child tilted their head, listening the way other people listened to music, and their mouth moved around a silent syllable.

"No," Ansel said quickly. "No singing. No humming. No whatever-that-is. We are sneaking, Wren. Sneaking is quiet by definition."

Wren gave them a look of profound pity and pointed down.

Ansel looked. And understood, in the slow cold way one understands things at dusk, what they were walking on.

Footprints. Not in the dust — *in* the road itself. Faint shapes pressed into the earth, glowing softly where the light touched them wrong: boot-heels, bare toes, hooves, the long drag-marks of someone who had once been carried. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Layer over layer, going back further than footprints had any right to go, each one holding its little ember of memory the way a wick holds fire.

The road remembered every traveler who had ever walked it.

Which meant, Ansel realized with a lurch, that the valley's great silence — the thing that had made it sanctuary, the reason magic could not follow them in — had never covered the road at all. The road was a seam. A scar. The one place the world outside still touched, still kept its accounts.

Every person who had ever left the valley had left a footprint here. Every person who had ever arrived.

Including, presumably, a seven-year-old child being carried north by someone whose footsteps Ansel suddenly very much wanted to find.

"Not now," Ansel told themselves firmly, because Wren was watching, and because if they started hunting their own past in a glowing road while hunters closed in behind them they would never stop. "Later. Walk."

They walked. And the road hummed under them, and the hum changed.

It was subtle at first — a warmth in the note, a brightness — and then Ansel worked out what it was doing and had to fight down something dangerously close to laughter. The road was greeting them. Each new footprint they left sank into the earth and glowed, and the hum gathered it in, and somewhere far ahead and far behind, the road was telling itself about them. *Two travelers. One tall, one small. Frightened. Going north.*

"It knows we're here," Ansel muttered. "Wonderful. The only honest witness in the valley, and it's a road."

Wren squeezed their fingers. Then, with the grave deliberation the child brought to everything, Wren pointed at the newest footprints, then at Ansel, then made a shape in the air — small hands pressed together, then opened wide.

"Yes, fine, I'm frightened, thank you for the announcement."

Wren shook their head. Pointed at Ansel again. Made the shape again — but this time the small hands opened *outward*, generously, like a door swinging wide.

Ansel stared at the child.

"That's not fright," they said slowly. "You think I'm — open? Right now? My entire life is closed shutters and locked ledgers, child. I have spent thirty years making sure nothing gets in or out." They gestured at the road, at the hills, at the whole treacherous evening. "This is the least open I have ever been."

Wren smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, entirely unconvinced, and it did something unfair to Ansel's chest.

They walked on. Behind them, far down the valley, a light kindled — small, moving, deliberate. Then a second.

"Hunters," Ansel said. All the warmth went out of their voice, which was how Ansel knew they were more afraid than they'd admitted even to the road. "Corvane travels with six, usually. Quiet ones. The kind who can walk behind you for a day and you never know." They glanced back once, briefly, the way you check a wound. "We have maybe until moonrise."

Wren pointed north. Then made a fist, and struck it into their palm, once, twice — the rhythm of running.

"I know," Ansel said. "Run. Yes. Running is the plan. The plan has always been running; I just prefer to do it after a meal and a good night's sleep and several years of preparation."

They ran.

* * *

Wren knew roads. Roads were the oldest things, older than names, older than the First Kingdoms, older than the word that lived folded up beneath Wren's tongue like a moth in winter. Roads remembered because roads could not help it. Everything that passed over them passed *into* them, and Wren had walked roads in the old country — before, before, before — that sang whole histories, coronations and funerals and one very long stretch that did nothing but grieve.

This road was young compared to those. This road had mostly forgotten people, which meant its memories were bright and shallow and easy to hold, like pebbles in a stream. Wren could feel them through their boots: a peddler's delight at finding a coin he'd lost. A woman carrying a baby north, her fear and her stubbornness braided together so tightly Wren couldn't tell which was holding which. A man who had turned back three times at this exact spot, his footprints overlapping like a stutter.

And now: two new prints, sinking in warm. The tall one and the small one.

Wren liked the tall one's footprints best. They were strange prints — uneven, the left heavier than the right, the stride shortening whenever the small one's hand slipped from theirs and lengthening the moment it returned. The road would keep those prints a long time, Wren thought. Roads kept the prints of people who were paying attention.

Behind them, the hunters' prints were already coming, and those were bad prints. Cold ones. Prints that took more than they gave, that lay on the road like frost. There were four of them, not six, and they did not hurry, which was worse than hurrying. Hurrying meant you might lose someone. Walking meant you already knew where they were going.

Wren looked up at the tall one — Ansel, Ansel, the stitched-together person, the one with a hole inside them shaped like a door — and saw the fear sitting on them like a coat they'd forgotten to take off.

Fear was useful. Fear was a kind of remembering. But the tall one was carrying too much of it, and it was slowing their feet, and the frost-prints were gaining.

So Wren did a small thing. A forbidden-small thing, a crumb of the old speech, the kind of thing that cost almost nothing — a grain of sand from a beach of years.

Wren crouched, laid one palm flat on the road, and breathed a single syllable into it.

*Sel.*

*Remember kindly.*

The road shivered with pleasure. All along its length, the old footprints flared — the peddler's coin-finding joy, the mother's stubborn love, the turn-back man's three hesitations — and the hum deepened and spread, rolling north ahead of them like a tide, wrapping the two of them in a sound that was, unmistakably, though it had no words, *welcome*.

Ansel stumbled, caught themselves, and stared down at the glowing road with an expression Wren filed away carefully: terror and wonder fighting, and wonder winning by a nose.

"What did you *do*?"

Wren shrugged, the universal gesture of the innocent.

"The road sounds — it sounds *happy*. Roads don't get happy. Stones don't get anything. That's the entire point of this valley, nothing here feels anything, that's why it's *safe*—" Ansel stopped. Pressed both hands over their face. Took a breath. When the hands came down, the eyes above them were wet and furious and alive. "You're spending something. Doing that. I can see it — you're smaller when you finish than when you start. Don't lie to me, I'm a professional liar, I can tell."

Wren considered lying anyway, out of principle. Then pointed north, where the hum was brightest, and made the running shape again.

"Fine. Fine! But we're discussing costs later, and I'm keeping a ledger, and my ledgers are *ruthless*, ask anyone—"

Ask anyone. Ansel's voice caught on it, just slightly, because there was no one to ask. There had never been anyone to ask. Wren reached up and took the tall one's hand first this time, instead of waiting to be taken, and felt the fingers close around theirs with the desperate care of someone holding onto a ledge.

They ran north, and the road ran with them, singing.

* * *

By full dark the lights behind them had become one light, moving fast, and Ansel had stopped pretending it wasn't there.

"One hunter," they panted. "He's split off. That's either very good — the others lost us — or very bad, and given that everything else tonight has been very bad, I'm betting on bad."

The road climbed now, out of the valley's bowl, and with every step the air changed. Thinner. Colder. And underneath the cold, something else — a texture, a pressure, like standing near a window before a storm breaks. Ansel had no word for it. They had never needed a word for it. Nothing in the valley required words like *magic* or *power* or *the hair rising on your arms because the world is awake out here and it has noticed you.*

Wren had stopped running. The child stood in the middle of the road, head tipped back, arms slightly out, drinking the night air like someone who had been underwater for years.

"Wren. *Wren.* We don't have time for — whatever communion this is—"

The hunter's light crested the hill behind them.

Ansel spun. It was one figure, tall, unhurried, lantern held high — and even at this distance Ansel knew the silhouette, because Ansel had taught it half of what it knew. Vetch. Corvane's steadiest hunter. A man who had eaten at Ansel's table perhaps forty times and never once told them anything true.

"Vetch!" Ansel called, because there was no point pretending. "Evening!"

Vetch raised the lantern higher. His voice came down the road, mild as milk. "Thackeray. You've got something of Corvane's."

"I've got a *child*, Vetch, not a parcel—"

"You know what I mean." Vetch began to walk. Not run. Walk. "Ledger says the debt's come due. Ledger always comes due. You taught me that."

And Ansel, heart hammering, opened their mouth to say something clever and found nothing there — and in that gap of nothing, the road spoke.

Not in words. In footprints. Because Vetch was walking on it, and the road remembered him, and it remembered him *generously*, because of the syllable Wren had fed it hours ago — remember kindly — and so it showed Ansel, rising soft and gold from the earth around Vetch's boots, the shape of every step Vetch had ever taken on this road.

Including steps that came *south*. Years of them. Including, on one long-ago night, a pair of small bare footprints walking south beside Vetch's large ones, and a bundle in his arms, and a hum in the road that sounded, even now, even faded, like grief.

Vetch stopped walking. He was looking down at the glow too. And Ansel saw — unmistakably, in the lantern light — the big man's face crack open like ice in spring.

"Go," Vetch said quietly. "North, past the shoulder of the hill, there's a cart track the road doesn't sing about. Go now, and I'll tell Corvane I lost you at the ford."

Ansel did not move. "Vetch. Those footprints. Whose—"

"*Go.*" It came out rough. "Before I remember whose side I'm paid to be on."

Ansel grabbed Wren's hand and ran, up past the sleeping shoulder of the hill, lungs burning, the road's song fading behind them — and just before the track swallowed them, Wren looked back once and saw Vetch kneeling in the middle of the road, both palms flat on the earth, reading the small bare footprints over and over, like a man learning to pray.

The cart track was dark and silent and smelled of pine. Somewhere ahead, beyond the hills, the world without the valley waited — the world where Wren's words could catch fire.

Ansel could not see it yet. But they could feel it, faint and enormous, like warmth through a wall.

"Hold on to me," they told the child. "Whatever happens next — hold on, and don't let me talk myself into going back."

Wren held on. And far behind them, at the mouth of the valley, the road went on humming their names — the names it knew, and the one it didn't — into the dark.
