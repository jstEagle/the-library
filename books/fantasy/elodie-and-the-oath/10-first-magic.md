## 10. First Magic

The world outside the valley was loud.

Wren had no word for *loud* in the old tongue — the First Kingdoms had not needed one, because the First Kingdoms had listened the way rivers listen, all at once and without surprise. But here, past the border stones where the humming stopped and the air went thin and bright, everything shouted. Grass shouted green. Wind shouted through the pines in a hundred separate voices. And beneath it all, so faint Wren had to press their palm flat against the earth to be sure, the ground itself whispered.

*Remembered,* the ground said. *Walked upon. Kept.*

The valley behind them had been a wound — a place stitched shut, where nothing could speak and nothing could hold. Wren had lived inside that silence for as long as they could remember, which was not long, because remembering was the first thing the silence ate. But out here, on the north road under a sky like spilled milk, the world remembered everything. Every footstep Ansel took sank a little way into the road and stayed there, glowing faintly to Wren's inner sight, a trail of small warm lamps leading away from home.

Ansel walked ahead, shoulders hunched against a cold they clearly felt and Wren only knew about from watching them shiver. The thief kept glancing back — quick, guilty little looks, the kind people gave to things they were afraid of wanting.

Wren understood that look. Wren had been wearing it since the storm night, since the cottage, since the moment the tall thief with the careful hands had opened their door and not run screaming.

*You are stitched into the wound,* Wren thought at them, the way Wren thought most things — wordlessly, in colors and pressure and the taste of old stone. *But you are also the stitching.*

They walked until the sun leaned west, and then Ansel stopped at a place where two roads crossed and an abandoned shrine slumped against an oak like a drunk uncle at a wedding. Its little roof had fallen in. Its statue — some forgotten hearth-god, round-bellied and smiling — wore a hat of moss.

"Shrine," Ansel announced, in the flat tone of someone narrating for a person who could not answer. "Which means travelers used to stop here. Which means—" they patted their coat with the smugness of a cat presenting a dead bird, "—there may be pickings."

Wren tilted their head. *Pickings.* They turned the word over. It sounded like something small and stolen and slightly rotten.

Ansel crouched by the shrine's crumbling base and ran their fingers along the mortar, eyes half-closed, doing whatever quiet thieving thing they did. Wren watched their hands. They were good hands — quick, deft, always moving, always *taking*. But when Wren looked at them with the other sight, the sight that saw the shape of things beneath their shapes, those hands looked wrong. Not evil-wrong. *Hungry*-wrong. Hands that had been empty so long they'd forgotten they were hands and started believing they were hooks.

Something in Wren's chest ached, low and specific, the ache that had no name in any language but felt, in the old tongue's grammar, like *I want to give you something back.*

So Wren sat down in the shrine's doorway, folded their legs, and cupped their hands together in their lap.

And spoke.

Not aloud — never aloud; the words of the First Kingdoms did not travel through air but through *agreement*, through the patient consent of things to be more than they were. Wren shaped the syllables inside, three small sounds Wren had carried since before the silence, since before the palace, since before — well. Since before the part of themselves they'd spent to bend stone in the undercrofts. Each word cost. Wren had learned that early, the way children learn stoves. But this word was small. A pebble-word. It would cost only a little: the memory of a particular smell, maybe. The color of a room.

*I am willing to be less,* the word meant, in its oldest sense, *so that something else may be more.*

Light bloomed in Wren's cupped hands.

It came up softly, the way bread rises — pale gold, then warmer, then a color that has no name outside the old tongue but which the First Kingdoms had called *hearth-kin*, the light that fire makes when it is happy rather than hungry. It pooled in Wren's palms and overflowed gently down their wrists like warm honey, and the mossy shrine straightened by a hair's breadth, and the forgotten hearth-god's smile seemed, for a moment, genuinely pleased.

Wren looked up.

Ansel had gone very still.

Thieves, Wren had noticed, moved constantly — even asleep, even standing still, some part of them was always calculating exits. But now every calculating part of Ansel Thackeray had simply stopped, and what remained was a person kneeling in dead leaves with dirt on their fingers and their mouth slightly open, staring at a child's glowing hands the way starving people stare through bakery windows.

"Oh," Ansel said.

Just that. One syllable, cracked down the middle.

Then, recovering, in the voice of someone who had definitely not been moved: "That's — hm. That's new."

*It is not new,* Wren thought, amused. *It is the oldest thing there is. You are the new thing. You are so new the world hasn't decided what to do with you yet.*

Ansel crawled closer — actually crawled, then caught themselves and converted it into a crouch, badly, like a heron pretending it had meant to do that. "Does it hurt? The light. Does it cost you?" Their eyes flicked over Wren's face with a professional's attention, cataloguing signs of payment. "Because I've seen magic take tolls before. Well. I've heard about it. Secondhand. In the valley we don't—"

They stopped. Of course they stopped. In the valley, nothing came. That was the whole point of the valley. That was the cage dressed up as sanctuary.

*Show me your hands,* Wren thought, and because thoughts are not commands, Wren instead did the next best thing: reached out, took one of Ansel's hook-hands, and turned it palm-up.

Ansel let them. That was the remarkable thing. A thief letting a child take their hand — Wren felt the flinch travel up the arm like a bird startled off a branch, felt the muscles argue, felt them lose.

Then Wren tipped their own cupped hands, and poured.

A single bead of the hearth-light rolled off Wren's palm and into Ansel's.

For one heartbeat, it sat there. Warm. Small. A coin of morning.

And then it went out.

Not faded — *out*, snuffed, the way a candle dies in a closed room. The gold touched Ansel's skin and simply ceased, as though it had fallen into a hole too deep to echo.

Wren stared. Ansel stared at their own empty palm.

"Well," Ansel said lightly, and their voice did the thing voices do when they're carrying something much heavier than they look. "Valley rules, I suppose. Nothing sticks." They laughed — a short, practiced sound, worn smooth from use. "Story of my life. Things come near me and just... don't take."

But Wren had felt it happen. Had felt the light not merely go out but be *refused* — or worse, not refused. *Unreceived.* Like pressing a gift into the hands of someone whose hands had been taken away.

Wren looked closer, with the other sight, and what they saw made the ache in their chest crack wider.

Ansel Thackeray was full of holes.

Not metaphor holes. Real ones — soft-edged absences scattered through them like moth-work in a winter coat, places where a self should have been and wasn't. Wren had sensed the stitching before, back in the valley, had known Ansel was sewn into the wound of that place. But here, outside, in the loud remembering world, the holes showed plainly. Something had been *taken* from this person. Many somethings. Taken so long ago and so cleanly that the taking had healed over smooth, and Ansel walked around inside the scar tissue calling it a life.

*You weren't born hollow,* Wren thought, grief arriving in their throat like a swallowed stone. *You were emptied. Someone did this to you, and they were gentle about it, and gentleness is not the same as kindness.*

And underneath the holes — deep, deep down, below where the light had died — Wren felt something else. A seed-shape. A word-shape. Something folded very small and hidden very well, the way mothers hide things in children's coats.

Wren almost reached for it.

Almost.

But touching another person's buried word without asking was, in the old tongue's law, the gravest rudeness there was — graver than theft, because theft at least admitted it wanted something. So Wren closed their eyes, and let it lie.

When they opened them, Ansel was building a fire.

Well. Attempting. The thief had produced flint and steel from some impossible pocket and was striking sparks at a nest of twigs with the grim determination of someone who had done this ten thousand times and hated it afresh each evening. Sparks leapt. Twigs declined. Sparks again. More declining.

"You know," Ansel muttered, "where I grew up, this would've been considered a basic survival skill. Wherever that was." A beat. The joke landed crooked, the way jokes landed when they had a hole underneath them. "Funny how I keep saying 'where I grew up' like it's a place I remember."

Wren watched the failing fire, then looked at their own hands.

The word for flame was still there. It was a friendly word, as fire-words went — *the red that agrees to sit still*. It would cost more than the pebble-word. Maybe the memory of a song. Maybe the face of someone Wren had loved before the palace, if anyone's face remained unspent.

Wren weighed it, the way Wren weighed everything now: what is it worth, what will it leave behind, who will I still be after?

Then they looked at Ansel — hunched, spark-struck, joking at their own emptiness — and decided.

Wren leaned forward and breathed the word onto the twigs.

No sound. Just breath, and agreement. The red-that-sits-still woke in the twig-nest like a cat stretching, curled once, twice, and settled into a proper fire with a satisfied crackle. Warmth rolled outward. The mossy god smiled down on them both.

Ansel jerked backward, flint clattering. "Warn a person!" Then, quieter, staring at the flames: "That's real fire. Actual fire. From your *mouth*." They rubbed their arms, and Wren saw the truth under the joke: the warmth reached Ansel's skin, but only the skin. It warmed the outside of them and slid off, water off wax. Even heat couldn't find purchase in the holes.

Ansel must have felt it too, because their expression did something complicated — gratitude and hunger and a grief so old it had fossilized into manners.

"It's lovely," Ansel said. "The fire. Thank you." And then, because the silence was apparently unbearable: "When I was small — assuming I was ever small, which the evidence suggests — I think I must have lived somewhere with fires like that. Big ones. Hearth-fires. I have this..." They gestured vaguely at their own chest. "Shaped absence. Right here. Hearth-shaped, if that makes sense. Like a room I can't remember with a fire in it and someone —"

They stopped. Hard. The way people stop when they've nearly stepped off a cliff they didn't know was there.

"Anyway," Ansel said briskly, and busied themselves with a bundle of dried meat that had certainly not been paid for honestly.

Wren hugged their knees and watched the fire and let the silence be kind.

*Hearth-shaped,* Wren thought. *Oh, you poor stitched thing. You carry the shape of it and not the thing itself. Someone burned your childhood for fuel and left you the fireplace.*

The stars came out — thick, shameless, the sort of stars the valley's flat grey sky had never permitted. Wren lay back on the cold ground and let them pour in. Above the oak, the constellations arranged themselves in patterns the First Kingdoms had named, and Wren read them idly, the way one reads familiar handwriting: *the Loom, the Drowned King, the Seven Sisters Who Were Actually Nine Until Two of Them Left.*

Ansel lay down too, at a careful distance, on the far side of the fire. For a while neither of them existed out loud.

Then Ansel spoke into the dark, in the loose unguarded voice people only use when they believe no one is listening — forgetting, perhaps, that a child who does not speak has become very good at listening.

"I keep thinking about what it would be like," Ansel said. "To touch it. The magic. Just once. Just to know what everyone else gets." A log settled; sparks climbed. "Corvane always said it was poison. Said the valley keeps it out for our protection, like a wall keeps out wolves." A dry laugh. "He says a lot of things. He's the only person who's been in my whole life, so I suppose he'd know. Except lately I keep noticing that everything I know about myself comes from him. Everything. My trade, my history, my name even fitting my mouth right. It's like being handed a portrait of yourself and being told it's a mirror."

The fire popped. Somewhere an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"And then you show up," Ansel went on, softer now, "humming stones and making light, and the first thing I feel isn't wonder. It's *envy*. Ugly, mean little envy, at a child, for having a self that works." The words came faster, spilling where they shouldn't. "Do you know what that's like? Watching light die when it touches you? Knowing that if there's any justice in the world, it's because you deserve the hole?"

Wren sat up.

*That,* Wren thought, fierce as a struck bell, *is the stupidest thing the holes have ever made you say.*

Wren got up, crossed the fire-circle — stepping carefully over the flames, because even miracle-workers respect hot coals — and sat down beside Ansel in the dark, close enough that their shoulders touched.

Ansel went rigid. "You should sleep," they said, in the strangled voice of a person being comforted against their will.

Instead, Wren took Ansel's hand again — the hook-hand, the hungry hand — and pressed it flat against the ground. Against the road. Against the old remembered earth.

*Listen,* Wren told it, without words, and told the earth the same. *This one is mine to mind. Be gentle.*

And the earth, which remembers everything, did what old things do when asked nicely by a child of the First Kingdoms: it answered. Faintly, through Ansel's palm — too faint for anyone but Wren to see — the ground sent up its warmth. Not magic. Older than magic. Just the plain memory of every traveler who had ever slept safe on this road, every fire built, every child carried, every ordinary night survived. It rose through the soil and into Ansel's hand and, for once, for one small mercy, the holes let it stay.

Ansel's breath caught.

"That's —" they whispered. "What is that? It feels like —" Their voice failed and restarted. "It feels like being *kept*."

Wren nodded, though Ansel couldn't see it in the dark.

*Yes,* Wren thought. *That. That is what you have been giving everyone else your whole life, you ridiculous thief, selling people back their own warmth coin by coin. The world owes you a fortune in exactly this.*

They sat like that a long time, shoulder to shoulder, while the fire burned down to its honest orange and the stars wheeled their slow business overhead. Eventually Ansel's breathing lengthened into sleep — real sleep, unguarded, the calculating parts finally quiet — and Wren alone kept watch, as Wren always would now. Keeper of the watch. Keeper, someday, of everything else too.

Near dawn, Wren felt it before hearing it: a wrongness on the south wind. A taste like ledger-paper and iron. Like a name being written in a book that did not belong to the person named.

Far behind them, back along the road that remembered footsteps, something was following. Several somethings. Moving fast, and paying their way in other people's memories as they came.

Wren touched Ansel's shoulder, and the day began.

* * *

They broke camp quickly and walked north while the mist still owned the fields, and by midmorning the land changed underfoot — marsh country, black water threaded with silver channels, and cutting across it all a river wide and slow and the color of strong tea. At its bank waited a flat-bottomed ferry and a ferryman who looked, Wren thought, like a heron that had given up and put on clothes.

"Toll," the ferryman said, before anyone had said anything at all. His voice was mud-flat mild. "Crossing's free. Toll's not."

Ansel's hand drifted toward their coin purse out of pure habit, and the ferryman smiled with the patience of a man who had corrected this mistake ten thousand times.

"Not coin," he said. "Memory. Standard rate: one recollection per passenger, payable at the rail. Something true. Something yours." His pale eyes slid to Wren and lingered, and something in his mildness sharpened. "Or something *held in trust*," he added delicately. "I'm not particular whose grief it originally was. Trade's trade."

Wren felt Ansel go still in that new way — the way of a thief recognizing a bigger thief.

"One memory each," the ferryman repeated, and held out his hand, palm up, open as a door.

And Wren, watching Ansel's face as the thief understood what the crossing would cost and began — visibly, terribly — to calculate what they could afford to lose, felt the old cold slide back into the world.

Because Ansel was smiling. The practiced smile. The one worn smooth from use.

And Wren already knew which memory Ansel would pay with.
