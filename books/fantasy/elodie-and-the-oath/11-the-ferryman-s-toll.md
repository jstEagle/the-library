## 11. The Ferryman's Toll

The river found them two days north of the valley, the way rivers find everything eventually—patiently, and with an air of having expected you all along.

Ansel heard it before they saw it: a low, conversational murmur beneath the birdsong, as though the water were discussing them with the reeds. They crested a bramble-choked rise with Wren's small hand tucked in theirs, and there it was—the Sorrow, broad and slate-grey and moving far too fast for so flat a country, running beside the road like a dog that had decided to escort them whether they liked it or not.

"Well," Ansel said. "That's inconvenient."

Wren tilted their head at the water and made a sound—not a word in any tongue Ansel knew, not even the old one, just a soft *hnn* of opinion. It was remarkable, Ansel had learned over the past weeks, how much opinion a child could pack into a single syllable.

"I know," Ansel said. "I don't like it either."

They had been walking since before dawn, following the humming road north. The road still remembered footsteps—it shivered faintly under their boots with each stride, a sensation Ansel had stopped finding unsettling around day three and now merely found rude—and the road, being old and stubborn, ran straight toward the capital. The river, apparently, had other ideas about where the road ought to go, because ahead of them the ground simply gave up. The banks dropped away into brown water, and the road continued on the far side as if nothing had happened, smug and intact and entirely unreachable.

Between the near bank and the far ambition of the road sat a small wooden dock, and tied to the dock sat a boat, and standing in the boat stood a man.

He was the sort of man who looked as though he had been assembled from spare parts of other men—a tall man's shoulders on a short man's frame, a young man's hands folded over an old man's belly. His face was pleasant in the way that a chair is pleasant: functional, unremarkable, designed to be sat upon by strangers. He wore a hat that might once have been fine and was now merely damp.

Ansel's stomach performed a small, professional calculation. Ferryman. Border-adjacent. A man who dealt in passage the way Ansel dealt in memory, which meant he would have prices, and prices meant negotiation, and negotiation meant Ansel could do what they did best: lie charmingly.

"Ahoy," Ansel called, because ferry-men were traditionally addressed that way and it never hurt to honor tradition.

"Ahoy yourself," the ferryman said. His voice was mild and slightly bored, like a man reading a list aloud. "Crossing?"

"That was the general idea."

"Everyone's general idea." He gestured at the boat with one chin. "I take people across. I don't take coin."

"Of course you don't," Ansel sighed. "Nobody sensible takes coin anymore. What do you take? Chickens? Favors? Firstborn children? I should warn you, I've only got the one child and they're spoken for."

Wren squeezed Ansel's hand. *Hnn,* said Wren, with the unmistakable inflection of *don't volunteer me.*

"Not chickens," the ferryman said. "Not favors." He looked at Ansel then, properly, and his eyes were the grey-brown of river water, and they did not blink quite often enough. "Memories. One crossing, one memory. Small ones preferred. I'm not greedy."

* * *

Ansel had stolen memories for eleven years. They had lifted grief from merchants and sold it back to them at the border; they had skimmed first kisses off young lovers and returned them, slightly worn, for a fee; they had once taken a soldier's memory of a battle he'd been trying to forget anyway and charged him double for the mercy. Ansel knew memories the way a fishmonger knows fish—which ones kept, which ones spoiled, which ones fought when you grabbed them.

But taking was different from giving. Taking, you chose what to lift, and you kept your own hands clean of your own head. Giving meant opening the door from the inside.

"Small ones," Ansel repeated. "Define small."

The ferryman shrugged his borrowed shoulders. "A meal you enjoyed. A face you can't quite place. The smell of rain on a particular street." He smiled, and the smile fit him badly, like the hat. "Most travelers are relieved, honestly. Everyone's carrying too much. I'm a service."

"You're a toll," Ansel said.

"I'm both. Most necessary things are."

Wren tugged Ansel's sleeve and pointed at the water. Ansel followed the pointing finger and immediately wished they hadn't, because the river, up close, was full of things. Not fish. Shapes. Pale suggestions drifting beneath the surface, round and slow-moving, like thoughts that had sunk. As Ansel watched, one of them rose near the pilings—a glimmering, translucent something, roughly the size and shape of a summer afternoon—and dissolved against the current with a sound like a sigh heard through a wall.

"What happens to them?" Ansel asked quietly. "The ones you take."

The ferryman considered this. "They go in the river," he said, as though that answered anything.

"And the river does what with them?"

"The river keeps them." He said it without malice, without ceremony, the way a farmer says *the field keeps the seed.* "It's what the river's for. Everything that flows north ends up somewhere. Even remembering."

Ansel looked at Wren. Wren looked back with those enormous dark eyes that had seen the valley as a wound in the world, that perceived things stitched and torn that ordinary eyes couldn't find. The child's expression was unreadable—but their hand had gone tight around Ansel's fingers, and their breathing had gone careful, the way it did when the stones hummed.

*We could walk around,* Ansel thought. *Rivers end. We could follow it south until it thins into a stream and step over.*

But the renewal was coming. Seven years, Corvane had said, and the seven years were nearly spent, and somewhere in Aldermont children were already being counted and weighed and measured for tithe. Every hour of detour was an hour closer to the square filling up with small frightened bodies. And behind them—Ansel glanced back down the road, empty and shimmering—Corvane's hunters were not the sort of men who lost a trail and shrugged about it.

"One memory," Ansel said. "And the child crosses free."

"The child crosses free regardless," the ferryman said. "Children don't carry enough yet. Nothing worth the dipping." He said it kindly, which somehow made it worse. "Just you."

"Just me," Ansel agreed, and hated how easily the words came, how practiced the arithmetic of sacrifice had become. *One memory for one crossing. Cheap. You've paid worse.* But the voice saying it was Corvane's voice, the ledger-keeper's voice, the voice that had taught them that everything has a price and the trick is only ever making someone else pay it.

Ansel crouched to Wren's level. "Stay on the dock. Watch the shapes in the water, but don't touch them, and whatever happens—" they tapped the child's sternum gently, "—you hold on to everything. You're better at holding than I am lately."

Wren stared at them. Then, slowly, deliberately, the child raised one small hand and pressed it flat against Ansel's chest, right over the heart, and held it there for three long breaths. Ansel felt something pass between them—not magic, the valley's absence still clung to them both even out here, or maybe it was simply too subtle for a thief's senses—but something. A weight. A promise shaped like a weight.

Then Wren stepped back, folded their arms, and gave the ferryman a look of such profound childish suspicion that the man actually laughed.

"All right," Ansel said, before their courage could file a formal complaint. "How does this work?"

* * *

It worked like this: you sat in the boat, in the middle of the river, where the water was deepest and most attentive. The ferryman poled you out without speaking. The reeds stopped murmuring. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Now," the ferryman said, resting the pole across the gunwales. "Think of something small. Something you won't miss. Don't tell me what it is—I'll know it when it comes loose. That's how you'll know it's the right size."

"That's ominous."

"That's accurate. There's a difference."

Ansel closed their eyes. This was the part they knew—inverted, mirrored, but known. When they took a memory from a traveler, they reached in with careful fingers and unhooked it, gentle as lifting a ring from a sleeping hand. Giving meant reaching into themselves, and Ansel discovered immediately that they were a stranger in there. Eleven years of hoarding other people's pasts had left their own interior cluttered, dim, hard to navigate—rooms stuffed with furniture that belonged to other houses.

*Something small. Something you won't miss.*

They went looking, and what they found frightened them more than the river: almost nothing was small. Their own memories had gone strange and thin, crowded out by inventory. The taste of bread. The color of their mother's—no. Not that. Never that; that room was locked and they'd thrown away the key years ago, or Corvane had, and either way.

They drifted past a stranger's joy.

It sat near the front of the hoard, recent, barely catalogued: a wedding, lifted from a young woman crossing south three months ago. She'd sold it cheap, laughing, saying she'd buy it back on her return trip, saying she wanted to feel it fresh twice. Ansel had never gotten around to returning it. It was a lovely thing, that memory—sunlight through chapel glass, a groom's hands shaking, the specific golden roar of a crowd of people who all loved the same two people. It wasn't Ansel's. It had never been Ansel's. It was the easiest thing in the entire hoard to give, precisely because none of it was theirs to lose.

*Perfect,* said the practical voice. *Give him that. It costs you nothing.*

Ansel hesitated for exactly one heartbeat, and in that heartbeat they heard the woman's laugh again, bright and trusting: *I'll be back for it in the spring.*

She'd be coming north soon, looking for a thief who was no longer there.

"It doesn't matter whose it is," Ansel whispered. "It matters that it's small."

The ferryman said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing. It was probably the main qualification for the job.

Ansel unhooked the wedding.

It came loose the way a tooth comes loose—with resistance, then a sickening ease, then a strange raw socket where it used to live. They felt it leave them, felt it pass through their chest like a swallow of cold water going the wrong direction, and then it was in the air between them, visible for one impossible moment: a little golden shimmer, sunlight through glass, the roar of love.

The ferryman caught it—actually caught it, plucked it from the air like an apple—and examined it with the detached appreciation of a craftsman.

"Oh, that's a nice one," he said. "Wedding? Good vintage." He turned it over in his fingers. "You didn't flinch much. Most people flinch."

"It wasn't mine."

"Ah." Something moved behind the river-water eyes—not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. "That's the trick of it, isn't it. The ones that aren't yours go easiest. And then one day you look down and everything you're carrying belongs to somebody else, and you can't remember why you started carrying."

"Are you philosophizing at me? I'm paying you."

"You are." He dropped the memory over the side. It hit the water without a splash—it sank like light sinks, softly, going gold and then green and then gone—and somewhere below, Ansel knew, it joined the pale drifting shapes, a stranger's happiest day sinking slowly north toward wherever kept things.

The boat lurched. The pole bit water. They were moving.

That was when the hollowness arrived.

Ansel had expected emptiness—they'd felt the edges of it every time they took from someone else, the little vacuum a stolen memory leaves behind. But this was different. This was inside-out. Because the wedding hadn't just been cargo; for three months it had been sitting in Ansel's chest doing quiet work, warming them from the inside the way a stone warmed by a fire warms the bed. Sunlight through glass. Shaking hands. Being surrounded by people who loved you. Ansel had never had those things, not that they could recall, and some starving cellar-part of them had been living in that memory at night, sneaking in like a cat, curling up in someone else's happiness because it was the closest thing to happiness available.

Now it was gone, and the space it left was shaped like everything Ansel had never had.

"Oh," Ansel said quietly. It came out very small.

"Told you everyone's carrying too much," the ferryman said, poling steadily. He didn't look at them. "Breathe. It passes. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"There's usually a residue. A dent. You'll notice it in odd moments—someone mentions weddings and you'll feel a draft." He shrugged. "Cheaper than drowning."

On the dock, growing smaller behind them, Wren stood with their fists clenched at their sides, watching the golden afterimage sink. As the boat drew away, Ansel saw the child raise one hand—not waving. Pressing it, again, flat against their own chest. *I'm holding on,* the gesture said. *I've got yours and mine both.*

Ansel pressed their own hand to their breastbone and found the hollow and tried, absurdly, to memorize its shape. If they were going to keep paying like this—if the road to Aldermont was going to be paved with rooms emptied out of their own chest—they wanted at least to know what they'd lost. That was the whole difference between a thief and a monster, as far as Ansel had ever been able to determine: the thief counts.

The far bank arrived sooner than expected. Rivers are like that; the worst parts of any journey always move quickly, so you can't study them properly. The hull scraped gravel. The ferryman steadied Ansel's elbow as they stepped out—an oddly gentle gesture from a man made of spare parts—and then stood in his boat, pole ready, regarding them with those insufficiently blinking eyes.

"For what it's worth," he said, "you gave up easier than most. And you checked on the child first. In my experience, the ones who check on the children first are the ones the river likes." He paused. "The river remembers who it likes. Comes in handy, later."

"Is that a threat or a blessing?"

"Yes," said the ferryman, and poled away.

* * *

The road on the north bank was the same road—same hum, same shiver of remembered footsteps—but Ansel felt different walking it. Lighter, the way a purse feels lighter, the way a body feels lighter when it's lost blood rather than weight. Wren walked close, shoulder brushing Ansel's hip, and every hundred paces or so the child would glance up and do a small silent inventory of Ansel's face, checking for cracks.

"I'm fine," Ansel said, the fourth time.

*Hnn,* said Wren, in the tone of a person who had audited the claim and found it wanting.

They walked. The afternoon went long and amber. Around a bend where the road climbed away from the water, Ansel stopped, because ahead of them the land opened up, and there—far off, hazed in distance, crowned with towers that caught the low sun like needles catching thread—was a city.

Aldermont. The crowned city. Somewhere in it, a king paraded in glass-eyed splendor while his shadow moved a half-second late. Somewhere in it, a crown-thing fed. Somewhere in it, lists were being drawn up of children, and Wren's name—or whatever name the palace used for the tithe—was surely near the top.

Ansel felt the hollow in their chest shift in the wind coming off the river, a cold draft blowing through a room where a wedding used to be.

"We'll get it back someday," Ansel said—to Wren, to themselves, to nobody. "The memory. We'll come back with a bucket or something. Drain the river. Very reasonable plan."

Wren looked up at them, and then at the city, and then did something they had never done before: spoke. Not the old language, not the humming syllables that bent stone—one plain, cracked, rusted word of common speech, dragged up from wherever the child kept the few ordinary words they'd salvaged:

"Hungry."

Ansel laughed—a real laugh, surprising them both, ringing off the humming road—and dug the last heel of bread from their pack.

"Well," they said, tearing it in half and handing over the bigger piece, because some habits of care survived even a hollowed chest. "If we're going to storm a palace, we'd better do it fed."

The road hummed beneath their feet, carrying their footsteps north, remembering them forward.
