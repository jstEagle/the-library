## 12. The Crowned City

The road to Aldermont was the first road I had ever walked that went somewhere on purpose.

In the valley, roads were more suggestion than promise. They wandered off toward the passes and then thought better of it, doubling back like sheep. But this one — the King's Road, the ferryman had called it, with the smugness of a man who had just taken a stranger's joy as fare — ran straight and sure as a stitch through cloth, and it pulled us north with the gentle insistence of a hook through a fish.

Wren walked beside me with their hood up and their hands cupped around nothing, which was how they carried light now when they wanted to feel rich. There was no actual light in there. Not anymore. The valley's silence had closed over them again the moment we'd crossed back through its border stones, and outside it, Wren had learned quickly — too quickly, I thought sometimes — that every wonder cost them something. So they practiced in thimbles: a spark no bigger than a moth, a warmth like a stone kept by a fire. Small coins. I recognized the habit. It was mine.

"You're staring at your hands," I said. "People will think you're a fortune-teller."

Wren looked up. Their mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a small, crooked pulling at one corner, like a door opening onto a room you weren't sure you were invited into. Then they held out their empty cupped hands toward me, solemn as a priest offering relics.

"I'm not paying for a fortune," I said. "I've seen my future. It's mostly walking."

They laughed without sound — shoulders shaking, eyes bright — and for a while that was enough. That was most of what we had, out there on the road: a mute child's silent laughter, my own running commentary, and the strange new fact of grass that bent when the wind touched it *because the wind told it to*, not because some old grief in the ground had ordered it to stay bent forever.

I want to be honest about what leaving the valley felt like, because everyone assumes it felt like freedom. It didn't. It felt like taking off a coat you'd worn so long you'd forgotten it was heavy — and then discovering that the coat had been holding you together. Every mile north, the world got louder. Not with sound. With *attention*. Trees noticed us. Rivers paused mid-sentence to consider us. Once, a crow landed on my shoulder and regarded me with an expression of such frank appraisal that I said, "I'm not buying," and it flew off offended.

Magic, it turned out, was not a force. It was a neighborhood. And we were strangers walking through it with mud on our boots.

* * *

We saw Aldermont a full day before we reached it, which is the sort of thing cities do when they're proud of themselves.

It sat on its hill above the river plain like a fat cat on a warm hearth — white walls, slate roofs going blue in the distance, and above it all the palace, the Crownspire, its towers catching the afternoon sun and throwing it back in long golden banners. Bells reached us across the miles, thin and sweet, ringing some pattern that was almost a song but never quite resolved, like a tune that kept forgetting where it was going.

"Home of the king," I said, because Wren deserved narration. "Aldric the Restorer, they call him. Saved the kingdoms when I was — well. When someone was small." The gap in me yawned open the way it always did when I reached for my own history, that hole shaped exactly like myself. I stepped over it. I was getting good at stepping over it. "There'll be food in the city. Real food. Bread with things *in* it."

Wren's stomach made a noise like a drawbridge lowering, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

But as we came down off the last ridge and joined the stream of travelers on the road — carters, pilgrims, a family of goose-herders whose geese regarded Wren with what I swear was reverence — I noticed something I couldn't name yet. The closer we got to the city, the quieter the people became. Not sad-quiet. Careful-quiet. A carter ahead of us hummed a drinking song, and his wife touched his arm, and he stopped, glancing at the walls as if the song might be counted against him.

"Cheerful place," I murmured.

Wren had gone still. They were looking at the city with their head tipped, the way they looked at things the rest of us couldn't see. Their hand found my sleeve and gripped it — not tugging, just holding, the way you hold the rail of a ship.

"What?" I asked. "What do you see?"

They shook their head slowly. Then they pointed at the Crownspire, and drew one finger down through the air, all the way to the ground, and then pressed that finger flat against their own chest, over the heart, and held it there until I thought they'd forgotten to breathe.

*Something up there reaches all the way down here,* that gesture said. *Into everyone.*

I told myself it was a child's dramatics. I was, as usual, wrong, but I want it noted that I told myself very firmly.

* * *

The gates of Aldermont took a tithe of their own, though nobody called it that. A copper a head, a question about business, a bored guard waving us through a gatehouse so tall the pigeons nesting in it had their own weather. Inside, the city opened up like a purse: streets cobbled and swept, shopfronts painted in colors I hadn't seen since — since ever, actually, since nowhere, since the valley sold colors back to travelers secondhand and faded. Spice sellers. A fountain with a bronze boy pouring water from a jug forever. Dogs. Actual dogs, trotting about with the confidence of citizens.

Wren stopped dead in front of a baker's window and stared at a honey-bun with the naked longing usually reserved for religious visions.

"Oh, we're not doing that," I said, and bought two.

We ate them on the steps of a public fountain, and I watched Wren experience sweetness, and I will confess something I don't say lightly: for about four minutes, in the sun, with sticky fingers and a child laughing silently at a dog trying to bite the fountain spray, I forgot to be afraid. I forgot Corvane and his ledger full of names I didn't remember giving. I forgot the ferryman's toll and the hollow it had left in me, a stranger's joy gone out of my chest like a tooth. I forgot that we were fugitives in a city whose bells rang a song that never ended.

Then the trumpets started, and the crowd began to press toward the processional way, and I remembered everything at once.

"The king," said a woman beside me, hoisting her son onto her shoulders. "Come on, love, you'll see him. You'll see the man who saved us all."

*Saved us all.* She said it the way people say grace. Rote, worn smooth, a coin rubbed featureless by handling. Around me the whole street said versions of it — *the Restorer, blessed be, seven years of plenty, praise the crown* — and every voice had that same rubbed-smooth quality, and every face wore the careful quiet I'd noticed on the road.

Wren had stopped eating. Their honey-bun hung forgotten in their hand, dripping.

"Wren?"

Their eyes were fixed on the processional way, where the crowd was packing itself six deep behind rope barriers, and their expression was one I'd only seen once before — the night they'd arrived at my cottage in the storm, when the stones had hummed under their feet. Dread and wonder, braided tight. Wonder losing.

"We can watch from the back," I said. "Or not at all. We could go around—"

They shook their head. Gripped my sleeve again. *No.* We would watch. Whatever they needed to see, they needed to see it whole.

So we watched.

* * *

He came at the golden hour, of course. Everything about it was golden hour — the light itself seemed hired for the occasion, slanting down the great avenue and setting the palace windows ablaze, gilding the banners, turning the cobblestones to a river of amber. Trumpets. Drums. A hundred horsemen in white and gold, riding with the terrible synchronized grace of people who have rehearsed joy.

And then the king.

King Aldric rode a white horse down the center of the avenue, and the crowd roared, and I understood immediately why they roared. He was beautiful the way winter sunrise is beautiful. Tall, broad, silver-streaked hair caught by the light, a face carved for coins and cathedrals. He wore white and gold, and on his brow — I felt Wren go rigid beside me before I saw it — he wore the crown.

It was not a large crown. That was the first wrong thing. Crowns in storybooks are mountains of gold and jewels; this was a slim circlet, almost modest, worked into shapes that might have been leaves or might have been flames or might have been, if you let your eye slide along them too long, fingers. It sat close against his temples. Close the way a hand sits close when it's holding something.

And his eyes —

"Glass," I whispered, before I could stop myself. No. Not glass. Glass would have been kinder and simpler. His eyes were *clear*, that was the horror of it — clear as deep water, clear as a window into a room where the lamps had all been carried out. Beautiful, calm, and lit from nowhere. The crowd saw splendor. I saw a house with nobody home, kept tidy for visitors.

Beside me, Wren made a sound. Their first sound in days — not a word, just a small airless *hnh* in the back of their throat, the noise a person makes when they see a fall coming.

And then I saw the shadow.

The sun was low and behind him, so the king's shadow stretched long down the avenue ahead of his horse, thrown huge across the cobbles, and the children in the front row were playing with it, jumping to put their feet on the giant's head. Children do that. Nobody stopped them. Nobody ever stops children from touching a royal shadow; it's tradition, probably, some old superstition about luck.

Except the shadow wasn't doing what shadows do.

I am a thief of memories. I have spent my life watching people closely enough to steal the shape of what they feel, and I am telling you that I know the difference between a trick of the light and a thing that is real but shouldn't be, and this was the second kind. The horse moved. The rider moved. The shadow *lagged*. Half a heartbeat — less, a half-second, the space between lightning and thunder compressed to almost nothing — the shadow arrived late. The horse's foreleg came down, and a breath afterward, the shadow's foreleg came down. The king raised his hand to bless the crowd, and the shadow's arm rose after, following like a servant who has not quite been dismissed.

Nobody else saw it. I checked. I looked at faces all down the barrier — delight, awe, that rubbed-smooth devotion — and not one pair of eyes flickered downward. Either they couldn't see it, or some part of them had agreed, long ago and thoroughly, not to.

Wren saw it. Wren was gripping my sleeve hard enough to leave bruises, and their other hand had risen halfway toward the avenue, fingers spread, as if they could catch the shadow like a falling plate.

And then the king's horse passed directly opposite us, close enough that I could see the stitching on his glove, and the king turned his head — the slow, ceremonial sweep a king gives his people, left to right, generous as sunlight — and his clear glass-water eyes crossed mine.

Stopped.

For half a second — the same half-second, I would have sworn it, the same stolen interval — the emptiness in those eyes *moved*. Something behind them leaned forward, the way a prisoner leans toward a barred window when footsteps pass in the corridor. The beautiful mouth didn't change. The wave didn't falter. But something in the deep clear water of him looked at me — at *me*, a mud-booted stranger with honey-bun stickiness on his fingers — and what I felt come off him was not menace.

It was a plea. Faint, frayed, half-drowned, like a shout heard through a wall years after the shouting stopped. *You see me. You see me. Please—*

The moment passed. The eyes slid on. The parade rolled forward, trumpets climbing, crowd roaring, and the king's shadow followed him down the golden avenue half a beat behind, faithful as a dog on a short leash.

Wren's grip eased. They were shaking. I put my arm around them and steered us out of the press, away from the ropes and the roar, into a side street where the noise dropped to a muffle and a pie-seller gave us a look that said *you're blocking custom* and meant nothing else by it.

"It saw us," I said quietly. "Or he did. One of them did."

Wren nodded. Then they took my hand, turned it palm-up, and wrote on it with one finger, slow and deliberate, the way they wrote when it mattered:

**NOT THE KING.**

"No," I agreed, my stomach settling somewhere around my boots. "I rather gathered it wasn't the king."

They wrote again, faster, pressing harder:

**THE CROWN IS HUNGRY. IT KNOWS THE WORD.**

I looked back down the side street, to the slice of golden avenue visible between the buildings, where the tail of the procession was passing and the great shadow swept along the walls, late, always late, a half-second out of step with the man it belonged to.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something and humor was the only weapon I'd ever been any good with, "then it's lucky we're leaving town first thing tomorrow."

Wren looked up at me. Slowly, regretfully, they shook their head.

Then they pointed — past the pie-seller, past the leaning timber houses, toward the palace on its hill, where the Crownspire's windows burned in the dying sun like a hundred watchful eyes — and tapped their own chest twice.

*We're going in.*

Behind us, faint and sweet and never resolving, the bells began the song that forgets where it's going, and high on the hill, in a window I could not even see, something wearing a king's face turned toward the sound of our footsteps and listened.
