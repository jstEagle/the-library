## 13. The Thing Wearing Him

Wren had learned, in the valley, that everything had a sound if you listened with the right part of yourself. Not the ears—ears were for birdsong and Ansel's grumbling and the rain on the cottage roof. The other listening. The one that lived behind the breastbone, where the old words slept.

The valley had sounded like a held breath. Like a room full of people who had all been told to be quiet at the same moment, and had obeyed so long ago they'd forgotten there was ever another way to be.

Aldermont did not hold its breath.

Aldermont *screamed*.

Not with the mouth of it—the city's mouth was busy enough, hawking chestnuts and shouting the king's praises and arguing about the price of ribbon. With the other part. The part Wren heard from the rooftop of a dyer's shop, three streets back from the processional road, where Ansel had paid a nervous man an absurd sum to let two "memory-broker's assistants" sit among his bolts of cloth until sundown.

The whole city rang like a struck bell that no one else could hear. And under the ringing, under the cheering, wound one long thread of wrongness, thick as a cable, running from the palace at the heart of everything out into the streets and into the people themselves.

Wren sat very still on a bolt of indigo wool and listened to it, and felt their stomach turn over slowly, the way it did before storms.

*There,* said the thread. *There. There.*

The parade was coming.

* * *

Ansel crouched beside them in the dye-stink and the dust motes, peering through a gap in the shutters with the expression of someone who found pageantry personally offensive. "You're doing the thing," Ansel murmured. "The staring-at-nothing thing."

Wren blinked and looked at them.

"You do it when you're listening. Your eyes go somewhere else entirely. It's unsettling, is what it is." Ansel shifted, resettling the borrowed broker's satchel against their hip. It was a good disguise, Wren thought. Ansel had a gift for looking like they belonged anywhere, which was probably the same gift that let them steal the inside of a person's head without breaking the skin. "Well? You've been humming since dawn. What do you hear?"

Wren considered how to answer. Words were still a country they visited more than lived in; the old tongue was home, and the common speech was full of doors that didn't fit their frames. They settled on the truest small thing.

*Loud.*

"Loud," Ansel repeated flatly. "Wonderful. A capital city is loud. I could have told you that for free, and saved us the dyer's fee."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. Then the bells began, and the crowd below surged forward like water finding a channel, and the thread of wrongness pulled taut, and Wren forgot how to smile at all.

They went to the shutter gap and looked down through it, and saw him.

King Aldric rode a white horse at the center of it all, and the crowd loved him—you could feel the love coming off them in warm waves, simple and unguarded, the love people give to a story they need to be true. He was tall. His armor was silver chased with gold, and his cloak was the blue of deep winter sky, and his face was handsome in the worn way of a coin passed through too many hands.

And he was *empty*.

That was the first thing Wren saw, really saw, once they stopped looking with their eyes. Behind the handsome face there was a room with the furniture covered in white sheets. Behind the bright eyes there was a long corridor with every door standing open and nothing in any of them. The man on the horse was not gone. That was the terrible part. He was *there*, pressed thin, spread like butter over the whole surface of himself, present in every smile and absent from every place a self should pool.

And riding him—not on the horse, but *on him*, wrapped around the crown of his head like a second skull made of something older than bone—was the thing.

Wren made a small sound. Ansel's hand came up fast and gripped their shoulder. "What? What is it?"

Wren couldn't answer yet. They were looking at the crown-thing, and the crown-thing was—

It wasn't what they had imagined. In the valley, in the dark of the cottage with the stones humming, they had imagined teeth. Claws. Something hungry-shaped, something you could run from.

But the thing wearing the king was an *oath*. That was all. That was everything. It was a promise that had been spoken so hard and held so long that it had grown a body out of the holding. Wren could see its shape now that they knew to look: it was built of words, layered words, centuries of them, the way a pearl is built of layers around a single grain of hurt. *I swear. I will keep. Until. Forever.* Each layer gleamed. Each layer was beautiful, in the way that ice on a river is beautiful right up until the river stops moving underneath it.

And at the center of it, the grain, the seed around which all those shining layers had grown—

A fear. Old and enormous and utterly sincere. *If we do not bind ourselves, we will lose everything.* Someone had been afraid, a very long time ago, with a kingdom dying around them, and they had sworn, and the swearing had worked, and the working had never stopped.

The crown-thing turned its attention across the crowd, slow as a lighthouse beam, and Wren felt it pass over them like cold water.

It stopped.

It came *back*.

For one heartbeat—one bell-toll, one breath—the vast layered attention of the oath-that-had-flesh rested on a dyer's shop roof, on a bolt of indigo wool, on a small mute child who could hear the shape of promises.

Then a chestnut cart overturned in the street below with a crash and a great deal of swearing, and the crowd laughed, and the beam moved on, and Wren discovered they had been holding their breath long enough to see spots.

Ansel's grip on their shoulder had gone white-knuckled. "It looked at you," Ansel breathed. "I don't know how I know that, but I know it. Don't move."

Wren didn't move. But inside, where the old words lived, they did something they hadn't done since the valley: they reached for the thread of wrongness, gently, the way you'd touch a snared bird, and they *listened to what it was eating*.

* * *

This was what Wren understood, sitting in the dye-dust while the parade rolled beneath them and the crowd roared itself hoarse:

The crown-thing did not devour the king the way a wolf devours a lamb. There was no tearing. There was no blood. It was worse than that, and gentler than that, and it took Wren a long moment to find the shape of it even in their own mind.

It fed on *consent*.

Every time Aldric smiled and waved, some small true piece of him slid down into the shining layers and became part of the promise. Every time he signed a decree he could barely read, every time he stood at a window and felt, distantly, that something was wrong but decided—decided, chose, consented—to believe the feeling was only tiredness, another morsel went down. The thing could take nothing by force. Force would break it. It was made of agreement; it could only eat what was given.

And so it kept him agreeing. It dulled the edges of his doubt. It fed him little comforts, little certainties, the warm blanket-thoughts of a man who is told, daily, by everyone around him, that he is beloved and wise and well. It was patient the way winter is patient. It did not need to win. It only needed him to keep saying yes.

And the worst of it—the part that made Wren's throat close and their eyes sting, the part they would carry for the rest of their life—was this:

*Aldric knew.*

Not clearly. Not in words. But somewhere down in that sheet-covered room, in the corridor with all the open doors, the king had left himself a message, the way a drowning man scratches marks on the inside of a barrel. Wren could feel it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: a stubborn splinter of *no* buried so deep in the man that the crown-thing had never managed to swallow it. It wasn't hope, exactly. Hope needs a shape and a plan. It was smaller and harder than hope. It was the last inch of a man who had been eaten for seven years and seven years and seven years, refusing, in the only place left to him, to say yes.

He was still in there. He was still fighting. He had been fighting alone, in the dark, with weapons no bigger than a doubt, for longer than Wren had been alive.

Wren had come down from the mountains ready to hate the monster. Nobody had warned them they would pity it instead—and pity its host most of all.

Because here was the other thing, the thing that made their hands go cold on the windowsill: the crown-thing was afraid too. Under all those gleaming layers of *I swear, I will keep, forever*, the original fear still burned, and it had never ended. The oath had been sworn by terrified people to save everything they loved, and the thing it had become was still trying to save everything it loved, in the only way it knew, which was to hold on and hold on and hold on until the holding crushed what it held.

It was not evil. Wren turned that thought over and over, examining it from every side, wishing it were otherwise. It was a promise that had forgotten it was ever made by people. It was a hand that had clenched so long it had forgotten it was ever meant to open.

Someone would have to teach it to let go. Or break every finger.

* * *

Below, the parade was passing. The king raised his hand to the crowd, and the crowd made a sound like the sea, and Wren watched the shadow.

That was the detail that undid them. Not the glass brightness of the eyes, not the too-even smile. The shadow. Everyone else's shadow leapt and swung with the torchlight along the route, quick and lively. The king's shadow lagged. Just a half-breath. Just enough that if you weren't listening with your breastbone you'd call it a trick of the light—but Wren was listening, and the shadow moved late because the shadow belonged to *him*, to the thin pressed-down real man underneath, and the real man was half a beat behind his own body, following it through the day like a child led by the hand through a crowd.

*He can't even walk at his own speed,* Wren thought, and something in their chest cracked sideways, and hot tears arrived without permission.

"Wren." Ansel's voice had gone low and careful, the voice they used for spooked animals and bad news. "You're crying. Talk to me. Well—do the thing where you talk to me."

Wren grabbed their sleeve. Pointed down, at the king, then pressed their own hand flat over their heart, then made the gesture they'd taught Ansel on the road north: fist closing, slowly, over nothing. *Holding. Holding too tight.*

"I know," Ansel said grimly. "I see it. The shadow. Half a beat late. I've been watching it for an hour and it's making my skin crawl." They hesitated. "Is that him? Underneath?"

Wren nodded. Then, because Ansel deserved the whole truth and Wren was tired of carrying truths in silence, they pointed at the king again, and then at their own mouth, and shook their head slowly—not *I cannot speak*, but *he cannot speak*. *He is me. He is locked the way I was locked.*

Ansel stared at them. Wren watched the understanding arrive in stages, like weather coming over hills: confusion, then horror, then a kind of furious tenderness that Ansel would have denied to their grave.

"Oh," Ansel said softly. "Oh, that's—" They stopped. Started again. "Seven years, the records said. Renewed every seven years. How long has he been—"

Wren held up fingers. Counted them. Held up all ten, twice, and then four more.

"Twenty-four years," Ansel whispered. "Gods. He's been drowning for twenty-four years, and everyone in this city is cheering the water." They scrubbed a hand down their face. When they spoke again, their voice had changed—flatter, harder, the broker's voice, the thief's voice, the voice Wren had learned meant Ansel was about to do something dangerous and call it practical. "Right. We can't do anything from a dyer's roof. I need to get closer. Inside, eventually. And you—" They caught themselves, glanced at Wren, and Wren saw them wrestle with the sentence *you should stay somewhere safe* and lose. "You stay where I can see you. That's not negotiable. If that thing looks at you again, I want to be standing between."

Wren blinked at them. Then, deliberately, they leaned over and bumped their forehead against Ansel's shoulder—the old gesture, the valley gesture, the one that meant *yes, and thank you, and don't be an idiot about it*.

"Yes, well," Ansel muttered, going pink above the collar of the borrowed coat. "Someone has to keep you fed. You eat like a starling."

Below them, the parade reached the palace gates. Trumpets. The crowd pressing forward. And King Aldric, at the threshold of his own home, paused—just a moment, just a hitch in the pageantry that nobody else seemed to notice—and turned his head, very slightly, toward the rooftops.

Toward the dyer's shop.

His glass-bright eyes found the shutter gap. Found, Wren was certain, though it should have been impossible, the two faces in the dark behind it.

And the thin pressed-down man underneath lifted one finger—so small a motion, so brief, lost entirely in the wave of his greeting—and tapped it twice against the pommel of his saddle.

Tap. Tap.

Like a man tapping on the inside of a barrel.

Ansel went rigid beside Wren. "Did you see—"

Wren was already nodding, already crying again, already reaching for Ansel's hand.

The king had seen them. Or something in the king had. And tomorrow, Wren understood without knowing how, everything would depend on getting past the gates—past the guards, past the courtiers, past the thing of layered light and ancient fear that sat upon his head—before the crown-thing finished deciding whether what it had glimpsed on a dyer's roof was a threat, or a meal.
