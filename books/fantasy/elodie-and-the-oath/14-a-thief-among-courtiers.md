## 14. A Thief Among Courtiers

The trouble with disguises, Ansel had learned long ago in the valley, was not the clothes. Clothes were easy. Anyone could rent a merchant's coat and powder their face with the right shade of exhaustion. The trouble with disguises was that people did not look at what you wore. They looked at what you *knew*.

So Ansel spent two days in Aldermont learning to know things.

They learned that memory-brokers in the capital wore silver pins shaped like closed eyes, and that the pin's clasp told other brokers which guild-house you belonged to — or claimed to. They learned that brokers were greeted with a particular mixture of courtesy and dread, like tax collectors who worked in feelings instead of coin. They learned that the fashionable complaint among courtiers was not gout or debt but *heaviness* — too many memories of too many seasons, weighing on the mind like wet wool.

And they learned, most usefully of all, that the palace at Aldermont held a weekly salon where brokers were invited to lighten the nobility of their burdens, for a fee that would have made Ansel laugh if they hadn't been so busy memorizing it.

"Third door past the tapestry of the Hunt," said the innkeeper's wife, who talked the way rivers run, constantly and without much direction. "You'll want to arrive early. Lady Vessaline gets first pick and she haggles like a fishwife, no offense meant to fishwives. And don't let Lord Cassel corner you by the wine. He'll sell you his grief for a song and then weep about it for a week and blame you."

"No offense taken," Ansel said gravely. "Fishwives are honest traders."

The woman laughed so hard she had to sit down.

Ansel paid for the room with coins earned honestly enough — three days of lifting headaches and heartburn from pilgrims outside the temple district, small ailments rather than memories, because Wren had made them promise. *No taking what isn't given,* the child had signed, hands quick and fierce in the lamplight of their rented room. *You promised.*

*I promised,* Ansel had agreed, and had only felt a little like a dog being trained.

Wren stayed behind now, hooded and still, in the room above the inn, practicing the old language in whispers so soft the floorboards barely hummed. Each word cost the child something. Ansel had watched it happen — watched a year drain out of a face that had perhaps ten years left to give — and every hour spent learning the layout of the palace felt like time stolen twice over.

So: competence. That was the disguise. Not the coat, not the pin. The walk of someone who belonged.

Ansel practiced it in front of the inn's spotted mirror until the reflection stopped looking like a thief and started looking like a professional.

* * *

The palace swallowed people whole and politely.

That was Ansel's first impression, standing in the queue of brokers outside the third door past the tapestry of the Hunt, surrounded by men and women in dove-grey and midnight-blue, all wearing closed-eye pins, all radiating the particular boredom of people who had seen everything worth seeing and sold half of it back.

A steward with a voice like oiled hinges checked each pin against a ledger. When he reached Ansel, he glanced up.

"House?"

"Independent," Ansel said, in the flat tone of someone who found the question beneath them.

The steward's eyebrows rose a fraction — independent brokers were rare, and independents who got invited to the salon rarer still — but the invitation Ansel carried had been lifted, gently and without malice, from a broker named Master Ferrowl who had drunk himself into a deep and dreamless sleep at the Drowned Swan two nights ago. Ferrowl would wake tomorrow with a headache and a vague sense of having missed something. Ansel had left him coins for the trouble. It was, by Ansel's standards, practically charity.

"Independent," the steward repeated, and wrote something. "Mind the rules. No lifting without consent and contract. No touching the royal family. No working the servants' wing." He looked up again, and this time there was something almost like sympathy in his hinge-voice. "And whatever you do, don't look at the king's shadow."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You won't," the steward said, and waved them through.

The salon was held in a long gallery lit by a hundred candles that never guttered — Ansel noticed that immediately, noticed the way the flames stood perfectly straight as though standing at attention, and filed it away next to everything else wrong with this city. Courtiers drifted between brokers like moths who had learned to negotiate. Somewhere a quartet played music that was technically beautiful and emotionally vacant, like a portrait of someone smiling.

Ansel took a glass of wine they didn't drink and began to work.

This was the part that came easily, embarrassingly easily, the part Corvane had drilled into them across a thousand valley evenings: *the trade is nine parts listening, little thief. Everyone wants to be heard. Give them an ear and they'll hand you their head.*

A duchess wanted to forget her second husband. A general wanted to forget a battle he'd won, which Ansel found more interesting than any defeat. A young lordling wanted to forget a woman whose name he couldn't say without blushing, and Ansel quoted him a price high enough to make him reconsider, because some things deserved to be kept even when they hurt.

And through all of it, Ansel listened for one thing: anyone who had been present, seven years ago, on the day King Aldric was crowned.

* * *

The courtier found Ansel, which was convenient, because Ansel had already identified him as the best candidate in the room and had been maneuvering toward him for an hour with the patience of a spider who had read books on etiquette.

His name was Lord Aldous Pemberton-Hale, and he was the kind of man who filled silence the way sand fills a boot — persistently and to everyone's discomfort. He was sixtyish, pink-faced, dressed in a waistcoat embroidered with tiny golden crowns, and he had attached himself to Ansel near the wine table with the desperate energy of a man who had been waiting all evening for someone new to talk to.

"—and of course I said to her, Marguerite, I said, you cannot serve quail at a mourning dinner, it's *gauche*, and she said—"

"My lord," Ansel interrupted smoothly, "forgive me, but I couldn't help noticing your pin."

Pemberton-Hale blinked and touched the small gold circlet at his lapel. "Chamberlain's office. Thirty years, my dear broker, thirty years. Served the old king, gods rest him, and then—" He stopped. Something flickered behind his eyes, quick as a fish turning.

"And then?" Ansel asked softly.

"Well. Then the new king, naturally." He smiled, and the smile was the strangest thing Ansel had seen all evening, because it arrived a half-second before the words did, like a servant opening a door before the knock. "Wonderful occasion. Wonderful. You were not there, of course."

"I wasn't honored, no."

"No, no. Small affair, really. Very small." He said it twice, as if reassuring himself. "For a coronation. Odd weather. Or — no, lovely weather. Lovely." His hand had begun, very slightly, to tremble against his wineglass. "I remember it perfectly."

Ansel had spent a lifetime around people lying about their memories, and knew every species of the animal. There was the boastful lie, the shameful lie, the kind lie. This was none of these. This was a man repeating a sentence he had been made to believe, the way a bird repeats a song it was taught in a cage.

"The thing about memories, my lord," Ansel said, warming the voice, letting it go soft and confiding, the way they spoke to travelers on cold nights when the trade began, "is that they get heavy. Even happy ones. Especially happy ones, sometimes. All that gold and ceremony, pressing on a man year after year."

"Heavy," Pemberton-Hale repeated. His eyes had gone slightly unfocused. "Yes. Yes, it is heavy. Isn't that funny? Everyone says I should treasure it, and all I feel is—"

"Weighed down," Ansel finished for him.

"A stone," the old chamberlain whispered. "Like a stone under my tongue. Every night. I dream of the crown, broker. I dream it is *looking* at me." He laughed, high and thin, and the laugh frightened him more than the words had. "Forgive me. Too much wine. You must think me mad."

"I think you're carrying something alone that was never meant for one pair of shoulders," Ansel said. "It happens to the best of men. It's why my profession exists."

Pemberton-Hale stared at them. And then, with the relief of a man setting down luggage he'd forgotten he was holding, he said: "How much?"

"For a lightening? Depends on weight." Ansel glanced around — the quartet, the drifting courtiers, the candle flames standing at attention. "Not here. Privacy matters, in my trade. Is there somewhere quiet?"

The old man's study was three corridors away, and he led Ansel to it chattering the entire way — about quail, about Marguerite, about the shocking price of candle-wax — while Ansel counted doors and guards and marked, with a thief's automatic precision, the route back out. Two guards at the west stair, bored. One at the gallery, yawning. The crown room itself lay somewhere above them; Ansel felt it before they saw any sign of it, a faint pressure behind the eyes, like a headache that hadn't decided yet.

The study smelled of ink and lavender and, underneath both, something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit just past its prime.

"Now then." Ansel settled Pemberton-Hale into a chair by the fire and took the opposite seat, close enough that their knees nearly touched. "The technique is simple. You'll feel a slight coolness. Like water running backward. Don't fight it. Think of the night in question — the coronation — and simply... hold it out to me. Like offering bread."

"That's all?"

"That's all. Most men find it a relief."

It was, in truth, nothing of the sort. What Ansel actually did bore no resemblance to the gentle ritual they described. Lifting a memory was a theft performed inside another person's skull, delicate as pickpocketing a sleeping giant. You found the thread — every memory had a thread, a texture, a color — and you drew it out slow, so slow the sleeper-mind barely registered the tug.

Pemberton-Hale's coronation-memory was wrapped in iron.

Ansel had lifted thousands of memories. Grief came wrapped in wool. Joy came wrapped in silk. Shame came wrapped in brambles. But this — this was chain-link, cold and regular and *manufactured*, and woven through it ran a single word, repeated like a stitch pattern:

*consent. consent. consent.*

Ansel's hands went still above the old man's temples.

*He gave consent,* the wrapping seemed to say. *Everyone gave consent. There is nothing here but what was permitted.*

But underneath the iron, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something else pulsed. Something that had been pressed down so hard it had gone flat, folded into the creases of the binding like a letter hidden in a hem.

Ansel drew it out anyway.

It came in pieces, the way drowned things come up: the great hall, banners, the old king's bier still fresh with lilies. A boy-prince kneeling — Aldric, young, hollow-eyed, his kingdom starving behind him, plague in the low towns and the harvest rotted black in the fields. And the crown coming down — not placed, *descending*, lowering itself onto his head with the slow deliberation of a spider onto a fly — and the hall full of nobles all nodding, nodding, nodding, their heads moving in unison like wheat in wind, and Pemberton-Hale among them, nodding, weeping without knowing why, and a voice that was not a voice saying words that were not words—

*Saved. All saved. For a small and reasonable price, renewed in seven years' time—*

Seven years.

Ansel sat back. The fire crackled. Pemberton-Hale blinked slowly, looking mildly confused and enormously lighter, exactly as advertised.

"There," Ansel said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Feel that? The stone's gone from under your tongue."

"It has." The old man flexed his jaw wonderingly. "Gone. Gods. Twenty years I've carried—" He stopped, frowning faintly at the space where the memory had been. "What did I carry? I seem to have..."

"A burden," Ansel said. "Best forgotten. That's the service."

"Yes." He smiled his half-second-early smile. "Yes, best forgotten. Will you take tea? Marguerite makes—"

"Another time, my lord. I have other clients tonight."

Ansel walked out of the study at a professional pace, neither hurrying nor strolling, past the yawning guard, down the west stair, through the gallery where the candles burned straight as spears. Only when they reached the street did they allow themselves to stop, press a hand against cold stone, and breathe.

*Renewed in seven years' time.*

Seven years ago, a crown had descended on a dying king, and the whole court had nodded yes. Which meant the renewal was due now. This year. Perhaps this season. Perhaps —

Wren. A child who had fled the palace speaking a dead language, carrying a word no one dared say aloud.

Ansel turned the stolen memory over in their mind as they walked, feeling its edges, its terrible stitched seams. The court had consented. But consented to *what*, exactly? Saved from what, at what price? The iron wrapping hid the middle of the story the way a bandage hides a wound — and somewhere in the palace, in sealed records or sealed minds, the missing piece waited.

At the inn, Wren was awake, sitting cross-legged on the bed, and took one look at Ansel's face before signing, rapid and sharp: *What did you find?*

"A coronation," Ansel said, sitting down heavily. "And a bill come due." They met the child's eyes. "Wren — how many years since you left the palace?"

Wren's hands went still.

Then, slowly, the child held up fingers. One. Two. Three.

Three years. Three years since Wren fled — which meant the tithe had fallen due a year after the escape, and the crown had gone unpaid, and had been waiting, patient as debt, ever since.

"Oh," Ansel said quietly. "Oh, you poor little fool. You didn't run away from the palace."

Wren stared.

"You *are* the payment. And it's four years overdue."
