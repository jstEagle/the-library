## 15. What Was Sworn at the Coronation

The memory sat in my pocket like a hot coal wrapped in silk.

I had carried stolen memories for eleven years. I knew their textures the way a baker knows dough — the springy brightness of a child's first snowfall, the dense sourdough weight of a grief properly aged, the flaky, forgettable pastry of a market day nobody would miss. This one was different. This one had a pulse.

I'd lifted it from Lady Vessamine Corrow three nights ago, in the amber-lit hush of her townhouse in Aldermont, while she wept over her wine and I wore the borrowed face of a memory-broker named Master Quillon — a face I had bought off a dying actor and kept folded in my coat like a spare shirt. She had pressed the vial into my hands herself, trembling.

"Take it," she'd whispered. "I can't keep watching it every time I close my eyes. Take it out of me."

I should have asked what *it* was. A professional asks. A professional prices the goods before the seal breaks. But she was crying, and I have always been a soft touch for crying, which is a terrible quality in a thief and an even worse one in a fence, and so I took it, and I paid her far too little, and now here I was at midnight in a rented room above a chandler's shop, with Wren asleep on the narrow bed behind me and a stranger's worst memory burning a hole through my coat pocket like it wanted to be set free.

Wren stirred. I turned. The child slept curled around the little clay lamp like a comma around its clause, and even in sleep their lips moved sometimes, shaping syllables from the dead tongue that made the floorboards hum faintly, apologetically, as if the house itself were dreaming.

I looked at the vial again.

"You know," I told Wren's sleeping back, "in a just world, I would sell this unopened to some collector and be rich by Thursday."

The world, as established, was not just. I uncorked the vial and pressed the memory to my temple, and the room fell away.

* * *

I was standing in a great hall, but I was not myself. I was inside Lady Vessamine's younger eyes, twenty years old, wearing a dress the color of new copper, and everything was wrong with the world in the specific way that things are wrong when a kingdom is dying.

The hall was Aldermont's coronation hall — I knew it from travelers' memories, all gold leaf and banners — but in this memory the gold was tarnished, the banners hung gray as dishwater, and the air itself seemed thin, like bread stretched too far. People stood in clusters, whispering. The whispering had a sound I recognized from somewhere deep and old: the sound of people deciding what to believe before they're told what's true.

*The blight,* Vessamine's mind supplied, the way minds do, generous with context. *Three years of it. The wheat came up black. The wells went bitter. The mages said the land's own magic had soured, that the First Kingdoms' old bargains had rotted through, and then the mages started dying too, one by one, quietly, like candles pinched out by someone very polite.*

At the front of the hall, on the dais beneath the empty crown stand, stood a young man in armor too big for him. Prince Aldric. He looked — Vessamine thought, and I thought with her — *young*. Younger than his years, thinner than his portraits, with the particular pallor of someone who has not slept in a month because everyone keeps asking him to save them.

Beside him stood a figure that made Vessamine's skin crawl and made mine crawl worse, because I knew what I was looking at even before the memory told me.

It wore the shape of a courtier. Tall, gray-robed, face smooth as an egg. But it stood *wrong* — not still, exactly, but held, the way a puppet is held between performances. And where its shadow should have fallen, there was instead a faint darkening of the air, a smudge, as though reality itself couldn't quite be bothered to render it properly.

*They say it came out of the mountain,* Vessamine's mind whispered. *They say it offered the prince a bargain.*

Aldric stepped forward. His voice, when he spoke, was steady in the way of someone gripping a railing very hard.

"The kingdom dies," he said. "I have watched it die for three years. I have buried my father and half my council and two of my sisters." His jaw worked. "I will pay any price. Name it."

And the thing in the gray robe smiled. It had spent so long not smiling that the smile looked like a crack in porcelain.

"The price is already named, little king. It has been named since the first kingdoms fell. You know the shape of it. All bargains have the same shape."

"I want it spoken," Aldric said. "Plainly. Before witnesses. If I am to sell something, I will know what I've sold."

The thing tilted its head, considering, and then — and this was the part that made my stomach turn, there in the chandler's rented room, made me grip the windowsill — it spoke plainly. It *wanted* to speak plainly. That was the horror of it. It had no shame to hide behind.

"You will live," it said. "You will reign, and the land will heal, and the wheat will come up gold, and your mages will wake from their dying. In exchange, every seven years, on the night of the renewal, you will give me seven children. Chosen by lot. Given freely, by your own decree, or the bargain voids and everything returns to its dying — only faster, for the delay will have been borrowed against them."

The hall went silent in the specific, ringing way of a thousand people forgetting to breathe.

"And the children?" Aldric asked. His voice cracked on the second word. "What happens to the children?"

"They will serve," the thing said pleasantly. "They will be *useful*. Memory is the coin of my realm, little king, and children's memories are unminted gold. They will forget their names, their mothers, their sorrows. They will forget that they were ever given. Is that not a mercy? To be spared the knowing?"

Vessamine, twenty years old and copper-dressed, felt the crowd around her do the terrible arithmetic that crowds do. Seven children. Every seven years. Out of how many thousands? Out of how many tens of thousands? She felt the man beside her relax, fractionally, and hated him, and understood him, and hated herself for understanding him.

Aldric stood alone on the dais, and I watched — through her eyes, through her horror — as he looked out at all those faces, all those lives balanced on his answer, and I watched him understand that the crowd had already answered for him. That they were waiting. That some of them were already silently subtracting their own children from the count, moving them behind other people's children in their hearts.

"No," Aldric said.

Hope went through the hall like a fish through water.

"No," he said again, louder. "Not children. Not *anyone* given who does not choose it. I will pay in my own—"

"The bargain requires innocents," the thing said, and its voice did not change at all, which was somehow worse than if it had thundered. "That is its shape. It has always been its shape. Refuse, and I return to the mountain, and the blight returns with me, and in ten years there will be no kingdom left to save and no one left to blame you, because they will all be dead."

Silence.

And then Aldric — young, exhausted, armored in a father's crown he hadn't earned yet — closed his eyes and said the most heartbreaking sentence I have ever heard a man say:

"Then let the lots fall on my house first. Let my line be counted among the tithed. Let it begin with anyone of my blood before it touches a farmer's daughter or a smith's son. Swear that, and I swear the rest."

The thing smiled its porcelain smile.

"Clever little king. Yes. Your line shall be counted first, always first, every renewal, until the bargain ends." It extended a hand like a glove with nothing in it. "Sworn?"

And Aldric took the hand, and the hall erupted — relief, cheering, weeping, people embracing strangers — and Vessamine cheered too, because she was twenty and the wheat would come up gold, and only later, much later, lying awake, would she wonder why the celebration had felt so much like a funeral where nobody would admit whose funeral it was.

The memory should have ended there.

It didn't. Memories are greedy; they keep what they catch. There was more — a fragment, a coda, the kind of thing that sticks to the edge of a recollection like a burr. Vessamine, older now, at her fourth renewal, standing in the square as seven names were read from a scroll, watching a mother scream, and hearing the woman beside her murmur, comfortably, the way one remarks on weather:

"Well. Better than the blight."

* * *

I came back to myself sitting on the floor. I don't remember getting to the floor. The vial was empty in my hand and my whole body was shaking, and outside the window the chandler's dog was barking at the moon with the uncomplicated enthusiasm of an animal who has never heard of a tithe.

Seven children. Every seven years. *Given freely, by your own decree.*

And the thing about freely — I knew this trade, I knew bargains, I knew how the fine print ate people — the thing about freely was that it never stayed free. Give a kingdom one renewal and call it mercy, and by the third renewal it's tradition, and by the fifth it's law, and by the seventh the mothers screaming in the square are an embarrassment, a vulgarity, something polite people look away from. *Better than the blight.* Four words, and a kingdom's soul filed down to a nub.

I thought of the valley. Of course I did. My valley, my sanctuary, the place where magic could not follow, where I had built my little honest dishonest living lifting memories from travelers and selling them back at the border. I had always believed the valley was an accident — a scar, a blind spot, a place the world's magic simply forgot to reach.

But memories are coin. The thing in the gray robe had said so itself. *Memory is the currency of my realm.* And what was a valley with no magic, if not a vault? What was a place where nothing supernatural could enter, if not a locked box?

And what did a broker do with a locked box?

She put things in it that she never wanted found.

My mouth had gone dry. I got up, poured water from the pitcher, drank it too fast, and stood there in the moonlight feeling like a man who has just realized the floor of his house is a lid.

Seven children. Where did the crown-thing keep them? It said they would *serve*, that they would *forget their names, their mothers, their sorrows*. Forget. That word again. That whole trade. Somewhere in my chest, an old suspicion I'd been feeding scraps to for weeks rolled over and opened one eye.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't sit still: the tithe happened every seven years. The renewals had been running for — how long? Vessamine's memory put her at her fourth renewal, twenty years after the coronation, and the coronation itself was decades back. Call it fifty years, sixty. Call it eight or nine renewals. Sixty-three children, seventy. A small village of the forgotten.

And where, in all my years in the valley, had I ever met a traveler who remembered being a child anywhere else? Where were the valley's own old folk? Why did everyone in that place seem to have arrived from nowhere, carrying pasts with soft edges, gaps shaped like missing rooms?

I had told myself it was the valley. The valley eats memory; that was its nature; that was the price of sanctuary.

But what if the valley wasn't eating anything?

What if it was *storing*?

I laughed — one short, ugly syllable of a laugh — and clapped a hand over my mouth, and glanced at the bed. Wren hadn't moved. Good. There would be time enough for nightmares to be shared around fairly, like everything else in this wretched business.

Because that was the other thing. The thing I was circling and refusing to land on, the way you circle a wound refusing to look at it directly.

*Your line shall be counted first, always first, every renewal.*

Aldric's line. The royal blood, tithed before all others, every single time. Which meant that across nine renewals, the crown-thing's collection would be seeded thick with the king's own kin — children of the royal house, carried off generation after generation, forgotten on purpose, hidden somewhere magic couldn't follow.

Somewhere like a valley.

Somewhere like a mute child who appeared out of a storm speaking the dead tongue of the First Kingdoms, whose words made stones hum, who had fled *something* and run, of all the places in the wide world, to the one place the crown-thing's servants could not easily follow.

I looked at Wren's sleeping shape. Small. Sharp-elbowed. Prone to stealing the crusts off my bread with the stealth of a much larger criminal. Utterly ordinary, except for the language older than kingdoms, except for the way the valley hummed when they dreamed.

"Oh no," I said softly, to no one, to the moon, to the dog still barking his fool head off outside. "Oh, no, no, no."

The child stirred at the sound of my voice. Sleepy eyes opened, found me standing pale in the moonlight with an empty vial in my fist, and — being Wren — immediately reached under the pillow for the knife I'd given them, because I had raised a sensible monster.

I shook my head. Held up both hands. *Nothing tonight. Nothing yet.*

Wren subsided, watchful, and I stood there doing sums I didn't want to do, and the biggest sum of all refused to stay unsolved:

If the tithe ran every seven years —

And if the last renewal was due —

Then this year, right now, in Aldermont, seven more names were about to be read from a scroll. And a child who could speak the unbinding had run away from the palace rather than be one of them.

Which meant I didn't need to wonder anymore whether Wren mattered to the crown-thing.

I needed to wonder how long it would take the crown-thing to notice its payment was missing — and what it would send to collect.
