## 16. Wren Is the Tithe

The courtier's memory sat in my pocket like a hot coal wrapped in silk.

I had carried stolen memories for fifteen years. I knew their weight the way a baker knows flour — by feel, by habit, by the small differences that told you what you had. A memory of grief was heavy and damp. A memory of joy was light as a bird's bone and twice as likely to escape you if you weren't careful. This one — a fat, gilded recollection of a coronation, lifted from a wine-flushed chamberlain who would wake tomorrow certain he'd drunk too much and dreamed of trumpets — this one was heavier than any of them.

Not because it was large. Because it was *wrong*.

I sat in the rented room above the Drowned Bell tavern with Wren asleep on the narrow bed, curled around a pillow like it might be taken from them at any moment, which — fair enough. It was how I slept too. Old habits. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the tavern keeper argued with his wife about the price of eels, which struck me as a fight neither party could win.

I pressed the memory to my temple and let it open.

*Trumpets. Banners the color of new blood. A young king walking the long aisle of the Great Hall on legs that shook only where no one could see them. Aldric, before the crown. Aldric, with eyes the color of a river in autumn and a face that hadn't yet learned to be furniture.*

And then the crowning. And then —

I had expected the memory to go dark there, the way stolen things sometimes did when they brushed against magic. What I had not expected was for it to go *loud*.

*A voice. Not spoken aloud — sworn. Sworn into the marrow of everyone present, so that even now, years later, the chamberlain's bones remembered the shape of it:*

**THE KINGDOM DIES. THE OATH SAVES IT. THE OATH ASKS ONLY WHAT KINGDOMS HAVE ALWAYS PAID.**

*And the young king, kneeling, glass-eyed already though no one noticed, no one ever noticed, answering in a voice that was half his own:* "What does it ask?"

**SEVEN YEARS. THEN SEVEN AGAIN. AND IN THE SEVENTH YEAR OF THE SEVENTH —**

*The memory shuddered. The chamberlain's mind had flinched away from the rest, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth and worries it anyway. But I caught the shape of what came after, the way you catch the shape of a word through a wall:*

**— THE TITHE. A CHILD FOR THE CROWN. ONE WHO CARRIES WHAT MUST NOT BE SPOKEN.**

The memory closed. My hands were shaking.

Below me, the eel argument continued. The candle guttered again, and I sat very still in its unsteady light and made myself think the thought I had been refusing to think since we crossed the valley's border.

Wren had not wandered out of the mountains.

Wren had *fled*.

---

I want to be honest about how long it took me to see it, because honesty is the one trade I've never mastered. I had spent weeks telling myself a comfortable story: a mute child, lost, orphaned by some border skirmish, wandering until the valley — sanctuary, accident, whatever it was — took them in. A foundling narrative. Very tidy. Very marketable, if I'd been fool enough to sell it.

But foundlings didn't speak dead tongues that made stones hum. Foundlings didn't know, without being taught, that the valley was a wound. Foundlings didn't look at the road south with an expression I had mistaken for homesickness and now recognized as something much older and much worse.

*Recognition.*

Wren had looked at the road south the way I looked at Corvane's ledger. Like something that knew your name was written in it.

I got up quietly — though quiet meant nothing to Wren, who woke the way cats do, all at once and already judging me — and crossed to the bed. The child watched me with those dark, patient eyes. Patient the way wells are patient.

"Wren," I said. "The palace. You came *from* the palace."

Nothing. Of course nothing. The child hadn't made a sound since the night they arrived on my doorstep speaking a language that predated language, and every attempt since to coax speech had produced exactly the same result: silence, and a look that said, with tremendous dignity, *you heard me once; be grateful.*

"I'm not angry," I lied, gently, the way you lie to a frightened animal or a business partner. "I just need to understand. There was a coronation. Years ago. Something was — sworn. And every seven years, something gets paid." I crouched down to their level, which put me eye to eye with a child who had the unnerving habit of looking *through* me rather than at me. "A tithe. A child."

Wren's expression didn't change.

But their hand did. Slowly, deliberately, they raised one small finger and pointed at their own chest.

The room went very cold, or I did; from the inside it's hard to tell the difference.

"No," I said.

They nodded.

"No," I said again, louder, because repetition has solved nearly everything in my life except the things that mattered. "No, that's — you're a child. That's the whole point of children. They're the ones the terrible things are supposed to happen *around*, not *to*. That's the entire arrangement. I've read books."

Wren blinked at me with the weary patience of someone who has also read books, better ones probably, in a language I couldn't pronounce.

I sat down on the floor. Actually sat, back against the bedframe, because my knees had filed a formal complaint. "When," I managed. "When were you supposed to be — delivered?"

Wren held up seven fingers.

"This year."

Seven fingers. Seven years. The arithmetic was simple and obscene. The chamberlain's memory had been four years old at most — I'd tasted its age the way I tasted all of them, that faint sourness of time — which meant the last renewal had come and gone. Which meant the next one was due. Which meant somewhere in Aldermont, in a palace full of glass-eyed courtiers and a king whose shadow moved a half-second late, someone was counting the days until a mute child with a word under their tongue was walked into a crown room and given to the thing that wore Aldric like a coat.

Except the child had run.

"You escaped," I said slowly, testing each word like rotten floorboards. "You ran. Before they could —" I couldn't finish it. I made myself finish it. "Before they could give you to it."

Wren's chin lifted. Just slightly. Just enough.

And there it was — not fear, which I would have known how to handle, but *defiance*, which I had no tools for whatsoever. This small person had looked at an oath old enough to have eaten kingdoms and decided, in the wordless way of the very young and the very stubborn, *no.* And then they had run north, through mountains, through weather, into a valley where magic couldn't follow — because of course, of course, the one place the crown-thing's reach went thin. They hadn't stumbled into sanctuary.

They'd *aimed* for it.

"You knew about the valley," I whispered. "You knew what it was before you ever saw it."

A shrug. Small, eloquent, infuriating.

"And the word. The thing you carry. The thing nobody dares say." I leaned forward. "Is it the unbinding? Is that what's under your tongue, Wren? Is that why they need *you* specifically — not just any child, but one who carries the way to end it?"

For a long moment the child simply looked at me. Then, with the grave deliberation they brought to everything, Wren reached out and pressed two fingers against my lips.

*I'll tell you,* the gesture said. *But not here. Not yet. Not while your hands shake.*

My hands had stopped shaking, actually. But I appreciated the sentiment, and I resented it, and both of those felt appropriate.

---

Here is what protective fury looks like on a man who has spent his life avoiding confrontation: it looks like pacing. It looks like pacing at two in the morning in a rented room while a child sleeps — or pretends to sleep, Wren's eyes were shut with theatrical precision — and the eel argument downstairs has finally collapsed into exhausted truce, and the thief walks a groove into the floorboards thinking about all the ways he has failed without knowing he was failing.

Because that was the part that kept catching in my chest like a fishhook. Not the danger — danger and I were old acquaintances; we nodded to each other at markets. It was the *ledger* of it. Every day Wren had spent in my cottage, chopping turnips badly and humming soundless hums and falling asleep by my fire, had been a day the crown-thing's people were searching. Every kindness I'd offered had been offered to a fugitive with a kingdom-sized bounty of significance, and I'd never once asked the right question, because asking questions was bad for business and I had built my whole life on not asking them.

Corvane had taught me that. *Curiosity is a tax, Ansel. Successful thieves pay it early and rarely.* Fifteen years of paying it rarely, and the bill had come due all at once, with interest, in the form of a sleeping child who was worth more to a monster than every memory I'd ever stolen put together.

And Corvane. Corvane, who had appeared at my door not three days after Wren did, asking questions with a smile on. Corvane, who wanted the child "handed over for their own safety." I had assumed greed. Greed I understood; greed was practically a colleague.

But if Corvane knew what Wren was — if Corvane was working for the palace, for the *thing* in the palace — then the fence who made me, who fed me, who gave a starving guttersnipe a trade and a name, had been reaching toward a child the way a spider reaches, and I had almost —

I stopped pacing. I stood in the middle of the cold room with my heart going like a drum in a parade and understood, with total clarity, that I would burn down every bridge I had before I let Corvane's soft voice and softer ledger anywhere near Wren.

That was new. That was terrifying. Loyalty, I had always believed, was a debt, and debts were paid in coin or memory, and here was a debt I intended to pay in something with no market value at all.

Wren opened one eye. Caught me mid-revelation. Closed it again with the air of someone who had reached the same conclusion hours ago and was waiting, graciously, for me to catch up.

"Don't be smug," I told them. "It's unbecoming."

Both eyes opened. The look they gave me could have curdled milk at forty paces.

"Yes, all right," I sighed, sitting back down on the floor. "Smugness is arguably earned. You outran an oath. I once stole a memory of a man's first kiss and he thanked me for it." I rubbed my face. "Listen to me. Tomorrow we figure out what happens next. Whether we run further, whether we — I don't know, find someone, break something, steal something enormous. But tonight—" I pointed at the bed, then at the child, in my firmest parental gesture, an impression assembled entirely from observation and guesswork, "—you sleep. Actual sleep. Not the performance you're giving me right now."

Wren lay down properly. Pulled the blanket up. Then, just before closing their eyes, they signed something — quick, fluid, a sentence I only half-followed, but the shape of it was unmistakable.

*Thank you* was in there somewhere. And so was *them*, and *coming*, and a sharp little gesture near the throat that I chose, generously, to interpret as *they will try to take me* rather than anything more specific.

"I know," I said. "Let them come. I'm very annoying when provoked. Ask anyone I've robbed."

The child smiled — actually smiled, brief and crooked, the first one I'd seen since the valley — and closed their eyes, and within minutes was breathing the slow deep breaths of the genuinely young, who can sleep beside an avalanche if the blanket is warm enough.

I did not sleep. I sat by the window with the stolen coronation memory in my lap and watched the moon move over the rooftops of Aldermont, over the palace on its hill where a king's shadow lagged behind him like a dog that had learned not to trust its master, and I counted.

Seven years since the last tithe. The chamberlain's memory was four years old. Which left — my stomach dropped through the floor and kept going — which left no time at all. Weeks, perhaps. Days. Somewhere in that glittering pile of stone, preparations were already underway. Children gathered. A ceremony rehearsed. A throne room swept and waiting.

And a queen — I'd seen her today, from across the square, silver-haired and remote, waving at crowds with a smile fixed like paint — a queen who stood beside the thing wearing her husband and said nothing.

Unless she wasn't saying nothing. Unless she was like me, all those years in the valley: content, careful, and complicit, keeping the peace by keeping her eyes down.

I didn't know. But I was going to find out, because tomorrow I was going to do the stupidest, bravest thing of my career, and that was saying something, given the competition.

I was going to walk into the palace, and I was going to start asking questions.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin over Aldermont, and with it came a knock at the door of our rented room — three soft raps, courteous as a knife laid on a table.

Wren was awake instantly, pressed against the wall, eyes wide and fixed on the door. I put one finger to my lips and crossed the room without a sound, and through the gap beneath the door I saw what I had been afraid of: the shadow of boots. Two pairs. Standing patient. Waiting.

Then a voice, muffled through the wood, warm as mulled wine and twice as poisonous:

"Ansel, dear. It's Maren. The queen. I believe we should talk about the child — and about what my husband's crown intends to do with them. And I believe," the shadow shifted, and I could have sworn I heard exhaustion underneath the silk, real exhaustion, years of it, "you are the only person in this city who might help me stop it."

I looked back at Wren. Wren looked at me.

Of all the doors in all the world, hers was the one I least expected — and the one, some traitorous instinct whispered, I should perhaps have feared most.
