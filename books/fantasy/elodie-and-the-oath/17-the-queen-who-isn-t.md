## 17. The Queen Who Isn't

There was a particular way the king's shadow moved wrong, and Maren had learned to love him anyway.

That was the sentence she would have written, if queens were permitted to write sentences like that. If queens were permitted to keep diaries at all, which they were not, because diaries were evidence, and Maren Vael-Aldermont had spent eleven years making certain there was no evidence of anything.

She stood at the window of the east gallery instead, hands folded, watching the morning procession assemble in the courtyard below. King Aldric—she still called him that in her head, because a woman had to call her husband something, and *the thing* seemed unkind even if it was accurate—sat his white horse with perfect grace. His banner-bearers fanned out behind him. His smile caught the light and held it a half-second too long, like a cup that wouldn't stop brimming.

And his shadow lagged. Just slightly. Just enough that if you knew—if you had spent eleven years learning every way a man could move so you could notice the one way he didn't—you would see the shadow arrive late to everything, a loyal servant always one step behind a master who no longer waited for anyone.

"Your Majesty." Her lady-in-waiting, Sef, appeared at her elbow with the particular softness of a woman who had learned never to startle the queen. "The king requests your presence on the dais for the Procession of Gratitude."

"Of course he does," Maren said pleasantly. "It is gratitude. One must be present for it."

Sef hesitated. "You said that last year as well, Majesty."

"Did I? How consistent of me. Consistency is the soul of dignity." She turned from the window, arranging her face into the expression the court expected: serene, faintly amused, a queen who had weathered everything and been improved by it. "Come along. If I must watch my husband be adored by ten thousand people while wearing another creature's patience, I should like to do it near the shade."

* * *

The dais was hot. The dais was always hot, because the crown-thing liked warmth the way candles liked flame—it drew crowds toward it, made them lean in, made them feel chosen. Maren sat beside her husband-who-was-not-quite and smiled at the crowd and thought about bread.

This was her secret discipline. Not prayer—prayer required believing someone was listening, and Maren had long ago concluded that whatever listened in Aldermont was not the sort of listener one prayed to. No: bread. When the horror pressed too close, when the thing wearing Aldric's hand reached over to take hers and its grip was exactly right, exactly warm, exactly a husband's grip, she thought about the baking of bread. The weighing of flour. The patience of yeast. Small, honest processes that did what they promised and nothing more.

"The harvest tithe approaches," the king said. His voice was Aldric's voice. It had been Aldric's voice for eleven years, and it still said things like *the harvest tithe approaches* with a smoothness no living man could manage, because living men found such sentences difficult and hated themselves a little for saying them.

"So it does," Maren said.

"You are fond of the children this year. The selection will please you."

"I am sure it will," said the Queen Who Wasn't Foolish Enough To Say Otherwise.

She looked out at the crowd. Ten thousand faces, upturned, grateful. That was the part no one warned you about—not the monster, but the gratitude. Eleven years ago the kingdom had been dying. She remembered it properly; that was her curse, remembering properly. The blight in the wheat, the fever in the low towns, the borders shrinking like a wound closing in reverse. And then the coronation, and the Oath, and the morning after, when the fields stood green and the fevers broke and the whole city wept with relief.

The price had been written in a contract nobody read. Or rather, everybody read it, and everybody agreed it was terrible, and everybody agreed it was necessary, and then everybody arranged never to think of it again, and the kingdom's collective forgetting had been so thorough and so willing that Maren sometimes wondered whether the crown-thing had even needed magic to accomplish it. People were very good at not knowing things they preferred not to know.

She had preferred not to know, once. For about four days. Then she had walked into the king's chambers at midnight and found him sitting upright in bed, staring at his own hands, whispering *what am I, what am I, what am I* in a voice that frayed at the edges of itself—and by dawn he had smoothed over again, glassy and gracious, and the thing inside him had looked at her with Aldric's eyes and said, *You saw nothing, my love. It is kinder this way.*

And she had agreed. That was the rot of it. Not the creature—the agreement. She had kissed its cheek and said, *Yes. Kinder,* and gone back to bed, and slept, because exhaustion is a country all its own and she had been living there ever since.

"Majesty?" Sef again, at her shoulder, with watered wine. "You've stopped smiling."

"Have I?" Maren took the wine. "How careless. Thank you, Sef."

Below, the procession moved. Children scattered flowers. Somewhere a choir sang the Hymn of Renewal, which had new verses now, cheerful ones, about abundance and protection, and none of the singers knew what the renewal renewed.

Maren drank her wine and thought about bread, and did not weep, because queens do not weep on daises, and because she had used up all her weeping somewhere around year six and had never found a shop that sold more.

* * *

It was three days later, in the library, that the trouble began.

Not the great trouble—that came later, wearing different clothes—but the first small trouble, the kind that starts as an itch and ends as an avalanche.

Maren went to the library most evenings. It was the one room in the palace where the crown-thing rarely followed her; it had no interest in books, having read all of them already, or perhaps having written all of them already, or perhaps simply finding paper dull compared to the taste of consent. Whatever the reason, the library was hers, and she guarded that hour the way misers guard coins.

She was reading—a history of the First Kingdoms, badly translated, delightfully inaccurate—when she heard the step.

Not Sef's step. Not a guard's. A light step, quick, wrong-footed, the step of someone moving through a palace as though the palace were a market they intended to shoplift from.

Maren closed her book without hurrying. She had learned that hurrying told people things.

The intruder came around the shelf of genealogies: a slight figure in a broker's grey coat, hood back, young-faced and sharp-eyed, with the particular stillness of a person who has just realized they have been seen and is deciding how much that matters.

A memory-broker. Maren knew the type—or knew the rumor of the type. They drifted through the capital in festival seasons, selling recollections in little vials and locket-glasses. *Relive your wedding day. Taste your mother's cooking again.* Half of them frauds, all of them illegal, and none of them, as far as Maren knew, supposed to be in the royal library at midnight lifting things off shelves.

Except the figure wasn't lifting anything off shelves. The figure was standing very still, looking at Maren with an expression Maren recognized intimately, because she wore it herself in mirrors: the look of someone calculating whether a door is worth going through.

"You're a long way from the festival quarter," Maren said mildly.

"I'm lost," said the broker.

"In the royal library. Past two locked gates and a sleeping guard."

"The guard was very deeply asleep."

"He was drugged. I pay for the drug myself; it's cheaper than paying for gossip." Maren set down her book. "You have clever hands, broker. I watched you come around that shelf ready to talk your way out, and you haven't yet decided what the talking is. Most liars decide faster. You're either very good or very new."

Something flickered across the young face—wry, tired, startled at being read. "Very tired," the broker offered.

"Ah. The third option. The honest one." Maren rose. Up close the broker smelled faintly of road dust and something else, something odd and clean, like rain on stone that had never been rained on. "Give me one reason I shouldn't call the guard."

"I could give you several," the broker said. "Most of them lies. But here's one that isn't: you don't want the guard. Guards ask questions in daylight, and daylight questions have answers, and some answers in this palace would be—" the broker paused, choosing with visible care— "*inconvenient* for everyone. Including you. Especially you."

The library went very quiet. Somewhere below, a clock counted the hour with the smugness clocks reserve for being right.

Maren felt the old exhaustion settle over her like a familiar coat. Eleven years of pretending had given her many skills, but the chiefest among them was recognizing pretense in others, and this young person was pretending hard at being merely a thief. Thieves did not look at queens with grief. Thieves did not glance, twice, at the sealed cabinet against the far wall—the one holding the king's private records—with an expression like hunger.

"You know what's in that cabinet," Maren said.

"I know what I've been told might be in it."

"And who told you?"

"A friend," said the broker, which meant an enemy, or a debt, or both. "Look—Your Majesty. I'll go. Quietly, the way I came. Nobody has to know I was here, and believe me, nobody knowing things is a specialty of mine."

It was, Maren thought, the most honest sentence spoken in this palace in a decade.

She should have let the child—because that's what the broker was, under the grey coat and the sharp eyes; a child playing adult with remarkable commitment—she should have let the child slip away and returned to her book and her bread-thoughts and her beautiful, suffocating peace.

Instead she heard herself say: "What is your name?"

The broker's jaw tightened. "Ansel."

"Just Ansel?"

"Just Ansel." A beat. "Names are inventory, Majesty. You learn not to carry more than you need."

Maren studied the young face a moment longer. There was something underneath it—some gap, some absence shaped like a person—that made her chest ache in a way she hadn't permitted since year six. She knew absences. She had married one.

"Ansel," she said. "You are going to get yourself killed in this palace. Not by guards. Guards are simple; guards can be bribed. You'll be killed by *politeness*, by courtiers smiling at you, by invitations you shouldn't accept, by a king who will take your hand and tell you that you're welcome here, and mean it, in the way a spider means welcome." She stepped closer. "So before you go—before you vanish back into whatever gutter raised you so carefully—answer me one question, and answer it truly, because I will know if you don't. I have had eleven years of practice knowing."

"What question?"

"Why did you really come here?"

And Ansel—for one unguarded moment, one crack in the broker's careful mask—looked at the sealed cabinet again, and then at Maren, and the wryness fell away entirely, and what remained was fury. Cold, banked, patient fury, the kind that keeps a person walking for weeks.

"For the children," Ansel said quietly. "The ones gathered in the square at the next renewal. I want to know where they go. And I think you know too. I think you've known for eleven years, and I think you're so tired of knowing that you drugged your own guard just to have one hour where nobody can see your face."

The clock ticked. The candle fluttered. Maren stood very still, and felt the strange sensation—unfamiliar, almost painful—of being *seen*. Truly seen, by a stranger, in a palace full of people who had perfected the art of looking through her.

She could deny it. Of course she could. Denial was her native tongue; she spoke it more fluently than the court language.

But she was so tired. Gods, she was so tired, and here stood a furious child with clever hands and honest eyes, asking the one question no one had asked aloud in eleven years, and some long-buried piece of Maren Vael-Aldermont—the girl she'd been before the crown, before the Oath, before the arithmetic—stirred in its grave and said, *finally.*

"Sit down, Ansel," said the Queen Who Isn't, and heard her own voice change—drop the silk, drop the serenity, become for the first time in years simply a woman speaking in a quiet room. "Before you take one more step in this palace, you and I are going to have a conversation. And I warn you now: it will cost us both."

Ansel hesitated. Then—whether from curiosity, or exhaustion, or the same buried instinct that makes drowning people grab at ropes—they sat.

Maren rang a small bell for Sef, and when her lady-in-waiting appeared, she said only, "Bring the dark gallery key, and bring the good wine. We'll be some time."

Sef's eyes widened fractionally—the dark gallery, the sealed records, the *good* wine, all in one breath—but eleven years of service had taught her that questions were a luxury. She bowed and withdrew.

Maren turned back to the thief at her table, and found herself almost smiling. It felt strange on her face, like a shoe kept in a box too long.

"Now," she said. "Tell me everything you think you know. And then, Ansel, I will tell you what you actually know—which is less, and worse, and begins with a bargain I made eleven years ago that I have regretted every single day since."

Outside, far to the north, thunder rolled over hills that neither of them was looking at, and neither of them noticed the storm coming—neither the one in the sky, nor the older, quieter one already walking toward the palace with a mute child's hand in theirs.
