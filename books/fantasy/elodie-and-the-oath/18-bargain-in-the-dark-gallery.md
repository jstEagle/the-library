## 18. Bargain in the Dark Gallery

Queen Maren had learned long ago that the most dangerous room in a palace is never the throne room. The throne room was theater—everyone arrived knowing their lines, and the crown-thing on her husband's head performed its part beautifully. No. The dangerous rooms were the ones where no one was watching.

The Long Gallery at midnight, for instance. Forty paces of dark oak paneling hung with the portraits of dead Aldrics, lit by a single lamp she carried herself, because she had dismissed the footmen an hour ago with the lie that she wished to be alone with her grief. Grief was useful that way. Nobody questioned a grieving queen who wanted darkness.

She heard him before she saw him—the soft scuff of boots that belonged to someone pretending not to walk like a thief. Thieves, in her experience, walked *loudly* when they were trying to be quiet, the way liars blinked too much. She had married a kingdom full of both.

"You're late," she said to the shadows between the portraits of Aldric the Third and Aldric the Fourth.

The shadow swore, quietly and with feeling. Then it resolved into a person: narrow, travel-worn, dressed in the gray-and-silver of a minor memory-broker's guild livery that Maren knew had been stolen off a line in Tallow Street three days ago. Ansel Thackeray. Or whatever name they were using this week. The palace spies called them "the broker from the valley," which was accurate as far as it went, the way calling a wolf "a large dog" is accurate.

"You dismissed your guards," Ansel said. Their voice was steady, but their hand had found the knife at their belt anyway, out of what looked like habit rather than intent. "That's either very trusting or very stupid."

"I've been married to a monster for eleven years. I've had time to calibrate my stupidity." Maren lifted the lamp so the light fell properly across her own face, letting them see her expression: tired, unamused, and entirely without fear. Fear was currency here, and she refused to pay it. "You've been in my husband's study. Twice."

"I've been in many studies."

"The second time you left through the window. In the rain." She paused, savoring it. "You broke a rosebush. It was planted at my coronation. I have been informed of its death in detail by the head gardener, who wept."

Something flickered across the thief's face—not guilt exactly, more the particular discomfort of a professional being reviewed by a client. "I'll replace the rosebush."

"With what money? You paid the ferryman on the Vell with a stranger's wedding day. I know about that too." Maren began walking, slowly, down the gallery, and did not check whether they followed. They would follow. Curiosity was the one lock thieves could never resist picking. "You should know that I have spent eleven years learning everything that happens in this palace, including the things that happen to people who think no one sees them. Especially those."

* * *

The gallery portraits watched them pass, as portraits do. Maren had spent years of her marriage talking to these paintings in her head—Aldric the Second, stern and kind-eyed; Aldric the Fifth, who had died young and smiled like he knew something; and there, at the gallery's end, the newest portrait, painted four years ago. Her husband's face rendered in oils, glassy and perfect and utterly wrong, because the painter had worked from life, and life by then was already wearing the crown-thing like a mask worn so long the face beneath had begun to forget its own shape.

She stopped beneath it. She made herself stop beneath it every time she came this way. That was the discipline. You did not look away from what you had allowed.

"Do you know why I summoned you?" she asked.

"Because I'm good at my work and you need something stolen."

"Because you're good at your work," Maren agreed, "and I need something *found*. There is a difference, though I suspect only three people in this kingdom understand it, and one of them is me." She turned. The thief stood a few paces off, lamplight catching the wary set of their shoulders. Beside them—half-hidden behind them, the way small creatures hide behind large ones—stood the child.

Wren. Maren let herself look at the child directly, because looking away from children was how all of this had started. Eleven years ago, seven years ago, two years ago. Every renewal. Every tithe. Every small face assembled in the square while she stood on the balcony in her ceremonial gray and told herself that a queen's first duty was peace, that the arithmetic was terrible but the arithmetic was *correct*, that if she wept privately enough it balanced the ledger somehow.

It did not balance. She had checked.

The child stared back at her with eyes that seemed to hold weather. Then Wren did something that made Maren's breath catch: raised one small hand and touched their own throat, then pointed at Maren, then touched their throat again. A question. *Do you have words? Are any of yours still yours?*

"No," Maren said softly, answering the question rather than the gesture. "Not many. But some. I keep them like coins in a boot, where the crown can't count them."

Ansel shifted, placing themselves slightly between queen and child—an instinct, clearly, unexamined, the way one flinches from a stove. Maren catalogued it. Protective fury was listed in her husband's journals as the great enemy of careful plans, and also, she suspected, the only thing that had ever actually saved anyone.

"You have the journals," Ansel said. Not a question. Their eyes had gone sharp, broker-sharp, reading her the way they read a mark. "You caught me going through his study, and instead of calling the guards, you're standing in a dark gallery with one lamp, offering—" they gestured at her, at the portraits, at the whole gilded silence "—whatever this is."

"This is an offer, yes." Maren set the lamp on its bracket beside Aldric the Fifth's smiling portrait. "But before I make it, you're going to answer one question honestly, and I will know if you don't, because I have had eleven years of practice telling lies from truth and I am very, very good at it now. It is the one skill my marriage has given me, and I intend to get value from it."

She stepped closer. Close enough to see that the thief's eyes were the color of the valley—gray-green, the color of a place that has forgotten what color means.

"When you read my husband's journals," she said. "When you found the pages inside the pages, the pleas written into the margins of his own memories—what did you feel?"

Silence. The lamp flame ticked against its glass.

"Pity," Ansel said finally. "And horror. And—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "And recognition. He writes like someone who remembers being free and can't prove it. I know what that's like."

Maren closed her eyes for one heartbeat. Two.

There it was. The thing she had needed to hear, the thing the crown-thing could never have faked and even a skilled liar would have dressed up differently. Pity first. Pity before advantage. She opened her eyes and felt something in her chest loosen that had been clenched since the last renewal, when a girl named Sef had been taken with a ribbon still in her hair.

"Then we can bargain," Maren said. "Come. Walk with me. Both of you." She resumed down the gallery, past the dead kings, toward the plain door at the end that everyone assumed led to a linen closet, because she had spread that rumor herself eight years ago and it remained one of the best investments of her reign.

* * *

The room beyond the door had been her mother-in-law's sewing room once—a small square chamber with one window overlooking the inner court, chosen precisely because it overlooked nothing anyone wanted. Now it held a writing desk, a cold stove, and a locked iron chest that Maren unlocked with a key she wore on a cord around her neck, under her clothes, where even her ladies-in-waiting assumed it was a locket holding a portrait of a dead sister.

In a sense it was.

"Eleven years ago," Maren said, laying her hands flat on the chest, "my husband rode out to meet the Blight at the border of the Weald, because the Blight was eating the eastern provinces down to the stones, and the mages said nothing could stop it, and the king said *then I will go and argue with it personally*, because that was the kind of man he was. Stubborn. Ridiculous. He used to steal honey cakes off his own banquet tables and blame the dog. We didn't have a dog."

Ansel made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"He came back alive," Maren continued, "which the mages called impossible, and the kingdom called miraculous, and I—" her voice did something she hated, wobbled, and she flattened it by force, "—I believed for six days. Six days I believed. Then I saw his shadow move a half-second after he did. Then I noticed he no longer stole honey cakes. Then I found him standing in the orchard at three in the morning, perfectly still, in the rain, and when I touched his shoulder he turned to me with my husband's face and said, in my husband's voice, *Maren, lovely Maren, come inside, you'll catch your death,* and there was nothing behind the words. Nothing. Like knocking on a beautiful door and having it open onto a wall."

She unlocked the chest. Inside lay journals—dozens of them, leather-bound, water-stained, some with clasps of silver and some tied shut with plain string—and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of state papers sealed with the privy mark.

"The official records of the renewals are kept by the Lord Chamberlain. Those are lies, carefully kept. These are the true ones. My husband wrote them—or the piece of him that remains wrote them, in the cracks, in the margins, wherever the crown-thing does not think to look, because it cannot imagine that a man might hide the truth inside his own memories. It eats memories, you see. It assumes no one else values them enough to bury treasure in them."

Ansel stood very still, the way people stand before things they want too much. "You're giving me the king's private papers."

"I am offering to lend them to you. Tonight. Here, in this room, where the walls are stone two feet thick and I have personally inspected them for listening charms, which is a sentence I never imagined saying aloud." Maren drew out the top journal and set it on the desk. "You will read. You will find what you're looking for—I don't know precisely what that is, but I know it involves the old language, and the child, and the word no one dares speak. And tomorrow night you will return the journals to this chest, and then you will leave my palace and take the child far away from the renewal, and you will succeed or die trying somewhere I don't have to watch."

"That's a generous bargain," Ansel said slowly, "for a queen who's kept the peace for eleven years by doing nothing."

The words landed where they were aimed. Maren let them. She owed herself that sting, at minimum.

"I did not do nothing," she said. "I did worse than nothing. I did *arithmetic*." She met their eyes. "Every seven years, the crown-thing comes to me—it always comes to me, never him, it enjoys that—and it tells me the number. How many children. And I sit at my desk and I think: if I refuse, it takes the whole valley next, then the Weald, then the coast towns, and the Blight comes back, and ten thousand die instead of nine. Nine instead of ten thousand. That is the sum. I have done that sum eleven times, and every time the sum says *yes*, and every time I say yes, and every time I tell myself a queen must be able to carry that."

Her hands, she noticed, had gone white on the edge of the desk.

"But last spring I watched a woman in the market whose son was taken two renewals ago. She doesn't mourn him—she can't, the tithe erases them from the remembering, that's part of the working, did you know that? The parents wake and the child's crib is empty and there is a hole in their hearts shaped like a name they can't find. She sells apples. She stops sometimes, mid-transaction, and looks at her hands like they've failed her, because something in her knows she's lost something enormous and the world insists she hasn't. And I thought: I built that. Me. With my sums." 

She straightened. "So. Yes. I am done being careful, and I am done being complicit, and you are the first weapon I have ever found that might actually cut. Which brings us to my condition."

"There it is," Ansel murmured. "There's always a condition."

"There is always a condition," Maren agreed. "Mine is simple and it is absolute and if you break it I will feed you to my husband's shadow myself, and I say that with love." She took a breath. "Aldric must never learn what I allowed."

The thief blinked. "The king? Never learn—that you helped us?"

"That I helped *anyone*. That I knew. That for eleven years I sat at table with the thing wearing him and passed the salt and signed the renewal orders and did my sums, and that when salvation finally came it came because a memory-thief and a mute child did what I should have done a decade ago." Her voice cracked along its length and she let it, here, in the stone room, in front of the only two witnesses who would never repeat it. "He must never know. Do you understand? When he comes back—if he comes back—he will be a man waking from a nightmare eleven years long, and he will ask what happened to his kingdom, and I will have to live with whatever I tell him. But he cannot know that his wife fed the fire to keep the house warm. He was the one person in this world who believed I was better than I am, and I cannot—"

She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth briefly. Took her hand away.

"I cannot bear to lose the only person who thinks well of me," she finished, dryly. "There. That's the shameful truth of it. Queen Maren of Aldermont, protector of the realm, terrified of her husband's good opinion. Write it in your ledger."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Wren stepped forward—past Ansel, past caution—and tugged at Maren's sleeve. The queen looked down. The child studied her face with those weather-holding eyes, then reached up and pressed one small palm flat against Maren's sternum, over the heart, and spoke.

Three syllables. Soft ones. The old language, the dead tongue, and the stones of the wall hummed faintly in sympathy like struck glass, and warmth spread through Maren's chest—not magic, or not only magic, but the specific heat of being *seen*, completely, by someone who had every reason to hate her.

"What did you say?" she whispered.

"She said you're not the worst thing in this room," Ansel translated quietly. "And that the word for 'queen' in the old tongue is the same as the word for 'the one who stays.' " A pause. "I don't think it was a compliment. I don't think it was an insult either. I think it was just... true."

Maren laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound, the first real laugh the palace had wrung out of her in years. "Eleven years of courtiers flattering me," she said, "and the honest assessment comes from a child who can't lie because lying requires a language the rest of us use." She wiped her eyes briskly with the heel of her hand. "Fine. Fine. Take the journals. Read. I'll return at the third bell with food—the cook makes a honey cake that His Majesty used to steal, and I find I've developed a spiteful appetite for them lately."

She swept the journals into Ansel's arms, blew out nothing because there was nothing to blow out, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch.

"Broker," she said, not turning around. "One more thing, gratis, from a woman who has watched monsters negotiate for a decade. When the crown-thing offers you a bargain—and it will, soon, because it can smell what you're carrying—it will offer you exactly what you most want, priced at exactly what you least suspect. My husband wanted to save his kingdom. It charged him himself." 

She opened the door onto the dark gallery, where the painted kings smiled their painted smiles.

"Don't pay it," Maren said, and was gone, her footsteps fading down forty paces of portraits, leaving the thief and the child alone with the truth of a kingdom, stacked in leather and string, waiting to be read.

Ansel looked down at the top journal. Looked at Wren. The child nodded once, gravely, the way small generals approve campaigns.

Somewhere above them, muffled through two feet of stone, the palace bells began to ring the hour—and beneath the bells, faint and wrong, came a sound Maren would not hear until morning, and would spend the rest of her life regretting: the slow, patient scrape of the crown-thing turning its attention, like a lighthouse, toward the sewing room window.

It had smelled the journals opening. And it had begun, at last, to count its enemies.
