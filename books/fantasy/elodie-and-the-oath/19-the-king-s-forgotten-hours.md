## 19. The King's Forgotten Hours

The key Maren gave me was small and cold and heavier than it had any right to be.

"You'll have one night," she had said, pressing it into my palm in the dark gallery, her gloved fingers not quite touching mine. "The king's private study is behind the third door past the tapestry of the Hunt. No guards. I've seen to that. But Ansel—" she had caught my sleeve, and her voice had dropped to something almost human, almost frightened, "—whatever you find in there, you must understand that I could not stop it. Whatever he wrote, whatever he begged for—I could not stop it."

I had promised her nothing. Promises are inventory, and I'd learned long ago never to give away stock for free.

Now the third door past the tapestry of the Hunt stood before me, and the tapestry itself watched with its woven hounds and its woven riders, all of them frozen mid-gallop toward a stag that had, if you looked closely, been stitched with its head turned backward—as though even the artist's prey had known something the hunters didn't.

"Cheerful décor," I murmured to myself, because talking to oneself is a habit of thieves who spend too many hours alone, and because in a palace where shadows move a half-second late, a familiar voice—even your own—is a comfort.

The lock turned with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The king's private study was smaller than I expected. Palaces lie about size the way merchants lie about weight; every room in Aldermont was built to seem like something more than it was. But this room was honest. A desk scarred with ink rings. A chair worn pale at the arms where hands had gripped it, year after year, while its owner read or wrote or simply sat and tried to remember how to be a man. A window overlooking the inner garden, shuttered now. And shelves—shelves upon shelves of books, most of them unremarkable treatises on drainage law and grain tariffs, because kings must pretend to be bored before they're permitted to be anything else.

But beneath the window, set into the stone floor, was an iron box.

It wasn't hidden. That was the first strange thing. Anyone could have found it. It sat there in plain sight, plain iron, with a lock so old the mechanism had rusted into a kind of stubborn honesty—it opened when I pressed the key Maren had given me, which meant she had known about it, which meant she had chosen this box for me specifically, out of everything in the sealed study.

Inside were journals. Forty-one of them, bound in leather gone soft as old skin, stacked in chronological order with a care that bordered on devotion. The earliest was dated thirty-five years ago—the year of Aldric's coronation. The year the kingdom should have died.

I pulled out the first journal and began to read.

* * *

*They tell me I am saved,* the young king had written, in a hand still eager, still round with youth. *The Oath has taken my sickness and my debts and the famine from the eastern fields. Father would have wept to see the granaries full. I only wish I remembered agreeing to it. There is a gap in my evening—a hole shaped like an hour—and everyone smiles at me across it as though the hour were still there.*

I turned pages. The handwriting changed over the years—grew thinner, then cramped, then strangely careful, like a man writing while someone watched over his shoulder. Because someone was.

*Year three. I lost today again. I know I lost because I found mud on my boots and no memory of the garden. Wren—*

I stopped. Read it again.

*Wren came to me in the corridor and said, "Papa, you went away." And I said, "Where did I go?" and she said, "You don't come back all the way anymore."*

My chest did something complicated. I set the journal down on the desk and looked at the shuttered window for a while, at the dust motes hanging in the moonlight like a held note.

Wren. His daughter. Of course. The child who carried the unbinding word, who fled the palace, who had appeared soaked and silent at my cottage door in the valley that forgets—she hadn't materialized from nowhere. She had run from *this*. From a father who kept going away and coming back wrong.

I picked up the journal again, because grief is a luxury and I was on a schedule.

* * *

By the tenth journal, the entries had changed character entirely. The early ones were a man's diary—complaints about ministers, joy at his daughter's first words, sorrow at his queen's long silences. But somewhere around year seven, the writing became something else. Something deliberate.

He had started hiding things inside his own memories.

Not in the way I hide things—false bottoms, hollow heels, a memory folded small and tucked behind a more boring one. Aldric had no such craft left to him; the crown-thing saw everything he saw, felt everything he felt, and I understood now that it *tasted* his recollections the way I tasted travelers'. It fed on what he remembered. So he couldn't hide the truth anywhere the thing might look.

So instead, he hid it in plain sight. He wrote his pleas *as memories*. He recorded ordinary days—grain shipments, a feast, a diplomatic visit—in exhaustive, tedious detail, and buried inside the tedium, in sentences designed to look like a tired man's rambling, he spoke directly to whoever might someday read these pages.

*The miller's second son has a limp but a strong back, and I have put him to work at the north granary, and I want whoever finds this to know that I did not choose this. I want you to know that on the night of the harvest festival I stood in the square and laughed at the jugglers and meant none of it, and the thing wearing my face laughed with me, and I was screaming underneath, and if you are reading this then some part of me got out, some part of me is still getting out, night after night, onto these pages.*

*Please.*

*Please break the Oath.*

*I do not remember what the Oath says. It will not let me remember. But I remember that it can be broken, because hope is the one thing it cannot digest—I have tested this for years. It eats my birthdays. It eats my mother's face. It eats the sound of my daughter laughing. But it cannot eat the hope that someone will read this and act. I have fed it that hope every day and it chokes on it every day, and that, friend stranger, is my small victory, and I cling to it like a drowning man clings to driftwood, except my driftwood is made of spite, which floats surprisingly well.*

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, startled, and then it turned into something else halfway through, and I had to sit down in the king's worn chair and press the heel of my hand against my eyes for a moment.

Because that was the heartbreak of it, laid bare in ink: he was *funny*. Thirty-five years devoured from the inside, and the man's humor had survived like a flower growing through a crack in a flagstone. He joked because joking was rebellion. He was tedious on purpose, page after page of grain tariffs and drainage law, because boredom was camouflage, because the crown-thing skimmed and grew fat and lazy on the dull parts, and in the dull parts, Aldric lived.

*If you are reading this,* he wrote in the twenty-second journal, *you are either a thief, my wife, or my daughter grown. In any case, welcome. Mind the ink, it smears. And know this: I consented once, in an hour I cannot recall, to a bargain I cannot name, and I have spent every day since trying to unsay it. If you can find the terms—if you can find the original memory of the swearing—then the whole rotten edifice comes down. An oath is only as strong as the remembering of it. Unmake the memory, unmake the chain.*

*And if you cannot find it—*

*Then at least tell Wren her father loved her. Tell her that the parts of me that went away were never the parts that loved her. Those parts stayed. They are staying still. They are the last thing the thing in my crown cannot reach, because I built a little room around them, deep down, and I nailed the door shut from the inside, and in that room it is always the evening she said her first word, and I am always hearing it for the first time.*

I closed the journal and held it against my chest like something wounded.

* * *

There was a knock at the study door. One soft rap.

I was on my feet with a letter opener in my hand before my mind caught up with my body—old habits, good habits—and then a voice came through the wood, low and dry: "It's me. Don't stab the queen, it reflects poorly on both of us."

Maren slipped inside, hooded, carrying a candle she didn't light. She looked at the open iron box, at the journals spread across the desk, at my face.

"You found them," she said. Not a question.

"I found him," I corrected. "Or what's left of him. He's been writing to whoever would listen for thirty-five years. He's been begging." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Did you know? Did you ever read these?"

She stood very still. "Once. Early. Year four." Her jaw tightened. "I read one entry and I never opened them again, because if I read them all, I would have had to do something, and doing something would have killed him. Do you understand? The Oath renews through *breach*, not through ignorance. As long as no one moved against it, Aldric lived. Miserably. Half-devoured. But alive." She looked at the journals with an expression I recognized—I'd seen it in my own mirror for years, the look of a person who has made a cage out of their own caution and named the cage mercy. "So I let him write. I let him beg into the dark. It was the only kindness I could afford."

"And the tithe children," I said quietly. "Was that also a kindness you could afford?"

Her face went white. "That was survival. There is a difference, and I have spent thirty years telling myself the difference is larger than it is." She crossed to the window and unlatched the shutters an inch, letting in a knife of moonlight. "What did he write? At the end. What did he say?"

I hesitated. Some things feel like theft even when they're freely given. But she was his wife, and she had earned the wound.

"He wrote instructions," I said. "Find the original memory of the Oath. Unmake it. He says an oath dies when its remembering does." I paused. "And he wrote that he loves his daughter, and that the loving part of him never left."

For a long moment Maren didn't move. Then she made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob, something that had been waiting thirty years in her throat for permission—and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"The original memory," she whispered. "He thinks it can be found."

"Can it?"

"It was taken from him the night of the coronation. Sealed away. I don't know where. The crown-thing keeps it close—it *is* the Oath, in a sense, the living record of the swearing. To unmake it..." She shook her head slowly. "You would have to take it from the thing itself. Or find where it was first made." Her eyes came up to mine, sharp suddenly, queenly. "Why do you ask, memory-thief? What do you know about making oaths?"

And I thought of the valley. The magicless valley, the wound in the world, the place where magic could not follow—where, I now suspected, something enormous had once been sworn, and the ground itself had flinched away from the remembering of it. Where a mute child had arrived speaking a dead tongue. Where my own oldest stolen memory had a gap shaped exactly like me.

"I think," I said carefully, "that I may know where oaths go to be forgotten."

Maren studied me for a long time. Outside, somewhere across the sleeping city, a bell tolled the third hour, and I counted the seconds afterward out of habit, watching for the shadow that lagged—but the gallery beyond the door was empty and punctual, for now.

"One night becomes two," she said at last. "Read the rest. All forty-one. If he spent thirty-five years hiding a way out in those pages, I want it found. And Ansel—" she paused at the door, her hand on the latch, not looking at me, "—when you see my husband next, when you see the thing wearing him... try to remember that there's a man in a little room inside it, listening to his daughter say her first word, forever. Be gentle with what's left of him."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with forty-one journals and a candle I finally lit.

* * *

I read until my eyes burned. Journal thirty. Journal thirty-five. The handwriting deteriorating, then rallying, then deteriorating again, a tide chart of a soul. And in the fortieth journal, dated just two years ago, I found the entry that stopped my breath:

*Something new. A whisper in the little room. The thing is afraid—I can taste its fear, it tastes like copper and cold iron. It fears a word. It has begun hunting for a child, and I do not know which child, but I know the fear is old, older than the Oath itself, older than the kingdom. Whatever word it fears, it fears it in a language I have never heard and yet somehow recognize, the way you recognize a lullaby from before you could speak.*

*If the child exists—if it reaches the child first—God help us all. But if someone else reaches the child first—*

*Friend stranger. Thief. Wife. Whoever you are.*

*Find the child. Find the word. Find the valley where the stones forgot how to sing.*

*I think that is where they buried the beginning.*

I sat back. The candle guttered. Somewhere far below, in the undercrofts of the palace, I heard—or imagined I heard—a door closing, slow and heavy, and footsteps that did not echo quite when they should have.

And beneath the footfalls, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, came a voice I knew. Smooth. Patient. Warm as a fence's handshake.

Corvane had come to court.

I snuffed the candle, gathered the journals back into their iron box—except one, the fortieth, which I tucked inside my coat against my heart, where stolen things belong—and moved to the window as the study door below creaked open, and a voice like old honey said pleasantly:

"Now then. I'm told the queen has been leaving interesting doors unlocked."
