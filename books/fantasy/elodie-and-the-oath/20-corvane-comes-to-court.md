## 20. Corvane Comes to Court

The invitation arrived on silver paper.

Not parchment—silver, thin as a moth's wing, with letters pressed into it rather than written, so that they caught the light and seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at them. It had been delivered by a page who wouldn't meet my eyes, which in Aldermont was less an insult than a survival strategy. Courtiers here had learned early that eye contact was a currency, and most of them were broke.

*The Lord Corvane requests the pleasure of Master Thackeray's company at evening wine,* it said. *Third gallery, east wing. Come alone.*

I read it four times, standing in my borrowed rooms with Wren asleep under three blankets on the window seat, and each time the words rearranged themselves slightly, like a card sharp shuffling a deck you'd already counted.

"Come alone," I said aloud, to no one. "He says that like I've ever done anything alone in my life."

Wren stirred but didn't wake. The child slept the way soldiers sleep—one hand curled around something invisible, ready to hold on to it if the world tried to take it away. Since the undercrofts, since the words that bent stone at the cost of years, Wren had been fading. Not visibly. Not in any way a physician could name. But there was a lightness to the child now, a translucence around the edges, as though the world had begun to forget Wren before Wren had finished being in it.

I tucked the blanket higher and went down to meet the man who'd taught me everything I knew about taking things that didn't belong to me.

* * *

The third gallery of the east wing was the kind of room built by people who wanted history to know they'd had money. Portraits lined the walls—kings, mostly, and one dog that had apparently earned its place through sheer excellence of jowls. Candles burned in sconces shaped like swans, and the wine on the table was older than either of us.

Corvane stood with his back to the door, studying a portrait of King Aldric's grandfather, and for one absurd moment I felt seven years old again, waiting outside a fence's back room while he decided whether my stolen goods were worth feeding me for.

"You've gotten taller," he said without turning. "Or the valley shrunk things. Hard to say."

"Corvane."

"Thackeray." He turned then, and smiled, and the smile was exactly as I remembered it: warm at the edges, cold at the center, like a hearth built over a well. He looked good. That was the worst part. Twenty years of trading in other people's pasts should have left marks, and instead Corvane looked like a man who'd just come back from a holiday he'd thoroughly enjoyed. "You look terrible. Have you been eating? Don't answer. Sit. Drink. This vintage predates the Oath, which means it's the last honest thing in this building."

"I don't drink with brokers."

"You drank with me for fifteen years." He poured two glasses anyway, because Corvane had never once accepted an answer as final. "Though I suppose technically you drank with a fence. Brokers are what we become when we move up in the world." He slid a glass toward me. "And I have moved up, Thackeray. You should see the commissions."

I didn't sit. I didn't drink. I stood in the doorway like a fool with a knife he wasn't sure how to hold, and Corvane watched me do it with the patient amusement of a man watching weather.

"The crown-thing sent for you," I said.

"The crown-thing." He tasted the words like the wine. "You know, in thirty years of this trade, I've learned one rule above all others: never use the client's name wrong. It's not a thing. It's not a creature wearing a king. It is an *Oath*, given flesh, and it has better manners than anyone in this palace. Call it what it is or it will notice, and it notices everything." He lifted his glass. "It noticed you, by the way. Weeks ago. It knows about the child. It knows about the journals. It knows you've been reading the king's sealed memories like bedtime stories."

My hand found the knife at my belt without my permission.

"Oh, put that away." Corvane waved at it fondly. "You got that from me too. Second-hand, badly balanced, overpriced. Everything you are, you got from me, and none of it works against me because I built all your habits myself." He finally sat, folding himself into a chair with the ease of a man settling into his own skin after a long trip. "Sit down, Thackeray. Please. I came a long way, and I'd rather not deliver bad news standing up. Bad news travels better seated. Ask any priest."

I sat. I didn't touch the wine. These felt like victories.

* * *

"You want the child," I said.

"I want what I've always wanted," Corvane said. "A clean transaction. You have something the client needs. The client pays. Everyone walks away richer, or at least walking." He turned his glass slowly, watching the candlelight swim in it. "The tithe is due in nine days. Seven children, taken to the crown room, given to the Oath, and the kingdom gets another seven years of not dying. Drought ends. Plague passes. Borders hold. It's a good bargain, honestly. The best bargain anyone ever struck. The only problem with it is the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic."

"Seven children every seven years." He said it the way a merchant says a price he's quoted ten thousand times—flat, practiced, worn smooth. "Two hundred and forty-one children since the coronation. I should know. I've brokered a hundred and ninety of them."

The room went very quiet. Even the candles seemed to lean in.

"You—" My voice came out wrong, thin, like someone else's. "The tithe-children are gathered by the crown's men. Soldiers. Priests."

"Soldiers gather them. Priests bless them. And a broker sells them." He shrugged, and the shrug was the same shrug he'd used over fenced lockets and forged deeds, the shrug that said *the world is what it is*. "Someone has to find them. Someone has to value them. A peasant's brat and a duke's bastard aren't worth the same to the Oath—it prefers children with old blood, old names, old *memories*. Memory is the coin, Thackeray. You know that better than anyone. The Oath doesn't eat flesh. It eats everything a child remembers, and then it eats the child, and the kingdom gets to keep its harvests." He smiled again, gently. "I find them. I appraise them. I sell them. It's the same trade you practice at the valley's border, just with a longer ledger."

"That is not the same trade."

"No?" He tilted his head. "You lift memories from travelers and sell them back. I lift children from mothers and sell them forward. We both call it commerce. We both sleep at night. Well—I sleep at night. You look like you've stopped."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply reported for duty. "I'm going to kill you."

"You're going to try, and it's going to embarrass us both." But he said it without heat, almost kindly, and gestured me back down with two fingers. "Sit. There's more, and you're going to want to be sitting for it. Trust me. I've delivered this particular piece of news twice before, and both times the recipient fell over."

"There were others who knew?"

"Others who *learned*," Corvane corrected. "Knowing is different. Knowing is a trade secret. Learning is a wound." He set down his glass and looked at me directly for the first time, and whatever was in his face made my stomach drop before he said a word.

"Thirty years ago," he said, "there was a woman in the northern hill country. A hedge-witch, half-mad, full of the old language—the dead tongue, the First Kingdoms' tongue, the kind of speech that makes stones hum. She'd figured out pieces of the unbinding. Dangerous knowledge. The Oath wanted her gone, and it wanted her daughter gone with her, because the daughter had been born knowing three syllables she shouldn't have."

The room tilted. Or I did. One of us.

"She fought," Corvane went on, mercilessly gentle. "She fought harder than anyone I've ever sold. But the Oath's reach is long, and mine is longer, and in the end I took the child north, away from the tithe routes, away from the crown's men, and sold her to a buyer who wanted a blank slate. No memories. No name. Nothing but survival instinct and quick hands." He paused. "The buyer was me, in a sense. I kept her. Raised her. Taught her the trade. Made her useful."

The candle flames had gone still. The whole room had gone still, the way the valley goes still, the way magicless places go still, and I understood suddenly that some part of me had been holding this truth behind my teeth for twenty years, chewing on it, refusing to swallow.

"Her mother spoke the unbinding word," I said. My voice sounded like it was coming up from the bottom of a well. "At the first attempt. And she was unmade for it."

"So you've heard the story."

"I *lived* the story." The words came out of me like something breaking. "I was seven years old. I watched her burn out of the world. I ran north. I ran until I forgot why I was running, and then I ran until I forgot I'd been running, and then there was a valley where nothing could follow me—not even my own past—and a man who found me there and fed me and taught me to steal, and I thought—" I laughed, and it came out cracked. "I thought I was lucky. I thought the valley had saved me."

"It did save you," Corvane said quietly. "From the tithe. From the Oath. From the word your mother hid inside her last sentence, which would have gotten you unmade alongside her. I took that memory out of you myself, Thackeray. Your mother's death. Your escape. Your name. All of it, cut clean, and buried where you'd never dig." He spread his hands. "You've spent twenty years stealing other people's pasts because yours was taken first. I made you a thief of memory because you were already its orphan. There's a symmetry there. I've always appreciated the symmetry."

Every thread of my life pulled taut at once, and I saw the shape of the loom. The valley. The trade. The mentor who'd appeared out of nowhere with food and flattery and lessons. The ledger with names I didn't remember giving. The gap in my oldest memory, shaped exactly like myself. It hadn't been a crack in the armor. It had been a door, and Corvane had walked through it my whole life, and I had held it open for him and called him family.

"Why tell me now?" I whispered.

"Because the client asked me to." He said it simply, without apology. "The Oath wants the child, and it wants you compliant, and it has learned—over two hundred and forty-one transactions—that the fastest way to break a person is to give them the truth at the worst possible moment. Truth is a tool, Thackeray. Same as a knife. Same as kindness." He rose, straightened his coat, and became once more the respectable broker the court saw. "Deliver the child to the crown room in nine days, and you'll never see me again. Refuse, and I'll take the child myself, and I'll do it with the same skill I used to erase you, and you'll watch it happen remembering every single thing, because I won't take *that* memory from you. The client insists. It wants you to carry it."

He walked to the door, then paused, and glanced back with something that in a better man might have been regret.

"For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much—you were the best thing I ever stole."

Then he was gone, and I was alone in the gallery with the portraits and the swan-candles and the wine I'd never touched, and somewhere above me, in a room with a window seat and three blankets, a fading child slept with one hand curled around something invisible.

Nine days.

I had nine days, a knife I'd bought second-hand, and a truth that fit the hole in me perfectly—which was, I realized, wiping my eyes with the heel of my hand, exactly how I knew it was real.

Tomorrow I would confront him. Tonight I climbed the stairs to Wren's room, sat on the floor beside the window seat, and kept watch until dawn, memorizing the sound of a child breathing, in case it was the last thing I got to keep.
