## 21. The Memory I Sold

The palace at night smelled of candlewax and old lies, and Corvane had always smelled of both.

Ansel found him in the west gallery, of all places—the long corridor where the kings of Aldermont hung in their gilded frames, each one painted looking slightly to the left of the viewer, as though the artist had caught them mid-thought about something better. Corvane stood before the portrait of Aldric's grandfather with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring art rather than waiting for an ambush he had almost certainly arranged.

"You came," Corvane said, without turning.

"You asked."

"I asked." He turned then, and his face did the thing it always did—arranged itself into warmth the way other men arranged their collars. "I confess I half expected you to run again. You have a talent for doors, my dear. Windows too. It's why I never worried about you in the valley. A thief who can't be caught is only ever running *toward* something."

"And what am I running toward?"

Corvane smiled. "That," he said, "is precisely what we're going to discuss."

* * *

They walked. Corvane liked walking when he talked; he said it kept the blood honest, which was a strange thing for a man to say whose entire profession was keeping everything else dishonest. The gallery stretched ahead of them, lit by lamps that guttered in a draft Ansel couldn't find, and somewhere below them the palace went on breathing—guards changing shifts, kitchens banked for the night, and beneath all of it that faint wrongness Ansel had felt since arriving, like a clock ticking a half-second behind itself.

"Twenty years ago," Corvane began, "give or take a season—I was younger, you were smaller, and the valley was exactly as forgettable as it is now. You'll forgive me if I don't pretend I don't know why you've come. You read the king's journals. You spoke to the queen. And now you stand in my gallery wearing the particular expression of someone who has found a loose thread and cannot stop pulling."

"The tithe-children," Ansel said. "You sold them. For decades."

"I *placed* them," Corvane corrected mildly. "Sold is such a butcher's word. I placed children who would otherwise have been dragged screaming into the crown room, into homes where they were wanted, or at least where they were fed. That some of those homes were the homes of people who paid me well—" he spread his hands, "—is the nature of every market. Bread feeds the baker's family too."

"One of them escaped."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes. Not guilt—Ansel had spent twenty years reading faces, and guilt had a shape, a wetness at the corners, a downward drag. This was something more careful. This was a man checking his footing on ice.

"One," Corvane agreed. "One escaped."

"A child from the valley."

"Yes."

Ansel stopped walking. The gallery stretched away in both directions, all those painted kings watching with their leftward gazes, and Ansel heard their own voice come out very quiet. "Tell me about the child."

"Ah." Corvane looked up at the portrait of the grandfather king as if consulting him. "You know, I wondered how long it would take you. I gave it a week. Wren gave you three days. The queen, I'm sorry to say, bet against you entirely—she thought you'd take the records and flee north and never open them. People are so rarely good judges of themselves. It's why I made my living in memories rather than portraits."

"*Corvane.*"

He sighed, and for just a moment—for the length of a single lamp-gutter—he let the warmth fall off his face like a coat set down after a long day. What was underneath wasn't cruelty. It was tiredness, deep and old, the tiredness of a man who had been carrying something heavy for so long he'd forgotten it could be put down.

"Sit," he said. "There are benches in galleries for a reason. My knees are seventy years old and what I have to tell you deserves a seated audience."

There was indeed a bench, small and velvet-topped, positioned before the portrait of some forgotten queen. Ansel sat. Corvane remained standing, which Ansel understood a moment later was deliberate: he did not want to be beside them for this. He wanted to be *facing* them, the way a man faces a door he expects to be struck through.

"Her name was Maren's idea, actually, the naming of the tithe. Before her time it was called the Renewal, which has a nicer ring and is a great deal worse. Every seven years, the crown takes seven children. The Oath requires it. The crown-thing requires it—it eats beginnings, Ansel. Childhoods. First memories. A child's whole life ahead of it is the richest meal there is, and the kingdom learned, seven years at a time, not to ask where the children went." He paused. "Most of them didn't go anywhere terrible, at first. They were emptied and placed. New names, new families, gaps in their heads patched over with whatever I could buy cheaply. I told myself it was mercy. For perhaps the first dozen, I even believed it."

"And the child who escaped."

"The child who escaped was seven years old, and had been marked for the tithe because her mother—" Corvane stopped. Started again, differently, like a man stepping around a hole in the floor. "Her mother had tried something foolish. Something with words. The crown-thing unmade her for it, and took the child anyway, as payment plus interest. I was given custody of the transfer. Me, specifically, because I was discreet, and because the crown-thing trusted me, and because—" his voice did something complicated, "—because I had a reputation for never losing merchandise."

"But you lost this one."

"On the road north. There was a storm. There was a ferry crossing, and a ferryman who owed me a favor, and in the confusion of the storm the child simply—wasn't there anymore. Gone into the rain. I searched for a season. I lied about searching for two more. And eventually the crown-thing accepted a substitute, because the Oath is flexible about bodies if it isn't flexible about numbers, and I paid for that substitution out of my own ledger for eleven years." He looked down at Ansel, and his eyes were very steady and very sad. "Do you want me to say it plainly? Because I will say it plainly, and once I do, neither of us gets to unsay it."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. Somewhere far off in the palace, that half-second-late clock ticked. "Say it."

"The child was you, Elodie."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Ansel sat on the little velvet bench and listened for the splash and heard nothing at all.

"No," Ansel said. It came out reasonably, almost conversationally, which felt absurd. "No, I grew up in the valley. I grew up—"

"In the valley," Corvane agreed gently. "Where magic cannot follow. Where memory thins like butter scraped over too much bread. Where a child with a torn-open mind and no past could arrive at a cottage door and be taken in by a fence with a guilty conscience and a spare bed." He crouched then, finally, bringing himself level—not to loom, Ansel realized, but so that a frightened person wouldn't have to look up. "I found you three years later. Do you remember? No. Of course you don't. That's rather the point. I found a girl selling scraps of other people's lives back to travelers at the border stones, clever as a knife, and I recognized the work immediately because I'd taught the trade to half the fences in the north. And I recognized *you*, because I'd spent a season tearing the north apart looking for you, and because your mother had your exact scowl."

"My mother." The words tasted like someone else's. "My mother was unmade. You said—"

"She spoke the unbinding word, or most of one, in front of the crown room, and the Oath took her apart for it. I was there. I held you afterward while you cried and didn't know why." His voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and then smoothed itself over with the ease of long practice. "And when I found you again in that valley—whole, alive, safe, *forgetting*—I made a decision. The cruelest kindness of my life. I lifted what was left of your memory of the tithe. Of the road. Of me. Of her. All of it. Because a girl who remembers being stolen doesn't stay in the one place in the world where nothing can follow her. She runs. She runs straight back to Aldermont with a child's courage and a child's plan, and the crown-thing finds her, and finishes its meal eleven years late."

The gallery was very quiet. One of the lamps had gone out; neither of them had noticed when.

"You erased me," Ansel said.

"I saved you."

"You *erased* me!" It came up out of them like floodwater through a floorboard, twenty years of a life tilting sideways all at once. "Every memory I have of being a child—you decided whether I got to keep it! My name—my *mother*—you took my mother's face out of my head and you never—" Ansel stood up because sitting was unbearable, stood up and turned away from him toward the painted kings because facing him was worse. "I built a whole self out of what was left. I decided I was careful. I decided I was alone. I decided I didn't need anyone, and I thought those were *choices*, Corvane, I thought they were mine—"

"They were yours," Corvane said quietly. "Everything you built on the empty ground was yours. I only cleared the ground."

"You don't get to make that sound noble. You don't get to—" Ansel pressed the heel of a hand against their eyes. "Wren. The child. The tithe-child in my cottage right now. Was that coincidence? Did you send them to me? Tell me you didn't—"

"I didn't." And here Corvane's voice went strange, stripped of all its polish. "I swear it on whatever of me is still worth swearing on. Wren ran. Wren chose your door. If I had sent them, I'd have sent them anywhere but to you, because I knew—I *knew*—that the moment you saw a tithe-child standing in the rain, some part of you would start remembering. Some part of you would recognize the shape of it." He laughed, short and hollow. "I was right. Look at you. You're already halfway to being her again."

"Her?"

"Elodie Thackeray." He said it carefully, the way a man sets down something fragile. "Your mother's name was Sera. Your father I never met—gone before the tithe, by war or wandering, she never said which. You were born in a village called Harrow's End, forty miles south of here, and your mother baked bread that could make grown men weep, and she taught you letters on flour-dusted boards, and when the collectors came for you she stood in the square and she began to speak words that made the stones bleed light." He swallowed. "You watched. That's the worst of it, Elodie. You watched all of it. You were seven years old and you watched, and the crown-thing took you anyway, and I have spent twenty years telling myself that taking the watching away from you was the single decent act of my miserable career."

Ansel's hand found the bench and gripped it, because the world had developed a distinct lean.

Because here was the thing—the horrible, vertiginous thing—that Corvane, in all his careful confession, had not understood. Or had understood and not said.

*I remember,* Ansel wanted to say. *Some of it. I remember a woman's voice saying a word that hummed. I remember rain and a ferry and a man's coat that smelled of ink. I thought those were dreams. I thought everyone had dreams like that, fragments that didn't fit, a voice without a face, a word without meaning. I filed them under dreams and sold travelers' memories for twenty years and never once looked at my own.*

But they didn't say it. Because saying it meant admitting that Corvane's erasure had been imperfect. And an imperfect erasure meant the edges were still there. And the edges were still there meant—

"Wren," Ansel said instead. "The unbinding word. My mother spoke it. Partially."

"Partially," Corvane said. "And it cost her everything. Which is why I am going to say the last thing I have to say, and then I am going to leave this gallery, and you are going to hate me properly instead of in installments." He rose to his full height, and the old warmth came back over his face like frost forming in reverse. "The word won't save you. It didn't save her. It won't save Wren—it's already costing that child years of their own mind every time they speak it; you've seen it, don't lie to yourself. There is no version of this where the word gets spoken and everyone walks away. There is only arithmetic. Seven children now, or one child and one fool forever. I know which choice I made twenty years ago. I can live with it. Can you?"

He walked away. Ansel heard his footsteps go down the gallery, unhurried, past all the painted kings, and stop once—just once—and then continue, and then the door at the far end opened and shut, and Ansel was alone with the dead monarchs and the one working lamp and the enormous, ringing silence where a self used to be.

*Elodie.*

They said it aloud, experimentally, to the empty air. It fit strangely, like a shoe bought for someone with similar feet.

Then, slowly, Ansel reached into the inner pocket where they kept the things too dangerous for the cache, and drew out the oldest stolen memory they owned—the one they'd carried for twenty years without ever quite daring to look at directly, the one shaped like a gap, the one that had felt, all this time, like holding a key to a lock they couldn't find.

They held it up to the lamplight.

And for the first time, they saw it clearly: not a gap at all. A seam. Stitched closed with Corvane's neat, practiced, professional hand—and coming undone, thread by thread, ever since a mute child had walked through a storm to their door.

Beneath the stitching, faint as breath on glass, something glowed.

It was humming.

* * *

Ansel was halfway down the servants' stair before they let themselves think the thought whole, because thinking it whole meant acting on it, and acting on it meant leaving the palace tonight, and leaving the palace tonight meant—

Wren.

The realization arrived all at once, cold and complete, the way water finds every crack at once when the dam goes. Corvane knew where the cottage was. Corvane had always known where the cottage was. And Ansel had just spent an hour confirming, in front of the crown-thing's own broker, that the last tithe-child was awake, was remembering, was carrying the unbinding word in a mouth too small to defend it.

Corvane hadn't come to confess. Corvane had come to *measure*—to see how much of Elodie was left, how fast the seam was unraveling, how long he had before he needed to solve the problem of them both.

Ansel took the stairs three at a time.

Outside, the night over Aldermont had gone the color of a bruise, and far to the north, past the walls and the fields and the border stones, the valley waited—smaller than memory, darker than home, and holding a child who was spending themselves syllable by syllable to keep a door open that should have stayed shut.

Ansel ran.
