## 22. Running on Borrowed Ground

The undercrofts beneath Aldermont smelled of wet stone and older secrets, and Wren thought—running, always running—that a city should not have so many cellars. It was as if the people above had built their fine houses on top of a buried animal and kept digging rooms into its hide.

Behind them, boots. Many boots. The crown-thing's bound servants did not shout, which was worse than shouting. Shouting meant fear. Silence meant certainty.

Wren's hand found Ansel's sleeve in the dark. *Left,* they signed, the way they'd taught each other in the valley, in the cottage, in the small hours when speech was too loud for the world they were hiding from. *There is a door.*

*How do you know?* Ansel's hands asked back, quick and shaking.

*The stone told me.*

This was true, though Wren could not have explained it to anyone who hadn't felt it. The old language was not words the way Ansel understood words—not sounds stacked on meanings like bricks. It was closer to asking. You spoke to a thing, and if you spoke truly, the thing remembered what it was made for. Stone remembered being mountains. Doors remembered being open.

But everything remembered at a price, and lately the price had been coming due in a currency Wren was running out of.

They ducked through the door—a low arch that groaned open at a single syllable, *aeth*, *open*, spoken soft as a lullaby—and came out into a cistern vast enough to have its own weather. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of centuries. Their footsteps echoed wrong, doubled, tripled, as if other children were running alongside them in the dark, all the children who had ever been brought down here.

*Which way?* Ansel signed.

Wren closed their eyes and listened with the part of them that wasn't ears. The city above hummed through the stone like a struck bell that never quite stopped ringing. North, the hum was warm and tangled—the palace, the crown-thing, the king slowly being eaten alive inside his own glory. South, the hum thinned out toward the river gates. And east—

East there was a hole in the sound. A silence shaped like a valley.

*Not east,* Wren signed firmly. They were not going back to the valley. Not yet. Not while the word was still unfinished inside them, still half-swallowed, three syllables given and more withheld because giving them cost exactly this: years. Days. Names. Wren could no longer remember the color of the sky over the First Kingdoms. They had traded it two corridors ago for a wall that would let them pass.

Ansel squeezed their hand. *South, then.*

They ran.

* * *

The third time the hunters nearly caught them, Wren spent something precious without meaning to.

It happened like this: a junction of tunnels, torchlight blooming at both ends, no door, no arch, nothing but a wall of fitted stone that some mason five hundred years dead had been very proud of. Boots closing from the left. Lantern-glow swelling from the right. Ansel pressed against the wall, breathing fast, one hand already moving toward the knife at their belt—the knife they never used, the knife that was mostly a promise to themselves.

And Wren, who was tired, who was frightened, who had been spending themselves coin by coin all night, put both palms flat against the stone and said, aloud, in the tongue that made the world sit up and listen:

*Aeth-velum-karai.*

Open, little brother. Let us in.

The wall sighed. It did not open so much as *relent*, stones sliding apart like a crowd making room, and Wren pulled Ansel through into darkness beyond, and the wall closed behind them with a sound almost like regret.

In the new dark, lit only by the thin gray seep of light through the seams, Ansel stared at them. *What did that cost?*

Wren started to sign *nothing*, which was the first lie they had ever told Ansel, and their hands knew it. The lie came out crooked. Ansel caught their wrists gently and turned their palms up, as if the answer might be written there.

It was, in a way. Because Wren tried to remember what they had paid and found a smooth place where something used to be. Not a big thing. A small thing. A warm thing.

"I had a—" they began, and stopped. There had been a song. Their mother's mother's song, maybe, or just a song, sung over bread, over washing, over small sleeping children. Four notes rising and one falling. They could feel the shape of it the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth.

"A song," Wren said aloud, and their voice cracked on it. "I had a song. I think I loved it."

Ansel's face did something terrible and tender. They pulled Wren against their chest, right there in the smuggler's dark between walls, and held on.

"Stop paying," Ansel whispered into their hair. "Do you hear me? We'll find another way. There's always another way—I've made a living finding other ways."

But Wren shook their head against Ansel's shoulder. Because there wasn't. That was the whole rotten arithmetic of it. The unbinding word lived in Wren the way a river lives in a country, spread through all of them, and every syllable they gave away to save their skin was a syllable carved out of the whole. They were spending the word itself, drop by drop, to stay alive long enough to speak it.

They didn't know how to explain this. So instead they signed, with tired fingers: *I am not spending myself. I am spending the parts of me that were never mine to keep. Everyone else got to forget the old things. I'm just catching up.*

It was a beautiful lie and they were almost proud of it.

* * *

They emerged near midnight into a stretch of undercroft that had once been a street. Wren could tell because the doors were still there—doors to houses that now opened onto earth and root and the slow black water of a buried stream. Someone had lived here. Someone had hung herbs in a doorway and scratched a child's height-marks into a jamb, and then the city had grown up and over them like moss over a fallen tree, and everyone above had agreed, in the way crowds agree, to forget.

Wren touched the height-marks. The stone remembered the child: quick feet, a gap-toothed laugh, a name that sounded like rain stopping.

"We can rest," Ansel signed. "Just a moment. Just until my legs stop arguing with me."

They sat in the doorway of the forgotten house, shoulder to shoulder, sharing the heel of bread Ansel produced from somewhere inside their coat, because Ansel was the kind of person who always had bread hidden about their person, the way other people had knives or prayers.

"You know what's funny," Ansel signed around a mouthful. "I spent ten years stealing memories from travelers and I never once stole a happy childhood. Couldn't get a good price. Nobody wants to buy what they can't use." A pause. Their hands slowed. "I'm starting to think that was the whole trick of this kingdom. It sold off everything warm and kept the ledgers."

Wren leaned their head on Ansel's shoulder. Above them, faint through forty feet of earth, they could hear Aldermont going about its evening—cart wheels, a drunk singing, a dog with opinions. The city hummed its endless note. Somewhere in the middle of the hum was a king who barely remembered his own name, writing pleas inside his own memories, and somewhere else was a queen keeping her peace by pretending, and somewhere else was Corvane, walking the bright streets with his ledger full of sold children.

Including, Wren now knew, Ansel.

That knowledge sat in Wren like a swallowed coal. They had watched Ansel learn it, chapter by chapter, and had watched the person they loved most in the world come apart and reassemble into someone harder and kinder at once. Wren wanted to give them something back. But everything Wren had was either needed for the word or already spent.

So instead they did the only thing left. They took Ansel's hand and traced, slowly, on the palm, the first three syllables of the unbinding—one for each night in the cottage, each lesson by candlelight, each burning hole punched in the valley's silence.

*You still have these,* Wren signed. *Whatever happens. Whatever I lose. These are yours now. I put them in you on purpose.*

Ansel went very still.

*Why on purpose?*

*Because I saw how the last unbinding failed.* This was true; Wren had seen it the way they saw most old things, sideways, in the grain of stones. *A woman spoke it alone. She was brave and she was ready and it killed her anyway, because the word needs somewhere to live after the speaker is gone. It needs a keeper.* Wren looked up at Ansel in the dark. *I chose you before I ever knocked on your door. The storm was real. Knocking was a choice.*

For a long moment Ansel didn't move. Then they laughed—a small, cracked, astonished sound—and pressed their forehead to Wren's.

"You terrible, wonderful creature," they breathed. "You picked a thief. Out of everyone in the world, you picked a thief to keep the holiest word in the old tongue."

*Thieves are the only ones who understand that some things must be taken to be saved,* Wren signed. *Also, you fed me soup.*

"The soup was a bribe. I was hoping you'd leave."

*I know. It was excellent soup.*

And for one moment—one whole, unmeasured moment—they were just two people laughing quietly in a dead house under a living city, and the price of everything was forgotten, and Wren's missing song almost surfaced, four notes rising and one falling, close enough to taste.

Then the water in the buried street went still.

All of it, all at once, from bank to bank, flat as hammered tin.

* * *

"They've found the cistern," Ansel signed, already on their feet. "Or something has. Move—"

Wren was already moving, but they also reached out with the listening part of themselves, and what they felt made their stomach drop through the floor and keep falling.

It was not the hunters. Hunters were human noise—boots, breath, lantern-oil, fear. This was smooth. This was patient. This was the hum of the city bending, the way a face bends around a smile that isn't real, and Wren knew it the way you know your own nightmare returning: the crown-thing had sent something of itself down into the dark. Not a body. A *pressure*. An attention. It moved along the tunnels the way cold moves under a door.

And it was following Wren's trail—not footprints, but *speech*. Every syllable Wren had spent tonight had left a scar in the stone, and the scars glowed, in whatever sense the crown-thing glowed, like a road of lit windows leading straight to them.

*It can hear where I've spoken,* Wren signed frantically. *Every word is a mark. We have to go where I haven't—*

*Where you haven't spoken.* Ansel's eyes went wide. Then, horribly, they began to smile. "Then we go down. All the way down. To the oldest part. The part even the city forgot."

*Ansel, if even the city forgot it, how do you—*

"Because I stole a map from a drunk surveyor six years ago and sold it back to him at the border, and I have an excellent memory for things that aren't mine." Ansel was already pulling them along the buried streambank, signing one-handed, fast. "There's a foundation under the foundations. Older than Aldermont. Older than the First Kingdoms' roads. The surveyor swore his compass spun there and he cried and he didn't know why."

Wren understood then, with a thrill that was equal parts terror and homecoming, what Ansel was proposing. The oldest stone. Stone from before the Oath, before the tithe, before the kingdom learned to be afraid. Stone that had never heard the old language and so had never been scarred by it.

Stone that might remember what the word was *for*.

They ran along the black water, past roots thick as arms, past a stairway that went up to nowhere, down and down, Ansel navigating by a thief's memory of another man's map, until the air changed. It grew colder and somehow cleaner, and the city's hum faded above them like a bell carried away on wind, and the silence underneath it was not empty but *attentive*.

The tunnel ended at a wall. Not fitted stone—no proud mason here—but raw rock, faceted, faintly luminous, as if the mountain had been cut open and had not entirely finished objecting.

And across its face, worn nearly smooth, ran lines. Characters. Old ones. The same family as the syllables living in Wren's chest, but older—grandparents, ancestors, the tongue before the tongue.

Wren stepped forward and laid both hands against it.

The rock did not hum. It *recognized*.

And in that instant of recognition, Wren felt the pressure behind them falter—the crown-thing's attention recoiling from this deep place like a hand snatched back from a stove—and heard, distantly, furiously, boots and voices: the hunters, arriving at the mouth of the tunnel they had just come down, blocking the only way back.

Trapped between the thing that hunted them and the wall that knew them, Wren did the reckless thing, the spendthrift thing, the thing Ansel would beg them not to do.

They read the wall aloud.

The first line cost them the memory of their own birthday. The second took their grandmother's face. The third—

The third line was a warning, and Wren understood it fully a heartbeat too late, and by then the stone was already opening, and the hunters were already running, and Ansel was shouting their name—

—and far above, in a gallery smelling of candlewax and old decisions, Queen Maren stood suddenly at her window, looking north, looking down, as if she had heard something underground call a name she had spent seven years pretending not to know.
