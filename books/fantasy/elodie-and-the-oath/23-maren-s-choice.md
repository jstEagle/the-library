## 23. Maren's Choice

The crown-thing had begun to hum.

Maren noticed it first in the silver. Every spoon in the royal pantry had developed a faint vibration, as though the metal remembered being ore and wished to return to it. Then the mirrors followed—the great looking-glass in her dressing chamber had taken to showing her face a half-breath after she made it, so that Maren spent her mornings watching a stranger imitate her expressions with slightly too much enthusiasm.

"You're frowning," the mirror-Maren said, a heartbeat late, and smiled.

"I'm always frowning," Maren told it. "You'd know that if you were actually me."

The mirror said nothing, which was its way of sulking.

Twenty-three years she had lived in Aldermont beside the thing that wore her husband's crown and, less convincingly, her husband's face. Twenty-three years of learning its habits the way one learns the habits of a dangerous animal: when it fed, when it slept, when it grew curious about the servants' children. She had become an expert in a subject no university would teach and no sane person would study. She could read the crown-thing's moods in the way candle flames leaned. She knew that when it was pleased, the palace clocks all ran two minutes fast, and when it was angry, the wine turned to vinegar in the cellars, and when it was *suspicious*—

When it was suspicious, it invited her to breakfast.

* * *

The breakfast table was laid for two in the small morning room, which meant the crown-thing wanted privacy, which meant the crown-thing wanted something. It sat across from her wearing Aldric's body with the careful competence of a very good portrait painter working from memory—everything correct, everything positioned correctly, and yet somehow the whole of it slightly wrong, like a room where someone had moved every chair three inches.

"Maren." It said her name warmly. It had learned warmth the way a scholar learns a dead language: fluently, and without any native speaker to correct its accent. "You look tired."

"I look fifty-two," Maren said, taking her seat. "It's the same thing."

"Have the eggs. Cook has outdone herself."

The eggs were perfect. That was the other sign. When the crown-thing was suspicious, the food was always perfect, because perfection required attention, and attention was what suspicion felt like when it dressed for company.

Maren ate her perfect eggs and waited.

"The memory-broker you've been sheltering," the crown-thing said at last, delicately, the way one mentions a stain on a guest's sleeve. "Thackeray. Ansel Thackeray."

"I don't shelter brokers. I barely tolerate the courtiers, and they bathe more often."

"It came through the west gate four days ago. Disguised, rather charmingly, as a broker of memories—which is to say, not disguised at all, merely honest about its profession for once." The crown-thing buttered its toast with Aldric's hands, and Maren watched those hands and hated how well she still knew them. "It visited the undercrofts. It spoke with the ferryman. And then it vanished into the palace like smoke through a keyhole, and here is the interesting part, my dear—" it leaned forward, and the candle flames leaned with it, toward Maren, like courtiers currying favor, "—it vanished *toward your apartments*."

"Perhaps it was lost. The palace is a maze. I've been lost in it for twenty-three years."

"Yes," the crown-thing said softly. "You have."

Silence. Somewhere below them, a clock struck nine, two minutes fast.

"I want to be fair to you, Maren." The crown-thing set down its knife and folded Aldric's hands, and for a moment—for one vertiginous moment—it let the mask slip, and what looked back at her from behind her husband's eyes was old beyond old, patient beyond patience, a bargain given flesh and a flesh given appetite. "Fairness is not something I am often accused of, so savor it. You have kept this kingdom whole. You have kept the peace. You have done it by pretending, beautifully, for over two decades, that you do not know what sits at your breakfast table. I have found your pretense... restful. Like a well-made bed."

"How flattering. I'll have it embroidered on a cushion."

"But pretense only works while both parties honor it." It rose, walked to the window, stood silhouetted against the grey morning with its hands clasped behind its back exactly as Aldric had once clasped them while watching rain. "The child is in my city. The child carries the word. And someone in this palace helped the child escape the tithe-gathering—and someone else has been reading sealed journals in my husband's private library."

Maren's heart performed a maneuver she can only describe as attempting to exit through her ribs.

"The journals are warded," she said.

"They were warded by me. Nothing wards against me." It turned. "Here is my arithmetic, wife. I will lay it out plainly, because you have earned plainness, if nothing else."

* * *

It laid it out plainly.

The crown-thing spoke the way an accountant speaks, if accountants dealt in souls instead of sums. The renewal was due. Seven years had passed since the last tithe; the ledger demanded payment; the payment was children—not their lives, worse than that, their *consent*, harvested young and pliable, bound into the Oath before they were old enough to understand what they were agreeing to. Wren had been this year's payment. Wren had fled. And now the whole elegant machine creaked.

"If the tithe fails," the crown-thing said, "the Oath does not simply expire. Oaths do not expire, Maren. They default. And when an oath of this magnitude defaults, it collects." It gestured at the window, at the city beyond, at the seven thousand rooftops going gold with morning light. "Aldermont was dying when I saved it. Do you remember? Of course you don't; you weren't there. The fields were ash. The wells were poison. Your husband's father had sold the treasury twice over. I gave them forty years of springtime. I ask so little in return."

"Children," Maren said. "You ask children."

"A handful. A rounding error. A handful of children against a kingdom of thousands—is that not arithmetic? Is that not mercy, even?" The crown-thing's voice softened into something almost reasonable, which was the most frightening register it owned. "But the ledger is damaged now. The tithe-child ran, carrying the unbinding word in its mouth like a lit coal. If that word is spoken, everything I have built unravels. The springs fail. The fields remember they are ash. And your husband—" it paused, and here it did something crueler than any threat: it hesitated, genuinely, "—your husband dies. Not sleeps. Not waits. Dies. He is held together by the same thread that binds the Oath, Maren. Cut the thread and the puppet falls, and there is no puppeteer kind enough to catch him."

"And if I deliver the child?"

The crown-thing smiled with Aldric's mouth, and Maren thought: *twenty-three years, and I still flinch.*

"If you deliver the child to me—whole, willing, before the renewal completes—then I will release him."

The words landed in the morning room like coins dropped down a well. Maren heard them fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"Release him," she repeated.

"Fully. His mind, his name, his mornings. I will step out of his shadow and into whatever vessel consents next—or into nothing, if the Oath permits, and I confess I have never tested that clause. He would wake as a man of seventy-odd years who has lost two decades, weeping, perhaps, and alive. Alive, Maren. You would have him back. Grey and bewildered and *his*."

She should have said no immediately. She knew that. Some part of her, some cold clerk in the back office of her soul, stamped the file REJECT and slid it across the desk. But another part of her—the part that had watched Aldric's shadow move a half-second late for twenty-three years, the part that had lain beside the thing wearing him and memorized the ceiling because she could not bear to memorize its borrowed face—that part reached for the offer the way a drowning woman reaches for anything, driftwood or crocodile.

And the crown-thing saw her reach. That was the worst of it. It saw, and it waited, patient as interest accruing.

"You hesitate," it observed.

"I'm doing sums," Maren said.

"Ah. Arithmetic." It returned to the table, sat, poured her tea she hadn't asked for. "Then let us do it together, as partners do. One child, delivered, against one king restored. Weigh them."

"One child who fled *because* the alternative was being devoured by consent."

"All children flee things they don't understand. Vegetables. Bedtimes. Eternity."

"This child understands better than you think. This child's mother understood." Maren heard her own voice go quiet and dangerous. "There was a woman, once, who tried to speak the unbinding. What happened to her?"

The crown-thing's teacup stopped halfway to its lips. For an instant the porcelain trembled—a tiny, honest fear, the first true emotion Maren had ever seen it wear.

"She was unmade," it said. "As all who speak it are unmade. Which is why the word is a foolish weapon, wife. It costs the speaker everything. Whoever wields it must hold the Oath's origin entire, and no living mind can carry that weight without dissolving. The word is not a key. It is a *price tag*. Think of it as such."

"I think of everything as such," Maren said. "I've had practice."

"Then think of this." It leaned close, and its breath smelled of cold stone and old honey. "You have until the renewal completes. Three days. Bring me the child, and Aldric wakes. Fail, and I will assume your treachery is total—and I will not punish you, Maren. Punishment is for subjects. Instead I will simply stop pretending, in front of everyone, forever. The court will learn what sits beside their queen. The kingdom will learn what its peace cost. And your husband will spend his final hours knowing that his wife bargained away a child and failed anyway. Everyone loses their illusions at the end. I merely control the schedule."

It rose, smoothed Aldric's coat, and paused at the door.

"The eggs really are excellent today," it said. "Do finish them. Waste offends me more than treachery."

The door closed. The candles straightened. Somewhere in the walls, the humming stopped, satisfied, the way a creditor stops humming once the debt has been acknowledged.

* * *

Maren did not finish the eggs. She sat in the morning room until the tea went cold, doing the arithmetic the crown-thing had assigned her, and finding—as she had known she would find—that the sum refused to balance.

One child against one king. It sounded clean. It sounded like the kind of equation a reasonable woman could live inside. Deliver Wren, save Aldric, spend whatever remained of her life paying a debt she could explain to herself every night in the mirror.

But the mirror showed her face a half-second late these days, and she no longer trusted what it told her she could live with.

Because here was the flaw in the crown-thing's arithmetic, the error hidden in the margins: it had promised Aldric's life in exchange for the child, and the crown-thing lied the way water lies—smoothly, reflectively, showing you only what stands on the bank. Twenty-three years of marriage to a monster had taught her its grammar. When it said *release*, it meant *discard*. When it said *alive*, it meant *animate*. A king emptied of the thing that sustained him, waking old and broken in a kingdom whose forty years of springtime would be already curdling back to ash—she would get one morning with her husband's true eyes, and then they would both watch the world he had been kept alive to anchor come apart around them.

The trade bought a death, not a life. It just dressed the death in a reunion.

Unless—

Unless the Oath itself was cut. Not defaulted, not renegotiated. *Unbound.* Spoken down by someone willing to pay the price tag, someone holding the origin entire, someone whose mind could carry the weight without dissolving.

No living mind could do it, the crown-thing had said.

But it had said the word was foolish, and it had flinched when she named the woman who tried. Monsters only flinch at things that frighten them, and things only frighten monsters when the monsters aren't certain.

Maren rose, cold tea abandoned, and walked the long gallery toward her apartments with the particular gait of a woman who has stopped balancing ledgers and started committing crimes. The guards bowed. The portraits watched. In the third window, morning light fell across the floor in a long gold bar, and she stepped over it like a threshold.

Three days. The broker was still somewhere in the palace—she had felt it, these past days, the faint wrongness of a stranger moving through her rooms' blind spots, competent and quiet and, according to the ferryman's gossip, traveling with a fading child who paid for bread with words that made stones hum.

If the crown-thing wanted an answer in three days, then Maren would give it one.

But not the one it had ordered.

She reached her chambers, dismissed her maids, and knelt—kneeling, at fifty-two, being an indignity she had long since made peace with—before the loose stone beneath the window where she kept the things that mattered: Aldric's first letter to her, water-stained and ridiculous; a lock of hair from a son who had never been born; and the scrap of paper on which, three nights ago, a grey-eyed thief had pressed a charcoal sketch of a corridor and a single line in a slanted hand:

*Your gallery. Midnight. Come alone, bring nothing you can't deny.*

Maren had come alone the first time. She would come alone again tonight.

Outside, the bells rang ten, two minutes fast, and the gold bar of light crawled slowly across the floor toward her like something patient keeping an appointment.
