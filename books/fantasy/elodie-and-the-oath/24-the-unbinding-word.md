## 24. The Unbinding Word

Wren had stopped humming.

That was how I knew, before anything else, before the trembling hands or the way the child's eyes sometimes slid past my face as though I were a window instead of a person. Wren hummed constantly—tuneless little fragments of songs from a kingdom older than any map, melodies that made the teakettle whistle along whether it wanted to or not. The humming had been the soundtrack of our weeks on the run. And now, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a smuggler's attic in Aldermont's lower city, Wren was silent.

"Wren." I crouched down to the child's level. "Hey. Look at me."

Wren looked at me. That was somehow worse. The look was patient, polite, the look you give a kind stranger who keeps saying your name like they know you.

"Do I—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Do you remember me?"

"You're Ansel," Wren said slowly, carefully, the way someone recites an answer they studied rather than something they know. "You steal memories. You're grumpy before breakfast. You pretend you don't like me but you gave me your blanket."

Relief hit me so hard I nearly sat down in the dust. "I do like you."

"You said that was a secret."

"It was."

"Secrets are heavy," Wren said, and then frowned, and touched their own temple, and I watched the frown deepen into something frightened. "Ansel. There's a hole. Here. It's getting bigger. I can feel the edges of it."

I knew what was making those edges. Every word of the old language Wren spoke cost years. In the undercrofts beneath the palace district, hunted by Corvane's hunters and worse things wearing guard uniforms, Wren had bent stone and blinded lanterns and once—I still didn't fully understand this—made a staircase forget it was a staircase, so that six armed men fell gently down nothing for a full minute while we ran. Each working had taken its toll. The child who had arrived at my cottage in a storm, mute and blazing with a dead tongue, was being spent coin by coin, and there was no fence in any city who could sell those years back.

"Then we stop spending," I said. "We hide. We wait them out."

"We can't wait." Wren shook their head, and their hair—it had been black as crow feathers when they came to my door—was going grey at the temples. Grey. On a child. "The renewal is in four days, Ansel. When the Oath renews, it takes the tithe whether I'm here or not. It'll just take different children. And if I'm not there to speak the word, it'll keep taking children every seven years until the end of everything." A pause. "Also I think I used to know a song about a goose. I can't find it anymore. It's just gone."

"The goose can wait too."

"No." Wren took both my hands in theirs, small and warm and steady, steadier than mine. "It has to be now. And I have to give you the word. All of it. Before there isn't enough of me left to give it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about receiving a gift that could unmake the world: it looks exactly like a child holding your hands and breathing strangely.

Wren closed their eyes. The attic went quiet—not the ordinary quiet of a room, but the listening quiet of the undercrofts, when stone pays attention. Dust motes stopped mid-fall. Somewhere below us, the smugglers' dog, which had been barking at rats all evening, sat down abruptly and cocked its head.

"I'm going to put it in you," Wren said. "Like I put the light in my hands. But bigger. Don't be scared."

"I'm not scared."

"You're sweating."

"That's a merchant thing. We sweat when we're thinking about money."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. Then their grip tightened on my hands and they began to speak.

The first syllable went through me like cold water through a fever. I felt it settle somewhere behind my ribs, a weight, a shape, a *thing* with edges. The second syllable made the attic boards groan and the nails sing in their holes, and outside, three streets away, every bell in the lower city rang once, softly, though no rope had been pulled. The third—

The third syllable never came.

Wren opened their eyes. They were crying without seeming to notice, tears tracking through the grime on their face.

"It's not whole," they whispered. "I gave you what I have and it's not whole. There's a piece missing. The—the root of it. The part that makes it true instead of just loud." Their small brow furrowed, searching a memory that kept sliding away like fish through fingers. "Mother said... no. Not mother. Someone said. Someone with flour on their hands? No, that's your memory leaking into mine, I'm sorry, I'm taking pieces of you by accident now, I'm sorry—"

"Shh. Hey. Breathe. What do you need?"

"The original," Wren said. "The first speaking of the Oath. The moment it was sworn—that memory holds the root of the word. The word grew out of it, like a plant out of soil. You can't pull up the plant without the soil." They looked at me with terrible clarity. "You have to hold the Oath's own beginning inside you when you speak it. Otherwise it's just noise. Beautiful noise. The bells will ring and the stones will hum and nothing will change."

"And where in all the hells would we find the Oath's own beginning?"

Wren looked at me the way you look at someone who has asked where they might find bread in a bakery.

"Where all beginnings go when people want to forget them," the child said. "Into the valley."

* * *

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me sideways and ugly, and Wren flinched, and I knelt there in the dust holding a child's hands and laughing like a madman because of course. Of *course.*

"You understand," I said, "that the valley eats magic. That's the entire point of the valley. Nothing enchanted survives past the border stones. You want me to carry the most powerful word ever spoken into the one place in the world where words like that go to die."

"It won't die," Wren said. "It will sleep. Like it's been sleeping. Ansel—the valley isn't magicless. The valley is *full*. Everything that was ever pushed out of the world went there. It's not an empty cage. It's a locked chest, and everyone forgot there was anything inside it." They squeezed my hands. "Except you. You've lived on top of the chest your whole life. You told me yourself—the stones hum for me but they hum *around* you, like they know you."

"They hum around everyone."

"They don't. I listened. I listen better than anyone alive, because listening is most of what I have left." Wren's voice dropped. "And there's more. The word needs a holder. Someone who carries the Oath's original memory. But memories don't survive in the valley either—they leak out, they fade, travelers lose them on the road. So whoever speaks it has to hold the memory *and* the word together, in the same breath, at the border where the valley ends and the world begins." A pause. "Or maybe inside it. I don't know. The rules are old and I only have pieces."

"So I need to walk into the cage that raised me, carrying the key, and figure out the lock on the way."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"It sounds bad in every way it can be said."

But even as I said it, some thief-bred corner of my brain was already turning the problem over, already seeing the shape of it the way I saw the shape of a mark's pockets. Because here was the thing, the awful elegant thing: I was a memory-thief. Stealing recollections was the one trade I had ever mastered. And somewhere in that valley—buried in my hoard, hidden in the stones, sleeping in the soil of the place that forgot everything—was the single memory in the world that mattered.

All my life I'd been picking locks with other people's treasures. Now I had to rob the valley itself.

"I know that look," Wren said warily. "Corvane had that look. Right before he sold somebody."

"That's slander. Corvane had a much worse look. His had a ledger in it." I stood, knees cracking, and went to the attic's one grimy window. Below, Aldermont sprawled in its glass-eyed splendor, lantern-lit and oblivious, four days from feeding its children to a crown. Somewhere up in the palace, King Aldric was parading his half-second-late shadow for the courtiers. Somewhere in a fine coat, Corvane was counting his commissions. And somewhere south, days away if we were lucky, a valley sat with its mouth open, waiting to swallow us back.

Going back felt like defeat. Going back felt like the trap closing around my ankle with a friendly click.

But traps close around you either way, I thought. The question is whether you're holding something sharp when they do.

"All right," I said. "We go home."

Wren's face did something complicated—grief and relief and fear all fighting for the same real estate—and then the child simply nodded, the way soldiers nod, the way people nod when they've been afraid of an answer and received the brave one.

"One condition," I added.

"You want the blanket back."

"I want the goose song. When this is over, you find the goose song again. That's the deal. Kingdom saved, goose song recovered. Non-negotiable."

For a moment—one bright, impossible moment—Wren laughed. It was a real laugh, a child's laugh, and for that moment the grey at their temples seemed less like erasure and more like frost that would melt. Then the moment passed, and Wren swayed, and I caught them before they hit the floorboards.

They weighed almost nothing. That frightened me more than the hunters, more than the crown-thing, more than the word burning its incomplete shape behind my ribs. Children are supposed to weigh something. Children are supposed to be heavy with all the years they haven't spent yet.

"Sleep," I murmured, laying them on the blanket—my blanket, formerly, currently, forever. "Tomorrow we buy passage south. Quietly. Nobody's friend, nobody's enemy, two pilgrims going to pray at nothing in particular."

"Ansel?" Wren's voice was already slurring toward sleep. "When you hold the word... does it hurt?"

I considered lying. I was, after all, very good at it.

"A little," I said. "Like swallowing a coal that hasn't decided yet."

"Mm." A long pause. Then, barely audible: "My mother held it once. I think. I think that's why I have it. She put it somewhere safe before the end." The child's eyes drifted shut. "Somewhere safe. I keep almost remembering where."

I sat by the window the rest of the night, watching the bells of the lower city and feeling the three syllables turn over behind my ribs like coins in a purse, incomplete, insufficient, waiting. Three syllables out of however many. A key with most of its teeth filed off.

And underneath the fear, underneath the arithmetic of days and miles, one thought kept surfacing, patient as a bubble rising through dark water:

*Somewhere safe.*

A mute child arrives in a valley that swallows memories, carrying a word no one dares speak. Of all the places in the world, the child had come to *mine*. I had always assumed it was chance. Storms blow strange birds onto strange doorsteps.

But thieves know better than anyone that nothing valuable is ever lost. It's only ever *hidden*—by someone who loved it enough to make sure the wrong hands stayed empty.

Wren slept. The bells stayed silent. Behind my ribs, the unfinished word waited for its missing piece, and I began, quietly, methodically, to plan the greatest theft of my life:

from the only place I had ever called home.
