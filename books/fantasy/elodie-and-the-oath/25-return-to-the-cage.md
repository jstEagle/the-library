## 25. Return to the Cage

The valley announced itself the way it always did: by taking things.

Ansel felt it happen in stages as they crested the last ridge before dawn. First the wind went flat and dumb against their face, no longer carrying the smell of pine and far-off rain but nothing at all, a blankness pressed against the skin. Then the birdsong behind them thinned out mid-phrase, like a singer forgetting the tune. And finally—this was the one that always made Ansel's teeth ache—the light changed. Not dimmer. Just *less interested*. The valley's sun rose every morning without any particular opinion about what it illuminated.

"Home," Ansel said, and heard how the word sat wrong in their mouth, like a coat that had been let out for someone broader.

Wren didn't answer. Wren hadn't answered much of anything since the undercrofts, since the night the child had spent years of their own memory bending stone and shouting syllables that made the city's foundations weep. The child walked beside them with their small hand fisted in the hem of Ansel's cloak, and every so often they would stop, tilt their head, and mouth something silently—a word testing its own weight—and then shake their head and keep walking.

Ansel had stopped asking what the words were. Some part of them understood that each silent word was a coin Wren was deciding not to spend.

*You're spending yourself,* Ansel wanted to say. *Every step north you're paying tolls nobody asked you to pay.* But the child knew it better than anyone alive, and pity from a thief would only insult them both.

The road down into the valley was the same road Ansel had walked a hundred times in the other direction, leading travelers in, lifting their memories at the border stones, selling them back at the far edge when they left. It should have felt like safety. Instead it felt like walking backward into a room where all the furniture had been rearranged by someone who hated you.

"It's smaller," Ansel said aloud, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the wind's non-wind. "I swear it's smaller."

Wren looked up at them with an expression that managed, even now, to be faintly amused. The child couldn't speak—not here, not in the silence the Oath had laid over this ground—but they had developed an entire vocabulary of looks, and this one said, quite clearly, *It was never big. You were just small.*

"You don't know that. You've never seen it before."

Another look. This one said, *I have seen kingdoms.*

"Well." Ansel adjusted the bundle on their shoulder—the journals, the records, everything Maren had given them wrapped in oilcloth. "Kingdoms are showy. Anyone can be impressed by a kingdom."

They descended together through the grey-green terraces, past the first border stone, and Ansel watched the magic drain out of Wren the way color drains from a bruise healing in reverse. It wasn't dramatic. That was the cruelty of it. Outside, Wren's cupped hands could hold light like water; inside, the child's fingers were just fingers, and when Wren glanced down at them there was a moment—brief, quickly mastered—of pure animal confusion, a creature checking a limb it had dreamed amputated.

"I'm sorry," Ansel said. "I know I keep saying it. I'm going to keep saying it until it stops being true that I'm sorry, which will be never, so brace yourself."

Wren bumped their shoulder against Ansel's hip. It meant, in the language of looks: *Stop talking. Walk.*

So they walked.

* * *

The cottage was exactly as they'd left it, which was somehow worse than if it had been ransacked. Ransacking would have implied the world had noticed they were gone. Instead the kettle sat on its hook, the two bowls waited in their places, the loose floorboard over the memory-cache lay undisturbed under its scatter of dried herbs. Dust had begun on the windowsill, a fine grey fur, three weeks deep.

Ansel stood in the doorway and felt the full weight of the bittersweet thing settle onto them like wet wool. This had been enough, once. This cottage, this trade, this careful small life balanced on the edge of other people's forgetting. They had told themselves for eleven years that the valley was sanctuary—that magiclessness was a mercy, a place where nothing could follow you, where nothing could *reach* you.

Now they knew the truth, and the truth made the walls look thin. Sanctuary and cage are the same architecture. Only the direction of the door tells you which one you're standing in.

"In," Ansel said, holding the door wide. "Mind the third board. It squeaks with intent."

Wren crossed the threshold, paused, and then did something that cracked Ansel open a little: the child walked the whole length of the single room, touching things. The kettle. The chipped blue bowl. The blanket on Ansel's bed, worn soft as moth-wing. Each touch was deliberate, reverent almost, the way you'd handle relics in a temple—which, Ansel supposed with a lurch, is exactly what these were. Evidence that someone had lived here. That someone had chosen, day after day, to stay.

Wren stopped at the little shelf above the hearth, where Ansel kept the only decoration they owned: a river stone, smooth and grey, carved with a single line that might have been a bird or might have been a wave. Ansel had carried it for as long as they could remember, which, they now understood, was not actually very long at all.

Wren touched the stone. Looked back at Ansel. And their expression asked a question the silence couldn't shape.

"My mother's," Ansel said. "Or—I think it's my mother's. I found it in—" They stopped. Swallowed. "In the cache. In the oldest memory I own. There's a version of me in there, Wren. Seven years old, standing in a square, watching a woman say a word that unmade her. I've had that memory for eleven years and I never once looked closely at it, because looking closely at your own hoard is bad business. A thief who studies the goods starts wanting to keep them."

Wren crossed the room and took Ansel's hand. Small fingers, cold from the road, closing around theirs with a grip that had more stubbornness in it than size could explain.

"We'll look," Ansel said quietly. "Tomorrow. Tonight we bar the door and sleep like honest people, which neither of us is, and tomorrow we look."

But sleep, when it came, came badly, and not only because Ansel kept hearing hoofbeats in the flat dumb wind.

* * *

They had been followed. Ansel had known it since the ridge—known it the way you know weather coming, in the joints, in the old scar along their ribs that Corvane's first lesson had given them. The crown-thing did not send its bound servants hurrying; it sent them *unhurried*, which was worse, because unhurried meant certain. Three riders had crossed the southern pass at dusk, moving at the pace of men who had been promised their quarry could not run forever.

And the terrible joke of it, the joke Ansel could taste like copper, was that the valley would hide them perfectly. No magic could follow across the border stones. The hunters' bindings would go quiet, their scrying dead, their oaths muffled. For a few hours, maybe a day, Ansel and Wren would be invisible to everything except human eyes.

Which was precisely why the hunters would simply walk in. Magic couldn't cross. Men could. Men had always been able to cross. The valley had never protected anyone from anything except wonder.

"They can't sense us in here," Ansel murmured on the second morning, crouched at the window with a sliver of polished tin—the closest thing the valley permitted to a spyglass, since real ones were made with charmed glass and charmed glass sulked at the border. "That's our one gift. We see them coming. We get perhaps the length of the terraces to decide what to do about it."

Wren sat at the table, chin propped on fists, watching Ansel with the patient attention of someone who had decided long ago that this tall anxious thief was worth the watching.

"And before you suggest it again," Ansel added, "no. We are not running further north. North is Aldermont, and Aldermont is where the tithe gathers children in the square, and I have read what happens in that square, Wren, I have read it in his own hand, in his own *begging*, and I am not delivering you anywhere near it while those riders walk our road."

Wren's look this time was complicated. It said several things at once, and Ansel caught only some of them: *I was always going back.* And: *You cannot cage me either.* And, underneath, gentler than the rest: *But thank you for trying.*

"Don't look at me like that. I'm bigger than you. I get to make the plans."

The tin sliver flashed. Ansel went still.

Three figures on the lower road. Not riding now—walking, dismounted, spread across the track in the loose formation of men who expected no resistance and would be polite about whatever they found. At their head walked a fourth figure, and Ansel's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cache beneath the floorboards, through the bedrock, and kept going.

Corvane.

He moved differently than Ansel remembered. That was the first horror. The man who had taught Ansel the trade—who had lifted their first memory with hands gentle as a priest's and told them *the world forgets people, little wren, we just help it along*—had always carried himself like a folded letter, compact, self-contained. Now he walked unfolded. Loose-limbed. Smiling at the terraces as though the valley were a garden he'd inherited.

The crown-thing's broker. Of course he had come himself. You did not send servants to collect something this old, this personal. Corvane had sold tithe-children for decades. He had sold, long ago, a seven-year-old girl out of this very valley—or rather, he had failed to sell her, because she had escaped, and he had erased her memory of escaping to keep her useful, and he had spent eleven years calling her *little wren* while a child named Wren starved herself of words half a kingdom away.

"He's here," Ansel said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. Perhaps steadiness was also something you stole, eventually, from travelers who had needed it more. "He's brought two of the bound ones. He'll knock, Wren. He'll smile. He'll offer me tea and tell me about debts I can't verify, and underneath all of it he'll be measuring the room for how best to take you."

Wren stood. Crossed to the window. Looked out at the three small figures climbing the terraces, and then did the thing that frightened Ansel more than any rider ever could:

The child smiled.

Not a child's smile. Something older wore it briefly, the way the crown-thing wore Aldric—an expression borrowed from somewhere deep, from a language that predated kingdoms, and it said, in perfect clarity: *Let him come. I have been waiting to show him what his ledger costs.*

"Wren." Ansel caught the child's shoulders, knelt, brought them eye to eye. The old tongue hummed faintly under Wren's skin even here, even in the dead air—Ansel could feel it now, had learned to feel it, a warmth like a banked coal the valley couldn't quite smother. "Listen to me. Whatever you're planning—and I know you're planning, you have your mother's whole stubbornness and I've only met her in a memory—you do not spend another word on him. Do you hear me? Every syllable is years of you. He is not worth your years. *No one* is worth your years."

Wren's eyes searched Ansel's face. Then, slowly, deliberately, the child raised one hand and pressed it flat against Ansel's chest, over the heart, and held it there.

Ansel understood. It was the closest thing to speech the valley allowed, and it said: *Neither are yours.*

Outside, gravel crunched under confident boots. A shadow fell across the window, long and familiar and utterly wrong, and a voice that had once been the nearest thing to family Ansel possessed called out with terrible good cheer:

"Elodie! Come out, little thief. Let's talk about what you've taken—and what I've come to take back."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Ansel rose, drew the knife they hoped not to use, and reached for the door.

Behind them, softly, in a voice like the first crack in winter ice, Wren spoke one syllable—and the hearthstone, dead for centuries, began to glow.
