## 26. Corvane's Last Lesson

The storm had followed us home.

Not the honest kind of storm—the valley didn't allow those, or anything else with teeth—but a gray, grudging drizzle that seemed to seep in under the door and pool in the corners of the cottage like it was taking notes. Wren sat by the hearth with their knees drawn up, watching the fire the way they watched everything: as if it might say something worth remembering.

I was counting spoons.

This is not a figure of speech. In my trade you learn to count things—spoons, coins, memories, lies—and I had learned long ago that the counting itself keeps you steady. Eleven spoons, one chipped. Two cups, both mine. One child, mute, half-erased, carrying a word that could unmake an empire. One mentor, presumably coming to kill me.

The knock came at midnight exactly, because Corvane had always believed punctuality was a form of intimidation.

Wren's head came up. The fire hummed—or perhaps that was the stones again, the old stones beneath the floor that remembered more than any of us did. I set down the spoon I was holding and looked at the door for a long moment.

"He won't break it down," I said, though Wren couldn't have understood the words so much as the tone. "He taught me everything I know about doors. He'd consider kicking one vulgar."

I opened it.

Corvane stood on my step with rain beading on his shoulders like he'd negotiated some private arrangement with the weather. He was smaller than I remembered him being, which was strange, because I had never once thought of him as small. His coat was good wool, dark green, and his ledger was tucked under his arm the way other men carried scripture.

"Thackeray," he said. "You look terrible."

"You look rich," I said. "Funny how those two things keep happening together."

"May I come in? It's filthy out."

"It's drizzling, Corvane. You've threatened men in worse weather than this before breakfast." But I stepped aside, because some habits are older than sense, and he walked past me into my cottage the way he'd walked into every room of my life: like he already owned it.

* * *

He looked around with the air of a man appraising a property he intended to repossess. His gaze snagged on Wren by the fire, and something moved behind his eyes—not cruelty, exactly. Arithmetic.

"So," he said. "The tithe-child."

"They have a name."

"I'm sure they do. Names are cheap." He settled into my only decent chair without asking, which was also traditional. "You've caused me a great deal of trouble, Ansel. Aldermont is in chaos. The queen has lost her nerve. And somewhere between here and the capital, three of my hunters are sitting in ditches wondering why their own legs stopped listening to them." He glanced at Wren with something almost like professional appreciation. "The old tongue. I haven't seen it used since—" He stopped himself, neatly, the way he stopped himself from saying most true things.

"Since my mother," I said.

The silence that followed had weight. Rain ticked against the window. Wren had gone very still, watching him, and I noticed their hands were pressed flat against the hearthstone, reading it, the way they read everything.

"You've been to the palace," Corvane said. "You've read the king's journals. Don't bother denying it; I sold the lock that was on that cabinet, and I know what's missing from it now." He leaned forward. "So let's not waste the night pretending this ends any way but one. Give me the child. Walk away. I'll even give you back your ledger page—all those little debts you can't verify anymore. Clean slate. That's generous, Thackeray. That's more than generous."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I take the child, and I take you, and I sell what's left of your memory by the pound. There are buyers in the capital who'd pay well for a thief's whole life. The skill alone. The hands." He smiled, and it was the smile from all those years ago, the first lesson, the one about how memory was just another currency and sentiment was bad bookkeeping. "You were my best student. Don't make me close your account."

I looked at him for a while. Really looked.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about teachers: you spend years assuming they know more than you, and then one day you realize the curriculum ended and they simply never mentioned it. Corvane had taught me to lift a memory cleanly, to leave no ragged edges, to price grief fairly and joy dishonestly. He had taught me everything except what to do when the person across from you was the one thing you should never have trusted.

But I'd figured that part out myself. Somewhere on a humming road, with a fading child beside me and a kingdom's sins in my pockets.

"You asked once," I said quietly, "why I kept every memory I ever stole instead of selling them all. Do you remember?"

"Sentiment," he said. "Bad bookkeeping."

"Mm. That's what you said then, too." I reached into my coat and brought out the pouch. It was heavier than it looked. Most people assume memories weigh nothing, but guilt has mass; anyone who's carried enough of it knows. "I kept them because returning them felt like the one honest thing left in the trade. Because somebody, someday, would come asking for what they'd lost, and I wanted to be able to hand it back whole." I turned the pouch over in my hands. "And because I suspected—I've suspected for years—that one day I'd need to return something to *you*."

His eyes narrowed. "Ansel."

"Every memory you ever sold," I said. "Every one you bought cheap and sold dear. Every farmer's wedding day, every widow's last afternoon with her husband, every child's summer—they're all here. Forty-one years of your ledger, Corvane, and I lifted the contents off you in the palace undercrofts while you were busy gloating at the queen. You really ought to guard your own vault better. You taught me that too."

He was on his feet fast, faster than a man his age should move, and his hand went inside his coat—

"Don't," I said, and I didn't raise my voice, but the stones under the floor hummed, low and long, and Wren stood up beside the fire with their eyes full of the old light, and Corvane's hand stopped where it was. Not because of Wren. Because of the stones. Because the valley itself, magicless, caged, oath-bound valley, seemed suddenly to be paying attention.

"You can't use them here," Corvane said, and for the first time there was something under his voice besides arithmetic. "There's no magic in this valley. That's the whole point of it. Nothing works here. That's why we built our trade in its shadow."

"That's true," I agreed. "No magic. No enchantment. No craft at all." I untied the pouch. "Which means what happens next isn't magic. It's just memory. Plain, ordinary, human memory—the only thing that's ever worked anywhere, in any valley, under any oath. You taught me that, too, though I don't think you meant to."

"What are you doing?" And there it was. Fear. I had waited forty years to hear it in his voice and found, to my surprise, that it made me sad rather than glad.

"The last lesson," I said. "The one you never gave me. Sit down, Corvane."

* * *

I will tell you how it works, since no one else alive knows.

A returned memory is not a gift. When I sell a traveler back their stolen grief at the border, it slides home soft, like water finding its old channel, because they want it. They paid for it. They came to the border of the valley on purpose, aching, ready.

But a memory forced on a man who doesn't want it—that's different. That goes in like a nail.

I took Corvane's hands. He tried to pull away and found that Wren had taken his elbows, gently, the way you hold someone who might fall. Then I opened the first memory and let it go.

It was a small one, to start. A miller's daughter, seventeen, dancing at a harvest fire with flour still in her hair. She'd sold it to Corvane for bread during the famine year, and he'd sold it to a lonely clerk in Aldermont who liked to feel young. I gave it back to the man who'd brokered it, and I watched it land.

Corvane flinched.

"That's nothing," he said. "That's a peasant's—"

The second one: a soldier's last letter home, unsent, held in memory for thirty years until the war ate him. Corvane had bought it off a corpse and sold it twice.

The third: a mother's voice singing. The fourth: a boy's first snow. The fifth, sixth, seventh—a wedding, a funeral, a forgiveness, a betrayal witnessed and regretted, a dog's warm weight against a cold child's back, a father's apology arriving one year too late to matter and mattering anyway.

They went into him one after another, and Corvane stopped talking somewhere around the tenth, and by the twentieth he was making a sound I had never heard him make, a small continuous sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and by the thirtieth he was on his knees on my floor with his head in his hands and the memories pouring through him like floodwater through a broken dam.

Because here is what forty-one years of brokering looks like from the inside. Not a ledger. Not numbers. Faces. Hundreds of faces, thousands, each one coming home to roost with its whole life attached, each one whispering *you did this, you did this, you took this and sold it and counted your coin.* There is no wall high enough. There is no arithmetic that holds. A man can spend a lifetime refusing to imagine the people behind his transactions, but imagination is exactly what memory is, and I was giving him four decades of it at once, undiluted, unpriced, unreturnable.

"This is cruelty," he choked.

"No," I said, kneeling so I could speak near his ear, the way he'd knelt beside me when I was nine and learning the wrist-turn of the lift. "Cruelty would have been the knife. This is mercy. That's what makes it unbearable—you'll want, for the rest of your life, to deserve it, and you won't be able to."

"I built roads," he whispered. "I fed—during the famine, I fed—"

"You fed yourself first," I said. "And then you fed the crown-thing. And the children, Corvane. Tell me about the children." I hadn't planned to say it, but Wren was standing there, half-transparent at the edges, spending years of themselves to hold a monster still, and my voice came out with edges. "Tell me about the tithe. Tell me about the girl who escaped in the night twenty-six years ago. Tell me her name."

He was crying now, openly, horribly, a man drowning in his own conscience exactly as designed, and through the tears he said the name I had been carrying in a gap in my chest my whole life without knowing its shape.

"Elodie," he said. "Her name was Elodie. Oh—oh, gods, I can see her, I can see the whole night, I erased it, I erased it from both of us, I—"

"I know," I said, gentler now. "I know. I found her myself, in my own hoard. She's waiting for me in a cache under the floorboards, along with everything else I ever stole—including, as of tonight, everything you ever stole." I stood. My knees ached. I was suddenly, enormously tired. "You're going to live, Corvane. That's the punishment. You're going to live with all of it, every face, every price, and you're going to spend whatever years the gods grant you giving it back. Every memory. To everyone. Starting with the families of the tithe-children. I have a list. You're going to help me check it twice."

He nodded against the floor. Or maybe he was bowing. With Corvane it had always been impossible to tell the difference between surrender and strategy, and I supposed I'd find out which this was soon enough.

Wren released his arms and swayed. I caught them before they fell, and they were lighter than they should have been, the way candles are lighter after they've burned. They looked up at me with the old light still flickering in them and pressed one small hand flat against my chest, right over the gap, and though they said nothing aloud, the stones hummed a note I was beginning to understand.

*Stay,* the note said. Or possibly *soon.* The old language was still mostly beyond me. But I was learning.

* * *

We put Corvane in the good chair, because I am my mother's daughter even if I didn't know it until last week, and my mother would never have let a man drown on a bare floor. He sat there shaking, muttering names—names, names, hundreds of names, the ledger finally speaking in voices—and Wren sat cross-legged at his feet listening, cataloguing, keeping.

Near dawn he surfaced long enough to say, hoarsely, "Why? You could have killed me. After everything. Why *this*?"

I was banking the fire. I didn't turn around.

"Because my mother spoke a word once and was unmade for it," I said. "Because the crown-thing eats consent and calls it loyalty. Because everything rotten in my life started with someone deciding that a person could be spent like coin." I set the poker down. "I'm done spending people, Corvane. All the way done. Consider it the last thing you ever taught me."

He laughed then—a cracked, wet, ruined sound, the laugh of a man whose books have finally, permanently failed to balance. "Elodie," he said, trying the name out like a coin of doubtful minting. "Your mother chose well."

"She chose," I said. "That was the point of her."

The rain had stopped. Through the window, the eastern sky was doing something pale and hopeful, and the road north—the humming road, the remembering road—waited with all its patient ghosts. Somewhere past it lay Aldermont, and a square filling with children, and a king who barely knew his name, and a word I now carried whole in my chest, my mother's last syllable, burning like a coal I could not put down.

Seven days. Perhaps fewer. The renewal was beginning.

I looked at Wren, fading and fierce, and at Corvane, broken open and—for the first time in forty years—honest, and at the floorboards beneath which my entire stolen life slept in a cedar box.

"Wren," I said. "Help me move the table. It's time to open the hoard."
