## 28. I Was There

The memory begins the way all her mother's memories began: with bread.

Not eating it—though there was eating too—but making it. Seven-year-old Elodie sat on the flour-dusted bench in the kitchen of a house she could no longer picture from the outside, swinging legs that didn't yet reach the floor, watching her mother work dough like it had personally offended her.

"It's not fighting you, Mama," Elodie said. "It's just sitting there."

"Everything is fighting me," her mother said, without looking up. "The dough. The weather. The king." She punched the dough once more, decisively, as if the king were in it somewhere. "Especially the king."

"Why the king?"

Her mother's hands went still. Flour hung in the light from the window, turning slowly, the way snow falls when it has nowhere important to be.

"Because he made a bargain," her mother said at last, "and bargains are like dough. You can't unmake them. You can only decide what shape they'll be when they're finished rising."

Elodie, who was seven and whose entire understanding of bargains was that her friend Tam's older brother had traded two frogs for one excellent beetle and regretted it immediately, nodded gravely. "Did the king trade badly?"

"The king traded everything," her mother said. "And now the thing he traded with wants to be paid."

* * *

She remembered the house being full of people that week. That was the strangest part, looking back—the way the whole village had filled up like a jug under a tap. Cousins she'd never met slept in the good room. Strangers with mud on their boots argued in low voices by the hearth until three in the morning. A woman named Sera, who had hands like tree roots and a laugh like a door hinge, taught Elodie a clapping game while the grown-ups talked about things in words that were too heavy for children: *Oath*, *tithe*, *unbinding*, *the word*.

"What word?" Elodie asked Sera, mid-clap.

Sera's hands stopped. The door-hinge laugh didn't come.

"Not a word I can say to you, little bird," Sera said. "And pray you never have cause to learn it."

Which was, Elodie would think much later, from inside a life built on the ruins of this one, exactly the wrong thing to say to a seven-year-old. Because seven-year-olds were small and quick and nobody watched them, and seven-year-olds heard *everything*.

* * *

Here was what the grown-ups did not know their children knew:

They knew that the king was dying, or worse than dying—that something wore him. They knew the kingdom had been saved, seven years ago and seven years before that, going back further than grandmothers could count, and that each time it was saved, children went into the palace and did not come out. They knew the grown-ups called it *the renewal* and said it quietly, the way you'd say the name of a sickness.

And they knew—I mean *they* knew, the children of that village, huddled in haylofts and under tables—that their mothers and fathers were planning something enormous and probably stupid, and that it involved the old language, the dead tongue, the one that lived now only in songs and in one battered book wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose hearthstone.

Elodie knew about the book because she had seen her mother take it out at night, after Elodie was supposedly asleep, and read it by candlelight with an expression Elodie had never seen on her before. It wasn't fear. It was the face her mother made kneading dough, right before she decided the dough had lost.

* * *

The night it happened, there was a storm, which felt correct. Storms usually attended important events; even then, Elodie understood that the world had a sense of occasion.

She was supposed to be asleep. She was instead crouched at the top of the stairs in her nightgown, watching through the banister slats as the crowded front room became something else entirely. Candles everywhere. People standing shoulder to shoulder—farmers, a blacksmith, Sera with her root-hands, the miller's wife holding a sleeping toddler, and at the center, her mother, with the oilcloth-wrapped book open in her arms.

Her mother was speaking. Not shouting. Speaking the way you speak to a frightened animal, low and steady, and the language coming out of her mouth was nothing Elodie had ever heard, and yet—

And yet it made the room hum.

That was the part Elodie could never explain afterward, in whatever life came next, to whoever might listen: the humming. The floorboards hummed. The candle flames stood up straighter, like soldiers hearing their own names. The rain against the windows fell in time, drumbeat-steady, as if the storm itself had stopped to listen.

Someone in the crowd wept. Someone else laughed—a wild, terrified laugh—and was shushed.

Her mother raised her free hand, and Elodie saw, with a clarity that would follow her down decades like a faithful dog, that her mother's hand was shaking. She was afraid. She was doing the biggest thing anyone in the room would ever do, and she was afraid, and she was doing it anyway.

Elodie learned later that this had a name. Several names. But in that moment, seven years old on a cold stair, she only knew that she wanted to be brave exactly like that when she grew up. She wanted her hands to shake and to keep moving anyway.

Her mother spoke the third syllable.

* * *

What happened next did not happen in any order Elodie's heart could accept.

There was light—not bright light, not comfortable light, but light like the memory of light, light the color of the space between lightning and thunder. It came up through the floorboards, out of the walls, out of the book itself, and the book caught fire without burning, pages glowing like embers breathing.

And her mother—

Her mother was still speaking. That was the horror of it, the part that lived in Elodie's chest forever after like a swallowed stone: the syllables kept coming, steady and low, even as the edges of her mother began to go soft. Not bleeding. Not screaming. *Unmaking*. Her fingertips blurred first, like ink touched by wet cloth. Then her sleeves. The hem of her dress unraveled upward into threads of that same between-light, and the threads drifted, and the light took them, and her mother's voice never wavered, never stopped, kept speaking the unbinding word to the very last—

Because the word needed a speaker, Elodie understood somehow, even at seven. And the Oath did not permit speakers. The Oath ate them.

The crown-thing, far away in its palace, had felt her mother reaching for its leash. And it had reached back.

"Mama," Elodie said. She hadn't meant to. Her voice came out tiny and enormous at once, and every head in the room turned toward the stairs, toward the small figure in the nightgown, and someone said *oh no* and someone else said *get her back upstairs* but it was already too late, because her mother looked at her.

Looked at her with eyes that were already half-light, already going, and smiled.

It was not a frightened smile. That was the other thing Elodie carried forever. Her mother looked at her daughter and smiled the exact smile from the kitchen, the *this dough thinks it can beat me* smile, and her voice—her voice found strength somewhere, rose over the humming, and she spoke again, fast now, urgent, not the great rolling syllables of the unbinding but something shorter, something small, aimed straight at her daughter like an arrow across a burning field—

And then the light closed over her like water, and the book dropped from empty air onto the floorboards, and where her mother had been there was only a drifting brightness that faded the way breath fades from a window, and the room was dark, and the storm sounded ordinary again, and everyone was screaming except Elodie.

Elodie was walking down the stairs.

She walked past the adults, past Sera whose root-hands reached for her, past the miller's wife with the wailing toddler, past all of them, and she knelt down in the spot where her mother had been standing, and she put her hand flat on the warm floorboards, and she said, in a voice that shook exactly the way her mother's hand had shaken:

"I'll finish it."

She was seven. She didn't know the word. She didn't know what she was promising or what it would cost or that she was promising it to a room full of witnesses who would spend the rest of their lives trying to protect her from herself. She knew only that her mother had been brave with shaking hands, and that bravery, apparently, was inherited.

Sera knelt beside her. Took the small cold hand off the boards and held it in both of her root-hard ones.

"No, little bird," Sera whispered, and her door-hinge voice broke clean in half. "You'll live. That's how you'll finish it. That's the only way anyone ever finishes anything."

* * *

Afterward—after the crying and the burying of an empty coffin because there was nothing left to put in it, after the neighbors stopped meeting her uncle's eyes in the road—came the forgetting.

Elodie didn't understand it at first. She thought grief was simply doing what grief does, hollowing her out. When she couldn't remember her mother's face, she blamed the tears. When she couldn't remember her own name being spoken, she blamed the silence of a house with no one in it to say it.

But there was a man who came to the village that autumn. A quiet man with a ledger and soft boots and a smile that arrived a half-second after his eyes suggested it should. He bought things and sold things and asked gentle questions, and he paid special attention to the orphan girl with the faraway look, the girl the village whispered had *seen it happen*.

He told her uncle he ran a school. He told the girl he could give her a fresh start, somewhere the whispers couldn't reach.

His name, though she would not learn it for twenty years, was Corvane.

And the first thing Corvane ever stole from her—before her childhood, before the storm, before her mother's face and her mother's voice and the promise shouted from a staircase—the first thing he lifted out of her seven-year-old head with fingers that smelled of lamp-oil and old paper, was the sound of the unbinding word itself.

Because a word like that, half-heard through banister slats from a dying speaker's mouth, lodged in a child's memory like a splinter.

And Corvane, even then, especially then, knew exactly what a splinter like that was worth.

* * *

But here is what he missed. Here is what no thief, however careful, however thorough, however armed with ledgers and soft boots and twenty years of practice, ever managed to find:

Her mother had known.

Had seen her daughter on the stairs, had known what the Oath's brokers would come to do, had known the great word would be torn out of every witness head in that room within the season. So in her final seconds, with light already taking her hands, she had done what she always did with dough, with weather, with kings.

She had shaped it differently.

She had folded the true unbinding—not the great three-syllable working that the Oath devoured speakers for, but its hidden heart, the secret kernel her mother had spent seven years digging out of that battered book—she had folded it small, and warm, and harmless, into the only place no broker would ever think to search.

Into the last thing she would ever say to her child.

Four words. Ordinary words. Kitchen words. Words so plain that when Corvane tore the memory out, he skimmed right past them, dismissing them as a mother's goodbye, worthless, sentimental, safe.

He was wrong.

Deep in the hoard of a thief named Ansel Thackeray, in a stolen memory sealed away for twenty years, a dead woman's last sentence waited—patient as buried seed—to be read again by the only person alive who had been there to hear it.

And Ansel, who did not yet know she had ever been Elodie, was about to open the cache.
