## 29. The Mother's Syllable

The memory did not let go of me gently.

I had expected it to end — the night my mother was unmade, the failed unbinding, the seven-year-old child I had been crouched behind a rain barrel with her hands over her ears. Memories ended. That was the whole trouble with them; that was the entire reason I had built a life out of catching them before they could slip away. A memory ends and you are left holding the shape of it like a cup after the water's gone.

But this one kept going.

I sat in the dark of the cottage with the opened cache spread around me — dozens of small glass vials, each one glowing faintly with somebody else's yesterday — and the oldest of them, the one shaped like a gap exactly my size, poured itself into me and then *kept pouring*.

The rain barrel. The screaming. My mother's voice rising into the old language, three syllables of it, and the crown-thing's shadow turning toward her like a weather system deciding where to strike —

And then, instead of ending, the memory folded.

It folded the way Corvane used to fold a letter he didn't want read by the wrong eyes: once, twice, a third time, until the dangerous part was hidden in the creases. And inside the crease there was more. There was *her*.

*Come here, little thief,* said my mother, in a voice I had not heard in twenty years and recognized in half a heartbeat, the way you recognize your own hands.

---

In the memory, I was small. Seven years old, all elbows and stolen apples, standing in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and burnt honey — she had never once in her life made honey-cakes that didn't burn, and I had never once in my life cared. She knelt down so we were eye to eye. Her hands were shaking. I hadn't noticed that when I was seven. Seven-year-olds don't notice their mothers shaking; they assume the world is supposed to tremble.

"Listen to me," she said. "And don't listen with your ears. Ears can be searched. Listen with the place behind them."

Outside, far off, something howled that was not a wolf. In the present, sitting cross-legged on my cottage floor with Wren asleep against the wall and the storm of what I'd learned still settling over me like ash, I felt my whole body go cold.

She had known. She had known the Oath would come for her, known the unbinding would fail, known — gods, she must have *known* — that her daughter would survive, and forget, and be found again someday by someone selling memories out of jars.

So she had done the only thing left to a woman with no time and one child: she had hidden the truth where no oath, no broker, no devouring crown could ever think to look.

Inside a goodbye.

"I'm going to say a word to you now," my-memory-mother said, and her voice did the thing voices do when they're holding themselves together with both hands. "It's just a word. It means nothing. It's a silly sound I made up for you when you were a baby, because you wouldn't sleep and I was desperate, and every mother has one of those. Say it back to me."

And seven-year-old me — gap-toothed, furious about bedtime, unaware that I was being handed the most dangerous object in the history of kingdoms — scrunched up my face and said:

"*Elowen-sai.*"

"That's right." She smiled, and the smile cracked down the middle like river ice in spring. "Again."

"*Elowen-sai.*"

"Again. Until it lives somewhere ears can't reach."

We said it eleven times. I counted, in the memory, the way children count things without knowing why counting matters. Eleven times, my mother's mouth shaping the syllables, correcting my tongue on the third one — "*sai*, not *say*, let it fall off the edge of your voice" — and each repetition, the memory shivered slightly, as though the word were too large for the vessel holding it.

Because it wasn't a nonsense sound. Of course it wasn't. It was the unbinding — the *true* unbinding, the complete one, the one Wren carried only a wounded fragment of — folded into baby-talk and tucked into a child's long-term memory like a knife sewn into a coat lining.

"*Sai*," my mother whispered, the last time. "When you're grown, and if the world is ever cruel enough that you need it — *sai*. It will open anything. Even the things that were locked from the inside."

Then the memory's edges began to burn away, because that was the moment the door came down, and I understood suddenly why this vial had been the hardest thing in my cache to hold, why even Corvane had never dared sell it: my mother had spent her final minutes not fighting, not fleeing, but *teaching*, and the last thing she ever gave her daughter was homework.

The memory ended. This time it stayed ended.

---

I came back to myself on the floor of my own cottage with tears running into my collar and no memory of having started crying, which, given my profession, felt professionally humiliating.

Wren was awake. Of course Wren was awake; the child slept like a cat in a house full of dogs, one ear always cocked for the future. They sat up against the wall and looked at me with those old, old eyes and signed, slow and careful: *You found it.*

"I found her," I said. My voice came out sanded rough. "I found — Wren, she hid it in a lullaby. She hid the word that kills the crown-thing inside a sound she invented to get me to *sleep*." I laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and disbelieving. "Twenty years. Twenty years I've been carrying it around. I've *sold* memories to people at the border while humming it under my breath. I probably hummed it at Corvane. I probably hummed it at the *crown-thing's own couriers* and none of them knew, because who suspects a mother's nonsense syllable?"

Wren's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile but weighted differently — grief and wonder sharing a bench. They signed: *That is how the old keepers hid words. In love. Love is the one vault no oath can pick.*

"Well, she always did say I'd amount to a lockpick eventually," I managed. "She meant it as an insult at the time. I'd been stealing spoons."

Wren laughed — a real one, brief and startled out of them, the first I'd heard since the undercrofts of Aldermont had started costing the child years of their own remembering. It was worth every danger we'd walked through just to hear it. Then the laugh faded, and their hands grew serious.

*Say it,* they signed. *Show me.*

I took a breath. And here is the strange part, the part I will be turning over for the rest of whatever life I have: I didn't have to dig for it. I didn't have to search the way I search a traveler's mind, careful and cold. The word simply rose, the way your own name rises, the way *home* rises — because it had never been stored in me at all. It had been *grown* there, watered eleven times in a burning kitchen by a woman with shaking hands.

"*Elowen-sai.*"

The cottage changed.

Not dramatically — no thunder, no light show, nothing the ballads would bother with. But the stones hummed, deep and low, the same note they'd sung the night Wren first arrived on my doorstep, except this time the hum had a direction. It pointed north. It pointed through the valley walls, past the border where I'd spent my whole adult life buying and selling other people's yesterdays, all the way to a crowned city where a king was being eaten alive in slow motion and children were being gathered in a square.

Wren went very still. Then they pressed both palms flat against the floor, and I watched the hum travel through them like current through water, and their face — their fading, fraying face, missing pieces I was afraid to count — filled with something I can only call relief.

*Complete,* they signed, and their hands trembled. *The word is complete. Mine was broken — they broke it when they broke me, piece by piece, taking years. But yours is whole. Yours was loved whole.*

"It's been in my head since I was seven," I said. "Alongside the names of every person I've ever robbed."

*Yes,* Wren signed, and now the almost-smile returned, gentler. *A thief's head was the safest place in the world for it. No one robs a thief.*

"Don't be certain of that. I've met Corvane."

The name landed between us like a dropped glass. Because that was the other thing sitting in the room with us, patient as debt: Corvane was coming. He had cornered us at the cottage two nights ago and I had drowned him in his own sold-back conscience — mercy as weapon, and it had worked, mostly, mostly — but men like Corvane do not stay drowned. Men like Corvane float. And somewhere south of us, the seven-year renewal was already grinding its gears, and children were being counted into carts, and a queen was standing at a window deciding whether her soul was worth more than her husband's life, and a king was forgetting his own name one hour at a time.

Every day we delayed, the tithe collected interest.

I looked down at my hands. Thief's hands. Spoons, then memories, then — if the gods had a sense of humor, and I was beginning to suspect they did — a kingdom.

"She said *when you're grown*," I told Wren quietly. "She said, *if the world is ever cruel enough that you need it*." I flexed my fingers. "The world has been cruel enough for about twenty years. I think I'm grown. Late developer, but here we are."

Wren nodded once, solemn as a stone circle. Then they stood, swayed — the cost of the undercrofts showing in how long it took them to find their balance — and signed with terrible clarity:

*Aldermont. Before the renewal finishes. Before they take the children.*

"Aldermont," I agreed. And then, because I am who I am, because my mother had raised a spoon-thief and died making sure the thieving instinct got put to proper use: "One condition. We stop at the border on the way out."

Wren tilted their head.

"There are forty-one vials in that cache," I said, nodding at the opened hoard glinting on the floor — strangers' joys, travelers' griefs, a merchant's wedding day, a ferryman's childhood dog, all the small coins of other people's lives I'd hoarded to survive. "I've been meaning to return them anyway. Seems poor manners to go save a kingdom while still holding stolen goods." I paused. "Also, one of them belongs to a woman named Marta who runs the tollhouse, and if I die at Aldermont I want her to at least get her first kiss back. She's mentioned it four times. It was clearly a good one."

Wren stared at me. Then they laughed again — longer this time, helpless, wincing like laughing hurt, which it probably did — and shook their head, and signed one-handed, the way people sign when they've given up on dignity:

*Your mother hid the fate of kingdoms in a lullaby, and you are worried about a tollhouse kiss.*

"Marta deserves nice things," I said with dignity. "It's called integrity. I'm told it's new."

But underneath the joke, quiet as the hum still living in the floorboards, something else was settling into place — something warm and unbearably heavy. All those years I had thought myself empty. All those years I had mourned the self that was stolen from me, the name I couldn't remember, the childhood with a hole shaped exactly like me. And the whole time, the most important thing anyone had ever given me had been riding along in the dark, patient as a seed, waiting for spring.

My mother had lost everything. Her kingdom, her husband, her life, her very existence in her daughter's mind. And she had beaten them anyway. Not with armies. Not with magic. With eleven repetitions of a nonsense word and a burnt honey-cake kitchen and the simple, stubborn, unbreakable arithmetic of love: *they can take everything except what I give away freely.*

"Sai," I said softly, to the empty air, to her. "Let it fall off the edge of your voice."

The stones hummed north.

I packed the vials. I woke the fire down. And at grey dawn, with Wren's small hand in mine and a kingdom's worth of unfinished business humming in my skull, Elodie Thackeray — yes, I was trying the name on, and no, I hadn't decided yet, and yes, it fit better than I wanted to admit — walked out of the valley for the second time in her life.

This time, I remembered leaving.

This time, I was going back to finish what my mother started — and gods help anything that stood between me and Aldermont, because I had a word in my head, a child at my side, and absolutely nothing left to lose except everything I'd just learned I had.
