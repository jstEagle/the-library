## 30. March on Aldermont

The valley let us go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long—slowly, finger by finger, with a reluctance you could feel in your teeth.

I had lived there eleven years. I had thought of it as home for perhaps three of them, and even that had been a kind of lie, a word I used because *hiding place* sounded worse at market. Now, walking north with Wren's small hand in mine and the road unspooling ahead like a rope thrown to a drowning man, I understood what the valley had really been: not a sanctuary but a scab, and we had picked it.

Wren stumbled on the first mile marker and I caught them under the arms before they went down.

"I'm fine," Wren said—or rather, their hands said, quick shapes in the grey morning light. Their voice had been going for days now, spent syllable by syllable bending stone in the undercrofts of Aldermont, buying us our escape one collapsing archway at a time. The old language took payment in memory, and Wren had been paying like a drunkard pays with his father's silver.

"You're not fine," I said. "You didn't know my name this morning."

They looked up at me with those eyes that saw wounds instead of faces, and after a long moment their fingers moved again. *I knew something was missing. The missing thing had your shape.*

"That's not comforting."

*It wasn't meant to be.*

There it was—that dry flicker of humor that had survived everything the crown-thing's brokers and hunters and seven-year tithes had thrown at it. I laughed, which surprised us both, and then I lifted Wren onto my back because pride is a luxury for people who aren't racing an apocalypse with a fading child on their shoulders.

"Tell me where we're going," I said, walking faster. "Out loud if you can spare it. Small words."

"North," Wren whispered, and the whisper cost them; I felt it as a shudder through my spine. "Aldermont. The square. Seven years since the last renewal. The children will be gathered at moonrise."

"How do you know?"

A pause. Then, so quietly I nearly missed it: "Because I was gathered once. Before I ran. Before—" Their hand pressed flat against my shoulder blade, over the spot where a scar I'd never been able to explain sat like a signature I couldn't read. "Before you were there too."

I didn't ask. There wasn't time, and besides, I had spent the last two days learning that most of my questions had answers buried in a hoard of stolen memories I'd carried around like a magpie carrying other birds' eggs, never once wondering whose nest I'd robbed.

We walked. The road remembered travelers—it always had, though outside the valley its memory worked differently, humming faintly under our boots with the footfalls of everyone who'd ever passed—and I found myself matching my stride to a rhythm that wasn't mine, some farmer dead two hundred years who'd walked this way to market every Thursday. It was oddly companionable. The world outside the valley was full of ghosts and none of them wanted anything from me except company.

That lasted until noon, when we crested the hill above the crossroads inn and saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Too much of it, too black, rising from the direction of the border villages in fat ugly columns. And along the road below, moving toward us—or rather, being moved—a procession.

Children.

Dozens of them, walking in a line so straight it could only have been arranged by adults, flanked by men in the grey-and-silver livery of the crown. They weren't crying. That was the worst part. They walked with the careful blankness of children who have been told that whatever happens next, making a fuss will make it worse, and I recognized that walk because—I realized with a lurch that had nothing to do with the road—I had invented it myself, at seven years old, on a night I still couldn't remember.

"They've started early," Wren breathed against my ear. "The gathering. They're taking the outlying villages first."

"Can we get around them?"

"No." Wren's grip tightened on my collar. "Ansel. Look at the rearguard. Third rider."

I looked. Even at this distance, even through the dust, I knew the silhouette: broad, unhurried, sitting his horse the way he did everything, as if the world were a ledger he'd already balanced. Corvane.

My mentor. My maker. The man who taught me that a memory lifted cleanly leaves no scar, and who had apparently spent decades selling children to a thing made of oaths—including, if the journals were right, me.

"He can't see us," I said. "The dip in the road, the hedgerow—"

"Ansel." Wren's voice cracked like river ice. "He sold me. He'll know his own goods."

* * *

We hid in a drainage culvert while the procession passed, Wren pressed against my chest, both of us breathing through our mouths to keep quiet, and I listened to the children go by. One of them, a girl of maybe six, was counting her steps under her breath. She got to four hundred and twelve before the sound faded. I have stolen ten thousand memories and I would trade every one of them to forget the sound of a child counting steps toward a thing that eats children.

When the last hoofbeat faded, Wren pulled back and looked at me, and I saw—really saw, for the first time since the undercrofts—what the old language had taken.

It's hard to describe a person fading. It isn't like pallor or thinning. It's more like watching someone become a sketch of themselves: the details going soft, the edges losing certainty. Wren's face was still Wren's face, but it was Wren's face as drawn by someone describing them from memory. A week ago those eyes had seen the whole architecture of the world. Now they saw, I suspected, mostly me.

"What do you remember?" I asked. "Right now. Everything you can."

Wren held up fingers and folded them down as they spoke, each word deliberate, rationed like the last water in a flask. "The word. All of it. Your mother's voice saying it. You, small, behind a door. The valley. You, now. The plan." A breath. "That's most of it. That's nearly all of it."

"Nearly all."

"The rest is gone, Ansel. I paid it. Stone doesn't come cheap." They almost smiled. "Ask me my own name sometime when we're not busy."

"We'll fix it. After. When the Oath is broken, memories come back—the king's journals said—"

"The journals said *might*." Wren touched my cheek, and their hand was cold. "Listen to me. If I fade past the word—if the word goes—I need you to carry it alone. Can you do that? Can you hold a thing that big without dropping everything else?"

"I dropped everything else years ago," I said. "Apparently I'm very good at it."

* * *

We traveled through the afternoon and into the evening, cutting across fields, avoiding roads, Wren navigating by means I didn't question—some instinct older than kingdoms, a compass needle pointing at the wound in the world. Twice we heard riders and flattened ourselves in ditches. Once a shepherd's dog found us and, after a long inspection of Wren, decided we were acceptable and lay down across my legs as if guarding us, which was either kindness or the dog equivalent of pitying the damned.

The sun set red behind us like a door closing. Ahead, Aldermont showed itself: the crowned city, all spires and banners, lit gold against the darkening sky. From this distance it looked beautiful. From this distance everything does.

And in the great square before the palace—visible even here, five miles off, because they had built bonfires tall as watchtowers—I could see movement. Crowds. And among the crowds, small figures being arranged into rows.

"Moonrise," Wren whispered. "We have until moonrise."

"Then we run."

We ran. I won't pretend it was heroic. I carried Wren piggyback and my lungs burned and my thighs screamed and twice I went down hard enough to taste dirt, and each time Wren's cold hands patted my shoulders—*up, up*—and I got up, because somewhere in that burning city was a king who barely remembered his own name, a queen who had spent seven years pretending not to see what wore her husband's face, and a monster that renewed its lease on a kingdom every seven years with children's lives as rent.

And somewhere in that crowd of small blank-faced children was, very possibly, the next Wren. The next me.

The gates were chaos—refugees from the outlying villages pressing in, guards waving families through with the harried urgency of men following orders they don't like. We slipped in with a miller's family, heads down, Wren tucked against my side beneath my cloak. Inside, the streets were wrong: too clean, too quiet, the way a house is quiet when the argument has stopped and everyone is waiting.

"Which way?" I breathed.

Wren raised one hand—not signing now, just pointing—and their finger trembled, not with fear but with the effort of holding on to something. "The square. But Ansel—" They grabbed my sleeve. "The word needs the Oath's memory. Yours. In the valley, you found it—you found *her*. But speaking it here, in the heart of it, with the crown-thing listening—" Their eyes searched my face, looking for the missing pieces, finding the ones that remained. "It will take everything. Not just the word. *You.*"

"I know."

"Do you? Because I watched my own self pour out of me word by word, and I am telling you, thief, it does not feel like sacrifice. It feels like drowning in warm water. It feels *fine*, right up until—"

"Wren." I crouched to their level, took their cold face in my hands. Somewhere behind us the bonfires crackled and the crowd murmured and the end of the world cleared its throat. "Eleven years ago a mute child walked into my valley and made stones hum, and I have been less bored and more terrified ever since. Whatever it costs, it costs. But no more children count their steps to that thing. Not tonight. Not ever again. Agreed?"

For a moment Wren just looked at me. Then they signed, slow and formal, the way they did for things that mattered:

*Agreed, Elodie.*

My mother's name. My true name, given back to me on a road by a fading child, and hearing it now, here, I felt it settle over me like a coat cut for someone braver.

"Say it again after," I said, standing. "After it's done. Promise you'll say it again."

*If I remember,* Wren signed. And then, seeing my face: *I'll remember. For both of us. That was always the deal.*

We came to the edge of the square as the moon rose.

It was worse than I had imagined, and I had imagined it well; horror has always been my best genre. Bonfires threw light like stage-lamps on rows of children—forty of them, fifty—standing in a great circle around a raised platform, and on the platform stood King Aldric in glass-eyed splendor, his shadow lagging half a heartbeat behind him like a dog that had learned not to trust its master. Queen Maren stood beside him, white-knuckled, smiling the smile of a woman holding a door shut from the inside.

And moving through the crowd, collecting something from the guards at the circle's edge—small clay tokens, I realized. Names. Each child's name written and sealed, because the crown-thing liked its meals labeled—was a broad, unhurried figure in grey and silver.

Corvane.

He looked up, as if he'd heard my heart stop from across the square. His eyes swept the crowd, unhurried, thorough, a fence appraising goods.

And then he smiled, and mouthed two words across fifty feet of firelight and fear:

*Hello, Elodie.*
