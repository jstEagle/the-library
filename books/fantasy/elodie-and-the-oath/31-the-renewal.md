## 31. The Renewal

Maren had learned, over nineteen years of marriage to a king who was only sometimes a king, that the worst moments never announced themselves. They arrived dressed as ordinary days. A Tuesday. A mild autumn morning with the smell of bread from the palace kitchens drifting up through the east windows. A servant humming something tuneless while she laid out Maren's gown.

That was how the renewal always began. Not with trumpets. With laundry.

"Which color today, Majesty?" asked Sela, holding up two sashes.

"The blue," Maren said, because she always said the blue, because the red one had been Aldric's favorite and wearing it felt like wearing a lie over a wound. She caught her own reflection in the glass — a woman of fifty-one with grey coming in at her temples like frost claiming a windowpane — and thought, not for the first time: *you look like someone who has done nothing wrong.* That was the cruelest trick the years had played. Guilt should carve a person. Hers had left no mark at all.

Down in the square below her window, carpenters were building the platform.

They built it every seven years, and every seven years they built it exactly the same, because the Oath did not tolerate improvisation. Four posts. A rail. A small iron bell that had not rung in living memory but which everyone agreed must be there, though no one could say why. The workmen moved with the cheerful efficiency of men who believed they were preparing for a festival, because that was what the heralds called it. The Renewal Festival. There would be bunting. Someone in the College of Heralds had decided, decades ago, that bunting made everything easier, and he had been right, which was its own quiet horror.

Maren pressed her fingertips against the cool glass.

*This is the last time,* she told herself, the way she told herself every time. *Next cycle, I will find another way. Next cycle I will be braver.*

She was fifty-one years old and she had been promising herself next cycle since she was thirty-two.

* * *

The children came at midmorning.

They were gathered — that was the official word, *gathered*, as if they were wildflowers or mushrooms, things that simply appeared in fields waiting to be collected — from across the city and the surrounding valleys. Boys and girls between seven and nine years old, the age the Oath demanded, though no record said why seven-to-nine and no one had ever been fool enough to ask the crown aloud.

There were eleven this year. Maren knew the number before she saw them because she always knew the number; she kept it somewhere behind her ribs where it sat like a swallowed stone until the next cycle pushed it deeper and added another.

Eleven children in their best clothes, because their parents had been told — by letters bearing the royal seal, letters Maren had signed without reading for so many years that her hand now produced the signature automatically, the way a clock produces its ticking — that the king wished to honor the kingdom's youngest subjects with a festival of blessing. There would be sweets. There would be music. The king himself would lay his hand upon each child's head.

And then there would be the part the parents were not invited to witness.

Maren watched from the balcony as they assembled in the square. A girl in a yellow dress was braiding and unbraiding the same strand of her hair over and over, a nervous metronome. Two boys had already become fast friends, sharing something whispered and hilarious, doubling over, elbowing each other. One very small boy — seven, maybe younger, the Oath's arithmetic was generous in only one direction — was holding a wooden horse carved badly enough that Maren could see, even at this distance, that one leg was longer than the others.

He held it up toward the platform, showing it to the workmen as if to say *look what I brought to the festival.*

One of the workmen looked. The man's face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, and he turned back to his hammering, and Maren understood that this too was part of the machinery. Everyone in Aldermont had a moment like that, once every seven years. A moment when the truth brushed past them smelling of wood shavings and childhood, and everyone chose, together, silently, to look away. It was the most efficient spell in the kingdom. Not the Oath. The looking away.

"You shouldn't watch from here, Majesty."

Maren didn't startle. Lord Cassel had a gift for arriving at the exact moment a person least wanted company, which she suspected was not a gift at all but a profession. He stood in the balcony doorway in dove-grey silk, hands folded, face arranged into an expression of bland concern that he wore the way other men wore livery.

"The king will want you beside him for the blessing," Cassel said.

"My husband," Maren said, "does not want anything. That is rather the point of him."

Cassel's smile didn't waver, which was how she knew it wasn't a smile at all but a door held shut. "The ceremony begins within the hour."

"I know the schedule, Cassel. I wrote half of it myself, back when I still believed scheduling was the same as governing." She turned from the window. "Tell me about the girl in yellow."

"I'm afraid I don't—"

"Braid. Yellow dress. Nervous hands. Whose daughter is she?"

Something flickered behind Cassel's eyes — irritation, quickly banked. "A cooper's daughter. From Tanner's Row."

"And the little one with the horse?"

"A farrier's son." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down on silk. "Why do you ask, Majesty?"

Because in nineteen years I have never once asked, and the asking is the first honest thing I have done since my coronation, Maren thought. Because if I learn their names, I cannot pretend they are flowers being gathered. Because Ansel Thackeray showed me a journal written in a dying man's hand, page after page, *please, whoever finds this, please*, and I have been carrying those pages in my chest for three days like a coal I refuse to spit out.

"Curiosity," Maren said instead. "A failing of old queens. We get interested in people."

Cassel bowed and withdrew, and Maren noted — filed away, the way a general files away the position of enemy archers — that two additional guards now stood in the corridor outside her chambers. They had been assigned that morning. *For your safety,* Cassel had said. *With the festival crowds, one can't be careful enough.*

Her safety. Yes. The crown-thing had begun to suspect her. It had been circling for days, wearing her husband's beautiful ruined face, asking questions in Aldric's voice with pauses in slightly the wrong places. *You seem tired, my love. You seem distant. Where do you go at night, my love?* And last night, the worst of it: Aldric's mouth forming the words *I would never let anything happen to you*, spoken with the flat sincerity of a thing reading a phrase it had found useful in a book.

It had offered her a bargain at breakfast. That was how it negotiated — over eggs and toast, with the servants out of earshot, sunlight making the whole obscenity golden and domestic.

*Deliver the child,* it had said, in the voice she had loved for thirty years, *and the king lives out his natural span. Warm. Fed. Smiling. You will grow old together, Maren. Isn't that all you ever wanted?*

And the terrible thing, the thing she would carry even if she somehow carried nothing else, was that for one full breath she had wanted to say yes. One breath. Then she had looked at Aldric's hands — his real hands, trembling faintly around the teacup, the hands of a man drowning inside his own skull — and the breath passed, and she said, "I'll consider it," which was the answer of a coward and also, thank all the patient gods, bought her time.

Now there was no more time. The platform was built. The children were gathered. The bell waited.

* * *

The blessing began at noon.

Maren stood on the dais beside her husband in the full glare of the sun, smiling the state smile, the one that used muscles she had long ago stopped connecting to any feeling. Below, the crowd packed the square — good folk of Aldermont in their festival clothes, eating honeyed nuts, laughing, waving up at the royal family. Bunting snapped in the breeze, red and gold. Somewhere a fiddler was playing. The gentle horror of it all nearly undid her: how lovely they had made it. How much care had gone into making the cage beautiful, year after year, generation after generation. People could forgive almost anything if you gave it music.

Aldric stepped forward to begin the blessings.

She watched him move and hated how well he performed it. To the crowd, he was radiant — tall and silver-templed, his voice warm as hearth-smoke, thanking them for their children, praising the strength of the young, speaking of continuity and blessing in phrases polished smooth by repetition. Only Maren, standing close enough to feel the heat coming off him, could see what lived underneath: the half-second lag between his intention and his motion, like a puppet whose strings ran through syrup. The way his eyes, when they swept across the children, held nothing at all — not cruelty, worse, *appetite without preference*, the way a fire regards whatever is nearest.

His shadow, on the sunlit stones, moved a heartbeat late. It always had. Nineteen years, and no one else had ever noticed, or ever dared.

The first child climbed the steps. The girl in yellow.

Her name, Maren had learned that morning from a household ledger she had no right to read and read anyway, was Petral. Petral Cooper, eight years old, missing two front teeth, fond of eel pie and her grandmother's cat. Maren had read it twice and then pressed the ledger flat under her palms as if it might fly away and take its contents with it.

Petral reached the king. She looked up at him with the wide, uncomplicated trust of a child raised on stories in which kings were kind, and she curtsied, wobbling slightly, and Aldric laid his hand on her head.

The crown drank.

Maren could not see it happen — the feeding was silent, invisible, a transaction conducted entirely in the country of the soul — but she felt it, the way you feel a door slam in a distant room. A pressure. A wrongness. Petral swayed. Her smile stayed fixed on her face but her eyes went briefly, terribly elsewhere, somewhere far and grey, and then she blinked, and laughed at some private joke with herself, and walked down the steps lighter than she had come up.

Lighter. That was the detail Maren had noticed at her first renewal, thirty-two years old and newly crowned, watching a boy descend the platform looking *lighter*, and had spent a sleepless night telling herself it was relief, children were shy, the blessing was over, all was well. It took her three more cycles to name what she was seeing. They came off the platform with less of themselves than they'd carried up. Something scooped out and carried away to feed the thing that kept the kingdom's harvests fat and its borders quiet and its king alive.

Seven children followed. Each one climbed, curtsied or bowed, received the hand, descended lighter. The fiddler played. The crowd cheered each one, warmly, generously, the way crowds cheer anything that looks like joy.

Then came the farrier's boy with the wooden horse.

His name was Dannel. He was so small he had to take the steps with both feet on each stair, and halfway up he paused to hold the wooden horse aloft again — toward the king this time, offering it, because it was a festival and you brought gifts. A ripple of laughter went through the crowd, affectionate. Even Maren felt her mouth twitch despite everything, because the child's solemnity was perfect, absolute, a seven-year-old presenting a one-legged horse to the most powerful man in the kingdom as tribute.

And Aldric — the thing wearing Aldric — paused.

For a moment, just a moment, something moved behind the glass of the king's eyes. Not the appetite. Something older and more drowned. His gaze fixed on the little carved horse and his lips parted, and his hand — his real hand, the trembling one — rose halfway toward it, fingers spread, reaching.

The lag stretched. One second. Two. Three. The crowd murmured, delighted, certain the king was performing some charming bit of pantomime.

Maren stepped closer. Close enough to whisper. "Aldric," she breathed, using the name like a key, like a rope thrown into dark water. "Aldric, can you hear me?"

His eyes came to hers. And there — God help her, there — was her husband. Flickering up through the ruin like a lamp seen through fogged glass, brief and desperate and *his*. His mouth shaped words with no air behind them, and she read them because she had spent nineteen years learning to read that mouth across banquet halls and council chambers and sickbeds:

*Don't let them take me with them.*

Or perhaps: *Don't let me take them with me.* The grammar of the drowned was uncertain. Both were true. Both had been true for nineteen years.

Then the fog rolled back over the lamp, and the crown-thing smiled with Aldric's mouth, and its hand completed its descent onto the head of Dannel the farrier's son, and the pressure came again, the door slamming in the distant room, and the boy walked down the steps lighter, clutching his horse, waving to the crowd, cheered by hundreds.

Two children remained.

* * *

Later — though "later" was generous; it was perhaps four minutes later, four minutes in which Maren stood on the dais watching the ninth and tenth children receive their blessings while her heart conducted a war against her ribs — she would try to identify the exact moment she broke.

She had expected it to be dramatic. In the version she had rehearsed in her head for nineteen years, there was thunder, or at least a raised voice. She stood up in council and denounced the Oath. She flung open the sealed records. She was brave in large, legible strokes.

Instead, she broke over a ribbon.

The eleventh child — the last, a girl named Isbet who had a gap where a tooth was coming in and a ribbon tied in her hair by a mother who clearly loved bows more than the child did — reached the top step and stopped. The ribbon had come loose. It hung by one loop, dragging. Isbet stopped dead on the platform, in front of the entire court and the king and the queen and the assembled clergy of three faiths, and refused to move another inch until it was fixed, because she was seven, and seven-year-olds have an unshakeable conviction that the universe runs on order, and a loose ribbon was a crisis of the first magnitude.

"It's crooked," Isbet announced, to everyone, accusingly.

The crowd laughed. The clergy smiled. The crown-thing extended its hand toward her head regardless, hungry, punctual.

And Maren — before she knew she was moving, before thought caught up with body — knelt.

Right there, on the dais, in front of the whole city, Queen Maren of Aldermont knelt in her ceremonial blue and retied a small girl's ribbon. Her fingers shook. She made the bow twice because the first one was crooked — fitting, she thought wildly, everything today was crooked, the whole kingdom was a bow tied crooked for nineteen years — and then she smoothed it flat and looked up into Isbet's face, round and impatient and gloriously unafraid.

"There," Maren said. Her voice came out strange. Rough. "Perfect. Now — go home after this. Do you understand? Whatever happens next, you turn around and you walk home to your mother."

Isbet frowned, sensing — as children sense — that the adult in front of her had gone briefly mad. "...After the blessing?"

"After the blessing, straight home." Maren's hands closed gently on the girl's shoulders. "Straight home, and don't stop for sweets, and don't look back at the castle, and tell your mother the queen said so."

Behind her, the crown-thing said, in Aldric's warm hearth-smoke voice, pitched perfectly for the crowd: "And now, the final blessing!"

"No."

The word came out of her before she had authorized it. Small, at first. Then Maren rose to her feet, and the word came out again, larger:

"*No.*"

The square did not fall silent all at once. Silence moved outward from the dais in a ring, the way ripples move, the fiddler trailing off mid-phrase, the crowd's murmur thinning to confusion, to hush. Somewhere a honey-nut seller dropped his tray. The sound of scattered almonds on cobblestones was, absurdly, the loudest thing in Aldermont.

"Majesty," murmured Chancellor Vess at her elbow, grey-faced, "what—"

"The festival is concluded," Maren said, and her voice carried now, trained by thirty years of speeches, aimed like an arrow. "The children are dismissed. All of them. Home, now, with my thanks and the king's." She turned and looked directly at the thing wearing her husband's face, and for the first time in nineteen years she did not lower her eyes. "All of them."

Its expression did not change. But the temperature of the air changed — she felt it, a cold rolling off the dais like winter leaking through a cracked window — and beneath her feet, faintly, the iron bell that had never rung in living memory gave a single low tone, though no one had touched it.

"Maren," it said, gently, privately, Aldric's voice wrapped around a stranger's patience. "You are tired. You have been ill. Let us finish the blessing and go inside, and I will make you tea—"

"I have watched you feed on children for nineteen years," Maren said, loudly enough for the third row to hear, and gasps bloomed through the square like bruises. "I signed the letters. I tied the ribbons. I told myself next cycle, next cycle, next cycle. There is no next cycle. Guards!"

The palace guard lined the square's edges, forty of them, spears bright in the sun. Forty faces turned toward her. And here was the moment she had dreaded even more than the crown-thing itself: not knowing whether they were hers or its. Every guard captain for two decades had been appointed by councils the crown quietly steered. She had no idea, standing there, whether she commanded an army or an audience.

"Captain Brannoc," she called. The guard captain — broad, weathered, a man whose daughter Maren had once nursed through a fever with her own hands, back when she still did such things openly — stepped forward and saluted.

"Your Majesty?"

"Stand the children down. Escort them home. Every one of them — including—" her throat closed, opened, "including Petral Cooper and Dannel Farrier's son. Bring them to their parents. Now."

Brannoc hesitated. One second. Two. Maren watched him weigh it — the crown against the queen, habit against something older — and she saw his eyes flick to the children, to the girl in yellow being led blinking toward the platform stairs, to the small boy clutching the wooden horse.

And Captain Brannoc did the most beautiful ordinary thing Maren had ever witnessed. He turned to his forty guards and said, in the bored voice of a man announcing a change in shift rotation:

"You heard Her Majesty. Festival's over early. Escort duty — all eleven, home to their doors. Move."

Forty spears came off their rest as one. The ring of boots on stone sounded like rain beginning.

The cold on the dais deepened. The crown-thing stood very still, Aldric's handsome face tilted, studying her the way a scholar studies a specimen that has done something theoretically impossible. Around its brow, the actual crown — gold, ancient, harmless-looking as jewelry — seemed to tighten, very slightly, the way a fist begins.

"You understand," it said softly, only for her, "what you have undone cannot be re-tied. The Oath holds the borders, Maren. It holds the harvests. It holds *him* alive. Break the tithe and you break all of it — and when it breaks, it breaks *through him* first."

"Then it breaks through him first," Maren said. "He has been asking me to let him go for nineteen years. I have finally learned to listen."

It smiled. It was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it was Aldric's smile, the real one, the one that had charmed her at nineteen across a ballroom — worn like a stolen coat.

"Then we proceed to the renewal of terms," it said. "There is another way to pay, wife. There has always been another way. And it is walking through my gates at this very moment."

Maren felt the words land like ice water. "What did you say?"

But the crown-thing was already turning, raising Aldric's arm to the confused, frightened, murmuring crowd, beginning — with flawless instinct for theater — to transform her rebellion into pageant. And down in the square, at the edge of her vision, near the great western gate of the palace, Maren saw the guards stirring, and heard shouting, and saw a small figure running through the parting crowd toward the keep — running with the desperate, staggering urgency of someone carrying something that was killing them with every step —

— and behind that figure, a second, taller one, fighting through the palace guard with a thief's grace and a face Maren knew from three days of dangerous secrets.

Ansel Thackeray had come back. And the crown-thing was smiling like it had been expecting them both.
