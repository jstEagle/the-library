## 32. Storming the Crown Room

The bells of Aldermont were ringing wrong.

Ansel had heard them ring for festivals on the way in—bright, staggered, a little drunk, the way city bells always were. These were different. These were one note, over and over, flat as a struck coin, and every time it landed Ansel felt it behind their teeth like a word they were being made to forget.

"They're calling the servants," Wren signed. The child's hands had grown slower these last three days, each gesture costing something Ansel didn't want to count. "The ones who are *his*. It's not an alarm. It's an instruction."

"Then we'd better be rude guests and ignore it."

Wren's laugh was small and breathy, but it was there, and Ansel clung to it the way a drowning person clings to a plank that is also, unfortunately, their best friend.

They came up through the undercrofts where Wren had bent the stone days ago—the passages still remembered the child's words, groaning softly when they passed, walls leaning inward like old men trying to eavesdrop. Ansel kept one hand on the stone as they walked. It was warm. Everything Wren touched stayed warm a little longer than it should.

Above them, through a grate, they could hear the square: the gathering of children. The seven-year renewal had begun at dawn. Ansel had seen it from a rooftop—rows of small figures in white, parents standing too still, the particular silence of people who have agreed not to look at something for so long that looking has become impossible.

Maren's guards had stood down. That was the only reason this plan had a spine. Whatever the queen had said to her captains, whatever arithmetic she had finally refused to do, it meant the palace gates had opened like a mouth deciding, at the last moment, not to swallow.

It did not mean the way would be clear.

* * *

The first of the bound servants found them at the foot of the servants' stair—a woman in palace livery, standing perfectly still in the dark, waiting. Her eyes were the problem. They were glass-bright and fixed on nothing, and yet Ansel felt *seen*, the way you feel watched by a portrait that has been painted by someone who hated you.

"Halt," the woman said, pleasantly. That was the worst part. She said it the way a host says it. "The crown requests your patience."

"The crown," Ansel said, "can write me a letter."

The woman moved. Not fast—*smoothly*, horribly smoothly, like a puppet whose strings had been replaced by water—and her hand shot out with strength no scullery maid should own. Ansel ducked, caught her wrist, and used her own momentum to put her into the wall. She crumpled, rose again without expression, and came on.

"I don't want to hurt you," Ansel said, backing up the stair. "You're not even in there, are you? You're somewhere far away, having a much nicer day."

The servant smiled. It was Aldric's smile. Ansel recognized it from the parade route—that glass-eyed splendor, the shape of a face arranged by something that had studied smiling from a manual. Seeing it on a stranger's face made Ansel's stomach drop.

Wren stepped forward and spoke one syllable.

It wasn't loud. It barely qualified as sound—it was more like the memory of sound, a thing that happened in the ear and then kept happening in the chest. The servant stopped mid-lunge. Her head tilted. And then, slowly, like ice leaving a window, the glass went out of her eyes. She blinked. She looked at her own hands. She looked at Ansel.

"What—" she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "What year is it. Where is my daughter. I was told—I was *told* I'd be paid—"

"Your daughter's safe," Ansel lied, gently, because some lies are load-bearing. "Go home. Go now, and don't stop for anyone wearing a crown."

She ran. Ansel turned to Wren, and the question must have been all over their face, because Wren answered before it could be asked.

"It doesn't free them," Wren signed. Their fingers trembled. "It just... opens a door. For a moment. Long enough for *them* to walk out." A pause. Then, smaller: "It cost me my grandmother's face. I don't remember what she looked like anymore."

Ansel knelt and took the child's hands, which were cold as river stones.

"We're going to end this today," they said. "Every step after this one is borrowed anyway. Spend them here."

* * *

The upper halls of the palace were full of bound servants, and the bound servants were full of Aldric's smile, and it turned out that fighting your way through thirty copies of a king's pleasant expression was its own special nightmare.

Ansel fought the way Corvane had taught them—not like a soldier, like a *thief*. Thieves don't win fights; thieves make fights unnecessary. Ansel pulled tapestries down across doorways. Ansel overturned the brazier in the east gallery and let smoke do half the work. When two guards cornered them on the grand staircase, Ansel lifted the memory of a dance right out of the nearest one's head—a waltz, some court function, the man's partner laughing—and shoved it back in sideways, tangled, and the guard simply sat down on the step and began weeping with joy while his colleague tried to figure out why.

"Sorry," Ansel said, stepping over him. "You'll want that back later. It's a good one."

"You steal *memories*?" one of the unbound kitchen staff whispered from a doorway, watching them pass.

"I return them," Ansel said. "Badly, sometimes. But mostly I return them." And then, because the situation demanded honesty: "Today I'm mostly stealing a king."

The staff member nodded as if this were a normal Tuesday and shut the door.

Wren walked beside them through all of it, speaking sparingly—one syllable here, one there, each one a coin spent from a purse that was running visibly empty. Ansel could see it happening. The child was getting *lighter*. Not thinner—lighter, as though the world had begun to doubt Wren's weight and Wren had stopped arguing. Between syllables, Wren's hands moved less. Once, in the long gallery, Wren stopped entirely and stared at a painting of a hunt for a full minute before signing, slowly:

*I think I liked dogs. Was it dogs?*

"It was dogs," Ansel said firmly. "You love dogs. Terrible ones. Ones with too much ear."

Wren's whole face changed, and they walked on holding that like a coal in cupped hands.

That was the arithmetic Ansel couldn't stop doing, no matter how they tried: every door Wren opened brought them closer to Aldric and further from Wren. The unbinding word needed a speaker who still held the Oath's original memory, and that memory lay in a valley that forgot, in a hoard that Ansel had spent ten years building without knowing why. There was still a mountain between here and there. There was still everything between here and there.

But first: the king.

* * *

The crown room was at the top of the oldest tower, because of course it was. Everything cruel in this kingdom lived at the top of something.

The last stretch of stair was guarded by six of the bound, standing shoulder to shoulder, and Ansel knew—knew in the thief's cold arithmetic of the body—that they could not go through six. So they went *around*, which is to say they went up, out onto the tower's outer wall, in a wind that smelled like coming rain, along a ledge wide enough for a cat or a person who had once been paid to be both.

"Don't look down," Ansel murmured.

Wren, signing against Ansel's back where the child rode piggyback, hands hooked in their collar: *I'm looking at the sky. It's nicer.*

"That's the spirit."

Halfway along the ledge, the wind gusted, and below them the square erupted—the renewal procession moving, white-robed children being funneled toward the tower's base, toward the crown room's lower vault. Time was spending itself faster than either of them could earn it back. Ansel climbed. Ansel did not think about the ledger of costs. Ansel thought about a mute child who loved terrible dogs, and put one hand above the other, and climbed.

They came in through a window built for light, not thieves, which is to say it was built exactly for thieves and had simply never been told.

* * *

The crown room was smaller than Ansel expected. Smaller and colder and quieter, the way a throat is quiet right before speech.

King Aldric sat on a plain chair beneath the empty crown stand—no throne, just a chair, as though someone had once understood that thrones were for kings and chairs were for men. He was dressed for the ceremony in white and gold, and he was talking to himself. That was the first thing Ansel understood. He had been talking to himself for a very long time.

"—and the eastern fields rotate fallow, that's correct, that's correct, and the tithe is *necessary*, the tithe is *kind*, the tithe keeps the roads open and the wells sweet, and my name is—"

He stopped. His brow furrowed, a real human furrow, lost and small.

"My name is," he said again, and waited for himself to finish, and didn't.

Something moved in the shadow of the crown stand. Ansel saw it clearly for the first time—not the half-second-late shadow from the parade, but the thing itself, coiled around the stand like a crown that had grown bored of heads. It had no face. It had consent. That was somehow worse.

Aldric looked up. His eyes found Ansel, and for one heartbeat they were clear—terrified, pleading, *present*—and then the glass slid back over them and the pleasant smile arrived like weather.

"Guests," the king said warmly. "How wonderful. The ceremony is nearly ready. You'll want seats near the front. Everyone wants to see how generously the kingdom repays its debts."

"Your Majesty," Ansel said, and bowed, because a thief knows that the fastest way through a locked room is often to act like you own the key. "I've come about your journals."

The smile flickered.

"You've been writing letters inside your own memories," Ansel went on, stepping closer, keeping their voice low and steady the way you speak to a spooked horse—or a haunted man. "Begging whoever finds them to break the Oath. I read them. All of them. Every sealed hour. You asked for help, Your Majesty. I'm the help."

"No," Aldric said—but it was thin, it was *paper-thin*, and underneath it something else said, in a voice like a man surfacing from deep water: "*Yes.* Yes, please. I don't remember my mother's face. I don't remember why I agreed. I only remember agreeing. Please—"

"*Silence*," said the crown-thing, through the king's mouth, in the king's voice, and the temperature of the room dropped.

The bound servants began to come up the stairs. Ansel could hear them—dozens of feet, unhurried, pleasant. Below, the children in the square had begun to sing. It was a lovely song. That was the trap of it; it was genuinely lovely, and it was a counting song, and it was counting down.

Wren tugged Ansel's sleeve and signed, hands slow as honey:

*He can't hear us while it holds his mouth. But he wrote. Writing goes deeper than speech. Give him back what he wrote.*

Ansel's heart did something complicated and painful, because they knew what Wren was asking, and they knew what it would take. The journals' memories—Ansel had carried them since the dark gallery, stolen fair from Maren's keeping, wrapped and weighted like coins in a cut purse. To give them back as living memory meant opening every seal in front of the thing that had written them shut.

It meant showing the crown-thing exactly where the knife was.

Below, the song counted: *...four...*

The bound servants reached the landing. The door handle turned with terrible courtesy.

Aldric looked at Ansel, and behind the glass, faint as a candle behind frosted pane, something *knocked*.

Ansel reached into their coat.
