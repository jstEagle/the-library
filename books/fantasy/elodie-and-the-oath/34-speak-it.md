## 34. Speak It

The crown room smelled of candlewax and old iron, and beneath both—faint as a lie told well—the scent of the valley in autumn. That was my first warning. The second was that the guards had stopped fighting.

They hadn't fallen. They stood where they'd been standing when the last of them went still, swords half-drawn, faces slack as laundry left out in rain. Between me and King Aldric's throne stretched forty feet of marble and forty bodies I would have to cross, except that none of them moved, because none of them had been ordered to move, because something else wanted to talk first.

Wren leaned against me, small and burning-hot, half of them already worn away like a coin rubbed between fingers for too long. Their breathing was a thin whistle. When I shifted my grip they made a sound—not a word, not anymore; they'd spent too many words bending stone and door and lock to have many left—and pressed their forehead against my ribs.

"I know," I said. "Almost there."

The throne at the far end of the room was not a grand thing. Aldric's reign had begun in austerity—a king who'd sworn to sit no higher than his poorest subject—and the chair itself was plain oak, worn at the arms by hands. It was the man in it who hurt to look at.

Aldric sat very straight, very still, glass-eyed and splendid in his ceremonial gold, and his shadow on the wall behind him moved a half-second late, the way it always did now. But his mouth was moving too, silently, shaping words over and over like a man praying or counting or begging. I'd read his journals. I knew which it was.

*Whoever reads this: break it. Please. Whoever you are. Break it.*

"Ansel Thackeray."

The voice came from everywhere—from the candles, from the marble, from the slack mouths of the guards—and it was warm. That was the worst part. It wasn't the cold silk-and-venom voice I'd braced myself for. It was the voice of someone who has known you a long time and is glad to see you, tired and fond and a little amused, the voice Corvane used to use when I came home soaked through with stolen goods and he'd already set tea on the fire.

"You've had a long road," it said. "Sit down. You must be exhausted."

"I'm fine standing."

"Of course you are." A pause, and then, gently: "You get that from your mother."

I didn't flinch. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere keeps records anymore—I did not flinch. But my hands tightened on Wren, and Wren felt it, and lifted their head to look at me with one eye that was still theirs and one that had gone pale as river ice.

"It lies," Wren whispered. Or tried to. The old language came out of them easier than ours did now, and the stones under our feet hummed a low warning chord. *It lies. It only ever offers what it means to take.*

"I know," I said. And then, because honesty is a habit I picked up late and badly: "But listen anyway. You always said a thief should know exactly what she's stealing before she steals it."

The crown-thing laughed. It was a good laugh. I hated how good it was.

* * *

It rose out of the throne without disturbing Aldric at all—which told me everything about how little the body mattered to it—and became, for my benefit, a shape. Not its true shape. I don't think it has one, or rather I think its true shape is *agreement*, is *yes, yes, forever yes*, given flesh. What it showed me was a tall figure in traveling clothes, gray-haired, weathered, kind around the eyes. A innkeeper's face. A neighbor's face. A face like everyone's and no one's.

"There," it said, spreading its hands. "Is this better? People find faces restful."

"People find faces *necessary*," I said. "You're wearing one because you want something."

"I want to give you something." It began to walk toward me, unhurried, and the guards parted around it without waking, drifting aside like weeds in current. "That's all I've ever done, Ansel. Given. Your kingdom was dying—plague in the lowlands, famine in the hills, three claimants sharpening knives. I gave it thirty more years. Thirty years of children growing up, of harvests coming in, of songs being written. Ask anyone over forty whether those years were worth—" it gestured at itself, at the whole gilded room, "—the arrangement."

"The arrangement ate children."

"The arrangement asked for a tithe. Every bargain asks for something. You of all people know that—you built a life on asking travelers to pay for their own memories back." It stopped ten feet away, close enough that I could see the face had no pores. Smooth as an egg. "And here's what I find so interesting about you, thief. You never thought of yourself as cruel. You told yourself the trade kept people fed. Kept *you* fed. You were surviving, same as me. Same as the king, in his way. Same as the queen, God rest her conscience. We're all just making arrangements."

Wren's hand found mine. Small, feverish, gripping hard.

"What do you want," I said.

"To make you an offer. One offer, honestly given, no tricks—tricks are for brokers like your Corvane, and we've both seen how that ends." The smooth face arranged itself into sympathy. "Walk away. Take the child. Go home to your valley."

The valley. My chest did something complicated and shameful at the word, and the thing saw it, because of course it saw everything.

"No one follows you there," it said softly. "No magic can. No hunters, no brokers, no kings. The Oath cannot reach into that place—it's the one blind spot in all the world, and you found it, or it found you, and either way it's yours. Go back. Keep your little trade. Lift memories from travelers and sell them back at the border. Keep the ones you love safe inside a cage nothing can enter."

"And Wren?"

"Wren comes with you. Whole." It said it simply, the way you'd say *and the bread comes with the stew*. "Every year they spent bending stone to escape me—returned. Every memory they burned, restored. They'll be a child again, whole and mute and safe, humming at your table while you cook. Isn't that what you want? Isn't that what you've wanted since the night they knocked on your door?"

It was. That's the truth of it, and I won't dress it up. Some cold practical part of me had been doing arithmetic since the ferry, since the undercrofts, since Wren gave me syllable after syllable and paid for each one out of their own skin: *the child is spending themselves to death, and for what, for a kingdom that sold them in the first place.* Seven-year-olds gathered in squares. A queen who looked away for twenty-one years. A king who begged strangers in his own handwriting to break the thing keeping him alive.

Let the kingdom rot, that part whispered. Let Aldric keep his crown and his shadow and his silent screaming. You owe these people nothing. You were one of their tithes and they never came looking.

"And if I say no?" I asked.

"Then I take the child tonight, the way I took the others, and the renewal proceeds, and in seven years there will be another square full of children, and another, forever, and you'll live in your valley knowing that every traveler whose memory you lift is walking away from a country that eats its young because *you* chose your supper over their sons and daughters." The face didn't change. The voice stayed warm. "I'm not threatening you, Ansel. I'm describing Tuesday."

* * *

Silence, then. The candles crackled. Somewhere below us, in the square, I could hear the gathering crowd—the families of the tithe-children, come to watch, because watching was compulsory and grief was not permitted to be loud. A child was crying down there. High, furious, the crying of someone too young to have learned yet that some things are not cried about but fought.

Wren tugged my sleeve. I looked down.

They couldn't speak. They'd spent nearly everything. But they raised their hand—slowly, tremblingly, the way an old woman raises hers to shield her eyes from sun—and pointed at my chest. Then at their own. Then they laced their small fingers together, interlocking, and held them up.

*I remember for you,* the gesture said. *You carry me. We are knotted.*

"Don't," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "Don't spend words on gestures, you need—"

Wren shook their head. And smiled. That was the thing that undid me, that I will carry into whatever dark place takes me next: the child, half-unmade, hunted for years, sold before they could walk, looked up at the monster wearing a neighbor's face and *smiled*, the way you smile at weather, at rain you've decided to walk through anyway.

The crown-thing watched this with something almost like respect.

"They're brave," it said. "They get that from their mother too, incidentally. There's a great deal of it going around. Bravery, I mean. It's terribly inconvenient." It stepped closer—close enough now that I could smell the valley-autumn scent again, woodsmoke and turned earth, and understood suddenly, sickeningly, that it had taken that smell from somewhere, from someone, that it wore pieces of everyone it had ever bargained with. "So let me make it simple. Not the kingdom. Not the king. Just you, and the arithmetic you already know how to do."

It held up one smooth finger.

"Say nothing. Walk out. Wren whole, valley sealed, safety forever. The price: one word, unspoken. One word, Ansel. You've sold memories for less."

A second finger.

"Or speak it. And here is what speaking costs—because I am honest, I am always honest, honesty is the finest trap ever built." Its voice dropped, gentle as a nurse. "The Oath is made of memory. To unbind it, you must feed it everything you are. Every theft, every kindness, every winter, every name. You will stand in this room and give it all, and when it's done there will be a woman standing where you stood who knows no one, least of all herself. The child will remember for you—they've offered, I heard them, gestures count—but think what that means. Think of waking tomorrow as a stranger in your own skin, being told stories about yourself by a child, deciding which ones to believe. Is that freedom? Or is it just a different cage, with thinner bars?"

The third finger came up slowly.

"Or refuse entirely. Fight. And I will show you what twenty-one years of consent looks like when it stops being patient."

It lowered its hand and spread both palms, an innkeeper welcoming guests, a mentor offering tea.

"One word," it said. "That's all the silence costs. One word, and everyone you love is safe, and the world goes on exactly as it has, and in thirty years no one will remember there was ever any other way to live. That's my true offer, Ansel Thackeray. No tricks. No broker's ledger. Just the oldest bargain there is: your comfort, weighed against strangers' children. Everyone in this room has made it. The queen made it. The king made it, dying, to buy his people thirty years. Even your mother—" and here the smooth face softened into something horribly like tenderness, "—even she hesitated, at the end. Half a heartbeat. I've often wondered, in my long dull centuries, what she'd have chosen if the child hadn't been watching."

The crying from the square had stopped. That was worse than the crying.

Wren's fingers were laced together still, held up between us like a small knot of bone and stubbornness. *We are knotted.* And I thought about the valley—really thought about it, not the postcard version the thing was selling but the truth of it. The fog at the borders that ate names. The travelers who left lighter than they came and never knew why. Me, thirty years old, sitting alone in a cottage with a hoard of other people's sunsets, calling it contentment because I'd forgotten there was anything else to call it.

The cage with thick walls. The cage it was offering to let me keep.

"That's the trick," I said quietly. "There isn't one. That's the whole trick. You're offering me exactly what I already had."

Something flickered across the smooth face—not anger. Surprise. Maybe, in whatever passes for its heart, a flicker of disappointment, the way a card sharp feels when the mark folds without betting.

"Yes," it admitted. "That is the offer."

"Then here's mine." I shifted Wren fully behind me and took a breath that tasted of candlewax and iron and the last hour of my life. "You let the king go. You let the children go. You unravel, quietly, without taking anyone else down with you—and in exchange I don't say the word my mother died hiding inside a sentence, the word I've been carrying since the valley, the word that's been sitting on my tongue for six hundred miles getting heavier."

The crown-thing looked at me for a long moment.

Then it laughed again—that warm, fond, terrible laugh—and shook its head like a teacher hearing a beloved student say something hopelessly naive.

"Oh, Elodie," it said, using the name like a knife laid flat against skin. "Your mother also offered me a deal. Right at the end. Did your stolen childhood tell you that? She stood where you're standing, with the word half-spoken, and she offered to forget it—to let me take the knowing clean out of her, no unmaking, no pain, she'd have lived her whole life never remembering she'd had a daughter worth saving—if I'd only let the tithe pass that one year." It tilted its head. "She was smarter than you in one respect. She knew I'd refuse. She offered anyway, because offering is what the brave do instead of running. And then she spoke the word, and it cost her everything, and it *still didn't work*—because the word needs a vessel that remembers the Oath entire, and she'd already given pieces of herself away for years, buying her little girl's escape one memory at a time. She was too hollow to hold it. She burned for nothing."

The room was very quiet. Down in my chest, where I kept the recovered memory of that night—the smoke, the square, my mother's mouth shaping her last word to me—something settled into place with a click I felt in my teeth.

"She wasn't too hollow," I said.

"Child—"

"She wasn't too hollow. She was *saving it*. The word doesn't break the Oath. It *carries* it. It has to be spoken by someone holding the whole of it—" I looked at Wren, at the pale eye and the bright one, at the laced fingers slowly lowering, "—or by someone willing to become the whole of it. She couldn't give what she'd already spent. She spent it on me. On purpose. So that someday the word would arrive here inside someone who'd spent nothing, hoarded everything, kept every stolen memory like coins in a cache under the floorboards." My throat was tight and my eyes were hot and I was smiling, I realized, actually smiling, at the thing wearing a neighbor's face. "Thirty years of thieving. Every sunset I ever lifted. You thought I was greedy."

The crown-thing went very still. Stillness, from a thing made of agreement, is a terrible sound.

"You would gamble everything," it said, "on a theory. About a dead woman's arithmetic."

"Not a theory." I reached into my coat and closed my hand around the journal pages against my heart—Aldric's pleas, Maren's confession, Wren's syllables learned at the cost of years. "A family trait."

Behind me, Wren made a sound. Small. Cracked. But it was a word—one of ours, not the old tongue—and it was my name, the name I'd carried since a storm-night knock on a cottage door, and they said it like a hand letting go of a rope: *Ansel.* Meaning *go on.* Meaning *I have you.* Meaning, in the grammar of children who have lost everything twice, *whatever it costs, I will remember.*

The crown-thing sighed, and the candles all bent sideways at once, though there was no wind.

"So be it," it said, and the warmth drained out of its voice like water from a cracked bowl, leaving something older underneath, vast and patient and finally, finally honest. "Then speak, thief. Feed me everything. Let's see what your mother's arithmetic buys."

I opened my mouth.

I thought of the valley, and let it go.
