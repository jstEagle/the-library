## 36. Unmade, Unbound

I have no word in the old tongue for what happens next.

That is not quite true. I have many words. I have a whole language that hums in the stones and sleeps in the roots of trees, and somewhere in it there is certainly a word for the moment when an oath given flesh forgets how to be flesh. But I am half-erased myself, and my memory is a house with most of its windows broken out, and so when the crown-thing begins to come apart above King Aldric's kneeling body, all I can think, in the plain speech of travelers and thieves, is: *oh*.

*Oh* is a good word. It fits in small spaces. It fits inside a girl who has spent years of herself buying passage through stone and silence, who gave away her grandmother's laugh and her own birthday and the color of her mother's eyes to keep one syllable safe behind her teeth. *Oh* fits in the space where those things used to live.

The crown-thing unravels like a rope cut at both ends.

I do not mean it falls apart dramatically, though there is drama enough — the throne room of Aldermont is full of it, dust falling from rafters, guards on their knees, Queen Maren standing very straight with her hands folded as if she is at a funeral she has been rehearsing for twenty years. What I mean is that it comes apart the way a lie comes apart when someone finally says the true thing out loud. Slowly, then all at once, then slowly again, because even endings are stubborn.

Ansel spoke the word.

I gave it to them only minutes ago — pressed it into their hands the way you press a bird into someone's palms, wings folded, heart hammering, trusting them not to squeeze. The unbinding was never really three syllables. It was never really seven. It was a sentence, and the sentence was a door, and the door could only be opened by someone holding the Oath's first memory, which meant walking back into the valley that forgets, which meant Ansel opening their hoard of stolen lives and finding, buried under everyone else's griefs and joys, the night their mother spoke the word and was unmade for it.

They found it. I felt them find it, even across the miles, the way you feel a lamp lit in a far window.

And then they came back. They came back through the humming road and the ferryman's water and the gates of a city gathering children in its square, and they stood in front of the thing wearing their king, and they said *no* in a language older than kingdoms, and they paid for it with everything.

That is the part I keep circling. That is the splinter in the soft place under my thoughts.

The Oath demanded a life of memory. Not a life — a life would have been simple, clean, the kind of arithmetic kings understand. It wanted memory. All of it. Every room in the house. And Ansel, who had already given up their hoard to find the door, who had nothing left but the self they had only just learned they had — Ansel said yes anyway.

So now I watch the crown-thing unravel, and I watch Ansel stand blank-eyed among the falling gold light, and I try to hold both truths in my two hands without dropping either one.

* * *

Here is what unraveling looks like, up close, when you are the only person in the room who can see it properly:

The crown-thing is made of consent. That is what I saw the first day, watching Aldric parade through his city in glass-eyed splendor while his shadow moved a half-second late. It is not a parasite, exactly. Parasites take. This thing was *given*. A dying kingdom, seven years from starving, and a young king on a new throne who swore an oath he did not fully read — or perhaps read fully and understood fully, which is worse and also more forgivable — and the oath grew fat on his yes, year after year, renewal after renewal, until it wore him like a coat wears a hanger.

When Ansel speaks the unbinding, the consent is withdrawn. Not by Aldric — he is too far gone, too hollowed, too much a man-shaped absence — but by the word itself, which is older than consent and does not care about bargains. The old tongue does not argue. It simply states what is true, and truth, in that language, is not negotiable.

So the crown-thing unravels, and as it unravels it tries to hold on, and this is the part I will carry in whatever is left of me forever: it looks *scared*.

It is a monster. It ate children. Seven-year renewals, tithe after tithe, decades of small frightened hands carried north in covered carts, and Corvane's ledger listing names in tidy ink, and a queen pretending not to see, and a whole court learning to look slightly left of the truth. I know all this. I watched Ansel's face when they read the coronation memory; I watched the revulsion move through them like weather. I have every right to hate it.

But I was raised — before I was raised by silence — in a kingdom that remembered things. And the oldest thing my people remembered was this: oaths are made by the desperate, and desperation is a kind of innocence, and innocence does not stop being innocent just because it grows teeth.

So when the crown-thing frays, and its edges go gold and then gray and then thin as breath on glass, and it turns — I swear this — it turns toward the king it has worn for twenty years, and something in its unraveling reaches for him, not to feed but the way a drowning man reaches for a plank, and I feel pity move through me like cold water.

It fed on his consent. But he fed it his consent. They starved together, all these years, in a gilded room, each mistaking the other for food.

The last of it goes out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. There is a sound — not loud, more felt than heard, a hum passing down through the floor, through the foundations, through the bones of the mountain the city stands on — and every stone in Aldermont sings one low note, the note of a word being finished, and then the throne room is just a room.

Dust settles. Torches gutter and steady. Somewhere outside, a bell rings, confused, having forgotten why it started.

And King Aldric collapses.

* * *

He collapses human, and human is not a graceful thing.

Twenty years of being worn leaves a man like a garment left out in weather. He goes down sideways, catches himself on the arm of the throne — the actual throne, the ugly iron one beneath the gilded ceremonial one, the one nobody sits on — and slides to the floor, and then he is a big man in rumpled state robes kneeling on cold stone, and he begins to weep.

Not nobly. I want to be clear about that, because the ballads will get it wrong within a season. He weeps the way men weep who have forgotten how, badly, hiccupping, snot-nosed, both fists pressed against his mouth as if he can push the sound back in. His shadow lies still beside him on the floor, moving exactly when he moves, late by nothing at all, and I think that might be the truest miracle in the room: a shadow keeping honest time.

"Elodie," he says, or tries to say. His voice is rusted. "You — the thief — you were—"

Ansel does not answer.

Ansel is standing where the crown-thing used to be, and Ansel's face is doing something I have never seen it do, and I have studied that face the way scholars study dying languages, because it was going to be my work to learn it back. Ansel's face is doing *nothing*. It is open and calm and empty as a bowl set out in rain, and their eyes move across the room — across Maren, across the guards, across the king on his knees — and there is no recognition in the movement. None. Not even the ordinary recognition you give strangers, the polite cataloguing of *person, guard, queen*. They look at Aldric the way you look at furniture.

"Ansel," I say.

Nothing.

I should have expected this. In some broken-windowed room of my mind, I think I did expect it, and chose not to look at the expectation directly, the way you don't look directly at a debt coming due. The Oath took a life of memory. Ansel gave theirs. All of it. Which means the woman standing in the ruin of the throne room, breathing, alive, upright, is a person with no past at all — not even a false one, not even the stitched-together valley-self, because that went into the hoard, and the hoard went into the door, and the door is open, and everything walked through it.

Even me. Even Wren. Whatever Ansel knew of me — the storm, the cottage, the humming stones, the three syllables I taught them that burned holes in the world's silence — gone. I am a stranger standing in a strange room, and the only person who ever bothered to learn my language cannot remember that I have one.

Guards are stirring. Maren crosses the floor to her husband and kneels beside him, and I watch her put her hand on his shoulder, and I watch her face, and here is a thing worth noting: she is crying too, but quietly, the way she has clearly practiced for years, and underneath the tears is something that looks almost like anger, and underneath the anger is relief so vast it frightens her. She loved a ghost for two decades and pretended the ghost was a husband. Now the husband is back and the ghost is gone and she does not know yet whether that is mercy or a second loss. Give her time. Queens are slow deciders and fast regretters.

Aldric looks up at Ansel. "What did it cost?" he rasps. "Child, what did you pay?"

And Ansel — blank-eyed, bowl-faced, empty-handed Ansel — smiles.

It is a small smile, puzzled, pleasant, the smile you give a stranger who asks directions. "I'm sorry," they say, and their voice is their voice, warm and dry and a little rough at the edges, but the person shaped around the voice is missing. "Do I know you? Where is this?"

The king makes a sound like something breaking quietly.

And I make a decision.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being half-erased: what remains becomes louder.

I lost my grandmother's laugh, my birthday, my mother's eyes. I lost years of myself buying passage through stone. But what stayed — the old tongue, the feeling of words like held birds, the knowledge of what oaths weigh — what stayed got *brighter*, the way a candle seems brighter in a darkening room. And standing in this ruined throne room, watching a king weep and a queen shake and a thief stand empty as a rung bell, I find that what is brightest in me is not grief.

It is purpose.

Because I know something none of them know. I know what Ansel did, minute by minute, choice by choice, from the moment a mute child knocked on a cottage door during a storm to the moment they opened their hoard and let their whole life walk out the door to buy a kingdom's freedom. I was there for nearly all of it. I remember the way they talked to the ferryman — grumbling, generous, slipping the man an extra coin they couldn't afford. I remember them teaching me to whistle with a blade of grass on the humming road, badly, both of us laughing while hunters' torches bobbed behind us. I remember them reading the king's journals by candlelight and pressing the book flat against their chest afterward, saying nothing, because some heartbreak doesn't fit through the mouth.

I remember Ansel. Completely. Every story, every scar, every joke, every kindness done in secret and every cruelty avoided at cost.

Which means that somewhere in this world, in this exact square of floor, walks the last copy of a person the rest of creation has forgotten.

I am eleven years old and I have given away most of myself and I am tired down to the marrow, and I look at Ansel's empty, pleasant, waiting face, and I decide — the way you decide to breathe, the way you decide to keep walking — that I will give it all back. Story by story. Day by day. However long it takes. I will tell them about the valley and the trade and the merchant who recognized his own grief. I will tell them about Corvane, and the ledger, and the mother whose final word hid a kingdom's freedom inside a goodbye. I will tell them their name.

Their name. I still have it. Of course I still have it. It was the first thing they ever told me, on the second morning, over burnt porridge, with the specific embarrassment of a person who thinks their name is unremarkable.

Ansel Thackeray. Thief of the valley that forgets. Keeper of a hoard that bought a door. The bravest coward I ever met, and the best liar, and the worst whistler in either of our histories.

They turn and look at me — really look, the way you look at a stranger who is staring — and tilt their head, and there is something underneath the emptiness, some sediment the Oath's flood didn't reach, and it makes them frown faintly, the way you frown at a tune you almost know.

"Do I—" they start, and stop, and frown harder. "Why do I want to say *porridge*?"

My chest does something complicated and painful and glad.

"Not yet," I tell them, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. "But soon. I have a lot to tell you, and it starts with porridge, and you're going to be embarrassed about the whistling."

Around us, the kingdom begins to notice that its long nightmare has ended — bells finding their reasons, guards rising on shaking legs, the gathered children in the square below being told, at last, that they can go home. Aldric is helped to his feet by a wife he must relearn and a court he must forgive. The crown lies on the flagstones where it fell, and it is only metal now, dented, ordinary, and no one wants to touch it.

Ansel looks at the crown, then at me, then at their own empty hands.

"So," they say, polite and lost and somehow still, unmistakably, themselves at the bottom of the well. "Who exactly am I supposed to be?"

I take their hand. Their fingers curl around mine without knowing why, and that is enough, that is everything, that is the first page.

"You're going to be someone I tell you about," I say. "Come on. We should start while the porridge is still a fresh memory."
