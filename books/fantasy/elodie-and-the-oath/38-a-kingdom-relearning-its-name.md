## 38. A Kingdom Relearning Its Name

The first thing Aldric did as a king newly returned to himself was cry in front of his entire court.

He had not planned it. He had planned a speech—three drafts of it, actually, written in the small hours when sleep still came to him like a stranger at the door, uncertain and easily startled away. The first draft was dignified. The second was contrite. The third was mostly an apology to the children.

What came out of his mouth instead, standing on the dais in the great hall of Aldermont with the crown-thing's ashes—or whatever one called the residue of an oath unmade, a fine grey dust that had taken the servants three days to sweep from the flagstones and that Maren had insisted be kept in a jar, "for evidence," though he suspected she simply liked having it where she could see it defeated—was none of the above.

"I don't remember any of you," he said.

The hall went very quiet. Four hundred courtiers, guards, clerks, and one extremely nervous Master of Coin looked at their king, who was weeping openly into a handkerchief embroidered with his own crest by someone whose name he did not know.

"Twenty-two years," Aldric continued, when he could. "You have been governing yourselves around me for twenty-two years, and I have been—" He gestured vaguely at his own head. "Elsewhere. I know some of your faces because it wore my face looking at them. I do not know your names. I do not know which of you I promoted and which of you I ruined. I do not know if I ever thanked the woman who runs the kitchens, and I am told her name is Bess, and I am told she has fed this court through famine and festival alike, so Bess, if you are here, thank you. And I am sorry."

Somewhere near the back, someone began to clap. It caught like fire in dry grass, and for a moment the sound filled the hall, and Aldric stood in it feeling like a man being warmed by a hearth in a house he'd only just learned he owned.

Then Lord Halloway—the same Lord Halloway whose memory Ansel had lifted in the palace corridors, though Aldric would not learn that detail for another week—stepped forward with the particular expression of a man about to say something helpful and ruinous.

"Sire," Halloway said, "the tithe rolls are prepared for the renewal, and given the, ah, irregular circumstances, the council thought perhaps—"

"No."

"Sire, the Oath is broken, but the kingdom's protections—"

"Halloway." Aldric descended one step from the dais. Then another. He found that walking toward people, rather than being carried past them by something wearing his legs, was a pleasure he intended to practice daily. "There is no tithe. There will never again be a tithe. Gather the rolls and burn them. Gather the children who were taken and bring them home. Gather every record of every bargain, every ledger, every name, and put them in a room where anyone may read them, because the fastest way to grow a monster is to keep its feeding schedule secret." He paused. "And then gather everyone responsible, starting with me, and let us see what can be repaired."

Halloway opened his mouth.

"That was not a request, and also I appear to have become a person who gives orders rather than suggestions, which is new to both of us, so let us all be patient while I learn."

That got a laugh. A real one, startled out of people who had spent two decades laughing only when the thing in the crown laughed first. Aldric decided he liked the sound more than any anthem.

* * *

The house of returned memories took four months to build, and it was Maren's idea, though she delivered it the way she delivered everything: sideways, at breakfast, disguised as a complaint.

"You cannot simply burn the ledgers," she said, buttering toast with the precision of a surgeon. "People will want to know what happened to their children. Where they went. What was paid. You'll have mothers camped on the palace steps within a fortnight, and they will be right to."

"So we tell them."

"We tell them what? That a broker named Corvane sold their daughters to a crown for twenty years?" She set down the knife. Her hands were steady; they were always steady now, in a way that Aldric had come to understand was itself a kind of wound. Steadiness, in Maren, was what pretending had calcified into. "Aldric. Half the noble houses of this city bought their safety with other people's children. If you publish those ledgers, there will be blood in the streets."

"There may be," Aldric agreed. "But there will be truth in the streets too, and truth washes out faster than blood. My journals taught me that much." He reached across the table and covered her hand before he could think better of it—a habit he was rebuilding, touch by touch, like a man relearning a language. She did not pull away. Progress. "Maren. Twenty-two years I watched you carry this alone. I remember almost nothing of it, but I remember the shape of it. The weight of you. Don't ask me to start our marriage over by keeping secrets together. Let's try keeping none."

She looked at his hand on hers for a long moment.

"A house, then," she said finally. "Not a burning. A house. Every record copied, every name written plain, open to any soul who walks in. Let people find their own griefs at their own pace. And let the ones who profited walk in too, and read their own signatures, and live with the neighbors knowing." A thin smile. "Shame is a better punishment than the gallows. It lasts longer and requires less rope."

"You've thought about this."

"I've had twenty-two years to think about everything," Maren said, "and no one to say it to. You have no idea how much of me there is backed up."

And so the House of Returned Memory rose in the old granary square, which everyone agreed was fitting—a building that had once held grain for the many would now hold the past for the many. Aldric insisted on laying the first stone himself, badly, with mortar up to his elbows, because he had read in his own recovered journals that he had once promised the people a king who worked, and he intended to honor promises made by the man he used to be even when the man he used to be had been mostly puppet.

The ledgers went in behind glass, page by page, Corvane's neat accounting hand exposed to daylight at last. Beside them, on open shelves, sat the memories themselves—or what remained of them. When the Oath broke, everything it had hoarded had come loose like water from a burst dam, and Wren had spent three days in the valley directing the flood, sending recollections home along roads that remembered footsteps. Not all of them arrived whole. Some arrived as fragments, sensations, a smell of bread or a snatch of lullaby. The House kept those too, in labeled jars, and people came and sat with them for hours, holding a jar of someone else's wedding day just to feel the warmth of it, and leaving a coin in the box, and sometimes leaving a note: *If this is yours, I'm sorry. If it isn't, I hope whoever lost it finds something sweeter.*

On the first morning the doors opened, Aldric stood in the square and watched the line form. It stretched past the fountain, past the chandler's, out of sight down Cooper's Lane. Old women. Soldiers. A baker still in his apron. Children too young to have lost anything, dragged along by parents who wanted them to see what remembering looked like.

Ansel stood at the front of the line.

They looked smaller than Aldric remembered—which was absurd, since he had met them exactly twice, once bleeding in his throne room and once blank-eyed in its ruins. But something about them had gone quiet. They stood the way a man stands in a house he's been told is his but cannot recall entering.

"You don't have to queue," Aldric said, coming down to meet them. "You built half of this. Well. You didn't build any of it, physically, but morally—"

"I like queues," Ansel said. Their voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone handling their own words like borrowed tools. "Queues are fair. Everyone waits the same amount. I remember liking that." A pause. "I don't remember anything else much. But I remember liking queues."

Something in Aldric's chest folded. He knew that voice. He had heard it in his own head for twenty-two years, muffled behind the crown-thing's humming: *I remember liking that. I don't remember anything else.*

"The child," Aldric said carefully. "Wren. They said they'd stay with you."

"They're around." Ansel glanced back down the lane, and for half a second something moved behind their eyes—not memory exactly, but the place where memory would go, like light through a curtained window. "They keep telling me stories. About me. I get to decide which ones are true." A beat. "So far I've decided most of them are true, which is either flattering or suspicious."

"It's both," Aldric said. "In my experience that's the definition of a good story about oneself."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost. It was there and gone like a fish under ice, but Aldric had spent two decades learning to read faces for the thing behind them, and he saw it: not happiness, not yet, but the ground where happiness might take root.

"Go on in," Aldric said. "Read whatever you like. Start with the ledgers if you're brave, or the jars if you're not. And Thackeray—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "No. That's not right anymore, is it. I'm told you have another name."

"Wren says I do," Ansel said. "I haven't decided yet."

"Well," said Aldric, "when you do, I hope it fits. Mine never did until recently. Turns out it was 'Aldric' all along and something else was wearing it."

* * *

Winter came early that year, soft and white, and with it came the first session of what the court clerks insisted on calling the Commission of Restitution and what everyone else called, with affection, the Apology Office.

It met in a long room in the House of Returned Memory, at a table long enough to seat twelve petitioners and twelve officials facing each other, which Aldric had designed deliberately so that no one would sit higher than anyone else. He chaired it himself, every third day, over the objections of three separate advisors who felt kings should not spend their mornings listening to grievances.

"My advisors feel kings should not listen to grievances," he told the room on the first day. "I have reminded them that I spent twenty-two years not listening to anything, and look how that turned out. So. Who's first?"

The first petitioner was a cooper named Dannel whose daughter had been taken in the eleventh year of the tithe. He did not shout. He stood at the table with his cap in his hands and said, "I don't want gold. I want to know if she was frightened."

And Aldric, who had read the ledgers cover to cover, who knew the dates and the sums and the broker's marks, had to say, "I don't know. I'm sorry. I don't know."

But a clerk did know—one of the young archivists, a sharp-eyed woman named Pell who had made it her business to interview every survivor—and Pell stepped forward and said, "By all accounts, master cooper, the children were treated gently in the palace years. Frightened at first. Less so after. One of them, a girl called Sera, taught the others a counting game to pass the evenings. Your daughter was the best at it."

Dannel sat down slowly, like a man setting down something very heavy, and wept quietly into his cap, and said thank you, and Aldric thought: *this*. This was the repair. Not the proclamations, not the stones and mortar. This—a stranger handing another stranger one true sentence about their lost child, and the sentence being enough to bear the weight.

The weeks turned. Petitioners came and were heard. Ledgers were copied and posted. Three barons resigned in disgrace; two were tried honestly and sentenced fairly; one fled north and was reported, months later, to be running a ferry crossing and being miserable about it, which the court agreed was justice of a particularly elegant design.

And Maren stayed.

Aldric had braced for her to leave. He had drafted, in his worst hours, the speech he would give her—*you owe yourself a life outside these walls, you owe yourself a decade of saying what you think*—and rehearsed it in the mirror until he hated it. But she stayed, and not the way she had stayed before, keeping vigil over a hollow husband. She stayed loudly. She argued with him in council and won, frequently. She reopened the queen's court, which had been a fiction for two decades, and filled it with merchants' wives and scholars and the mothers of tithe-children, and made the nobility attend, and enjoyed their suffering immensely.

One evening in early spring, he found her in the gallery where she had once bargained with Ansel in the dark, standing before the window with a cup of wine, watching the lamps of the House of Returned Memory burn late.

"Do you regret it?" he asked. "Staying."

"Every day," she said pleasantly. "Usually around the time you misplace your reading glasses in the soup."

"Maren."

She was quiet a moment. "I regret the years I can't give back to you. To any of them. I regret that I kept the peace by pretending, and that pretending is a habit I may never fully break—you'll notice I still butter toast like I'm defusing it." She turned to him, and in the lamplight her face was tired and open and entirely her own, no performance anywhere in it, and Aldric thought he had never seen anything braver. "But no. I don't regret staying. Someone has to hold this kingdom accountable, and I have a list, and it's alphabetical."

"Is my name on it?"

"Under A," she said. "Obviously. Right after *arrogance*."

He laughed, and she smiled, and outside the window the lights in the House burned on, and somewhere in the city a child slept in her own bed for the first time in seven years, dreaming whatever children dream when the monsters have been swept out and the doors left unlocked.

Repair, Aldric was learning, was not a single act. It was a thousand small returns. A name spoken correctly. A debt confessed. A jar of borrowed joy given back to its owner. A wife who said what she meant. A king who cried in front of his court and was not struck down for it.

It was slow work. It was the only work worth doing.

He was thinking this—standing at the window beside his wife, watching the lamplight, feeling the strange new weight of an ordinary evening—when a knock came at the gallery door, and a footman entered looking faintly bewildered.

"Begging your pardons, Majesty. There's a child below asking for you. Says she's from the House. Says—" the footman consulted a scrap of paper, frowning, "—says the thief has picked a name. Says you'll want to be there when she says it out loud."

Maren raised an eyebrow at Aldric. Aldric was already reaching for his coat.

"Well," he said. "Let's not keep her waiting. I'm told she's been deciding for quite some time."
