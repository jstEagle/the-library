## 39. Teaching the Thief Their Name

The cottage in the valley had never been a home. It had been a shop, a trap, a place where Ansel Thackeray kept their hoard behind loose stones and slept with one ear open for travelers on the humming road.

Now it was something else, and neither of us had a word for it yet.

I did most of the talking. That was the arrangement, and it was not a small one. Ansel—Elodie, though the name still sat on them like a coat borrowed from someone taller—had given every memory they owned to break the Oath, and what remained was a person-shaped room with the furniture gone. They knew how to speak. They knew how to boil water and mend a hem and lie convincingly to a customs officer, which I suspected would outlast language itself. But who they had been, why they flinched at the smell of woodsmoke, why they hummed three notes of a lullaby and then stopped as if the fourth note were a cliff edge—all of that lived in me now.

I remembered everything. That was my burden and my gift, and some mornings it felt like both at once.

So we built a ritual. Every evening after supper, I told them one story about themselves, and they got to decide whether to keep it.

"Decide," Elodie said the first night, frowning at me across the table. "You mean decide if it's true?"

"I mean decide if it's yours," I said. "Those aren't always the same thing."

They considered that for a long time, turning their cup in their hands the way they turned everything—carefully, looking for the seam where it might be pried open. Old habits. Then they nodded once, and that nod became our law.

* * *

I started small, because you do not hand someone an entire self on the first night any more than you feed a starving traveler a whole loaf.

"Your hands," I said. "You used to juggle."

Elodie looked at their own hands as though meeting them. "Thieves don't juggle."

"This one did. Badly. You practiced with river stones behind Corvane's counting house because he said idle hands made bad thieves, and you decided that if your hands had to be busy, they'd be busy at something he hadn't taught you." I smiled. "You dropped them constantly. You broke a window once. You blamed a goat."

"There are no goats in this valley."

"There were no goats *then*, either. That's what made it such a good lie. Corvane spent two days searching for a goat that had never existed, and you laughed so hard you gave yourself hiccups, and he knew—he absolutely knew—and he let it go anyway, because even then, even selling children to a crown-thing, there was some part of him that liked you."

Elodie was quiet. The fire ticked. Outside, the valley hummed its low, empty note—the sound of a wound in the world, though it was softer now, since the Oath had unraveled. Some nights I thought the valley was learning to be merely a valley.

"Do I want to keep that one?" they asked finally.

"It's yours to want."

They flexed their fingers. And then—slowly, awkwardly, with all the grace of a man learning to walk on new legs—they picked up an apple from the bowl and tossed it into the air and caught it against their chest when it escaped.

"The goat story stays," they said, hiccupping.

* * *

Some stories were harder.

The third week, I told them about the merchant whose grief they'd pocketed and sold back to him twice. I told them about the memory they'd kept—the one they should have returned—and how carrying it had been the first crack in the armor Corvane had fitted onto them.

Elodie listened without moving. When I finished, they said, "Do I feel guilty?"

"You don't have to feel anything I tell you to feel."

"No—I'm asking what I did feel. Before." Their eyes came up, sharp and honest. "If I'm going to rebuild myself, I'd rather build from true stones."

"You felt guilty," I said. "And you kept doing it anyway, because guilt is a luxury when you're hungry. And then you hated yourself a little more each time, and you told yourself the trade was mercy—*selling people back to themselves*, you called it—and some days you almost believed it."

"That's ugly."

"It's true. Those aren't always the same thing either."

They laughed—a short, startled sound, as if laughter had surprised them by living here too. "You've been saving that line."

"I learned it from you. Week two. You said it about the name Elodie."

They went still again. The name still landed hard sometimes; I could see it settle over them like weather.

"Tell me another," they said. "A better one. Balance the ledger."

So I told them about the winter they'd fed a family of travelers for free—not sold back their memories, but fed them, bread and stew and a bed by the fire, and sent them over the border with their sorrows intact. How they'd lied to themselves about why. How the mother of that family had pressed a wooden bird into their hand at the door, carved clumsy and sweet, and said, *For the keeper of the pass*, and how Elodie had kept that bird for eleven years and told no one, including themselves, what it meant.

Elodie stood up without a word, went to the loose stone behind the hearth, and drew out the cache.

It was empty now, of course. Every stolen memory surrendered, every hoarded year poured out to break the Oath. But things remained that were only things: the wooden bird, worn smooth by a thumb's long attention. A child's shoe. A copper coin from a kingdom that no longer existed.

They held the bird for a long time.

"I remember holding it," they said slowly. "Not the giving. Not her face. Just—the weight. Like a word on the tip of the tongue." They looked up at me, and their eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once. "Is that mine? The weight?"

"That's yours. I'd swear it on anything you like."

"Then I'm keeping it." They set the bird on the mantel, facing the room, where a thing loved can see the ones who love it. "The rest of the cache—" They nudged the child's shoe with one toe. "Whose is this?"

"Yours," I said. "You were seven."

They looked at the shoe for a long moment, and I watched them decide—not to believe me, exactly, but to trust me, which is a braver act and a different one.

"Not tonight," they said quietly. "That story can wait for a bigger fire."

* * *

We argued sometimes. I want that recorded somewhere, because the ballads—if anyone ever writes ballads about this, and Aldric's court poets are already circling like well-fed gulls—will make it sound solemn and clean, the wise child restoring the broken hero. Mostly it was us bickering over supper.

"You said I was funny," Elodie said one night, suspicious. "Constantly funny. I don't feel funny."

"You're extremely funny. You just haven't noticed yet, because noticing requires memories of being funny, and I currently hold the entire archive."

"Give an example."

"When Corvane asked you to steal a memory from a man who owed him money, you told him you couldn't, because the man's head was 'already mostly receipts.' Corvane didn't speak to you for a week. He was furious. He also wrote it down in his ledger, under your name, which means it was one of his favorite things anyone ever said."

Elodie stared at me. Then, helplessly, they grinned—and for a moment the grin fit their face so perfectly that I understood something I'd been circling for weeks: the stories weren't building a new person. They were excavating one. The grin had been there all along, buried under the blankness like the road under snow.

"All right," they said. "Maybe I'm funny. What else am I?"

"Stubborn. Kind in ways that annoyed you. A terrible singer who sang anyway. Frightened of deep water. Loyal past the point of sense—you crossed a kingdom for a mute child you'd known a fortnight."

"That last one I remember," they said softly. "That one stayed."

"Some things stay," I agreed. "The Oath took what you gave it. It couldn't take what you *were*. There's a difference, and you're finding the seams of it."

They reached across the table and put their hand over mine—the hand that had spoken the unbinding word until it burned, the hand that had held mine through the unraveling. Their grip was warm and slightly sticky with stew.

"Thank you," they said.

"Don't thank me yet. Tomorrow's story involves a hat."

"What kind of hat?"

"A feathered one. You wore it for a month. You were magnificent. Everyone suffered."

* * *

But there was one story I saved, and saved, and saved, because I was afraid of it—not for them, but for me.

It was the story of how we met. Or rather, the true version of it, because Elodie remembered the shape of it: a storm, a knock, a mute child on the doorstep. What they couldn't know was what I had been doing before the storm, or why I had come to that door at all, or what the old tongue rising in my throat had cost me before I ever spoke a word of it aloud.

So one evening, when the fire was high and the rain was doing its patient work on the roof, I set down my spoon and said, "Tonight's story isn't about you."

Elodie looked up, wary in the old way. "Whose is it?"

"Mine. But you're in it, and you should hear it from me rather than find it in some ledger someday. We've both had enough ledgers."

I told them about the palace undercrofts. About the tithe-children gathered in the dark, waiting for the seven-year renewal, and how the older ones sang to the younger ones in a language no one taught them because it lived in the blood. About how I had been chosen early—not for the crown-room, but for training, because the crown-thing's brokers had discovered that a child who could speak the old tongue could carry words that bound as well as unbound. About Corvane coming for me specifically, with his soft voice and his ledger, and how I had run, and hidden, and finally fled north with hunters behind me and the unbinding word burning a hole in me like a coal I couldn't spit out.

"And the storm," I said. "I chose your door. I'd heard rumors of a thief in the magicless valley who traded in memory, and I thought—magicless means the crown-thing can't follow, and a thief means someone who understands that some things shouldn't be owned. I gambled everything on knocking."

Elodie was very quiet. "You used me."

"I sheltered with you. There's a difference, but it's thinner than I'd like." My voice cracked, and I let it. "And then you fed me. And juggled badly to make me laugh. And when Corvane came, you chose me over the only life you had, and by the time we reached Aldermont I wasn't using you anymore. I was—" I stopped. Started again. "You asked once why I stayed beside you after the Oath broke, when I could have gone anywhere, remembered by everyone, restored to whatever the old kingdoms would have made of me. This is why. Because you're the first person who ever chose me without knowing what I cost."

The fire settled. Rain on the roof, soft as an apology.

Elodie reached over and took the wooden bird off the mantel and pressed it into my hands.

"For the keeper of the pass," they said. "That's what she called you, isn't it? In the story. She meant you, not me. She saw it before either of us did."

I hadn't known that. I had carried that memory for years and never seen it, because I had been looking at the wrong person the whole time. That is the trouble with remembering everything: sometimes you need someone with nothing to point at the thing right in front of you.

I cried, a little. Elodie pretended not to notice, which was, I am fairly certain, something the old Ansel had also been excellent at.

* * *

Spring came to the valley the way spring comes everywhere—without permission and slightly ahead of schedule. The humming road grew green along its edges. Travelers began to pass through again, real ones now, with their memories intact, and some of them stopped at the cottage because word had traveled: the thief who broke the Oath lives here, and the child who speaks the dead tongue, and they'll give you soup if you knock politely.

One afternoon a woman knocked. Gray-streaked hair, a carter's shoulders, eyes that went around the room cataloguing exits before they found Elodie's face.

She stared. Elodie stared back, polite and blank.

"Sorry," the woman said, flustered. "You look—forgive me. Someone I knew. Years ago. A girl who worked the border trade." She shook her head. "She'd be grown now, like you. It's nothing. Thank you for the soup."

She left. Elodie stood in the doorway a long time, watching the road take her.

"Wren," they said at last, without turning. "Was that—"

"I don't know," I said honestly. "There were hundreds of travelers. I remember the ones that mattered to you, and you never told me about everyone." I hesitated. "But I can tell you what I *do* know, and you can decide."

They came inside. Sat down. Put their hands flat on the table, ready.

"Tell me," said Elodie, who had been Ansel, who had been nobody, who was becoming someone new out of old stones. "Who was I?"

And I opened my mouth to answer—but before I could, they raised one finger, and the ghost of a grin crossed their face, and they said:

"Wait. First—was I ever actually a girl named Elodie, or is that the part I get to choose?"
