## 40. Elodie

Spring came to the valley the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

I noticed it first in the stones. For thirty years they had been silent under my feet, dumb as any other stones anywhere in the world, because that was the valley's whole trick: nothing here remembered anything, not the road, not the rain, not me. But on a morning in late March, when I stepped out of the cottage with two cups of tea and nearly dropped them both, the path beneath my boots gave a hum so faint I thought at first it was my own blood in my ears.

"Wren," I called through the open door. "The road is doing something."

"It's been doing it for a week," Wren said, appearing at my elbow with the particular smugness of someone who has known a thing for a week and let you discover it on your own schedule. "You're just finally quiet enough to hear it."

"I am always quiet."

"You hum when you sweep."

"I do not."

"You do. It's terrible. The stones have started humming back out of pity."

I looked down at the path. It was true—the hum had a shape now, a low patient note, like a held breath or a friend waiting for you to finish a story. The valley was remembering. Not everything, not all at once; the Oath's cage had been broken open, but a wound that old doesn't close in a season. What remained was this: small recoveries. Stones that hummed. Travelers who came through the pass and left with their memories still their own, blinking at us in confusion when we offered to sell anything back, because there was nothing to sell anymore. The trade was dead. I had killed it myself, along with everything else I'd been.

Most mornings that felt like freedom. Some mornings it felt like standing in an empty house where all the furniture had been mine.

Wren watched my face do whatever my face does when it does that thing—they'd learned to read me by now, which felt unfair given how long I'd gone unreadable even to myself—and took one of the cups from my hands before I could spill it.

"Sit," they said. "I brought you something."

"You live in my house. Everything you bring me is technically already mine."

"Not this." They sat down on the step beside me, knees drawn up, the way they always sat, as if ready at any moment to bolt—which, once, had been the truth of them, and now was just habit, like my humming. "This is yours from before either of us knew each other. Before you knew yourself, actually. Which is the point."

They reached into their coat—that coat, the palace coat, too fine for the valley, that they refused to give up—and drew out a scrap of leather, folded soft with handling. Inside it lay a strip of paper, old, water-stained, written over in a hand I didn't recognize and then recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own reflection in a window you weren't expecting.

My handwriting. Or what had been my handwriting, once, in a life I had paid away syllable by syllable on the steps of a crown room.

"What is it?" My voice came out careful. Everything about me came out careful these days. When you've given up every memory you own, you learn to hold the present tense very gently, like a full cup.

"Your name," Wren said.

* * *

Here is what I knew of myself, in the spring after the end of everything: I knew I had been a thief. I knew I had been a tithe-child, sold south every seven years' worth of a kingdom's cowardice, and that I escaped, and that Corvane had scraped the escape out of me to keep me useful, and that my mother had spoken the unbinding word with her last breath and hidden its heart inside her final word to her child. I knew these things because Wren had spent the winter giving them back to me, story by story, sitting across my own table in the lamplight like a merchant laying out wares—except the coin was true, for once, and the goods were me.

It was strange work, being rebuilt. Wren would tell me a piece and watch my face, and sometimes the piece would land somewhere in my chest and ring true—a memory of cold, a memory of running, the smell of tallow candles that made my stomach clench for reasons I couldn't name until Wren named them: the crown room, the night of the failed unbinding, the night my mother burned bright and brief as a struck match. And sometimes the piece would land wrong, would sit in me like a stone in a shoe, and I learned—I am still learning—to say so.

"That's not how it happened," I told Wren once, early on, when they described seven-year-old me as brave. "I wasn't brave. I hid under a table."

"You hid under a table," Wren agreed, "after you went back for the other children. Those are both true. You were the kind of brave that hides under tables afterward."

I hadn't known what to do with that. I still don't, entirely. But I kept it.

And here is what I did not know, what no story had restored, because there had never been a story for it: my name. Corvane had taken it first of all things, before he took anything else—"a thief needs no name," he used to say, "names are for people with something to lose"—and so Ansel was the name I'd worn like a borrowed coat for thirty years, and when the Oath took everything else, it took even the borrowing, and I woke on the crown room floor knowing no one, least of all myself.

Wren had chosen to stay. That was the part I still couldn't make fit. The child who remembered everything—for both of us, they said, that was the bargain they'd made with themselves without telling me—could have gone anywhere, been anyone, carried the old tongue back to the old places where people still whispered it. Instead they stayed in a magicless valley with a hollowed-out memory thief, teaching me my own life one evening at a time, correcting my sweeping-hum, stealing the last of the tea when they thought I wasn't looking.

"Why?" I asked them once, in the deepest part of winter. "You could be anywhere. You could be *someone*. Why here?"

Wren considered the fire for a long time. They had a way of considering things that made you feel considered too.

"Because everyone remembers the king," they said finally. "Everyone will remember the queen, and the battle, and the word spoken on the palace steps. Someone has to remember the rest. The small things. What you looked like when you were happy before you knew why. What tea tastes like to someone drinking it for the second first time." A shrug, elaborately casual. "Also the valley is less boring with you in it."

That was Wren. Devotion without debt, delivered deadpan.

Now they sat beside me on the step and held out the paper, and my hands shook taking it, and I hated that they shook, and Wren pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

* * *

The paper was a page torn from a ledger—Corvane's ledger, I realized, the one with names I'd never remembered giving. But this entry was different. Older ink. A different hand beneath the entries, cramped and hurried, a note scrawled in a margin:

*Girl, ~7 yrs. Won't say where she's hurt. Won't cry. Calls herself—*

And then the name.

*Elodie.*

The world did not tilt. I want to be honest about that, because I think we expect the big moments to come with trumpets, and mostly they don't. The world stayed exactly where it was. The path hummed its low patient note. Somewhere down the valley a bird was being obnoxious about spring. And I sat on my own doorstep holding a strip of paper with a stranger's name on it, except it wasn't a stranger's, except maybe it was, except—

"Elodie," I said aloud, to try it.

Nothing happened. No thunder, no hum from the stones, no sudden flood of a childhood returning. It was just a word, four syllables, soft in the mouth, ending open the way a door ends when you leave it ajar.

"It was your mother's mother's name," Wren said quietly. "Your mother wrote it in the margin herself. She brought you to Corvane's door—did I tell you this part? I don't think I told you this part. She brought you to his door the night before she went south, because she knew what was coming, and she made him write it down. Made him. Put a knife to the ledger and everything. She said—" Wren stopped, and I watched them gather the words carefully, the way you carry water in your hands. "She said, *whatever you take from her, she'll have this much of herself left.*"

The cup trembled in my hands. Tea rippled.

"She fought Corvane," I said. "For my name."

"For one word. Out of everything. She couldn't save your memories, couldn't save you from the tithe, couldn't save herself. So she spent her one victory on four syllables." Wren's voice did something complicated. "I've been thinking about that all winter. About what it means, that of everything a mother could keep for her child, she chose the name."

"And Corvane kept the paper? He kept it all these years?"

"He kept everything. That was his sickness—he threw away people and kept the receipts." Wren nodded toward the road, north, toward Aldermont, where Corvane sat now in a cell of his own returned memories, drowning gently in thirty years of everything he'd ever sold, every face, every price. We had done that to him with mercy, and mercy, I had learned, cuts deeper than knives and heals slower. "When they cleared out his offices, this was pinned inside the ledger's spine. The archivist sent it up with the rest. I asked if I could be the one to bring it."

"Why you?"

"Because I'm the keeper now." Wren said it simply, without ceremony, the way you'd say *I'm tall* or *it's Tuesday*. "Of what was lost. That's the whole job. And this—" they tapped the paper "—was lost longest, and belonged to you most."

We sat with it. The bird kept going. The tea cooled. The name sat in the air between us, patient as the humming road.

* * *

I want to tell you that I decided immediately. That I stood up, dusted off my skirts, and claimed my birthright with a flourish. But the truth is I sat on that step for a long time, turning the paper over and over, and what I felt was not triumph. It was fear.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about getting yourself back in pieces: every piece changes the shape of the whole. Ansel I understood. Ansel was the thief, the survivor, the person who had hoarded other people's joys because they had none of their own, and who had broken the world open anyway, at the cost of everything, and was now learning to drink tea slowly and let people stay. Ansel was a name built out of absence, and absence, at least, I knew the contours of.

Elodie was a girl under a table. Elodie was a mother's four syllables. Elodie was someone who had existed before the valley, before the trade, before the armor—and I had no memory of her at all. Taking the name felt like putting on a dress fitted for a seven-year-old and expecting it to hold a woman of forty-odd years and several apocalypses.

"Ansel worked," I said at last. "It got me through. It's not nothing."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's not nothing. It's a good coat."

"But?"

"But coats are for weather." Wren pulled their knees tighter. "Names are for people. Look—I'm not going to tell you what to do. That's the whole point of giving it back. It's yours. Yours to take, yours to refuse, yours to keep in a drawer and look at on bad days. I just—" They exhaled. "I watched your mother spend her last win on it. I'd hate for it to die in a drawer."

Down the path, the stones hummed. And I swear—not loudly, not dramatically, but I swear—the hum shifted when the name hung in the air. Found a new note. Like an instrument retuning itself around a sound it had been waiting thirty years to hear.

Or maybe I imagined it. Maybe grief and hope sound alike in the ears. But Wren heard it too; I saw it land in their face.

"The valley remembers you," they said softly. "Before you do. Isn't that something? All those years it forgot everything, and the one thing it held onto, underneath, was you."

* * *

I packed light. Old habit—you don't survive as a thief by loving your possessions—but also new habit, because I owned almost nothing that was truly mine, and I found I liked it that way. A change of clothes. The tea tin. The knife I'd stopped carrying for work and started carrying for comfort. And the paper, folded soft, in the pocket over my heart, where important things go.

Wren watched me pack from the doorway with an expression I'd learned to distrust.

"You're coming, then," I said. Not a question. Wren's whole body had been answering it since dawn.

"I'm the keeper of what was lost," they said. "You're about to walk out of the valley carrying the single most lost thing there is. Where exactly did you think I'd be?"

"Aldermont is a long walk."

"I've survived worse walks. I've survived *you* teaching me to bake. Nothing frightens me anymore."

"My bread was structurally sound."

"Your bread could have been used to shore up the ferry dock. The ferryman still talks about it. Fondly, but still."

I laughed—actually laughed, out loud, in the doorway of the house where I had spent thirty years being careful—and the sound surprised us both. There had been laughter over the winter, small ones, experimental ones. This one was bigger. It had the name in it somehow. I can't explain that. Some things you just know the way you know a door is unlocked.

At the gate—the old border-stone, the one where travelers had once handed me their griefs and walked away lighter and poorer—we stopped. Because that was the line, wasn't it? For thirty years, everything past that stone had been danger, and everything before it had been safety, and I had built my entire self on the difference between the two sides of a rock.

Wren put a hand on the stone. It hummed under their palm, louder than I'd ever felt it.

"It's not a wall anymore," they said. "You know that. It hasn't been since the Oath broke. It's just a rock that remembers being a wall. Kind of like you."

"That's morbidly accurate. Thank you."

"You're welcome." They looked up at me, and for a moment all the deadpan fell away, and underneath it was the child from the storm, the one who had arrived speaking the dead tongue while the stones sang, the one who had carried the unbinding word in a mouth too small for it, the one who had spent years of their own memory bending stone so we could run, and who had chosen—freely, stubbornly, without debt—to remember me when I could not. "Whatever name you walk out with," Wren said, "walk out. Don't hide. Hiding was the valley's job and it's retired."

I looked down the road, north, toward a kingdom relearning its name—toward a king who wept human tears now, and a queen who had stopped pretending, and a house of returned memories where people lined up daily to take back pieces of themselves, some weeping, some laughing, one man who reportedly hugged the archivist so hard they'd instituted a policy about it. Toward work, probably. Toward trouble, certainly. I was still, at bottom, a thief, and there were so many things in the world that had been stolen—memories, children, years, names—and somebody ought to steal them back.

Somebody with experience.

I took out the paper. Unfolded it one more time. Read the cramped, hurried, desperate, victorious hand of a woman who had lost everything except one word, and spent that word on me.

*Elodie.*

"Ansel got me through," I said again, quieter now, to the paper, to the stone, to the woman in the margin. "But I think you'd have liked to know it did. And I think—" I folded the paper closed and pressed it flat over my heart. "I think I'd like to answer to the name you won."

The stones hummed. The road hummed. Even the obnoxious bird, briefly, shut up, which felt like applause.

"Elodie," Wren said, trying it, grinning. "Elodie. Oh, that's *much* better. Ansel always sounded like a sneeze."

"It did not."

"It absolutely did. Elodie and Wren." They shouldered their bag and stepped over the border-stone without ceremony, without looking back, the way you cross a threshold when you're not afraid of what's behind you. "Come on, then. The kingdom's waiting, the archivist owes me money, and I've been saving up a story about your grandmother that is going to *ruin* you."

I followed. Of course I followed. I stood one moment longer on the safe side of the stone, just one, long enough to feel the hum rise up through my boots and settle somewhere behind my ribs, like a name finding its place.

Then Elodie Montrose—no. Just Elodie. Just beginning.

Walked out of the valley, not to hide, but to start.
