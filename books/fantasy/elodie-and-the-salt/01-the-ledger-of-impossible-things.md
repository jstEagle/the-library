## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, Ines Galloway had learned, was that they were never actually impossible. They were merely unaccounted for.

She sat in the counting-house of the Guild of Impossible Things with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a stub of graphite behind each ear, and she made the impossible balance. That was the whole of her employment, though the guildmasters preferred grander language: *Keeper of Reconciliation*, the brass plate on her desk read, as if she reconciled lovers or warring kingdoms rather than columns of figures. But the guild's accounts were strange beasts. A debt owed in moonlight. A payment rendered in the memory of a dead man's name. An asset listed as *one (1) door, which opens only when wept upon*. Ines entered them all in her careful hand and found, night after night, the sum at the bottom of the page where a sum had no right to be.

Tonight the ledger open before her was the third-quarter reconciliation of harbor dues, and it would not balance by exactly eleven marks and a promise. She could feel the discrepancy the way a cook feels a missing spice — not as a number but as an absence, a small cold hole in the fabric of the page.

"Eleven marks," she murmured, and dipped her pen.

Outside the counting-house windows, Saltmere Harbor lay under a sky the color of wet slate. The lamps along the quay had just been lit, and their reflections trembled on water so still it looked like poured glass. And above the masts, above the customs house, above the fish-market awnings being folded for the night, the gulls wheeled and screamed their evening arguments with one another.

Ines listened to them the way other people listened to music.

She did not understand everything they said. Their speech was not words, not exactly — it was weather and hunger and territory, grievance and appetite, the long gossip of wings. But she understood enough. Enough to know that the gull screaming over the customs house was complaining about a stolen herring and a rival's nest built too near its own. Enough to know that the three birds circling the breakwater had found something dead beneath the surface and were working up the courage to dive for it.

And enough to know that they never, ever spoke to her while anyone was watching.

That was the discipline of her life, the shape her days took: by daylight, silence. Her gift stayed folded away like good linen brought out only for funerals. By night, when the counting-house emptied and the clerks went home to their suppers, she opened the window a hand's width and let the harbor's voices in, and she was — briefly, secretly, dangerously — herself.

Her aunt Maren had not been so careful.

Ines did not let herself think of Aunt Maren often. Thinking of her was like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt; it always did. But sometimes, in the blue hour between day and dark, the memory came anyway, uninvited: Maren standing on the sea-wall with her arms out and her face turned up, laughing, talking to the gulls in broad daylight because she believed — she had genuinely believed — that the world had grown gentler since the old times. The tribunal had taken four days. The burning had taken less than an hour. Ines had been nine years old, and she had stood in the crowd with her grandmother's hand clamped over her mouth, and she had learned the first and greatest lesson of her life:

*A voice like ours is a debt collected in fire.*

So she kept her ledgers. She balanced what could not be balanced. She said nothing to the gulls but *good evening* and *fly safe*, and only after the last lamp in the counting-house guttered out.

She reached for the window latch now, meaning to do exactly that, and that was when the door burst open.

* * *

The sound went through her like a whipcrack. No one came to the counting-house at this hour. No one came to the counting-house *ever*, really — the guildmasters conducted their business in the marble rooms upstairs, and the clerks arrived with the bells, and the building after dark belonged to Ines and her columns alone.

The man in the doorway did not look like a client. He looked like something the tide had refused.

He was soaked through despite the dry evening, and his coat — a warden's coat, she thought, though the insignia had been torn away leaving only pale rectangles of thread — hung from him in ribbons. Blood had dried black down one side of his face from a wound above his ear, and fresh blood spread across his side with every heartbeat, dripping onto the threshold stones in a rhythm she would later find herself counting against the ledger lines. His eyes were the worst part. They were wide and white-rimmed, and they swept the room once, twice, looking for something — or fleeing something — before they found her and stopped.

"You're the keeper," he said. Not a question. "The little clerk who makes it come out even."

"I'm the night reconciler," Ines said, rising slowly, one hand finding the heavy glass paperweight on her desk. "If you're injured, there are surgeons on Cordwainer Street—"

"They're watching the surgeons." He laughed, and the laugh broke into a cough that bent him nearly double. When he straightened, there was blood on his teeth. "They're watching everywhere. That's rather the point." He took a step inside and kicked the door shut behind him with his heel, and the sound of it closing rang through the tall room like a struck bell.

Ines's heart was going hard against her ribs. Every instinct she owned told her to look at this man and see nothing, know nothing, be nothing — the grayest possible clerk, a woman made of ink and indifference. But his eyes had already fixed on her with the terrible specificity of a dying man who has no time left for indirection.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," he said. "Do you know what's on your own shelves? Do you know what you keep?"

"I keep accounts," Ines said. "I don't ask what they're for."

"No," he agreed, swaying. "Nobody does. That's how it's gone on so long." He fumbled at his coat, at an inner pocket, and Ines saw his fingers were pruned white and split at the knuckles, as if he'd spent days in salt water. "A hundred years, keeper. A hundred years since the Gate broke, and everyone says *tragedy, tragedy, the sea rose and drowned the covenant*, and no one asks why the guild's fortunes began that very same year. No one asks what was bought with the drowning."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," he said again, gently now, almost kindly. "But you will."

He crossed the room in three lurching strides, and before she could step back he had caught her wrist — his grip shockingly strong for a body so far spent — and pressed something into her hand. It was stiff and crackling, oilcloth wrapped around something flat, tied with waxed cord gone soft with damp. He folded her fingers over it with both of his hands, as if in benediction, as if in burial.

"The map," he whispered. "Of the drowned places. The true ones — not the charts they sell, the *true* ones. It leads to the Salt Gate. What's left of it. What's left of *them*." His breath whistled. "You have to take it below. Someone has to speak for them. No one has spoken for them in a hundred years, and the tide is done waiting."

"Sir," Ines said, and heard her own voice go thin, "you've mistaken me. I'm nobody. I balance numbers."

His mouth twisted into something that was almost a smile. "Yes," he breathed. "That's why it has to be you. The ones who make it come out even — you're the only ones who'd notice what's missing." He sagged then, catching himself on the edge of her desk, scattering her graphite stubs, dragging a bloody smear across the third-quarter reconciliation of harbor dues. "Listen. Listen to me. There isn't time. They'll burn this place looking for it, and they'll burn whoever held it last, and I am so sorry for that, I am sorry, but there was no one else. The others are dead. I watched them—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "The animals here. The birds. You talk to them. I've seen you at the window. Don't lie to me, keeper, I haven't the strength to survive being lied to."

The floor seemed to tilt beneath Ines. Ten years of discipline, ten years of gray wool and lowered eyes and *good evening, fly safe*, and this stranger had seen through it in a week of watching from the shadows. She felt naked in a way that had nothing to do with clothes. She felt nine years old, standing in a crowd, smelling smoke.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, and it was the second lie of her life, and it tasted worse than the first.

Something in his face crumpled — not anger; grief, or pity, she couldn't tell which. He let go of her wrist and slid down until he was sitting on the floor against her desk, his legs out before him, his breathing coming in long rattling pulls.

"Well," he said. "Then I'll die mistaken, and the map will find someone worthier. Though I'll say this." He looked up at her, and the lamplight moved in his eyes. "The gulls stopped singing tonight. Did you notice? All around this quarter, not one bird calling. They've gone quiet because the tide-born know the map has come ashore. They know, and they're listening for who carries it." His head tipped back against the desk drawer. "The salt remembers who owes, keeper. That's what they say, down there. The salt remembers who owes. And the guild owes them a century of voices."

He spoke perhaps six more words after that. She could never afterward remember what they were — something about a name, about telling them *Merrick sent me*, or perhaps it was only her own name he was trying to say, though he couldn't have known it. Then the rattle in his chest went quiet, and the lamplight steadied, and the man who had been Merrick, or had claimed to be, sat very still against her desk with his hands open on his knees like a man waiting to receive payment.

Ines stood in the middle of her ordered room, among her ordered columns, holding an oilcloth packet against her sternum, and felt the whole architecture of her careful life shift beneath her like a pier at low tide.

* * *

She did not scream. That surprised her afterward — that her first act, on finding a dead man in the counting-house, was not to cry out but to stand very quietly and listen. To the street outside, where nothing stirred. To the stairs, where nothing climbed. To the harbor, where—

Where the gulls had gone silent. Truly silent. She went to the window and looked out and saw them: dozens of them, scores, perched motionless along the quay railings and the mooring posts and the ridgeline of the customs house, every head turned toward the counting-house, every beak closed. Not sleeping. *Waiting.* The stillness of them raised the hair on her arms. It was like standing in a courtroom and realizing every seat was full.

*I'm sorry,* she thought at them, uselessly, in the old way, the way she had thought at birds since childhood. *I don't know what he's given me.*

One gull, an old one with a scarred bill, opened its beak and made a single low sound — not a cry, nothing like a cry. More like a word. Then it closed its beak, and they all went on waiting.

Ines drew the curtain.

She worked quickly after that, and her hands, she noticed distantly, did not shake. Shock does strange things; hers apparently took the form of competence. She fetched the canvas sheet from the storeroom and covered Merrick — or whoever he was — decently, folding his torn hands over his chest. She banked the lamp low. She wiped the blood from the threshold stones with a rag she would later burn. And then she sat down at her desk, and she unwrapped the oilcloth, and she looked at what a dying man had paid her.

It was a chart, drawn on skin — she chose not to wonder whose — cured thin and marked in ink that gleamed faintly green-gold, like weed under clear water. It showed the coast she knew, Saltmere and the harbors south of it, but the coastline itself was wrong, older, drawn where the shore had stood a hundred years ago before the tides came up. And inland from the old shoreline, in the places that were now dry fields and dead villages, the mapmaker had marked depths. *Seven fathoms, here, where the orchard stands. Twelve, at the church. The road runs under the sandhills.* Drowned places that were no longer drowned — a sea that had been rolled back, or stolen. And at the center of it all, drawn in a heavier hand, ringed round and round with ink until the vellum had nearly worn through, a gate.

Two pillars and a lintel, standing in the shallows of some vanished bay. Beneath it, in a spidery script, the same words over and over, dozens of times, as if the writer had needed to say it until his hand gave out:

*The salt remembers who owes. The salt remembers who owes. The salt remembers who owes.*

Ines sat for a long time with the map in her lap.

She should burn it. That was the sane course, the safe course, the course that kept her aunt's fate from becoming her own. Feed the stove, feed the fire, go home, sleep, arrive with the morning bells, report the dead man, weep appropriately, answer questions with a steady voice. She had watched her own life the way she watched the gulls — from a distance, silently, safely — for twenty years, and she knew exactly how to keep doing it.

Instead she opened her ledger drawer, the deep one where the guild kept its records of debts outstanding past redemption, and she lifted out the fattest volume — *Register of Unresolved Obligations, Vol. IX* — and she hollowed her hands around the map, folding it small, small, smaller, and laid it in the well beneath the register where the binding's shadow hid it completely.

A debt, she thought. He had called it a debt. And debts, whatever else they were, whatever else they cost — debts were the one thing the Guild of Impossible Things existed to honor.

Somewhere above her, a floorboard creaked.

Ines closed the drawer without a sound, put both hands flat on her desk, and listened as boots — deliberate, unhurried, official — began to descend the counting-house stairs.
