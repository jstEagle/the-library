## 2. What the Gulls Refuse to Say

The silence began at dawn, and it was worse than any cry.

Ines had grown up learning the harbor's grammar. The gulls argued over the fish-carts at first light, a squabbling liturgy of greed; they wheeled and screamed at the tide-turn; they cursed the cats in long descending insults from the counting-house roof. Their noise was so constant that most people heard nothing at all, the way a man who lives beside a waterfall stops hearing water. But Ines heard everything. She had always heard everything — the gulls' grievances, the rats' negotiations beneath the floorboards, the slow patient gossip of the barnacles on the pier pilings. It was her inheritance and her death sentence, worn quietly for thirty years like a knife sewn into a hem.

So when she climbed the counting-house stairs that morning with her ink-stained fingers still smelling of the dead man's blood, and the harbor made no sound at all, she knew.

They were not gone. That was the terrible part. She could see them — hundreds of them, white and grey, perched along the warehouse ridgelines and the crane-arms and the weathervane of the Salt Chapel, every one of them facing her window. Silent. Watching. The way a courtroom watches the accused before the verdict is read.

*They know,* said something cold and tidal in the base of her skull. *The drowned know what you carry.*

She set down her tea without drinking it. Her hands were steady. She noted this with the same detached precision she brought to a column of figures that refused to sum: *hands steady, pulse elevated, judgment required.* Thirty years of ledgers had taught her that panic was simply an error not yet corrected.

The map lay where she had hidden it, folded inside the great ledger of uncollectable debts — Volume Nine, the one no clerk had opened since the year of the ash-famines, because everyone recorded in it was either dead or ruined past recovery. A fitting archive, she thought now, for a dying stranger's last possession. She did not take it out. She did not touch it. She sat at her desk, opened the day-book, and began to record the morning's transactions in her small exact hand, as though the world were still the shape it had been yesterday.

Outside, the gulls did not make a sound.

* * *

The enforcers came at mid-morning, and they came politely, which was how Ines knew to be afraid.

Guild enforcers were never polite when they suspected nothing. They kicked doors, they joked with the porters, they drank the clerks' coffee and left rings on the wood. But the two who entered the counting-house that morning — a broad woman with a face like a closed strongbox and a thin man whose eyes moved over the room the way an auditor's eyes move over a suspicious column — removed their hats, bowed slightly, and asked for Master Clerk Dunmore by name.

"I am she."

"Then you'll have seen him." The woman produced a slate bearing a charcoal sketch: the dead man, younger than he'd looked bleeding, with a scholar's hollow cheeks and salt-white streaks through his black hair. "Washed up against your steps last night. Died inside. The Guild requires a full inventory of his effects."

"He had none," Ines said.

It was the first lie of her life, and it came out smooth as poured wax, and she hated herself for how smooth it was.

The thin man tilted his head. "None?"

"Beggars die on these steps twice a winter. He carried nothing but the clothes he stood in. I sent for the watch, and the watch took—" she paused, and let the pause do its work, "—took what there was to take."

"And you searched him yourself?" the woman asked. Not accusing. Worse than accusing: *checking*, the way one checks a figure already believed wrong.

"The porter did. I witnessed." Another lie, laid neat as a brick. There was no porter; there had been no search; there had been only Ines alone with a dying man and a map pressed into her hands and six words spoken with his last breath. *The salt remembers who owes.* She had spent the night telling herself they were delirium. The silent gulls outside the window told her otherwise.

The enforcers worked through the counting-house for two hours. They were thorough in the way of people who already know what they are looking for: they weighed the coin-drawers, counted the quills, ran their fingers along the spines of the ledgers. The thin man stopped at Volume Nine. His hand actually touched it. Ines, copying a freight manifest three desks away, kept her pen moving in perfect strokes and felt her heartbeat in her teeth.

"This one's old," he said.

"Uncollectable debts," Ines said. "Closed forty years ago. Nothing in it but grief and bad arithmetic."

He drew it out anyway, and for one endless moment held it, and Ines understood with total clarity that if he opened it she would have to kill him or die, and that both options were absurd, and that she was no longer the person she had been yesterday. Then the woman called his name — "Vetch. The harbormaster's next" — and he shelved the ledger with its spine exactly aligned, and they left.

At the door, the woman turned back. "Master Dunmore. If anything of the dead man's surfaces — a paper, a token, anything — the Guild pays for promptness. And remembers slowness."

"We are a house of accounts," Ines said. "We understand both."

When their footsteps faded, she became aware that she had not breathed properly in two hours, and that her manifest copy contained the phrase *forty barrels of herring, payable in silence* in her own flawless hand.

* * *

That night she took Volume Nine home.

This was itself a crime — removing guild property from the counting-house was a flogging offense, third time dismissal and branding — and she committed it with the calm of a woman balancing a column, wrapping the ledger in her shawl and walking out past the night-porter with a nod. The porter nodded back. Everyone trusted Tamsin— trusted *Ines* Dunmore; that was the whole architecture of her life, trust built up in increments so small no one had ever thought to audit them.

Her rooms above the chandlery had one window facing the harbor. She lit no lamp. She unwrapped the ledger in the dark, and the gulls on the chapel roof watched her through the glass, silent as painted things, and she spoke to them before she could stop herself.

"You could tell them to go away," she whispered. "You could tell them I haven't decided anything yet."

The gulls said nothing, but one of them — an old grey-backed bird with a torn wing-feather she had known for years, a beggar of the fish-market, a gossip, a coward — dipped its head once, slowly, like a bow. Or a warning.

She unfolded the map by moonlight.

She had not allowed herself to look at it properly until now, and looking at it felt like opening someone else's mail, or someone else's grave. It was drawn on skin — she had known that since last night and refused to think it — cured thin and marked in iron-gall ink gone brown as old blood. It showed the coast, but not the coast as any chart showed it. Underneath. The drowned streets of the old harbor town, the flooded mine-shafts running inland like veins, and at the center, drawn with a care that bordered on devotion, a gate. Two pillars of green stone beneath the water, and between them a doorway rendered not in lines but in words, tiny and dense, a sentence built into the very stone:

*SPEAK AND BE ANSWERED.*

Around the gate's margins, in a different hand, cramped and desperate, someone had written marginalia like a man annotating his own doom: *the terms were never broken, only buried.* And: *ask the ledger-keepers what was paid, and to whom.* And, underlined twice, the words that had followed her all day like a shadow with teeth:

*the salt remembers who owes.*

Ines sat back. Below her window the tide was coming in, and she could feel it — not hear, *feel*, a pressure against the inside of her skull like a word waiting to be spoken. For thirty years she had used her gift for small mercies: calming a carthorse, learning from the rats where the grain-weevils nested, once, terribly, understanding a gull's account of men drowning off the point and being unable to make anyone believe her. Small sins of concealment. Tonight she had lied to enforcers, stolen a ledger, and sat holding a dead man's skin in the dark while the sea listened to her breathe.

And the awful thing — the thing she would examine later, guiltily, the way one examines a pleasure — was that she did not feel damned. She felt *awake*. Every precise, careful, obedient year of her life had been a kind of sleep, and the map was a cold key turning in a lock she hadn't known was in her chest.

"They sold it," she said aloud, testing the marginalia against everything she knew of the Guild of Impossible Things, whose accounts she balanced, whose secrets passed through her hands daily in columns of figures she had been trained not to read. "Whatever happened at the gate — someone was paid. And the payment would be recorded. Everything is recorded. That's the one law above all the others."

Everything is recorded. Which meant somewhere in the counting-house vaults, in some ledger older than Volume Nine, there was a line item for the breaking of the covenant between the human realm and the tide-born. A price. A signature. Perhaps — and here her blood went cold and quick — perhaps even her aunt's name, in the margin of some warrant, in some clerk's careful hand.

On the roof, the gulls shifted, hundreds of bodies settling at once, and still made no sound. They were not threatening her, she realized suddenly. They were *waiting*. The tide-born knew she held the map, and they had not sent the sea after her, had not drowned her in her bed. They were waiting to see what the ledger-keeper would do with an entry written in blood.

Ines folded the map back into Volume Nine, among the dead and the ruined, where no auditor would ever look. Tomorrow she would return the ledger to its shelf and be the perfect clerk again, seamless, unremarkable. But tomorrow night—

Tomorrow night she would begin reading the old accounts. All of them. Back a hundred years if she had to.

She blew out the candle she had never lit, and in the silence a single gull finally cried — one harsh note, directly overhead, unmistakably deliberate — and Ines, beast-speaker, liar, thief, understood it perfectly.

It said: *hurry.*
