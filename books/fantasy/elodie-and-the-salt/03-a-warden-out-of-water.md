## 3. A Warden Out of Water

The rain in Greyharbor came down like a debt being collected.

Corren Vane sat in the back room of the Drowned Anchor with his boots on the table and a cup of something that had stopped pretending to be wine an hour ago, and he watched the water run down the window in long silver ropes and thought, as he did every morning for ten years, that the sea was owed and the sea would collect. That was the whole of his philosophy now. It fit on a coin. It weighed about as much.

Ten years ago he had been Warden Corren of the Salt Gate, third of his line to hold the post, sworn in salt and iron to keep the covenant's threshold. Ten years ago he had stood on the drowned causeway with the tide going out wrong — out too far, out past the marker stones, out until the harbor floor lay naked and stinking under a sky the color of pewter — and he had known, in the marrow-deep way wardens know things, that something had come loose below. He had sent three messages to the Guild of Impossible Things. He had received one reply, signed by a guildmaster whose name he still could not say without tasting copper: *Hold your post. The accounts are in order.*

The accounts were in order. That was what they told him while the gate broke behind him like a promise snapped at the seam, while the tide-born fled their own threshold, while the water rose and took the low villages and the guild's clerks wrote it all down in neat columns under the heading *Actuarial Losses*. He had held his post. He had held it until the water reached his chest and the last of the eels went past him swimming hard for deep water, and not one of them looked back, because there was nothing left down there worth looking back for.

They stripped him of the grey cloak in a hearing that lasted forty minutes. They gave him ten years of harborside drinking instead, and a pension paid quarterly by the same guild that had broken everything, which was either an insult or a bribe depending on how sober he was when he counted it.

He was not sober now. But he was awake enough when the knock came — three raps, official spacing, knuckles that had been trained to knock — and he knew before he opened the door that the ten years were over.

* * *

The man from the guild was young and dry and carried a leather case the way priests carry relics. He sat across from Corren in the Anchor's foulest booth and did not touch the drink Corren bought him, which told Corren more than anything else about him.

"Vane. Formerly Warden." The young man opened the case. Inside lay a single sheet of paper, and even folded, even through the rain-dim light, Corren felt it the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth. "A stranger died in the counting-house four nights ago. Stabbed twice, walked half the city to get there, died holding this." He smoothed the paper flat. A map. Inked lines of coast and channel, but marked with places no chart showed — the Ninefold Deep, the Weeping Stair, and at the center, drawn in a hand that shook with either age or reverence, a gate. "It's a map of drowned places. The guild wants it found."

"It's in front of you."

"The guild wants the *original* found. This is a copy made for our records. The original passed out of the counting-house before the enforcers arrived. Someone took it." The young man's eyes were pale and patient. "There was a clerk present. Ines Galloway. She keeps the ledgers. She was alone with the body for some minutes."

Corren looked at the map. At the gate. His hands, he noticed distantly, had gone very still on the tabletop, stiller than they'd been in years, the way a dog goes still when it scents something it has spent its life dreaming about.

"And you're telling me this why? I'm not a warden anymore. I'm furniture."

"You're the only man alive who knows the old approaches to the gate. And you're cheap." The young man slid a purse across the table. "Find who took the map. Report to me and no one else. The guildmaster prefers discretion in matters touching the covenant."

*Covenant.* The word landed in Corren's chest like a hook finding flesh. Ten years of not saying it aloud, and here was a boy in a dry coat tossing it across a tavern table like small change.

"I'll need the dead man's effects inventory," Corren said. "And the clerk's file. Galloway."

Something flickered in the young man's face — surprise, quickly filed away. "She's a ledger-keeper. Third rank. No family of note. An aunt, once, but—" He stopped himself, which meant the aunt's name was on a list somewhere, the kind of list Corren had spent ten years learning to read between the lines of.

"An aunt," Corren said. "Once."

"Executed. Before your time. Beast-speech." The young man said it the way you'd say *consumption*, a disease of the blood, embarrassing to mention. "The gift runs in families, they say. Why do you ask?"

"No reason," said Corren, and took the purse, and lied for the first time in ten years with a steadiness that surprised him. "I'll start with the counting-house."

* * *

He started with the gulls.

This was not what the guild had taught him — the guild taught ink and interrogation and the careful arithmetic of suspicion — but the sea had taught him better, in the years before and the years since. You want to know where trouble is in a harbor town, you don't watch the people. People lie with their whole bodies. Watch the birds. Birds have no guild, no pension, no reason to dissemble. Birds tell you where the dead fish are, where the fights are, where the secrets are thrown away.

For three days he watched the counting-house district, and for three days the pattern held true and then broke in a way that made the hair on his arms stand up.

Gulls mobbed every rooftop from the fishmarket to the customs stairs. Except one. Above the narrow house where the ledgers said Ines Galloway lodged, the air was empty. Not quiet — empty, deliberate, the way a room is empty after everyone has agreed to leave it. And on the fourth morning, before dawn, Corren climbed the warehouse opposite her window and saw why.

She was feeding them.

Not scattering bread like a child. She stood at her sill in the grey light with her palm open and her head tilted, and the gulls came to her one at a time — came close, closer than any wild bird comes to any living person — and they *spoke*. He couldn't hear what passed between them; the wind took it. But he saw the shape of it. He saw her listen, truly listen, the way you listen to someone you owe. He saw her face change as she heard whatever they brought her: fear first, then a hard bright thing underneath it that he recognized, because he had worn that same expression once, standing on a causeway, watching the water go out.

Beast-speech. In the niece of a woman the guild had hanged for it.

Corren crouched in the dark of the warehouse roof with rain soaking through his coat, and he should have written the report that night. Should have sealed it, sent it, collected the second half of the purse, gone back to the Anchor and the wine and the long gray arithmetic of his remaining years. Every part of him that had survived the last decade counseled exactly that. *The accounts are in order,* said the voice in his head, the guildmaster's voice, the voice that had let the gate break. *Report what you find. Hold your post.*

But he had held his post once. He knew precisely what it was worth.

Below him, the girl closed her hand gently over a gull's beak — a greeting, or a blessing, he couldn't tell — and the bird stayed, unafraid, and something in Corren's chest that had been rusted shut for ten years turned on its hinge with a sound he felt rather than heard.

Because here was the thing he had never told the hearing, never told the wine, never told anyone: in the last hour before the gate broke, when the eels fled past him and the water rose, one old seal had surfaced beside the causeway and looked at him. Looked at him the way a creditor looks at a debtor. And he had understood, without words, without gift, that the sea had come to *ask* — not to take, not yet — to ask whether the shore still remembered what it owed. And he had had no answer to give. He had been a warden with no one to speak for him on either side of the water, and the seal had gone under, and the gate had broken, and ten years later he was drinking in a room named for the drowning of everything he'd failed to save.

Now here was a girl who could give the answer. Who perhaps already had.

* * *

He followed her for six days, and he was honest enough with himself to admit it wasn't surveillance anymore.

He watched her balance impossible accounts by day — he bribed a junior clerk for glimpses of her columns and found numbers that shouldn't reconcile reconciling, debts from a century ago being quietly, meticulously traced — and he watched her slip out by night along the harbor wall with a folded paper against her ribs, and he watched the animals bend toward her like grass toward sun. Rats paused when she passed. A cart-horse turned its head to watch her go. Once, on the mudflats, a heron stood aside from its fishing pool and let her take the path, and if you didn't know what you were seeing you'd call it chance, and Corren had stopped believing in chance around water a long time ago.

On the sixth night she bought passage east on a coal barge, inland, upriver — inland, where there was no sea, which meant the map had shown her something under the land, some drowned place the tides had abandoned. She moved like a woman who had finally stopped lying to herself, and Corren stood in the shadow of the coal chutes and let the barge cast off without him, because he had decided something in the last hundred yards of dark:

He was not going to report her.

He was going to follow her. All the way, wherever the map led — to the dried seabeds, to the gate, to whatever accounting waited at the end of it. Not for the purse. Not for redemption, though God knew he'd have taken it like a starving man takes bread. But because for ten years he had been the only witness to the worst night of the world, and witnesses who stay silent are just accomplices with better posture.

He went back to the Anchor one last time, packed nothing, left the purse on the bar for the landlord's widow, and walked out into the rain feeling lighter than he had any right to feel.

Behind him, in the harbor, the water lay flat and listening. Ahead of him, upriver, a girl with a stolen map and a forbidden voice was walking toward the truth the guild had buried in its ledgers a hundred years ago.

Corren turned his collar up and followed.

He did not know — could not know — that by morning the gulls would be silent over every road she traveled, and that the silence would follow them both like a tide coming in.
