## 4. Debts Written in Brine

The map did not lead to the sea. That was the first wrongness of it, and Ines felt it like a splinter under the skin every mile the coach rattled inland.

She had studied it for three nights by candle-gutter light in a room above a chandler's shop where no one asked her name. The parchment was salt-stiff and smelled of low tide at its edges, but the inked lines ran away from the harbor, up through the chalk hills, past the last of the oyster beds, into country she had only ever seen as columns of figures — freight costs, grain tariffs, the price of hauling stone to nowhere in particular. At the center of the map, drawn with a care that bordered on devotion, was a village called Brackenmere, sitting in what had once been a bay.

Once. The cartographer had shaded the water in faded blue and then, in a later hand, crossed it out entirely, stroke after stroke after stroke, until the bay was nothing but hatching. A sea erased by penmanship.

Ines knew about erasure. She kept ledgers for a living.

She told herself she was going because the dead man deserved his map delivered. She told herself she was going because the guild would send someone eventually and better an unremarkable clerk than an enforcer with questions. She told herself a great many things, in the tidy voice she used for balancing columns that refused to balance, and none of them were true. She was going because the gulls had gone silent, and silence, in her experience, was how the world held its breath before it demanded something.

* * *

Brackenmere announced itself by smell before sight: brine gone stale, the ghost of a tide that hadn't touched these shores in a hundred years. Then the road crested a hill of shingle — actual shingle, wave-rounded stones stacked in ridges like the skeleton of some beached leviathan — and there it lay below.

A village built on a seabed. Houses of grey stone stood where water had once stood taller than their chimneys. The streets were streets, but they curved the way currents curve, and the doorways all faced the same direction, toward the drowned horizon, as if the village had never quite agreed to turn around. Boats sat in gardens. Not wrecked boats — *moored* boats, tied to posts driven into dry earth, hulls patched and repainted, maintained by people who could not have said why except that their great-grandparents had told them to.

And everywhere, the salt. It crusted the stones white, bloomed in the gutters, glittered on the window ledges like frost that refused winter's excuse. Nothing grew tall here. The orchards were orchards of stunted things, trees that had spent a century learning to drink from ground that remembered being ocean.

An old woman was mending a net in front of the first house, though the nearest fish was forty miles away. Her hands moved through the knots without looking at them. Her eyes found Ines the moment she crested the shingle ridge, and they were the pale, washed-out blue of things left too long in water.

"You'll be from the Guild," the old woman said. Not a question.

"I'm—" Ines stopped. The lie she'd prepared, *clerk on guild business, nothing more*, sat ready on her tongue. But the old woman's hands had gone still on the net, and stillness, Ines had learned among the gulls, was its own kind of speech. "I'm Ines Galloway. I keep accounts."

"Accounts." The word came out like something spat. The old woman set down the net. "Then you'd best come see ours."

* * *

They kept it in the meeting-house, which had been a chapel, which had been, before either, something older whose purpose no one living could name. The ledger of Brackenmere was not a book. It was a wall.

Names. Hundreds of them, carved into the stone in ranks and generations, each with a date and, beside the date, a small symbol Ines recognized with a lurch: the tide-mark, the coiled spiral the tide-born pressed into covenant-stone, the same mark that spiraled at the heart of the dead man's map.

"What is this?" she asked, though her fingers already knew, tracing the grooves the way they traced ledger lines.

"The payment schedule," the old woman said. "That's my grandmother, third row. And her mother above her. Every family in Brackenmere gave someone. One voice per household, every generation, delivered to the Salt Gate and given over to the water-folk." She said it flatly, the way one recites a tax rate. "That was the arrangement, after the Gate broke. The tide-born had us at their mercy — one moon, the stories say, and every coast town drowns. So the Guild negotiated. On our behalf. Very generously."

Ines turned. "Negotiated *what*, exactly?"

"That we'd keep sending speakers. Human voices, bound into the salt, to hold the covenant shut from inside. One per family, forever." The old woman's mouth twisted. "Except here's what the carved names don't say, girl who keeps accounts. The covenant never needed *ours*. Read your histories right and you'll find the original terms plain enough: one speaker from each realm, *willing*, bound in trust. Two voices meeting in the middle. The tide-born honored it for three hundred years. Their speaker came to the gate every season, singing, waiting for ours."

"And ours stopped coming."

"Ours stopped coming," the old woman agreed, "because the Guild discovered something more profitable. You can't enslave a partner. But you can enslave a debtor. So the Guild rewrote the terms — forged the sacrifice clause, made it sound like the covenant always demanded human voices given wholly to the salt — and then sold the tide-born a century of stolen speakers. Sold them, mind. Each name on this wall, the Guild invoiced. There's a fee schedule. I've seen it. My grandmother's voice went south at four crowns the head, plus carriage."

The meeting-house was very quiet. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the stunted orchards, and the boats creaked in their earth-moorings, rocking on a sea that wasn't there.

Ines thought of the counting-house. Of the ledgers she balanced, the impossible entries she smoothed into sense — payments to sources she'd been told not to examine, disbursements marked *coastal maintenance* that had run, uninterrupted, for longer than any pier stood. She had assumed corruption. Small, ordinary, human corruption. Skimming. She had not imagined architecture. A hundred years of theft, load-bearing, holding up the entire guild, and her own careful hands keeping the books of it clean.

"They're still down there," she said. Her voice sounded strange to herself. "The tide-born. Still owed."

"Still owed," the old woman said. "And still coming to collect. Why do you think the gulls won't speak to your kind anymore?"

* * *

She stayed three days. She copied the wall — every name, every date, every mark — into the back pages of her own ledger, in her smallest, most exact hand, because if the world was going to make her a criminal then she would at least be a meticulous one. The villagers watched her do it without helping, without hindering, the way people watch weather.

On the fourth morning, she found him sitting on the shingle ridge above the village, boots dusty, coat travel-worn, a flask in his hand he wasn't drinking from.

Corren.

Her whole body went cold and then hot. She had seen him twice since the counting-house — once as a shape across a market square that might have been anyone, once as a silhouette on the night coach, unmistakable, doing nothing, saying nothing, just *there*, the way a debt is there. She had told herself she was imagining him. She was done telling herself things.

"Warden," she said, climbing the ridge. Her legs were steadier than she expected. "Come to arrest me?"

"For what?" He didn't look at her. He was looking at the village below, at the boats moored in gardens, and his face had a cracked, unhoused look, like a man reading his own name on a gravestone. "Theft? You stole nothing. Treason? Against whom — the guild that sold a hundred years of souls at four crowns the head?" He laughed, short and joyless. "I've spent ten years thinking I failed. That the Gate broke under me because I was slow, or drunk, or both. Do you know what I've just spent two days learning in that meeting-house?"

"That it didn't break," Ines said. "It was *sold*. Broken on purpose, so there'd be a crisis worth charging for."

"And I was the warden they posted to make sure no one looked too closely at the wreckage." He turned the flask over in his fingers. "Ten years I've been drinking to a failure that was a *job description*."

The wind came up off the dead sea and pushed between them, tasting of salt and old stone. Below, the old woman had come out to stand in her doorway, net forgotten, watching the ridge.

"Why haven't you reported me?" Ines asked. It was the question she'd carried for fifty miles, and it came out smaller than she meant. "You've had a dozen chances. You followed me instead. Why?"

Corren was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he reached into his coat and drew out a journal — swollen with damp, corners soft, stuffed with loose pages — and held it out to her.

"Because ten years ago I heard something at the Gate that I put down to drink," he said. "A voice in the water, asking — not threatening, *asking* — why the humans had stopped speaking. And everyone told me the tide-born don't ask. They take. And I wanted so badly to believe them, because if the thing in the water could ask, then it could be answered, and I hadn't answered, and that was unbearable." His jaw worked. "Then I watched you on the harbor stairs, talking to gulls that talked back, and I thought: there's the answer. Walking around with a ledger under her arm, pretending to be nobody."

He nodded at the ledger in her satchel — her ledger, the one with the Brackenmere wall copied into its final pages, the one with the dead man's map folded against its spine.

"So before I decide what I am — warden, drunk, or something else — I want to read the account. All of it. What the dead man gave you. What the wall says. What the guild owes." He met her eyes at last, and behind the ruin of him there was something steady, something that had survived the drowning of everything else. "Show me the page, Galloway. Let's see what the numbers say."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she knelt in the shingle, opened the ledger across her knees, and began, in a clear voice, to read the debts of a hundred years aloud — while below them, far out on the dry seabed, the earth shivered once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.

And every gull in the grey sky above Brackenmere began, at the same instant, to sing.
