## 5. The Drowned Court

The eel came to them at the edge of the dry sea, where the map's last inked line dissolved into cracked clay that had once been harbor mud.

It was not a large creature — a ribbon of grey-green muscle no thicker than Ines's wrist, scarred along one flank as though something had tried to bite it in half and failed. It lay in a shallow pool left by the spring tide, breathing with visible effort, and when Ines knelt at the pool's rim it lifted its blunt head and looked at her with eyes like drops of black glass.

*You are the one who hears,* it said, and its voice arrived inside her skull rather than her ears, tasting of iron and old storms. *I am called Vessel. I have carried messages for sixty years. I have never once carried one this far inland.*

"You're hurt," Ines said, before she could stop herself. It was the first thing she'd said aloud to anything other than a human being since her aunt died, and it came out rough, unpracticed, like a door long rusted shut swinging open.

*I am always hurt,* said Vessel. *The water above is not kind to messengers anymore. The guild's nets do not ask what they catch.* The eel's body shuddered through a slow ripple. *But I did not come to be pitied. I came because the court felt you cross the threshold of the drowned places, and the court does not feel much these days. Come. The shafts are open. The mine flooded forty years ago and no one bothered to pump it out, because there was nothing down there worth drowning for.*

Corren stood a few paces back, arms crossed against the wind off the dead seabed, his coat whipping around him. He had not spoken since they crossed the salt flats together — three days of silence broken only by practicalities, water rations, the angle of the sun. But now he said, quietly: "Flooded mineshafts."

*Yes,* said Vessel.

"And you expect us to breathe."

*No,* said the eel. *I expect you to trust. It is very different, and considerably harder.*

Ines looked at Corren. He looked back, and she saw in his face the same arithmetic she was doing herself: ten years of guilt on his side, a lifetime of hiding on hers, and beneath both of them the map pressed flat between the pages of her ledger, warm from her body as if it were alive.

"The gulls went silent for me," she said. "Three days ago. All of them, all at once, all along the coast." She turned back to the eel. "Was that you? Was that the court?"

Vessel's scarred body coiled slowly in the shallows. *That was recognition,* it said. *We know our own kind of silence when we hear it. A speaker who hides is louder than a speaker who sings, to ears like ours. You have been shouting your whole life, little ledger-keeper. We simply only just learned where to listen.*

* * *

The mouth of the mine was a black arch in the hillside above the dried seabed, half-swallowed by broom and gorse, timber props rotted to lace. Someone had carved marks into the lintel stone long ago — spirals, wave-forms, a language of curves that made Ines's teeth ache to look at, the way a high note aches in the ear. Tide-born script. She was certain of it without knowing how she was certain.

"Miners didn't cut those," Corren said. His voice had gone strange — stripped of its usual gravelly irony, almost young.

"No," said Ines. She ran her fingers over the spirals. The stone was cold in a way stone shouldn't be, cold like the underside of a wave. "This gate was old before the mine was ever a thought. They built the shafts *down to it*, not the other way round."

*Down,* agreed Vessel, sliding past their boots into the dark. *Everything we are asking of you is down. That is the nature of tides. They only ever go one way until someone learns to argue.*

The first hundred yards were dry enough. Then the water began — ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then waist-deep, and always bitterly cold, and always faintly luminous, a green-gold shimmer rising from below as though the deep itself were lit from within. Ines kept one hand on the wall and the ledger-map wrapped in oilcloth slung across her back, and she listened.

That was the thing no one understood about her gift, she thought, wading into the dark. Everyone assumed it was speech — that beast-speakers talked to animals the way merchants talked across counters. But it had never been talking. It was listening. The world was full of voices that humans had agreed not to hear, and Ines had spent thirty years agreeing harder than anyone, and now here she was, chest-deep in a drowned mine, finally letting herself hear.

And the mine was *full*. Not of fish — of presence. Old timbers groaning in voices like tired men. Water speaking to itself in long slow sentences. And underneath everything, so deep she felt it in her sternum rather than heard it, a rhythm like a heartbeat the size of a county.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Corren had gone rigid beside her. "What's what?"

"You can't hear it?"

"I can hear water. I can hear my own teeth chattering." He paused. "Though I'll admit the water sounds... organized."

*She hears the Gate,* said Vessel, from somewhere ahead, its voice arriving soft and vast. *Or the Gate hears her. Hard to say which comes first, with speakers. Keep walking. The court dislikes waiting, and it has had a century of practice at disliking things.*

* * *

They dove three times.

Each dive was longer than should have been survivable, and each time Ines surfaced gasping in an air-pocket cathedral hollowed out of the rock, green-lit, dripping, alive. Vessel gave no explanation and offered none, but Ines noticed that the water seemed to part around her more readily than around Corren; noticed that when she swallowed a lungful by accident during the second dive, the water in her throat tasted not of drowning but of instruction, and she coughed it out and kept swimming and did not die.

On the third dive, Corren caught her arm before they submerged. His face was close in the green gloom, older than his years, the lines around his eyes carved deep by a decade of drink and regret.

"When we get there," he said, "whatever they show you — whatever they say about the guild, about the breach — remember I was there. Ten years ago. I stood on the wall at Greyharbor the night the tide came in wrong, and I did nothing, because my orders were to do nothing. Whatever judgment falls, some of it belongs to me."

Ines looked at him. "Is that why you followed me instead of arresting me?"

"It's why I'm diving into a flooded mine after a criminal," he said. "The rest is arithmetic I haven't finished yet."

Then he filled his lungs, and they went down.

* * *

The court of the tide-born opened around them like a held breath released.

They surfaced in a cavern that no human hand had hollowed — or if hands had, they were hands the size of weather. The vault of it rose beyond sight, ribbed with pale stone that might have been coral grown over centuries into architecture, and everywhere the light came up from below, green and gold and moving, so that the whole space seemed to breathe. Galleries ringed the walls at a dozen heights, and the galleries were not empty.

The tide-born had come to look at her.

They were beautiful the way storms are beautiful, and Ines's first coherent thought was that every human story had lied. Not monsters, not mermaids, not the sleek devils of guild pamphlets — they were tall and angular and their skin shifted through colors like light through water, greens shading to slate shading to sudden silver, and their faces were long and grave and *familiar*, the way a face in an old portrait is familiar, some ancestor's jaw repeated in a stranger. Their eyes were the eel's eyes, black glass, and there were hundreds of them, and every one of those eyes was fixed on the two humans treading water in the center of the court.

Ines's gift rose in her unbidden, and for the first time in her life she let it.

She heard them.

Not words, not at first — a pressure, a chorus, the sound of a city thinking. Grief moved through it like a current. Anger, older than anger. And threaded through everything, so persistent it had become texture: *waiting, waiting, waiting, a hundred years of waiting, the shore-kin stopped coming, the shore-kin stopped coming, the shore-kin—*

"They stopped coming," Ines said aloud, and her voice cracked across the cavern, and the chorus went silent.

Every eye on her. The silence was total, oceanic, attentive.

"I'm sorry," she said, not knowing yet what she was apologizing for, knowing only that it was owed. "I don't — I don't understand yet. But I'm sorry."

A figure descended from the highest gallery. It came slowly, unhurried, trailing ribbons of pale light, and Ines saw that it was old — old the way the lintel stone was old, its silvered skin marked with the same spiral script she had touched above. When it spoke, it spoke aloud, in human speech worn smooth as sea glass, each word placed with the care of a mason setting a keystone.

"I am the Tidemother," it said. "I held the eastern seat when the covenant was whole. I have held it since. Sit, speaker. Both of you. What must be shown cannot be shown to standing legs."

The water beneath them firmed — not solidified, but *agreed* to hold them, and Ines sat upon the sea itself, weightless and supported, while Corren lowered himself beside her with the expression of a man whose worldview was filing formal complaints.

The Tidemother raised one long hand, and the water of the cavern became a page.

Images rose in it, green-lit, moving: a gate of white stone standing in open sea, twin towers, a causeway busy with figures — human figures walking down to the water and tide-born walking up, meeting in the middle, trading. Words passing. *Words* passing — Ines saw a human woman standing at the gate's center, singing, and the song went into the water and came back changed, and the tide-born bowed to it, and she understood: a speaker. A human speaker, standing in the gate, making each realm audible to the other.

"This was the covenant," the Tidemother said. "Not a wall. Not a treaty signed and shelved. A *conversation*, renewed at every turning of the great tide. One voice from the shore, one voice from the salt, bound in trust, each carrying the other's words home. For four hundred years, the shore sent us a speaker. We sent them ours. We taught your ancestors the paths of the fish and the temper of the storms, and they taught us the names of stars, which we had wondered at but never owned." The old one's hand closed, and the image collapsed into dark water. "And then, one hundred years ago, the shore stopped coming. No messenger. No word. We waited one turning. Then five. Then fifty. The gate, untended, forgot how to stand. And the sea, untended, forgot how to be gentle."

"That's not what happened," Corren said hoarsely. "What we're taught — the covenant was *broken*. The tide-born breached it. There was a war—"

"There was a *sale*," said the Tidemother, and the word fell into the water like a stone into a well. "Your guild found that the gate's stones, ground fine, held the tide obedient. Found that our people, netted and starved of salt-water, could be made to work the deep currents, hauling your ships' cargoes along the seafloor faster than any wind. Your guildmaster's grandfather signed papers. We have copies. We have had a century to make copies." Its black-glass eyes turned to Ines, and in them she saw reflected her own small, pale, land-made face. "The breach was invented, speaker. Wars need reasons. Slavery needs silence. And the shore's side of the conversation did not stop coming by accident. It was *ended*, by men with ledgers, and the ledgers were burned — all but one."

Ines's hand went, without her willing it, to the oilcloth bundle at her back. The map. The dying man's map of drowned places, drawn in a hand she now recognized as tide-born script learned by a human copyist.

"He was the last keeper," she whispered. "Wasn't he. The one who came to the counting-house."

"He was my son's son's son," said the Tidemother, "raised on land, hidden in plain sight among your clerks, keeping the true accounts for a hundred years in whatever fragments he could salvage. And now he is dead, and the accounts have passed to you." The old one leaned close, and the whole court leaned with it, hundreds of tide-born inclining like kelp in a current. "So tell me, Ines Galloway, who hears what others agree not to hear. Tell me what your gift is *for*."

And Ines opened her mouth to answer — to say *I don't know*, to say *I'm only a clerk* — and found that she could not lie here, in this place, in this water. The truth rose in her like a tide of its own:

"To keep accounts," she said. "True ones. However much they cost."

The Tidemother regarded her for a long moment. Then it smiled, and its smile was old grief wearing patience like a mask.

"Good," it said. "Because tomorrow, at slack water, we will show you the terms of surrender. And you will learn what reforging the covenant requires — and what it costs — and then, little ledger-keeper, you will understand why your aunt was executed, and why the guild fears a listening ear more than a drawn blade."

Behind her, Ines heard Corren exhale, slow and shaking, a man watching the last of his cynicism drown.

Above them, far above, through a hundred fathoms of green-dark water, the first tremor of a storm was already gathering over the coast — and the court felt it, and did not flinch, and began, softly, to sing.
