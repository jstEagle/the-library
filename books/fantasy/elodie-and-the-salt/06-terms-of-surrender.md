## 6. Terms of Surrender

The court of the tide-born did not sit in a hall. It sat in the water itself.

Ines understood this slowly, the way one understands cold — first at the edges, then everywhere at once. She stood on a shelf of black stone deep beneath the drowned mine-shafts, and around her, in every direction, the sea arranged itself into shapes that were almost figures: columns of current, crowns of kelp, faces that assembled and dissolved like reflections on disturbed water. The eel who had guided them down coiled now at her ankle, its scarred body trembling with something she chose to read as pride.

Corren stood a half-step behind her. She could hear his breathing — too fast for a man who claimed to have made peace with drowning years ago.

"They're waiting," he murmured.

"I know." She could feel it, the way she felt the gulls' hunger and the harbor cats' contempt: as pressure, as attention. A hundred years of it, all of it turned toward her at once. "They've been waiting since before I was born."

*Then speak,* said the water, in a voice that was not a voice — a chorus of small sounds, shell-hollow and salt-bright, arriving not through her ears but through her skin, her teeth, the water in her own blood. *You came down carrying the map. Speak.*

Ines lifted the map from where it lay against her heart. The stranger's blood had darkened to rust along its folds, but the ink still held, every drowned place marked in a hand that had shaken only twice. She had thought, descending, that she would know what to say. She had rehearsed speeches in the dark of the shafts, formal words, ledger-words. Now they all left her.

"My name is Ines Galloway," she said instead. "I keep accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things. And I think — I think you already know what's written in them."

Silence, of the particular quality that meant listening. Even Corren went still.

She told them what the map had shown her, what the dried seabed village had confessed in its cracked foundations and its shrine to a gate no longer there. That the covenant had not broken. That it had been sold. That the year the Salt Gate went silent was the same year the guild's ledgers recorded, in her aunt's careful predecessor's hand, an entry she had read three times to be sure of it: *One hundred souls, tide-bound, delivered per contract. Payment rendered in full.*

Her aunt had found that page. Her aunt had tried to carry it out. Ines had been nine years old when they burned her for it, and she had spent her whole life since learning to be quiet.

"I'm not here to apologize," Ines said, and heard her own voice go strange and hard. "Apologies are a currency, and you've been paid in counterfeit long enough. I'm here to balance the account."

The water moved. All of it, all at once — a slow turning, like a great body shifting its weight, and the shapes around her resolved further, closer, until she could see them plainly: the tide-born. Taller than men and thinner, their skin the grey-green of deep water, their eyes without whites, entirely dark, entirely patient. Their queen — if queen she was; Ines could not tell where she ended and the current began — drifted forward until the two of them were separated by a hand's width of brine.

*You speak our tongue,* the queen said. *Not the words. The under-words. The ones between.*

"I hear animals," Ines said. "I always have. I hid it my whole life because the last person who didn't burned."

*Yes.* Something moved across the queen's face that might, in a human, have been grief. *We knew your aunt. She stood where you stand. She could not hear us — but she read our names in the guild's papers, and she came to say them aloud. They took her before she reached the shore.*

Ines's breath caught. Nineteen years of wondering, and here it was, delivered by a drowned queen in a sentence: her aunt had died trying to do exactly this. Had died four miles short of it.

"Then let me finish what she started."

*That,* said the queen, *is why we called you. And that is why we must now tell you the price.*

* * *

They showed her the terms the way a creditor shows terms: without flinching, without apology, laid out plain.

The original covenant, preserved in the court's memory the way amber preserves insects, had required two voices. One from the shore, one from the salt, bound in trust, meeting at the gate each turning of the year to renew the words. Simple. Sustainable. A marriage of realms, not a sacrifice.

But the rite as it would have to be performed now — now, with the gate broken a century, with the binding-words scattered and the threshold collapsed — was another matter. To reforge what had been shattered, the covenant demanded a foundation. And a foundation, the queen said, must be permanent.

*A voice given wholly to the salt,* she said. *Yours, speaker. You would descend past the gate and speak the binding from inside, and the words would take root in you, and you would never return. Your voice above the water would be gone. Forever. The sea would hold it, as the sea holds everything given to it.*

Ines felt Corren shift behind her. Felt him start to speak and stop himself, which was, from Corren, more eloquent than any argument.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

The queen did not soften. That was the thing Ines would remember longest — that mercy, when it finally came, arrived with teeth bared.

*Then the tides come for the coast towns within one moon. Not in anger. In arithmetic. The covenant held the sea back from the land and the land back from the sea, and without it there is only the old arithmetic: the water rises, the people drown, the drowned rise, and so it has always gone. We did not make the terms. We have lived a century under them, enslaved in your guild's contracts, and we are done waiting. One moon, speaker. Reforge the covenant, or we let the water remember what it is.*

"You'd kill thousands."

*Your guild killed thousands of us,* the queen said, and there was no cruelty in it at all, which made it worse. *We are not asking you to be good. We are asking you to choose which debt gets paid.*

* * *

They gave her a chamber to consider it — a pocket of air in the deep stone, dry-floored, lit by lures of pale light that drifted like slow thoughts. Corren sat across from her, elbows on knees, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"You should say no," he said finally.

Ines looked up. "You want me to let the coast drown?"

"No." He rubbed his face. His hands, she had noticed, were a warden's hands even disgraced — scarred, steady, always half-curled around a rope that wasn't there. "I want you to understand what you'd be giving up. I've spent ten years underwater in every way but the literal one, Ines. Guilt is a kind of drowning. You don't come back from it. You just learn to breathe wrong." He met her eyes. "Don't volunteer to breathe wrong forever."

"And the alternative is Harrowmouth under thirty feet of water. The fishing villages. The children." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "You know what the worst part is? It's a fair price. That's what I can't stop thinking about. I've read ten thousand unfair entries in those ledgers — debts invented, interest compounded on nothing, whole villages billed for their own rescue. This is the first honest term anyone's ever offered me. My voice, for the coast. Clean trade. No hidden clause."

"There's always a hidden clause."

"Then help me find it."

He was quiet a moment. Then, slowly, he said: "The covenant was reforged once before. Before either of us. After the first breach, three hundred years back. There's a record — I saw it, in my wardenship days, before they sealed the archives. The rite then didn't require a permanent sacrifice. It required two speakers, together, at the gate." He frowned. "But the archives were sealed the same decade the gate broke. Same signatures. Guildmaster Vane's predecessors. If the terms changed—"

"—then someone changed them," Ines finished. Her heart was beating very fast, and not from fear. From recognition. From the specific thrill of a ledger that doesn't add up. "Someone edited the covenant. Someone took a rite built on partnership and rewrote it as a rite built on sacrifice, and they did it in the same years they were selling tide-born souls by the hundred." She stood. "Why? Why make it cost more?"

Corren looked at her, and she watched the answer arrive in his face the same moment it arrived in hers.

"Because a price that high guarantees no one ever pays it," he said. "It's not a clause. It's a lock. They didn't want the covenant reforged. They wanted it impossible."

The pale lights drifted. Somewhere far above, Ines imagined the surface, the harbor, the counting-house with its green lamps and its endless honest-seeming columns of figures. Below her, the sea waited with its century of patience nearly spent.

"I'm not signing anything tonight," she said. "Not down here, not on their terms, not on terms written by Vane's kind. If the sacrifice clause is forged, the proof is in the original deed — the physical one, the one the guild keeps in its vault because they've kept everything else of ours for a hundred years." She turned to Corren. "The court gave us a moon. I need three days. Get me back to the surface, and get me into that vault."

"That vault has killed better thieves than either of us."

"Good thing I'm not a thief," Ines said. "I'm an auditor."

* * *

They rose through the shafts in grey dawn light, the eel guiding them up through water that grew warmer and shallower and full of the small busy sounds of things beginning their day. At the last pool before open air, where the mine-tunnel broke through into a flooded quarry, the queen's voice followed them up one final time, arriving through the water against Ines's boots.

*Three days, speaker. Then the moon begins to count.*

And then the surface, and the ordinary world, and the ordinary world was waiting for her with its own arithmetic.

She saw the notice nailed to the quarry-side customs post before Corren did — a broadsheet, fresh ink, the guild's seal stamped fat and black at the bottom. She made him stop. Made herself read it standing straight, hands folded, the way she'd read a thousand routine postings in the counting-house.

BY ORDER OF THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS:

INES GALLOWAY, CLERK, STANDS ACCUSED OF THEFT OF GUILD PROPERTY, CONSPIRACY WITH OUTLAWED POWERS, AND THE MURDER OF A GUILD COURIER.

REWARD: TWO HUNDRED CROWNS, ALIVE FOR QUESTIONING. FIFTY DEAD.

Below it, smaller, in the clerk's hand she knew better than her own — the hand she had trained, the hand she had corrected apprentices in for six years — a description. Her height. Her hair. The scar on her left thumb from a paper knife, noted with terrible precision, because whoever wrote it had known exactly where to look.

They had killed the courier themselves, of course. Or he had died of his wounds running from them, and they had written his death onto her account the way they wrote everything onto other people's accounts. Two hundred crowns. Enough to make honest men of every farmer between here and Harrowmouth.

Corren read it twice. "Three days," he said quietly. "A manhunt. And a vault that eats thieves." He glanced at her sideways, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen in ten years of his drinking and five days of his shadowing — something that might, in a better man, have been called faith. "Still feeling like an auditor?"

Ines pulled her hood up over her hair, tucked the map against her heart, and looked down the long road toward the city, where the storm-light was already gathering over the counting-house roofs.

"I'm feeling," she said, "like the audit's finally coming due."

Behind them, far below, the sea began, very softly, to count.
