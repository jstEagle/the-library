## 7. The Reading of Accounts

Corren had spent ten years learning how to be nobody, and it turned out that surrendering was the easiest thing he had ever done.

He walked up the guild steps at dawn, when the counting-house clerks were still lighting their lamps and the harbor fog lay against the windows like something waiting to be let in. His boots were wet. They had been wet for three days, since the flooded mine-shafts, since the Drowned Court, since Ines Galloway had stood before a throne of coral and old bone and refused — magnificently, impossibly — to sign anything. He could still hear her voice down there, ringing off stone that hadn't heard a human word in a century: *I don't buy mercy with other people's drowning.*

The doorwardens knew him. That was the bitter joke of it. Ten years of disgrace and they still straightened when he came through the doors, some old reflex of rank buried in their spines like a coin in mud.

"Corren," said the senior one, whose name Corren had never bothered to learn because names were things you gave to people you expected to see again. "You're wanted. There's a warrant—"

"I know." He set his warden's badge on the marble counter. It was green with salt corrosion, the crest worn to a ghost of itself. "That's why I'm here. I'm turning myself in."

The man blinked. Around them the clerks paused, quills hovering, the whole great room of ledgers and lamplight going quiet in that particular way counting-houses go quiet — not with awe but with arithmetic, everyone calculating what this would cost them.

"And the girl?" someone asked from the back. "Galloway. The thief."

"There is no thief," Corren said, and heard his own voice come out flat and clean, like a blade laid on a table. "There's only a ledger no one has read. I'd like to make a statement. All of it. On the record. Before your enforcers waste another day chasing a woman who's done nothing but carry your debts for you."

* * *

They put him in the testimony room, which was really just an interrogation cell with better furniture, and for six hours he talked.

He told them about the breach of the Salt Gate — not the official version, the tidy version where the tide-born turned treacherous and the gate failed through no fault of anyone with a guild seal. He told them about the year 89 After Binding, when the covenant's original deed had been quietly removed from the vault and replaced with a copy, and the copy had been altered, and the alteration had been initialed by a guildmaster whose portrait still hung in the stairwell with gilt on its frame.

He told them about the dried seabed villages. About how the water hadn't simply receded after the breach; it had been *diverted*, sold in acre-feet to irrigation consortiums, the drowned lands parceled and deeded while the tide-born who lived there were written off as casualties of their own supposed treachery. He told them about the ledgers he'd found in the warden-archive before they stripped him of rank — entries in a dead clerk's hand, neat as prayer: *Proceeds of saline reclamation, quarter three.* As if the sea were a field. As if the people of the deep were a crop.

He told them about Ines Galloway. Carefully. Too carefully, he knew, and didn't care. He described her as a witness, nothing more, a clerk who had come into possession of a map and followed it out of simple bookkeeping conscience. He did not say she could speak to gulls. He did not say that in the flooded dark below the mountain he had watched a half-drowned eel rise to her voice like a hound to a whistle, or that when she spoke the binding-tongue the water itself seemed to lean in and listen. Some truths were too valuable to hand to men who kept their inkwells locked.

"What I want," he said, when his voice had gone raw, "is time. She needs a moon. One moon, and the coast towns stand. Take my testimony now, verify it slowly, convene whatever committees you like. But call off the enforcers until the verification's done. You can't hang a witness mid-statement; even your charter says so. Article nine."

The guildmaster's advocate — a narrow woman named Pell with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like closed shutters — studied him across the table for a long moment.

"You understand," she said, "that this testimony implicates the office you're giving it to."

"I'm counting on it," Corren said. "It's the only reason any of you will sit still long enough to hear it."

They took him down to the holding cells beneath the counting-house, three floors of stone and dripping pipes, and locked the door with a sound like a full stop.

He sat on the cot and found, to his mild surprise, that he wasn't afraid. Fear required a future, and he had spent ten years without one. What he felt instead was lighter than hope and harder to name — the feeling of a debt finally entered into the book. Somewhere above him, Ines Galloway was running, and every hour they spent verifying his words was an hour she breathed free.

He thought about the way she'd looked in the Drowned Court, salt-damp hair stuck to her cheek, refusing a throne's mercy because mercy bought with blackmail wasn't mercy at all. He thought: *she'll come back. Not for me. For the accounts.* And he thought, with the honesty that only cells and storms allow: *and I would wait a hundred years in this stone box for the chance to watch her read them aloud.*

Then he slept, and dreamed of green water, and did not dream of drowning for the first time in ten years.

* * *

Ines read his journals by candlelight in the loft above the chandler's shop, where Marla the chandler — who owed Ines's aunt an old kindness and asked no questions — let her hide among the tallow and the rope.

She had stolen them. That was the truth of it, and she made herself hold the word: *stolen*. From the evidence-room cart outside the courthouse, in the confusion of Corren's arrest, while two enforcers argued about whose jurisdiction the seizure fell under. Her hands had shaken so badly she'd nearly dropped the oilskin bundle. She had never stolen anything in her life before the map. Now she was becoming fluent in it, and the fluency frightened her more than the enforcers did.

There were eleven journals. They spanned twenty-two years — his whole wardenhood, from the bright first volume with its careful copperplate to the last, where the handwriting slumped like a man walking into weather. She had taken them expecting evidence. Testimony she could use, dates and figures, ammunition. What she found instead was a man.

The early volumes were all duty. *Patrol log, north shore. Gate integrity: nominal. Tide-born sightings: none, as usual.* Page after page of a young officer doing everything right, believing in the covenant the way you believe in a bridge — not thinking about it, just walking across.

Then the breach year, and the entries changed.

*Gate failed at slack tide. No warning. The court below has gone silent. Requesting permission to descend and assess. Denied. Requesting permission to send a diver. Denied. Requesting — denied.*

And then, in a different ink, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn the page:

*Nobody will tell me why we are not going down there. I have served eight years without a blemish and they will not tell me why the water is empty and the gate is broken and no one in this entire guild will look at the sea. I am starting to think the silence is not grief. I am starting to think it is policy.*

Ines turned pages with fingers that weren't quite steady.

The middle years were the drunk years, though he never once used the word. He wrote around it. *Slept badly. Slept worse. Did not sleep.* He wrote about the harbor at night, about standing on the breakwater listening for anything — a gull, a bell-buoy, a voice — and hearing only the ordinary noise of a sea that had stopped answering. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, like a man returning to a window:

*I miss them. Not the idea of them. Them. The specific weight of being watched by something older than you. The eels used to follow the patrol boats. The gulls used to report to us — actual reports, fish-runs, storm-signs, the positions of drowned hazards. We were spoken to once. We answered with a deed of sale.*

She stopped reading. Sat very still in the tallow-smelling dark, the candle guttering, and understood that she was crying, which was inconvenient and absurd, because these were not her tears to spend.

Because she knew that silence. She had grown up inside it — the gulls that wouldn't land near her aunt's cottage, the harbor that had gone mute the year they burned her aunt on the quay. She had thought the silence hated her. Reading his journals, she realized the silence had been grieving, and that this man had been standing in it for twenty years, sober or drunk, promoted or disgraced, keeping faith with a conversation that had ended before he was born.

Near the end — the last volume, the last pages, written in the weeks after he'd begun following her — the entries shortened. Less report, more confession, though he plainly didn't know he was confessing.

*She balances books the way the tide balances the moon. I have arrested people for less than what she's innocent of.*

And, two pages later, in ink so faint he must have been writing by memory in the dark:

*Ten years I thought my punishment was the silence. It wasn't. The silence was the gift. It taught me to listen. And now there is finally something worth hearing, and I am going to lose it to a cell, and that is correct, and I would sign the order myself.*

*If she reads this: the accounts are in the third journal, the one with the water stain. Everything the guild buried is there. Use it. Don't use it for me. There's nothing left of me worth the paper.*

Ines closed the journal and held it against her chest and let herself, for exactly one minute, be a woman and not a fugitive.

Then she opened the third journal, the one with the water stain, and began to read the true accounts of the Guild of Impossible Things — and halfway down the first page she went cold, because there, in the margin of the oldest entry, in a hand she recognized from her aunt's few surviving letters, was a note in the binding-tongue.

Her aunt had seen these numbers. Her aunt had died for less than this page.

Below the note, fresh ink, Corren's cramped hand: *Found this translation tucked in the archive. It says: the gate was never broken. It was locked from our side. Someone still holds the key.*

Outside, the wind was rising off the harbor, and the first gulls of the storm were coming in low over the rooftops, and every one of them, as they passed the chandler's loft, turned its head and cried — once, sharply, deliberately — in a direction Ines had learned, in the last fortnight, to translate without thinking.

*Toward the gate,* they said. *Come now. The water is rising, and it is rising angry.*
