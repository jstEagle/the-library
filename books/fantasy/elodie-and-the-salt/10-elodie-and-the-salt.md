## 10. Elodie and the Salt

The gulls came at dawn, as they always did now.

Ines stood on the threshold of the Salt Gate with her ledger open against her forearm, the way she had once balanced accounts in the counting-house, and listened to what the birds brought her. A fisherman's widow in Greyharbor asked after the tides for her son's wedding. A child in the dried seabed village — Brackhollow, they called it again now that the water had begun to return — wanted to know if the eels remembered him, because he had fed one from his hand before the frost. And far out past the shoals, a voice like cold silk through green water: *the deep vaults are ready for inspection, keeper. The drowned have found your guild's old ledgers, and there are errors.*

There were always errors. That was the nature of accounting, and of forgiveness, and of the long slow work of making two realms into one household.

"Tell them I'll come at the turn of the tide," Ines said aloud.

She had no voice above water. She had not spoken with her own throat since the day of the Speaking of Salt, when the covenant had taken her words the way a river takes rain — not stealing them, only carrying them onward, so that everything she said arrived somewhere else. When she spoke on the shore, gulls repeated it in the fishing towns. When she spoke beneath the surface, every eel in every flooded shaft carried it down to the court. Her voice belonged to the water now, and the water was generous with it. She had never been so widely heard, nor so quietly alone.

But the gulls understood her anyway. They always had. One wheeled down from the grey morning sky and landed on the gate's broken arch, head cocked, one bright eye fixed on her face.

"You heard me," she said, smiling.

The gull said nothing, because gulls say nothing to anyone. But it dipped its wings once, formally, the way a clerk acknowledges receipt, and lifted off toward Greyharbor with her promise folded into its feathers.

* * *

The new covenant was written in no ink at all, which was fitting, because the old one had been written in too much of it — in the guildmaster's careful hand, in the forged sacrifice clause, in the death warrant that had borne her aunt's name. This one lived in the salt itself, in the memory the stranger had told her about with his dying breath: *the salt remembers who owes.* It could not be burned or stolen or locked in a vault. Every tide renewed it. Every voice that crossed the threshold reaffirmed it.

Its terms were simple, as true things usually are:

The tide-born were heirs to the shore — its harbors, its markets, its songs sung in taverns where the beer was bad and the company good. Humans were heirs to the deep — its gardens of cold light, its drowned mines seeded now with pearl-farms, its silence, which Corren swore was not empty but merely patient. Neither realm owed the other servitude. Both owed each other witness. And between them, keeping the accounts, stood a living ledger: a woman who had once hidden her gift in the margins of other people's numbers, and who now wrote in the great book of the sea itself.

Elodie would have laughed at it. Elodie, who had taught her that listening to beasts was a sin worth burning for; Elodie, whose name Ines had given to the covenant's first clause in defiance of every warrant ever signed — *let this be called the Elodie Accord, for all the voices the ledgers failed to record.* The guild clerks had objected. The clerks objected to everything, right up until the day the tide rose politely to the doors of the counting-house and waited, patient as interest accruing, until the guild agreed to answer to a higher authority than itself.

Now the Guild of Impossible Things kept two sets of books, openly, for the first time in its history: one for the human realm, and one — audited quarterly by an eel of terrifying thoroughness named Vess — for the sea.

* * *

Corren came across the threshold at midmorning, as he did most days, wading out of the green-lit water with his warden's coat slung over his shoulder and his hair dripping salt. Ten years ago he had been a drunk drowning in the guilt of a gate he'd failed to guard. Now he was something rarer: a man with two homes and honest work in both.

"Messages," he said, and handed her a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Letters from the shore towns, questions from the court, a child's drawing from Brackhollow rendered in charcoal on fish-skin — a stick figure with wild hair standing on a gate, surrounded by birds. "And complaints. Vess says your last audit entry was 'poetic rather than arithmetical.'"

"It was both."

"She wants it redone."

"She can take it up with the tide." Ines untied the bundle, breathing in the paper-and-brine smell of correspondence that had traveled through a drowned world. "Anything from the guild?"

"One thing." His face did something complicated — the old cynicism and the new hope wrestling briefly, as they still sometimes did. "They've opened their archives. All of them. The fraud ledgers, the breach contracts, the warrants." He paused. "Your aunt's file is there. They're offering restitution. Whatever form the families want."

Ines looked out at the water. The morning light lay on it like hammered tin, and beneath the light, far down, the drowned court glimmered — towers of coral-grown stone where her other people kept their own records, older and more honest than anything made on land.

"What form do I want," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked through any account, "is for the file to be read aloud. Every page. In the market square at Greyharlow, by the guildmaster himself, with the whole town listening. Not as punishment." She shook her head. "Punishment just moves the debt around. As *reading*. You can't heal a ledger you won't open."

"I'll carry it down," Corren said. "Vess will weep with joy. Eels love procedural reform."

He said it dryly, but his hand found hers, salt-warm, and neither of them let go. Love, they had learned, was mostly this: ferrying. Carrying each other's words across the water, back and forth, year upon year, and finding that the crossing itself had become the home.

* * *

At noon she went down.

The flooded mine-shafts had become roads. Lantern-jellies hung in the dark like streetlamps; kelp gardens grew where ore-carts had once rattled; and everywhere the tide-born moved through the green water with their slow grave grace, nodding to her as she passed. *Keeper,* they said, in the pressure-language she read through her skin. Some of the eldest still remembered the first covenant, a century gone, and the century of silence after — the years when the human side simply stopped coming, stopped speaking, let the terms rot while the guild grew rich on enslaved pearl-divers and stolen breeding-grounds. Inherited shame sat in her chest some days like swallowed stone. She had learned not to swallow it alone. Shame shared across a threshold, she had found, weighed half as much.

The court received her in the Hall of Accounts — their name for it, adopted gleefully from hers. Vess was waiting, coiled on the dais beside the great abacus of whale-bone and moonstone that served as the sea's treasury, her silvered body flickering with impatience.

"The deep vaults," Vess said without preamble. "We found the original deed. The one your guildmaster stole from us during the breach."

Ines went very still. "The forged one?"

"No." The eel's voice slid like current over stone. "The true one. The first covenant, before any amendment, before any forgery. There is a clause neither of you knew existed. We have been translating it for three days." A pause, deliberate, savoring. "It seems the covenant was always meant to be *rewritten*. Each generation of keepers was to amend it — add their own terms, strike what had rotted. Your ancestors' crime was not only that they stopped coming. It is that they hid the pen."

Ines felt the words move through her the way her own voice moved through water — outward, outward, carried by every listening thing. Somewhere above, she knew, gulls were repeating fragments of her silence to fishermen. Somewhere below, eels were already carrying the news to the far trenches.

"And the amendments?" she asked. "Who writes them?"

"That," said Vess, uncoiling, "is why we sent for you, Keeper. The court has drafted its proposals. The shore must draft theirs. And the treaty requires—" the eel managed, somehow, to look smug, "—a joint reading. Both keepers. One voice each. At the gate, at the turning of the spring tide."

Which was tomorrow.

* * *

That evening she and Corren sat on the broken arch of the Salt Gate, their feet dangling over water that glowed faintly green, the way it always did now near the threshold — the gate singing its low continuous note, the note she had helped restore, the note that was beginning, at last, to sound less like mourning and more like a house being built.

"Joint reading," Corren said. "Both keepers. One voice each." He turned something over in his hands — a small worn notebook, one of the journals she had once read in a guild cell, the ones where he had confessed sideways, on paper, a love he couldn't say aloud. He still kept them. He still wrote in them. She had never told him she knew; some accounts were better left quietly compounding.

"Terms of surrender," she murmured. "That's what the court called it, that first time. Mercy with teeth."

"And instead you rewrote the teeth into a handshake."

"Into an inheritance." She leaned her head against his shoulder. Below them the deep opened its patient green miles; behind them the shore lights kindled one by one along the coast, harbors and hearths and the counting-house windows where clerks worked late under new management. Her home was both places and neither. Her home was the crossing.

"Do you think we'll get the amendments right?" he asked. "This generation? Or will someone in a hundred years find our ledgers full of errors too?"

"Oh, they will." She smiled. "There are always errors. That's the point of keepers — not to be perfect, but to be *readable*. To leave the books open." She watched a night-heron cross the darkening water, low and deliberate, a message from somewhere to somewhere else. "Elodie taught me that listening was a sin. I'm going to spend the rest of my life proving it was the first sacrament."

Corren was quiet a moment. Then he took out his journal, uncapped his pen, and began to write — and she did not need to see the page to know what he was recording, because she heard it in the way the water stilled around them, in the way the gulls settled silent on the arch, in the way the gate's green song bent slightly, tenderly, around the shape of his words:

*Tomorrow we write the next terms. She speaks for the shore. I speak for the deep. And whatever we get wrong, we will leave the pen where the children of both realms can find it.*

Far out past the shoals, something vast and old turned over in its sleep and began, very softly, to sing back.
