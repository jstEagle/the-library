## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with balancing impossible things, Ansel had learned, was that they balanced.

That was the whole horror of it. Bottled thunder could be entered at fair market value if you knew the going rate in Vell and the exchange fee for decanting into glass that wouldn't scream. Borrowed years amortized cleanly over the borrower's remaining span. Even the guild's rarer stock—a widow's second chance, sold back to her at interest; a hanged man's last word, purchased by his enemy and kept corked—these all obeyed arithmetic. Magic was chaos, everyone said so. But magic *traded* was something else entirely. Magic traded wanted its columns to sum.

Ansel Fairweather, junior bookkeeper of the Gilded Scale, third desk from the drafty window, was very good at making them sum.

They sat now with their sleeves rolled and a blotter under their wrist, working through the autumn audit by lamplight while rain ticked against the guildhouse glass like a clerk counting out someone's change. Around them the counting hall breathed its usual evening sounds: quills, coughing, the great iron safe downstairs groaning as it digested another day of receipts. Someone had left a cold meat pie on the corner of Ansel's desk as a kindness or a bribe. Ansel hadn't decided which yet.

"Fairweather." Master Osric Quennel's voice came down the row of desks, dry as old vellum. "You're still on the Vell thunder consignment?"

"Three barrels short," Ansel said without looking up. "Or one barrel and a very generous rounding."

"Round generously. The client is a duke."

"The ledger doesn't care if he's a duke."

Quennel paused, and Ansel felt the older man's attention settle on them like a hand on the shoulder. It was the closest thing to affection the Gilded Scale permitted before nine o'clock. "No," Quennel agreed. "It doesn't. That's why we love it." He moved on, and his footsteps faded, and Ansel was alone with the numbers again, which was how Ansel liked it best.

Invisible. That was the word. Not hidden exactly—the guildhouse had no use for people who hid—but *unremarked*. A bookkeeper who did flawless work, asked no questions, took no credit, wanted nothing. Six years Ansel had been that person. Six years since a poacher named Tam Vetch had shoved them onto a cart under sacking with a forged travel writ and said, *Don't come back, don't write, don't even think your own name too loud.* Six years since Ansel had become Fairweather, junior, third desk from the window, a person whose entire history fit in one column: hired, promoted once, punctual.

It should have been enough. Most nights it was.

Ansel dipped the pen and reached the next line of the audit, and stopped.

The entry sat there between *one borrowed winter, returned damaged* and *half a luck, split two ways*, and it had not been there when Ansel turned the page. That much Ansel would swear on anything. They had read every line above it twice. Ledgers did not grow entries the way mold grew on bread. Ledgers were the one honest thing in the world; that was why Ansel had chosen them, or been chosen by them, after everything else burned.

*One soul, payable beneath.*

No name of seller. No name of buyer. No date of sale, only a date of collection—solstice, ninety-one days hence—and a rate column marked not in crowns or marks but in a currency symbol Ansel had never seen: a small circle with a line drawn through it, like an eye closed by a blade.

And the handwriting.

Ansel set down the pen very carefully, the way you set down a thing that might be listening.

It was their handwriting. Not similar. Identical—the same cramped ascendants, the same leftward slant on the numerals, the same little hook on the terminal stroke that Maren Solane had spent a whole summer trying to beat out of them and had finally pronounced *a flaw with character*. Ansel had written ledgers in this hand for six years. Thousands of pages. They knew it the way they knew their own pulse.

But they had not written this.

Ansel looked around the hall. Quills scratched on, indifferent. Rain ticked. Downstairs, the safe groaned again, longer this time, like something swallowing. Nobody was watching them. Nobody ever watched them; that was the arrangement, the whole quiet genius of being Fairweather.

They touched the ink. It was dry. Dry meant hours old at least, maybe days—and yet the audit had been clean when they'd worked this exact page that morning. Ansel remembered the morning. Remembered the sum coming right on the first pass, which almost never happened, which had made them feel briefly, foolishly blessed.

Unless the sum had come right because the entry had already been there, and Ansel's eye had slid across it the way an eye slides across a familiar face in a crowd.

*One soul, payable beneath.*

Beneath what? Beneath whom? There was no beneath in Vell. There was the mountain, but the mountain was a mountain; it had rock in it and snow and, according to the surveyors, absolutely nothing else, because the surveyors had surveyed it twice and were very smug about it. Below the city there were cellars and sewers and one scandalous crypt belonging to a family whose name Ansel had helpfully recorded in a different ledger, and none of those places bought souls. Souls, per the guild's own tariff schedule—which Ansel had copied out as a training exercise and still knew by heart—were classed as *non-transferable assets*. Page forty-one. There was a footnote about exceptions, and the footnote said *see restricted schedule*, and the restricted schedule lived in Corvane's office behind a door that opened for exactly one person.

Guildmaster Corvane. Who signed Ansel's wages. Who had, six years ago, taken in a soot-streaked runaway with no papers and no past and said only, *Can you add?* — and when Ansel said yes, had said, *Then you're worth feeding.*

Ansel's mouth had gone dry somewhere around the second look at the handwriting. Now it went drier.

Because there was one more thing about the entry, one more wrongness stacked on top of the others like bad debts compounding. Every other line in the Gilded Scale's master audit carried a broker's initials in the margin. O.Q., T.B., H.M., the whole alphabet of the trade floor. This line carried nothing. Except—

Except in the far right margin, where the collection date sat, someone had pressed a seal into wax that wasn't wax. It looked like dried blood gone brown, and the impression in it was not a sigil Ansel knew, and it was not the Scale's scales. It was a door. A simple door, shut, with no handle.

Ansel became aware that they were holding their breath, and let it out slowly, professionally, the way you'd ease air out of a sealed cask.

Options. That was what Maren had always said, back when there had been a Maren and a summer kitchen that smelled of tansy and hot iron: *When the world turns strange, love, you don't panic and you don't gawk. You make a list. Panic is just arithmetic done badly.* Ansel had watched her work hedge-craft the way other people cooked—tasting, adjusting, muttering *needs more salt* over charms—and she had taught them that underneath all the wonder, craft was bookkeeping too. Everything owed. Everything recorded. Everything paid.

Maren Solane had been dead eleven years. Burned in the market square of Harrow Fen for unlicensed craft, while the magistrate read out the charges in a voice like a man reciting grain prices. Ansel had been fourteen. Ansel had run. And the magistrate who'd signed the order—that signature Ansel also knew by heart, though they'd have paid real money never to see it again—had a name that still tasted of smoke: Hallowell.

None of which helped with the present problem. Present problem: a soul, entered in Ansel's own hand, due at solstice, payable *beneath*, witnessed by a shut door.

List. Do it properly.

One: the entry existed, therefore someone had put it there. Two: it was in Ansel's hand, therefore either Ansel had written it and forgotten—which was impossible, because Ansel forgot nothing, forgetting was a luxury for people with uncomplicated pasts—or someone had written it *as* Ansel, which required either forgery of a quality that would collapse the entire economy of proof the guild ran on, or something stranger that Ansel refused to begin speculating about at half past eight on a rainy Thursday. Three: souls were non-transferable, therefore the tariff schedule was wrong, or the entry was wrong, or the world had quietly changed terms and nobody had circulated the amendment.

Four—and here Ansel's pen hovered, because four was the one they didn't want—four: whoever wrote it had used Ansel's hand because the debt was somehow, in whatever way the deep ledgers recognized, *Ansel's to write*. Debts needed a responsible party. Ink needed an author. And the ledger, which was honest, which was the one honest thing, had reached into six years of careful invisibility and picked Ansel out by name.

Not by the name anyone here knew. By the name under it.

"Fairweather." Quennel again, passing with a stack of receipts, and Ansel flinched hard enough that the pen jumped and blotted a small black star next to the entry. "You look like you've seen the year-end figures early."

"Just tired," Ansel said. Their voice came out level. Six years of practice; the voice worked even when the hands didn't. "I'll finish the audit tonight."

"Leave the last folio till morning. Nothing in it but the routine consignments." Quennel paused. "You're sure about the Vell rounding?"

"Generously rounded," Ansel said. "The duke will weep with gratitude."

"Hm." And he moved on, and the hall swallowed him, and Ansel sat alone with the blot-star drying next to *one soul, payable beneath*, and understood that they were going to do the stupid thing. The un-Fairweather thing. The thing that invisible people did not do.

They turned to the last folio anyway.

Routine consignments. Quennel had said it lightly, and Quennel was a good man within the limits of the guild, which were the same limits that bounded a well-made cage. But the last folio was not routine. Ansel saw that within three lines. The entries were ordinary—two borrowed years, a bottled fog, a luck-pawn redeemed—all ordinary except that each one had been routed through the same intermediary account, and Ansel knew that account, had reconciled it quarterly for six years without ever once asking whose money sat behind its number, because not asking was the job, was the sanctuary, was the deal.

Account 77. Magistrate's discretionary fund, Office of Judicial Affairs. Hallowell's account. The man who had burned Maren Solane had been buying things through the Gilded Scale for years, quietly, through a number instead of a name, and Ansel had balanced his purchases with their eyes politely shut, quarter after quarter after quarter.

The comfort of being invisible curdled all at once, the way milk does—not gradually, but in one warm moment, irreversibly.

Ansel sat very still. Rain ticked. Somewhere below, the safe had gone quiet, and the quiet was worse than the groaning.

*One soul, payable beneath,* in their own hand. Ninety-one days. And threaded through the tail of the audit like a fuse, account 77, buying and buying, and nobody upstairs asking questions because the guild never asked questions, that was the founding principle, that was the whole sacred architecture of the place: *we move the goods, we keep the sums, we know nothing.* Ansel had loved that principle for six years. It had kept them fed and nameless and alive.

Now it meant that if the ledger was telling the truth—if souls could be sold, if someone beneath the world was collecting—then the guild had already brokered it, already taken its commission, already filed it away behind a restricted door, and the only person in Vell who would notice was a bookkeeper with a flawless eye and a past they'd sworn never to think about too loudly.

Tam Vetch's voice came up out of eleven years: *Don't even think your own name too loud.* Well. Too late for that.

Ansel closed the folio. Did not copy the entry. Did not show anyone. Instead they did the one thing that was both perfectly ordinary and perfectly damning: they finished the audit, ruled the columns, wrote *balanced* at the foot of the page in their own unmistakable hand, and initialed it—A.F.—directly beside the entry they had not written, so that now, officially, ineradicably, the impossible thing bore their mark.

If the ledger wanted an author, it had one.

They banked the lamp, gathered their coat, and stood a moment in the darkened hall with the rain loud on the glass. Down in the vault, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the safe began to groan again—and this time, Ansel could not help noticing, in a rhythm. Four beats. Pause. Four beats. Like knocking. Like something on the other side of the iron, patient as a creditor, keeping count of the days until solstice.

Outside, dusk was falling early over Vell, and along the border hills, though Ansel couldn't have known it yet, the first ward-stone was beginning, very quietly, to die.
