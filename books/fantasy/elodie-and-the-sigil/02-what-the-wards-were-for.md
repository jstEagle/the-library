## 2. What the Wards Were For

The wards failed at dusk, and Ansel Fairweather was the first person in the Gilded Scale to notice, because Ansel was the only person in the Gilded Scale who bothered to look out the window.

They had been reconciling the third-quarter entries for bottled thunder—a tedious business, because the storm-brokers of the Vell Coast rounded their figures in threes and the guild's auditors rounded in fives, and somewhere between those two habits a small fortune in lightning went missing every season. Ansel had found eleven drams of it so far, hidden in freight surcharges like coins sewn into a smuggler's coat. It was good work. It was invisible work. Nobody thanked a bookkeeper for finding money that had never technically been lost.

The light through the counting-house window changed color.

Not the usual dusk—amber going to violet, violet going to blue. This was amber going to nothing, a gray like wet ash sliding down from the hills, and along the ridge road where the border ward-stones stood at half-mile intervals, each stone wore a faint silver shimmer that Ansel had stopped seeing years ago because it had always been there, the way you stop seeing your own hands.

The shimmer guttered. Stone by stone, west to east, like candles being pinched by an unhurried hand.

Ansel set down the pen.

For a moment they did nothing but breathe and watch, because there is a particular discipline to being invisible: you do not react first. You do not raise your voice. You become furniture until the danger passes over you. Maren Solane had taught them that once—or someone had; the memory came with a smell of woodsmoke attached and no face, which was how most of Ansel's childhood arrived these days.

Then the last ward-stone on the ridge went dark, and the gray light reached the city walls, and every dog in Lowmarket began to howl at once.

* * *

By full dark, the Gilded Scale's great hall smelled of tallow and fear. Clerks who normally went home at sundown clustered by the hearth in twos and threes, speaking in the low voices people use when they are pretending not to be afraid. Someone had lit every lamp in the building, which was against guild regulation and which nobody had objected to.

Ansel stood at the edge of it all with a cup of bad wine they weren't drinking, doing what they always did: watching, cataloguing, keeping their back to a wall.

The strangest thing—the thing that made the hair on their arms stand up whenever they let themselves think about it directly—was the sky.

There were no stars.

Not clouded. Not obscured. *Gone.* The city's smoke-haze could soften the heavens but never erase them; even on the worst coal-smoke nights of winter, the Bright Sisters still burned through like needles. Tonight Ansel had walked out into the courtyard, tipped their head back, and found a black so total and so smooth it looked painted. The moon was up somewhere—it had risen fat and gibbous an hour past sundown—and its light arrived dimmed, as if coming through cloth.

"Fairweather."

Guildmaster Corvane's voice carried without effort. He stood at the head of the hall on the dais beneath the guild's great brass scale, and he did not look frightened, which Ansel noted and filed away as either courage or complicity. Corvane was a narrow man, gray as ledger-paper, with the kind of stillness that came from decades of sitting behind desks while other people's fortunes were decided around him.

"The wards," said Warden-Clerk Ossory. "The border stones are dead. All of them. And the—"

"I know what the stones are doing." Corvane's voice was mild. Mildness, in Corvane, was worse than shouting. "I have received word from three chapterhouses. I am aware of the sky."

"What do we tell the clients?" Ossory again. He was a big man, honest-faced, the sort whose worry showed in his shoulders. "Half the contracts in the vaults reference the wards as security. If the wards are down, the collateral terms—"

"There will be no refunds."

Silence moved through the hall like a draft.

"Guildmaster," Ossory began, "the Vell Coast storm-brokers alone hold—"

"No messages." Corvane lifted one finger, and the hall went quiet enough to hear the lamps hiss. "No letters leave this building tonight or tomorrow. No pigeons. No runners. No refunds, no renegotiations, no public statements. If a client asks, the wards are undergoing scheduled maintenance. If a client insists, refer them to me personally." A pause. "And no questions. Not among yourselves. Not tonight. Whatever has happened, it happened above our pay grade, and the fastest way for this guild to die is for forty clerks to start speculating in the streets."

He looked out over them, and his gaze passed across Ansel the way it always did—without catching, without snagging, the look of a man seeing a chair—and then he stepped down from the dais and left the hall through the private door, and the murmur broke loose behind him like water through a cracked dam.

Ansel put down the untouched wine and slipped out after him.

* * *

Corvane's office sat above the vault, which Ansel had always thought was either sentiment or strategy: the man slept directly on top of everything the guild actually valued. Ansel didn't knock. Knocking announced yourself, and besides, Corvane had summoned them with a single glance on his way out—not at them, precisely, but at the space beside the door, which for Corvane constituted a shout.

"You noticed first," Corvane said, without looking up from the papers on his desk. "The wards. Before any runner, before any alarm. How?"

"I saw the light change."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Ansel kept their voice level, their hands loose. "I was looking out the window. Most people weren't."

Corvane finally raised his head. In the lamplight his eyes were the color of old ink, and for a moment he studied Ansel with something that wasn't quite suspicion—more like a merchant weighing a coin he suspected might be clipped.

"Three years you've kept my books," he said. "Cleanest ledgers I've ever seen. No vices I can find, no debts, no family, no history before the day you walked in and asked for work. Do you know what that makes you, Fairweather?"

"Invisible," Ansel said.

"Valuable." Corvane almost smiled. "Invisible too, yes. Sit down."

Ansel didn't sit. Corvane didn't insist. Instead he drew a sheet of paper toward himself and turned it so Ansel could read it: a supply manifest, guild letterhead, columns of figures in Corvane's own cramped hand.

"Ward-stone maintenance," Corvane said. "Every stone on the northern ridge was re-cored within the last eighteen months. New heartstones, new binding-sigils, the full service. It was supposed to buy us another twenty years." He tapped the bottom line. "Tell me what you see."

Ansel read. They were good at reading numbers; it was the one language that never lied about having lied. The manifest listed the contractor, the delivery dates, the inspection fees—and the payment account.

One account. Every stone, every fee, every core and sigil and binding-wax, drawn down through a single client account held in trust.

Ansel knew the account number. Every bookkeeper in the building knew it, the way sailors know the name of the sea. It was the largest standing account in the guild's custody, older than most of the clerks, replenished quarterly by a retainer that had never once been questioned.

It belonged to Magistrate Hallowell.

"Hallowell paid for the ward-stones," Ansel said slowly.

"Hallowell's office pays for half the civic infrastructure in this district. That's what magistrates do. Roads, bridges, granaries, wards." Corvane leaned back. "It's also what magistrates do when they want to know exactly where every nail is buried."

The room felt colder. Ansel made themselves say it plainly, because saying things plainly was a kind of armor: "You think he bought the wards in order to break them."

"I think," Corvane said, "that a man does not purchase twenty years of protection and collect on six months unless he intends the protection to fail. I think the stars went out over this city tonight, and I do not believe in coincidence, and neither do you—I've read your audit notes. You don't believe in anything you can't balance." He folded his hands. "What I do not know is why. And I am not going to find out by asking questions I can't afford to have answered. Neither are you."

"So we do nothing."

"We do our jobs. We keep the books. We keep the guild alive." Corvane's voice stayed mild, and that mildness now sounded to Ansel like a lid screwed down over something boiling. "You've been sheltered here three years, Fairweather. Whatever you ran from, whatever you were before you were a bookkeeper—this guild doesn't ask, and in return it keeps you fed and unnamed and safe. Remember that arithmetic. It's the only one that matters tonight."

Ansel remembered it. Ansel would go on remembering it for a long time, turning it over like a coin with two faces, one reading *shelter* and the other reading *silence*, and it would take them weeks to notice that both faces bore the same mint-mark.

"Yes, Guildmaster," they said, and left.

* * *

Midnight found Ansel in the counting-house with every lamp lit and the soul-entry copied out fair on a fresh sheet, pinned under their palm like a moth.

*One soul, payable beneath.*

The handwriting was still theirs. They had checked four times against their own current hand—against the way they crossed their sevens, the little backward lean of the ascenders, the habit of finishing an *e* with a downward flick instead of a loop. Identical. Not similar. Identical, which was impossible, which meant either forgery of a very high order or something Ansel had no framework for yet, and Ansel hated having no framework more than they hated almost anything.

Around them the city made small frightened noises. Bells that shouldn't ring were ringing in far parishes. Somewhere a mob had gathered at a shrine and was singing, badly, a hymn to stars that weren't there. Through the counting-house window the sky lay overhead like a closed lid.

Ansel worked.

This was the other discipline, the one underneath the invisibility: when the world goes wrong, make lists. Name what you know. Name what you don't. The act of writing imposes a shape on chaos, and shapes can be examined, and examined things lose some portion of their power to terrify.

*Known,* they wrote. *The border wards failed at dusk, all stones simultaneously, west to east. Simultaneous failure suggests a single cause, not decay. The stones were all serviced within eighteen months, all paid through the Hallowell account. The stars are extinguished. Corvane knows more than he said. Corvane ordered silence within the hour, which means he anticipated the need for silence, which means—*

The pen stopped.

Which means he expected this. Or something like it. Which means the Guildmaster of the Gilded Scale heard the words *no refunds, no messages, no questions* in his own head before the sky went dark, and chose not to share them.

Ansel sat back. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if afraid to move.

And beneath all of it, under the wards and the stars and Corvane's quiet arithmetic, lay the entry itself. *One soul, payable beneath.* A debt recorded in Ansel's own hand for a soul belonging to no name Ansel could find in any index—because the entry referenced no client, no contract number, no counter-signature. Just the debt, hanging in the ledger like a hook in deep water, waiting for something to swim close enough.

Ansel had spent three years learning every secret the Gilded Scale kept in its books. Bottled thunder. Borrowed years. Widow's pensions invested in siege futures. The guild traded in impossible things and called them instruments, and Ansel had told themselves that was merely corruption, human-scale, the ordinary greed of ordinary men.

But souls were not instruments. Souls were not a column in a ledger. Whoever had written that entry had used Ansel's hand to do it, and whoever had broken the wards had bought them first through a magistrate's account, and the same night held both disasters like two halves of a closing trap.

Fear had been the first hour's weather. Now, sitting alone in the lamplight with the impossible entry under their palm, Ansel felt the fear cool and harden into something more useful.

Suspicion.

Someone had spent money making the world darker. Money left trails. Ansel pulled the Hallowell account's annual abstract toward them, uncapped the pen, and began, methodically, to read eighteen months of a magistrate's spending.

Two hours later, near dawn, they found the first thread: a disbursement, dated the week before the ward-stone servicing, to a contractor Ansel had never heard of, for goods described only as *ceremonial glass, quantity twelve, delivered below.*

Below.

Ansel looked at the word for a long time. Outside, the false dawn came up gray and starless over a city pretending very hard to be calm, and somewhere in the stacks behind them, in a ledger nobody had opened since spring, a debt waited in Ansel's own handwriting for a soul that had a name—

—they just hadn't found it yet.
