## 3. A Debt in Someone Else's Hand

Ansel did not sleep.

They told themselves it was the audit season, that any bookkeeper worth their salt would lie awake with figures crawling behind their eyes—bottled thunder depreciating at a tenth per annum, borrowed years compounding against their lenders like rot. That was true enough. But when the guildhouse bell rang the small hours and the candle had burned down to a coin of wax, Ansel was still at their desk with the impossible entry open before them, reading it the way you'd read a letter from someone dead.

*One soul, payable beneath.*

The handwriting was theirs. Not similar. Not close. Identical down to the way the *y* in *payable* hooked leftward instead of right, a flaw Ansel had spent years training out of their hand and never quite managed. They turned the page to the light. They held it upside down. They checked the watermark of the ledger paper against the stock kept in the supply room two floors down—the same mill, the same year, the same faint grain of ash in the pulp that meant it came from the eastern mills, where the guild bought cheap because the rivers ran gray.

No forgery they knew of could copy a hand so completely. And Ansel knew forgeries. Forgery was half of what the Gilded Scale sold, dressed up as brokerage: contracts for things that shouldn't be contractable, written in ink that made them real. The other half was silence.

So if the hand was theirs, either someone had stolen it—and hands could be stolen, Ansel had seen it done, peeled off a debtor's signature like skin—or the entry had been waiting for them, patient as a trap, until the night they were assigned this ledger.

Ansel blew out the candle and sat in the dark and let themselves think the thought they'd been avoiding since dusk, when the wards fell and the stars went out one by one like lamps being carried away down a corridor.

*Someone is collecting.*

* * *

Sleep, when it finally took them near dawn, brought Maren Solane back.

Not the way the magistrates' broadsheets had shown her—wild-haired, unlicensed, dangerous—but small and ordinary in her kitchen at the edge of the village, sleeves rolled, teaching Ansel to grind valerian root with a mortar worn smooth as a riverstone. Ansel had been eleven. Their mother had died in the spring; their father drank what remained; and Maren had taken them in the way hedge-witches take things in, without ceremony, because something broken had wandered into her yard and she had never learned how to turn anything away.

"You're grinding it angry," she said. "Angry root makes bitter tea."

"I'm not angry."

"Good. Then stop grinding angry anyway." She took the mortar, demonstrated: slow circles, wrist loose. "Everything you do to a thing goes into the thing. Remember that. It's the whole of craft, more or less. What you do goes in."

"What's the rest?"

"The rest is knowing what you're saying." She tapped Ansel's chest with the pestle handle, gently, between the ribs. "Every spell is a statement, little wren. A true one, or it doesn't hold. You can't charm a door shut by saying it's locked if anyone can see it isn't. Magic is testimony. You're standing up in front of the world and swearing to how things are. And the world—" she smiled, and there was something tired in it even then, years before the fire, "—the world keeps better books than we do."

Ansel woke with the ghost of the pestle handle between their ribs and Maren's voice fading, and lay still in the gray light while the memory settled over them like ash.

Maren Solane, burned for unlicensed craft on a market day when Ansel was fourteen. Ansel remembered standing in the crowd—they had been made to stand in the crowd, all her students had, the magistrate's idea of a lesson—and Magistrate Hallowell reading the charges in his dry, unhurried voice, as though he were reading a list of debts. Which, Ansel understood now, in a way they hadn't then, he was. Unlicensed craft. Unregistered testimony. The crime, stripped of its language, was that Maren had been making true statements about the world without paying the court for permission.

She hadn't screamed. Ansel remembered that most clearly, the thing they built their nights around not remembering. She had looked out over the crowd once, found no one—found Ansel, who had looked down at their shoes—and then she had closed her eyes and gone quietly, the way a candle goes when pinched.

Three days later, Tam Vetch had appeared at Ansel's window with a mule, a sack of bread, and no explanation beyond *"You're coming with me, wren, unless you fancy being next."*

Tam Vetch. Poacher, smuggler, occasional fence for goods whose provenance was best not asked after. He'd known Maren—had bought her poultices for years with money he claimed came from honest work, and neither of them had believed him and both of them had pretended to. He'd smuggled Ansel out of the village in a cart under sacking, past the first checkpoint, past the second, bribing where bribery worked and running where it didn't, three days and four nights to the city, and at the guildhouse door he'd pressed a coin into Ansel's hand and said, "Don't send word. Don't look back. Live small and live long, and that'll be thanks enough."

And Ansel had lived small. Had changed their name twice before settling on Fairweather, had buried Maren's lessons so deep they sometimes wondered if they'd invented them, had become invisible—a clerk, a cipher, a person the world looked through. They had repaid Tam Vetch exactly nothing, because Tam Vetch had asked for nothing, and because looking back meant looking at the fire.

Now the ledger wanted a soul, payable beneath, in Ansel's own handwriting, and Ansel was going to find out whose.

* * *

The Gilded Scale's archive occupied the cellar levels, below even the vaults, because the guild's deepest principle was that some things should be hard to reach. The archivist, a desiccated man named Pell who had worked there so long his skin had taken on the color of vellum, required no bribe and no persuasion—he required only that Ansel sign the access book, which Ansel did, watching their own name appear in their own hand and feeling, for the first time, uncertain of it.

Cross-referencing an entry was simple procedure. Every contract in a guild ledger carried a client code, a broker's mark, and a collateral notation. The soul-entry had all three. The client code resolved to a holding account—anonymous, standard practice, nothing to see there. The broker's mark was Corvane's own, which Ansel had expected and hated expecting. But the collateral notation was a name.

Guild practice was to record collateral as property: *one hearth, encumbered. One trade, pledged.* Souls were recorded differently. Souls were recorded as persons, because a soul was the only collateral that could walk into the office and ask about itself.

*Tamuel Vetch. Of Fenlow's Crossing.*

Ansel read it four times. The letters didn't rearrange. The cellar air stayed cold and smelled of dust and old ink, and somewhere above, the guildhouse went about its morning as though the stars hadn't gone out, as though the wards hadn't fallen, as though the world above were still the world.

Fenlow's Crossing. Ansel's village. Tam Vetch, who had come to the window with a mule and a sack of bread. Tam Vetch, who had asked for nothing.

Ansel sat down on the archive steps because their legs had opinions of their own, and put their head in their hands, and did the arithmetic they had been avoiding for nine years.

What did Ansel owe? Everything. There was no honest ledger anywhere that balanced otherwise. A life bought with a mule and bread and three days of danger. A future purchased by a man who couldn't write his own name but had understood, better than Ansel ever had, that the magistrate's fire would want feeding again. And what had Ansel paid? Nothing. Worse than nothing—Ansel had done what Tam asked. Lived small. Never sent word. Never looked back. Had let the debt sit unentered in any book, unacknowledged, accruing interest in the only currency that mattered, which was time, and time had run out.

Because the entry wasn't a sale pending. Ansel knew the codes now, had spent six years learning them. The entry was a collection notice. The soul was already owed. Payable beneath—beneath the mountain, beneath the city, beneath everything—by solstice.

Nine days.

Ansel walked back up through the guildhouse in a daze, past clerks balancing bottled thunder and borrowed years, past the great brass scales in the atrium that weighed nothing and impressed everyone, and stopped at the message desk on impulse, and asked—casually, a bookkeeper's idle question—whether a Tamuel Vetch of Fenlow's Crossing had any business on file.

The desk clerk checked. Looked up. Frowned.

"He does, actually. Odd. Appointment request came in last night, after the wards went." The clerk slid a slip across the counter. "Tomorrow, midday. Says he wants to speak to whoever handles his account."

Ansel took the slip. Their hand, their real hand, was steady. Inside them, nothing was.

He was coming here. Half the city's stars dark, the wards down, the mountain quiet in a way mountains shouldn't be—and Tam Vetch was walking toward the Gilded Scale to ask about a debt he couldn't possibly know the shape of.

Ansel climbed to their office, locked the door, and pulled the ledger toward them one more time. *One soul, payable beneath,* in their own handwriting, and beneath it, in smaller script they could have sworn hadn't been there yesterday, a single line of marginalia:

*He saved you. Now save him. — M.*

Ansel stared at it until the ink seemed to move, and downstairs, faint through the floorboards, the appointment bell rang early—and a voice they hadn't heard in nine years, roughened and wrong somehow, asked the desk clerk:

"Is there a clerk here called Fairweather?"
