## 4. The Client Who Wasn't There

Tam Vetch had been dying for three days and it felt mostly like being late.

That was the only way he could describe it. Everything arrived a half-step after it should—the sound of his own boots on the cobbles, the cold of the morning air, the thought that told his legs to keep moving. He'd notice a thing happen and then, a beat later, feel it. Like watching himself through a window with bad glass.

His shadow was the worst of it.

He'd first seen the lag in a puddle outside Marrowgate, dawn light stretching him long across the water, and his shadow had still been bent double from a step he'd taken two strides ago. He'd stood in the road like an idiot, lifting his arm up and down, watching the dark copy of him catch up each time, slow as syrup off a spoon. A boy selling eels had watched him do it and asked if he was drunk. Tam had said yes, because the truth was worse.

He knew what had been done to him. He just didn't know who'd done it, or how to make them take it back.

The paper in his pocket said *the Gilded Scale*. It had appeared under his door in Hollowfen with no seal and no signature, just those words in ink so black it looked wet even when dry: *The Gilded Scale guildhouse, Copperlane, ask for the night bookkeeper.* He'd ridden two days on a horse he'd traded a winter's worth of snares for, and the whole ride the shadow on the road behind him had run a half-length back, like something following at a polite distance.

Half-hollowed. That was the word the hedge-wife in Marrowgate had used, spitting salt over her threshold after she'd looked at him too long. *You've been half-sold, poacher. Half of you is already paid for.* She wouldn't say more. Nobody would say more. People saw the way his shadow moved and found reasons to be indoors.

But he wasn't dead yet. Whatever they'd bought, they hadn't collected all of it, and while there was breath in him he intended to find out whose hand had signed him away.

The Gilded Scale rose out of Copperlane like a fat gold coin someone had dropped and decided not to pick up—three stories of gilded brick, windows glowing amber against the grey morning, a sign above the door showing a balance scale with a sun on one pan and a moon on the other. Guild colors. Trade colors. Money colors. Tam had spent his whole life avoiding places like this; men like him didn't walk through front doors, they went under fences and through hedges and over walls at night.

He walked through the front door.

Inside smelled of ink and tallow and old paper, the particular dry smell of a place where everything important had been written down by somebody else. A clerk behind a counter looked up, took one glance at Tam's mud-caked boots and unshaved face, and began arranging his features into a refusal.

"I'm here about a debt," Tam said.

The clerk's face changed. Not softened—*changed*, the way a lock changes when the right key turns. "Name?"

"Vetch. Tam Vetch."

"Wait here."

The clerk vanished through a door. Tam waited, and his shadow waited, and its waiting arrived a beat after his did, so that for one strange moment they were both standing perfectly still but not at the same time, and Tam had to close his eyes against it.

When he opened them, a young person was coming down the stairs—slight, brown-haired, dressed in guild grey with an ink-stain on their cuff, carrying a ledger under one arm like a shield. They stopped three steps from the bottom and stared at him.

And here was the thing Tam would think about later, turning it over in the dark like a stone with a warm side: the look on that clerk's face wasn't the look of a stranger seeing a stranger. It was the look of someone seeing a ghost they'd been praying wouldn't haunt them.

"You're the night bookkeeper?" Tam said.

"Yes," they said. Their voice came out thin. "Ansel Fairweather. You wanted—who sold you? Is that what you—"

"Someone sold me." Tam pulled the crumpled paper from his pocket, though he didn't know why; it said nothing useful. "Three nights ago I woke up and half of me was gone. My shadow's late. My own heartbeat arrives late. The hedge-wife says I'm half-paid-for, and I don't know to whom, and I don't know for what, and every soul in Marrowgate suddenly can't look at me." His voice cracked on the last part and he hated it. "I'm a poacher, mistress. Nobody spends good money on a poacher. Somebody made a mistake, or somebody meant it, and either way I want a name."

The bookkeeper—Ansel—came down the last steps slowly. Up close their eyes were odd, a color Tam couldn't fix, the kind of eyes you forgot the moment you looked away. They were looking at him like he was a letter they'd written to themselves years ago and only now dared open.

"Do I know you?" Tam asked. Because the look demanded it. "Have we met?"

"No," Ansel said. Too fast. Too flat. "No. We haven't met."

They held out their hand for the paper. Tam gave it. Their fingers brushed his, and Tam's shadow, arriving late as ever, finally caught up—and in that half-second of overlap Tam felt something lurch in his chest, some deep animal part of him straining toward this stranger like a dog toward a whistle only it could hear.

"You'll help me," Tam said. It wasn't quite a question.

Ansel looked down at the blank paper in their hands, and then at Tam, and their face did something complicated and terrible—grief and guilt and fear all trying to fit through the same doorway at once.

"Yes," they said. "I'll find who sold you. I swear it."

* * *

Ansel watched Tam Vetch walk out of the guildhouse into the grey morning, and watched his shadow follow him the way grief follows a funeral—present, but not participating.

Then they sat down on the bottom stair and put their head in their hands and shook, quietly, the way they'd taught themselves to shake years ago: silently, briefly, without letting it become anything anyone could use.

*Tam.*

Tam Vetch, who had carried a nine-year-old child through three miles of frozen marsh on his back while the village burned behind them. Tam, who had bribed a toll-keeper with a brace of hares and lied to a warden with a straight face and gotten Ansel onto the mail coach to Corvane City with six coppers sewn into their coat hem. Tam, who had crouched down in the snow, put his big cracked hands on either side of Ansel's face, and said, *Don't remember me, little owl. Remembering's how they find you. Forget the village, forget Maren, forget me. Live small and live long.*

So Ansel had forgotten. Mostly. The forgetting had never been total—it was more like a wall with cracks in it, and through the cracks came smells and shapes: woodsmoke and wet feathers, a laugh like a door hinge, hands steadying a child's face in the cold. Ansel had spent eleven years not asking what was behind the wall, because asking felt like pulling a thread that might unravel the whole disguise, the whole safe gray life, the whole invisible self stitched together out of arithmetic and ledgers and nobody-looking-twice.

And now Tam Vetch had walked into the Gilded Scale and looked straight at Ansel and seen nothing. No flicker. No recognition. Nothing behind his eyes but a stranger's desperate hope.

*Forget me,* Tam had said. And Ansel had obeyed so well that the debt between them had become invisible in both directions.

Except the ledger hadn't forgotten. The ledger never forgot. *One soul, payable beneath,* in handwriting identical to Ansel's own, and now here was the soul itself, walking and talking and half-hollowed, asking Ansel to find the hand that sold him.

Because Ansel knew. That was the rot at the center of it. Last night, cross-referencing the entry against the guild's client rolls, Ansel had traced the purchase order through three shell accounts to its origin, and the name at the top of the chain had been Magistrate Hallowell. Hallowell, who had burned Maren Solane in the village square. Hallowell, whose ward-stone purchases had failed the border the same night the stars went out. And now Hallowell's money had bought the man who saved Ansel's life.

It was too neat. It was a knot pulled tight around Ansel specifically, and the more they looked at it the more it looked less like coincidence and more like design—as if someone had reached across eleven years and arranged these pieces deliberately, knowing exactly which threads would lead Ansel where.

Ansel climbed back upstairs to the counting room. The evening shift wouldn't arrive for hours; the room belonged to the dust motes and the smell of lamp oil. They unlocked the drawer where they kept the soul-entry page and laid it flat under the lamp.

*One soul, payable beneath.*

Their own handwriting. Not similar—identical, down to the way the descender of the *y* curled left instead of right, a quirk Ansel had never been able to train out of themselves. Which was impossible. Unless it wasn't handwriting at all, but something wearing handwriting like a mask. Or unless—

Unless somewhere in the past Ansel couldn't see, there was a version of themself that had written this. That had known. That had left it here the way you leave a note under a door for someone who isn't home yet.

"Don't," Ansel said aloud, to themself, to the page. "Don't go down that road tonight."

But the road was the only road. Tam had maybe weeks—the hedge-wives' arithmetic on half-hollowed men was grimly consistent; the hollow part spread like frost across glass, and by solstice there would be nothing left to save. To find out how to unmake the sale, Ansel needed the full contract, and the full contract lived in the deep archive, in the sealed folios, in the section of the guild's records that required Guildmaster Corvane's key.

Corvane. Who had ordered silence about the fallen wards. Whose quiet, careful guild had somehow brokered a soul-sale without anyone noticing—or without anyone being allowed to notice.

Ansel blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the building settle, feeling the shape of the next few days assemble itself like figures in a column: get into the archive, read the contract, find the loophole. Simple. Bookkeeping. Balance the impossible entry.

Underneath the arithmetic, quieter, the other thing turned over and over: *he didn't know me. He looked at me and there was nothing.*

Eleven years ago Tam Vetch had told a frightened child to forget him, and the child had done it, and now the forgetting had come all the way around like a shadow running late, and it had caught up to both of them at once.

Ansel picked up the soul-entry page, folded it small, and slipped it inside their shirt, against their skin, where the guild's rules and the magistrate's reach would both have trouble getting at it.

Tomorrow night, the archive. Tonight, one more thing: they took a fresh sheet and wrote, in their own careful hand, everything they could remember about the burning—Maren's name, the square, the smoke, the sound the crowd made. Facts. Testimony. Ink that remembered what flesh had been made to forget.

If the past was going to collect its debts, Ansel decided, it could start by paying interest.

Somewhere below, the front bell chimed—a late client, a clerk's muffled voice. Ansel didn't move. But when the chime faded, they noticed their own shadow on the wall by the window had shifted, just slightly, ahead of their hand rather than behind it.

As if it already knew where they were going, and was impatient.
