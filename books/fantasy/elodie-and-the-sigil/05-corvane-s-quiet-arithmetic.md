## 5. Corvane's Quiet Arithmetic

The Gilded Scale kept its master ledger in a vault beneath the counting floor, behind three locks and a ward that smelled like burnt sugar. Guildmaster Corvane opened all four every morning at the sixth bell, and he did it alone, and he did not like company.

Ansel had been waiting in his antechamber since the fifth bell, sitting very still on a bench built for petitioners, hands folded, face arranged into the blank competence of a junior bookkeeper with nothing on their mind. It was a performance Ansel had rehearsed for six years. Being invisible was mostly craft.

Corvane came down at half past, keys chiming against his hip, and stopped when he saw them. He was a narrow man, dry as an audit, with spectacles he wore low so he could look over them rather than through you. He looked over them now.

"You're early."

"I'm thorough," Ansel said. "Guildmaster. There's an entry I can't balance. I've tried for two days. It's beyond my authority to correct, and I judged it beyond my authority to ignore."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—quick, controlled, gone. He unlocked his office door. "Come in, then. And close it."

* * *

The office was small and immaculate: one desk, one chair for him, one stool for visitors, a window of thick green glass that turned daylight the color of old coins. On the desk sat a scale made of black iron, its pans empty, its needle perfectly still. Ansel had never once seen it used. They suspected it wasn't for weighing anything that could be put on a pan.

"Show me," Corvane said.

Ansel set the copied page on his blotter—the entry from the night ledger, *one soul, payable beneath*, in handwriting identical to their own—and stood back with their heart going like a dropped coin spinning on marble.

Corvane read it. He read it the way he read everything, top to bottom without moving his lips, and then he read it again, and this time his finger touched the margin note beside it: *brokered in good faith, fee waived.*

"Where did you get this?"

"The night ledger. It surfaced during the solstice-quarter reconciliation." Ansel kept their voice level. "I copied it before I reported it, because—" they hesitated, chose honesty shaped like caution— "because I wanted to understand it first. A soul entry is outside guild charter. Souls aren't collateral. The charter's explicit."

"The charter is explicit about many things," Corvane said. He folded the copy once, precisely, and set it under the black scale's near pan. Nothing happened. He seemed disappointed by that, or relieved. "Sit down, Fairweather."

Ansel sat. Nobody used their surname. Nobody knew it mattered to them either way—that was the whole architecture of their life, nameless as a clerk and twice as safe.

"How much do you know about the founding of the border wards?" Corvane asked.

"That the guild maintains them. That maintenance fees are assessed against member houses. That they failed last week, and that the guild has issued no statement, which is itself a statement."

"Mm." Corvane took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which was what he did instead of sighing. "You balance books. So let me give you figures. Forty years ago, the wards were failing everywhere—not just here, along the whole spine of the mountains. Things coming up from underneath. Villages losing people the way a purse loses coins: one at a time, quietly, until someone shook it and found it empty. The crown wouldn't fund a fix. The temples called it punishment. So a group of merchants—the guild's founders, though we weren't called a guild yet—went looking for someone who could actually pay for a wall that tall."

"And found someone."

"Beneath the mountain there is a court. Old law. Creditor's law. They don't take coin—they have no use for it—but they honor contracts absolutely. Better than any court above ground ever has. The founders brokered an agreement. The under-court funded the wards. In exchange, payment terms." He put the spectacles back on. "You're a bookkeeper. What does a debt need to stay alive?"

"Interest," Ansel said. "And a debtor who acknowledges it."

"Precisely. The city acknowledges it every time a ward-stone hums. Every safe night is a payment received. People think the wards keep the dark out. They don't. They keep us *current*." He spread his hands. "That is the arithmetic this guild was built on. Not thunder in bottles. Not borrowed years. Balance. Somebody has to hold the account, or everything falls."

"And the soul entry," Ansel said. "Is that interest?"

Corvane didn't answer immediately. Through the green glass, the light shifted; somewhere above, the counting floor was filling with clerks who would never know what their ledgers fed. When he finally spoke, his voice was quieter, stripped of the lecture.

"Yes. One soul, per term. Chosen by lot among—among eligible accounts. The under-court names criteria; we identify candidates; a broker executes. It has happened eleven times in forty years. You will find no record of the other ten, because those entries live in a ledger you haven't clearance to open, and because the families were compensated, and the city stayed standing, and nobody asked."

*Nobody asked.* Ansel thought of Tam Vetch's shadow lagging half a step behind him, of the hollow look, the way he'd begged a stranger for help. "Who was this one? The eleventh?"

"You know," Corvane said. "Or you suspect strongly enough that you'd call me a liar if I dodged. Tam Vetch. Poacher, out of Millbrook way. No family to object. No guild ties. Eligible by every criterion the court set." He met Ansel's eyes over the spectacles. "And yes. Before you ask. The hand on the entry is yours. Or close enough that the difference doesn't matter. That's how the broker-work functions—it borrows the nearest competent signature. It doesn't mean you wrote anything. It means the ledger needed a hand it trusted, and it reached into this building and found yours."

The room tilted, slightly. Ansel gripped the stool's edge. *It reached into this building.* Which meant the ledger knew them. Had known them longer, maybe, than they'd been a clerk at all.

"Who sold him?" Ansel asked. "Not the court. The court only buys. Someone brought him to market."

Corvane's jaw worked. For a moment Ansel thought he'd refuse entirely. Then he said, "Magistrate Hallowell holds the warrant of eligibility. His office certifies candidates. His office certified Vetch."

"Certified him for what crime?"

"There isn't one. That's not how eligibility works." Corvane's voice had gone dry again, but the dryness now sounded like a man walking on ice and listening to it. "Hallowell came to us after the wards failed. He said the court had named its criteria and Vetch fit, and that if we declined to execute, the default clause would trigger. Do you know what the default clause is, Fairweather?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the point. It's written in the founding contract, in language our own lawyers couldn't parse, and the one founder who might have explained it went under the mountain forty years ago and didn't come back. Hallowell told us: execute the sale, or find out what default means with the wards down and the stars going out." He laced his fingers together on the blotter. "So we executed. Fee waived, as noted, because the guild refuses to profit. Small comfort. But it's the only line we drew."

Ansel sat with it. The story was coherent. It was even, in its cold way, sympathetic—a guild cornered between a magistrate and a mountain, choosing the smaller evil with a bookkeeper's precision. It explained the silence, the sealed records, Corvane's tired eyes.

It did not explain why Hallowell, of all people, held the warrant. It did not explain why the failed ward-stones had all been purchased through the magistrate's account. It did not explain why the ledger had reached for *Ansel's* hand.

But it was a story, and stories were how debts got dressed.

"You believe him," Ansel said. "About the coercion."

"I believe Hallowell is capable of exactly what he described," Corvane said carefully. "I also believe that if I accuse a magistrate of coercing the Gilded Scale, the guild dies inside a season—seized, chartered away, scattered. And the wards fall with us. Whatever else is true, that arithmetic holds."

"And Tam Vetch?"

"Will be collected at solstice. Twenty-two days." Corvane said it flatly, a figure, not a fate. "Unless the contract is satisfied some other way, which our brokers assure me it cannot be. I'm sorry. If it helps—I am sorry. But I have four hundred members and a hundred thousand souls behind these walls, and I will not spend them on one poacher."

"One poacher," Ansel repeated.

"One man whose death buys a hundred thousand lives. Yes. I'd have thought a bookkeeper would respect the math." Corvane leaned forward. "Which brings me to why you're really here, and what happens next. You've seen an entry that doesn't exist. If you speak of it—if you breathe a word of souls or courts or solstice to anyone inside or outside these walls—you will be dismissed, your references burned, and the guild's protection withdrawn. Do you understand what that means for someone like you?"

Ansel understood. Someone like them—papers thin as onionskin, history a locked box, sanctuary the only thing between themselves and whatever they'd run from. Six years of invisible safety, contingent on silence.

"I understand the terms," Ansel said.

"Good." Corvane relaxed by a degree. "Then log the anomaly as a transcription error, initial the correction, and go back to work. That's an order, Fairweather. Not a threat. An order from a man doing the best he can with bad numbers."

Ansel stood. At the door they paused, hand on the frame, and heard themself say: "For the record, Guildmaster. Bad numbers don't stop being wrong because you balance them."

Corvane looked up sharply. Then, slowly, he nodded once—an acknowledgment, almost respectful. "No," he said. "They don't. Get out."

* * *

Ansel walked the long way back to the counting floor, through the gallery where the guild's honors hung: charters, commendations, the painted portraits of eleven founders, ten of whom had died comfortably in beds. The eleventh portrait was curtained. It always had been. Ansel had assumed moth damage.

Now they stopped beneath it, in the drafty quiet, and looked at the drape, and thought: *he went under the mountain, and he didn't come back, and you curtained him like a stain on the wallpaper.*

Twenty-two days. Somewhere in the city, a man with a lagging shadow was drinking himself brave in a rooming house, telling strangers he felt fine, feeling less fine every hour. He had carried Ansel out of a burning village on his back, over the ford, through the bracken, humming a song off-key to keep them calm, and he didn't remember doing it. Didn't remember them. And the guild that sheltered Ansel had sold him anyway, with the fee waived, as a kindness.

Loyalty or truth. Sanctuary or a stranger's soul. Corvane had laid it out like columns in a ledger, neat and final, and expected Ansel to initial the correction.

Ansel stood under the curtained portrait a moment longer. Then, glancing once down the empty gallery, they slipped the drape aside.

The eleventh founder stared out of the paint—a woman, sharp-faced, inkstained fingers holding a quill like a knife, her collar pinned with a small silver sigil: a circle broken by a single vertical stroke. Ansel's chest went strange and tight. They knew that sigil. They knew it the way you know a smell from childhood—before knowing, below knowing. Maren Solane had worn one just like it on a cord around her neck, and it had swung free of her collar the day they arrested her, catching the firelight, and Ansel had looked at it while the world ended.

Below the portrait, a brass plaque read: **SOLANE, FIRST LEDGER-KEEPER OF THE ACCORD.**

Ansel read the name three times. Their teacher's name—or near enough to make the air ring. The woman who had taught them letters and hedge-craft and how to hide, burned as an unlicensed witch while the guild she'd helped build watched from behind its walls.

The bell rang for the second hour. Down the corridor, clerks' pens scratched on, balancing thunder and years and stars, keeping everyone current.

Ansel let the curtain fall, and walked back to their desk with their blood loud in their ears, and began, very carefully, to plan a theft.
