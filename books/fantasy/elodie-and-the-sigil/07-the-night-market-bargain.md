## 7. The Night Market Bargain

The night market opened where the river forgot to be a river.

That was how Sefa described it, and she was right in the way that mattered: the water simply stopped, mid-channel, and the dry bed filled instead with lanterns that burned without flame—cold white lights strung on wires of shadow—and stalls that had no right to stand upright on silt. Ansel had heard of the night market the way you hear about a fever. You know it exists. You hope it never learns your name.

Tam leaned against a pilaster of the drowned bridge, breathing in the shallow way he'd learned this week. His shadow stood a full pace behind him now, patient as a creditor.

"You don't have to come," Ansel said.

"Never asked you to save me." Tam's voice was gravel poured slow. "Saying it again so it's on the record."

"It's on the record."

They went down into the riverbed together, and the lanterns turned toward them like flowers that had given up on the sun.

* * *

Quill found them before they found Quill—which was, Sefa had warned, the only way anyone found Quill. The broker was small and neat and sexless in the way of well-kept ledgers, wearing a coat with too many pockets and a smile calibrated to the half-inch.

"The bookkeeper," Quill said. "I've been expecting someone who balances columns for a living. You people make the best customers. You understand that everything costs."

"I want passage toward the mountain," Ansel said. "Under the wards. Under the patrols. All the way to the pilgrim road."

"Mm." Quill's eyes did arithmetic. "And the poacher comes too, or does he wait here to finish hollowing?"

"He comes."

"That doubles the price. Hollowed men are heavy cargo. They drag." Quill plucked a lantern from the air and hung it on nothing. "Let's talk payment. Coin is insulting. Years are traditional. Memories are preferred—they keep better, less spoilage, no complaints about vintage. What have you got?"

Ansel had prepared for this. Had spent two days cataloguing their own head the way they'd catalogue a guild storeroom, shelf by shelf, deciding what could be spared. Maren's face—that was load-bearing; they'd already lost her once to fire and once to their own contract, whatever the contract had taken, and they would not lose her again. Their mother's hands kneading dough. The smell of the village after rain. Those were structural too.

What remained was smaller stuff. And there was one category that kept surfacing no matter how Ansel pushed it down: birthdays.

Nine of them, remembered whole. The seventh, when Tam himself had left a snared rabbit on the doorstep and pretended he hadn't. The eleventh, when Maren had baked a cake with sigils piped in honey that made everyone at the table tell the truth for an hour. The fifteenth, the last one before the burning, celebrated early because Maren said you should never let a debt of joy sit overnight.

Small things. Small, awful, load-bearing-in-a-way-Ansel-hadn't-admitted things.

"Three years of birthdays," Ansel said. "Remembered ones. Any three I choose."

Quill's smile widened by its calibrated half-inch. "You've done inventory. I respect that. Three birthdays, buyer's choice of which three? Or seller's?"

"Seller's."

"Then we're nearly honest with each other, which is more than most deals get." Quill produced a scale from one impossible pocket—brass, old, its pans worn silver-bright. "Passage for two, to the mouth of the sunken road, safe-conduct past three checkpoints. Against: three years of remembered birthdays, seller's selection, delivered now." A pause. "And a truth-toll besides. Everyone who trades at this market pays one true thing they'd rather not say. That one's free. It's my favorite part."

Ansel looked at Tam. Tam was watching the lanterns with the flat attention of a man whose shadow had started making decisions without him.

"Ask me," Ansel said.

"What did the poacher do that makes you willing to pay this?"

Ansel had expected something harder. This one came up easy, which meant it had been waiting near the surface a long time.

"When I was eleven years old," Ansel said, "the magistrate's men came for my teacher, and every adult in the village suddenly discovered business elsewhere. Every adult but one. Tam Vetch carried me out through the fen in a fish barrel with holes drilled for air, and when I cried he told me the holes were so I could see the stars. He lied beautifully. He lied for six hours. He gave me a new name at the border and taught me to write it until my hand stopped shaking." Ansel swallowed. "He doesn't remember any of that now. Something under the mountain is eating it out of him, entry by entry. So no—I'm not paying for passage. I'm paying back a debt, and I'm late."

The market had gone quiet around them. Lanterns leaned in. Somewhere in the dark, something sighed with what sounded horribly like satisfaction.

Quill set the scale on the silt. "Truth received. Now pay."

Ansel closed their eyes and chose. Not the seventh—not the rabbit, not yet, that one could be the last coin if it ever came to coins again. Not the fifteenth. The eleventh, though—the honey-cake, the hour of enforced honesty, Maren laughing with flour on her nose. Gone. Ansel reached for it and found a smooth blank place, shaped exactly like a birthday, and knew only that something had been there because the shape was still warm.

The second: the nineteenth, spent alone in the guildhouse cellars, counting stock by candlelight, eating a stolen pear and telling themselves invisibility was a kind of freedom. Gone. And with it went the person who'd believed that, and Ansel felt the belief go too, like a tooth coming out whole.

The third: the twenty-second. There had been someone. There had been a name—no, the name went with it, that was the cruelty of the currency, the name was part of the year—and a morning-after and a promise neither party had meant to keep. Gone. Ansel stood in the riverbed holding three warm absences and could not remember what they'd paid for the privilege of forgetting.

"Paid in full," Quill said softly, and something in the broker's voice was almost gentle. "It always looks smaller from the outside. It never is."

* * *

They traveled that night in a cart of turnips, which Tam found funny in a distant, flickering way—he laughed twice and then forgot why he was laughing, and the laugh died in his face like a lamp pinched out.

By the second checkpoint his shadow walked four paces behind. By the third, he'd begun introducing himself to strangers with names that weren't his, and each false name fit him a little better than the last.

At the mouth of the sunken road—a stairway going down into older dark, guarded by nothing but a toll-gate and a smell of cold stone—Tam caught Ansel's wrist. His grip was still strong. That was the worst part, how strong it still was, while everything else drained.

"Listen," he said. "Whatever you're doing. Whatever this is." He gestured vaguely at the road, the dark, Ansel's face. "I never asked to be saved. You hear me? If it costs you—if it costs anyone—then put me down and walk away. I've been cargo before. Fish barrel, once. Holes drilled in it so I could see the stars." He smiled, and for one second the smile was entirely, unmistakably his. "Best lie anybody ever told me."

Ansel's throat closed. The seventh birthday sat in their chest like a coal, the last one, the one with the rabbit on the doorstep and Tam's boots walking away whistling, pretending he hadn't.

"I know about the barrel," Ansel managed.

"Do you." Tam's eyes were going somewhere else already, some room inside him being emptied and swept. "Funny. Feels like someone should've told me that story. Feels like I almost—" He frowned, pressed his palm flat against his own sternum as if checking whether anything was still knocking. "Doesn't matter. Doesn't matter. Just remember what I said. Nobody asked you."

"Nobody asked me," Ansel agreed, and helped him down the first stair.

Behind them, at the top of the steps, the toll-gate swung shut on its own, and in the new silence Ansel heard—faint, unmistakable, impossibly far above—the sound of a ledger page turning.

Someone below knew they were coming.
