## 8. Hallowell's Open Door

The magistrate's estate had no guards worth the name.

Ansel learned this in the worst way possible: by spending three days watching it and finding nothing to watch. No ward-stones on the gateposts—those had all gone dark with the stars anyway. No patrol on the wall walk except a single bored man who walked it once at dusk and once at dawn and spent the hours between drinking something amber from a clay flask. The gardeners were deaf, or pretended to be. The dogs were old and more interested in the kitchen scraps than in strangers.

It was wrong. Ansel knew it was wrong the way a bookkeeper knows a column that sums too neatly. Estates like this one—Hallowell's estate, seat of the man who had burned Maren Solane in the market square of Harrow's Ford—were supposed to be fortresses of paranoia. Magistrates who trafficked with the court beneath the mountain did not leave their doors open.

Unless they wanted someone to walk through them.

Ansel went in anyway. That was the thing about being invisible for eleven years: you got very good at telling yourself that no one could possibly be waiting for *you*.

They went over the wall at the hour when even honest people are ashamed of what they're thinking, dressed in the gray-brown nothing-colors of a guild courier, carrying nothing but a hooded lantern turned down to a coal-glow and a copy of the soul-entry folded against their ribs. The gravel path took them past a fountain that had been shut off for the season, its stone nymph holding an empty urn, and up to the house itself—a long, low building of pale stone, its windows lit warm and gold against the starless dark.

Every window lit. At midnight. In a city where every star had gone out and the guildmaster had ordered silence.

Ansel stood in the shadow of a topiary bear and let the fear finish arriving. It had been traveling toward this moment since the ledger entry appeared in their own handwriting; now it settled into their chest like a stone dropped into a well, and they heard it land.

Then they moved, because standing still was how invisible people got caught.

The side door—the servants' door, the one every great house pretends it doesn't have—was unlocked. Not unlatched. Unlocked, the bolt drawn back, the iron cold under Ansel's fingers when they tested it.

Inside smelled of beeswax and old paper. That was the first surprise. Ansel had expected the smell of Hallowell's kind: hot metal, scorched hair, the ghost of a burning. Instead the corridor smelled like a library, like Maren's cottage had smelled, and the recognition hurt worse than any smell of smoke would have.

A single lamp burned at the end of the hall, beside a door left open exactly the width of an invitation.

Ansel's hands were steady. They noticed that about themselves with a strange detachment—their hands were always steady now, had been since the night market, since three birthdays went out of them like coins from a torn pocket. Fear lived somewhere else these days, deeper down, where it couldn't reach the fingers.

They walked the length of the hall. Their boots made no sound; they'd wrapped them in wool strips in the alley behind a chandler's shop, and the habit of silence sat so deep in them that they sometimes forgot they were doing it.

The room beyond the door was a study. A real one—desk, chairs, shelves of ledgers bound in sober leather, a cold fireplace swept clean. And standing at the window with his back to the door, hands clasped behind him like a man admiring a garden he couldn't see in the dark, was Magistrate Hallowell.

He was smaller than Ansel remembered. Eleven years will do that—not to him, but to the memory. In Ansel's mind he had been a tower, a bonfire, a voice reading charges in a square full of people too frightened to breathe. In person he was a narrow man in a plain dark coat, silvering at the temples, with the stooped shoulders of someone who spent his life bent over documents.

"I counted on you coming tonight," he said, without turning. "Tomorrow at the earliest, tonight at the likeliest. You're punctual. That's the guild training, I suppose."

Ansel said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill too.

"The wards failed because I bought them," Hallowell went on, conversational, still facing the window. "You've worked that out—I can see it in the way you're standing, weight on your back foot, ready to run. Yes. Every ward-stone around this city passed through my account. I didn't break them. I merely made certain that when they broke, no one would be able to trace who had profited from the purchase." He turned then, and his face was mild, tired, almost kind. "You came for the entry. One soul, payable beneath. Tam Vetch."

Hearing the name in that mouth did something to Ansel that three years of birthdays hadn't done. Their vision went white at the edges for half a heartbeat.

"You know his name," Ansel said. Their voice came out flat and quiet, which surprised them; they'd expected it to shake.

"I know everyone's name. That's most of what a magistrate is." Hallowell crossed to the desk and sat behind it, folding his hands, and gestured to the chair opposite with the courtesy of a host. "Sit. Or stand by the door if you prefer. I imagine you prefer the door."

Ansel stayed by the door.

"You want to know who sold him," Hallowell said. "You've been pulling that thread through the guild's books for two weeks—you're better at it than Corvane thinks, which is saying something, because Corvane is terrified of you and hasn't admitted it even to himself. And you've found the sale but not the seller, because the seller isn't in the guild's ledger at all." He smiled faintly. "The guild brokered the trade. I purchased the debt. But neither of us sold Tam Vetch's soul, clerk. He sold it himself. Sixteen years ago, to buy a boy out of a village that was about to burn."

The words went into Ansel like a blade finding a gap in armor.

Tam had never said. All those years ago, on the road out of Harrow's Ford, with the firelight still orange behind them and Maren already taken—Tam had laughed and said the magistrate owed him a favor, said don't worry about the how, just keep moving, keep breathing. Ansel had been fourteen and hollowed out with grief and had believed him, because believing him was easier than asking what a poacher's favor from a burning magistrate could possibly cost.

"He didn't know what he was signing," Ansel said.

"No one ever does. That's rather the point of contracts." Hallowell opened a drawer—not a dramatic gesture, not a flourish, just a man retrieving a document—and set a book on the desk between them.

Ansel knew it before they saw the cover. Knew it by the shape of it, the weight of it, the way the lamplight seemed to sit differently on its leather. A grimoire, hand-bound, its spine cracked from use, a burn scar across the lower corner where an ember had once leapt from the hearth and Maren had swatted it out with her bare palm, laughing, saying *books forgive, Elodie, that's why I love them.*

Maren's grimoire. Confiscated the day she burned. Eleven years in a magistrate's evidence locker, and here it was, sitting on a desk like a hostage.

Ansel's whole body leaned toward it before their mind gave permission. They caught themselves. Made themselves straighten. Made themselves look at Hallowell instead, and found him watching with the patient satisfaction of a fisherman who has felt the first pull on the line.

"You recognize it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"It's stolen property," Ansel said. "Evidence in a murder."

"A lawful execution," Hallowell corrected mildly. "Unlicensed craft. She taught outside guild charter, outside crown sanction. Whatever else you believe about me, clerk, believe that I have never once broken the law. I have only ever *used* it." He rested one hand flat on the grimoire's cover, and Ansel hated how much the small possessive gesture hurt. "Her methods are in here. Her theory of testimony-magic—the idea that every spell is a true statement about the world, and that the world must honor true statements spoken correctly. Dangerous stuff. Subversive. It says, in essence, that truth obligates power. You can see why the crown burns people for it."

"Then burn me," Ansel said. "Or whatever you do to apprentices these days. But give me the book first."

Something flickered across Hallowell's face—surprise, quickly mastered, and beneath it something that might almost have been respect. "There it is. Solane's temper. I wondered if it survived the hiding." He leaned back in his chair. "No. I'm not going to burn you. I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to hate how reasonable it sounds, and then you're going to take it or you aren't, and either way we'll both sleep tonight. I find I value sleeping."

"Say it, then."

"Bring me the guild's master ledger. The original, not a copy—the one Corvane keeps warded in the vault beneath the Gilded Scale, the one that records every impossible trade the guild has brokered for thirty years. Bottled thunder. Borrowed years. Souls." His voice didn't rise at all. "In exchange, I return your name."

The word landed in the room and rang there.

*Your name.* Not a metaphor. Ansel felt it in their teeth, the way you feel a tuning fork. Somewhere in the world's great accounting there was a name that belonged to them—Elodie, Maren's voice saying it, *Elodie, come look at this*—and eleven years ago they had buried it so deep that even they couldn't dig it up. They had walked into the Gilded Scale at fifteen with no history and no papers and a talent for arithmetic, and the guild had shrugged and hired them, because the guild asked no questions it didn't want answers to. Invisible. Safe. Nameless.

And Hallowell sat there holding the name like a coin he'd found in the street.

"How," Ansel said. It came out as a whisper. They cleared their throat. "How could you possibly—"

"Because I witnessed the transaction that took it." Hallowell's eyes were steady and pitiless and, horribly, sincere. "I was there, clerk. Harrow's Ford. The night your teacher burned. You think you ran away on your own legs? Someone paid your passage. Someone signed as witness while a frightened child traded away everything that made her traceable—her name, her history, her memories of the woman who taught her—in exchange for safe passage out of a village that wanted a witch-hunt and would have settled for a witch's student." He tilted his head. "I witnessed it. Which means I hold the counter-copy. Which means I can unmake it. Names are only entries, in the end. I am very good with entries."

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. Ansel gripped the doorframe without letting it show.

Maren. They couldn't remember Maren. They knew facts about her—that she'd existed, that she'd burned, that she'd taught them—but the woman herself was a hole shaped like warmth. They had assumed grief had eaten the memories. Grief, or trauma, or time. Everyone assumed that. Everyone had told them, gently, over the years, *of course you don't remember, you were a child, it was terrible.*

It had never once occurred to Ansel that the absence might be a line item.

"And if I bring you the ledger," they managed, "what happens to the guild?"

"What happens to any criminal enterprise when its books reach a magistrate?" Hallowell spread his hands. "Prosecution. Forfeiture. Dissolution. Corvane in irons, probably—though honestly, Corvane is a coward and a middleman, and I may offer him terms. The brokers go free. The clients—" he smiled thinly, "—the clients are mostly magistrates and merchants, and they'll be fine. It's the institution that dies. The guild that sheltered you. The walls you've hidden inside for eleven years." He paused, delicately. "The guild whose silence is currently the only thing keeping Tam Vetch's soul from being collected early."

"There it is," Ansel said quietly.

"There what is?"

"The poison in the honey. You dress it up as justice—give me the guild's crimes and I'll give you yourself back—but the price isn't the ledger. The price is Tam. Because the moment the master ledger leaves that vault, the court beneath the mountain gets to call every debt in it due. Including his. Solstice collection becomes immediate collection." Ansel stepped away from the doorframe, into the lamplight, and watched Hallowell's expression settle into something unreadable. "You knew that. You built the offer so that either I refuse and stay a nameless clerk chasing a dead man's soul, or I accept and kill him faster. Either way, the guild falls, and either way, you end up holding the only complete record of soul-debt in the province. With your own purchases redacted, I'd wager. You're very good with entries."

For a long moment the magistrate said nothing. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the fat, complacent sound of money.

"You're right," Hallowell said at last, "about nearly all of it. The timing is deliberate. The redactions are real. I am not a good man, and I'm not offering you justice—I'm offering you a transaction, which is the only honest thing anyone offers anyone in this province." He pushed the grimoire an inch across the desk. Not far enough to take. Just far enough to see. "But consider what you'd actually be buying, clerk. Your name. Your memories. The face of the woman who died for teaching you. Eleven years of exile ended in an afternoon. And all it costs is paper—paper that records the sins of people who profited off a child's erasure and called it bookkeeping." His voice softened, and that was the worst thing yet, because the softness sounded genuine. "You owe that guild nothing. It hid you the way a cellar hides things. I'm offering you the sky."

Ansel looked at the grimoire. Maren's burn scar on the corner. Maren's handwriting visible along the page edges, cramped and slantwise, alive. Eleven years of wanting, compressed into one object on one desk, and beside it the promise of everything else—the name, the past, the self.

*Books forgive,* Maren had said. *That's why I love them.*

People, apparently, did not.

"No," Ansel said.

Hallowell sighed, and it was the sigh of a man confirming a figure he'd already calculated. "I thought not. The loyalty is genuine, then. How tiresome for you."

"Not loyalty." Ansel's heart was slamming now, finally, all that buried fear coming up at once, but their voice held. "Arithmetic. You're offering me one soul's worth of past in exchange for one soul's worth of future—his. That's not a bargain, that's a laundering scheme, and I balance books for a living. I know a false entry when I see one." They backed toward the door, one step, two. "Keep the book. Keep my name. When this is over, I'll take both from you myself."

"You won't get out of the city," Hallowell said, without heat, without threat, which made it worse. "By morning, every ward-stone I purchased will know your face. The guild's wards will bar you too—I'll see to it Corvane understands his options. You are about to become a fugitive with no sanctuary, no guild, no name, and no stars to steer by." He picked up his pen, already turning to other papers, dismissing them. "Run home to the Gilded Scale, clerk. Say goodbye to your cellar. You have until dawn."

Ansel was three steps down the corridor before the answer arrived, fully formed, the way answers do when they've been assembling themselves in the dark for weeks:

They weren't going home.

They were going to the night market, where a broker named Quill traded in remembered birthdays, and where three years of Ansel's life had already been spent learning the exchange rates for the road beneath the mountain.

Behind them, faint through the study door, they heard Hallowell murmur—almost fondly, almost sadly:

"Punctual to the end. Solane's student after all."

* * *

The wall again, going the other way, the empty fountain, the sleeping dogs. Ansel dropped into the street beyond the estate and walked fast, hood up, breath smoking in the starless air, and did not let themselves run until the corner, and did not let themselves stop running until the river district swallowed them and the night market's crooked lanterns bloomed ahead like fever-flowers.

Three years of birthdays had bought passage partway. Quill had said so, counting coins that were actually memories, weighing each one on little brass scales. *Halfway gets you to the sunken road,* the broker had said. *Past the road, prices change.*

Ansel had more birthdays. Ansel had a name, technically, locked in a magistrate's strongbox—worthless as currency, priceless as everything.

They had until dawn. After dawn, they would be nothing to anyone: no guild, no city, no name, no past.

Which meant, Ansel thought, stepping down into the market's blue-lit sprawl, that for the first time in eleven years, they were finally free to spend themselves.

Quill looked up from the scales and smiled like a knife being opened.

"Back so soon," the broker said. "Let's talk about what's left of you."
