## 9. Refusal, and Its Consequences

The grimoire sat on the desk between them like a third person in the room.

Ansel knew it without touching it. Maren Solane's book had a smell—tallow smoke and crushed nettle and the particular sourness of ink mixed with rainwater because she was always too impatient to wait for clean water. Eight years since the pyre on Wicker Hill, and Ansel could still have found that book blindfolded in a burning house.

Hallowell watched them look. He was not what Ansel expected. In the stories told in low voices over guild wine, Magistrate Hallowell was a monster: tall, cold, eyes like coins. In fact he was a comfortable man of fifty with soft hands and reading spectacles pushed up into thinning hair, and he looked at Ansel the way a fishmonger looks at a trout he has already weighed.

"You recognize it," he said.

"I recognize what it cost."

"Yes." Hallowell turned the grimoire a quarter-turn, idly, the way a man shows off a watch. "Witchcraft is expensive. For everyone involved. Sit down, clerk."

Ansel did not sit. The study was warm, lamplit, lined with law books whose spines had never been cracked—the kind of library bought by the yard to impress visitors. Somewhere below, dogs shifted in their kennels. Somewhere above, a servant walked across a floor. Ansel catalogued all of it the way Maren had taught them to catalogue a room before you ever needed to run from it, back when there had been time for lessons, back before—

Back before the part they couldn't remember. The hole in their memory sat where it always sat, smooth-edged and painless, like a tooth long gone. They were getting used to building around it.

"You said," Ansel said carefully, "you'd give me my name back."

"Your *old* name." Hallowell smiled. "Fairweather is such a thin thing, isn't it? Paper over paper. I'm offering you the name you were born with. The one your mother used. The one that exists in parish rolls and tithe books and—" he tapped the grimoire, "—in her records. Maren Solane kept excellent notes on her students. Names, ages, aptitudes. You were her best, I'm told. A pity she never licensed any of you. A pity she burned."

The word went through Ansel like a nail through wet wood. They let it. Showing it would cost more.

"And in exchange?"

"The Gilded Scale's master ledger. The true one—not the fair copy Corvane keeps in the counting room for inspectors. Every entry. Bottled thunder, borrowed years, souls payable beneath the mountain. All of it." Hallowell folded his soft hands. "Bring me that, and I will tear up every record connecting you to Maren Solane. You'll be a free citizen with a history. No wards hunting you. No magistrate's docket with your description on it. You could walk out of this city in daylight, head up, and no one would ever look at you twice. Isn't that what you've wanted for eight years? To be no one *safely*?"

It was. That was the obscene thing about it. Hallowell had done his arithmetic correctly. Ansel had spent eight years being invisible, and invisible was only bearable if it was chosen. Offered the choice to stop hiding—and to see Maren's murderer's whole machine dragged into a courtroom while doing it—

Except.

"The ledger has Tam Vetch's soul in it," Ansel said. "If I hand it to you, you don't burn the guild. You burn the evidence. The sale becomes deniable. Hallowell the reformer, cleaning up corruption—except the sale completes at solstice either way, doesn't it? The court beneath doesn't need the paper. It just needs the debt. And you keep the grimoire, and the guild takes the fall, and Tam dies in the dark, and I get a name."

Hallowell's smile didn't move but something behind it did—a small recalculation, like a moneylender hearing a number he hadn't expected.

"You've been reading marginalia," he said. "Maren's tricks. Clever. Dangerous. Do you know what happened to the last person who read her margins?"

"She burned."

"She burned *slowly*," Hallowell corrected gently. "Because she made the mistake you're about to make. She thought truth was a weapon. Truth is testimony, girl—or boy, or whatever the ledger says you are; I confess I don't care—and testimony requires someone with power willing to hear it. I am the power in this city. Bring me the ledger and I will hear everything. Refuse, and you're just a runaway apprentice shouting in a well."

Ansel thought of Tam standing in the guild doorway, half-hollowed, his shadow arriving late to his own gestures. Thought of him saying *I never asked to be saved* in a voice like a door left open in winter. Thought of the smooth-edged hole where their childhood should be, and how badly they wanted it filled, and how much worse it would be to get it back as a bribe.

"No," Ansel said.

The word came out smaller than they meant. They said it again, louder, and it grew teeth on the way out.

"No. Keep your poison. I'll find another way to hang you."

For a moment the study was perfectly still. Then Hallowell sighed, took off his spectacles, folded them, and set them down with a small click, and the comfortable man was gone the way a mask is gone when the wearer stops bothering.

"There it is," he said. "Sentiment. I did offer."

He didn't shout for guards. He reached under the edge of the desk and pressed something, and the sigil worked into the floorboards lit up white, and Ansel was moving before the light finished blooming—out the window, actually through the glass, because doors were already going to be a problem. Shards rained into the rose garden. Behind them Hallowell's voice followed, unhurried, almost kind:

"Ward-stones, clerk! Every stone on my grounds was purchased through my own account. Guess who they answer to!"

Ansel ran.

* * *

They ran for an hour through streets that stopped being friendly one district at a time.

It began subtly. A ward-lamp over a tavern door flickered as Ansel passed, then steadied. A beggar's protective charm—a cheap stamped tin thing—gave a single high whine and died. By the river district the lamps weren't flickering; they were turning, all of them, tracking Ansel like a field of pale flowers following a sun that shouldn't exist. Word moved faster than feet. Hallowell had spoken a name into the civic network, and the network was repeating it downward, lamp to lamp, charm to charm, until the whole city's skin was prickling with it.

*Fugitive.* Not even a name. Hallowell didn't know Ansel's old name—that was the prize he'd offered—and so the docket described a shape, a gait, a face seen once across a desk. *Fugitive, unlicensed craft, approach with caution, report to the magistrate's office.*

Ansel pulled their collar up, changed their walk to Corvane's second clerk's apologetic shuffle, and cut through the tannery rows where the stink drove off anyone with a working nose, including, hopefully, pursuit.

Which left exactly one place in the world that might still take them in.

The Gilded Scale guildhouse rose ahead, gold-leafed scales glinting on its sign, windows warm. Home. The word arrived uninvited and Ansel let it stand for exactly three steps, because those were the last three steps of their life in which it would be true.

The wards hit at the threshold.

Not violently. That was the worst part. An expectorating blast, a lightning strike, some drama—that would have meant anger, and anger could be argued with. What hit Ansel instead was a flat, polite, absolute *no*, like a hand on the chest, like a shopkeeper's palm closing over the counter. The guild's boundary stones hummed a note Ansel felt in their back teeth. The door stood open six feet away. Lamplight spilled out. Inside, visible through the glass, clerks bent over their desks, safe, warm, ordinary, and every one of the boundary stones between Ansel and that light said the same thing:

*You are not inventory here anymore.*

Corvane came to the door himself.

The guildmaster was a narrow man assembled mostly of angles and disapproval, and Ansel had spent two years learning to read him the way they read ledgers—for what was entered, and for what was conspicuously not. Tonight Corvane's face held nothing readable at all. It had been wiped. Prepared. Ansel recognized the expression because they wore its twin most days: the face you put on when witnesses are watching.

"Master Fairweather," Corvane said loudly, for the benefit of the clerks behind him, "is no longer affiliated with this guild. His credentials are revoked. Any transaction conducted in his name after this hour is void. Good evening."

*His.* Even now, even disowning them, Corvane gave nothing away. Ansel could have kissed him for it or killed him for it and wasn't sure which.

"Guildmaster," Ansel said, matching his volume, playing the scene he'd written for them, "I only want—"

"You want nothing from this house. This house wants nothing from you." Then, lower, pitched under the words like a second ledger line only Ansel was meant to read: "*Walk away. Now. You're making it necessary to mean this.*"

Ansel walked away. It took everything they had. At the corner they looked back once. Corvane still stood in the doorway, arms folded, a man guarding his own frontage against a stranger—and behind the glass, briefly, the senior clerk Pell glanced up from her desk and met Ansel's eyes, and very deliberately turned her ledger face-down, and went back to work.

A message, maybe. Or a kindness. Or nothing. Ansel carried it anyway. You carried what you could.

* * *

They ended up, of course, at the night market.

Where else does a person go when every lawful door closes? The market had set itself up in the flooded foundations of the old customs house, stalls on stilts over black water, lanterns that burned colors lanterns shouldn't burn. Here the city's wards were suggestions at best. Here the fugitive brand on Ansel's skin—it wasn't a literal brand yet, but it felt like one, a heat along the shoulders where the lamps' attention had rested—here that brand was worth exactly nothing, because half the market's clientele were running from things with better resources than Hallowell.

Ansel sat on a crate at the edge of Quill's stall, not buying anything, which Quill tolerated the way a cat tolerates weather.

Quill was a broker of small impossibilities—passages, permissions, forgettings—and had sold Ansel three birthdays' worth of remembered celebrations two nights ago without blinking. Their face was a collection of features that refused to add up to anyone in particular, which Ansel suspected was professional equipment rather than nature.

"You have the look," Quill said, sorting vials, "of someone who has recently lost a home, a name, and a plan, in that order."

"In reverse order, actually."

"Mm. Worse." Quill capped a vial. "The mountain road is closed to you now, you know. Hallowell's docket follows travelers past the first waystation. And the guild will sell your description to anyone who asks—they call it *cooperating with authorities*, and it pays better than thunder."

"I know."

"So you have no passage, no coin worth the name, no guild, and eleven days until solstice, when a poacher's soul gets collected and you become a footnote in someone else's audit." Quill finally looked up. "Why am I describing your situation to you? You were there."

"Because I'm deciding whether to ask you for help, and I wanted to hear how bad it sounds out loud."

"It sounds terrible." Quill smiled with perhaps a third of their mouth. "Ask anyway."

Before Ansel could, a voice cut across the market noise—level, worn, and carrying the particular authority of someone who had once been obeyed and resented every minute of it.

"That's them. That's the clerk from the magistrate's docket."

Ansel came off the crate. The woman stepping between the stalls was broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, wearing a warden's coat with the insignia torn off but the tailoring unmistakable. Her hand rested on a truncheon not in threat but in habit, the way other people touched amulets.

"Sefa Ilori," she said. "Formerly of the city wardens. Formerly of quite a lot of things." She stopped an arm's length away and studied Ansel with the unhurried attention of someone conducting an arrest in her head and deciding against filing it. "You broke into Hallowell's estate tonight. Through a window. Over a wall with four ward-lines on it."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've been watching that house for a year waiting for someone stupid enough to try it, and tonight somebody managed it." Something that was nearly respect crossed her face and was firmly escorted back off it. "You got in, you got out, and you made him angry enough to burn you across the whole civic network inside an hour. I want to know what you saw in there. And then," she added, before Ansel could speak, "I want to know why a guild bookkeeper cares whether a poacher named Tam Vetch lives or dies. Because I pulled his file today, and it's wrong. It's wrong in ways files don't get wrong by accident. And wrong files are the only honest thing left in this city."

Behind the stall, Quill had gone very quiet, which from Quill was the equivalent of standing and applauding.

Ansel looked at the two of them—a disgraced warden who believed in justice the law had fired her for pursuing, and a broker who believed in nothing except payment, and both of them, impossibly, looking at Ansel like Ansel might be worth betting on.

Eleven days. One soul. No shelter anywhere in the world above.

"All right," Ansel said. "But we should sit down. It starts with a ledger entry written in my own handwriting, and it gets worse from there."
