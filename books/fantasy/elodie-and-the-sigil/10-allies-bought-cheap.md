## 10. Allies Bought Cheap

The safehouse was a defunct tannery behind the Salt Row tenements, and it smelled like it. Ansel sat on an upturned pickle barrel with the copied ledger pages spread across their knees, and tried not to think about how many people had already betrayed them this week.

Corvane's disownment had been public, theatrical, efficient. *A clerk of no standing, acting alone, in violation of guild charter.* Ansel had watched from the back of the Gilded Scale's courtyard as the man who'd sheltered them for six years read their name—*Ansel Fairweather*, spoken aloud for the first time since they'd run—into the record of expulsion. It had felt less like a knife than like a door closing very gently. That was worse.

Now there were three of them in a room that stank of old brine, and two of them were strangers.

Sefa Ilori stood by the window with her arms crossed, watching the street through a gap in the shutters the way she probably watched everything: like it owed her money or was about to commit a crime. She was broad-shouldered and gray at the temples, wearing a warden's coat with the buttons torn off—the city's way of stripping rank without bothering to strip the coat. She'd been a border warden for nineteen years. She'd been a disgraced one for four days.

"They know I talked to you," she said without turning. "Hallowell's clerks came to my lodging yesterday. Asked me questions about a runaway bookkeeper." She glanced back. "I told them I'd never heard of you. Which was true at the time."

"And now?"

"Now I've heard of you." She turned fully. Her eyes were the flat brown of river stones, and they did not blink enough. "So let's not waste each other's time. You think Magistrate Hallowell sold a man's soul to something under the mountain. I think Hallowell has been buying ward-stones with public funds for three years and writing them off as road maintenance. We're both right, and neither of us can prove anything inside the law, because he *is* the law in this district. So." She unfolded her arms. "Tell me what you have."

Ansel told her. The ledger entry in their own handwriting. The failed wards, all purchased through Hallowell's account. Tam Vetch, hollowed out, his shadow lagging behind him like a dog on a slack leash. Corvane's confession, such as it was—that the guild had brokered the sale, that Hallowell had coerced them, that solstice was the date of collection.

Sefa listened without interrupting, which Ansel would learn was her most frightening quality.

When they finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The wards didn't fail. They were *released*. There's a difference. A ward-stone fails when its charge runs out—it gutters, it flickers, it dies slow. What happened at dusk was clean. Someone fed the stones a release-phrase all at once." She tapped her own temple. "Only the account holder can do that. Or someone holding his seal."

"Hallowell."

"Hallowell. And here's what your ledgers won't tell you, bookkeeper." Her voice dropped, went rough. "Four years ago I filed a report on missing persons along the sunken road. Eleven people over two years, all from villages near the mountain's shadow. All poor. All unclaimed. My captain shelved it. Then my captain got promoted, and the promotion order bore Hallowell's countersignature. I kept filing. Third report, I got reassigned to a customs post in the wetlands. Fourth—" she gestured at the buttonless coat "—they found a discrepancy in an evidence locker. One I didn't make. But I'd signed for the locker, so."

"You falsified nothing?"

"I've falsified one thing in my career." Something moved behind her eyes, quick and gone, like a fish under ice. "And I'll tell you when it matters. Not before. If we're going to do this, you should know I'm not clean. Nobody who survives this city is clean. But I believe in one thing, and it isn't the law." She said it plainly, no heat in it at all. "The law is a tool. Tools get bought. Justice is what's left when the tool breaks. I've been waiting four years for somebody with actual evidence to walk into my life." She looked at the ledger pages. "You walked into my life."

Ansel felt something loosen in their chest that they distrusted immediately. Hope, probably. Hope was how you got caught.

* * *

Quill arrived after midnight, which was the only hour Quill arrived anywhere.

They came through the tannery's roof hatch without knocking, dropped eight feet to the floorboards, landed soft as spilled flour, and stood up a small person wrapped in layered gray—gray cloak, gray scarf, gray gloves, face a pale smudge between hood and collar. Age indeterminate. Gender irrelevant. Species arguably negotiable. Ansel had met Quill exactly once before, three nights ago, across a night-market stall that hadn't existed an hour earlier and wouldn't exist an hour later.

"You brought me to a tannery," Quill said. Their voice was light and dry, like paper turning. "Sentimental. Is this where you plan to cure hides? Because I have news about yours."

"Then say it."

"Two things. Payment first." A gray-gloved hand extended, palm up.

"We agreed on terms—"

"We agreed on terms for *passage*. This is *news*. News is priced separately. Everyone knows this." The hand waited, patient as furniture.

Ansel dug in their coat and produced the last of Maren's silver—a thin ring, worn smooth, worth maybe a week's meals. Quill took it, bit it out of habit despite clearly having no mouth-related intentions, and pocketed it.

"News one," Quill said. "Your magistrate has posted watchers on the pilgrim road. Not soldiers—soldiers ask questions, and questions leave records. These are paid quiet men. Six of them between here and the mountain gate. They have your description. They also have hers—" a gloved finger indicated Sefa, who had shifted her weight forward almost imperceptibly, "—and mine, though theirs is wrong, which amuses me."

"News two?"

"News two costs more."

"No."

"Yes." Quill tilted their head, and somewhere inside the hood, something considered. "But I'll extend credit. Against future consideration. Because—and don't let this reach anyone—I like you, bookkeeper. You pay your debts, and you hate owing them, and that combination is rarer than honest gold." The hand extended again, empty this time. "Shake on the credit. Terms to be named later, by me, at a time of maximum inconvenience."

"That's not a contract, that's a trap."

"All contracts are traps. Good ones are just traps both parties agree to walk into." The hand stayed out. "Shake, or hear only the first piece of news and die on the road tomorrow."

Ansel looked at Sefa. Sefa looked back with an expression that said, clearly, *you are going to shake it, so stop performing reluctance.* Ansel shook.

Quill's grip was cool and dry through the glove. "Excellent. News two: the court beneath the mountain has opened its toll-gate early. Solstice collection, yes—but the gate accepts petitioners now, ahead of the date. Which means your poacher's soul can be contested *before* it's collected. Afterward, it's property, and property disputes go to the court's own judges." A pause. "You do not want to argue property law with those judges. I've seen one eat a barrister. Slowly. As a form of citation."

"So we move now."

"We move tonight, ideally. Except." Quill raised one finger. "The gate has a toll. Not coin. It takes a truth. Something true about yourself that you would rather hide. Spoken aloud, at the threshold, witnessed by the door. Lie to the gate and it keeps the part of you that lied. Permanently." The hood tilted toward Sefa. "Wardens love gates like that. All confession, no absolution."

Sefa said nothing. But her jaw set in a way that suggested she'd already chosen what she'd be paying.

* * *

They planned until the candles drowned in their own wax.

It went the way planning goes among people with nothing left to lose and different ideas about what losing means. Sefa wanted routes and contingencies; she drew the sunken pilgrim road from memory on the tannery floor in chalk, every checkpoint, every culvert, every stretch where the old paving sank below the waterline and travelers waded waist-deep under arches built by a faith nobody practiced anymore. Quill wanted prices; they itemized the journey in a little gray notebook—bribes, ferries, the cost of silence at three separate tolls—and totaled it against what Ansel had left, which was nearly nothing, and then against what Quill might extend on credit, which was everything, which was worse.

Ansel mostly listened. That was the bookkeeper's role, they supposed. Balance the columns. And underneath the listening, a thought kept circling like a moth around a lamp:

*These are the people the system discarded.*

A warden broken for asking questions. A broker who lived outside every wall the city built. A runaway apprentice hiding inside a guild that had just spat them out. None of them belonged anywhere. And yet here they were, in a dead tannery that smelled of brine, drawing a map to hell in chalk because a stranger's soul was scheduled for repossession.

"Why?" Ansel asked finally. It came out blunter than intended. "Both of you. Why help? Sefa—you don't know Tam. Quill—you don't care about anyone. What's in it for you?"

Silence. Sefa looked at Quill. Quill looked at Sefa. It seemed to be the first time they'd looked directly at each other all night.

"Wardens take an oath," Sefa said at last. "Not to the crown. To the *recorded truth*. That every person's suffering gets written down somewhere it can't be erased. I failed that oath for four years because I was afraid. Helping you write this down—" she nodded at the ledger pages "—is the only way left to keep it."

Quill was quiet longer. When they spoke, the dry paper voice had something underneath it, faint as a watermark. "Everyone believes in payment," they said. "That's what I tell myself, because it's simpler. But once, a long time ago, someone paid my debt without being asked, and I have spent a considerable amount of effort failing to understand why. Perhaps this is research." The hood shrugged. "Or perhaps I'm extending credit against a version of myself I'd prefer to exist. Brokers hedge. Even in themselves."

Ansel looked at the two of them—the buttonless coat, the gray layers, the chalk map sinking toward the mountain—and felt the moth-thought land at last, burning itself still:

This wasn't a rescue party. It was a found family, assembled cheap, from parts the world had thrown away. Fragile as wet paper. Possibly doomed. Held together by nothing but a shared refusal to let a thing stand that shouldn't stand.

"Solstice is nine days," Ansel said. "The gate opens early. We leave at dawn."

"Dawn is expensive," Quill said. "Dawn means bribing the harbor watch before their shift changes. Dawn means—"

"Quill."

"...Dawn," Quill conceded, and wrote it in the notebook, and underlined it twice, which Ansel suspected was the closest thing to affection they would ever receive.

Sefa snuffed the candles. In the dark, the chalk lines glowed faintly, the way old roads do when they remember being processions.

"One more thing," she said into the blackness. "Tomorrow night, when we camp, I'll tell you both what I falsified. Before the gate asks me. I'd rather it come from me."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Outside, the starless sky pressed down on Salt Row like a lid, and somewhere beneath the mountain, a jarred soul flickered nine days closer to forever.

Ansel lay down on a tanner's bench, put Maren's last memory of her voice behind their teeth like a stone to carry, and slept the shallow sleep of the newly homeless—which is to say, barely, and listening.

At dawn, they would walk down into the world's cellar.

At dawn, the road would start taking its tolls.
