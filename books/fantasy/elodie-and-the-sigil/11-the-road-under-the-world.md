## 11. The Road Under the World

The stair down went further than stairs should.

Sefa Ilori counted them out of habit—wardens count, that's what wardens do, steps and heads and hours until relieved—and she gave up somewhere past four hundred. The sunken road had been a pilgrim way once; you could still see it in the flagstones, worn smooth down the center by generations of knees and feet all headed the same direction. Down. Toward the mountain's root. Toward the court.

"Who walks to hell on purpose?" Quill asked, from behind. The broker had a lantern with no flame in it, just a grey glow that made their face look like paper left out in rain.

"Pilgrims," Sefa said.

"And us."

"And us." She adjusted the strap of her pack and didn't look back at Ansel, walking third in line, silent as a held breath. Three days since Corvane had read Ansel's name off the guild rolls like striking a match and dropping it in water. Three days since every door in the Gilded Scale had quietly become a wall. Sefa knew what that did to a person. She'd been disowned by an institution herself—the Wardens of the Verge, her badge melted down, her name struck from the rolls for the crime of asking who had signed off on Hallowell's ward-stone purchases. She knew the particular cold of it. You didn't stop being what you were. You just stopped having anywhere to be it.

The road sloped. The air changed first—that was the thing nobody warned you about. Not colder. *Thinner*, as if the world above were something you'd imagined, a story someone told you about weather.

They walked.

* * *

The first village announced itself by smell: woodsmoke and bread, absurdly domestic, drifting up out of a hollow where the pilgrim road bent around a fallen slab of mountain. Sefa called a halt and they crouched behind a ridge of rubble, looking down at lights—not star-colored, not lamp-gold either, but a soft ash-grey, dozens of them, windows of low stone houses stacked into the hollow like swallows' nests.

"No wards," Ansel said. Sefa heard it in their voice, the professional flicker of it. "No boundary stones, no sigils on the lintels. Nothing."

"They don't need any," Quill said. "That's the product."

They went down anyway. You couldn't cross the under-road without passing through the settlements—it was the whole design of the place, Sefa realized later. The road funneled you into the villages the way a ledger funnels you into debt. A woman met them at the edge of the first cluster of houses, middle-aged, broad-shouldered, flour to her elbows, and she smiled at them the way you smile at weather.

"Travelers. You'll want supper. There's stew. It's good stew." She said it simply, without performance, and Sefa felt the wrongness of that simplicity like a draft under a door. Nobody was this unafraid of three armed strangers. Nobody was this unbothered by anything.

"Thank you," Sefa said carefully. "We're just passing through."

"Everyone's just passing through," the woman said pleasantly. "Until they aren't. Come sit."

* * *

The common room was warm and full and quiet in a way that took Sefa a while to name. She'd spent eleven years policing border towns. She knew loud rooms, drunk rooms, frightened rooms, grieving rooms. This room was none of those. It was *settled*, the way a grave is settled. People ate and talked softly and laughed at small things, and when a child dropped a bowl nobody flinched, and there were no stars anywhere—not painted, not carved, not wished upon—and when Sefa finally understood why the quiet felt wrong, she set down her spoon.

"There's no night sky here," she said to Ansel, low. "But look at the walls. No charms against nightmare. No dream-catchers. No prayer niches. These people have traded away the thing everyone else prays to, and they don't even miss it."

Ansel was staring at a man across the room—a grandfather type, playing some kind of tiles game with a boy of maybe eight. "Ask him," Ansel said. "Ask him anything about his past."

So Sefa did. She sat down across from the old man, bought a round of the thin cider he was drinking, and asked him, warden-polite, where he'd come from before the road.

He looked up with mild, clear eyes. "From here," he said. "I've always been here."

"You came down the pilgrim road? Years ago?"

"I've always been here." He said it again, same warmth, same certainty, and Sefa watched his hands keep moving over the tiles, arranging them, and she saw that they never arranged them the same way twice, because he wasn't remembering a pattern, he was making one up fresh each time, and he didn't know the difference anymore.

"What was your mother's name?" Sefa asked gently.

The old man smiled. "I had a mother?" And then he laughed, delighted, genuinely delighted, like she'd told him a joke. "Imagine that. Imagine having a mother. What a strange, rich life I must have had."

Sefa stood up so fast the bench scraped. In the corner, the boy with the tiles watched her go with eyes too calm for eight years old.

* * *

Outside, under the ash-grey lamps, Ansel had stopped in the middle of the lane. Sefa came and stood beside them. For a long moment neither spoke.

"That could be me," Ansel said finally. "Down here. Fed and warm and safe, with no wards to maintain and no guild hunting me and no magistrate's seal on my fugitive papers. Just—" their hand moved vaguely at the village, at the lamplight, at the whole soft terrible arrangement— "*this*. Stew and tiles and no past."

"It's a trap dressed as mercy," Sefa said.

"Is it, though? That's what frightens me." Ansel's voice was very steady, which Sefa had learned meant it wasn't. "I've been running my whole adult life on being no one. False names, forged papers, a face kept deliberately forgettable. I built myself into almost exactly this, Sefa. The only difference between me and that old man is that I *kept the pain*. I kept the guilt and the fear and the memory of Maren burning. That's the whole difference. That's the entire distance between me and happy."

Quill drifted up out of the shadows, lantern held high. "You two are doing the thing," they said, "where you stand in the street being eloquent instead of walking. The court doesn't care how you feel about villages. Move."

But as they shouldered packs again, Sefa caught Ansel looking back once at the lit windows, and the look on Ansel's face was not dread. It was longing. And that was worse.

* * *

Two days deeper, the second village.

This one was larger, built into a cavern where the road passed under a rib of living rock, and this one had a broker's stall right on the square—a neat little booth with a striped awning, run by a plump person with ink-stained fingers who weighed memories on brass scales the way a spice merchant weighs saffron. Sefa watched the trade happen. It was banal. That was the horror of it, the pure administrative horror of it: a farmer's wife sold the memory of a flood that had killed her sister, and paid a tithe of it—*a tithe*, the court took its cut, everything here took its cut—and walked away lighter, smiling, dabbing her eyes at nothing, unable to say why.

"She'll sell the rest next year," Quill said, watching. "They always do. Grief is heavy. Why carry it?"

"Because it's hers," Sefa said.

"Is it, though?" Quill raised an eyebrow. "Memory is just inventory. I know, I deal in it. The court figured out something brilliant, warden. Pain is the only currency everyone carries. So they built a bank."

At the edge of the square, Ansel had stopped in front of a notice board—a real one, wooden, with pinned notices, because even down here people needed to find lost goats. One notice, older than the others, sun-bleached despite the absence of any sun, read:

**LOST: MY WEDDING DAY. I KNOW I HAD ONE. REWARD OFFERED FOR ITS RETURN. ASK FOR MIRA.**

Below it, in different ink, someone had written: *Mira sold the rest in spring. Stop asking.*

Ansel read it twice. Then they unpinned it, folded it, and put it inside their coat, over the heart, and Sefa decided not to ask, because there are questions whose answers you already are.

* * *

That night they camped in a shrine ruin just off the road—one of the old pilgrim shrines, pre-Court, its niche empty, its dedication stone worn blank. Sefa took first watch. Around the dead fire, Ansel slept sitting up, the folded notice still in their coat, and Quill slept like a cat, instantly and completely, the way people sleep who have never once feared a bill coming due.

Sefa watched the dark and thought about justice.

She had believed, most of her life, that justice was a structure—you built it, floor by floor, law by law, and you maintained it like a building, and if it cracked you repaired it with procedure. Eleven years of service had taught her otherwise, slowly, expensively: justice was not a building. It was a fire, and structures only contained it or smothered it, and sometimes a person had to carry it in their bare hands, and get burned. Hallowell had a structure. Hallowell had purchased wards and burned witches and sold souls through a licensed brokerage, and every piece of paper was correct, and the structure had approved every step. That was the lesson of the last month, distilled: the system had done nothing wrong, technically, ever, while doing everything wrong.

So she sat watch over a hedge-witch's student and a runaway bookkeeper and a memory-broker who'd sold Tam Vetch's soul without knowing it, and she thought: fine. If the structure won't hold the fire, I will.

Near dawn, Ansel woke without being woken—the way people wake who learned to sleep in guildhouses that might turn on them—and came and sat beside her on the broken shrine step.

"I keep thinking about Mira," Ansel said.

"The wedding day."

"If we fail," Ansel said, "if the court takes Tam and keeps the stars, I'm going to come back to one of these villages. I can feel it. I'll tell myself I'm hiding, and then I'll tell myself I'm resting, and one morning I'll sell the memory of why I came, and it'll be such a relief, Sefa. That's the part I can't stop seeing. Not the horror. The *relief*."

Sefa looked at them—at the sharp tired face, the ink-stained fingers, the whole small stubborn engine of a person who had chosen, over and over, to keep carrying it.

"Then we don't fail," she said.

It wasn't comfort. It was arithmetic. Ansel nodded anyway, and accepted it, and that was the moment Sefa privately promoted them from charge to comrade.

* * *

On the fifth day the road changed character. The flagstones gave way to something older and stranger—veined stone, faintly warm underfoot, and along its edges grew pale rootless flowers that turned to follow the travelers as they passed. The air tasted of ink. Far ahead, where the cavern opened into a vastness the lantern light refused to reach, there was a glow on the underside of the distant dark: thousands of points of cold white light, hanging below the ceiling of the world like a reflection of a sky.

Sefa stopped and stared, and her stomach dropped, because she knew what stars looked like, and these were stars, and they were *below*.

"They're not reflections," Ansel said quietly. They had gone very pale. "Those are ours. The ones that went out over the city. The court didn't steal them to hoard them in the dark. They hang them. They *light themselves with them*. We've been walking beneath a sky made of everything taken from above."

Ahead, the flowers all turned at once, every pale head facing the travelers, and somewhere far down the road—invisible, unhurried—a bell began to ring, once, evenly, like a clerk marking time.

Quill checked their ledger, licked a thumb, turned a page, and said:

"Gate's close. Everyone decide now what truth you're willing to pay."
