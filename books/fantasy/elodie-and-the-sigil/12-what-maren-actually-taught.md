## 12. What Maren Actually Taught

The pilgrim road had no night worth the name. The dark came down like a lid, total and starless, and the villages along the way burned lamps that gave off light but somehow not comfort—light without anything behind it, like a smile from someone who didn't know you.

They made camp in the shell of a waystation three days' walk below the last living village. Sefa built a fire out of old pew-wood from the chapel ruins, which burned greenish and smelled faintly of incense and rot. Quill counted their remaining payments twice, muttered something about "the tolls ahead," and wrapped themselves in a cloak that was probably stolen from a corpse and definitely warm.

Ansel sat with their back against a fallen pillar and tried to sleep.

Sleep had been hard to come by since the estate. Since Hallowell's open door and the grimoire laid out on his desk like bait, since the offer—*your old name back*—since the word *no* leaving Ansel's mouth before they'd even decided to say it. Since Corvane's face in the guildhouse courtyard, going carefully blank as he disowned them in front of forty clerks.

But exhaustion on the road under the world was a different animal. It didn't ask permission. It put its hand on the back of your neck and pushed your head down into the dark like a priest baptizing a sinner, and Ansel went under between one breath and the next.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of rosemary and burnt honey.

That was the first thing—the smell, arriving before anything else, so specific it hurt. Rosemary steeped in goat's milk for the fever-cures, honey left too long on the fire because Maren never watched anything she could talk to instead. The low ceiling of the cottage at Vell's Crossing, black with twenty years of smoke. The crooked table. The window with its jar of dead bees Maren refused to throw out because "they're still working, look at the pattern they make."

And Maren herself, standing at the hearth with her sleeves rolled up, stirring something she wasn't looking at, because she was looking at Ansel.

She looked the way Ansel remembered her before they'd stopped being able to remember her at all: gray-streaked hair escaping its braid, forearms freckled and burn-spotted, eyes the brown of river water over stones. Not young. Not idealized. Just *there*, solid as furniture, in the exact center of the room where Ansel had spent six years learning everything that got her killed.

"You're dreaming," Ansel said. Their voice came out younger than they wanted. "This is a memory. Or a trap. Hallowell has your grimoire—he could have seeded something in it."

"If I were going to trap you, girl, I'd have done it with pie." She tapped the spoon on the pot's rim. "Sit down. You're swaying."

Ansel sat. The bench was exactly as hard as it had been. That was the terrible thing about dreams this good—they got the details right, including the ones you'd have edited.

"I don't remember you," Ansel said. It came out flat, factual, the way they'd learned to say it. "I mean—I remember *of* you. I remember the fire. I remember running. But you—" They pressed a fist against their sternum, where a shape should have been and wasn't. "There's a hole where you were. Six years, and there's just a hole."

"I know," Maren said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you told me yourself. The night before they came." She set the pot aside and finally turned fully around, wiping her hands on her apron, and looked at Ansel with an expression that wasn't pity and wasn't grief and was somehow worse than both: it was *patience*. "You came to me shaking, and you said, 'Maren, there's a paper in my head with my own name on it and I can't read what's under the name.' Do you remember saying that?"

"No."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of what we're doing here tonight."

The fire popped. Outside the dream-window, the sky was doing something wrong—it kept trying to be the sky over Vell's Crossing, all its ordinary stars, and then flickering, like a page held up to a candle, into the starless dark of the road under the world. Ansel understood, distantly, that the dream was stitched together from two places: the past they'd sold and the present they were walking through. Maren's kitchen, wearing the mountain's darkness like a borrowed coat.

"What is this?" Ansel asked. "Why now?"

"Because you're about to do something stupid," Maren said, "and I only ever had time to teach you half of it, and the half you're missing is the half that keeps stupid people alive." She pulled out the other chair and sat across from Ansel, elbows on the crooked table, close enough that Ansel could see the flour still caught in the creases of her knuckles. "So we're going to have the last lesson. The one I kept putting off because I thought we had years."

"We don't have years. Tam has until solstice. That's—days."

"Then we'll be efficient." She folded her hands. "Tell me what you think magic is. Go on. First answer, don't polish it."

Ansel thought of the guildhouse. Of ledgers balancing entries for bottled thunder and borrowed years. Of ward-stones failing one by one across the border, purchased through Magistrate Hallowell's account. Of a soul entered in a book in handwriting identical to their own.

"Power," Ansel said. "Shaped. Directed. You gather it and you aim it."

Maren made a sound like a door closing. "That's what the guild thinks. That's what the court beneath the mountain thinks. That's what every magistrate who ever burned a hedge-witch thinks, because power is the only part of magic they can tax or fear." She leaned forward. "Magic is *testimony*, Ansel. Say it back."

"...Testimony."

"Every spell is a true statement about the world. That's all any spell has ever been. Fire doesn't come when you call it because you're strong. Fire comes because you stand in front of the world and you say, truthfully, *this wood is dry, this air feeds burning, here is a spark*—and the world, which cannot abide a true statement going unanswered, agrees. The power isn't yours. The power is the world keeping its word." She tapped the table once, hard. "A witch is just a witness who knows how to speak clearly."

Ansel sat very still. Something was moving in the hole where the memories should have been—not returning, nothing so kind, but shifting, like furniture dragged across a floor in another room. A shape they couldn't see but could feel the edges of.

"The wards," Ansel said slowly. "The border wards that fell. They weren't broken. They were—"

"*Contradicted.* Someone stood in front of each ward-stone and testified something false that was nonetheless *true enough*—that the stone had been paid for, perhaps, by a man with the right to unmake what he'd bought—and the world heard a truer statement than the one the wards were standing on, and the wards fell. Yes." Maren's eyes were bright. "You always did get there fast. It was the slow parts you hated."

"And the contracts." Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "The guild's contracts. Tam's contract. Mine."

"What is a contract, if you strip away the wax and the vellum and the clerk's pretty hand?"

"A... statement. About what's owed."

"A *true* statement," Maren corrected. "Or it does nothing. No court honors a lie; no magic does either. Every contract in every ledger your guild keeps is a spell—a testimony about debt, spoken clearly enough that the world enforces it. That's why the ledgers matter more than the swords, why the guild fears a loose page more than an army. The ledger isn't a record of the magic, child. The ledger *is* the magic."

The kitchen flickered. For half a heartbeat Ansel saw through it—Sefa's greenish fire, Quill's muttered counting, the pillar against their spine—and then Maren's hand closed over theirs on the table, warm and flour-rough, and the kitchen came back.

"I sold my memories," Ansel said. "When I was a child. To get out of the burning. There's a contract with my signature on it and I don't remember signing it, and the ledger-keeper under the mountain holds it, and Hallowell witnessed it." Their voice cracked on the last part. "How do I fight that? It's *true*. I signed it. The world heard me."

"Yes," Maren said gently. "It did. And it was true when you spoke it."

"But?"

"But listen to me now, because this is the whole lesson, and I will only say it once, and after tonight you'll have to carry it in whatever pieces survive the morning." She gripped Ansel's hand harder. "A spell is a true statement. Fine. But *true statements go stale*. The world changes around them. The dry wood gets rained on. The debtor pays. The witness dies. And a statement that was true when it was spoken can become false—and when it becomes false, the spell doesn't vanish. Spells are stubborn. They keep standing on ground that's moved out from under them, propped up by habit and enforcement and everyone's fear of looking too closely." Her voice dropped. "Which means every spell, no matter how old, no matter how sealed, no matter how much the court beneath the mountain would like you to believe otherwise, can be *contradicted*. You don't break a contract, Ansel. You testify against it. You stand in front of the world and you swear a truer thing."

"How?" The word came out raw. "Tam's contract is ironclad. His soul is *jarred*, Maren, I've seen what these people do—"

"Was the statement true when he made it? Whatever he swore, whoever swore it for him—was it true?"

"He was desperate. He was tricked. He didn't understand what he was agreeing to."

"Then the contract rests on a lie dressed as consent, and lies are load-bearing walls in every court ever built." Maren smiled, and it was the fierce smile, the one from the two times a year she let herself be angry, the one Ansel had seen in the moment before soldiers kicked the door in. "You find the lie. You drag it into the light where the whole court can hear it. And then you say the true thing out loud, in front of everyone, and make the world choose which testimony it honors." She paused. "Fair warning, though. The world takes testimony seriously. It always asks the speaker to stake something on it. Truth spoken cheaply binds no one."

"Stake what?"

"That depends on the truth." She released Ansel's hand and stood, moving back toward the hearth, and the dream began already thinning at the edges—the rosemary fading first, then the smoke-black ceiling going translucent, stars-that-weren't showing through. "One more thing, and then you'll wake and hate me for not telling you sooner, which is the traditional order of things between us."

"Maren—"

"You keep asking how to save him while staying hidden. How to fix it all without paying. How to remain invisible and do visible things." She stirred the pot again, though there was no fire under it anymore, no heat, no kitchen—just her, holding the shape of the habit. "Invisibility is also a spell, girl. It's a statement: *no one sees me, no one owes me, I am not part of this.* And it's gone stale. It went stale the day you opened that ledger and read a stranger's soul in your own handwriting. You can't testify from inside a hiding place. The court won't hear a witness who refuses to exist."

The kitchen was nearly gone. Maren was nearly gone. Ansel lunged up from the bench—

"What was the lesson really? The one you kept putting off? What did you actually teach me?"

Maren looked back at them through the dissolving room, and her expression was the one from the fever nights, when Ansel was small and scared and she sat up with them anyway.

"That the truth costs exactly what it's worth," she said. "No more. Never less. Now go and be expensive."

* * *

Ansel woke with their cheek against cold stone and Sefa's green fire burned down to embers, and for a moment they couldn't move at all. The shape of the lesson sat on their chest like a sleeping cat: heavy, warm, immovable.

*Magic is testimony. Contracts are spells. Spells rest on statements, and statements can go false, and false things can be contradicted—but only by a witness willing to stake something real.*

They lay in the dark and thought about Tam's contract. *One soul, payable beneath.* Signed—or sworn—in desperation, in ignorance, in a bargain struck by men who'd measured his life in coin before he understood there was a scale. If consent had been a lie, the whole structure above it was a wall built on sand. The court knew that. The ledger-keeper knew that. Which was why the court demanded truth as toll at its gates, why it hoarded honesty like a miser, why it would make anyone who came to contradict a contract pay for the privilege in the only currency it respected.

Ansel sat up. Across the dead fire, Sefa was awake—of course she was, she slept like a cat with one ear cocked—and watching them with those warden's eyes that missed nothing and judged less than people assumed.

"You dreamed something," Sefa said quietly.

"I remembered something I never knew." Ansel flexed their hands. They felt steadier than they had since the estate. Frightened still—more frightened, honestly, because now they understood the price of the door they meant to walk through—but steady underneath, the way a beam is steady when it finally finds its wall. "Tomorrow we reach the gate. The one where you have to name something true you'd rather hide."

"I've been thinking about what I'll say," Sefa admitted.

"Don't rehearse it. Rehearsed truths are just prettier lies." Ansel looked east, where the road bent down toward a darkness deeper than the starless night—the dark around the under-court's threshold, where the stolen stars were kept. "And Sefa. When we get inside, when we find the ledger-keeper—whatever happens, whatever they demand—don't argue for me. Don't offer to pay my debts. Promise me."

"Why?"

"Because the truth I have to tell is mine," Ansel said, "and testimony only binds if the witness stakes their own hand."

Above them, in the absolute black, something that might have been wind and might have been the mountain breathing shifted the ash on the dead fire—and far down the road, faint as a held breath, the gate began to glow.
