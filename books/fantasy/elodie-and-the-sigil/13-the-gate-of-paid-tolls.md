## 13. The Gate of Paid Tolls

Quill had brokered a hundred thousand bargains and never once been on the paying side of a gate, and they found they hated it with a purity of feeling that was almost refreshing.

The gate stood where the pilgrim road simply stopped pretending to be a road. One moment there was cracked flagstone winding down through the dark, and the next there was a wall of mountain that had no business being there, and set into it a doorway tall as three men and narrow as a coffin, framed in black stone shot through with veins of dull gold. No doors. No bars. Just an opening that breathed.

Cold air came out of it in long slow exhalations, like something sleeping on the other side had decided to keep them waiting.

Above the lintel, carved deep and worn smooth by centuries of hands, ran a single line of text in a script Quill knew from a thousand contracts: *state your toll.*

Below it, smaller, newer, cruder—scratched by some desperate traveler with a knife or a nail:

*it knows when you lie.*

"Charming," Quill said. "Someone should sue whoever maintains this thing. False advertising at minimum. It's not a gate, it's a confession booth with delusions of architecture."

Nobody laughed. Sefa stood with her arms crossed, warden's stance even without the badge, watching the dark in the doorway the way she'd watch a suspect's hands. Ansel stood apart from both of them, hood up, one hand pressed flat against the wall beside the gate—not touching the threshold, just resting there, the way you'd rest your hand on a horse before asking it to carry you somewhere terrible.

And behind them all, Tam Vetch sat on a fallen pillar with his shadow pooled wrong around his feet, moving half a heartbeat late whenever he shifted, flickering like a candle in a draft. He'd stopped talking an hour ago. That was worse than the talking.

"Well," Quill said, clapping their hands together. "Tolls. I've paid tolls my whole career. Coin, favors, secrets, firstborns—metaphorically, mostly. How bad can one more be?"

They stepped up to the threshold.

The cold exhaled over them, and the gold veins in the black stone brightened, and a voice came out of the gate itself—not from behind it, not from above it, but *from* it, the way sound comes from a struck bell. It was a dry voice. A ledger-voice. A voice that had been reading entries aloud for so long it had forgotten what its own opinion sounded like.

**"Name yourself."**

"Quill," they said. "Broker of the night market. Purveyor of rare goods, rarer information, and payment plans flexible enough to make a moneylender weep."

**"Name your toll."**

Quill opened their mouth to offer coin.

The gate did not wait.

**"Name something true that you would rather hide."**

The words landed in Quill's chest like a hook finding meat.

They were, professionally, an excellent liar. They lied the way other people breathed—easily, constantly, structurally. Half their fortune was built on lies so well-constructed they'd achieved a kind of truthiness, load-bearing falsehoods holding up entire reputations. They had prepared for this gate the way they prepared for everything: with inventory. Three confessions, pre-sorted by cost. The embezzlement from the Saltmarket chapter of the night market—cheap, embarrassing, survivable. The forged provenance on the Vetch sale documents—moderate. The—

The gate waited. The gold veins pulsed, patient as interest accruing.

And Quill discovered, standing there with three cheap truths stacked and ready, that the gate was not going to take them. That it could tell the difference between a confession and a transaction, and it despised transactions, and it was going to stand there breathing cold until Quill gave it something real.

"That's cheating," Quill said quietly. "That's exactly the kind of clause I put in contracts to ruin people."

**"Name your toll."**

Behind them, Tam made a small sound. Not a word. Just a sound, like a man dreaming of falling.

Quill looked back at him—at the poacher who was mostly shadow now, hollowed out day by day since the solstice countdown began—and felt something in their chest do a thing they refused to name.

Fine. Fine.

"You want true?" Quill said to the gate. "Here's true."

They turned to face the others, because it seemed important, suddenly, that this not be said only to a door.

"I brokered the sale of Tam Vetch's soul."

Sefa's head came around. Ansel went very still.

"Not knowingly," Quill said, and heard their own voice do the thing voices do when the excuse arrives before the regret. "Not knowingly—that's the whole point, isn't it, that's the *toll*, so let me say it properly. Fourteen months ago a client came to me through three intermediaries, which is standard, which is *caution*, and asked me to arrange a transfer of assets from a rural account to a court account beneath the mountain. I don't ask what the assets are. Asking is how brokers die. I verified the paperwork was authentic—which it was, every seal genuine, because Hallowell's office generates genuine seals—and I moved it through six exchanges so the origin would be untraceable, and I took my commission, and I bought a new coat, and I slept fine."

The gate's cold breath stirred their hair.

"I didn't know it was a soul. But here is the part I would rather hide, the part I've hidden even from myself: I *could have known*. There was a line in the contract I didn't read closely. A line describing the asset as 'non-corporeal, self-aware, transferable.' I read it and I thought, odd phrasing, and I moved on, because moving on is what I do, because curiosity is expensive and I have never once in my life paid retail." Their voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I sold a man for a coat. Not a coat—a *coat fund*. A fraction of a coat. And he's sitting right there getting fainter, and I'm the reason the paperwork was clean."

Silence. Even the wind off the mountain seemed to hold itself.

The gold veins flared white, then settled into a warm steady glow, satisfied.

**"Toll accepted. Pass when all have paid."**

Quill stepped back from the threshold with their heart hammering and their skin cold and a strange lightness in their chest that they distrusted immediately and completely. Relief, probably. Or shock. They'd invoice themselves for it later.

Sefa Ilori looked at them for a long moment. Then she nodded, once—the nod of a warden acknowledging testimony—and walked past Quill to the gate without hesitation, because of course she did, because Sefa had never in her life hesitated at anything except mercy.

She planted her feet before the threshold like a woman taking a oath.

"Sefa Ilori," she said. "Former warden of the Third District. My toll."

Her jaw worked. This one was not easy for her either, then. Good. Misery loved company and Quill was feeling sociable.

"Three years ago I built a case against a smuggler named Doss Meric. He was guilty—of smuggling. Small goods, untaxed cloth, nothing that deserved what came after. But I couldn't make the evidence reach far enough to convict him of what I *knew* he was part of: a ring moving stolen ward-stones, the same kind that failed on our border, the same kind Hallowell's office certified as sound. I had the ring's leader dead to rights in my head and nowhere else. So I falsified a ledger page. I wrote a shipment into existence that never happened, dated it, sealed it, and used it to hang the whole network on Meric."

She paused. Her hands had curled into fists.

"He got twelve years. His crew got scattered. And it *worked*—the ring collapsed, the ward-stone thefts stopped, and I told myself for three years that the arithmetic was clean. One lie, dozens protected. Warden's math." She turned her head and looked directly at Ansel. "But that's the same math Hallowell does. Same sum, different signature. I broke the law to enforce it and called it justice, and I have never said that sentence out loud before tonight, and I would rather hide it, and there it is."

The gate glowed. **"Toll accepted."**

Two down. Quill found themselves looking at Ansel with something they'd have called professional interest if they'd been billing for it.

Ansel hadn't moved. Hood up, hand still on the wall, and now Quill could see the tension running through them like a wire pulled to snapping—shoulders locked, breath shallow, the whole body organized around one purpose: *do not step forward yet.*

"Ansel," Sefa said gently. "It's just a door. It takes the worst thing and lets you keep walking. That's the bargain. That's a better bargain than most."

"It's not the worst thing I have," Ansel said. Their voice was flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "That's the problem."

Quill tilted their head. "Meaning?"

"Meaning I don't know what's true about me." Ansel's hand slid off the wall. "You two confessed things you remember. I have gaps. Big ones—years of them, whole seasons missing, and I've always told myself it was the fever, the flight, trauma, whatever story kept the gaps quiet. But the ledger entry was in my own handwriting. My own signature is on a contract I've never seen. What if—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "What if everything I'd give this gate is a guess? What if I confess to the wrong crime? What if the truth I'd rather hide is that I *don't know what I'm hiding*, and the gate can tell the difference between that and a lie, and it keeps us all standing here until solstice comes and Tam runs out?"

Tam lifted his head at the sound of his name. His eyes found Ansel's with that terrible blankness—he didn't know her, wouldn't know her, had carried her out of a burning village and been paid with total forgetting—and he said, rasping, "Don't pay for me twice. Whatever it costs. Don't—"

He coughed. His shadow lagged a full second behind the motion.

Ansel crossed to him fast and gripped his shoulder, and Tam stared at the hand on his shoulder like it belonged to a stranger, because it did, because that was the whole wound of it.

"I'll pay whatever's asked," Ansel said to him, low. "I've been telling myself that since the ledger. But I don't know if I *have* anything true left to give."

Quill watched the two of them—the hollowed man and the hollowed stranger who owed each other everything across a gap of forgetting—and felt the old broker instinct rise: *price it, structure it, find the angle.* And felt, underneath it, for the first time in living memory, the older instinct: *some accounts shouldn't be balanced. Some should just be settled.*

"Then don't guess," Quill said. "Ask it."

Ansel looked up. "Ask what?"

"The gate. It's a ledger-thing, yes? Everything under the mountain is ledgers and clauses and tolls. So treat it like a counterparty. Ask it what's outstanding on your account." Quill shrugged, keeping their voice light because the advice wasn't light at all. "Worst case, it refuses. Best case, you learn the shape of your debt before you sign anything. Never sign blind. First rule. Well—second rule. First rule is get paid up front."

Sefa snorted despite herself. "Your rules are why you're down here."

"My rules are why I'm *alive* down here."

Ansel stood there a moment longer. Then they turned to the gate, and squared their shoulders, and walked up to the threshold alone, and the cold rolled out over them like a tide.

"I'm Ansel," they said. "No family name. Guild bookkeeper, formerly apprentice to Maren Solane, of whom I remember almost nothing. My toll—" and here their voice caught, and steadied, "—my toll is that I don't know what I'd rather hide. So I'm asking: what's outstanding on my account?"

The gate was silent for a long, long moment. Long enough that Quill's stomach began to drop, long enough that Sefa's hand drifted toward her knife.

Then the gold veins flared—not white this time but a deep, hungry amber, the color of old ink, of sealed wax, of a page waiting to be written—and the bell-voice spoke, and for the first time it did not sound bored.

**"Account located. Signature matches. Outstanding balance: one contract, witnessed, sealed, unpaid since childhood. Toll deferred to the creditor's discretion."**

A pause, the length of a held breath.

**"The court below has been expecting you, Ansel-of-no-family-name. Enter, and settle."**

The darkness in the doorway deepened, and somewhere far beneath them, faint as a memory of a bell, something began to count.

Quill exhaled. "Well," they said. "That's either very good news or the single worst opening position I've ever seen negotiated."

"Which?" Sefa asked.

"Yes," said Quill, and followed Ansel through the gate.
