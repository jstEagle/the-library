## 14. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The gate had taken their truths. What it gave back was a road.

It unspooled beneath the mountain like a vein of light through black glass, and Ansel walked it with Sefa's hand on one shoulder and Quill's silence at their back, and every step made the air heavier, as though they were descending not into a place but into an argument that had been going on for centuries and had no intention of stopping for them.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you rehearse your own death so it won't surprise you."

Ansel almost smiled. "Maren used to say something like that. About fear." The name still worked in their mouth, mostly. Some memories had come back whole from the toll-gate—strange, that the court wanted hidden truths and returned them polished. Others were still missing, edges gone soft, like pages left out in rain.

"Then say it," Sefa said.

Ansel thought about it. "I'm afraid of the wrong things," they said finally. "Not of dying. Of being *processed*."

Ahead of them, Quill snorted—a sound like a coin dropped on stone. "Good instinct," the broker said. "Keep it."

The road ended where all roads under the world end: at a door.

* * *

There was no door, actually. There was a threshold marked by nothing but a change in the light—the stolen starlight, Ansel realized, the same cold silver that should have been hanging over the city, pooled here instead in vast slow rivers along the ceiling of a cavern so large that Ansel's eyes refused at first to accept its walls. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ. And between them, tier upon tier rising into the dark, were seats.

And in the seats were the creditors.

Ansel had imagined monsters. They'd had three weeks of walking to imagine them—things with too many teeth, things with voices like rockslides, the classic furniture of nightmare. What they got was worse. The creditors looked like clerks. Like magistrates. Like respectable people at the end of a very long meeting. Some were human-shaped and some were not; some wore shapes that seemed negotiated rather than natural, faces assembled from consensus. But every one of them sat with the posture of someone reviewing accounts. Every one of them held paper.

The sound reached Ansel next, and it was the sound that would follow them into sleep for years afterward if they survived to have sleep again: the scratching of pens. Thousands of pens, thousands of ledgers, a parliament conducting itself in ink.

"They're voting," Quill murmured. "Or counting. With this lot there's rarely a difference."

"How many are there?" Sefa breathed.

"Seats for nine thousand," Quill said. "Attendance varies by fiscal quarter."

A herald-thing approached—not walking so much as being filed forward—and regarded them with an expression of mild administrative interest. It carried a slate. On the slate, in handwriting Ansel knew better than their own reflection, were three words:

**STATE YOUR BUSINESS.**

Ansel's throat was dry. "We've come about an account," they said. "One soul, payable beneath. The debtor is present."

The herald tilted its head, which was apparently how such creatures smiled. It turned the slate over. New words had already appeared, as if the writing had simply been waiting on the other side:

**FOLLOW THE YELLOW LINE.**

There was, indeed, a yellow line. It glowed faintly against the black floor, winding away between the tiers toward the center of the cavern, and Ansel understood with a lurch that they had seen lines like it before—in the Gilded Scale guildhouse, painted on the floor to guide clients to the proper window. The under-court had invented the queue. Or perhaps the guild had learned it here. Perhaps everything above ground that Ansel had ever resented had been imported, wholesale, from this place.

They followed the line.

* * *

The central floor of the cavern was a clearing among the tiers, and in it stood the archives.

Ansel had spent six years among the Gilded Scale's vaults and had believed, until this moment, that they understood what a collection of records could be. The Gilded Scale kept money. This place kept *everything*. Shelves rose in canyons, each one holding jars, scrolls, ledgers, bottles, boxes labeled in scripts older than any kingdom Ansel could name. And in the jars—

Sefa saw it at the same moment Ansel did. Her breath went out of her in a hiss.

Souls.

Not all of them. Most of the shelves held more ordinary inventory—bottled thunder, borrowed years, last words, first loves, the smell of a specific morning in a specific year. But one long gallery, set apart behind a rail of black iron, held jars of a different quality. Each jar contained a small light. Not firelight, not starlight—something warmer and more personal, the way a face is warm. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One or two had gone nearly dark, and those jars were labeled in a way that made Ansel's stomach turn: **FULLY AMORTIZED.**

"There," Quill said.

Tam Vetch's soul hung on the third shelf, in a jar the size of a lantern, and it was flickering. That was the only word for it—flickering, like a candle in a doorway, like a man trying to remember why he'd walked into a room. The label read:

**VETCH, TAM. ONE (1) SOUL, SECONDHAND. PURCHASED VIA HALLOWELL, MAGISTRATE. MATURITY: SOLSTICE.**

Secondhand. Ansel stared at the word until it stopped looking like a word. Tam hadn't even been sold directly—he'd been bought and sold, passed down the chain like a coat, and somewhere in the middle of that chain was a guild ledger with Ansel's handwriting in it, and somewhere upstream of that was a magistrate who had once burned a hedge-witch on a Tuesday because the paperwork was in order.

"We take it and run," Sefa said. It wasn't a question, exactly. It was a warden's instinct—seize the evidence, secure the scene.

"No," Ansel said, and heard Maren's voice underneath their own: *a spell is a statement.* "If we steal it, we confirm we're thieves. The contract stands. We'd be running from it forever." They swallowed. "Contracts can be contradicted. I have to do it here, in front of all of them, or it doesn't count."

"And if they don't let you speak?"

"Then I'll have learned something important about bureaucracy," Ansel said, "which is that it always lets you fill out a form."

Quill laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of them—and then stopped laughing, because the shelves had begun to move.

* * *

It wasn't an earthquake. It was an *adjournment*. The tiers above emptied in perfect order, creditor after creditor filing down into the archive floor, each carrying their ledgers, each taking a seat on benches that hadn't been there a moment before, arranged in a great crescent facing the gallery of souls. An usher-thing materialized beside Ansel with a slate:

**YOU HAVE REQUESTED AN AUDIENCE REGARDING ACCOUNT NO. 4471-V. THE COURT WILL HEAR YOU. PLEASE STATE YOUR NAME FOR THE RECORD.**

And there it was. The trap Ansel had been walking toward since the night market, since the toll-gate, maybe since the day they'd run from their village with another man's blood on their sleeves and a poacher's hand shoving them up onto a cart. To state their name before this court was to enter it into the record—to make it collectible, taxable, *ownable*. Their invisibility, the one thing they had left, offered up like a coin on a scale.

"I'm here about the account," Ansel said carefully. "Not about me."

The usher considered this. The slate erased itself and rewrote:

**THE COURT REQUIRES A PARTY OF RECORD. UNNAMED PARTIES MAY NOT FILE CONTRADICTIONS.**

Of course. Of course it worked like this. You couldn't fight the machine anonymously; the machine had thought of that centuries ago. Somewhere in these shelves was probably a jar containing the last person who'd tried.

Ansel looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at Quill. And Quill, who believed in payment and nothing else, who had confessed at the gate to brokering Tam's sale without knowing it, stepped forward and said, "File me as co-party. My name's already in your books—might as well get some use out of it."

"Quill—"

"It's arithmetic," the broker said flatly. "You're the one who can contradict the contract. I'm just the collateral. Don't make it sentimental; I hate that."

Ansel gave their name.

It came out of them like a tooth. "*Ansel Fairweather.*"

The sound of it in that cavern was extraordinary. Every pen stopped scratching. Nine thousand creditors turned, in perfect unison, the way a field of flowers turns toward sun, and Ansel felt the name leave their body and enter the record and become, instantly, a thing with weight—a debt-shaped hole in the world that everyone present could see.

Somewhere far above, Ansel knew, wards were adjusting. Doors were deciding whether to recognize them. The name echoed down through the shelves and came back changed.

Then the pens resumed, and the crowd parted, and the ledger-keeper came forward.

* * *

Ansel had expected a monster. What came through the parted ranks was tall and gray and robed, and its face was—

Its face was a blur. Not hidden—a blur, the way handwriting is a blur when you know it too well, the way your own signature looks strange when you catch it by surprise. And when it spoke, Ansel felt the words land in their chest like hooks, because it spoke in a voice they had heard every working day for six years, dictating entries across a guildhall ledger room.

"You wrote in my book," the ledger-keeper said.

"No," Ansel said. "You wrote in mine. The entry. 'One soul, payable beneath.' In my hand. In the Gilded Scale master ledger, page four hundred and twelve."

"In your hand," the ledger-keeper agreed, "because it is the same hand. I keep all the ledgers, Ansel Fairweather. Yours above. Mine below. When the account matured, I entered it where the debtor would be most likely to find it." It tilted its blurred head. "You were the most likely. You were, after all, the bookkeeper."

Sefa's hand found Ansel's arm. "It's saying it used you. As a—"

"As a filing system," Ansel said. The horror arrived slowly, the way cold water rises. Six years of balancing impossible trades, bottled thunder and borrowed years, and every entry had passed through their hands, and their hands had been *its* hands, and the guild's great secret trade had been, all along, a branch office. "Why? Why show me? Why not let the entry sit until solstice?"

"Because you are the witness," the ledger-keeper said. "Every contract requires one. Your signature is on the second document—the one you do not remember. And a witness, when the account comes due, must attend the settlement." It extended a gray hand toward the gallery, toward the flickering jar labeled VETCH, TAM. "You have come to contest the debt. Very well. The Court hears contradictions. It is one of our oldest procedures." Something moved beneath the blur of its face that might have been pleasure. "But understand the terms. To contradict a contract, you must testify that it is false. And testimony, in this court, is paid for in proof. You will surrender the memory that proves the contract false—or you will fail to convince us, and the soul will be processed at solstice as scheduled."

"That's not—" Sefa started.

"That is procedure," said the ledger-keeper, and every pen in the cavern scratched once, in unison, like applause.

Ansel looked at the jar. At the small warm light guttering inside it, dimmer than it had been a month ago, dimmer than it would be in three more weeks. Tam Vetch, who had put them on a cart with bleeding hands and told them to live. Who had walked into the Gilded Scale half-hollowed and begged a stranger for help, because he didn't remember the boy he'd saved.

*He never asked to be saved,* Ansel thought. *He said it himself. So I'll save him anyway, and he can be angry about it later.*

"I'll testify," Ansel said.

The ledger-keeper inclined its head. "Then come to the table, witness. Bring your contradiction." Its blurred face turned, briefly, toward the highest tier, where a single seat remained empty—and on the seat, Ansel saw now, lay a folded gray robe and a magistrate's chain of office, waiting, patient, as if someone were expected. "And witness—one more matter of procedure. The Court has a second item on today's agenda." The pens paused. "Account No. 0001. The founding contract of this court. Signed by a child, witnessed by a magistrate, and maturing"—the blur of its face seemed to sharpen—"tonight."

The yellow line beneath Ansel's feet began, very softly, to glow.
