## 15. Ansel's Signature

The ledger-keeper did not look up when we entered its office.

That was the first horror of it. I had expected the under-court to be a place of teeth and flame, some cathedral of cruelty where souls were weighed on black scales. Instead it was a room. A long, low room of polished bone-white stone, lit by jars of stolen starlight hung from chains, and in the middle of it stood a desk so vast it might have been carved from a single fallen tower. Behind the desk sat the ledger-keeper, and the ledger-keeper was writing.

It looked like a clerk. That was the worst part. Grey robes, grey skin, a face like a thumbprint pressed into wax—features suggested rather than committed to. Its pen moved across an open folio with the patience of something that had never once been interrupted and did not intend to begin now.

Sefa's hand found my shoulder. *Wait,* the pressure said. Quill drifted toward the shelves along the wall, where ten thousand ledgers stood spine-out in perfect order, each one humming faintly, like wasps dreaming.

I cleared my throat. "We've come about an account."

The pen stopped.

"You've come," the ledger-keeper said, "about several." It turned the page. "The poacher Vetch, whose soul is jarred in the east gallery. The warden Ilori, who owes this court one falsified truth. The broker Quill, who owes it one honest confession, now paid." Its head lifted, and the wax face had no eyes, only two shallow dents where eyes would go, and I felt them anyway. "And you. The bookkeeper. Who owes nothing at all."

"That's not true," I said. "I owe—"

"You owe nothing," it repeated, with terrible gentleness. "You have already paid. In full. Years ago." It closed the folio before it. "Would you like to see the receipt?"

* * *

It rose, and I saw that below the desk its body simply became the chair, became the floor, became the building—the whole court beneath the mountain was, in some sense, this creature's body, or its body was the court's, and the distinction no longer mattered. It moved through the shelves without walking, and the shelves parted for it, and we followed because there was nowhere else in the world to follow.

"The guild above," it said as it went, "believes they invented impossible trade. Bottled thunder. Borrowed years. Charming. But every contract they broker passes through this office for countersignature. They are a branch office. They have never known it. Most debtors prefer it that way. Ignorance is a form of collateral."

Quill made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You're telling me the Gilded Scale has been your franchise for two hundred years."

"I am telling you," the ledger-keeper said, drawing a slim volume from a shelf at exactly my eye height, "that someone knew. Someone always knows. That is what witnesses are for."

It laid the volume open on a reading stand. The cover was plain grey leather, unmarked, and when it fell open I saw the handwriting and my knees nearly went.

It was mine.

Not similar. Not close. Mine—the way my hand slanted, the way my sevens crossed twice, the little hook I put on terminal letters that Corvane had once tried to train out of me and failed. Entry after entry in my own hand, dated years apart, and I had written none of them.

"What is this?" My voice came out very flat, which is what my voice does instead of shaking.

"Your account," said the ledger-keeper. "Opened when you were nine years old. You have been a client of this court longer than you have been a citizen of anywhere." It turned pages with one grey finger. "Here. Your first deposit: three years of remembered birthdays, sold at the night market, quite recently, to purchase passage. A sound investment; you got here, after all. And here—" the finger stopped, "—the original instrument. Dated the fourteenth of Embermonth, nineteen years ago. Would you like me to read it aloud?"

"No," said Sefa sharply.

"Yes," I said.

The ledger-keeper inclined its thumbprint face, almost courteously, and read:

"*One memory, complete and indivisible: all instruction received from Maren Solane, hedge-witch, including but not limited to craft, doctrine, technique, testimony, and love. Seller: the minor child present. Buyer: this court. Consideration paid: safe passage out of the village of Ashford-on-Wold during the fire, undetected, unhurt, and unremembered. Witnessed and sealed by the undersigned officer of the law.*"

It turned the volume so I could see the witness line.

Magistrate Aldous Hallowell. His signature, neat and official, the same signature I had spent weeks tracing through guild accounts, the signature that bought failing ward-stones and dark stars and Tam Vetch's soul. He had signed my childhood away with the same pen he'd used to sign Maren's death warrant. Probably the same evening. Probably the same ink.

"That's a forgery," Sefa said, but her voice didn't believe itself.

"It is not," said the ledger-keeper. "This court does not accept forged instruments. Forgeries do not bind. This binds." It tapped the page. "The child walked out of a burning village past six soldiers and a magistrate who was watching the door specifically to catch anyone fleeing the witch's house. The child was not seen. The child was not hurt. The child did not remember why any of it mattered. All three conditions were met precisely, because all three were purchased. The fire took the teacher. The contract took everything the teacher left behind. The magistrate considered it tidy."

The room tilted. I gripped the reading stand.

I had built a story out of my own absence, these nineteen years. I had assumed Hallowell took my memories—that he burned Maren and then burned her out of me too, some punishment, some erasure, and my amnesia was a wound he gave me. It had even been comforting, in a grim way. Wounds make victims. Victims are innocent.

But the handwriting was mine. Nine-year-old hands, crossing their sevens twice.

"I signed it," I said.

"You signed it," the ledger-keeper agreed. "You were nine. You were hiding in Maren Solane's cellar while her house burned above you, and a man came down the stairs—not soldiers, the magistrate himself—and he did not arrest you. He opened a book and explained, kindly, patiently, in words a child could follow, that there was a way out. No pain. No fire. No memory of anything worth grieving. Children are excellent negotiators of despair; they will trade anything for the pain to stop. You traded the only thing you had." It paused. "He let you keep your fear. Fear was not in the inventory. You kept the shape of the loss without its contents. That is why you flinch at burning things. That is why you cannot remember your mother's face but know she hummed. That is why you became a bookkeeper—you have been trying to read your own contract for nineteen years without knowing it."

Tam's shadow moving a beat behind him. Tam not knowing me. And all this time I had thought myself the one who remembered being forgotten.

"I came here to free him," I said. "To contradict his contract. To testify."

"Yes," said the ledger-keeper. "And you should know, before you do, what testimony costs in this court. Chapter Sixteen of your own story, if you like. But first—" it turned back through the volume, back, back, to the first page, and held it out to me, "—your signature. The original. Every account must be verified against its opening instrument. Court procedure. Indulge me."

I took the book. The paper was warm, like something alive.

There, at the top of the first page, in a nine-year-old's careful hand, the letters I had learned from Maren herself—she taught me my letters on a slate, I knew that suddenly, the way you know a smell—spelled out a name.

Not Ansel Fairweather. Ansel Fairweather was a coat I had sewn and worn so long I'd forgotten it was a coat.

The name on the page was my name. My real one. And seeing it was like swallowing a lit coal, because I could not read it—I looked directly at it and my mind slid off, the way water slides off oiled cloth—but I could feel it. I could feel that it was mine the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in the dark.

"Why show me that?" I whispered. "If I can't read it?"

"Because tomorrow," said the ledger-keeper, closing the volume gently over my fingers, "when you stand before the court and testify against your own contract, you will be asked to sign the contradiction. And the court will need to verify that the hand matches." Its dented eyes held mine. "You see the difficulty, debtor. To prove the contract was false, you must prove it was yours. To prove it was yours, you must remember signing it. And the consideration of the contract was that you would never remember. The spell protects itself. It is, I confess, one of the better instruments I have processed."

Somewhere far above us, under a sky with no stars in it, Tam Vetch was flickering in his jar, running out of nights until solstice.

"And if I can't?" I asked.

The ledger-keeper began, slowly, to smile. It had a great many more teeth than its face had shown a moment ago.

"Then the contract stands," it said. "The soul stays sold. And you walk out of here still nameless, still memoryless, still innocent—" the word came out like a coin flicked contemptuously onto a counter, "—having lost nothing but everyone else's everything. The court finds that outcome acceptable. The court finds most outcomes acceptable. That is what a court is."

Quill leaned close to me. For once, the broker had nothing clever to sell.

"There's another way," Sefa said quietly. She was staring at the shelf behind the reading stand—at the slim grey volume, and beside it, half-hidden, a second book, older, its cover scorched at one corner. Maren Solane's confiscated grimoire, catalogued and shelved like any other asset. "Hallowell offered you that book for the master ledger. It's been here all along. Her techniques. Her doctrine. *Testimony.*"

"Maren Solane's assets were seized under warrant," the ledger-keeper said. "They belong to the court."

"They belonged to her students," Sefa said.

The ledger-keeper regarded her for a long moment. Then it did something I will think about for the rest of whatever life I have left.

It stepped aside.

"Not a gift," it said. "A loan. Against collateral." Its dented gaze settled on me. "You have one asset remaining, debtor, that this court values above all others. Your ignorance. While you do not remember, the contract is safe. So learn quickly. Read fast. Because every page of her teaching that takes root in you is a page of the contract coming due—and the moment you understand enough to testify, you will understand enough to lose."

I reached for the scorched book.

My hand was steady. Nineteen years of balancing other people's impossible entries, and my hand was steady, and somewhere inside me a nine-year-old in a burning cellar was finally, finally being handed the pen again.

"Then I'll read fast," I said.
