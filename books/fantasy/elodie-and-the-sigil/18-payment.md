## 18. Payment

Quill had brokered a thousand bargains and never once watched one land.

That was the trick of the trade, the thing they'd learned in their first week at the night market, hunched under a tarp of stitched crow-feathers while an old broker explained the first rule: *you never watch them pay.* You take your cut, you close the book, you move to the next stall. Watching is how brokers go soft. Watching is how brokers end up poor, or worse—end up *feeling* something about a transaction, which in Quill's professional opinion was the same as being poor.

So Quill knew better. And still, when Ansel stepped up to the court's ledger-keeper with the pen already in hand, Quill watched.

Couldn't help it.

The court beneath the mountain was quiet in the way that only very old places are quiet—not silent, but *attentive*. The parliament of creditors sat in their tiers of bone-white benches, and every one of them had turned to look. Above, where the ceiling should have been, hung the stolen stars: hundreds of them, jarred and strung like lanterns at a festival, burning with the light they'd been drinking out of the sky for weeks. Tam Vetch's soul sat on the ledger-keeper's desk in its own small jar, flickering like a candle in a draft, and beside it lay the contract that had bought it, and beside *that* lay the second contract—the old one, the child-sized one, the one Ansel had signed without knowing what childhood signatures were worth.

Ansel looked smaller than Quill remembered. That was the strange part. They'd walked into this court half-starved and ward-burned and legally nonexistent, and somehow they'd gotten *smaller* since. Not from fear. Quill knew fear when they saw it; fear made people clutch things. Ansel wasn't clutching anything. Their hands were steady on the pen.

"State the terms," said the ledger-keeper.

It didn't have a face you could argue with. It was mostly hands—a dozen pairs of them, folded and unfolding, ink-stained to the elbow, and a voice like a page turning. Quill had dealt with entities like it before, or thought they had. But those had been hungry things wearing bureaucracy like a coat. This was the opposite. This was bureaucracy all the way down, patient as sediment, and somehow that was worse.

"The debt of Tam Vetch," Ansel said. "One soul, payable beneath. I'm paying it."

"You cannot pay another's soul-debt," said the ledger-keeper. "The court has ruled on this."

"I know." Ansel's voice didn't waver. "I'm not paying his debt. I'm buying him out. Equivalent value, freely given. That's allowed."

A ripple went through the benches. Somewhere in the tiers, a creditor made a sound like coins settling in a purse.

"It is allowed," the ledger-keeper conceded, after a pause long enough that Quill counted their own heartbeats through eleven of them. "Equivalent value. Name it."

And here was the thing Quill would think about later, in the years after, in the small hours when bargains came back to roost: Ansel didn't hesitate. Most people hesitated at that moment. Most people named something they could live without and told themselves it was equivalent. Ansel just said it, plain as reading off a price list:

"My name. My place in the world above. And my memory of why any of it mattered."

Sefa moved first. Of course she did—Sefa Ilori had never in her life met a sacrifice she didn't try to intercept. "Take mine instead. I've got a name nobody wants anymore, I've got a place I've been thrown out of, and there's plenty I'd be glad to forget—"

"The value must be *equivalent*," said the ledger-keeper, not unkindly, which was somehow more terrible. "Your name was taken by others. Taken names are discounted. Hers—" one of the hands gestured at Ansel, "—was kept. Guarded. Hidden at great cost. Hidden names are rare. Rare things are expensive."

"Then take my memories," Sefa said. "I'll give you the good ones. All of them."

"Sefa." Ansel turned, and Quill saw them smile—small, tired, real. "You spent three chapters' worth of trouble getting your justice back. Don't hand it to a clerk."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Ansel—"

"*No.*" And there it was, the steel under the tiredness, the same voice that had contradicted a contract before the whole court and paid for it in advance. "Nobody substitutes. Nobody pays for me. That's the entire point. That's been the point since the ledgers." Ansel looked around the court, at the tiers of creditors, at the jarred stars overhead. "You're all bookkeepers. You understand balance. So balance it."

The ledger-keeper's hands unfolded, all twelve pairs at once, and laid the pen across the open page.

"Sign," it said.

* * *

Quill had expected ceremony. There was no ceremony.

That was the horror of it, Quill thought later. No thunder, no tearing of veils, no dramatic wind. Just a person standing at a desk, writing their own name—which was itself a kind of joke, because the moment the pen finished the last letter, the name stopped existing anywhere except on that line, and even Quill, who had watched the whole thing, found they couldn't remember what the letters had been. They remembered that there had been letters. They remembered the shape of the writing, quick and slanted, a left-hander's hand. They could not have reproduced a single character of it under torture.

The pen lifted. The ink dried. And the world above them shivered.

Quill felt it first in their teeth. Then in the floor. Then everywhere at once—the great hoard of the under-court, the vaulted dark full of everything the court had ever collected, and it *broke open*, the way a dam breaks, the way a purse breaks when the stitching finally gives. Light poured upward through the mountain like water finding its level. The jarred stars tore loose from their strings and rose, streaming past the creditors' benches, past Quill and Sefa and the hollow-eyed stranger who had been Tam Vetch, up and up and out, and somewhere far above, Quill knew, the sky over the city was filling back in, star by star by star, the wards' failure undone not by any warden's craft but by arithmetic. A debt had been paid. The books balanced. The stars went home.

Tam's jar cracked down the middle and fell into two clean halves, empty.

And the man himself—Tam Vetch, poacher, smuggler, the man who had once carried a terrified apprentice out of a burning village on his back—sucked in a breath like a drowning man surfacing, and opened his eyes, and looked directly at Ansel.

"No," he said. His voice was rust and river-mud. "No, you didn't. Tell me you didn't—"

He reached for them. Ansel stepped back—one step, just one—and smiled again, that same small tired smile, and said something Quill couldn't hear, pitched low, meant for Tam alone. Whatever it was, it stopped Tam cold. He stared. His mouth worked. And then his face did the worst thing a face can do: it *smoothed over*, the way water smooths after a stone sinks, because whatever Ansel had said was already leaving him, already unwriting itself, the memory of the words dissolving even as they landed.

Because that was part of the payment too. Quill understood it a beat before anyone else did, because understanding prices was Quill's whole trade. Ansel hadn't just sold their name and their place and their reasons. They'd sold the *telling*. Every word that could carry Ansel's story from one mouth to another—gone. Sefa couldn't testify to who had freed Tam Vetch. Quill couldn't sell the tale, and Quill had checked, reflexively, professionally, and found to their genuine alarm that they had nothing to sell. The greatest bargain of the age, struck right in front of them, and it was *unrecordable*. The court had bought a silence along with everything else, and the silence was absolute.

Tam blinked at the stranger in front of him. Polite confusion. A man waking in a strange place among strangers, grateful and lost.

"Where am I?" he said. "Who—are you the ones who got me out?"

"Yes," said Sefa, hoarsely.

"Bless you," said Tam Vetch, meaning it, meaning all of them equally, meaning no one in particular. "Bless every one of you."

Ansel stood very still for a moment. Then they nodded, once, to Sefa, once to Quill—businesslike, a courier confirming delivery—and turned toward the pilgrim road with the unhurried walk of someone who has finished a job and has nowhere in particular to be.

Quill watched them go. Watched until the dark of the road took them.

Then Quill did the single most unprofessional thing of their career.

They followed.

* * *

The pilgrim road climbed for two days out of the under-country, through villages where people lived contented and starless, having traded their yesterdays for safety, smiling at strangers with the particular warmth of people who have nothing left to lose. In the third such village, Ansel stopped at a well, drank, thanked the woman drawing water, and asked—in a voice with no history in it—if there was work for a stranger who was good with figures.

"There's always work," the woman said. "Can you keep an honest ledger?"

"I can keep an honest ledger," said the stranger, and something in their voice caught, just slightly, on the word *honest*, like a splinter too deep to dig out.

Quill stood at the edge of the square and did not approach. Approach was a transaction. Approach implied terms. And there were no terms left to offer—Quill had checked, twice, obsessively, on the road. The stranger's name was gone. The guild's wards would bar them now, Corvane's disownment seeing to that. Hallowell's men would be hunting a fugitive who no longer existed, which was almost funny. Almost.

What stopped Quill, in the end, was simpler than sentiment. It was the books.

Quill was a broker. Brokers keep accounts. And Quill's account with this stranger read: *one passage purchased, three birthdays received, debt outstanding.* Ansel had traded three years of remembered birthdays for the road to the mountain, and Quill had taken the payment and delivered the service, fair and square—but the service had turned out to be worth more than quoted. The stranger had gone on to do things with that road that no birthday could cover. Unpaid balances itched. Unpaid balances kept brokers up at night, and Quill told themselves that was all this was, strictly professional, closing the books.

So Quill waited until the stranger was settled in the village—a cot behind the counting-house, a meal, a beginning—and then walked up the road alone, and sat down on a milestone, and waited for the stranger to pass on their evening walk, because the stranger walked every evening, Quill had observed, like someone checking that the world was still there.

"Got a minute?" Quill said.

The stranger looked at them with polite blankness. "Do I know you?"

"No," Quill said. "But I owe you money."

That got a flicker of interest. Good. Interest was a hook, and hooks were Quill's native language. "You don't owe me anything. I don't know you."

"Doesn't matter. Debt's debt. See, somebody did me a service once, and I can't prove it was you, but my books say it was, and my books are never wrong." Quill dug in their coat and produced it: a slim ledger, cheap binding, honest paper. "So I'm paying forward. Take it. No magic in it. No contracts. Just pages and arithmetic. A body ought to have somewhere true to write things down."

The stranger took the ledger slowly, turning it over in their hands like something fragile, though it was just paper and thread. Their thumb moved across the cover, back and forth, back and forth.

"Why does this feel—" they started, and stopped, and frowned at their own hand as if it had acted without permission. "Never mind. Thank you. What do I owe you for it?"

"Nothing," said Quill, standing, brushing off their coat, already turning away because if they stayed one more minute they were going to do something unforgivable like *cry* in front of a client. "It's paid. Somebody paid it. Best bargain I never brokered."

They walked down the road toward the city, toward Sefa and her tribunal and her illegally obtained justice, toward the wreckage of a career that had just taken its first honest loss. Behind them the village lamps came on one by one under a sky that was filling back up with stars, and in a cot behind a counting-house, a stranger with no name opened a blank ledger to its first page, and dipped a pen, and began—to Quill's certain knowledge, though they could not have said how they knew it—with a single line:

*One soul, returned, paid in full.*

Quill didn't look back. Brokers never watch them pay.

But walking down that road, lighter by one ledger and heavier by everything else, Quill broke the rule one last time, and glanced over their shoulder at the small lit window, and thought: *somebody ought to remember you existed.*

And far below the mountain, in a court built of patience, a dozen pairs of ink-stained hands turned to a fresh page, and wrote, in handwriting identical to no one's now, a new entry that would wait years for a reader:

*One name, held in trust. Payable when claimed.*

Somewhere, a poacher was starting to ask questions.
