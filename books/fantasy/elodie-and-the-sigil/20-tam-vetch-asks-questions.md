## 20. Tam Vetch Asks Questions

The first thing Tam Vetch did with his returned soul was get drunk.

Not because he wanted to. Because everyone told him he should. The warden woman—Sefa, all sharp elbows and sharper eyes—bought the first round and said, "You died and came back. That's worth a drink." The broker called Quill bought the second and charged him for it, which felt more honest somehow. By the third, bought by nobody, Tam sat alone in a tavern near the river gate watching his shadow move when he moved, exactly when he moved, and thought: *I don't remember any of it.*

That was the part no one had warned him about.

He remembered the poaching. He remembered every snare line in the Thornfell woods, every game trail, every winter he'd eaten crow and called it pheasant. He remembered smuggling a scared kid out of a village that wanted to burn a hedge-witch, remembered the kid's white face in the dark of his cart, remembered lying to the magistrate's men with a straight spine and a liar's smile.

He remembered everything except the last two years.

There was a hole where those years should have been, smooth-edged, like a stone worn round by water. He could put his finger on the edge of it and feel nothing. And everyone who might have filled it—the warden, the broker, the guildmaster who'd come to see him once with an expression like a man auditing his own conscience—everyone went quiet when he asked.

"Who sold me?" he'd asked Sefa on the second day, sitting up in the infirmary bed with his hands still trembling like a snared bird's.

"It's handled," she said.

"Who *saved* me?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Something crossed her face—not pity, exactly. More like the look you give a door you know is locked and have decided not to knock on again.

"Someone who can't be thanked," she said, and left.

Tam Vetch had spent thirty years reading sign. Broken twigs, pressed grass, the particular way a hare's track doubled back when it smelled fox. He knew how to follow what didn't want to be followed.

So he got sober, and he started asking questions.

* * *

The Gilded Scale guildhouse smelled of tallow and old paper, and the clerk at the front desk looked at Tam like he was something tracked in on a boot.

"Poacher," she said, reading his name off some internal list. "Account closed. Balance settled. There's nothing—"

"I'm not here about my account." He set a coin on the counter. Not a bribe. A down payment. "I'm here about the ledger."

"There is no ledger available for public—"

"Not the ledger." He leaned in. "The *hand*. Somebody wrote an entry about me. One soul, payable beneath. I want to know whose hand wrote it."

The clerk's mouth opened and closed. Behind her, through a doorway, Tam saw shelves of ledgers rising into shadow like a cliff face made of years. Somewhere in that cliff was a book with his name in it, written by someone who had then walked up a mountain and given away—what? Sefa wouldn't say. Quill, when pressed over the second drink, had only shrugged and said, "Payment's payment. You don't ask the coin where it's been."

But coins had histories. Tam had melted down enough stolen silver to know.

"Entry's sealed," the clerk said finally, quieter. "By order of the tribunal. It's evidence now."

"Evidence of what?"

"Of things you're better off not—" She stopped herself. Looked at him properly for the first time. "You really don't remember anything?"

"Two years gone. Like somebody cut them out with a knife."

Something in her face shifted. She glanced past him at the empty hall, then slid a slip of paper across the counter so fast it was almost a sleight of hand.

"The entry was logged under a junior bookkeeper's seal. Name struck from the rolls after the solstice. No record of departure." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "But struck names leave ink-ghosts. Ask at the scriptorium on Candle Lane. Ask for the one who teaches counter-hand."

"Counter-hand?"

"How to forge a hand so well you can unforge it." She pulled her arm back as if the paper had burned her. "Go. Before I remember I never spoke to you."

* * *

Candle Lane was three streets of narrow houses leaning together like conspirators, and the scriptorium was the one with light in the window past midnight.

The woman who opened the door was maybe fifty, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the flat wary look of someone who had survived something by keeping her head down and resented every year of it.

"You're the Vetch," she said. Not a question.

"People keep knowing me."

"People kept knowing Maren Solane too." She stepped aside to let him in. "I'm Petra. Sit down. This will take longer than you want and shorter than you deserve."

The room inside was a schoolroom stripped to its bones: desks, lamps, sheets of practice script pinned to every wall. Dozens of different hands, each rendered with obsessive precision. Tam recognized none of them and felt, absurdly, that he should recognize one.

"Maren Solane," he said. "The witch they burned."

"The teacher they murdered and called it law." Petra lit a lamp with a match, no craft in it, or none she showed. "You smuggled her students out of Thornfell. Two of them, before they took her. You don't remember that either, do you? No—you wouldn't. Your two missing years start before that."

"They start before—" Tam's mouth went dry. "The burning was six years ago. My hole is two."

"Yes." Petra sat across from him and folded her ink-stained hands. "Which means whatever happened to you happened *after*, and whoever paid your debt paid it with more than money. Sit. Listen. I'm going to tell you about handwriting."

* * *

She spread three documents on the desk between them.

A guild receipt. A page torn from a tribunal transcript. And a letter, old and browned, its edges charred—rescued, she said, from a house fire six years ago, though everyone knew there had been no fire until the magistrates brought one.

"Three hands," Petra said. "Look at the letter first. That's Maren's. The slope, the pressure, the way she crosses her t's low because she learned writing on slate and slate doesn't forgive high crosses."

Tam looked. He couldn't read the words—he'd never been taught much—but he could read the *shape* of them, the way he read tracks. Confident. Unhurried. A hand that knew exactly where it was going.

"Now the receipt."

This one was cramped, careful, the letters small as if their writer was trying to take up less room in the world. But underneath the smallness—

"The same," Tam said slowly. "It moves the same. Like the same person walking, trying to walk quieter."

Petra stared at him. "That is the single best description of palimpsest-hand I've ever heard from a man who claims he can't read."

"I read woods, not words. Same thing. Things leave how they move behind." He tapped the third document, the tribunal page. "And this one?"

"That's the problem." Petra's voice went carefully neutral. "This is the testimony of a witness at Magistrate Hallowell's tribunal. Anonymous deposition, entered into evidence, credited with breaking the case open. The tribunal never identified the witness. The witness's name appears nowhere in any record."

"And the hand?"

"The hand matches the receipt. Matches the letter underneath it. Matches entries in the Gilded Scale master ledger going back years." She met his eyes. "One person, Vetch. One hand, moving through all of it. The apprentice who copied Maren's style without knowing why. The bookkeeper who found the soul-entry. The witness who testified against Hallowell. And the one who paid for you."

The lamp flickered. Tam became very aware of his own breathing.

"What happened to them?"

"No one knows. They were seen leaving the mountain with the warden and the broker. The warden came back. The broker came back. The witness didn't." Petra gathered the papers with hands that weren't quite steady. "No body. No grave. No record of passage from any city gate. Just—" she hesitated— "just a rumor. From the starless villages east of the sunken road. Of a stranger who keeps a little ledger for the neighbors. Fair prices. Honest arithmetic. Won't say their own name because—" 

"Because they haven't got one," Tam finished.

The room was silent. On the walls, all those practiced hands hung in the lamplight, dozens of ghosts of style, and somewhere among them, Tam understood, hung the ghost of a person who had learned magic as testimony and then testified so hard the world took everything they had.

"Why?" His voice cracked on it. "Why would someone do that for me? I'm nobody. I'm a poacher. Whoever they were—a bookkeeper, a student, whatever—they owed me nothing."

Petra looked at him for a long time.

"Vetch," she said gently, "you carried two children out of a burning village in a cart full of manure while soldiers searched it. One of them grew up and never forgot. Even when they tried to. Even when forgetting was the price of living." She began rolling the documents closed. "Some debts aren't paid. They're just carried until you find a way to set them down. Someone set yours down for you. Now the only question left is—"

She stopped. Tam waited.

"—is whether you're going to carry it back to them."

* * *

He left at dawn with a nameless stranger's direction, a forged travel pass Petra made him in twenty minutes ("a gift; don't thank me, thank her"), and a new hunger that had nothing to do with food.

The road east was bad and getting worse. Past the sunken pilgrim road the stars thinned out overhead like a crowd dispersing, and the villages he passed through were warm, well-fed, and wrong—people smiling with smooth holes where memories should have been, trading their pasts for safety the way his grandmother had traded eggs for salt. Tam slept in barns and kept his hand on his knife and his memories close, counting them like coins. Every snare line. Every winter. The kid's white face in the cart.

Especially the kid's white face in the cart.

On the ninth day he reached a village with no name on any map, just a cluster of roofs around a well, and a small building at its edge with a painted sign, the letters plain and unmagical:

**LEDGERING & FAIR ACCOUNTS — NO CRAFT, ONLY ARITHMETIC**

Through the window he saw a figure bent over a desk, writing. Dark hair. Careful shoulders. A hand that moved across the page small and quiet, taking up as little room in the world as it could.

Tam stood outside for a long time. Long enough that the figure noticed him noticing, and looked up, and frowned politely at a stranger with road dust on his boots—no recognition at all, not even the faintest ghost of it, just the blank courtesy people give travelers.

His heart was doing something complicated and painful. Thirty years of reading sign, and he had never once been afraid of a door until now.

He thought about turning around. He thought about what Sefa had said: *someone who can't be thanked.* He thought about the smooth-edged hole in his own memory, and the person inside that building who had a hole the size of their whole life.

Then Tam Vetch, who had lied to magistrates and outrun wardens and carried children out of fires, pushed open the door of the little ledger-house and said the truest thing he'd said in two years:

"I hear you're good with accounts. I've got one that doesn't balance. Somebody paid a debt for me, and I mean to find out who—and I've got a feeling the trail ends at your desk."

The stranger set down the pen. Looked at him with eyes that held nothing he knew and everything he recognized.

And on the stranger's hand, catching the morning light, Tam saw a ring he had seen only once before—in a charcoal sketch on the wall of a scriptorium, drawn by a dead woman's surviving students: a sigil-ring that recognized deeds, not names.

It fit that hand perfectly.

"Sit down," the stranger said quietly. "Tell me about the debt."
