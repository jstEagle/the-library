## 1. The Taster's Ledger

There are three rules to surviving as a food-taster in Vellamarch, and I follow all of them religiously, which is ironic, because religion is the first thing this city burned.

Rule one: taste everything twice. Once for the poison, once for the panic — they have different flavors, and a man who confuses them dies of the wrong one. Rule two: never be interesting. Interesting men get invited to things. Rule three: count your charms every night, and if the number changes, leave before dawn.

I had followed rule three for six years without incident. Sixty-one charms, sewn into the lining of my coat like a miser's hoard of buttons: fever-charms, silence-charms, one very expensive charm against being noticed that I'd bought from a woman who charged extra for discretion and delivered it anyway. Sixty-one small sins, each one enough to see me hanged if the Spire's wardens ever patted me down with purpose instead of boredom.

The soup was leek and smoked eel, which Magistrate Corven insisted on despite it being out of season, because powerful men believe seasons are for other people. I took my spoonful in front of him, held it on my tongue, counted to ten, and waited for the little cold bloom at the back of my throat that means arsenic. Nothing. Just eel, leek, and the cook's heavy hand with the pepper.

"Alive, then," Corven said, not looking up from his papers. He said it the way other men say *good morning*.

"Alive, then," I agreed, and went home through streets that smelled of coal smoke and fear, past the Spire, which stands in the center of Vellamarch like a finger raised in permanent accusation. They built it after the Burning, white stone quarried from the mountain itself, and its whole purpose is silence. No magic within its shadow. No names spoken of those who used it. The city keeps its ledgers clean by keeping them empty.

My rooms above the chandler's shop on Tallow Lane are small, warm, and unremarkable, which is the point. I lit no lamp — habit — and set my ledger on the table to do the night's accounting. That's what I call it: accounting. A taster's ledger looks like inventory. Weights of spice, deliveries of wine, sums paid to cooks for their recipes so I could anticipate what I'd be tasting. It does not look like sixty-one charms listed in a cipher of my own devising, each with its purchase date and its price.

The price is the part I don't like looking at. Not the coin — coin is honest. The other price. Three years ago there's an entry I've never been able to read fully, written in my own hand but smudged somehow, blurred, like ink touched by wet fingers. I remember the winter of it. I remember being desperate — I'd been caught charming a warden's daughter's cough away, and the choice was the Spire or something worse, and I went down into the mountain through the old mine-roads to bargain. I remember coming back up with my immunity and no memory of the transaction itself. Three days gone, like a drunk's blackout, except I don't drink. The ledger says only: *Paid in full. One soul. Received: protection, perpetual.*

One soul. Whose? I told myself for three years that it didn't matter, that whatever I'd given was already given, that digging would only bury me. This is the great talent of cowards: we can make a philosophy out of flinching.

I was halfway through counting the coat-lining when someone knocked. Three knocks, spaced wrong — too long between them, like whoever knocked had to remember how knocking worked between each attempt.

I should not have opened the door. Rule two exists precisely for moments like this. But it was raining, and I am weak in exactly one way, which is that I cannot bear the sound of a child standing in weather.

She was perhaps seven, maybe eight, small for either, soaked to the skin, barefoot, with hair the color of wet ash and eyes that were doing something eyes shouldn't do — catching the light and holding it a half-second too long, like coins at the bottom of a well. She looked at me the way you look at a door you've been walking toward for a very long time.

"No," I said, which is the correct answer to everything on Tallow Lane after dark.

She said something back. And here is where my night stopped being merely bad and became the kind of night that reorganizes a life: I understood her. Not guessed, not inferred — understood, the way you understand your own name. The sounds were nothing from any kingdom's tongue. They came out rounded and old, syllables like river stones worn smooth, and they landed in some part of me that predates my schooling, my trade, my name.

*I was told you keep accounts,* she said, in that impossible language. *I have come about an entry.*

I did the sensible thing. I closed the door.

Through it, muffled by rain, she kept talking, patient as tide. *You may open it when you are ready. I have waited longer than you have been alive. I do not mind waiting more.* There was no threat in it. That was the worst part. Threats I know how to handle; threats are just commerce with knives. This was certainty.

I opened the door, because of course I did.

She walked in past me like a landlord, dripped on my floor, crossed to the table, and put one small finger on my ledger. And the entries moved.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left now. The ink did not smear. The letters *walked* — lifted off the page like insects disturbed under bark and resettled into new formations, new words, in my own handwriting, dated three years ago. Entries I knew I had written. *Charm, silence, purchased from the widow Ashe. Charm against noticing. Payment rendered.* And then, at the bottom, the blurred entry sharpening into terrible legibility:

*Sold: one soul, female, age indeterminate. Buyer: the Court Beneath. Seller: Edmund Fairweather. Paid in full.*

*Age indeterminate.* Because souls don't come with ages. Because what I'd sold wasn't a child yet. It was a child *eventually* — a life queued up somewhere beneath the mountain, waiting three years to be born into the body now dripping on my floor, speaking the First Tongue, the language they say died with the mages they burned.

She looked up at me, and the well-coins of her eyes were, I realized, familiar. I had seen that gray before. In a mirror. Every morning of my life.

"You know what you are," I said, in plain Vellamarchi, uselessly.

*Yes,* she said. *Do you?*

I sat down. My legs made the decision without consulting me. She climbed onto the opposite chair, tucked her feet under herself, and watched me with the terrible patience of the very old wearing the very young.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and somewhere across the city the Spire stood in the dark, keeping its silence, keeping its ledger of everyone who owed it. Winter solstice was eleven days away. I did not yet know why that mattered. I only knew that the entry in my book had a date attached now, fresh-inked, in my own hand, and the date was the solstice, and beneath it, three words I had definitely never written:

*Payment comes due.*

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat across from the child while she ate the bread and dried fish I gave her with the manners of a small wolf, and I did arithmetic I didn't want to do. Sixty-one charms. Six years of immunity. One soul, sold by me, returned to me, sold *by* me — the ledger was ambiguous in a way that felt deliberate, like a contract drafted by something that enjoyed watching people read it.

Near dawn she fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew almost, and I covered her with my coat, sixty-one sins and all, and went to the window. Tallow Lane lay gray and empty. On the corner opposite, a Spire warden stood in the rain, not moving, not leaving, face turned toward my window with the fixed attention of a statue that has recently learned to blink.

They knew something. Or the mountain did, and the mountain tells the Spire what it likes.

Eleven days. I looked back at the sleeping child — at Elodie, she'd said her name was, though she said it the way you repeat a word someone else taught you — and I felt the dread finally stop pretending to be curiosity and show its true face. Because I understood, suddenly, completely, the way you understand your own name: whatever I had sold three winters ago, it had a face now. It spoke the First Tongue. And it had walked through the rain to my door to collect.

The question that remained, the one that turned my stomach over and set my hands shaking, was simple enough to fit in a ledger line:

Was I going to let it?
