## 2. What the Immunity Cost

The ledger did not lie, which was the whole trouble with it.

I sat up most of that first night with a candle guttering and the girl asleep in my one good chair, her feet tucked under her like a bird on a wire, and I read my own handwriting as though it belonged to a stranger. In a way it did. Three years of entries in Edmund Fairweather's neat taster's script — *one charm, minor, luck-bend, sold to the cooper's wife. One charm, hearing-sharpened, sold to the night-watchman who pretends he can't see me.* Small trades. Honest ones, if anything in this city could be called honest. And then, near the back, in ink darker than the rest, an entry I had no memory of writing:

*One soul, unclaimed, sold to the court beneath the mountain. Payment received: immunity, perpetual. The court remembers so you don't have to.*

I read it eleven times. On the twelfth reading I noticed the date at the bottom, and my hands went cold in a way soup-tasting had never managed: the winter solstice. Nineteen days away.

The girl stirred. "You're reading it wrong," she said, in that language no kingdom has used for four hundred years, and then, catching my face, repeated it in broken Common. "Wrong. Not wrong-words. Wrong-heart."

"Thank you," I said, wryly, because wryness is the last coin a frightened man spends. "I'll be sure to read it with more heart next time."

She frowned at me, unconvinced, and went back to sleep. That is the thing about children. They trust the world until the world gives them a reason not to, and someone — something — had already given her every reason, and still she slept in a stranger's chair like it was a hearth. I found I couldn't look at her and the ledger at the same time.

* * *

Elodie's memory came to her the way water comes through a cracked wall: not all at once, but in the shape of where it wanted to go.

There had been a bench. A very long bench, longer than any table, longer than any road she had walked since, and behind it sat figures whose faces were borrowed — she understood even then, small and cold in the dark of the court beneath the mountain, that the faces were borrowed because the dead magistrates had none of their own. They wore the faces of everyone who had ever bargained there. A woman's face wept without ceasing. A soldier's face smiled at nothing. And one face — young, tired, with ink stains on its fingers — looked down at her and said, kindly, in the First Tongue:

"You're the payment. Don't be afraid. He didn't know what he was selling."

But that was the lie, wasn't it? She hadn't known it was a lie then. She knew it now, sitting in the taster's chair, watching him read his own book by candlelight with his face going greyer page by page. She knew it the way you know a stone is heavy: by carrying it.

He had known. Somewhere under the forgetting, he had known, and he had signed anyway, because the alternative was the Spire's silence, and the silence was worse than any bargain. She did not hate him for it. That was the strange part, the part she turned over in the dark like a river-smoothed coin. She did not hate him. She only wanted her name back.

* * *

For nineteen days I became a man of two professions, and only one of them paid.

By day I tasted the magistrate's soup — leek and smoked eel that week, faintly oversalted, which I noted aloud because noting flaws is how a taster proves he isn't flattering — and I kept my eyes down and my ears open. The wardens were restless. Two of them came to the kitchen door asking after "unregistered persons," and the head cook said he'd seen nothing, and I agreed with him so smoothly that afterward I made myself sick thinking about how easily the lying came.

By night I hid Elodie behind a false wall in my cellar, among the charms I no longer dared sell, and I asked her questions in my terrible approximation of the First Tongue, and she answered in fragments that rearranged everything I thought I knew.

"The court doesn't keep souls in cages," she told me, drawing in the dust with one finger. "It keeps them in *pages*. Your page. While your writing stays, I stay."

"So if the page were destroyed—"

"Don't." Her finger stopped. "Don't say it like it's easy. The court lets you tear your own page. It wants you to. Because tearing it means giving back everything you bought." She looked up at me, and her eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should be. "Your immunity. Your name. Everything they wrote down about you. You'd be a man with no ledger at all."

"A man with no ledger," I said, "is a man nobody can bill."

She almost smiled. Almost. "You'd also be a man nobody remembers."

* * *

I want to record, here, honestly, what the immunity cost — not in souls, that accounting comes later, but in the small daily cowardice it bought me.

Three years ago I watched a warden beat a charm-seller half to death in the fish market, and I walked past, untouchable, immune, and told myself the man had been careless. Two years ago a widow begged me for a warming-charm for her consumptive daughter and I refused her, because selling meant risk, and risk meant testing my protection, and I had grown fond of living. Last winter the magistrate joked at dinner that it was a pity magic couldn't be useful, only punishable, and I laughed. I laughed, gentlemen, and the soup was excellent.

That is what immunity is. It is not safety. It is a debt with the interest paid in other people.

And now the principal had come due, walking to my door on bare feet, speaking the oldest language in the world, sleeping in my chair.

* * *

On the eighteenth day the wardens came to my street.

Not for me — for her. Someone had talked; someone always talks. I heard the knock pattern they use on doors they intend to open whether or not they're opened, and I moved Elodie out through the coal chute while the head of the household stalled them with complaints about his rheumatism. We spent that night in the abandoned mine-road at the city's edge, where the mountain begins its long descent into the dark, and she held my hand not because she was frightened but because I was, and she had decided, with a child's terrible clarity, to lend me some of her courage.

"They'll come again tomorrow," she said. "And the day after. There's only one direction left."

I looked down the mine-road, into the cold breathing dark of the mountain, and I thought about my ledger sitting safe at home, and my immunity sitting safe inside it, and the nineteen days burning down to one.

"I know," I said.

"It will wear your face," she warned me. "Down there. The thing on the bench. It wears the faces of everyone who bargained. When you see yourself, don't believe it. It only knows what you've written down. It doesn't know why."

"And do you?" I asked. "Know why?"

She considered this with great seriousness, the way she considered everything. "Yes," she said finally. "But you have to earn it. That's the rule of the First Tongue. Nothing true is free."

So we went down together, the soul-seller and the soul, into the mountain where the dead magistrates keep their court, and I carried no weapon, because there is no weapon against a contract — only the pen that signed it, and the hand that holds it, and whatever is left of a man when both are spent.

The last thing I saw of the daylight was the ledger-entry in my mind's eye, the dark ink, the neat script: *payment due, winter solstice.*

Tomorrow was the solstice.
