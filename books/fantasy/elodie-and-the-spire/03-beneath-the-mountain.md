## 3. Beneath the Mountain

The mine-roads under Vellharrow smell like a cellar where someone once kept secrets and then died of them. I want that noted: I am a food-taster by trade, and my professional opinion is that the dark beneath the mountain tastes of iron, old wax, and something faintly sweet that I refuse to identify because identifying it would mean admitting I recognize it.

Elodie is supposed to be asleep in my flat with the door barred twice and a charm — one of my last, a dull little thing that only makes a room *uninteresting* — pressed under the threshold.

She is not asleep. She is three steps behind me in the dark, breathing the way children breathe when they have decided that being caught walking is better than being left alone, and she has been there since the second turning.

"I know you're there," I said, an hour ago. "The First Tongue has no word for *quiet*, does it?"

She had answered in the First Tongue, which was not an apology but was, I suspected, a fairly accurate description of me.

So we walk together, the sold soul and the seller, down roads the miners abandoned before either of us was born. The lantern gutters. My ledger rides against my ribs like a second heart, and it is heavier than a book has any right to be. Three years of entries. Three years of small trades — a charm against fever for a widow's silence, a luck-knot for a guardsman's wink at the right door — all of it bought, if I'm honest, with the one large trade I don't remember making. The court below remembers it for me. That's the courtesy of the dead magistrates: they keep your receipts.

"Edmund," Elodie says. She has learned my name the way she learns everything — instantly, and as though she'd always known it and was merely being polite about the delay. "It will ask you a question."

"What question?"

"The one you already know the answer to." She looks up at me, and in the lantern light her eyes are the exact gray of the ledger's paper. "That's how it works. It doesn't trick anyone. It just makes you say it out loud."

Wonderful. A court that deals in honesty. In this city we burned our mages precisely to avoid anything of the sort.

* * *

The mine-roads end at a door that is not a door. It is more accurate to call it a decision — a place where the stone simply stops pretending there was ever any other way forward. Carved above the lintel, in letters older than the kingdom, older than the language they burned the mages for using:

**ALL WHO BARGAINED ENTER WHOLE. ALL WHO BARGAINED LEAVE LESS.**

"Cheerful," I said.

"You wrote that," Elodie said quietly.

I looked at the letters again. The chisel-work was fine, careful, the work of a man who had time and terror in equal measure. And the hand was — I knew that hand. I sign my poison-clearance forms with that hand every morning of my life.

I did not remember carving it. But then, I did not remember selling a child's soul, and both facts were sitting in front of me now like two halves of the same dish, waiting for the magistrate to taste them.

I took her hand. Her fingers were cold, and they closed around mine with the absolute confidence of someone who has already seen how this ends.

"Don't let go," I said.

"You will," she said. Not cruelly. Just accurately. Children who speak the First Tongue do not lie; it's the one advantage of being made of something older than lying.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain is lit by no flame I could name. The light comes from the walls themselves, which are lined — floor to unseen ceiling — with pages. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every contract ever struck in this city's long cowardly history, pressed into the stone like flowers, still faintly legible, still faintly *bleeding*.

And at the bench: the magistrates.

There were nine of them once, I think. Now there is one shape wearing nine robes, and the face it turns toward me is —

Mine.

Not a copy. Not a mask. It is my face as I look in the mirror on a bad morning, the lines around the eyes deeper, the mouth set in the particular way my mouth sets when I've tasted something in the soup and decided, for everyone's safety, to swallow anyway.

"Edmund Fairweather," said my own voice from across the bench. "Royal taster. Keeper of charms. Seller of one soul, lot number—" it consulted nothing; it simply knew— "one."

"I've come to void the contract," I said. My voice held. I was almost proud of it. "The sale was made without my full knowledge. Under duress, or deception, or — whatever it is you do down here to make a man forget his own signature. By any law worth the name, that's grounds for nullification."

The thing wearing my face considered this with my own thoughtful frown, which was deeply unpleasant, like watching a dog beg in a mirror.

"The law," it said, "was written by the desperate, to protect the desperate. You were desperate. Therefore the law protected you. The contract stands."

"Then show me the moment I signed it. Show me what I said. If I chose this — if some part of me stood in this room and traded a child for my own neck — then I'll carry it. But I don't remember choosing. And a choice that leaves no memory behind isn't a choice. It's a theft. *You* stole from *me* first."

For a long moment the court was silent, and in that silence I heard the pages on the walls rustling, all those old bargains shifting in their stone beds like sleepers having bad dreams.

Then my face smiled at me, gently, the way I smile at widows when the price must go up.

"There is a loophole," it said. "There is always a loophole. We are, after all, a court."

* * *

Here is the loophole, delivered in my own voice, with my own kindness, which is the cruelest instrument in this entire mountain:

The soul called Elodie was sold as payment. Payment may be returned. But a court cannot return payment without receiving equivalent value — that is the oldest law, older than the walls, older than the First Tongue itself. Equivalent value for a soul is: another soul, freely given, of equal weight.

Or.

Or the original buyer may reclaim the purchase by assuming the debt entire — keeping his immunity, keeping his name, keeping his life exactly as it was, provided the purchased soul remains below. Permanently. She would be safe, the court assured me, in its gentlest register. Safe, fed, taught, honored. The dead magistrates are excellent guardians. They have nothing else to do.

And I would walk out into the winter morning with everything intact. My position. My protections. My accumulated years of immunity, every charm, every entry in the ledger, all of it preserved — because the contract would finally be *complete*, sealed, satisfied, unimpeachable.

All it costs is her.

"That's not a loophole," I said. "That's the same trap with the paint touched up."

"It is the law," said my face.

"Yes," I said. "I know. I've read your walls."

Because here is the thing I understood standing in that light, with my own smile aimed at me across the bench: the walls are the law. Every page pressed into that stone is a bargain someone regretted and could not undo, and the court is not the magistrates at all. The court is the *pages*. The magistrates are just the part of us that signs.

And a page can be torn.

My ledger came out from under my coat. The court watched me open it with what I swear was curiosity — genuine, hungry curiosity, the look I get from people who've heard rumors about my little trade and want to know what a charm costs.

Three years of entries. The fever-charm for Widow Sarelle. The luck-knot. The immunity, line after line after line, each one stamped with the seal I didn't remember pressing. And near the back, in ink darker than the rest, the sale. Lot number one. A child. Price: one life, continued.

"You should know," I told the bench, "that I've spent three years building a wall between myself and the Spire. Entry by entry. It's good work. I'm proud of it. And I am about to find out whether any of it was worth more than her."

"Edmund." Elodie's hand tightened in mine. "You will forget yourself. The city will forget you. There will be no one left who—"

"There'll be you," I said. "You speak the First Tongue. It predates their ledgers. It predates *them*. Nothing written down here can touch a word that was never written down at all." I smiled at her, and it was, I hope, my real smile, the one I never used on magistrates. "Remember me badly, if you have to. Just remember."

I gripped the page.

The court spoke with every voice it had ever worn — hundreds of faces flickering across the bench now, the desperate and the damned, all of them saying *wait, wait, wait* — and I tore my own page out of the ledger, root and branch, name and seal and all.

The sound it made was very small. That's the thing no one tells you. All that dread, all those years, and in the end it comes away with a sound like a breath.

The light went out of the walls. The bench emptied. Somewhere far above, I felt the city begin, gently, to forget.

The last thing I saw was Elodie's face, and the last thing I heard was my own voice, growing thin, asking her to please — please — say the words over me, so that somewhere, in a tongue older than ledgers, I might still exist at all.

* * *

The mine-roads are cold on the way up.

A girl walks them alone, carrying a lantern and a ledger with one page missing, and she is speaking softly in a language no kingdom has ever outlawed, because no kingdom has ever been old enough to try.

She is practicing a name.
