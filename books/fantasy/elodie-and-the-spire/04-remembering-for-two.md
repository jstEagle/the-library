## 4. Remembering for Two

The morning the city forgot him, I woke up knowing two things: that the bread on the counter had gone stale overnight, and that Edmund Fairweather had never existed.

The second thing was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, and that is the whole trouble with ledgers — they only work if everyone agrees to read them the same way.

I came downstairs expecting to find him grumbling over his tea, complaining that the mountain's cold had followed him home and settled in his knees. Instead there was a stranger's house around me. Not a stranger's, exactly. A *nobody's* house. The kitchen was clean in the way rooms get clean when no one remembers who dirtied them. His coat was still on its hook, but it hung like an empty skin, like something waiting to be claimed by whoever could prove ownership, and no one in all of Corvane could prove anything about it, because there was nothing left to prove.

His ledger lay open on the table.

I want to be honest with you, since honesty is the only currency I have left: my first thought was not grief. My first thought was *the soup*. Three years of tasting poison for magistrates who smiled at him like he was furniture, and now they would not even remember to hate him. The Spire had taken his name out of every mouth in the city, and the cruelest part was how easily the mouths agreed. Grief came second. It has been coming second ever since, always a half-step behind some smaller, stupider feeling, and I have learned to let it catch up at night when there's nothing else scheduled.

* * *

Here is what the court beneath the mountain does not understand, and what I intend to spend my whole life making them regret:

The First Tongue predates their ledgers.

Their bookkeeping goes back to the founding of Corvane, to the first magistrate who decided that magic was theft and silence was justice. But the old words were old before there were mountains to put courts under. The old words were how the world learned its own name. You cannot strike a line through them, because they were never written down to begin with — they live in the mouth, and the mouth belongs to whoever is brave enough to open it.

So on that first morning, alone in a dead man's kitchen — not dead, *erased*, which is worse, because dead men get graves and graves get visitors — I said his name out loud in the First Tongue.

*Edmund.*

It did not echo. That's the thing people get wrong about magic. It doesn't echo. It *settles*, the way snow settles, quietly, everywhere, on everything. The word went into the walls. The word went into the stale bread. The word went into me, deeper than it had ever gone, and I felt it take root, and I understood that I was now the only soil in the city where this particular man could grow.

Fine. I have always been good at growing in poor conditions.

* * *

The wardens came on the third day.

Not for me — I was nobody's daughter, nobody's purchase, a soul returned to circulation with no entry to explain her. They came because houses remember. Even scrubbed ones. There was a residue in the floorboards where a charm-seller had kept his stock, a warmth under the stairs where he'd hidden children during the last purge, and warmth, in Corvane, is evidence.

The warden who knocked was young and polite the way they train them to be, all apology and iron. He asked if anyone lived here. I said yes. He asked who. And here is the trick, the small beautiful criminal trick that Edmund taught me without ever meaning to: I answered in the First Tongue.

*I live here,* I said. *With my teacher. Who is sleeping. Who should not be woken.*

He heard words. He heard old words, older than his office, older than the Spire, words that made the hair on his arms stand up for reasons his training could not file anywhere. And then he did what every man in this city does when confronted with something older than the law: he apologized and left.

The law is very powerful. It is not powerful enough to arrest a language.

* * *

By winter I had reopened the trade.

Not the way Edmund ran it — he was a coward, bless him, a careful, counting, head-down coward, and his caution bought him three years and cost a child her soul, so let no one write him up as a saint. He'd sell a charm for a coin and a secret, and keep both locked away. I run it differently. I run it the way the court runs its justice, except mine actually works.

A charm from me costs one word. Any word. A word in the First Tongue, spoken back to me so I know it will survive in another mouth. The baker's widow gave me *morning*. The chimney-boy gave me *run*, which I liked so much I gave him two charms for it. An old woman who remembered too much and pretended too little gave me *Edmund*, unprompted, in the old tongue, and I had to turn away and pretend to sort herbs while my eyes did something unbecoming.

She shouldn't have known it. No one should have known it. But she'd bought a charm from him once, years ago, and the word had lodged in her like a splinter the ledgers couldn't reach. That's the flaw in the Spire's great erasure, the crack I intend to widen until the whole wall comes down: you cannot delete what people paid for. Memory given freely cannot be repossessed. It can only be *outlived*, and I am very young, and I plan to do a great deal of outliving.

* * *

The ledger stays open on the table where he left it.

His entries are still there — every sale, every tasting, every small bribe against disaster — but the name column reads blank now, row after row after row, a column of absences shaped exactly like a man. Underneath, in fresh ink, in a hand the city doesn't recognize yet, I've begun a new page. At the top, where a name should go, I wrote mine in the First Tongue, because ink in the old script cannot be struck through; the Spire tried once, on a captured page, and the letters simply grew back, patient as moss.

Below my name, the heading: *Debts Owed.*

First entry: *One soul, returned whole. Paid in full by E.F., whose account remains open.*

Second entry: *One city, asleep. To be woken gently.*

Third entry: *One teacher, remembered by one student. Interest accruing daily.*

* * *

On solstice night, one year after he went down and did not come back up — or came back up as weather, as rumor, as the strange kindness strangers sometimes show me and can't explain — I opened the door to find three children huddled on my step, breath steaming, eyes doing that thing hidden children's eyes do: checking the street, checking my hands, checking whether I was safe or merely pretending.

The smallest one spoke first. Not in the trade-tongue. In the First Tongue, broken and half-remembered, the way you'd speak a lullaby your mother sang before she was taken.

*Are you the one who keeps the words?*

I looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, the city glittered with its lawful, forgetful lights, and beneath us, far beneath, something in the mountain turned a page and found it blank and did not understand why its hands were shaking.

*No,* I told her, stepping aside to let them in, out of the cold, into the warm residue of a man the world insists was never there. *I'm the one who keeps the debts. The words keep themselves.*

Then I lit the lamp, set out the stale bread — I buy it stale on purpose now; he'd have hated that, and I find I miss hating things about him — and opened the ledger to a clean page.

*Lesson one,* I wrote, in letters that cannot be erased. *Everything they buried, we say out loud.*
