## 1. The Date in the Margins

The moonsteel sang against Zephyrine's spine with every step, a thin cold note only they could hear.

It was a good sound. An honest sound. Moonsteel didn't lie the way people did; it hummed at the frequency of its own purity, and if anyone had tried to swap even one of the twelve ingots sewn into the lining of Zephyrine's coat for lead or tin or gilded iron, the song would have curdled. Twenty years on the Thornfell passes had taught Zephyrine to trust three things: the weight of honest metal, the memory of their own feet, and the silence of roads nobody else wanted.

Everything else was negotiable.

The pass above Kellin's Notch narrowed as the sun went down behind the western teeth of the mountains, and the light came down in long amber blades that turned the scree fields to hammered copper. Zephyrine walked it without thinking about it, which was the point. The body knew this road the way a tongue knows its own teeth. Left foot wide at the third cairn, where the frost heave had tilted the stone. Right hand out at the bend where the wind came off the glacier like something with an opinion. Twelve miles from the last waystation to the shrine at Hollowmere, and Zephyrine could have done it blindfolded, drunk, and being shot at—not that they'd ever been foolish enough to test all three at once.

That was the whole architecture of their life, if Zephyrine were honest about it, and honesty was a habit they indulged mostly alone: build everything on what you can carry, know every road you take, owe nothing to anyone that can't be paid in coin or silence. Choose your own roads. That was the creed. Not written anywhere, not sworn before any altar—just worn smooth by use, like the handle of a good knife.

The Ashen Court's writ-men had checkpoints on the southern passes now. Zephyrine had watched them from a ridge two days back, gray-cloaked clerks with ledgers chained to their belts, weighing and measuring and writing down the names of travelers who would never learn they'd been entered anywhere at all. So Zephyrine had gone north instead, the long way, the cold way, over the shoulder of Mount Cerenne where the air bit like a dog and the stars came out hard and close.

Their feet had chosen the road before their head did. That was how it always worked. That was how Zephyrine liked it.

* * *

The shrine at Hollowmere wasn't much to look at, which was why it survived.

It stood where the pass widened into a shallow bowl of frozen grass beside a pond that never quite thawed—a squat arch of gray fieldstone older than any kingdom Zephyrine could name, roofed in slates furred with lichen, its little alcove sheltering nothing but a stone bench and a niche where travelers had left offerings since before the Ashen Court had existed to disapprove of them. A rusted lantern hook. A scatter of candle stubs fused to the sill by decades of wax. Someone had carved a row of small notches into the doorpost, one per year, so long ago that weather had worn them to soft suggestions.

Zephyrine stopped there because there was water, and because the wind dropped in the lee of the arch, and because twenty years of habit said: *this is a place you can rest your eyes.*

They unhitched the waterskin, drank, and might have walked on in another minute if the light hadn't caught the book.

It lay on the bench. Not hidden, not displayed—just *there*, half under a drift of old leaves, as though someone had set it down to rest their hands and forgotten it for a very long time. It was small, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its corners worn round. The cover bore no title. Only a stamped device, faint as a watermark: a door, and beneath the door, a sword laid flat.

Zephyrine should have left it. That was the rule of the passes—take nothing you didn't bring, because everything up here belonged either to the mountain or to someone desperate enough to have carried it. But the wind had gone still in that particular way wind does when it wants you to notice something, and the moonsteel along Zephyrine's spine had changed its note.

Not curdled. Not warning off a fake. Something stranger—the twelve ingots humming a half-step higher, like instruments tuning to a pitch they recognized.

"Well," Zephyrine said aloud, to the shrine, to the pond, to no one. "That's new."

They picked up the book.

It was heavier than it looked, the way old things are heavy—with more than paper and board inside them. The leather was cool despite the day's lingering warmth. Zephyrine thumbed it open and found the pages dense with handwriting, brown ink faded to the color of weak tea, a script formal and cramped, the work of someone who wrote the way masons build: to last.

It was a ledger of dates. That was the first thing Zephyrine understood. Page after page of dates, each with a few lines beneath it, and the lines were—

Zephyrine's mouth went dry.

*Prophecies.* Or the closest thing to prophecies Zephyrine had ever seen outside of fairground frauds. Each entry named a day, and beneath each day, a sentence in that same mason's hand, terse as a knife:

*On this night the ferryman of Greywater drowns, and his boat crosses empty.*

*On this night the bell of Saint Vess cracks along its third ring.*

*On this night a red-haired child is born in Lowmarsh who will not die of fever, though fever comes for her twice.*

And here was the thing that made the hair rise along Zephyrine's arms, the thing that made them glance up at the empty pass as though the mountains themselves might be reading over their shoulder: the margins. Someone else had come through this book after its author died—or stopped—and had written in the margins in a different hand, a looser, quicker script, checking the entries off.

*Confirmed. Confirmed. Confirmed.* Beside the bell of Saint Vess: *I heard it myself. Third ring. God forgive me.* Beside the red-haired child: *She lives. I have seen her selling fish in Lowmarsh market, hale as a horse.*

A century of checked boxes. A century of nights that some dead hand had named in advance, and some other, later hand had verified, one trembling confirmation at a time.

Zephyrine stood in the lee of the shrine with the book open on their palm and felt the first true cold of the evening, and it had nothing to do with the altitude.

Because there were pages near the end that hadn't been confirmed yet. Pages with dates that hadn't arrived. And the handwriting in those unarrived entries was different again—not the mason's careful brown ink, not the margin-writer's quick scrawl, but a third hand, sharp and slanting, pressed so hard into the paper that the letters showed through to the reverse side, as though whoever wrote them had been angry, or afraid, or both.

Zephyrine turned to the last written page.

The date at its head was tonight.

Not approximately. Not in the vague way fortune-tellers hedge, *when the moon is thus*. The exact date, written out in full, the month and the numbered day and—even now, even with the cold walking up Zephyrine's neck—the day of the week. Tonight. This specific rotation of the world, this specific dark coming down over the Thornfells while Zephyrine stood holding a dead stranger's book in a dead stranger's shrine.

Beneath the date, four lines, in that sharp pressing hand:

*A swordspeaker walks the high road carrying moonsteel, and pauses at the shrine of Hollowmere.*

*A passenger comes east with fire in her blood, and hires what she cannot buy.*

*The door of Vaelthorne opens, or the last of the fire goes out.*

*The swordspeaker's name is Zephyrine Nystrom.*

The pond made a sound. Ice shifting, Zephyrine told themselves. Just ice shifting, out there in the dark where they couldn't see it.

They read it again. Then a third time, the way you reread a ransom note hoping the words will rearrange themselves into something survivable. The words declined to rearrange. *Zephyrine Nystrom*, spelled out letter by letter, in ink a hundred years old, in a book that had been sitting on a bench in a shrine their feet had chosen on a whim—an hour ago? Two? They hadn't decided on Hollowmere until the fork at Cerenne's shoulder, hadn't truly decided until their boots were already pointing here. Nobody could have known that. Nobody could have known that unless—

Unless the choosing had never been theirs.

Zephyrine set the book down on the bench with exaggerated care, the way you set down a snake, and took two steps back into the open air of the pass, and breathed.

The mountains didn't care. That was the thing about the Thornfells—they were magnificent and indifferent, and Zephyrine had built twenty years of peace on that indifference. The peaks didn't watch. The roads didn't mean anything. A road was just a scar on the land where enough feet had agreed to walk, and Zephyrine had spent their whole life agreeing nowhere except by their own choice.

Except.

Except that the moonsteel was still singing that strange tuned-up note against their spine, and the book was still lying open on the bench, and the wind had come back around the arch in a slow deliberate circle, riffling the pages forward, past the entry for tonight, to the next page.

Which was blank.

Then past that. Blank. Blank. Blank—all the rest of the book, empty as snowfield, right up to the back cover. As though whatever hand had filled it had written exactly as far as it intended to go, and no further. As though the story had a shape already, and the shape ended somewhere Zephyrine couldn't see.

"No," Zephyrine said to it.

The book, being a book, said nothing. That was somehow worse.

They paced. Ten paces toward the pond and ten paces back, the old rhythm, the rhythm that had untangled a hundred bad situations—ambushes, betrayals, one memorable avalanche. Name the problem. Weigh the exits. There were always exits; that was the whole faith Zephyrine practiced. Leave the book. Burn the book. Walk south to Kellin's Notch and be someone the book had misnamed, a coincidence, a case of mistaken identity—how many swordspeakers walked these passes carrying moonsteel, after all? How many named Nystrom?

One. Zephyrine knew the answer was one, because the swordspeakers were a dead order, an extinct thing, oath-bound blade-speakers of a country that had been erased from every map and most memories before Zephyrine was born. Their grandmother had been one, and had taught them the old words in secret, in whispers, with the shutters closed—and had wept doing it, though Zephyrine hadn't understood then why a woman would cry over beautiful language. *You're the last,* she'd said. *God make it otherwise, but you're the last.* And when she died she'd left Zephyrine a sword they'd never once spoken to, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a lockbox in a cellar in Greyharbor, because speaking to it felt like answering a summons Zephyrine hadn't read.

So: one swordspeaker. One name. Written down a hundred years ago in a book that had waited on a bench for exactly the wrong traveler.

Zephyrine stopped pacing.

The dread wasn't fear of the Court, or of ambush, or of anything with a face. It was worse than that, quieter, colder: it was the feeling of looking down at your own life and finding footprints already there. Every road Zephyrine had ever taken, every choice they'd prided themselves on—the fork at Cerenne's shoulder, the shrine, the pause, the reaching out—had it been choosing? Or had it been *following*, tracing lines some dead hand had drawn in brown ink and sharp black, a character in a story walking obediently through scenes someone else had plotted?

A life built on choosing its own roads, and suddenly it had an author.

Zephyrine looked at the sky. The stars over the Thornfells were hard and white and arranged in patterns men had named, and for the first time in twenty years Zephyrine wondered whether the naming had been discovery or assignment—whether the stars had chosen their shapes or merely been written into them.

"Right," they said finally, voice rough in the cold. "Options."

Option one: leave the book on the bench, walk east, deliver the moonsteel, collect the pay, and spend the rest of their life flinching at benches. Except the entry hadn't ended with the shrine. *A passenger comes east with fire in her blood.* Fire in her blood. Zephyrine had smuggled a lot of things—moonsteel, salt-pearls, once an entire fugitive tax clerk rolled in a carpet—but never fire in anyone's blood, and the phrase sat in the mind like a splinter. If the book was true—if the margin-writer's hundred confirmations meant anything—then tomorrow, or the day after, someone was going to find Zephyrine and hire passage east. And Zephyrine was going to say yes. Because the book said so. Because refusing felt suddenly like trying to argue with gravity.

Option two: burn it. Zephyrine actually knelt and gathered dry grass and got as far as striking flint before the practical objection stopped their hand: burning a book that predicted the future didn't unwrite the future. It only unwrote your copy of it. Somewhere out there, the passenger with fire in her blood was already traveling, already real, already burning whoever touched her, and Zephyrine would meet her knowing nothing—which, the book implied, was not how the story was meant to be survived.

Option three.

Zephyrine looked at the blank pages. All that whiteness, waiting. Whatever the author had planned, it wasn't finished—and the last written line wasn't an ending, it was a hinge: *the door of Vaelthorne opens, or the last of the fire goes out.* Vaelthorne. Even the name tasted strange, like a word from a dream—Zephyrine knew it the way you know a song you've heard through a wall, grandmother's whispered syllables, a country with no map and no grave and no survivors that anyone admitted to. A door that opened. A fire that went out. A swordspeaker whose name was theirs.

The flint clicked shut in Zephyrine's fist. The grass scattered, unburned.

"Fine," they said to the book, to the shrine, to the author they were beginning to hate with a clean and useful hatred. "You get tonight. Tomorrow I choose my own road."

But even as they said it, sitting down on the cold stone bench with the book open across their knees and the moonsteel humming its strange tuned note against their spine, Zephyrine knew the sound of a lie. They'd heard enough lies in twenty years of smuggling to know one in their own mouth. Tomorrow they would walk east, because east was where the passes went, and the passes were where the passenger would come from, and the dread had already settled into their chest with the patience of a tenant paying rent.

Far down the valley, where the dark had pooled thickest, a light moved. Small. Lone. Coming up the pass toward Hollowmere with the slow, staggering determination of someone on foot who has been walking a long time and cannot stop.

Zephyrine closed the book on the entry for tonight and held it against their chest, and watched the light come, and did not run.

Later—much later, in the years when children asked her how the story began—Elodie Vasse would say it began with fire. But Zephyrine Nystrom, sitting alone in a roadside shrine with a dead author's prophecy open on their knees, would always have answered differently.

It began with a date.
