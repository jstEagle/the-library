## 2. A Passenger with Fire in Her Blood

The inn at Grayhollow had a name once, Zephyrine supposed, but the paint had flaked off the sign so long ago that only the ghost of the letters remained—a pale scab of white where *something* had been written, and beneath it, the honest truth: bad ale, worse beds, and a landlord who asked no questions. That was why Zephyrine drank there. Questions were a smuggler's tax, and Zephyrine paid them nowhere.

They sat in the corner with their back to the wall and their eyes on the door, as they always did, turning a cup of ale that tasted like rainwater strained through old boots. Outside, the Thornfell mountains stood black against a bruised evening sky, their passes already filling with the kind of dark that ate travelers whole. Somewhere up in those passes was a cache of moonsteel worth more than the entire town, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden under a cairn that looked like every other cairn in the range. Somewhere down in the lowlands were buyers who would pay for it without asking where it came from. Between those two points lay Zephyrine's entire life: a road, walked alone, chosen freely.

*Chosen freely.* The words had a bitter taste now, ever since the shrine. Ever since the book.

Three days ago Zephyrine had paused at the roadside shrine at the mouth of the Kestrel Pass—the same shrine they'd passed a hundred times, the little stone alcove with its rain-worn saint and its offering bowl full of pinecones and copper bits—and had found, wedged behind the saint's chipped shoulder, a book. Old leather gone soft as cloth. A century of dust. And inside, in a hand that looped and slanted like wind through wheat, a prophecy written by someone long dead, whose margins named dates, and places, and—Zephyrine's thumb still remembered the cold shock of it—a name. *Zephyrine Nystrom.* Their own name, written down before their grandmother's grandmother drew her first breath, in ink that had dried while the world still remembered a country called Vaelthorne.

They had left the book at the shrine. They had told themselves it was superstition, a trick, a coincidence of names. They had walked six miles toward the lowlands before turning back, because a smuggler who leaves valuable things lying in open shrines is not a careful smuggler, and because—though they'd have cut out their own tongue before admitting it—they needed to see it again. To touch the page. To confirm that the letters hadn't rearranged themselves into something kinder.

They hadn't. The name was still there. The date was still there. And tonight's date, according to the almanac nailed above the Grayhollow inn's hearth, was tomorrow.

So Zephyrine sat in the corner with their back to the wall, drinking bad ale, watching the door, and waiting for whatever the dead author of that book had scheduled to walk through it.

It came at moonrise, in the shape of a woman.

She came in sideways, the way people do when they've been running for days and their bodies have forgotten any other way to move. Hooded, mud to the knees, a travel cloak that had been good quality once and was now ruined beyond pretending otherwise. She scanned the room the way Zephyrine scanned rooms—fast, professional, cataloguing exits—and then she saw Zephyrine, and something in her posture changed. Not relief. Recognition. Which was worse, because Zephyrine had never seen her before in their life.

Except that wasn't quite true either. There was something about the set of her shoulders, the angle of her jaw beneath the hood, that snagged against Zephyrine's memory like a fishhook. The book. The margins. The second figure sketched in faded ink beside the first—a slighter silhouette, hooded, with fire drawn around its hands in strokes of red ochre that had bled into the paper over a hundred years until the flames looked like they were still burning.

The woman crossed the room and stopped at Zephyrine's table. Up close she was young—younger than Zephyrine had guessed, twenty perhaps, maybe less—but her eyes had the flat, exhausted watchfulness of someone much older. Someone who had not slept properly in a week.

"You're the swordspeaker," she said. Not a question.

"I'm a freight handler," Zephyrine said. "I move goods. Quietly."

"Your sword says otherwise." Her gaze flicked to the blade at Zephyrine's hip—an unremarkable thing, plain scabbard, plain hilt, deliberately forgettable. "It hums. Can't you hear it? It's been humming since I walked in."

Zephyrine could, actually. A faint vibration in the metal, like a struck bell heard through a wall. The sword did that sometimes, near certain people, near certain places, and Zephyrine had spent years perfecting the art of ignoring it. "It's the wind through the shutters," they said. "Sit down or don't. If you're buying passage, state your cargo and your destination. If you're not, find another table."

The woman sat. She pulled back her hood, and Zephyrine saw her fully for the first time: dark hair cropped short and unevenly, as if she'd done it herself with a knife; a face that might have been pretty if it weren't stretched so thin over the bones; and on her right hand, from fingertip to wrist, a tracery of fine scars, old and new, some silvered with age and some raw and pink. Burn scars. Dozens of them. Layered like tree rings.

"My name is Elodie Vasse," she said. "I need to go east. Through the Thornfell. As far east as you can take me."

"East is a big place."

"Further than that." She leaned forward, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "There's a door. In the mountains. An old one. I need to reach it."

Zephyrine kept their face still, which took effort, because every hair on their arms had just stood up. *A door.* The book had spoken of a door. The margins had named it in words that hurt to read, words in the old tongue that seemed to squirm away from the eye.

"I don't know anything about doors," Zephyrine said. "I know about passes. Rates are ten crowns to the far side of the range, twenty if we travel by night, thirty if anyone's chasing you."

"Someone is chasing me."

"Then thirty."

"I can pay forty." She reached into her cloak and set a purse on the table. It clinked with the heavy, muffled sound of gold, real gold, more gold than a girl in a ruined cloak should be carrying. "But I need to leave tonight. Before the moon sets."

Zephyrine looked at the purse. Looked at her. Looked at the burn scars climbing out of her collar where the cloak gaped—more of them, climbing her throat like ivy. And then Elodie Vasse made her mistake, or Zephyrine made theirs; afterward neither of them could agree which. She reached across the table to take back the purse after Zephyrine didn't move fast enough, and her bare fingers brushed the back of Zephyrine's hand.

The pain was instant and total. Like pressing a palm to a forge door. Zephyrine jerked back, knocking the ale over, and the whole common room turned to look, and Zephyrine didn't care, because the back of their hand was *blazing*, a red welt rising in the exact shape of four fingertips and a thumb.

Elodie had gone white. "I'm sorry," she breathed. "I'm sorry—I should have warned you—I don't—" She was pulling her sleeve down over her own hand, hiding the scars, hunching inward like something trying to make itself small enough to disappear. "I'll pay for a salve. I'll pay double. I never touch anyone. I haven't touched anyone in a year. I wasn't thinking."

"What *are* you?" Zephyrine hissed, cradling their hand.

And Elodie Vasse looked at them with those exhausted, ancient young eyes, and said, in a voice stripped of everything except truth:

"I'm what's left of Vaelthorne."

* * *

They talked in the stable loft afterward, because Zephyrine refused to say another word indoors, and because the loft had one ladder and a window facing the pass road, and Zephyrine trusted ladders and windows more than walls.

Elodie sat on a hay bale with her hands wrapped in strips of linen—gloves, effectively, though she said the gloves barely helped, that the fire came through cloth eventually, that everything came through eventually—and she told her story in pieces, in the flat reciting voice of someone who has told it before and been believed by no one.

She was, she said, the granddaughter of the last heir of Vaelthorne. She said the word the way people say the names of the dead. When Zephyrine didn't react—which cost them something, to keep their face empty while the prophecy's margins burned behind their eyes—she pressed on. A hundred years ago, she said, the Ashen Court had come for Vaelthorne. Not with armies, at first. With clerks. With ledgers. With writs and seals and a great slow bureaucratic erasure, until the country was struck from maps, its name struck from records, its people scattered or sworn silent, and its treasure—the last living dragon-fire in the world—seized for the Court's own purposes.

"But they didn't get all of it," Elodie said. She unwound the linen from her right hand.

Beneath, in the meat of her palm, buried under the skin like a coal under ash, something glowed. Faintly. A deep orange light, pulsing slow, like breath, like a heartbeat. Zephyrine felt the sword at their hip shiver against their thigh, humming now in earnest, a note that climbed up their spine and settled behind their teeth.

"It doesn't burn me," Elodie said. "Only everyone else. My grandmother carried it before me. And her mother before her. We're not vessels exactly. More like... hearths. The fire lives in a family, and each generation it chooses one person to keep it warm." She wound the linen back around her hand, and the glow went out, and the humming sank to a murmur. "My grandmother died in the spring. The night she died, it moved into me. And three days later, the Ashen Court's men arrived asking questions about the Vasse family. So I ran."

"To hire a smuggler."

"To hire *you*." She met Zephyrine's eyes. "The old women in my village still tell stories, even the ones who pretend they don't remember Vaelthorne. They say when the end comes—if it ever comes—it will come with a swordspeaker walking beside the fire-bearer. They describe the swordspeaker. Dark-haired. Scarred left eyebrow. Walks with a limp in cold weather." She paused. "It rained the day I arrived in Grayhollow. You limped off the pass road."

Zephyrine's scarred eyebrow itched. Their knee, cold-damaged years ago in a crevasse fall, ached dully, as it always did at altitude. They said nothing.

"There's a book," Elodie went on, softer now. "An old prophecy. About the door, and the fire, and the swordspeaker. My grandmother told me about it when I was small. She said the Ashen Court burned every copy they could find. She said if I ever met someone who matched the description, I shouldn't think about it, I should just run to them, because fate doesn't send invitations twice." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I've been looking for you for four months. Every smuggler's den, every crossroads inn between here and the coast. And today I walked into this tavern, and there you were, exactly as the stories say, and your sword started singing the moment our eyes met."

"It hums," Zephyrine said automatically. "Swords don't sing."

"This one does. You just don't want to hear it."

The stable was quiet except for the horses shifting below, and the wind worrying the loose shingles, and the faint, persistent hum of a blade that Zephyrine had carried for eleven years and never once thought of as anything other than a tool. They looked at Elodie Vasse—at the burn scars layered on her hands like the rings of a felled tree, at the linen wrappings, at the exhaustion carved into her young face—and felt the trap closing around them with a click they could almost hear.

Because here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: Zephyrine Nystrom had built a life on choosing. Choosing routes, choosing cargoes, choosing clients, choosing solitude. Every mile of every road theirs alone to pick. And now a dead stranger's handwriting claimed to have picked for them—a hundred years in advance, in ink, in a margin—and the girl sitting on the hay bale fit the sketch beside their name the way a key fits a lock, and the sword wouldn't stop humming, and the date in the almanac was tomorrow.

"I'm not a hero," Zephyrine said finally. "I'm not a chosen instrument. I'm a smuggler. I move moonsteel and stolen relics and occasionally fugitives, for money, and then I leave them wherever they're going and I don't look back. Whatever your old women say, whatever your book says, I am not bound to you. I don't even know you."

"I know," Elodie said. "I'm not asking you to be bound to me. I'm asking you to walk east. Forty crowns. One job." She held out the purse, carefully, by its drawstring, keeping her wrapped hand well back. "Please. They'll find me otherwise. And what they do to the fire..." She swallowed. "They won't kill me, swordspeaker. That's the worst part. They'll put it in a ledger."

Something in Zephyrine's chest went tight and cold. They thought of the book behind the saint's chipped shoulder, the looping handwriting, the margins that knew their name. They thought of a country erased by clerks, ground into ledger-lines, its fire locked in an archive somewhere, glowing in the dark like a prisoner. They thought of their own life, every road self-chosen, and how much they had loved it, and how suddenly, horribly, it felt like a life that had been on loan.

They thought about saying no. They rehearsed it, actually—*take your gold and find another fool*—and the words were right there, ready, easy.

Instead they heard themselves say: "Fifty. And you carry your own pack. And when we reach wherever we're reaching, we part ways, and I never hear the word 'prophecy' again as long as I live."

Elodie blinked. Then, slowly, she smiled—the first smile Zephyrine had seen on her, tired and crooked and disbelieving, like sun through smoke. "Fifty," she agreed.

"Fifty," Zephyrine said, and took the purse, and hated how easily their hand closed around it, as if some part of them had decided before the rest of them got a vote.

* * *

They left within the hour, while the moon still hung above the eastern peaks like a lamp in a high window. Zephyrine led, as always, taking the shepherd's track north of the pass road—slower, steeper, invisible from below. Elodie followed without complaint, her breathing steady despite her exhaustion, her wrapped hands tucked inside her cloak. Behind them Grayhollow shrank to a scatter of embers in the valley dark.

At the first switchback, Zephyrine stopped and looked back—not at the town, but at the woman climbing behind them, silhouetted against the stars, small and stubborn and burning underneath her skin.

This is a job, Zephyrine told themselves. Fifty crowns. East through the mountains. Nothing more.

But the sword at their hip had begun, very softly, unmistakably, to sing—a thread of sound too low for any ear but the one it was made for, a melody in the old tongue that Zephyrine had never learned and yet somehow knew. And far below, on the pass road they had avoided, torchlight bloomed: a column of riders, moving fast, moving east, carrying banners that even at this distance looked gray as ash.

Zephyrine watched them for a long moment. Then they turned up the mountain, toward the dark and the door and the date waiting in the margins, and said quietly, to no one, to the girl behind them, to the hundred-year-old ink:

"Keep up."

Elodie laughed once, breathless, and followed.
