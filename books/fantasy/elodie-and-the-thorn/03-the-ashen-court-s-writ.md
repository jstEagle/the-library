## 3. The Ashen Court's Writ

Elodie Vasse had been running for eleven days before she understood that she was not the one being chased.

She had thought, in those first frantic hours after leaving her uncle's house with nothing but a satchel of bread and a lie sewn into her collar, that the riders on the eastern road were looking for *her* — for the runaway heir of a minor house, a girl with an unremarkable face and a remarkable inheritance of debt. That was a chase she could understand. Girls ran away all the time. Girls were dragged back, married off, locked in towers, and the world went on turning as it always had.

But the man who came to Marrowgate on the twelfth day was not looking for a girl.

He was looking for a thing.

* * *

She saw him first from the window of the inn where Zephyrine had gone to negotiate passage east — a tall figure in ash-grey robes standing very still in the middle of the market square while everything around him moved. Marrowgate's market was a churning thing, all noise and elbows, fishwives shouting down fruit-sellers, carters swearing at goats, children threading between legs like minnows. The magistrate stood in the center of it the way a stone stands in a river, and the crowd parted around him without seeming to know why.

He wore no armor. He wore grey, and gloves, and carried a ledger under one arm with the casual reverence of a priest carrying scripture. Two knights flanked him — not the gilded parade-riders of the southern courts but hard, plain men in scorched steel, their tabards bearing a device Elodie had only ever seen stamped on tax documents and death warrants: a closed book, its pages sealed with wax.

"Ashen Court," she whispered, though there was no one to hear. The word came out of her like something swallowed wrong.

The magistrate raised one gloved hand, and the market *stopped*. Not slowly, not with confusion — it stopped the way a clock stops, all its parts freezing mid-motion, so that for three heartbeats the whole square held its breath around him. Then he opened his ledger, and his voice carried without effort to every corner of it, dry and precise and terribly reasonable.

"By writ of the Ashen Court, in the matter of the estate of Vaelthorne, dissolved by lawful entry these hundred years past: notice is given that certain property of said estate remains outstanding. Said property is described as follows."

A pause. Pages turned. Somewhere a fishwife's basket tipped and rolled, scattering silver-scaled catch across the cobbles, and no one moved to gather it.

"Said property is contained within a living vessel. The vessel is female, of age sixteen to twenty years, last recorded in the company of a smuggler operating out of the Thornfell passes. The property is to be recovered intact. The vessel is to be recovered *intact*." A faint emphasis, like a clerk underlining a line twice. "Compensation will be rendered to any party assisting in recovery. Penalties will be assessed against any party obstructing it. So it is written. So it is entered."

He closed the book. The market exhaled and resumed its noise, and people began — Elodie watched it happen with a horror that felt almost clinical — people began to *look at each other*. At their neighbors. At strangers. At the young women in the crowd, one by one, with the particular apologetic hunger of people doing arithmetic they did not want to do.

Elodie stepped back from the window.

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them, at the fine blue tracery of veins beneath the skin, and beneath that, deeper than blood, the coal. Her grandmother had pressed it into her the night before the end — had cupped Elodie's face in both hands and breathed something into her mouth that tasted like a struck match and a fever and a lullaby all at once, and had said, *carry this, little ember, carry it where the ash cannot follow.* Elodie had spent eleven days telling herself it was a gift. An inheritance. A grandmother's love made into armor.

Standing at that window, watching a hundred ordinary people begin to calculate what her capture might earn them, she understood for the first time that it was neither gift nor armor.

It was a price tag.

* * *

Zephyrine came back up the stairs two at a time, which for a person of their usual unhurried economy meant something was badly wrong. They were already pulling their coat off the peg, already counting coin back out of the purse they'd apparently just emptied.

"Change of plans," they said. "We leave now, through the stable yard, and we don't stop until—"

"I heard him." Elodie's voice came out steadier than she felt. "From the window. I heard the whole writ."

Zephyrine stopped. Looked at her. And Elodie watched something move behind their eyes — not pity, exactly; she'd seen pity before and knew its soft uselessness. This was closer to recognition. The look of someone confirming a suspicion they'd been carrying since a roadside shrine.

"Then you know," Zephyrine said quietly, "that I'm now officially obstructing a writ of the Ashen Court. Which is a hanging offense in three provinces and a burning offense in the other two." They shrugged the coat on. "Good thing I never much liked those provinces."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Zephyrine agreed, buckling their sword-belt with quick, sure movements. "It isn't. It's also not complicated. You paid me to take you east. I took your money. I don't return money, and I don't return passengers. Both habits have kept me alive longer than wisdom would have." They glanced at the window, at the square below, and their jaw tightened. "What I *didn't* expect was for the Court to move this fast. Someone told them which town. Someone told them which road." Their eyes flicked to Elodie, brief and unreadable. "Which means someone's been paid too. So. Stable yard. Now."

They were halfway down the stairs when the first scream came from the square.

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to take the memory apart and examine each piece — Elodie would understand that the fire had not started because of her. The Ashen Court's knights had cornered a girl in the wool-seller's stall, some merchant's daughter of roughly the right age, and when the girl's father objected, a knight had put him down with the flat of a blade and then, when he kept objecting, with the edge. The father's blood had gone into a brazier of coals set out for warming mulled wine. The brazier had gone over. The wool-seller's stock had caught.

That was how it started. Not with her. With a stranger's grief and a stranger's carelessness and a market full of dry goods in a dry autumn wind.

But Elodie would never fully believe that. Because in the moment it happened — in the moment the smoke began to climb and the screaming changed pitch from outrage to terror — the coal beneath her skin had *answered*. It had risen toward the heat like a face turning toward sunlight, and she had felt it press outward against the inside of her ribs, eager, joyful, hungry, and for one vertiginous second she had not known whether the fire below was the world's or hers.

"Elodie!" Zephyrine's hand closed on her arm — and snatched away instantly, palm smoking faintly, and they swore in three languages and shook their fingers. "Right. Still can't touch you. Wonderful. *Run.*"

They ran.

Not down — the stairwell was already filling with panicking guests and the street beyond it was filling with fire. Zephyrine hauled her up instead, up past the rented rooms, up a ladder-hatch to the flat tar roof, out into air thick with smoke and the smell of burning tallow from the chandler's shop next door.

"The roofs," Zephyrine gasped. "Marrowgate's built close. Roofs nearly touch for six streets if you know the gaps. Do you trust me?"

"Do I have a choice?"

"You always have a choice. That's the whole point of you." And there was something in the way they said it — something that sounded less like a smuggler's quip and more like a line from a book Elodie had never read — but there was no time to ask, because Zephyrine took three steps, measured the gap between this roof and the chandler's with a professional eye, and jumped.

Elodie stood at the edge. Below her, through the smoke, she could see the square. The magistrate still stood in its center, untouched by the chaos, unbothered by the fire spreading toward his position with what seemed like polite disinterest. He had not moved to help. He had not moved to flee. He had simply opened his ledger again and was writing in it, calm as a man taking minutes at a meeting, recording the destruction of a market town the way one records rainfall.

And beside him, one of the knights pointed upward. Toward the roofline. Toward her.

"*There!*" The shout cut through the smoke. "*Vessel sighted, roofline, moving east!*"

The coal in Elodie's chest flared white-hot with something that was not fear. It took her a moment, standing on that burning edge, to name the feeling, because she had never been permitted to feel it before: it was *anger*. Pure, clean, incandescent anger — at the word *vessel*, at the ledger, at the neat grey man writing her into a column, at a hundred years of dead country and a grandmother's dying breath and every hand that had ever decided what she was before asking what she wanted.

"They called me a *thing*," she said aloud, to no one, to the smoke.

"Yeah," Zephyrine called back from the next roof, holding out a hand they couldn't safely touch. "Get used to being angry. It's fuel. Now *jump*, ember, before the fuel runs out of roof!"

She jumped.

* * *

They crossed Marrowgate the way a spark crosses a powder store — roof to roof, gap to gap, Zephyrine ahead calling distances in a low running murmur (*three feet, easy — five, mind the loose slate — don't look down, look at me*) while behind them the town burned and the Court's knights swarmed the streets like ants whose hill had been kicked. Twice they heard boots on the roofs behind them and had to double back through a bell-tower, dropping down inside it past the great silent bronze, Elodie's palms leaving faint scorch-marks on the ladder rails. Once a knight got ahead of them, waiting on a rooftop junction with his sword drawn and his face grimly apologetic, and Zephyrine did something with a knife and a length of rope and a chimney that Elodie genuinely could not follow, and afterward the knight was hanging upside down from a gutter spout, unconscious, and Zephyrine was saying "don't ask" before she could.

The fire followed them. Or they followed the fire. By the time they reached the eastern edge of town, half of Marrowgate was alight, and Elodie kept catching glimpses of it through gaps between buildings — the orange bloom of it, the black petals of smoke — and every time she looked, the coal turned toward it like a child toward a mother's voice.

"It wants it," she panted, crouched behind a chimney pot while Zephyrine scouted the drop to the livestock pens below. "The fire. It wants the burning. Is that — is that normal? Is that what it does?"

Zephyrine came back, crouched beside her, and for a moment didn't answer. Smoke wreathed their face; their eyes, when she could see them, were old and tired and strangely gentle.

"I found a book in a shrine two weeks ago," they said. "Hundred years old. Prophecy. My name in the margin, tonight's date, and yours next to mine. Second figure. *The ember that walks.*" They said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report weather. "I've spent my whole life choosing my own roads, and I hate — *hate* — being handed a script. But I'll tell you what I've worked out since. Whatever that fire is, it didn't choose you either. Somebody chose *for* you. And the people hunting you? They're not hunting a runaway girl. They're hunting an entry in a ledger that won't balance until they've got you." They stood, offered their smoking-wrapped hand — they'd bound it in strips of their own shirt — and grinned, sharp and crooked. "So here's my professional opinion, for what it's worth. Let 'em chase the vessel. We'll go find the person."

Below them, the pens. Beyond the pens, the dark shoulder of the Thornfell foothills, and somewhere east of that, a door that a country had died behind.

Elodie took the wrapped hand. Even through the cloth, warmth bloomed — not pain, not yet, just heat, like holding a cup of tea poured by someone who loved you.

Behind them, Marrowgate burned. Ahead, the mountains waited. And somewhere in the smoke below, unhurried, unbothered, a grey-robed magistrate closed his ledger, having finished his entry, and turned his face toward the passes.

He knew where they were going. Elodie felt it with a certainty colder than fear, colder than the coal's heat was hot: the writ had named the Thornfell. The Court had always known about the door.

They weren't chasing her *away* from something.

They were herding her *toward* it.
