## 5. Oath and Anchor

The oath woke on the third night out of Brammel's tower, and it did not wake gently.

Zephyrine had felt it stir before—little tremors, the way a sleeping dog twitches its paws. A warmth along the old scar at their wrist where the words had been cut into them at nineteen, inked in ash and salt by a swordspeaker who smelled of pipe smoke and regret. *I will not forsake the one the fire chooses.* They had spoken it as a formality then, a rite of passage, half-drunk on ceremony wine, certain they would never be called to mean it. Swordspeakers were relics. The last oaths had gone unfulfilled for a hundred years. The order was a museum that happened to have living exhibits.

Then came the shrine, and the book, and the girl with fire under her skin.

Now, camped in the lee of a granite shoulder with Brammel snoring softly against his pack and Elodie curled a few feet away with her back to the coals, the oath finished waking.

It began as an itch beneath the scar. Zephyrine scratched at it without waking fully, muttered something foul, and tried to sink back into sleep. The itch deepened. Became a burn. Became—

Elodie cried out.

Zephyrine was on their feet with a knife drawn before thought caught up, scanning the treeline for Ashen knights, for wolves, for anything. The camp was still. The fire crackled. Elodie thrashed against her bedroll, caught in some dream, her breath coming in short, wounded gasps.

And Zephyrine's wrist was burning like a brand pressed to bone.

They stood there in the dark, knife out, heart hammering, and slowly understood two things at once. First: no one was attacking. Second: the pain in their arm was not their own.

*Oh,* they thought, with a clarity that felt almost like falling. *Oh, no.*

They sheathed the knife and crouched beside the girl, not touching her—they'd learned that lesson the first day, when a handshake across a coin purse had left their palm blistered for a week—but close enough to speak. "Elodie. Hey. You're dreaming."

She didn't wake. She whimpered something—a name, maybe, or a place—and in her sleep her hand clenched over her sternum, right where the coal lived, and Zephyrine felt it too. Not metaphorically. Felt it: a heat behind their own ribs, a pressure like a fist closing around something precious and terrible, and beneath all of it a grief so large and so young that it knocked the breath out of them.

This was what the oath had been keeping in reserve. This was what *I will not forsake* actually meant—not a promise about geography, not a vow to stay within arm's reach. It meant there was no longer any such thing as Zephyrine Nystrom alone in their own body. The girl's pain had been given a door into theirs, and the door did not lock from this side.

Zephyrine sat down hard on the cold stone and waited it out, breathing through the dream-grief like a cramp, until Elodie subsided into quieter sleep and the fire behind their own ribs banked down to embers.

Brammel's voice came from the darkness, dry as old vellum. "It's started, then."

"You knew." Zephyrine didn't make it a question.

"I suspected. You took her contract. That's all the old rites ever needed—an oath spoken, a charge accepted, freely given." The cartographer shifted; his joints sounded like a ship in a storm. "The books call it anchoring. The swordspeaker becomes the anchor-point for the chosen one. Whatever she carries, you carry the weight of carrying it."

"Did the books mention the part where I feel her nightmares?"

"The books were written by people who never expected it to matter again." A pause. "I'm sorry, swordspeaker. I should have warned you before you signed her on."

"No," Zephyrine said, and found they meant it, which annoyed them considerably. "You couldn't have stopped me. I read the date in my own name in a book older than my grandmother and I took the job anyway. I've been waiting for the trap to spring since the shrine." They looked at their wrist, at the faint silver scarring that now seemed to pulse very slightly, like a second heartbeat. "I just didn't think the trap would be *this*."

"What did you think an oath was?"

"I thought it was a promise. Promises you can keep. Promises have edges." Zephyrine lay back and stared at the wrong constellations beginning to wheel overhead—the ones that only appeared this far east, the ones Brammel said were the first casualties of Vaelthorne's erasure, stars that had lost their stories and drifted. "This doesn't have edges. This is just—her. Inside my skin. Forever."

Brammel was quiet a long moment. "Aye," he said finally. "That's an oath."

* * *

Morning made it worse, because morning made it ordinary.

Zephyrine had built their whole life on the principle of clean transactions. Cargo moved, coin changed hands, roads ended, partings happened. They had watched smugglers they liked die and buried them without weeping, because grief was a tax and Zephyrine paid exactly what was owed, no more. They told themselves it was professionalism. Some nights, in honest hours, they admitted it was fear—that if they ever let themselves feel the full weight of anything, they would never put it down again.

Now the weight had been fastened to them with a blacksmith's rivets.

By midmorning they had catalogued the mechanics of it, because cataloguing was how Zephyrine survived things. Distance mattered, but not much—at fifty paces the link was a murmur, at arm's reach it was a conversation. Her physical pain came through sharpest: the coal's slow burn, which she had lived with so long she barely noticed it anymore, registered on Zephyrine's end like walking on a sprained ankle all day, every day. Her hunger was their hunger. When she skinned her knuckles gathering firewood, Zephyrine hissed and stuck their own bleeding hand behind their back before realizing it wasn't cut.

And her feelings—gods, her feelings. Elodie Vasse kept her face like a locked strongbox during the day. Zephyrine had admired that, had read it as composure, as spine. Now they knew it was a dam. Behind it ran a river: terror of the Court, rage at her grandmother—rage that surprised even her, surfacing in flashes she clearly didn't examine—and underneath everything, so constant Zephyrine almost stopped noticing it, a loneliness so profound it had stopped feeling like loneliness and become simply the shape of her, the way a riverbed is shaped by water that has always run through it.

"You're staring," Elodie said, without looking up from the map Brammel had spread on a flat stone.

"I'm thinking."

"You're staring *and* thinking. It's unsettling." She glanced up. Grey eyes, sharp as a ledger clerk's. "Is this about last night? I talk in my sleep. My mother said I always—"

"It's not about that."

But it was, and she saw it was, and something flickered across her face—fear, quickly shuttered. "Then what?"

Zephyrine weighed lying. They were good at lying; it was most of their trade. But the oath sat between them like a third person at the fire, and lies felt suddenly heavy, like trying to smuggle lead past a customs officer who could smell it.

"When you took the job with me," Zephyrine said carefully, "you hired a guide. Someone who knows the passes, gets you east, keeps you off the Court's roads. That's the service. That's what the coin buys."

"Yes."

"The coin doesn't buy what's happening now."

Elodie went very still. "What's happening now?"

Zephyrine flexed their scarred wrist, watching her watch it. "My oath has decided your business is its business. When you hurt, I hurt. When you—" they hesitated, then committed, because hiding it would be worse— "when you grieve, I grieve. It seems I'm anchored to you. Permanently. It's not something either of us chose, and I want to be clear that I resent it thoroughly."

Silence. Brammel, wisely, pretended to be absorbed in his map, though Zephyrine noted he'd been holding it upside down for a full minute.

"That sounds horrible," Elodie said at last.

"It is horrible."

"For both of us." Her voice had gone quiet and careful. "You're saying I can't even have a nightmare without—"

"I'm saying," Zephyrine cut in, harsher than intended, "that I spent thirty years making sure nothing owned me. No guild, no crown, no lover, no god. I chose every road I ever walked, and I could leave any of them whenever the road stopped suiting me. That was the entire point of my life. And now there's a girl I met four days ago whose broken heart lives in my chest, and I cannot put it down, and I did not agree to this, and I am *angry* about it, and I needed you to know that before you got any ideas that I'm—"

"Noble?" Elodie's chin came up. Firelight—or sunlight, Zephyrine couldn't tell—seemed to catch in her eyes. "That I'd mistake resentment for devotion? Don't worry, swordspeaker. I grew up in a palace. I know exactly what duty looks like when it wishes it were somewhere else. My father wore that face at every dinner for sixteen years."

The words landed harder than she probably intended, because they were true, and because the oath carried her flinch of guilt straight into Zephyrine's gut alongside the blow.

"Good," Zephyrine managed. "Then we understand each other."

"We do." She rolled up the map with unnecessary precision. "You carry me because you must. I'll try to hurt quietly."

"That's not what I—"

"It's exactly what you said." She walked off toward the horses, spine straight, strongbox face firmly in place.

And Zephyrine stood there in the cold morning light, feeling her retreat like a door closing in their own house, and understood with sickening precision that they had just taken the one person chained to them and handed her a reason to apologize for existing.

*Well done,* they told themselves bitterly. *Thirty years of clean transactions, and the first time someone's pain is legally yours, you invoice her for it.*

* * *

They walked east through the afternoon, up into country where the pines gave way to grey birch and the air smelled of snow that hadn't fallen yet. Zephyrine ranged ahead out of habit, checking the trail, and discovered the first practical cruelty of the anchor: distance hurt. Not dramatically—nothing so merciful as a snapped rope—but a low, rising wrongness, like a tooth aching in a jaw, growing worse with each fifty paces between them. By the time they were a quarter mile ahead, it was a dull scream, and they turned back pretending it was to check on the mule.

So much for ranging ahead. So much for scouting the pass alone, doubling back, losing pursuit the way they'd always lost it—by being willing to go where a client couldn't follow. Every trick of their trade assumed they could split from the cargo. The oath had quietly outlawed the entire profession.

They tested other things, too, in the privacy of their own skull. Could they lie to her? Harder now—their own falsehoods snagged somewhere in the chest, uncomfortable, like swallowing thread. Could they hate her? They tried, briefly, summoning all the reasons: the prophecy that had hijacked their life, the Court that hunted them now by association, the coal that burned anyone foolish enough to offer her a hand. The hatred wouldn't hold. It slid off something—some stubborn, exhausted core of her that Zephyrine had seen at the roadside shrine, when she'd looked at a book naming her doom and asked, not *why me*, but *what do I do*. People who asked *what do I do* instead of *why me* were, in Zephyrine's long experience, worth dying next to, which was precisely the problem.

Because here was the bitter arithmetic they kept arriving at, no matter how they rejiggered the sums: the chain was hateful, and the girl was not. Duty and affection had arrived together, welded at the hip, and Zephyrine could no longer tell which was which. When they felt her pain and stayed anyway—was that the oath, or was it them? When they found themselves memorizing the way she hummed under her breath while saddling the mule, tuneless and unselfconscious, was that the anchor or was it Zephyrine, traitor to their own principles, developing an attachment like any common fool?

The oath made it impossible to know. That was the cruelest design of it. A forced kindness proved nothing; a chosen cruelty meant nothing. Everything Zephyrine now did for Elodie was suspect, evidence tainted at the source. They resented that most of all—not the pain, not the tether, but the theft of certainty. They would never again know whether they had stayed because they must or because they wished to.

Unless.

Unless someday the choice came clear. Unless someday the oath and the wish pointed opposite directions, and Zephyrine could see, in that clean bright moment of divergence, which one they followed.

They caught themselves hoping for such a moment and immediately distrusted the hope.

* * *

Dusk brought weather—the thin kind of snow that isn't quite snow, more a cold fog given ambition—and they made camp early in the hollow beneath an overhang, walls close, fire small. Brammel cooked barley and dried pork into something almost edible and talked about the old border stones, markers the Court had missed because they'd been sunk in a bog, still bearing Vaelthorne's crest under a century of moss. Elodie listened with her whole body, leaning toward him, hungry for every scrap of the erased country. Watching her, Zephyrine felt the ache behind her sternum sharpen into something with edges: homesickness for a home that had been murdered before she was born.

Then, abruptly, it wasn't hers.

The shift came between one breath and the next—her mood turning like weather, and Zephyrine's turning with it, helpless as a weathervane. One moment they were warm with her wonder; the next, cold flooded through the link, and dread, and a specific spiraling guilt that Zephyrine recognized from their own worst nights. Elodie had gone quiet, staring into the fire, and her hands had curled inward, and the link poured it all across: *they're following me, they're going to find us, Brammel will die, the swordspeaker will die, everyone who touches me burns or bleeds, I should never have hired her, I should turn myself in, I should—*

"Stop," Zephyrine said.

Elodie blinked. "What?"

"Whatever spiral you're falling down. Stop it. You're flooding the channel." They said it bluntly because bluntness was all they had left; gentleness felt like a currency they hadn't earned. "I can feel it. All of it. Your guilt has a taste, did you know that? Like copper coins. If you insist on drowning, drown quietly, because I'm tied to your ankles."

Shame immediately, pouring through hot—and then, remarkably, anger, which Zephyrine welcomed like rain after drought. "That's not fair."

"Nothing about this is fair. I mentioned that."

"No, I mean—" She stood, hugging her elbows, firelight jumping on her face. "You get to be angry about being chained to me, fine. But I don't get to be afraid? I don't get to lie awake doing the sums of who dies for me? You feel it for five minutes and complain. I've *lived* in it. Every person who's helped me since I ran—I keep the list. I keep it like a debt ledger. Your name's on it twice already, swordspeaker, once for the market town and once for whatever this oath thing cost you, and I didn't ask for any of it, and I would take it all back if taking it back didn't mean—"

She stopped. The sentence's ending hung unspoken, and the link carried its shape anyway: *didn't mean the fire dies with me. Didn't mean Vaelthorne stays dead forever.*

The silence stretched. Snow hissed faintly against the overhang. Brammel had gone very still over his pot, an old man pretending not to witness something sacred and awful.

"Sit down," Zephyrine said finally.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Elodie."

She sat. Zephyrine crouched across the fire from her, forearms on knees, and made themselves say the true thing, because the false things had gotten expensive.

"I don't want your ledger," they said. "Debts are how the Court thinks. Everything entered, everything owed, everything collected. I've spent my life staying out of ledgers." They turned their wrist toward the fire, letting the light catch the silvered scar. "This thing on my arm doesn't work in debts. I've been trying to figure out what it does work in, and I hate the answer, because the answer is that it works in *trust*. Somebody wrote this oath a thousand years ago and built it so that a swordspeaker can't help their charge out of obligation, because obligation rots. They built it so the swordspeaker has to feel everything and stay anyway, until staying stops being obedience and starts being—" they grimaced— "*choice*. Real choice. The ugly kind, made with full knowledge."

"And has it?" Elodie's voice was very small. "Started being that?"

Zephyrine wanted to say *no*. It would have been tidy. It would have preserved the clean transaction, the professional distance, the self they'd spent thirty years building.

Instead they heard themselves say: "I don't know yet. Ask me when the Court catches up to us. That's when it'll be real."

Something loosened in her—Zephyrine felt it physically, a knot behind their own ribs unknotting, which was going to take considerable getting used to. She almost smiled. "That's the least reassuring answer I've ever heard."

"I'm a smuggler, not a comfort. Reassuring costs extra."

Brammel snorted into his barley.

Later, when the girl slept—quietly this time, no thrashing, and Zephyrine chose to believe that was progress rather than exhaustion—and the old man's breathing had gone long and even, Zephyrine sat last watch with their bare wrist held near the flames, studying the scar like a text they were learning to read.

*I will not forsake the one the fire chooses.* Fine words. They wondered if the swordspeakers who'd carved the rite had known the fine print: that forsaking was impossible, and therefore faithfulness meant nothing, and therefore the only thing left at the bottom of the oath, underneath all the compulsion, was a single naked question that no magic could answer for you.

*Knowing you cannot leave—do you stay?*

Far to the west, back along the dark road they'd traveled, a point of orange light flickered where no traveler should be burning fires. Then a second. Then a third, spaced with the discipline of men who marched in ranks.

Zephyrine counted them, and reached over, and shook Elodie awake.
