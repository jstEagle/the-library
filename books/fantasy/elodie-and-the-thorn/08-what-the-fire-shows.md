## 8. What the Fire Shows

The hearth-song had ended an hour ago, but the fire in the heretic's hall had not gone out.

Elodie noticed it first as a wrongness in the light — the flames burning low and blue-gold, the color of the coal beneath her skin, instead of the honest orange of ordinary wood. The Vaelthorne exiles had drifted off to their pallets, Zephyrine among them, posted at the doorway as always, half in shadow with their sword across their knees. Old Maren, the hearth-keeper, had pressed Elodie's hand before retiring and said only, "It knows you're here, child. It's been waiting."

She should have gone to bed. Instead she sat cross-legged on the warm stone before the hearth, alone in the great low-ceilinged room, watching the fire watch her back.

"It doesn't *wait*," she said aloud, to no one. "It's just fire."

The flames leaned toward her like a listener.

She had spent nineteen years pretending the coal was nothing — a birthmark that burned, a fever she carried, a secret her grandmother had pressed into her palm the night the Ashen Court's clerks came to Vasse House with their ledgers and their tongs. *Hold this and never let go,* Grandmother Serafine had whispered, and then she had walked out to meet the magistrates with her chin high and her hands empty, and Elodie had never seen her again.

Never let go. As if it were a keepsake. A locket. A dried flower between the pages of a book.

The fire in the hearth flared, and the heat reached into her — not her skin, not her clothes, but somewhere deeper, behind her ribs where the coal lived in its cage of flesh. It knocked, the way a fist knocks at a door.

"No," Elodie said.

It knocked again, patient, and she understood suddenly that it had been knocking for nineteen years and she had been calling it fever, calling it temper, calling it the restlessness that made her palms itch and her dreams run gold. Nineteen years of answering the door without ever opening it.

She thought of Zephyrine's face when they'd spoken of the oath — that bitter, resigned set to their mouth, duty worn like a chain. She thought of Brammel's ruined eyes, burned for remembering. She thought of the magistrate's writ, declaring her *property*, and how she had run across the burning rooftops of Marrowgate feeling less like a person than a parcel being fought over.

Everyone wanted something from what was inside her. The Court wanted to seize it. The prophecy wanted to spend it. Even the exiles here, kind Maren and her hearth-singers, looked at Elodie with something like reverence, as if she were a reliquary walking around on two legs.

Well. If it was going to knock, she decided, then she would answer on her own terms. Not because a prophecy demanded it. Not because a grandmother had ordered it. Because she was tired of being a locked house.

"Fine," she told the fire. "Show me."

She opened her hand over the coals, and let go.

* * *

The falling was not like falling. It was like being *remembered*.

She stood in a valley she had never seen and knew instantly: green terraces climbing the mountainsides, orchards heavy with fruit the color of dawn, white walls and copper roofs catching the light. Dragons wheeled overhead — real ones, vast and slow as weather, their shadows passing over the fields like blessings. And everywhere, everywhere, the warmth. Vaelthorne did not heat its homes with wood or coal. The whole country was warmed by fire freely given, dragon-fire tended in a thousand hearths by families sworn to its keeping, passed hand to hand down generations like a living inheritance.

*Like me,* Elodie thought, and the vision shuddered.

Then the clerks came.

They came without armies at first — that was the horror of it, that was the thing no song could make her feel properly until now. They came with ledgers. Gray-robed men and women with ink-stained fingers who walked through the markets asking polite questions and writing down answers, and every answer they wrote seemed to weigh a little more than it should, seemed to press down on the world like a thumb on wet clay. *Who tends this hearth? What name is written in your family's fire-book? To whom does this flame owe its keeping?*

And wherever they wrote, things began to fade. Not burn — *fade*. A bridge lost its reflection in the river first, then its shadow, then the memory of why anyone had built it there. A village's name went soft in the mouths of its own children. Maps redrawn themselves in the clerks' satchels, valleys closing up like healed wounds.

The dragons fought. Of course they fought. She saw one fall burning into the sea, saw another simply *stop*, mid-flight, as if someone had crossed out the word for wings. The last one — she felt this in her chest, in the coal, in a grief so old and enormous it had fossilized into something harder than stone — the last dragon had come to the hearth-keepers and given them everything it had left. All its fire, divided into embers, one for each family that swore to carry it out of the country before the erasure closed like a mouth.

*Run,* the vision-Serafine said, young then, dark-haired, clutching an ember wrapped in oilcloth against her breastbone. *Run and don't stop running until you've found someone worth stopping for.*

The valley went gray. The terraces crumbled into ash that did not even have the decency to smolder. The door — she saw it, the plain stone arch, and heard the words carved above it in a language that made her teeth ache — and then the whole country folded itself away like a letter no one would ever read again.

Except the embers. Except the carriers. Except—

The vision turned, and turned, and found her grandmother again, older now, gray-haired, in a manor house in a foreign country, holding a newborn baby wrapped in wool. Holding *her*. And Serafine Vasse was crying, and she was talking to the ember cupped in her other palm as if it were a person, as if it were the last dragon itself, and her voice came through the decades clear as a bell:

"She didn't ask for this. None of us asked. But you'll keep her alive when they come, won't you? You'll burn for her? ... Then I'm sorry, old friend. I'm sorry, and thank you, and forgive me — I'd rather damn her with a burden than leave her naked to the Court. Take my hands' guilt and keep my granddaughter breathing."

And Elodie watched her grandmother press the ember into the newborn's chest — watched the tiny Elodie scream, watched the coal sink beneath the skin like a stone into snow — deliberately. Knowingly. With love, yes, unmistakably with love, but also with a calculation cold enough to frost the love over: *better a vessel than a victim.*

The vision held her there a moment longer, in that nursery, in that moment of her own making, and then it let her go.

* * *

She came back to herself on the hearthstones with her cheeks wet and her fists full of ash.

For a long time she couldn't move. The great room was dark except for the blue-gold fire, which had settled back into something almost ordinary, almost apologetic.

*A vessel.* The word rang in her like a cracked bell. All those years of fevers, all those years of gloves and long sleeves and sitting apart at dances because her skin scalded, all those years of wondering what was wrong with her — and the answer was: nothing was wrong with her. Something had been done *to* her. Before she could walk. Before she could consent. Her grandmother had opened her up like a strongbox and hidden treasure inside her, and called it protection, and died before Elodie was old enough to ask the only question that mattered:

*Why me?*

Because I was yours, she thought. Because I was the closest thing to Vaelthorne left in the world, small and soft and unable to refuse.

Rage came up in her like floodwater. She stood, shaking, and the hearth-fire leapt with her, roaring up the chimney, and she snarled at it — actually snarled, like an animal — "*Don't you dare take her side!*" And the fire ducked down, cowed, and she laughed, a horrible broken sound, because she was shouting at a fireplace in an exile's hall at three in the morning and the fireplace was listening.

"You knew," she said to it, quieter. "You've known all along. You've been in me since before I had words. Did you choose me too? Or did she just— put you in the nearest available child?"

The flames did not answer, but they did not look away either, and somehow that was worse.

She thought about throwing it off. She genuinely considered it, standing there with her heart hammering — some part of her, some deep animal part, knew how, knew that if she squeezed her will around the coal like a fist she could crush it, smother it, be only Elodie Vasse, ordinary and unclaimed, free of prophecies and courts and grandmothers' plans forever.

Her hands rose halfway to her sternum.

And stopped.

Because under the rage, when she made herself look, there was the nursery. There was Serafine's face, tear-streaked and terrified and *certain*, saying *I'd rather damn her with a burden than leave her naked to the Court.* Her grandmother had not been cruel. She had been cornered, the way everyone in this story was cornered — the dragons, the hearth-keepers, Brammel, Zephyrine, all of them making terrible choices inside a trap someone else had built. The ember had kept her alive. She knew that now with the knowledge of the vision: there had been a night in Marrowgate, feverish, when the Court's poisoners had come for the runaway heir, and something beneath her skin had flared white-hot and driven them back, and she had woken with singed sheets and no memory and a life she still had.

Grief arrived then, quietly, the way grief does when rage has finished shouting — grief for Serafine, dead in a ledger-line; grief for the dragon whose last gift she carried; grief for the little girl in the nursery who had never gotten to be just a little girl.

"I'm angry," she told the fire, and her voice cracked. "I'm allowed to be angry. But I'm not going to break it. Not today."

The fire burned steady. Waiting, again. But differently now — not the waiting of a thing that owns her. The waiting of a companion who has finally been introduced.

* * *

She didn't hear Zephyrine cross the room — she never did, the swordspeaker moved like a rumor — but suddenly they were there at the edge of the firelight, sword still across their back, watching her with those road-worn eyes that missed nothing.

"You screamed," Zephyrine said. "Or the chimney did. Hard to say."

"The fire showed me things." Elodie wiped her face with the heel of her hand, not bothering to hide that she'd been crying. She was done hiding things from this particular person; the oath had already bound them closer than most confessions could. "Vaelthorne. Its fall. My grandmother." She laughed, short and bitter. "You know what I learned tonight? Everyone in this story got to choose their burden except me. Brammel chose to remember. You chose to swear your oath — well, mostly chose. The dragons chose to give their fire. My grandmother chose to give me away. And I was *nineteen days old*."

Zephyrine lowered themselves onto the hearthstone beside her, slowly, the way they did everything since the salt road — as if their body had become a debt they were paying off in installments. For a while they said nothing. When they spoke, their voice was rough and quiet.

"When the oath took hold of me — back on the pass, after I took your contract — I hated it. Felt your pain in my own bones and resented every minute. Thought duty and affection were supposed to be different things, and the oath had cheated me by welding them together." They turned their hands over, studying the scars. "Took me a long time to understand nobody chooses clean. My oath-master used to say: a vow isn't a cage you're born in, it's a door you walk through. The walking is the choosing. Even if someone else built the door."

"That's very tidy," Elodie said.

"My oath-master was a very tidy woman. She also died badly, so perhaps don't take her word for gospel." Zephyrine glanced sideways at her. "But the point stands. Your grandmother made the door. She didn't make you walk through it. That part's still yours."

"And if I walk through it, I'm doing exactly what she planned. Exactly what the prophecy wants. Everyone wins except me."

"Or," Zephyrine said, "you walk through it because *you* decide to, on *your* terms, and it stops being their plan and becomes your road. I've smuggled contraband through these mountains for eleven years, Elodie. The cargo never matters. The route does." A ghost of their dry half-smile. "Choose your route."

Elodie looked into the fire for a long moment. The blue-gold flames held still, attentive, like a student.

When she spoke again, her voice had changed — steadied, hardened, the way steel changes when it comes out of the quench. "Then here are my terms. I'm not a vessel. I'm not property, whatever their writ says. I'm not the end of Vaelthorne — I'm not going to carry this coal to some door and pour it out like wine at a funeral and call that an inheritance." She lifted her hand, and the fire rose to meet it, curling around her fingers without burning, gentle as a cat. "I am the *hearth*. Do you understand? Hearths don't get emptied. Hearths get *tended*, and people gather at them, and countries get built around them. If Vaelthorne is going to live again, it won't live because I died at an archway. It'll live because I *refuse* to."

Something flickered across Zephyrine's face — hope, quickly buried, the way they buried everything. "The prophecy says otherwise."

"The prophecy," Elodie said, "is a book. Books can burn."

The words came out of her with a certainty that surprised her — as if the fire itself had handed them up through her throat. Zephyrine stared at her, and for the first time since she'd known them, the swordspeaker looked afraid. Not of the Court. Not of the road.

Of hope.

Before either of them could speak again, a horn sounded from the ridge above the valley — one long note, then two short, the signal Maren's watchers used for riders on the mountain road. Not traders. Traders didn't travel by night in armor.

Zephyrine was on their feet before the echo faded, sword clearing its sheath, and the blade began — softly, terribly — to whisper in the old tongue.

"They've found us," Zephyrine said. "Get Maren. Wake the others. And Elodie—" they paused at the edge of the firelight, silhouetted against the blue-gold glow, "—whatever you just decided to be? Be it fast."
