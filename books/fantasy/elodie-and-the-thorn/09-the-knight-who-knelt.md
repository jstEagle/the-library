## 9. The Knight Who Knelt

The rain had followed them for two days, thin and gray as dishwater, and Ser Aldous Vane had followed the rain.

He had told himself a hundred stories on the road behind them. That he was tracking the fugitive to bring her in gently. That a knight's duty was to the writ, and the writ was only paper, and paper could be interpreted mercifully by a man with a kind hand. That the Court would never know if the girl came back breathing, or came back at all.

He had stopped telling himself stories on the third morning, when he found the place where the swordspeaker had made camp and saw, pressed into the mud beside the cold fire-ring, a small boot-print. A child's boot-print, worn at the heel, the leather patched twice with mismatched thread.

He had knelt in the mud beside that print for a long time. Long enough that his squire — the boy he'd left at the last garrison, thank all the quiet gods — would have thought him ill.

*Stolen property of Vaelthorne,* the writ said. *To be recovered by any means and returned to the custody of the Ashen Court, there to be rendered unto the Ledger.*

She was nine years old. Or ten. She had a patched boot and she laughed at something the cartographer said — he'd heard it from the ridge, a bright scrap of sound torn away by wind — and the Court had written her down in a ledger as property, the way one writes down a horse, or a barrel of salt, or a country.

Ser Aldous Vane had served the Ashen Court for twenty-two years. He had burned villages on its order — not the people, never the people, he had been careful about the people, careful the way a man is careful when he is lying to himself about the size of the thing he is doing. He had escorted clerks who carried ledgers into towns and watched those towns forget their own grandmothers' names by sundown. He had told himself that order was a kind of mercy. That a world without the old fires was a world without the old wars.

He had never, in twenty-two years, knelt in the mud over a child's footprint and felt the specific, nauseating vertigo of a man who has walked to the edge of his own life and looked over.

* * *

They saw him coming before he meant them to. Of course they did. The swordspeaker moved like weather.

Zephyrine Nystrom stood in the middle of the road with the rain running off their blade — not drawn, but loose in its sheath, one hand resting on the pommel the way a shepherd rests a hand on a crook — and looked at him with eyes the color of a winter river.

"You're a long way from your writ," Zephyrine said.

"I am." Aldous dismounted slowly, deliberately, showing both hands empty. His armor was Ashen gray, the pauldrons stamped with the closed ledger-mark, and he had never hated the weight of it as much as he did standing in the road with three strangers and a child watching him from the trees. "I'm a long way from a great many things."

Elodie had come out from under the cart's canvas despite Brammel's hissed protest. She stood with her chin up and her hands balled at her sides, and Aldous saw the faint glow beneath the skin of her wrists, like coals wrapped in paper. Like a hearth seen through a shuttered window.

"You burned the salt road camp," she said. Her voice was steady in the way that small things are steady — through sheer refusal to shake. "The peddlers. You took their names."

"I did."

"You took a woman's *name*. She won't know it's hers anymore."

"I know what I took." His voice came out lower than he intended. "I have been taking things for twenty-two years, girl. Names. Songs. Towns. I took a country once — not with my hands, I was too young, but with my silence after, which is the same thing done slower." He looked at Zephyrine. "I am not asking you to believe me. Belief is a thing that must be earned and I have spent mine. I am asking you to let me walk behind you until I've paid some of it back, and to put a blade in me the moment I give you reason."

"That," said Brammel from the cart, "is the most honest speech I have heard from a gray cloak in forty years, and I still say we hang him."

"We don't hang people," Elodie said.

"You are kind, child. Kindness is how the world eats people like you."

Zephyrine hadn't moved. The rain ticked against their pauldrons. Aldous met those winter-river eyes and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own death warrant, that the swordspeaker was weighing him the way a smith weighs steel — not for shine, but for the sound it makes when struck.

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. One word. The whole question in it.

Aldous looked down at the mud between them. At the small boot-print there, half-dissolved by rain.

"There's a print by your fire," he said. "A child's. I knelt next to it this morning and I could not stand up as the same man." He swallowed. "The Court calls her property. I have read the writ. I have carried the writ. And I looked at that print in the mud and I understood that I have spent my whole life serving men who would write a child into a ledger, and that the ledger does not care if she laughs. It does not care that she is *alive*. It only cares that she is *owed*." He drew a slow breath. "I knelt down a knight of the Ashen Court. I stood up something else. I don't have a name for it yet."

The rain filled the silence.

"He's lying," Brammel said flatly.

"Probably," Zephyrine said. "Or he isn't. Or he's lying to himself and telling the truth by accident — that's the most common kind of man there is." They turned their head slightly, and Aldous saw the blade at their hip had begun, very faintly, to hum. A sound like a struck glass. A sound like a word about to be spoken. "The sword doesn't think I'm lying," Zephyrine said. "The sword doesn't think *he's* lying."

"The sword is a sword."

"The sword is older than your grudge, old man, and it has better hearing." Zephyrine's hand came off the pommel. "You walk behind us. You carry nothing sharp. You sleep downwind of the girl, and if you so much as dream in her direction wrong, I will speak a word that turns your bones to chalk, and I will not enjoy it, but I will not lose sleep either."

"Fair terms," Aldous said.

"They're not terms. They're a leash." Zephyrine turned and walked back toward the cart. "Leashes can be cut. Walk."

* * *

Elodie watched the gray knight from the corner of the fire all that evening, the way you watch a dog that has bitten someone.

He sat at the very edge of the light, where Zephyrine had told him to sit, and he ate hard bread and salt fish without complaint, and he kept his hands where everyone could see them, folded on his knees like a man at a funeral. His armor was gone — Zephyrine had made him pile it under a rock, and it sat out there in the dark like the shed skin of something that had finally died.

Without it he looked smaller. Older. There was gray in his beard and a scar through one eyebrow, and he sat the way soldiers sit when they've been sitting on hard ground for twenty years, which is to say he didn't sit so much as settle, like a building.

Brammel had not forgiven him. Brammel sat closest to Elodie and sharpened his little cartographer's knife and watched the knight with the flat patience of a man who has outlived everyone who ever wronged him and intends to keep doing it.

"You took the peddler woman's name," Elodie said at last. She hadn't planned to speak. The words came out of her the way the fire came out of her sometimes — on their own schedule.

Aldous looked up. "I did."

"Did it hurt her? When it was gone?"

"Yes." He said it without softening it, and she was grateful, though she couldn't have said why. "It hurt her the way losing a tooth hurts. A hole where something was. She kept touching the place where it had been." His hands tightened on his knee. "The clerks say the erasure is painless. The clerks have never had a name."

Elodie looked into the fire. Under her skin, the coal turned over in its sleep, warm as a hand.

"My grandmother gave me this," she said. "She put it in me and she didn't ask. Everyone keeps deciding what I am. The Court says I'm property. The prophecy says I'm a vessel. Brammel says I'm the last light of Vaelthorne." She looked up at the knight. "What do *you* say I am?"

Aldous was quiet for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, Zephyrine stood the first watch, a silhouette against the lesser dark.

"I say," Aldous said slowly, "that in twenty-two years I never once saw the Court ask that question. And that a child who asks it is more dangerous to them than any fire." He hesitated, and then did the thing that soldiers do when they have decided to stop lying: he said the true thing, plainly, like setting down a heavy pack. "The Lord Registrar is coming himself. I left the column four days ago, but the column is real. Three hundred spears, two clerk-circles, and the Registrar with his final ledger. He means to close the account at the door of Vaelthorne. He means to write the last line in the fire's own light."

The words dropped into the camp like stones into a well.

"He's coming," Brammel said. "The Registrar. In person."

"He's old," Aldous said. "Older than the erasure. He was a junior clerk the day they burned the maps. He has spent a century making the world quiet, and I think — I think a man like that cannot bear to die with one loud thing left in it." He looked at Elodie, and his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "You are the last loud thing."

Elodie's hands had gone cold, though the fire under her skin burned on. She thought of the visions — Vaelthorne falling, her grandmother's face, the deliberate weight of an inheritance pressed into a child like a coin into wax. Everyone wanted something from the fire. The Court wanted it dead. The prophecy wanted it delivered. Her grandmother had wanted it carried.

Nobody, she thought, had ever asked the fire what *it* wanted.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

Zephyrine's silhouette shifted at the edge of the dark. "Then we walk smarter," the swordspeaker said, voice carrying through the rain-hush. "Because a column of three hundred doesn't lose us in the Thornfell. It *erases* the Thornfell." A pause. "Which means tomorrow we cross into the blank places. The unmapped ground."

Brammel's knife stopped moving.

"Boy," the old cartographer said softly, to no one, to the dark, "I only remember half of it."

* * *

That night, Ser Aldous slept without armor for the first time in twenty-two years, and dreamed of nothing at all — which was, he thought, waking before dawn to a sky going gray over the peaks, the closest thing to forgiveness the world had offered him yet.

It was not forgiveness. He knew that. Forgiveness was a ledger of its own, and his account stood open, and the girl with the fire in her blood might one day close it, or might one day read his name into it and let him forget himself entirely. Either would be just.

Until then there was only the arithmetic. A child's boot-print in the mud. A woman's name, gone. A column of three hundred spears coming up behind them like a slow tide.

He picked up his pack and fell into step at the rear of the line, downwind of the girl, and the blank mountains rose ahead of them all, white and nameless, waiting to see what they were made of.
