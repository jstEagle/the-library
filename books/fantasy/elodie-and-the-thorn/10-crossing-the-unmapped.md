## 10. Crossing the Unmapped

The map ended at a stream no wider than a stride.

Zephyrine crouched at its bank and watched the water move—because it did move, cold and quick over grey stones—and understood that they were about to step off the edge of the world as the Ashen Court had drawn it. Brammel stood beside them, his half-burned copy of the Vaelthorne survey held open against his chest like something he was afraid the wind would take. Beyond the stream, the land rose into hills that were not on any page. Not blank paper, exactly. Blanker than that. The kind of nothing that made the eye slide away, that made you forget you had looked.

"Say it plainly," Elodie said behind them. She had her arms crossed against the morning cold, and the coal under her skin threw faint light through the veins of her wrists when she was frightened, which she was now, though she held her chin high enough to pretend otherwise. "What happens if we cross?"

"Nothing happens," Brammel said. "That's rather the point."

He said it gently, but there was no gentleness in what it meant. Zephyrine had heard the story twice now, once by firelight and once in the dark when Brammel couldn't sleep: how the clerks of the Ashen Court had come with their ledgers and their burning-ink, how they had struck Vaelthorne from every atlas, every almanac, every merchant's route-book and parish roll; how they had not merely erased the country but *unwrote* it, so that roads forgot where they went and rivers forgot their names and even the stars above it had been reassigned to other skies. What remained was this—a seam of unmade country, a hundred miles of it between the salt road and the door, through which no honest traveler could pass because no honest traveler could remember it existed long enough to try.

Only two people alive could walk it. One was an old man carrying a memory the Court had tried to burn out of his skull and only half succeeded. The other was a swordspeaker whose oath had been sworn in Vaelthorne's tongue before the country died.

Zephyrine flexed their sword-hand. The blade at their hip had been quiet since the ambush on the salt road—quiet, but not silent. It hummed sometimes at night, low, like a dog dreaming. It knew this place. That was the worst part. The sword remembered Vaelthorne better than Zephyrine did, and Zephyrine had never even been there.

"We cross," Zephyrine said, and stood.

Ser Aldous said nothing. He had said very little for three days—not out of sulking, Zephyrine thought, but out of the particular silence of a man recalculating everything he believed. He wore his Ashen grey still, because he owned nothing else, and the grey made him look like a ghost attending a family that hadn't decided whether to feed him. He checked the horses' girths without being asked. Small services. Payments against a debt no one had named aloud.

Elodie mounted last. As she swung up into the saddle, her hand brushed Zephyrine's shoulder—the oath flared, sudden and hot, Zephyrine feeling her fear as if it were their own heartbeat stumbling—and she snatched the hand back, murmuring an apology she'd made a hundred times.

"Don't," Zephyrine said. "It's not your fault the oath is a leash."

"It's not a leash if I'm the one holding it."

"That's exactly what makes it one."

She almost smiled. Then she looked past them, at the hills that weren't there, and the smile died.

They crossed the stream.

* * *

For the first hour, it was only strange.

The light came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale diffuse noon that cast no shadows worth navigating by. The grass grew in perfect, identical tufts, spaced like stitches. There were no birds. When Aldous's horse snorted, the sound fell flat, swallowed, as if the air itself were padded.

Then the road began to lie.

Zephyrine noticed it first as a wrongness in their knees—they had walked enough passes in their life to read grade and distance in the body, the way sailors read swell—and the body said they had been climbing for an hour while their eyes insisted the ground was level. Then the road bent. They all saw it bend left around a granite shoulder. Ten minutes later they crested the same granite shoulder from the same side, and the road bent left again.

"The loop," Brammel said quietly. "First one. We'll see six or seven today, worse tomorrow. The erasure didn't delete the roads, you understand—it deleted the *destinations*. A road is just a habit of going somewhere. Take away the somewhere, and the habit eats its own tail." He licked his thumb and turned a page of the burned survey, careful as a priest handling relics. Half the eastern quarter of the map was gone, charred to lace, and where the ink survived it had faded to the brown of old blood. "Keep talking to me, swordspeaker. Your blade can feel the true grain of the land underneath. Mine remembers what was written on top. Between us we might keep a straight line."

"And if we lose the line?" Aldous asked.

Brammel didn't answer, which was an answer.

Zephyrine drew the sword.

It came out singing softly, a note below hearing, and the moment the steel touched the unmade air the humming changed—deepened, steadied, like a tuning fork finding its pitch against bone. Zephyrine closed their eyes and let the oath-sense open. This was the part of swordspeaking no ballad ever mentioned: not the words of power, which cost years, but the quiet listening, which cost nothing except the willingness to feel everything. The land here was like a room after a quarrel. Underneath the blankness, the old country persisted—stone remembering stone, the buried spine of a wall, the ghost-pressure of a hearth-stone, a well shaft full of clean dark water. Vaelthorne wasn't gone. It was *holding its breath*, and had been for a hundred years, waiting for someone to say its name.

"There," Zephyrine said, pointing the blade north-northeast, toward a hillside that looked identical to every other hillside. "There's a ford under that slope. Old paving stones. The river still runs where it ran."

"You can feel paving stones?" Elodie asked.

"I can feel what the stones are still *being*." They sheathed the sword and the world went muffled again. "It's exhausting. Ask me again in a week."

They rode. The loop let them go on the third attempt, when Brammel recited a street name—"Coppersmith's Row, Coppersmith's Row, the ford opens off Coppersmith's Row"—and the words acted like a key, the landscape shuddering and agreeing to be somewhere. Zephyrine felt each correction as a physical tug, the oath pulling taut between their ribs and the buried bones of the country. By midday their head ached. By late afternoon the ache had moved behind their eyes and begun to speak.

*You could stop,* it said, in a voice that sounded suspiciously like their own. *Turn back. The salt road is right there. Sell your sword to some fat caravan master and forget you ever read a margin.*

"Shut up," Zephyrine muttered.

"Talking to me?" Elodie asked.

"No."

* * *

Night came wrong.

There was no dusk—the pale light simply thinned until it gave out, like a candle drowning in its own wax—and the stars that emerged were strangers. Zephyrine lay on their bedroll staring up and could name none of them. Worse: the constellations *moved*, slowly, visibly, wheeling in patterns no sky had ever sanctioned. Once, directly overhead, three stars arranged themselves into a shape that was unmistakably a doorway, held for the space of ten heartbeats, then scattered.

"The Court reassigned the heavens too," Brammel said from across the dead fire. They dared not light one; flame behaved strangely here, leaning toward Elodie no matter where she sat, as if the whole dark country wanted to warm itself at her. "Every star that once stood over Vaelthorne was given a new post. Astronomers call it the Great Correction. They wrote papers on it. Prizes were given." His voice cracked on the last word, dry as the burned map. "Prizes were given, girl. For the theft of a sky."

Elodie sat closest to the cold fire-pit, wrapped in both her cloaks, and the coal-light pulsed under her skin in slow rhythm, like breathing. "Does it hurt them? The stars. Being moved."

"They're stars," Aldous said. "They don't hurt."

"How would you know?"

He had no reply to that. Zephyrine watched him in the dark and saw the question land anyway, saw it settle into him like a splinter working deeper. Good, some small cruel voice in them said. Let it fester. But another voice—the one that had been growing louder since the hearth-camp, since the songs, since Elodie laughing for the first time over burnt rabbit—said: *he knelt to them once and it nearly killed him to rise. Don't make it harder than it has to be.*

Zephyrine took first watch, mostly to be alone.

Alone, in the unmapped dark, the oath spoke more freely. Not in words. In pressure. It pressed against the inside of their chest like a hand testing a door, and through it Zephyrine could feel the others sleeping: Elodie's dreams flickering ember-orange, restless; Brammel's deep and cluttered, a warehouse of maps; Aldous's thin and gray, a corridor with a locked door at the end. Zephyrine hadn't asked for any of this. The oath had been a formality once, a lineage thing, a few phrases learned young and never tested. Now it was a web with Zephyrine at the center, and every strand hummed with someone else's living.

*I chose my own roads,* they thought at it, bitterly. *That was the whole point of me.*

The dark did not answer. The dark never answered. That was the difference between the dark and the Court, Zephyrine supposed. The Court answered constantly, in writing.

* * *

The second day, the loops got worse, and so did the trust.

It began at a bridge that wasn't there. Brammel swore there was a bridge—an aqueduct bridge, seven arches, spanning a gorge called the Hollow Throat—and the gorge was there, abrupt and shocking after miles of gentle grass, a black crack in the earth a hundred feet down with a white thread of water at its bottom. No bridge. No trace of piers. Just the gorge, patient as arithmetic.

"The map says—"

"The map is *burned*, Brammel!" Elodie's voice cracked high. She was standing too close to the edge, and Zephyrine's stomach dropped with her fear via the oath, a sick sympathetic lurch. "Half of it is ash! You're guessing! You've been guessing since yesterday and each time you guess we ride in a circle and my head feels like it's being wrung out like laundry—"

"She's right that we circled," Aldous said. Careful. Reasonable. The voice of a man who had spent twenty years making reports.

"I have *not* been guessing." Brammel's hands shook around the survey. "I have been *remembering*, which in this place is the same as fighting. Every name I recall is a nail holding the road straight. Do you know what it costs, at my age, to hold a country together with nouns?" He jabbed a finger at the gorge. "Seven arches. I *attended* those arches. I measured them when I was younger than she is. The bridge is real. It is simply not currently *occurring*."

"Not occurring," Elodie repeated. "Wonderful. And when does it occur?"

"When someone says its name like they mean it."

Everyone looked at Zephyrine.

Zephyrine looked at the gorge, and at the far side, where the land continued—hills, a pass, the long gray line of distance that meant the door was getting closer instead of further. Then they drew the sword, and the low note rose, and they reached with the oath-sense down into the black throat of the Hollow and found, yes, at the bottom of a century of forgetting, the ghosts of seven arches still standing in the idea of the world, stubborn as teeth in an old jaw.

But the sense also found something else. Something that made the note in the blade falter.

Movement. Far off, beyond the far ridge, in the direction of the door: a column. Not men—*absence* of men, a marching hole in the country's attention, moving with the terrible efficiency of things that exist on paper. Ledgers. The Court had sent its ledger-things ahead, the clerks' instruments, the ones that unwrote as they walked. Brammel had described them once, in a whisper, and refused to describe them twice.

And behind the column, faint but unmistakable, a knot of ordinary human hearts. Hundreds. An army, following the hole the ledger-things cut, the way carrion birds follow a plow.

They're marching on the door already, Zephyrine thought. While we're losing arguments with a bridge.

"Swordspeaker." Brammel's voice, urgent. "The arches. Say them."

Zephyrine opened their mouth—and hesitated. Because speaking cost. They'd paid on the salt road, a handful of words in the old tongue, and woken with a new white streak in their hair and hands that ached for two days like frostbite. Every syllable of power was a year off the end of their life, and they had maybe a dozen syllables' worth of spending money left before the bill came due in earnest.

Elodie saw the hesitation. Of course she did. She'd watched them pay at the salt road; she kept count harder than anyone.

"Do it," she said quietly. "Please. I'll—we'll find another way to pay it back. I don't know how yet. But do it."

So Zephyrine planted their feet at the lip of the Hollow Throat, raised the sword, and spoke—not the deep words, not the killing words, just the naming words, the smallest coin in the purse:

*"Throatbridge, seven-arched, carry us over."*

The world convulsed. Stone groaned up out of the gorge like a whale surfacing—seven arches assembling themselves out of insistence, out of memory, out of a hundred years of a dead country refusing to lie down—and the sound it made was almost a voice, almost grateful. The company crossed in single file, horses trembling, none of them looking down.

On the far side, Zephyrine swayed in the saddle. Their reflection, caught in a pool at the roadside, had gone grayer at the temples. Another year, gone. Maybe two.

Elodie rode close and did not touch them, because she couldn't, because touching her burned—but she rode close, and matched their pace, and said nothing, which from her was its own kind of payment.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruin that shouldn't have existed: walls, a roofless hall, a lintel stone carved with words worn nearly smooth. Brammel ran his fingers over the carving and began, very softly, to cry.

"This is a tithe-house," he said. "From the borderlands. People left a tenth of everything here—for travelers, for the poor. Grain, wool, coins. It was the law of hospitality, older than the kings." He laughed, wet and broken. "The Court erased a country and left its *ruins* lying around like furniture. They took the name and the maps and the sky, but they couldn't dig up every stone. So the stones just sit here. Waiting."

Zephyrine set the watch rotation and sat apart, cleaning a blade that didn't need cleaning, and let the day's fear finally catch up. The loops. The stars. The bridge. And the column—always the column, marching in their mind's eye toward the same door they bled to reach. They were a week out, maybe less if the country allowed it. The Court was closer. The Court was always closer; that was the nature of institutions. They moved like weather.

A shadow settled beside them. Aldous.

"I scouted east before dark," he said. "Two miles. There's a ridge where you can see a long way." He paused. "Fires. Many fires, in a line, like a procession. Moving slow, but not stopping."

"You're certain it's them."

"I trained those fires into existence, swordspeaker. I wrote the marching orders for the vanguard." His jaw worked. "The Lord Registrar himself is with them. Which means this isn't a patrol. It's a *closing of accounts*."

Zephyrine looked across the dark camp: Elodie asleep with her ember-light pulsing, Brammel curled around his burned map like a mother around a child, the ruined tithe-house standing open-roofed to the wrong stars, generous to no one, waiting for a tenth of everything from travelers who no longer remembered to give.

One week. One army. One door.

"Aldous," Zephyrine said. "When we get to the arch—what does the Court do if it finds the door won't open for them?"

The knight was silent a long moment.

"They don't believe doors refuse," he said finally. "They believe doors are *uncooperative*. And they have ways of making entries cooperate." He glanced back toward the east, toward the distant glow of a hundred obedient fires. "Whatever you're planning to do at that door, swordspeaker—I'd plan it fast. They'll be here in three days."

Three days. Through a hundred miles of country that ate roads and lied about stars, with a guide held together by nouns and a passenger made of fire and an oath that fed on years.

Zephyrine looked at the blade across their knees, at the faint reflection of a face already older than its seasons, and began, silently, to count the words they had left.
