## 11. The Price of a Word

Elodie knew something was wrong before Zephyrine fell.

It began small, the way ruin always did. A hesitation at the stream crossing where there had never been hesitation before. A hand pressed to a birch trunk for balance that was not strictly needed. The swordspeaker's stride—long, economical, a walk built for mountain roads and narrow escapes—shortening by half a step, then another, as though the ground beneath them had begun to charge toll.

They had come down out of the unmapped country two days past, back into a world with names on its rivers and stars in their proper houses, and Elodie had thought the worst behind them. Foolish, she would think later. The unmapped dark had only been the first bill. This was the collection.

"Zephyrine." She said it lightly, the way you say a name when you are afraid of what answering will cost. "The village is close. Brammel's map shows a ferry at the confluence."

"Mm." Zephyrine did not stop walking. That was its own kind of answer. In all the weeks since the shrine, since the margins of a dead book had reached out and closed around them both, Elodie had never known the swordspeaker to give an answer so thin.

Then Zephyrine's knee simply *stopped*, mid-stride, like a door slamming, and they went down into the bracken without a sound.

Elodie was beside them before her own mind caught up, knees in wet leaves, hands hovering over a body that had always seemed carved from something harder than flesh. Zephyrine lay on their side, eyes open, breathing fast and shallow, and their face—

Their face was wrong.

She had watched this person take a knight's lance across the shoulder and laugh about it. She had watched them speak three words of the old tongue on the salt road and stand unshaken while the air itself flinched. But now the skin around their eyes had gone thin as parchment, fine lines branching from the corners like frost on a window, and when Zephyrine tried to push themselves up on one arm, the arm trembled like an old man's.

"Don't," Elodie said. "Don't get up. Just—don't."

"I'm fine." The voice came out sanded down, rougher than she'd ever heard it. "Just need a moment. The words on the road—the crossing spell, it took more than I—"

"You didn't cast anything today."

Zephyrine's jaw worked. Above them, the birches let down the last of their gold light in long slanted bars, and in that light Elodie could see what she had been refusing to see for days: the grey coming in at the temples where there had been none. Not streaking. *Spreading.* As if someone had taken a brush dipped in winter and worked it through the black hair while she slept.

"Three nights ago," Zephyrine said quietly. "In the blank country. When the road turned us back on ourselves and Ser Aldous's compass spun like a drunkard—I spoke the finding-words. Four lines of them. You were asleep."

"You said the stars were just wrong. You said we'd wait for morning."

"I said a great many things." Zephyrine managed, at last, to sit up, and Elodie hated how carefully they did it, how much effort sat visible in the simple geometry of rising. "Most of them true. Sit me against the tree, would you? My legs have filed a formal complaint."

She got her shoulder under theirs and walked them the six steps to the largest birch, and the weight of them frightened her—not because Zephyrine was heavy but because they leaned on her at all, actually leaned, trusting her with the whole of their balance, and trust from this particular person felt like watching someone hand over a blade hilt-first.

* * *

She made camp the way she had learned to: fire small and smokeless, bedrolls under the birch, water boiled with the pine needles Zephyrine had taught her held flavor better than they held poison. Zephyrine sat propped against the trunk with their sword across their knees—a thing they almost never did anymore, she realized. The blade had been quiet since the ambush on the salt road. Quiet, and perhaps waiting.

"Eat," Elodie said, pressing the bowl into their hands.

"I'm not hungry."

"That wasn't a question. It was a threat delivered in a friendly tone."

Something moved at the corner of Zephyrine's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the shape a smile left behind—and they ate. Three spoonfuls. Then they set the bowl down in the leaves with the deliberate care of someone conserving strength for a conversation they did not want to have.

"Ask me," Zephyrine said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do. You've been carrying the question since the salt road. I've watched you carry it. You look at my hands after I speak the old tongue the way people look at a wound they're afraid to touch." They turned their palm up in the firelight, and Elodie saw it plainly now: the fine lines, the loosened skin, the knuckles gone sharp as an old woman's. "So ask me, Elodie Vasse. You've earned worse questions than this one."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, water talked to itself among the stones.

"What does it cost?" she asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "When you speak it. The swordsong. The finding-words. What does it *take*?"

Zephyrine looked into the fire for a long time. When they finally answered, they did not look at her, and that, more than anything, told her the answer would be terrible.

"The oath is a loan," they said. "Every word spoken in the old tongue draws against something. Years, mostly. Sometimes more. My grandmother told me stories—swordspeakers who sang a fortress gate open and went grey by morning. One who spoke a storm apart and never spoke again at all, any tongue, plain or otherwise. We were taught the words were sacred. What we weren't taught—" a breath, ragged at the edges, "—is that sacred things are expensive. The songs don't spend strength, Elodie. Strength comes back. They spend *time*. The years I haven't lived yet. They take them out of me like coins out of a purse."

"How many?" Elodie heard herself ask. "On the salt road. How many did the song take?"

"A dozen years, perhaps. I've never kept count. Keeping count is a way of dying twice."

"And the night in the blank country?"

"Four or five. Small coin." Zephyrine's mouth twisted. "But the purse is not bottomless. And lately I find the counting has started whether I want it to or not. I wake in the mornings and I know, the way you know weather coming, exactly how much is left in me. It's less every day. It's less every *word*."

Elodie sat very still. The firelight moved on Zephyrine's face and made the new lines move too, deepening, and she understood suddenly why the swordspeaker had stopped meeting her eyes.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you'd stop asking me to use it. And I'd stop being able to refuse you." Now they did look at her, and their eyes were steady even as everything else about them frayed. "You carry a coal of living dragon-fire under your skin, and the Ashen Court wants it badly enough to burn a country once already. Every mile between you and that door is bought with something. I knew the price when I took the job. I took it anyway. That was my choosing, and I won't make it your grief."

"It's already my grief."

"No." Gentle. Infuriatingly gentle. "It's my account. You're only standing near the ledger."

And that—that phrasing, that cold bookkeeping calm—was what broke something loose in her chest. Because she had heard language like that before. Precise. Bureaucratic. A magistrate on a rooftop in a burning market town, reading aloud from a writ that declared her *stolen property of a country that no longer exists*, and the clerkish certainty of it, the sense that a human life could be entered in a column and balanced—

"Whose terms?" she demanded. "This oath of yours. Who wrote the price into it?"

Zephyrine was silent a moment. Then, slowly, they drew the sword across their lap and laid one hand flat along the flat of the blade, the way a person touches a sleeping animal.

"The same people who wrote the prophecy," they said. "The same hands that closed the door of Vaelthorne and burned the maps. Swordspeakers weren't always oath-bound, Elodie. Once we were just singers. People whose voices could move stone and weather and the hearts of enemies. The oath came later—it was written into the order a century ago, when Vaelthorne fell, when the Court needed weapons it could control and spend. They bound the song to a contract." Their thumb traced something on the blade, letters worn nearly smooth. "There are words engraved here, under the grip-wrap. I've never shown anyone. I couldn't read them until recently. Now I can read them too well."

"Show me."

"Elodie—"

"*Show me.*"

For a moment she thought they would refuse. Then Zephyrine exhaled, unwound the leather wrap from below the guard—fingers clumsy, she noticed, fingers that used to be quick as water—and tilted the blade to catch the firelight.

Letters. Old-cut, old-tongued, running in a spiral around the tang where no eye would ever fall by accident. Elodie leaned close. She could not read the script, but she could read Zephyrine's face as they translated, and that was worse than any script.

"'The door of Vaelthorne opens,'" Zephyrine recited, low and level, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant and determined to be dignified about it, "'only to a swordspeaker's life, freely given. Spoken at the threshold, in full song, unto the ending of the singer.'" A pause. The fire cracked. "'No other key. No other price. So sworn by the order, so accepted by the door.'"

The words went into Elodie like a knife going into a loaf—all the way through, clean, and then the spreading ruin after.

"No," she said.

"It's why the prophecy chose me. Why the margins named me. It was never about escorting you, Elodie. It was never about the road." Zephyrine rewrapped the grip with careful turns, hiding the letters again, hiding the whole truth under leather the way they'd hidden it under silence for weeks. "A swordspeaker brought you to the door because a swordspeaker *is* the key. I'm not your guide. I'm the payment."

"No." Louder now, and the fireflames bent toward her, toward the heat rising off her skin, and she forced it down, forced her breath slow, because the last thing Zephyrine needed was dragon-fire loose in a birch grove. "There's another way. There's always another way. Brammel said the door predates the Court, predates the oath—he said it was *old*, older than the binding. Maybe the engraving is a lie. Maybe the Court put it there. Maybe—"

"Maybe." Zephyrine shrugged, and the shrug cost them; she saw the wince they swallowed. "I've had weeks to turn those stones. Here's what I know: the door is real, the oath is real, and the words on this blade match the words in the prophecy book margin for margin. Two sources, a century apart, agreeing. That's not a lie, Elodie. That's architecture."

"Then we don't go to the door."

Zephyrine smiled then, tired and crooked and unbearably fond. "You'll go. You'll stand at that arch if I have to carry you, because everything you love that's still living is on the far side of it—or could be. A country made of hearths and songs doesn't die just because clerks erased its name. You told me that yourself, at the heretics' hearth, with ash still in your hair." They closed their eyes briefly. "And I'll be there to open it. Because that's the oath. Because it's the only version of me that gets to matter at the end."

"Don't talk like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you've already decided to die. Like it's arithmetic." Her voice cracked on the word, and she hated it, hated the tears standing hot in her eyes, hated that her hands were shaking around a tin cup of pine-needle tea that had gone cold. "You stood in front of knights for me. You sang the road straight in a country with no stars. You carried me across a river when my feet were cut to ribbons and complained the entire time about how heavy I am, which is a lie, by the way, I weigh nothing, I'm mostly anger. You don't get to file yourself under *payment*. Nobody gets to do that to you. Not a prophecy. Not a blade. Not a hundred-year-old contract written by men who thought a country was a line in a ledger."

Zephyrine said nothing. The firelight moved. And Elodie saw—in the set of their shoulders, in the way their hand stayed resting on the wrapped hilt like a hand on a coffin lid—that they had made their peace with it. Weeks ago, maybe. On some cold pass, in some grey dawn, quietly, without ceremony, the way they did everything. They had read the terms, weighed the purse, and chosen.

That was the horror of it. Not the dying. The *deciding*, done alone, done kindly, done in a way designed to spare her—when sparing her was the one thing she had never asked for and would never accept.

* * *

Later, when Zephyrine slept—truly slept, deeply, the sleep of a body that had stopped pretending—Elodie sat by the fire and held the tin cup and let herself shake.

The flames leaned toward her. They always did now. Under her skin, behind her sternum, the coal of dragon-fire pulsed like a second heart, warm and patient and utterly indifferent to her grief. Her grandmother's gift. Her grandmother's *sentence*, though she hadn't known it when she learned that truth in the visions—hadn't known that the old woman had carried the coal knowingly, deliberately, and passed it on like an inheritance, like a debt, making a vessel of a child. Elodie had raged at that. Had grieved it. Had finally, in the dark of the collapsed pass with hearth-songs in her throat, chosen to carry it on her own terms.

Well. Here were terms again. Everywhere she turned, someone had written terms.

Zephyrine stirred in the bedroll, murmured something in the old tongue—half-asleep, harmless, a single soft syllable—and even that small word took its tithe; she saw the new line at the corner of their mouth deepen in the firelight, faint as a hair, real as a scar.

Elodie set down the cup.

She thought about the door. About the arch of plain stone in a valley of ash, inscribed with the first words of the oath—Brammel had told them that much before the salt road took him, before his half-finished map became hers. *The door bears the opening words of the binding,* he'd said. *Which means, girl, it also knows the closing ones.* She hadn't understood him then. She was beginning to understand him now.

Words had opened the door. Words had priced it. Words had bound a swordspeaker's life into the lock like a coin soldered into a slot.

And words, she thought, looking at the sleeping, greying, stubborn, infuriating person who had become the axis her whole world turned on—words could *burn*. She had seen the Ashen Court burn a country out of memory. She had felt, in her visions, the heat that unmade names. And she carried in her chest the last living fragment of a fire that had once been a nation's hearth-flame, a fire that answered songs, a fire that had opinions, a fire that had waited a hundred years for someone to tell it what it was *for*.

The prophecy book rode in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth. Its margins named tonight, named tomorrow, named a swordspeaker's death at a stone arch. Its final page, Brammel had sworn, was blank.

Blank pages, Elodie thought, are just words that haven't been bullied yet.

She looked at Zephyrine—at the rise and fall of their breath, at the sword lying beside them with its hidden spiral of terrible scripture—and she made her own contract, silently, in the language of grandmothers and liars and people who intended to win.

"You'll live," she whispered to the sleeping swordspeaker, to the fire, to the door a hundred miles east that believed it knew what it was owed. "Do you hear me? Whatever the blade says, whatever the book says—you'll live, and I'll lie to every power in creation to make it true."

The coal beneath her sternum pulsed once, warm as a hand taking hers.

Far to the east, unseen, unheard, down valleys of ash where nothing had grown for a century, the Ashen Court was marching.
