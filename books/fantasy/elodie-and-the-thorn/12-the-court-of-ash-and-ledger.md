## 12. The Court of Ash and Ledger

The ink in the Hall of Entries never dried. That was the first thing Ser Aldous Vane had learned as a young knight, standing his first watch beneath the vaulted ceiling of the Ashen Court's great registry, and it was the thing he still could not stop noticing, thirty years on, whenever he came home from the field. Every other ink in the world dried. The Court's did not, because the Court's was not made of oak gall and iron the way honest ink was. It was made of ash—ash suspended in oil rendered from candle-tallow burned at funerals, mixed with a binder whose recipe only three living clerks knew. It went onto the page grey and stayed grey forever, a smear that looked wet even after a century, so that every entry in every ledger seemed freshly written, freshly mourned, freshly *entered*.

A country had been written into those ledgers once. And then written out.

Aldous stood now at the edge of the dais where the Lord Registrar worked, watching the old man's pen move across a page with the small, dry precision of a beetle boring through bark. Around them rose the shelves—ten thousand of them, floor to unseen ceiling, receding into a gloom lit only by the grey glow of the ash-ink itself, which gave off light the way drowned things give off cold. Ten thousand shelves. A hundred thousand ledgers. Somewhere in that dark, Aldous knew, was the volume marked *Vaelthorne*, its pages crossed through line by line by clerks long dead, each stroke of cancellation drawn in that same un-drying grey.

He had been twenty-two when they showed him that ledger for the first time. He remembered the ceremony—the new knights brought down into the deep stacks, the Registrar of that day laying the book open on a reading table like a father showing his children a family portrait. *This is what we do,* the old man had said, and there had been something almost tender in his voice, the tenderness of a surgeon describing an amputation. *This is what order costs. Look upon it and do not flinch.*

And Aldous had looked. He had seen the name of a country struck through—not blotted out, not torn away, but cancelled, ruled over with a single continuous line drawn by a clerk who had, according to the marginal note, taken four days to complete the work and requested twice during it that his supper be sent down because he did not wish to lose his place. Four days. A hundred years of songs, of borders, of hearths and harvests and children learning their letters—a country with a history older than the Court itself—and some tired man with ink-stained fingers had spent four working days drawing a line through it, pausing only for meals.

That was the detail that had stayed with him all these years. Not the horror of it. The *tedium*. The way evil, real evil, wore the face of paperwork. No one had screamed in the Hall of Entries the night Vaelthorne died. No one had wept or raged. A man had eaten supper at his desk, and a country had ceased to exist between one course and the next.

* * *

"You are staring at my hand, Ser Aldous."

The Lord Registrar did not look up from his writing. His voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud figures that balanced perfectly. He was very old—older than any man had a right to be who was not kept alive by something, and Aldous had long suspected the ash-ink itself sustained him, that he breathed the fumes of it the way other men breathed air, that somewhere in the ten thousand shelves was a ledger with the Registrar's own name in it, uncrossed, waiting.

"I beg pardon, my lord."

"No, you don't." The pen paused. "But courtesy costs you nothing, and I have grown fond of the sound of it. Come. Stand closer. You have been in the field too long; you have forgotten how to be indoors."

Aldous descended the three steps to the dais. Up close, the Registrar smelled of tallow and cold stone. His hands were remarkable—long-fingered, unblemished, the hands of a much younger man, and Aldous knew why. Every year, on the anniversary of the Erasure, the Registrar wrote one page in his own blood, and the ledgers gave back to him whatever the writing took. The Court called it *communion*. Aldous had stopped calling it anything at all.

"The writ you carried," the Registrar said. "The girl. Tell me about her."

"She is a child, my lord."

"She is a vessel." The pen resumed its movement. "She carries the last coal of Vaelthorne's fire. Her grandmother was cleverer than we credited—cleverer than the ledger allowed. We entered the fire as *destroyed*. An entry, Ser Aldous, is not a fact. It is a decision about what facts will be permitted. But decisions can be contradicted by reality, and reality has been contradicting us for a hundred years." He sighed, a small dry sound, like a page turning. "Do you know what happens to a ledger-line when the truth outgrows it?"

"No, my lord."

"It frays." The Registrar set down his pen at last and folded his hands. For the first time he looked up, and his eyes were the pale grey of paper left too long in smoke. "The Court has spent a century maintaining the Erasure. Every map corrected. Every memory taxed—we call it the Tithe of Forgetting, and it is paid in stories, in songs, in names spoken over graves. Every expatriate registered and monitored. It is the greatest administrative achievement in the history of governance, and it is failing, because a woman hid a coal in her granddaughter's blood and no clerk thought to write *and let none inherit it*." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "We were thorough, Ser Aldous. We were not omniscient. There is a difference, though my predecessors preferred not to acknowledge it."

"What would you have of me, my lord?"

"Nothing you have not already done. You hunted her. You failed to take her." The pale eyes held him without accusation, which was somehow worse than accusation. "You lost six knights at the salt road ambush, and your report describes the swordspeaker's blade as having *sung*. Is that accurate?"

"It sang words, my lord. In the old tongue. Words I have not heard since—" He stopped.

"Since before the Erasure. Yes. Words that should not exist anymore, because we entered them as *extinct*." The Registrar rose, slowly, joints cracking like a closing book. He walked to the edge of the dais and gazed out over the grey-lit shelves. "I am going to tell you something, Ser Aldous, because you are the last knight of the first rank I trust to hear it without either kneeling in ecstasy or running to the censors. The Court is marching on the door of Vaelthorne."

Aldous felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped down a well. "The door exists? The maps say—"

"The maps say nothing. That is rather the point of maps." The Registrar turned. "The door exists. It has always existed. We did not erase it because we could not—it is older than our ink, older than our office, perhaps older than the concept of entries. So instead we erased everything around it. The valley. The roads. The names of the villages within a week's walk. We made the door unreachable by making the way to it unthinkable. But the girl is walking toward it, Ser Aldous, with a swordspeaker and a cartographer who should be dead, and when she reaches it, the Erasure ends. Not gradually. Not gracefully. Everything we have built for a hundred years comes apart in an afternoon, and every debt we deferred comes due at once."

"And the Court means to stop her there."

"The Court means to *arrive* there." Something moved behind the Registrar's eyes—hunger, or fear, or the two wearing each other's clothes. "There is a difference. Stopping her is a clerk's ambition. Arriving is an emperor's. If the fire must come out of the world, it will come out into our keeping. If the door must open, it will open onto our authority. The final entry, Ser Aldous." His voice dropped, and Aldous heard in it, for the first time in thirty years of service, something almost human: exhaustion. "The last line in the last ledger. Vaelthorne's fire, recovered. Vaelthorne's door, sealed under seal. And then the books balance, and I may rest."

*Rest.* Aldous looked at the old man—at the unblemished hands, the smoke-grey eyes, the whole desiccated architecture of a life spent ruling lines through living things—and understood suddenly that the Registrar did not believe in the Court at all. Not anymore. Perhaps not ever. He believed in *finishing*. In the terrible momentum of a task begun by men dead a century, carried forward because stopping would mean admitting it should never have started. The Erasure was not a policy. It was a debt being serviced, generation after generation, by people who had inherited it the way one inherits a mortgage on a haunted house.

"And if the girl reaches the door first?" Aldous asked.

"Then the ledger frays past mending, and everything we burned comes back, and I will stand in this hall and watch a hundred years of order unravel in an afternoon." The Registrar picked up his pen again. "So she will not reach it first. You will ride tonight, Ser Aldous. Full company. You know the passes better than any officer living—you walked them hunting her, and failure teaches geography better than success. You will precede the main column by three days and secure the arch."

*Secure the arch.* Hold the door against the child it was meant for, until the army arrived to make the sealing official.

"My lord," Aldous said, and heard his own voice go flat and careful, "the writ declares the girl stolen property of Vaelthorne. A country that does not exist. If it does not exist, whom do I return her to?"

The Registrar did not look up. "To the ledger," he said. "Everything returns to the ledger eventually."

* * *

They gave him his orders in triplicate, sealed in grey wax, and a column of forty knights, and a supply train, and a chaplain-clerk whose duty was to enter each day's march in the campaign ledger so that the road itself would, in the Court's theology, become official before it was walked. Aldous watched the clerks load the campaign ledger into its lacquered case with the reverence of men handling relics, and felt something in him go quiet and cold and clear.

He rode at the head of the column out of the Ashen City at dusk, and behind him the towers of the Hall of Entries stood black against a bruised sky, and he thought about the clerk who had taken four days to draw one line. Had the man gone home afterward? Had he kissed his wife, eaten his supper, slept soundly? Had he ever, in the years remaining to him, woken in the dark with the taste of ash in his mouth, knowing what his hand had done?

Aldous would never know. But he knew himself, riding east through the falling night with forty sworn men at his back and a sealed writ against his breast, and he knew that somewhere ahead of him a girl with fire in her blood was walking toward a door with a swordspeaker and an old man's map, and that when the moment came—and it would come, it was already coming toward him down the road like weather—he would have to choose which ledger his name belonged in.

His horse's breath steamed in the cold. The stars came out over the Thornfell passes, wrong in the way they always were near the erased places, and Aldous found himself navigating by them anyway, because a knight of the Ashen Court learned early to find his bearing by things the Court had failed to correct.

Three days ahead of the column. Secure the arch.

He touched his spurs to the horse and rode faster, and did not let himself examine why.
