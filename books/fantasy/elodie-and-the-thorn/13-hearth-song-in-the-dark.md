## 13. Hearth-Song in the Dark

The mountain came down on them at dusk, without warning and without malice, the way mountains do.

Zephyrine heard it first—a sound like a held breath, deep in the rock above the pass—and had time to shout exactly one word before the world ended. Not a word of power. Just *run*. Elodie dove; Aldous threw his shield over Brammel's map-satchel as if the leather were flesh; the mule bolted and was gone, swallowed with half their provisions into the white roar of falling stone.

Then silence, thick as wool, and dust that turned the last grey light to smoke.

Zephyrine dug themselves out of the scree coughing, counting bodies by touch in the dark. A hand—Elodie's, hot as a forge-stone even through her glove. A gauntlet—Aldous, groaning, alive. Nothing where Brammel should be, then a wet rasp from beneath a slab the size of a coffin lid, and Zephyrine's heart dropped through the floor of them until the old man wheezed, "Mind my elbow," and they hauled him free, bleeding from the scalp but whole enough to curse with his usual artistry.

They were alive. All four of them. And sealed.

Zephyrine stood at the western face of the collapse and pressed both palms against it, feeling with the senses the oath had sharpened in them—the deep grain of the mountain, the way water still threaded somewhere far below, the cold dead weight of ten thousand tons of stone. There was no threading this needle. No word small enough, no song precise enough. Even if there had been, they'd learned what words cost. The eastern side was worse: a wall of rubble rising thirty feet to where the pass had been, packed tight as mortar.

"Trapped," Aldous said flatly. He said it like a soldier, cataloguing. "Air?"

"There's air moving." Zephyrine tilted their head. "Faint. From below. There's a way down and through, maybe—but not tonight. Maybe not for days."

"Provisions?"

"What the mule didn't take."

Aldous did the arithmetic silently, and Zephyrine watched him arrive at the same number they had. It wasn't a comfortable number.

Elodie said nothing. She sat on a fallen slab with her knees drawn up, and in the darkness her skin gave off its faint light now, the coal beneath it stirred by fear or exertion—a soft gold glow along her forearms, her throat, the hollow of her collarbones, like embers seen through cloth. She had been quiet since the ambush on the salt road. Quiet since Brammel bled. Quiet in the way of someone carrying something too heavy to set down and too precious to complain about.

"We should rest," Zephyrine said. "Move at first light."

No one argued. There was nothing to argue toward.

* * *

The dark in a closed mountain is a different dark than night. Night has edges—horizon, star, the grey promise of morning. This dark had none. It pressed. After an hour Zephyrine could not have sworn their eyes were open. The only light in the world was Elodie, banked low now, dozing against Aldous's pack, glowing faintly like a lamp behind a curtain.

Brammel broke the silence first, because Brammel always did.

"I knew a miner once," he said, to no one, in the tone he used for beginning stories no one had asked for. "Kesselby. Worked the moonsteel seams under Greywater. Got buried three days when a gallery folded. When they dug him out, do you know what he said? Not 'water.' Not 'food.' He said, *I forgot how the song went.*"

"Song?" Aldous asked.

"Miners' song. They sang in the deep workings—every seam, every shaft, every company had its own. Kesselby swore the singing kept the rock listening. Kept it friendly." Brammel's laugh was a dry rustle. "Superstition, I wrote in my notes at the time. I was young and certain. Certain men make the worst cartographers."

"The Court burned the Vaelthorne songs," Aldous said quietly. "I carried the writs myself. Hearth-songs, lullabies, work-chants. Possession of a forbidden verse was a flogging offense. Third offense, the tongue."

Silence again. But a different silence—one with something coiled inside it, waiting.

It was Elodie who moved. Zephyrine heard the scrape of her boots, saw her ember-glow strengthen as she rose, and when she spoke her voice was strange—half apology, half defiance.

"My grandmother used to sing one," she said. "She thought I didn't remember. She'd sing it while she worked the coals, low, so low I think she half-believed she was only humming. She never told me what it was called. She never told me anything." A breath. "But I remember it."

"You can't—" Aldous started, and stopped himself, because he was the one who had carried the writs, and the shame of it moved across his face like weather. "If anyone heard—"

"Who's going to hear us?" Elodie said. "We're inside a mountain. The Court erased a country. Let me un-erase one song."

And she began to sing.

Her voice was not trained. It cracked on the high line and went thin on the low, and it did not matter at all. The melody was old the way rivers are old—it moved in a mode Zephyrine had no name for, rising and falling in patterns that felt less composed than remembered, as though the song had been dug out of the earth rather than written. The words were in the old tongue, the tongue Zephyrine's blade spoke, and understanding came sideways, the way dreams make sense: *keep the fire, keep the fire, though the roof be stone and the roof be snow, keep the fire.*

Something happened to the dark.

Zephyrine would spend years failing to describe it afterward. The blackness did not lift; it *warmed*. The air itself seemed to lean in. And Elodie's glow—the coal beneath her skin—answered. It rose along her arms like dawn coming up over a ridge, gold spilling from her fingertips, pooling on the stone, and where it pooled the shadows drew back and the walls of their prison became walls again, visible, knowable, almost kind.

"Oh," Brammel whispered. His voice broke in the middle of the word. The old cartographer put his scarred hands over his face and wept without any effort at silence, the way men weep who have been alone with a certainty for forty years and suddenly hear it sung back to them. "That's the *Emberkeeping*. That's—gods, that's the Emberkeeping of the Low Quarter, that's the version they sang at the winter hearths in Carrowmere, I heard it once, I was nine, my mother took me to market—" He lowered his hands, and his face in the firelight was a boy's face for one unguarded moment. "I thought I was the only one left who'd heard it."

"You're not," Elodie said simply.

Aldous had not moved. The knight sat rigid, his ruined shield across his knees, staring at the light on the walls as though it were evidence of a crime—which, in a sense, it was, and he the instrument of it. "I burned a wagonload of songbooks in Drennmark," he said at last, very quietly. "Eleven years ago. I remember the smell." He looked up. "Sing it again."

So she sang it again. And this time Brammel joined, his voice cracked and wandering but finding the mode like a hand finding a rail in the dark, and the two broken voices together made something sturdier than either alone. The fire climbed higher. It was not heat, exactly, or not only heat—it was *company*.

Zephyrine stood apart, as Zephyrine always stood apart, at the edge of the light with their back against the cold stone, watching.

This was the shape of their whole life, they realized. Always the doorway. Always the perimeter. The oath-bound swordspeaker walks roads alone because the oath makes them a weapon and weapons are stored separately from the family silver. They had eaten a hundred meals within earshot of other people's laughter and never once been inside the laugh. They had told themselves it was choice. Freedom. *Choosing one's own roads*—the creed they'd built their life on, before a book in a shrine had taken even that away.

And now here was a thing happening twenty feet away that they wanted with a want that frightened them more than the Ashen Court ever had.

The song ended. In the ringing quiet, Elodie turned her head and found them in the dark—found them instantly, as if the light answered her looking—and held out her hand.

"Your turn," she said.

"I don't sing."

"Everyone sings. Some people just haven't been safe enough yet."

"I'm not safe now. We're sealed in a collapsed pass with three days of food and an army hunting us."

"Yes," Elodie said. "Exactly. So." Her hand stayed open. The firelight lay in it like something offered. "You've walked point every day since the shrine. You've taken every watch, eaten last, slept least. You think we haven't noticed? We're not your cargo, Zephyrine. We're your people. And your people are asking you to sing."

The words went through Zephyrine like the sword's words went through them—straight to the marrow, unavoidable. Their people. Their *people*. The oath had chained them to this girl, yes, had made her pain their pain, had stripped away every exit. But standing here in the warm dark, Zephyrine understood suddenly that the chain and the belonging had become impossible to tell apart, and that this was not the same thing as duty, and that duty had never once asked them to sing.

"I don't know any songs," they said, and their voice was rougher than they intended. "Not yours. Not anyone's. My mother hummed something when I was small. I couldn't tell you what. There's nothing in me to give."

Brammel snorted. "Nonsense. You carry the swordsong. Every swordspeaker does. It's not a weapon, boy—or girl—or whatever you are, forgive an old man's eyes—it's the oldest hearth-song there is. What do you think the oath was *for*, before clerks got hold of it? The blades were forged to guard the hearth-fires. The words in your steel are kitchen words. Doorway words. *Come in, be warm, I'll stand the watch.* Someone, sometime, sang that over a cradle, and it hardened into steel, and you've been walking around all these years thinking you were carrying a weapon."

Zephyrine stared at him.

"That's not true," they said. But even as they said it, they felt the blade at their hip, and the blade felt—warm. Attentive. Like a dog hearing its name.

"It's absolutely true," said Brammel, "and I have the maps to prove it."

Elodie laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, bright as a struck spark—and the fire flared with it, actually flared, gold leaping along the walls, and Zephyrine thought: *the mountain is listening. Kesselby was right. The rock is listening.*

"Come here," Elodie said. "Into the light. Sing the first line. Whatever it is. The first line your blade knows. Just—let it out. We'll catch you."

Zephyrine crossed the distance. It was perhaps fifteen feet of stone floor and it took them a year.

They drew the blade—not raised, never raised, laid flat across both palms like an offering—and closed their eyes, and let the sword speak the way it had spoken on the salt road, except this time they did not fight it, did not brace against it, did not pay attention to the cost. They opened their mouth and the first line came out of them in the old tongue, low and uncertain:

*I stand the door. I stand the door. However long the night, however long the night.*

Their voice cracked. Their voice was terrible. None of it mattered. Because on the second line Elodie's voice rose to meet theirs, and Brammel's underneath, and even Aldous—halting, guilt-hoarse, note-perfect anyway, soldiers learn songs the way they learn drill—even Aldous joined, and the four ragged voices wove the line together and sent it rolling down the sealed throat of the pass.

And the mountain answered.

The fire poured off Elodie's skin in a slow golden tide, filling the chamber, running along the walls like water finding its level, and Zephyrine—eyes open now, singing, aging not at all or caring not at all, unable to tell the difference—saw the light find the crack in the eastern rubble. The hairline seam they'd have missed in daylight. The light pooled there, pressed there, *insisted* there, and far off beyond the stone, faint as memory, something glimmered back.

"There," Brammel breathed, pointing with a shaking finger. "There's the way. The fire sees it. The fire *remembers* the pass—it was carved by Vaelthorne's engineers, it remembers being open—"

"Tomorrow," Aldous said. "We dig tomorrow. Tonight—" and the knight, the ex-knight, the man of ledgers and writs, hesitated, and then finished, "tonight we finish the song."

They sang until they could not. Hearth-songs Elodie half-knew and Brammel half-remembered, stitched together between the two of them into whole cloth; work-chants Aldous had confiscated and secretly copied; and twice, three times, the swordsong itself, which was not a dirge at all, never had been, but a lullaby grown strong, a promise grown into steel. Zephyrine sang until their voice was gone and found that what replaced it was better—something unclenched in their chest that had been clenched since the shrine, since the margins, since the date that bore their name. The prophecy had made them a character in someone else's book. But no author living could write this: four voices in a tomb, making light.

Much later, when the others slept in the gold-warm dimness, Zephyrine sat with the blade across their knees and felt it hum contentment, faint as a purr.

*Duty and affection are not the same thing,* they had written in their own heart, bitter, weeks ago. They amended it now, silently, in the old tongue, in the language the steel dreamed in: *Duty binds. Belonging chooses. And I choose.*

Above them, thirty feet of stone. Ahead of them, an army. Behind them, everything either of them had lost.

And here, in the dark, a fire that answered when called, and a song that was becoming a home faster than any house could be built.

Zephyrine closed their eyes and, for the first time since the shrine, slept without dreaming of the prophecy.

They woke to Brammel's hand clamped over their mouth and the old man's eyes wide with terror in the pre-dawn gloom.

"Listen," he whispered.

Zephyrine listened. Beneath the silence of the mountain, faint and rhythmic and unmistakable, drifting up through the cracks in the eastern rubble—from the far side of the pass, from the direction of Vaelthorne—

Someone was singing back.
