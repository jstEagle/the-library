## 14. The Traitor's Toll

The guide's name was Wren Halloway, and for eleven days Elodie had believed in him the way you believe in a bridge—without thinking about it, without ever once looking down.

He was a lean man with a climber's shoulders and a laugh like dry leaves, and he had appeared at the heretic's hearth three days after they buried their fear of the Ashen Court and started walking again. He knew the high passes east of the salt road, he said. Knew them the way a shepherd knows his sheep, by name and temperament. *The Widow's Stair. The Hollow Tooth. The Grey Larder.* He could take them over the Thornfell spine in six days instead of sixteen, above the cloud line where the Court's riders were useless and even the Registrar's long eyes couldn't follow.

Zephyrine hadn't trusted him. Elodie remembered that now with a clarity that felt like a knife going in—remembered Zephyrine standing apart from the fire that first night, arms crossed, watching Wren eat their stew and tell his jokes, and saying quietly, when Elodie came to sit beside them: *"Nobody knows these passes that well unless they've sold something on them."*

But Brammel had vouched for him. Brammel, who trusted almost no one, who had spent forty years being hunted for what lived inside his own head—who had looked at Wren Halloway across the hearth-fire and said, in the voice of a man reading an old map: *"I knew his father. Good family. Salt traders out of Corrow Vale."* And that had been enough. That had been enough because they were all so tired of suspicion, so starved for the shape of trust, that when it offered itself wearing a familiar face they had opened the door and let it in.

Now, on the twelfth morning, on a shelf of grey stone two hundred feet above a valley full of wrong-colored mist, Elodie watched Wren Halloway stand at the edge of the pass with a signal lantern in his hand and light it.

Three times red. Twice white. The old salt-road code, the one every smuggler and courier in the mountains learned before they learned their letters. She didn't know how she knew it—except that she did know it, the way she knew the fire under her skin, suddenly and completely, as if some part of her had been watching him for days and had only now been permitted to speak.

"Wren," she said.

He turned. And she saw it in his face—not guilt, exactly. Something worse than guilt. *Relief.* The look of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

"Don't," he said. Not unkindly. "Please don't make it harder than it is."

Below them, far down in the valley mist, horns began to sound. Three notes, then silence, then three again. The Ashen Court's marching call. Elodie had heard it once before, through the burning market town, and her body remembered it the way a burn scar remembers fire.

Behind her, Zephyrine's hand went to their sword.

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way disasters do—all the slow hours of walking compressed into a handful of terrible minutes.

Wren talked while the horns talked underneath him, and Elodie stood there and let the words hit her like sleet. They had his sister. Had held her since autumn, in a ledger-house in Corrow Vale, in one of those grey rooms where the clerks kept people the way they kept records—neatly, alphabetically, indefinitely. All he had to do was deliver the route. Where they'd cross the spine, when, how many. The Court's riders would do the rest, and his sister would walk out of her grey room with a receipt signed in triplicate, and Wren Halloway would go back to selling passages over the mountains and never sleep a whole night again for the rest of his life.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, and it was the most useless thing Elodie had ever heard. "You were kind to me. That made it worse, if it helps."

"It doesn't," Zephyrine said.

They had come up beside her without sound, the way they did, and Elodie saw that they weren't looking at Wren at all. They were looking past him, down the pass, reading the terrain the way Brammel read maps—with their whole body, with some sense older than sight. Their face had gone very still, the stillness Elodie had learned to dread, because it was the stillness of a person counting costs.

"How many?" Zephyrine asked.

"Two hundred," Wren said. "Maybe more. Knights on the lower road, arbalists on the flanks. The Registrar himself is with them." He hesitated. "There's a place. Half a mile on, where the pass narrows—the Hollow Tooth. One man with a blade could hold it. For a while." He said it flatly, the way you report weather. He had sold them the route; the arithmetic of what the Court would do with it was not his to carry. Or he had told himself it wasn't.

"You should run," Elodie said, and was surprised by her own voice—how level it came out, how cold. "While they're still climbing. Take your lantern and your clean conscience and run."

Something flickered across his face—hurt, maybe, or the ghost of the man he might have been—and then he was gone, down the scree, moving fast, choosing his sister the way everyone in this story eventually chose someone.

Elodie stood looking after him until Zephyrine's hand closed on her shoulder.

"He gave us one true thing," they said. "The Hollow Tooth. We can hate him and use it at the same time. That's the whole art of surviving, Elodie. Nobody teaches it because nobody survives teaching it."

Then they turned, and called the company together, and began—calmly, terribly calmly—to decide who would die.

* * *

Brammel decided it before anyone else could.

They argued, of course. Zephyrine argued hardest, which surprised no one and fooled no one either, because Elodie had learned by now that Zephyrine argued hardest precisely when they intended all along to lose. Ser Aldous offered himself—he was a knight, he said, holding ground was the one prayer he still knew how to say—but Zephyrine shook their head. Aldous was known to the Court. If they took him alive, everything he'd told them would be worth nothing, and worse, they'd parade him. Make an example. The defector who knelt, broken on a wheel of paperwork.

Brammel listened to all of it standing slightly apart, the way he always did, one hand resting on the strap of his satchel—the great oilskin roll that held forty years of forbidden cartography, the maps the Court had burned and he had redrawn from memory, and memory upon memory, until his mind was more country than country.

Then he said, in the mild voice he used for correcting other people's distances: "It has to be me. I'm the slowest. I'm the oldest. And I'm the only one they'll stop to kill."

No one answered, because it was true, and truth in that grey morning light sat among them like a fifth traveler.

"They'll want the maps," he went on. "The Registrar especially. A man who erases a country cannot rest while anyone remembers its coastlines. They'll come to me first, and they'll be careful, and careful men are slow men, and slow men lose passes." He smiled—a small, creased, unbearable smile. "Besides. I've been carrying Vaelthorne alone for forty years. It's time I put some of it down."

"Brammel—" Elodie started, and found she had nothing. No argument. Only the word itself, his name, held out like a cupped hand.

He came to her then, and took her hands, and turned them over, and studied her palms as though reading them—which, in a way, he was. The fire lived close to her skin now; she could feel it listening.

"You're the last map I'll ever need to draw," he said. "Do you understand? Everything I am goes with you. Here." He unslung the satchel and pressed it against her chest, both his hands over hers, folding her fingers shut around the strap. "Half-finished. The eastern valleys are blank—I never got them down before they burned the surveyors. You'll have to fill them in yourself. With your feet. That's the only honest way to map anything anyway."

"I can't carry this," she whispered. "I already carry—"

"Yes, you can." His voice gentled. "Child, listen to me. The Court thinks a country is a line in a ledger. Your grandmother thought it was a coal in a bloodline. They're both wrong. A country is a *map and a song and a grave*, and right now, in all the world, you are the only person carrying all three. That's not a burden. That's a coronation. It just doesn't feel like one yet."

Down in the valley, the horns changed. Closer now. The climbing had begun.

* * *

The Hollow Tooth was a gap between two leaning towers of granite, twenty paces wide, walled on the south side by a drop that swallowed its own echoes. Brammel walked to the middle of it and set down his walking staff and looked around him with the air of a man choosing a chair by a fire.

"The rest of you have half a mile of goat-track to the far saddle," he said briskly. "Goat-track means single file, single file means slow, slow means every second I give you is a life. So don't waste them arguing. Go."

Zephyrine went to him last. Elodie hung back on the track, pretending to check Aldous's bandages, watching them speak too low for her to hear. Whatever passed between them took less than a minute. At the end of it, Zephyrine drew their sword—not the speaking of it, just the steel, bare and plain—and touched the flat of it to Brammel's shoulder, left and right, the way you'd knight a man, or bless one.

Then they turned away fast, and Elodie saw their jaw working, and understood that some farewells are only survivable if you lie about what they are.

They climbed. Single file, as ordered—Aldous first, then Zephyrine, then Elodie with the satchel strapped tight across her back, heavier than any map had a right to be, heavy as a sleeping child. The goat-track switchbacked up the far wall of the pass, and from it she could see down onto the Hollow Tooth, and she hated herself for looking, and looked anyway.

The first riders came up out of the mist within the hour.

She heard it before she saw it—the ring of iron on stone, the jingle of harness, the wet snort of horses that had climbed too far too fast. Then they emerged from the cloud in ones and twos, ash-grey cloaks, visors down, the white ledger-line sigil stitched on every shoulder, and Brammel stood in the middle of the gap with his hands folded on his staff like a shepherd waiting out rain.

"Hold!" The lead rider raised a gauntleted fist. "In the name of the Ashen Court and the Lord Registrar, surrender the cartographer and the documents—"

"No," said Brammel, pleasantly.

What happened next, Elodie would spend years turning over, and never once find it smaller. Brammel reached into his coat and brought out papers—dozens of them, sheets covered in his fine cramped hand, coastlines and mountain-watersheds and street-plans of a city the world denied had ever existed—and he began, methodically, to feed them into the wind off the drop. Page after page after page, sailing out over the void like pale birds, like snow falling upward.

The riders surged forward to catch them. Of course they did. Erasure was their sacrament; knowledge was their enemy; and here was an old man scattering a country into the sky. They broke formation, crowded the narrow gap, horses screaming on the wet stone—

And Brammel pulled the keystone.

She never learned where he'd found the loose stone, or how long he'd known it was loose—whether he'd scouted it that morning or whether some cartographer's instinct, forty years deep, had read the fracture lines in the rock the moment he first stood in the gap. It didn't matter. The tower leaned, and groaned, and came down across the mouth of the pass with a sound like the word *no* spoken by the mountain itself, and the dust rose up white and total, and Elodie was still running, dragged by the sleeve, by Zephyrine, by the terrible momentum of the living, and the last thing she saw through the settling haze was nothing at all.

* * *

They walked until dark. No one spoke. Grief made its own weather, and they moved through it hunched, each alone inside it.

They camped in a hollow below the far saddle, a fold of stone with a view of nothing, and Aldous built a small fire because Aldous had learned that the living need fires whether or not they deserve them. Zephyrine sat apart, cleaning a sword that didn't need cleaning. Elodie sat with the satchel in her lap and could not open it.

It was Zephyrine who finally came and sat beside her. For a long time they said nothing. Then:

"When my oath took me," they said, "the first thing I lost was my teacher. She died buying me the hour I needed to run. I was nineteen. I decided grief was a debt, and debts get paid, and once paid you're free." They turned the sword slowly in the firelight. "Forty years later I know better. It's not a debt. It's an inheritance. You don't pay it. You carry it. And it gets heavier, and somehow you get stronger, and neither of those facts is comforting, but they're both true."

Elodie looked down at her hands. "He said I was the last map he'd ever need to draw."

"Then don't leave yourself blank," Zephyrine said. "Open it."

Her fingers were clumsy on the buckles. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, lay the maps—sheaf upon sheaf, Vaelthorne rising out of them in ink and memory: the terraced vineyards of the Sorrel Coast, the bell-towers of a capital whose name the Court had struck from every record, the little annotated margins where Brammel had written things like *good wells here* and *my mother was born in this valley* and, once, simply, *it was beautiful*. And beneath them all, the unfinished sheet. Eastern Vaelthorne. Blank beyond the river. Waiting.

The fire under her skin stirred, warm against her ribs, and Elodie understood—finally, wholly—what she was carrying. The coal. The map. The memory. The graves and the songs and the good wells. Not a burden. A coronation. It didn't feel like one yet. But she laid her palm flat on the blank eastern half of the country, and the dragon-fire pulsed once beneath her skin, like a heart answering a knock, and she thought: *I will fill you in myself. With my feet.*

"We move at first light," Zephyrine said behind her, rising. "The pass is closed behind us, but the Court has other roads, and the Registrar doesn't forgive delays. Two days' hard walking and we're into the unmapped country. After that—" they paused, and Elodie heard what they didn't say, the price waiting at the end of every road now, the arch and the oath and the life freely given. "After that, we'll see."

Elodie rolled the maps closed and bound them, and did not look at Zephyrine, because she had begun, that night, to practice her first real skill of leadership: the lie you keep in your eyes so hope can stay in someone else's.

Above the hollow, between the peaks, the stars were coming out—and they were wrong. Slightly, subtly wrong, arranged in patterns no navigator had charted, burning over country that existed on no map but hers.

Tomorrow they would step off the edge of the world. Elodie tightened the satchel strap across her chest, felt the fire settle against her heart like a second pulse, and slept sitting up, one hand on the blank half of Vaelthorne, keeping watch over the empty places she had promised to fill.
