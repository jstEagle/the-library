## 16. The Ashen Court Arrives

*Zephyrine*

The valley of ash had a silence to it that Zephyrine had never learned to trust.

Other silences were merely empty—the hush of high passes before snowfall, the held breath of a forest when wolves passed through. But this silence was *made*. It had been manufactured a century ago by clerks with ink-stained fingers and ledgers full of other people's names, and it lay over the valley like a lid on a pot, like a hand pressed flat over a mouth. Even the wind seemed reluctant here. It came down from the Thornfell shoulders in long, apologetic breaths, stirred the grey drifts against the door's stone arch, and died without echo.

Zephyrine stood at the lip of the ridge above it and felt their own heartbeat counting down.

They had known, in the way one knows weather, that this day would come. Brammel had told them—Brammel, who was ash himself now, scattered somewhere along the salt road behind them—and Ser Aldous had confirmed it with the grim arithmetic of a man who had counted ranks for twenty years: three thousand spears, two hundred knights in ashen plate, the Court's whole marching weight, and at its head a carriage of black lacquered wood drawn by six pale horses, in which rode the Lord Registrar himself.

Three days ago, Aldous had said. Two, after the traitor's toll. Now Zephyrine watched the dust rise beyond the western shoulder of the valley—a brown-gold smear against the morning sky, wide as a storm front—and thought: *today, then.*

"You should sit down," Elodie said beside them. She did not look away from the dust. Her hands were steady, which surprised him; her voice was steady too, and that surprised him more. "You've been standing since before dawn."

"I've been standing since I found that book in a shrine," Zephyrine said. "Everything since has just been different ways of not sitting down."

That earned him half a smile. Half. The other half of her face was turned toward the arch, toward the plain grey stones and the worn inscription across its lintel—the first words of the swordspeaker's oath, cut so deep into the rock that a century of ash had not filled them:

*WHAT IS GIVEN FREELY CANNOT BE TAKEN.*

She had been reading those words all morning. He had watched her lips move over them more than once, silent, like a woman rehearsing an argument she intended to win.

Below, in the shallow bowl of the valley, the others were making what preparations could be made, which was to say almost none. There was no fortifying an open field. There was no outfighting three thousand. What there was: a fire pit, banked low; a circle of bedrolls that would never be slept in again; Ser Aldous in his old armor, the ashen paint scraped away, kneeling with his sword across his knees like a man at prayer who no longer believed in the god but remembered the posture; and Marta of the hearth-folk, the youngest of the exiles who had joined them after the pass, sharpening a woodsman's axe with the patient strokes of someone milking a goat.

And the arch itself. Waiting. Patient as only stone can be patient.

Zephyrine's hand drifted to the hilt at their hip. The blade did not stir. It never stirred anymore unless spoken to, and Zephyrine had been rationing their words like a miser rations coin, because they knew now exactly what each one cost. A word of power was a year off the bone. A line of the swordsong was a decade. They had done the sums in the dark watches of the night, lying awake while Elodie pretended to sleep nearby, and the sums always came out the same: enough. Just barely enough. One full singing, and nothing after.

They had not told her the final number. They suspected she knew anyway. She had stopped asking questions whose answers she didn't want, which was its own kind of lie, and Zephyrine loved her for it and hated that love made liars of them both.

"They'll want to talk first," Elodie said. "Aldous says the Registrar always talks first. He likes to read the charges aloud."

"I know." Zephyrine had heard it from Aldous too—the ritual of it, the terrible bureaucratic theater. The Lord Registrar did not rage. He did not threaten. He *read*. He stood before the thing he meant to destroy and recited its entry from the great ledger of the Court, as though naming a thing were the same as owning it, as though ink were a chain. Vaelthorne had not fallen to armies first. It had fallen to a pen. The armies had only come to enforce the citation.

"And then?" Elodie asked.

"Then he tries to take you," Zephyrine said. "Or the fire. Whichever he can reach first. And we don't let him."

It was a simple sentence. It took everything they had left to say it lightly.

Elodie looked at them then—really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether to believe someone—and Zephyrine saw the coal under her skin answer the look. Faint light moved beneath the skin of her forearms, gold-shot with red, like embers seen through a shutter's slats. She had stopped hiding it days ago. Out here, at the end of every road, there seemed little point.

"Zeph," she said. "If it comes to the song—"

"It won't come to the song."

"If it does—"

"*It won't.*" Sharper than intended. The silence after it was worse than the dust on the horizon. Zephyrine breathed out. "Elodie. Let me have this. Whatever else the oath takes from me, let me keep the part where I get to choose how I spend it. That's all a swordspeaker ever really owns. The choosing."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her swallow whatever she had been about to say—the same words she'd been carrying since the night she'd learned the oath's terms, the words Zephyrine had been pretending not to hear forming behind her teeth for weeks. *There has to be another way.* She believed it. Gods help them both, she believed it with the whole ferocious weight of her, and some treacherous corner of Zephyrine's heart believed it too, and that corner was going to get everyone killed if it wasn't kept fed on lies.

The dust cloud crested the western ridge.

For a long moment nothing resolved out of it—just movement, vast and slow, like the mountain itself shrugging. Then the light caught the first rank of spearpoints, and the air filled with a sound Zephyrine had heard once before, years ago, from the far side of a river they'd been lucky enough to cross: the sound of three thousand feet finding a single rhythm. Not a drumbeat. A footfall. The Ashen Court did not march to music. It marched to its own certainty.

"There," Elodie whispered.

At the head of the column, small with distance but unmistakable, a figure walked alone. No horse. No armor. Grey robes, grey hood, and in his hands, carried open before him like a relic or a shield, a book.

The Lord Registrar had come to read the charges.

*Elodie*

She had imagined him wrong.

In the visions the fire gave her—in the burning memory of Vaelthorne's fall that lived behind her ribs like a second heart—the man who erased her country had been a monster. Vast, crowned in smoke, his shadow falling across a burning city, his voice a landslide. That was how grief drew its villains: large.

The man walking up the valley floor was thin. That was the first obscenity of him. He was *thin*, narrow-shouldered, moving with the careful economy of an old clerk who had spent fifty years at a desk, and when the column halted at his raised hand—a small gesture, hardly more than lifting a finger—he folded his hands over his book and waited, patient as a man waiting for ink to dry.

He was close enough now that Elodie could see his face. It was a forgettable face. That was the second obscenity. Grey eyes, grey stubble, a scar of old sunburn peeling at the nose. He might have been a tax assessor. He might have been anyone's uncle. And this, she understood suddenly, was the true horror of the Ashen Court—not cruelty, not madness, but *ordinariness*, evil wearing the face of a man who balanced accounts, who filed things correctly, who had looked at a living country of songs and kitchens and children and seen only a discrepancy to be corrected.

"Vasse," he said. His voice carried up the slope without effort, dry and clear, the voice of a man accustomed to being the only one permitted to speak. "Elodie Vasse. Descendant-line of Vaelthorne, last vessel of the unregistered flame." He opened the book on his palms. "I am Corvane, Lord Registrar of the Ashen Court, Keeper of the Ledger, Eraser of Entries. I have come to close the account."

Ser Aldous rose from his knees. The scrape of his armor was very loud in the made-silence of the valley.

"My lord Registrar," Aldous called, and his voice shook only a little. "You know me. Aldous Renne, knight-errant, twenty-two years sworn. I kneel—" and here he did kneel, deliberately, in the ash, before rising again to his full height, "—no longer. I bear witness instead. This girl committed no crime. The crime was committed against her, before her birth, by the ledger you carry."

Corvane regarded him with mild interest, as a man regards a document filed in the wrong drawer.

"Knight Renne," he said. "Your entry was amended this morning. Desertion, theft of arms, treason of sympathy." He turned a page. "It is a short entry. Most entries are short, in the end." Then his grey eyes lifted past Aldous, past the little company, past the banked fire and the bedrolls, and settled on Elodie herself.

She felt the fire flinch under her skin. She made it be still.

"Come down, child," Corvane said, and his voice gentled, which was worse. "You have misunderstood your inheritance. You believe you carry something precious. You carry a *debt*. The flame is an asset of the Crown of Ash, misappropriated a century past, and every day it burns outside our keeping, the account worsens—for you most of all. Fire unledgered burns its bearer. You have felt it. The fevers. The dreams. Give it into proper registration, and you will be free of it. Free of the hunting, free of the burning, free of the prophecy that chains you. I offer you"—he smiled, a small correct smile—"an erasure of your own. A clean page. Is that not what every hunted soul wants? To be unknown?"

Elodie came down the slope.

She heard Zephyrine move behind her—heard the creak of leather, the hiss of an indrawn breath—and lifted one hand without turning. *Wait.* Because she had thought about this moment, dreamed it, rehearsed it against the lintel's inscription, and she would not do it screaming or hidden or from behind another's blade. She walked down into the ash with the fire climbing warm up her throat and stood ten paces from the Lord Registrar of the Ashen Court, close enough to see that his eyes were the exact color of the ledger's paper.

"You're right about one thing," she said. Her voice did not shake. The fire steadied it, or she steadied the fire; by now she honestly didn't know which. "I am tired of being hunted. I'm tired of fevers and dreams and men with writs. But you're wrong about the debt." She let the light rise—not much, just enough, gold threading up her wrists and collarbone until the ash around her feet glimmered. "This isn't a debt. It's a *hearth*. And hearths aren't registered. They're *kept*."

Something moved behind Corvane's grey eyes. Something very old and very tired and, for one naked instant, afraid.

Then he sighed, closed the book, and stepped back.

"Take the vessel," he said. "Spare the swordspeaker if convenient. The rest are already written."

The column moved.

It did not charge—that was the nightmare of it, the thing Elodie would remember all her life. Three thousand spears did not roar or rush. They *advanced*, at parade pace, shields locked, boots finding the same rhythm, a wall of ash-grey iron coming up the valley floor with the unhurried inevitability of a tide or a deadline. Above them the knights wheeled out wide on both flanks, lances lowering, and the morning sun caught their visors and turned them into a hundred blank faces.

"ELodie!" Zephyrine was beside her, hauling her back up the slope, and behind them Marta was roaring the first note of a hearth-song—not a battle cry, never that, the exiles did not have battle cries—and Aldous set his borrowed helm on his head and drew his sword, and the fire in Elodie's blood rose to meet the dawn like it had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this morning.

"To the arch!" Zephyrine shouted. "Hold the arch! Everything we built—we hold it *here*!"

They ran for the stones, the four of them and the fire, and the wall of ash came on, and Elodie felt—clearly, calmly, the way the fire felt things—her whole found family gathering around one plain grey doorway, ready to spend themselves against a ledger.

Behind the Registrar, unseen by any of them, a page in his open book began, very faintly, to smolder.
