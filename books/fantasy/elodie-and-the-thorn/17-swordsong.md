## 17. Swordsong

The army came down out of the northern pass like ash falling uphill.

Zephyrine stood at the arch with their sword drawn and did not count the enemy. Counting was a thing for people who expected to survive by arithmetic. They counted instead what they were fighting for: Elodie's breathing behind them, quick and stubborn; Brammel's map folded against her heart; Ser Aldous on the left flank with a borrowed spear and a face like a man settling accounts; the hearth-song still caught in the stones of the valley, humming faintly where they had sung it in the dark of the collapsed pass, as if the land itself had learned the words.

And beyond all of it, the arch itself. Plain grey stone. The first words of the oath cut into its keystone in letters worn shallow by a century of forgetting:

*A blade is a promise. A promise is a life.*

They had read those words a hundred times since childhood and never once understood them. Now the understanding sat in their chest like a swallowed coal.

At the head of the host rode a figure in white-and-grey, unarmored, unhurried, holding a ledger open against his saddle as though the charging of an army were merely an errand between entries. The Lord Registrar. Zephyrine had never seen him before and knew him instantly, the way one knows a scar one has carried since birth. Here was the hand that had drawn the line through Vaelthorne. Here was the clerk who had burned a country into a footnote.

He raised one finger, and the army stopped.

"Zephyrine Nystrom." His voice carried across the valley without effort, flat and reasonable, the voice of a man reading aloud in a quiet room. "Swordspeaker, oath-bound, last of your line. You are named in a document older than you are. I have read it. I have *catalogued* it." He turned a page. "You stand before the door of a country that does not exist, defending a girl who is, legally speaking, stolen property. I am here to correct an error in the record. Stand aside, and I will note your cooperation favorably."

Zephyrine laughed. It came out of them rough and strange, half a sob dressed in armor.

"You keep a ledger," they called back. "Tell me—what column do the dead go in? The ones from Vaelthorne? The ones your clerks wrote out of existence? Is there a line for a hundred thousand hearths?"

The Registrar considered this with the patience of a man weighing grain. "There is a line for everything," he said. "That is the point of lines."

*That is the point,* Zephyrine thought, and the thought went through them colder than any blade. That was the whole of him. Not cruelty. Not even ambition, not really. Just the conviction that the world was a book to be balanced, and that anything which refused to balance—a fire that lived, a girl who ran, a country that remembered—was an error to be struck.

Well then. Zephyrine lifted their sword.

"I am an error," they said. "Come correct me."

* * *

The first wave broke against them like water against a sea-stack.

Aldous fought on the left, silent, economical, his spear moving with the terrible competence of a man who had spent thirty years learning exactly how knights die. On the right, three of the hearth-keepers from the hidden valley had come down overnight when word reached them—the old woman Sef with a woodcutter's axe, and two brothers whose names Zephyrine finally knew because they had sung together in the dark. The found family, such as it was, such as it had become without anyone declaring it, held the width of the arch.

But there were hundreds. There were so many.

Zephyrine fought the way they had always fought—fast, low, economical, a smuggler's fighting, a survivor's fighting, every motion chosen, nothing wasted. They took a knight's knee, another's throat, turned a halberd into the dirt and put their boot on the man's chest long enough to break his grip. Blood on their hands, none of it theirs yet. Behind them Elodie shouted something—they heard the fear in it, and the fury—and the air over the arch shimmered faintly gold, the dragon-fire answering her heartbeat, turning aside arrows that came arcing in.

It would not be enough. Zephyrine knew it the way you know weather coming. The line would bend, then break, and Elodie would be taken, and the last living fire would go into a ledger-line and be closed forever.

Unless.

Their eyes went to the keystone. *A blade is a promise. A promise is a life.*

They had known since the salt road. They had known since Brammel told them, gentle as a man setting down something fragile, what the full swordsong cost—not the few words Zephyrine had spoken in desperation, each one a year off their life, but the whole song, the true song, twelve lines of the old tongue that no swordspeaker had spoken complete in a hundred years. Twelve lines. Not twelve years. The oath did not deal in years. It dealt in *everything*, and it took in proportion to what it gave.

Brammel was dead. He had died holding the pass so they could reach this place, and he had pressed his unfinished map into Elodie's hands, and Zephyrine had sworn then—privately, silently, in the way that mattered—that his death would buy more than distance.

"Aldous!" they shouted. "Fall back to the arch! All of you—*to the arch!*"

"We can't hold—" Sef began.

"That's not why. Get behind me. *Now.*"

Something in their voice must have carried, because Aldous bellowed the order himself, and the defenders gave ground, stumbling back through the arch's shadow, and the Ashen host surged forward into the gap with a sound like a tide going over gravel.

Zephyrine planted their feet in the center of the archway, alone, facing south into the coming flood.

They let go of everything they had ever used to survive.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about the old tongue: it is not a language of commands. It is a language of *promises*. Every line of the swordsong is a vow, and the world keeps the vows, and the singer pays the keeping.

Zephyrine drew breath until their ribs ached, and spoke the first line.

*"By the road I chose and the road that chose me—"*

Power went through them like winter going through a shuttered house. Their hair went grey at the temples between one syllable and the next; they felt it happen, felt the years arrive like guests who had been waiting a long time outside. The first rank of knights staggered as the words struck them—an old tongue remembers every oath-breaker, and the Ashen Court was built on broken ones—and two fell simply from hearing it.

The Registrar's face changed for the first time. Not fear. Recognition. He knew what he was watching. He began, quietly, to write.

*"—by the door kept shut and the door kept true—"*

Second line. Zephyrine's hands aged, the knuckles swelling, old scars rising silver-white. But the sword grew lighter. No—it did not grow lighter; *they* grew stronger, the promise filling them the way fire fills a lamp, and when the third rank came on they met it with a stroke that sang, actually sang, a clear high note that sheared a pikehead from its shaft ten feet away.

*"—by the hearth I never had and the hearth I hold—"*

That line hurt worst so far, because it was true. They thought of the doorway of the heretic house, standing outside in the cold while Elodie was welcomed as kin. They thought of a shrine in the mountains, a book in the margins, a name written by someone dead a century who had nonetheless known them. Family, it turned out, was not the thing you were born into. It was the thing you stood in front of when the army came.

*"—by the map unfinished and the hand that drew it—"*

Brammel. The words tasted of ink and blood. Grey spread down Zephyrine's arms now, and deep lines carved themselves around their mouth, and somewhere behind them Elodie was screaming their name, and they could not turn around, because if they turned around they would stop, and if they stopped everyone behind them would burn or be taken.

*"—by the fire that lives though the country is ash—"*

The fifth line went out of them like a shout, and the whole forward rank of the Ashen host reeled, shields splintering, horses screaming and bolting. The dragon-fire above the arch flared gold in answer, and for one moment the valley was lit like noon, and every knight in that army saw, written in light on the stones of the door, the shape of the country their masters had erased—towers, terraces, orchards, hearth-smoke rising—there and gone like a reflection in disturbed water.

They saw what had been taken from them. Some of them lowered their weapons. Most did not.

*"—by the song we sang in the dark—"*

Six. Zephyrine's spine bent; they straightened it by will alone. Their vision blurred at the edges, the world going soft and bright, and they understood distantly that they were now older than Brammel had been, older than anyone they had ever loved, and the song was only half done.

The Registrar spurred forward through his own ranks, calm as a man crossing a courtyard, pen still moving. "Nystrom," he called, almost kindly. "Every word is an entry. I am recording the expenditure. When you fall, the record will show precisely what your defiance purchased. Nothing. A number. A closed account."

*"—by the oath unwritten and the oath rewritten—"*

Seven. And here Zephyrine did something no swordspeaker had ever done, something the song had no note for: they changed a word. Only one. They took the line that had bound their order for a thousand years—the line that said the promise was owed, that the life was *demanded*—and they sang it as a gift freely made. *Rewritten*, they sang, instead of *required*, and the power that came down into them was different in character, warmer, wilder, as if the old tongue itself had been waiting a millennium for someone to try.

The arch blazed. The keystone letters filled with light.

*"—by the child who carries what we could not keep—"*

Eight. Elodie. Zephyrine's knees buckled and locked again. White now, all white, hair and brows, and their hands were an old woman's hands, an old man's hands, both and neither, and still the sword rose and fell and each fall of it was a small thunderclap. A knight got inside their guard and Zephyrine took the man's blade on their forearm rather than yield a foot of ground, and the bone cracked, and the song did not falter, because the song was past needing the body that carried it.

*"—by the friend who lied to save me and the lie that was love—"*

Nine. Elodie's lie, told over bandages in the dark of the unmapped country—*the oath doesn't really require a death, I misread it*—the first kindness anyone had ever done Zephyrine by deceiving them. The words went out carrying that too, and three knights of the Ashen host, hearing it, dropped their swords entirely and would not pick them up again.

*"—by the traitor's toll and the price already paid—"*

Ten. For the guide who sold them. For the blood already spent, Brammel's, the hearth-keepers', all the coin this road had cost. The valley floor trembled. Cracks ran out from the arch like roots.

*"—by the door that opens not for death but for choice—"*

Eleven. And the Registrar finally stopped writing, because the eleventh line was not in any manuscript he had catalogued either. Zephyrine was composing now, live, dying and authoring in the same breath, and the old tongue rose around them like water around a drowning swimmer and became, impossibly, a current that carried instead of drowned.

One line left.

The host was broken, streaming back up the pass, but the Registrar sat his horse ten feet away, alone now, ledger open, watching them with an expression that was almost reverence. "Magnificent," he said softly. "I will enter it verbatim."

*"—and by my own name, which I give, and which no one may take—"*

Twelve.

* * *

Silence came down on the valley like snowfall.

The army was gone—fled, scattered, kneeling in places, a white-and-grey tide rolled back out of a valley that now smelled of lightning and hot stone. The Registrar turned his horse, slowly, and rode north without hurry, and Zephyrine, sagging against the arch, watched him go and could not lift the sword to follow, and understood that some part of them was glad, and hated that part, and let it be.

They slid down the stone until they were sitting.

Hands touched their face—young hands, warm hands, hands that burned if they stayed too long and stayed anyway. Elodie knelt in front of them, and her face was wet, and behind her Aldous and Sef and the brothers stood in a ragged half-circle, and no one spoke, because there was nothing left in the world that needed saying loudly.

"Well," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out papery, ancient, a stranger's voice. They looked at their own hands and laughed, a dry rustle. "I had plans for these."

"Don't." Elodie's voice cracked. "Don't do that. Don't make it a joke."

"It's the only kind I have left." They caught her wrist—gently; they had no strength left for anything else. "Listen to me. The twelfth line. Did you hear it? I gave the name. Freely. That's the terms, Elodie. That's how the door opens. Not taken. *Given.*"

Her jaw set in a way they knew very well by now, the way it set before rooftop escapes and refusals and every act of magnificent insubordination she had ever committed against fate. "I heard it," she said.

"And?"

"And I've been thinking," Elodie Vasse said, "about choices."

She rose. She walked toward the arch—to the keystone, to the blazing letters, to the door that waited for a swordspeaker's life—and Zephyrine, old and ruined and triumphant in the same breath, watched her go and felt the oath pull tight around their heart like a wire, and understood, with the last clarity of a spent life, that the prophecy was not finished with either of them.

The fire in her hands had begun to glow.
