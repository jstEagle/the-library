## 20. The Last Entry

The ledger weighed more than any sword Aldous had ever carried.

That was the first thing he understood about it, standing at the gate of the new holding in the Thornfell valleys with the book strapped to his back in a frame of iron and ashwood—the same frame the Court used to transport condemned documents, though he had reversed the straps so it rode against his spine rather than his chest, as if he could not bear to have it between himself and the world. Forty years of entries. Eleven thousand pages of vellum and pressed ash-pulp, bound in grey calfskin stamped with the Registrar's seal: a closed circle around an open eye. It weighed as much as a man. Perhaps that was the point. Perhaps a ledger meant to hold the weight of an erased country had to be built like a coffin.

The gate was not yet a gate. It was the arch—*the* arch—hauled stone by stone from the valley of ash over two seasons of labor, re-erected here where the Thornfell passes opened into green country, because Elodie Vasse had said that a country made of people needed a door, and doors were what Vaelthorne had always been good at. Someone had already hung a lantern beside it. Someone had carved a small shelf into the stone for travelers' offerings: a heel of bread, a copper bit, a child's carved horse. The arch's inscription still read, in letters worn nearly smooth:

*THE FIRST WORD OF THE OATH IS A NAME.*

Aldous stood before it for a long time without moving. He had ridden eleven days from the Court's winter seat, alone, in plain clothes, having resigned nothing because there was nothing to resign—a knight of the Ashen Court could not resign; he could only be struck from the rolls, and they had done that within the hour of his departure, striking him out with the same dry pen-stroke they had once used on a whole nation. There was a bitter symmetry in it he almost appreciated. Ser Aldous Merric Vale, entered and exited in the same ledger, the entry and the erasure written by the same hand.

Except that hand was ash now. The Lord Registrar had died at the arch—or not died, exactly; Aldous had seen what remained after Zephyrine's swordsong passed through the host like weather, and *remained* was too generous a word—but the ledger had survived, because ledgers always survived. That was their nature. Fire took houses, war took cities, grief took people entire, and somewhere in a locked archive a clerk with steady hands wrote down what was lost and called it order.

He had taken it on the last night. Walked into the archive past three sleeping clerks who did not stir, because some part of them, perhaps, had been waiting years for someone to come and do this thing, and their sleeping bodies knew better than their waking minds to stand aside. He had carried it out through the postern gate under his cloak like a thief, and the strangeness of that—that the single most complete record of the Court's crime should be so easy to steal, because no one guarded it, because no one thought anyone would want it—had kept him awake through every night of the ride east.

Now he unstrapped the frame and set it down at the foot of the arch, and the sound it made against the stone was the sound of something being laid to rest.

"You're the knight."

Aldous turned. A girl stood at the edge of the lantern light, maybe ten years old, holding a bucket of water as if she'd been sent to fetch it and had stopped to investigate instead. She looked at him the way children here apparently looked at everything—without fear, with a frank and total appraisal, as though the world were a market stall and everything on it might be haggled over.

"I was," he said.

"Elodie says you knelt." She set the bucket down, never taking her eyes off him. "In the middle of the salt road. In front of everyone. Brammel's cousin saw it before—" She stopped. Recalculated. Children learned quickly here which names were wounds. "Before. She says you knelt and you couldn't get up again."

"That's true."

"Zephyrine says you're a fool." She pronounced it carefully, the way one pronounces a foreign word one has been taught and does not fully believe. "But a useful fool. Those are the best kind, Zephyrine says, because they can't help themselves."

Aldous found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. "Your Zephyrine is generous."

"They're old," the girl said, with the brutal economy of childhood. "Older than my gran. But they're teaching us the sword-words, the ones that don't cost anything. The little ones." She flexed her small hand as if gripping a hilt. "They say when we're grown there'll be swordspeakers again who chose it. Nobody will make anybody carry anything."

"No," Aldous said quietly. "I don't think anyone will."

She considered him a moment longer, then picked up her bucket. "Supper's at the long hearth. If you're going to burn that thing, you should do it after. Burning makes people hungry. Gran says so, and she burned her marriage lines when the Court's writ came, so she'd know."

She walked off toward the lights of the settlement, and Aldous stood alone with forty years of dead men's handwriting, and laughed—a short, cracked sound, startled out of him—and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist like a boy.

* * *

The long hearth was everything its name promised: a fire trench thirty feet long, sunk into the earth of a great turf-roofed hall, built by expatriates who remembered the hearth-halls of Vaelthorne and had rebuilt them from memory and stubbornness. Around it sat perhaps eighty people—exiles and their children, Thornfell farmers who had joined them, a handful of heretic clerks who had fled the Court's archives with songs sewn into their coat linings. At the far end, close to the fire but not touching it, sat Elodie Vasse, and even across the length of the hall Aldous could see the faint glow beneath her skin, banked low these days, tended like a hearth-coal rather than worn like a crown.

And beside her, wrapped in furs despite the hall's warmth, sat Zephyrine Nystrom.

He had not seen them since the battle. From across the hall the change was a blow he had braced for and still failed to absorb. The smuggler who had cut through his knights on the salt road—who had sung twelve lines of the swordsong and held the arch alone against four hundred—had been perhaps forty years old, lean and hard-used, with a face like a cliff face. The person beside Elodie was ancient. White-haired, hollow-cheeked, hands like folded paper resting on the arms of the chair. Only the eyes were the same: grey, level, and utterly unsentimental, and they found Aldous in the doorway the moment he entered, as they had found him on the salt road through smoke and screaming.

"Well," Zephyrine said, to the hall at large. "The useful fool finally arrived. Somebody give him a bowl before he starts apologizing. He's terrible at apologizing. I've watched him try."

There was laughter—not warm, not yet, but real—and space was made on the bench nearest the fire, and Aldous sat among people whose country his had erased, and ate stew, and no one spoke to him cruelly, and no one spoke to him kindly either, and both mercies were beyond anything he had earned.

It was Elodie who came to him afterward, while the hall emptied by degrees into the night. She was twenty now, or nearly—he had stopped counting the years since the salt road—and she moved like someone carrying something precious and heavy, which she was, though the coal had settled deeper into her over time, less a burden than an organ now, part of how she breathed.

"You brought it," she said. Not a question. She had known; of course she had known. The fire showed her things, and besides, some news traveled ahead of itself.

"It's outside. By the arch."

"Forty years of entries."

"And eleven thousand pages. I counted the quires when I carried it. I don't know why I counted. It seemed important to know exactly how heavy it was."

"What will you do?"

"Burn it," Aldous said. "Page by page. All of it except—" His voice caught on the shape of the thing he had decided somewhere on the road, in some grey hour before dawn, and had been turning over ever since like a stone in the pocket. "All of it except the list of the dead. The names. They deserve to be read. Everything else—the writs, the assessments, the seizure orders, the Registrar's final entry, all of it—that was just arithmetic. Arithmetic doesn't deserve to survive. But the names were people, and people deserve to be said out loud."

Elodie was quiet for a while. The coal-light under her skin brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, like slow breathing.

"When my grandmother gave me the fire," she said at last, "she didn't ask me. That was wrong, and I've spent years being angry about it, and I think I'll spend more. But she gave me a choice about *this*—about what to do with it. She left me the carrying and let me choose the keeping." She looked up at him. "You're doing the same thing, do you see? You can't undo what the Court did. None of us can. But you can choose what survives it. Read the names, Aldous. All of them. However long it takes."

"There are nine thousand, four hundred and six," he said. "That I've found so far. There may be more in the later quires."

"Then you'd better start tonight," said Zephyrine's voice, dry as old paper, from the shadows by the hearth. The old swordspeaker had risen without either of them noticing—still silent, still a smuggler at root, a person built for not being heard. "Because I'm old, Merric Vale, and vain, and I intend to live long enough to hear every name, and you'll not waste my remaining years dawdling."

* * *

They burned it over seven nights.

The first night, half the settlement came. Aldous stood at the arch with the ledger open on a reading stand Brammel had built years ago and never finished sanding, and he broke the Registrar's seal—a closed circle around an open eye—and the wax crumbled like old bread, and a murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Then he began at the first page, which was dated one hundred and one years ago, and read aloud.

*"Entry the First. Country: Vaelthorne. Status: dissolved. Method: administrative. Note: no resistance recorded, as resistance requires a subject, and the subject has been reclassified."*

He fed the page into the fire Elodie kindled in a bronze basin before the arch—her fire, lent freely, offered openly, the first gift the coal had ever given that hadn't been taken from it—and the page curled and blackened and rose as sparks, and the crowd was absolutely silent.

Then the second page. And the third. And the fourth.

Some nights only thirty came. Some nights two hundred, when word went out along the passes, and exiles walked days through the snow to stand at the arch and hear. Aldous read until his voice failed, rested it with honey and silence, and read again. He read the seizure orders for the vineyards of the Callowmere valley. He read the requisition of the dragon-fire, itemized like grain. He read the Registrar's private marginalia, discovered late in the fourth quire—small, neat notes in which the architect of an erasure confessed, in the language of inventory, that he sometimes dreamed of doors—and he read those aloud too, because accountability did not stop at the crimes one was proud of.

And each night, woven through the arithmetic, came the names.

*"Struck from the rolls: Maren Ostreva, hearth-singer, of the city of Vaelthorne. Cause of erasure: refused to surrender her songbook. Disposition: executed, site unrecorded."*

An old woman in the crowd sang one line of a song that night, one line only, her voice breaking on the second, and the crowd carried the rest of the line for her, and Aldous waited until the silence returned before he turned the page.

*"Struck from the rolls: Tomas Ilvane, cartographer's apprentice. Cause of erasure: assisted in the mapping of forbidden territory. Disposition: fled east. Disposition amended, forty years later: deceased, Thornfell passes, holding a pass against the Court's knights so that others might flee."*

At that entry, Elodie stepped forward and laid her hand flat on the reading stand, over the page, and the coal-light shone through her fingers, and she said, "He gave me his map. Half-finished. I'm going to finish it." And Aldous read on.

*"Struck from the rolls: the House of Vasse. All members. Cause of erasure: inheritance of contraband property (dragon-fire, one measure, living). Disposition: scattered. Disposition amended: one member located. Current status: free."*

On the sixth night, near midnight, with only a dozen sleepless souls gathered at the arch and the snow falling soft and thick, Aldous reached the entry he had been dreading since the salt road, and found that his hands shook as he untied the quire.

*"Struck from the rolls: Ser Aldous Merric Vale, Knight-Errant, Third Writ. Cause of erasure: knelt. Disposition: at large."*

He read it aloud. His voice held. And then he said, to the small crowd, to the snow, to the arch, to the dead: "The cause of erasure is accurate. I knelt. I have been kneeling, in one way or another, ever since. I recommend it." And he fed the page to the fire, and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched for forty years—not forgiveness, which he had not asked for and did not expect, but something simpler and harder-won: the end of carrying it alone.

* * *

On the seventh night, the ledger was one page thick.

Aldous had saved it deliberately, cutting the surviving leaves apart with a knife over the previous evenings, separating the names from everything else the way one separates grain from chaff. Now he stood before the assembled settlement—every soul who could stand was there, three hundred or more, the long hearth banked and the bronze basin blazing—and he held the final sheaf of pages in both hands.

"These are the names of the dead of Vaelthorne," he said. "Nine thousand, four hundred and six that the Court recorded. There are surely more the Court never bothered to count, and those I name now too, all together, in one breath, because the Court's greatest crime was not the killing—it was the counting, the making of people into lines. So: the uncounted dead of Vaelthorne, all of you, named in one breath, and may your country's god forgive me for the brevity, because I know none of your names and I am sorry, I am sorry, I am sorry."

He drew breath. The crowd drew breath with him.

And then he read the roll. All of it. It took four hours. His voice cracked on the thousandth name and Elodie came and stood beside him and caught the names when he dropped them, reading alternate entries in a clear young voice while the coal-light pulsed gently under her skin like a second heartbeat. Zephyrine sat wrapped in furs at the front, eyes closed, lips moving, keeping count. When they reached the name *Brammel of the Passes*, the crowd said it with them, hundreds of voices, and the snow hissed softly on the basin's rim and did not put the fire out.

Near dawn, Aldous read the last name—*Ysolde Vasse, keeper of the fire, disposition: gave it away*—and closed the sheaf.

He did not burn it.

"I said I would keep one line," he told the crowd, "and I have kept more than a line—I've kept the whole roll, and I make no apology, because a line is not enough for nine thousand people. But I will keep it the way it should have been kept from the beginning. Not in an archive. Not under lock. Not as property of any court." He held the pages up. "These names belong to whoever will carry them. I am old, and I will die someday, and a roll of the dead should live in many hands or it lives in none. So I am asking—"

"We'll copy it," said the girl with the bucket, from the front row, with the terrifying practicality of the young. "Everyone learns a page. Like the hearth-songs. Nobody can burn a song that everybody knows."

Aldous looked at her, and at the faces behind her—farmers and exiles and heretic clerks and one scarred old knight who had nowhere else in the world to go—and he bowed his head, and could not speak for a while, and no one rushed him.

Later, when the crowd had gone in to break their fast, Zephyrine remained at the arch, leaning on a stick, watching the coals settle in the basin. Aldous stood beside them. For a long time neither spoke. The snow had stopped. The mountains east were going pink with sunrise.

"You'll stay, then," Zephyrine said at last. It wasn't quite a question either.

"If they'll have me. I can't give back what I took. But I can teach what I know—the Court's roads, its methods, its weaknesses—so no one is ever erased again. And I can carry the roll. Someone has to walk it to the other settlements. Every hearth should hear the names."

"Mm." Zephyrine poked the coals with the stick, unnecessarily. "You know what I kept, all these years? What I never told Elodie?" The old swordspeaker's voice was very quiet. "The prophecy book. From the shrine. The one that started all of this. I couldn't burn it—I tried, the first winter, and the fire wouldn't take it. So I kept it. And its last page is blank. Has been blank since the night Elodie drove the fire into it. Blank, and waiting." Grey eyes lifted to Aldous, sharp as they had ever been. "A blank page, Merric Vale, in a book that was never wrong about a date in a hundred years. Something means to be written there. And I am too old to go looking for what."

Aldous felt the cold of the morning move through him like a draft under a door.

"So," Zephyrine said, and there was, unmistakably, the ghost of the old smuggler's grin in the ruined face. "How do you feel about one last ride?"
