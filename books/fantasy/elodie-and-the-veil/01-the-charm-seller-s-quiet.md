## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

The night market in Vell opened at the hour when the Ministry's patrols changed shift, and Cyrus Iversen had learned fifteen years ago that forty minutes of unsupervised lamplight was enough to make a living.

She set up her tray on the third step of a shuttered dye-house, beneath a lantern she'd dimmed with a thumb-smudge of soot so it read as poor rather than interesting. Poor was safe. The Ministry of Silence had no interest in the poor. Her wares sat in rows of little wax-sealed vials, each one cloudy as river water, each one holding exactly one small memory: the taste of a peach eaten in summer, the sound of rain on a specific roof, the particular warmth of a dog asleep against your ribs. Small things. Forgettable things. Things nobody would miss and nobody would burn for.

Her rules were stitched into her thinking like a hem. *Small memories only. No names. No magic.* She didn't sell love — too big, it curdled in the buyer. She didn't sell grief — that was theft from the already robbed. And she never, ever sold anything that glittered. Glitter meant power, and power meant the Ministry came to your stall with iron gloves and took everything, including you.

Business tonight was thin. A dockhand bought the memory of standing in a doorway while someone laughed in another room — not his own laughter, but he said it helped him sleep. A boy with coal dust in his knuckles traded two coppers for the feeling of being forgiven, which Cyrus had lifted off a dying man who no longer needed it. She sealed each sale with a nod, no names exchanged, per rule two.

Then the widow came.

Cyrus knew her by sight before she reached the steps — Marta Ondrel, though Cyrus would never say the name aloud, because rule two cut both ways. Marta had been coming to the market every ninth night for a year now, since her husband drowned in the harbor works. She always wore grey. She always looked at Cyrus's tray like it was food she couldn't afford.

Tonight she stopped.

"The one," Marta said quietly, "the one you showed me last month. The kiss."

Cyrus's stomach did something complicated. "That's not for sale."

"Everything on the tray is for sale."

"That one isn't." It had come to her years back, plucked from a girl of maybe nineteen on the ferry to Vell — a first kiss, salt-lipped and terrified and joyous all at once, given behind a fish-drying shed by a boy whose name Cyrus had dutifully stripped out per rule two. She'd kept it because it was beautiful, and keeping it was the closest thing to sin her trade allowed. Merchants kept nothing. That was how you stayed invisible.

Marta set three silver bits on the step. That was more than Cyrus made most nights.

"I'm not buying it for me," the widow said. "I'm sixty-one years old, charm-seller. I kissed my husband for fifty years. I know what kissing is." Her voice didn't waver; that was somehow worse. "I'm buying it so it exists somewhere again. So that when I die, there is still one first kiss in the world, even if it isn't mine."

The market noise went on around them — hawkers, dice, a fiddler badly losing a fight with his instrument. Cyrus looked at the vial on the tray's second row, its wax seal gone soft with handling, and hated herself with great precision.

"Rule one," she said. "Small memories only."

"It's small. It lasted four seconds. He counted them."

Cyrus closed her eyes briefly. Rule three, then, the one she never spoke aloud because speaking it made it real: *no magic.* Selling a memory wasn't magic — it was commerce, extraction, a thief's sleight of hand done with a touch and a breath. But handing a stolen thing to a grieving woman under an answered-sky felt like opening a door you couldn't close.

"Four seconds," Cyrus said finally, and took the silver, and gave her the vial. "You drink it slow. It'll feel like yours for a minute. Then it goes back to being nobody's, which is what it was before. Don't thank me. Thanking makes it a favor, favors make it a relationship, and relationships get people burned."

"You're a very warm woman," Marta said dryly, and unstoppered the vial right there on the step.

Cyrus watched the widow's face as the memory landed — watched sixty-one years rearrange themselves around four borrowed seconds, watched her mouth go soft and young and salt-lipped. It was obscene, what Cyrus could do. It was also, she thought not for the first time, the only honest work left in Vell: everyone else sold lies; she sold true things that belonged to no one.

Marta exhaled. "There," she whispered. "It exists again."

And then — because grief is a prayer whether you mean it or not — the widow bowed her head over the empty vial and murmured something into it. Old words. outlawed words. A language the Ministry had spent a generation scraping off tombstones. Cyrus caught only the shape of it, the cadence of petition, addressed to a god with no name left to answer.

*No magic,* Cyrus thought. *Please. Rule three.*

The candle in her lantern flared.

Not guttered — *flared*, straight up, white-hot, throwing their shadows huge against the dye-house wall, and every other flame in the market did it at once. Forty lanterns, a hundred candles, the cook-fires along the canal — all of them leaping like startled birds, then settling. Then, one by one, they turned. Not extinguished. Turned — each flame bending gently toward the widow on the step, the way flowers lean at sun, and holding there, attentive, in absolute silence.

The fiddler stopped mid-note. Somewhere a dice cup hit the ground and no one picked it up.

Marta lifted her head. On her face was an expression Cyrus had only seen in memories — the ones she refused to steal, from people who had been loved completely and knew it. The widow began, very softly, to laugh and weep at the same time, and every candle in Vell's night market leaned closer to hear it.

"He heard," she breathed. "After fifteen years. Somebody heard me."

Fifteen years. The number dropped through Cyrus like a stone through dark water. Fifteen years since the old gods had gone quiet, since the Ministry's founding edict — *the gods are dead; those who claim otherwise are fuel* — since the burning pits behind the Hall of Records had gone cold for lack of customers. Fifteen years of silence, and now a widow's half-remembered prayer in a dead language had gotten an *answer*.

Cyrus's hands were steady. Cyrus's hands were always steady; that was the whole architecture of her survival. But inside, where she kept the locked room she never visited — the room with her own childhood in it, the one hole in her hoard she'd never been able to explain — something knocked. Once. Politely. Like a visitor who knows they're expected.

She snuffed the lantern with her bare palm and started packing the tray.

"Market's over," she told the crowd. "Go home. Nothing happened here. Flames do that sometimes."

"They don't," said the dockhand.

"They do," said Cyrus, "if you value your eyebrows," and the crowd, being Vellish, took the point and dispersed into the dark with the practiced speed of people who had learned that witnessing things was expensive.

Only Marta remained, cradling the empty vial like a relic.

"Charm-seller." The widow's eyes were wet and lit from within, which was a phrase Cyrus had previously considered literary nonsense. "I don't know why it chose to happen at your stall. I don't know what you are. But I know the old rites, a little, from my grandmother." She hesitated. "When I prayed just now — I didn't pray alone. There was something praying *with* me. Behind me. Like a second voice using my mouth's echo." She touched Cyrus's wrist, feather-light. "It sounded like it was coming from you."

Cyrus removed the hand, gently, the way you'd remove a lit fuse.

"Buy a peach-charm next week," she said. "Forget this. Rule two applies to gods too — no names."

But walking home through Vell's crooked streets, hugging the walls where the shadows pooled thickest, Cyrus could not shake the sensation of being *noticed*. Not hunted — hunted she knew, had known since childhood, the flat cold eye of the Ministry sweeping past. This was worse. This was attention without malice, vast and patient, like weather deciding where to land. The world she had spent fifteen years making invisible — herself, her trade, the locked room at the center of her — had looked up from whatever long sleep it had been sleeping and located her stall on the third step of a shuttered dye-house.

In her coat pocket, unbidden, her fingers found the one vial she never sold, never opened, never let herself think about. Cloudy as river water. Sealed with wax older than her trade. Inside, if the label she couldn't read was any indication, was a border village, a mother singing in an outlawed language, and a voice asking a child for refuge.

Something knocked again, deeper this time. Less polite.

Behind her, three streets away, a bell began to ring — not a patrol bell, not a market bell, but the cracked old alarm-bell atop the Hall of Records that hadn't sounded in fifteen years, ringing the one signal every soul in Vell knew and dreaded:

*They're awake.*

Cyrus ran.
