## 2. What the Ministry Burns

Three days after the candlelight, I told myself it was nothing.

I was good at that. Telling myself things were nothing. It was practically a trade skill. A widow's prayer answered by a guttering wick—coincidence, draft, bad tallow. Vell was full of drafts and bad tallow and old women talking to air. Fifteen years of careful, invisible work did not unravel because one candle behaved strangely.

So I went back to the night market.

The market lived under the aqueduct arches on Salt Row, where the Ministry's lanterns didn't bother reaching and the fog came in off the canal thick enough to chew. By midnight there were forty stalls or more: bone-readers, love-charm grifters, a woman who sold bottled sneezes to people who'd forgotten how to cry. I had my usual corner by the third arch, my tray of little glass vials laid out on black velvet like a jeweler's wares. Inside each one, a memory curled up small and sleeping—a first snowfall, a mother's laugh, the smell of bread at dawn. Harmless things. Small things. Nothing with a name attached, nothing with magic in it, nothing anyone could trace back to the skull it came from.

My rules. My survival.

"First kiss," I said to the evening crowd, holding up a vial so the candle behind it made the memory inside glow faint amber. "Warm, brief, entirely uncomplicated. The girl had flour on her hands."

A few smiles. A baker's apprentice blushed. Business as usual.

That was when the bells started.

Not the market bells—the Ministry ones, the flat grey bells from the Spire that nobody ever heard because the Ministry never announced anything. They rang three times and stopped, which somehow was worse than ringing all night. The whole market went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone says your name in it.

Then the lanterns came down Salt Row.

White ones. Cold, steady, unwavering—the kind of light that doesn't flicker because it isn't fire, it's authority. Twelve figures in dove-grey coats walked beneath them, and behind the twelve came carts, and on the carts were iron cages, and I didn't need to see what was in the cages because I could *feel* it, the way you feel a storm in your teeth. That humming. That pressure behind the eyes. The particular wrongness of a vessel being carried somewhere it didn't want to go.

"Ministry business," said the lead agent, in a voice like a receipt being issued. "By order of the Silence, this market is closed. All sellers of charms, relics, and devotional objects will present themselves for inspection."

Nobody moved. Forty stalls' worth of criminals collectively decided to be furniture.

"You have until the lanterns reach the third arch," he said.

I started packing. Fast, calm, hands doing what hands do—I'd been raided before, twice, both times a shakedown, both times survivable with the right vial slipped into the right pocket. But these agents weren't looking at trays. They were looking at *people*. Their eyes moved face to face, slow and deliberate, like they were reading us. And when their gaze crossed mine—

*Nothing,* I thought, as hard as I could. *I'm no one. Charm seller. Nobody special.*

One of them paused across my stall. A woman, young, with the pale lashless look of someone whose eyelashes had been ritually removed—Silence initiates did that, plucked themselves smooth so they couldn't be recognized by weeping, or so the story went. She looked at me for three full seconds. Then she looked at the vials.

"Sentiment," she said, almost gently. "Bottled sentiment." And she moved on.

I let out a breath I'd been holding since the bells.

Which is when the screaming started at the far end of the row.

---

They'd found Old Maret first. Or maybe she was just first alphabetically; either way, by the time I shoved through the crowd to the mouth of the square, they had her chained to a stake in the fountain basin, and the water around her feet was already steaming.

Maret sold luck-threads. Had for twenty years, longer than me. She was seventy if she was a day, half-blind, with hands like knotted rope, and she used to slip me candied ginger at midwinter because I reminded her of a granddaughter she'd sold the memory of once and regretted forever. She wasn't a threat. She wasn't anything. She tied red thread around wrists and took two coppers, and now here she was in the fountain with her skirts burning and her voice going up into a register I'd never heard a human voice reach.

"She's a vessel," the lead agent announced, to the crowd, to the record, to whatever ledger they kept in the Spire. "Vessel of the Drowned Sister. Confirmed by rite. Sentence: purification."

*Purification.* They'd brought braziers instead of stakes-and-rope. The new way. Fire, but ceremonial fire—white flame that burned without smoke, the kind that left nothing behind to bury. Someone in the Ministry hierarchy had decided that vessels shouldn't even get ashes.

And the crowd—God forgive us, the crowd just *watched*. Because that's what crowds in Vell do. We watched executions the way we watched barges come in, with that dull appetite people mistake for safety. As long as it's her. As long as it's never me.

Except it might be me. Except I was standing there with a tray full of other people's souls in my satchel and something in my own chest I'd never once let myself examine, and the white light of those lanterns kept sweeping the crowd, and every time it passed over me my skin prickled like the light was *reading* me.

Maret saw me.

I'll carry that to whatever grave I get. In between the screaming, in some gap where the pain hadn't reached yet, she looked straight across the square at me—and she didn't beg. She didn't mouth *help me*. She looked *relieved*. Like she'd seen a familiar face at her own funeral, and that was enough, and then her eyes went wide and fixed on my chest, on the space over my heart, and her mouth shaped one word that the fire swallowed before I could read it.

The white flame climbed her like ivy.

I turned and walked. Not ran—walked, fast, the way invisible people walk, and I got exactly four steps before something small slammed into my side and clung there.

"Wren," I hissed. "Let go."

She didn't. Wren was eleven, maybe twelve, all elbows and grease-stained fingers and enormous dark eyes that missed nothing on Salt Row, least of all which pockets held coin. I'd caught her lifting from my tray twice. Both times I'd grabbed her wrist, shown her the vials, said *these aren't money, kid, these are worse than money*, and both times she'd looked at me with that flat, unimpressed stare and said, "Then why d'you keep 'em?"

Now her fingers were knotted in my coat like she'd been sewn there, and her face was pressed against my ribs, and she was shaking.

"They came for the tallow-woman too," she whispered. "And the man who sells saints' teeth. They've got lists, Cy. Somebody gave them lists."

Lists. The word went through me colder than the lantern-light. You can't make a list of vessels unless you know how to find them. You can't find them unless something changed. Something had changed—the candle, the answered prayer, the gods waking up after fifteen years of silence—and the Ministry had noticed before any of us did, and they weren't panicking, they were *organized*.

"I'm not going anywhere with you," I said. "You're a stray. Strays run."

"Strays die alone." Her grip tightened. "You always say nobody watches the charm seller. Well, somebody's watching tonight, and they watched *you*, and that lady with no eyelashes looked at you like she was checking you off something."

She'd seen that too. Of course she had. Wren missed nothing; it was her entire economy.

The second victim went up while we stood there—the saint's-teeth man, whose real name none of us had ever known, screaming in a voice that cracked halfway through and came out doubled, layered, like two throats sharing one mouth. The god inside him dying loudly. The crowd flinched as one animal. Somewhere near me a woman began quietly, hysterically praying, and every head within ten feet snapped toward her like iron filings, and I thought: *that's next week's list being written right now.*

"Come on," I said, and hated how gentle it came out. "Don't watch. Never watch. Watching is how they find you."

We moved along the arch shadows, Wren still welded to my side, past stalls abandoned mid-sale, past a bone-reader sitting very still with her cards facedown and her lips moving in what I desperately hoped was just nervous habit. At the mouth of Salt Row I looked back once. The square was white-lit and smoking, and the lead agent was standing over the braziers reading aloud from a scroll, name after name after name, and the names were all market names, all *our* names, and I understood with a lurching, nauseous clarity:

This wasn't a raid. This was a census. And we were the livestock being counted before the culling.

"Cy," Wren said. "Where are we going?"

Good question. Home was out—my loft was known, my landlord talked for coin, my neighbors talked for free. Every bolt-hole I'd built in fifteen years assumed the Ministry wanted my *goods*. None of them accounted for the Ministry wanting my *blood*.

But there was one place. One person. A shrine nobody prayed at anymore, down in the flooded district, tended by a priest who'd been thrown out of his own church for believing in a country that didn't exist. Anselm. Mad old Anselm with his candles for Elodie, the erased land, the fairy-tale kingdom. I'd sold him a memory once—a sunset over mountains that weren't on any map—and he'd paid triple and wept, and I'd never asked why.

Because asking why is how you stop being invisible.

"Cy?" Wren again, insistent, her hand finding mine without asking permission, small and cold and gripping hard. "You're doing the thing where you go somewhere inside your head and lock the door. Take me with you or tell me the plan. Pick one."

"The plan," I heard myself say, "is a mad priest who worships nowhere."

"Oh good," said Wren. "I love nowhere. Nowhere's the one place they haven't made a list of."

Behind us, the white bells rang twice more. Ahead, the fog off the canal opened like a curtain, and somewhere inside it, an old man was lighting candles for a dead country—and I was starting to suspect that the dead country knew my name.

Wren's fingers tightened around mine, and we ran.
