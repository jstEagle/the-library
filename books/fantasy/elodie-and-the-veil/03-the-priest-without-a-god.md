## 3. The Priest Without a God

Wren didn't let go of my coat for six streets.

I counted. It was the kind of thing I did when fear needed somewhere to go — count doorways, count breaths, count the small betrayals of a body trying not to run. Six streets of the girl's fist knotted in wool while behind us the smoke from Candlemaker's Square still climbed into the dark like a question nobody wanted answered.

They'd burned three vessels tonight. I'd watched one of them — Marla, who sold stolen lullabies two stalls down from mine — go up shrieking in a language that wasn't hers. The Ministry agents hadn't shouted. That was the worst part. They worked like clerks, quiet and thorough, checking names off a list I hadn't known existed until it was too long to read.

"Where are we going?" Wren asked. Her voice had that flat steadiness kids develop when crying isn't safe yet.

"Away."

"That's not a place."

"No," I said. "It's a direction."

She considered this with the seriousness of someone deciding whether to file a complaint. Then she pointed left, down an alley I'd walked past ten thousand times and never once entered, because there was nothing in it worth stealing.

"There's a light down there," she said. "Old kind."

"What does old kind mean?"

"The kind you hear before you see." She tugged my coat. "Come on. You're slow when you're scared."

I wasn't scared. I was recalibrating. Fifteen years of rules — small memories only, no names, no magic, be furniture, be weather, be nothing anyone writes down — and all of it had come apart in one night because some forgotten god had lit a widow's candle and the Ministry had remembered what it was for. Invisibility hadn't failed me. Invisibility had been revoked.

We went down the alley anyway. Because Wren pulled, and because I was tired, and because — though I'd have cut out my own tongue before admitting it — the alternative was going home to a room where they might already know my name.

* * *

The shrine was nothing. That's how it survived, I think. A recessed doorway between a shuttered dyeworks and a wall that smelled of pigeon, holding a stone basin gone green with neglect, three stubs of candle, and a man.

He was kneeling when we found him, sweeping ash off the step with a whisk made of tied twigs, as if cleanliness mattered to a floor no one visited. Grey-haired, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who used to carry things heavier than brooms. He wore no vestments. Just a workman's coat with the sleeves rolled, and around his throat a cord from which hung a small hammered disc — blank, or so it seemed until he turned and the candle caught it and I saw the faint impression pressed into the metal: a border. A line where land met land.

A country's edge, worn onto a pendant by somebody's thumb, over and over, for years.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. Not unkindly. The way you'd say it to weather.

"We're being hunted," Wren said, before I could stop her.

"Wren—"

"It's true. They burned Marla." Her voice cracked on the name and she pushed through the crack like it was a door. "She sold songs. Songs. And they put her in the square and—"

"I know." The man set down his whisk. "I know what they do."

"Then you know why we need—" I started.

"Shelter?" He looked at me properly then, and something in his face shifted, the way water shifts when a stone drops through it. "Girl, do you know what you're carrying?"

The question went through me like cold water through cloth.

"Charms," I said. "Memories. Nothing—"

"No." He rose. Up close he was taller than kneeling had suggested, and older, lines carved deep around a mouth built for saying hard things gently. "Not that. Something else. I felt you coming three streets away. Everything old in Vell felt you coming."

Behind him, half-hidden in the alcove's shadow, I finally saw what the shrine actually held. Not the basin. Behind the basin, where the wall should have been smooth plaster, there was a patch of stone that didn't match — older, darker, carved with letters in a script I had never seen but somehow recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd from a dream. Below it, someone had painted a map in fading pigments. Coastline. Rivers. Mountains. Borders drawn in gold that had once, clearly, been renewed with care.

Borders that matched the pendant at his throat.

"Elodie," I said. The word came out of me without permission, and it came out wrong — not spoken so much as *remembered*, in a voice that wasn't quite mine. "That's Elodie."

The priest went very still.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Except Elodie doesn't exist. It hasn't existed for twenty-two years. There is no such country. Ask any clerk, any cartographer, any schoolchild. The maps were amended. The records were sealed. The language was outlawed and the shrines were cracked and the name itself was struck from every registry in Vell." He touched the pendant. "And yet here you are, saying it like a woman greeting home."

"My mother—" I stopped.

Because I had said *my mother*, hadn't I? Inside the word. Underneath it. Somewhere in the marrow of that single utterance there had been a kitchen and a voice singing in an outlawed tongue and hands kneading bread, and none of it belonged to me, and all of it had risen up like a fish surfacing, opened its mouth, and sunk again before I could net it.

"Cyrus," Wren said. She'd learned my name days ago, from watching me haggle; I'd never told her. "You've gone white."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying. You lie beautifully, actually. It's very impressive." She took my sleeve. "Sit down anyway."

There was a bench. Of course there was a bench; shrines exist for the sitting-down of the desperate. I sat. The priest crouched in front of the basin and, without asking anyone's leave, lit the three candle stubs with a taper from his pocket. Small flames. Old kind. I heard them before I saw them, exactly as Wren had said — a sound like breath held just under hearing.

"I'm Anselm," he said. "I was a priest of Elodie, back when Elodie was allowed to be anywhere. Now I sweep a floor nobody prays at." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, worn smooth as the pendant. "Except somebody prays at it now. Somebody has been praying at it for years, without knowing. Every night. Right here in Vell, in a dozen voices, in a thousand little stolen pieces." He looked directly at me. "Prayers travel, girl. Even the ones sold as charms. Especially those."

My hands were shaking. I folded them so Wren wouldn't see.

"The gods went quiet twenty-two years ago," Anselm continued, "when the country that fed them was unwritten. A god with no ground under it can't answer. It can only wait inside whatever vessel will hold it, and hope. Tonight the waiting ended. The veil is thinning. The Ministry knows it — that's why the burning. Every vessel they destroy is a door they nail shut." He leaned closer. "But doors don't burn, child. Doors only open."

"Stop calling me that."

"Vessel," he corrected, gently, which was worse. "Door. Hearth-stone. There are old words for what you are and I know all of them."

"I'm a charm seller. I steal memories and sell them to grieving people so they can feel something harmless for an hour. That's all I am. That's the whole inventory."

"Is it." He didn't make it a question. He made it a key. "Tell me about your childhood."

The candles flickered. Or everything else did.

"I don't—" My mouth opened. Closed. I reached for it the way I reach for any memory, mine or stolen, expecting the shelf, the jar, the familiar weight. And found — nothing. Not absence, exactly. Absence I knew; absence was smooth, was a wall. This was a *sealed door*. Painted over. Papered over. Fifteen years of small stolen sorrows stacked against it like furniture moved deliberately to block a room.

There was a room in me I had never entered. There was a room in me I had never once wondered about, and the not-wondering itself, now that I finally looked at it, had fingerprints on it. Someone had taught me not to wonder. Someone had locked that door so carefully that even the lock was hidden.

"I don't remember being a child," I said aloud, and the sentence fell into the alcove like a coin down a well.

Anselm closed his eyes. When he opened them they were wet.

"Twenty-two years ago," he said, "when they erased the country, every vessel of Elodie was given a choice. Burn, or forget. Forget the borders, forget the language, forget the god, forget their own first six years — because the god had been poured into children, into infants, into the smallest vessels, because only the small could hold it whole." His hand found the pendant. "One family fled north with an infant. The records say the child died crossing. The records also say Elodie doesn't exist, so perhaps we shouldn't trust the records."

Wren's grip tightened on my sleeve. She was looking at me with an expression I couldn't bear, so I didn't look back.

"You're saying I'm—"

"I'm saying the prayers stopped being scattered the moment you walked past this shrine. I'm saying your stolen charms aren't hiding from the Ministry, girl — they're hiding *you* from yourself. Every memory you never stole is a board nailed over a window. And tonight, with the veil thinning and the old god waking—" He spread his hands. "Tonight the boards are rattling."

The candle flames stood perfectly straight. Perfectly still. As if listening.

Somewhere far off, a bell tolled the Ministry's third hour, and beneath its bronze voice — faint, unmistakable, in a language outlawed before I was born — I heard singing. My mother's voice. From inside my own chest.

* * *

Later — I couldn't have said how much later — Anselm wrapped me in a blanket that smelled of ash and lavender and made Wren eat half a loaf he produced from somewhere, because priests are apparently hoarders of bread if nothing else. Wren ate watching me, chewing with the grim diligence of a soldier on duty.

"She'll stay till morning," Anselm said quietly, beside me. "Then you both move on. The Ministry will sweep this district by dawn; they always do after a burning. There's a road east. Roads, now, that only appear if you pray over them — the country is remembering its geography, and geography, thank every vanished god, is slower than soldiers."

"I'm not praying anything."

"You've been praying your whole life. You just sold the answers." He almost smiled. "Rest, Cyrus. Tomorrow we talk about opening your hoard — all of it, even the jars you don't remember filling. Especially those." He paused at the alcove's edge, silhouette against three small flames. "The past is a country too. Yours is waiting at the border with a lamp lit."

I lay back on the cold stone and did not sleep. Beside me, Wren eventually did, her hand still hooked in my coat like a boat tied to a dock.

And in the dark behind my eyes, the painted-over door stood waiting, and beneath it, patient as tide, something that loved me hummed a lullaby in a dead tongue — and I understood, with the last clarity of the night, that tomorrow I would have to open my own hoard and find out which of the memories in my jars were stolen.

And which ones had been left for me to find.
