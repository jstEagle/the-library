## 4. Memories I Never Stole

The charms lived in a lockbox under the floor of Anselm's shrine, and I had not opened it in three days.

That was the problem. Three days. In fifteen years of selling other people's pasts back to them by the thimbleful, I had never once gone three days without counting my stock the way a miser counts coins—thumbing each vial, each folded paper charm, each wax-sealed lozenge of somebody's stolen afternoon. It was ritual. It was inventory. It was the only prayer I'd ever trusted.

Now I sat on the cold flagstones with the box open in my lap and Wren asleep an arm's length away, curled against the base of Elodie's cracked altar like a cat who had decided this was her house now, and I made myself look at what was inside.

Forty-one charms. I knew every one.

Or I had known every one. That was the lie I'd been carrying since the night market, since the widow's candle lit itself from nothing, since Anselm had looked at me across his ruined shrine with an expression I recognized too late as recognition and said, *When did you last open your own hoard, thief?*

I hadn't. Not really. I opened it to count. Counting wasn't looking. Counting was touching the outside of the locked room and walking away.

I picked up the first vial. A memory of rain on a tin roof, harvested off a dockworker in the Salt Ward six years ago. I remembered taking it. I remembered the taste of his boredom, the gray weight of an afternoon he'd have sold for a crust of bread. Fine. Good. Ordinary.

Second vial. A child's birthday, candles and cheap honey-cake, lifted from a laundress who couldn't bear to keep it after her son died. I remembered that one too—the guilt of it, the way she'd wept while I worked, and how I'd told myself she was better rid of it. Fine.

Third.

Third was a village.

I sat very still. The vial was small, clay-stoppered, wrapped in the kind of wax paper I used for the delicate ones, and inside it glowed a soft amber that no memory of mine had ever glowed. My memories were workmanlike things. Gray, brown, the muted palette of Vell. This one burned like a lamp behind horn.

I knew what a border village looked like because I'd seen the old maps Anselm kept—maps of a country that officially had never existed, inked over so many times the parchment had gone soft as cloth. Stone walls. Terraced fields climbing a hillside. A well with a roof over it, which nobody in Vell would build because roofs over wells meant you expected to stay.

I had never been to a border village. I had never left Vell. I had never taken a memory I could not name the owner of, because rule one, the rule above all rules, was *small memories only, and know whose they are*.

My hands were shaking. I set the vial down and picked up the next one, and the next, and the room tilted.

A woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists. A door painted blue, repainted blue every spring, though there was no one to see it but her. A dog, yellow, missing an ear. And underneath all of it, threaded through it like a wire through wax—

Singing.

Not in Vellish. Not in any tongue the Ministry permitted, which was all of them except the ones they'd buried. This was a language I had never heard and knew instantly, the way you know your own heartbeat is yours: rounded vowels, a melody shaped like a lullaby but older, the kind of song you sing to a child so the child will carry it somewhere you'll never go.

The mother was singing it while she worked. The memory-holder—I could feel the edges of them, the way you can feel the shape of a hand through a glove—was small. Sitting on a step. Watching the flour fly. Safe. Loved. Memorizing the song without knowing that was what they were doing.

I put the vial down because my fingers wouldn't hold it anymore.

"Cyrus?" Wren's voice, sleep-rough.

"Go back to sleep."

"You're crying."

"I'm working."

She considered this with the flat skepticism of a nine-year-old who had survived the Stews, then rolled over and pretended to sleep, one eye open, watching me the way she watched everything. I let her pretend. Some things you don't do in front of children, and falling apart is one of them.

* * *

Anselm found me at dawn among the scattered charms, sitting in the middle of them like a woman in the wreckage of her own house, which was exactly what I was.

He didn't ask. He knelt, joints cracking, and began sorting them into rows with those careful priest's hands—nine fingers now, since the Ministry's parting gift years ago—and when he reached the amber-glowing ones he stopped sorting and just looked at me.

"How many," he said.

"Nine." My voice came out sanded flat. "Nine I can't account for. Nine I don't remember taking." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You understand what that means? Fifteen years. Fifteen years I've kept the ledger perfect. Every charm sourced, every seller paid or robbed, every memory catalogued. That ledger is the only thing I've ever been good at. And there are nine entries in my own box that aren't mine to have and weren't mine to take, because I never took them at all."

"Then where did they come from?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I picked up the singing one. Held it between us like a coal. "Because here's the worse thing, priest. They're not someone else's either. I've held ten thousand memories. I know the seam where a borrowed life joins yours—it itches, it sits wrong, it's a coat cut for another body. These don't itch. These fit." I turned the vial. The light moved. "These are mine. That's a kitchen I stood in. That's a step I sat on. That woman is singing to *me*, and I don't remember her face, and I don't remember her name, and I don't remember Vell, I don't remember ever arriving in Vell, I remember the Stews from age eight onward and before that there is—" I made a gesture, opening a door onto nothing. "There's a wall. Smooth as poured glass. I always thought that was normal. Everyone's childhood goes dark at the edges, yes?"

"No," Anselm said gently. "It doesn't. Not like that."

"Well." I looked at the wall of my own skull. "Someone bricked it up very well."

He took the vial from me. He didn't open it—he knew better than to handle another thief's stock uninvited—but he held it to the light from the broken clerestory, and the amber glow caught the lines of his face, and I saw him decide something.

"There's a rite," he said. "Old. From before the Silence. It lets a vessel look inward without drowning. We called it *opening the hearth*. Most priests needed a lifetime to learn it." He flexed his nine fingers. "I have time, and you have need, and frankly, Cyrus, you have run out of ways not to know."

"And if I look?"

"Then you'll know why the gods are waking. Why the Ministry burns vessels in squares. Why a candle answered a widow's prayer when it should have guttered." He set the vial down between us, deliberate as a man laying a card. "Why you."

Wren was awake. Of course she was. She was sitting up against the altar with her knees pulled in, watching us with that stillness she got sometimes, the stillness that wasn't childish at all, and when our eyes met she didn't look away.

"The lady in the song," she said. "Is that your ma?"

"I don't know."

"It sounded like your ma." She shrugged, defensive, as if I'd accused her. "Songs sound like things. That one sounded like you."

* * *

We did it at noon, in the shrine, with salt and a bowl of water and Anselm's voice working the old cadences—words in the outlawed tongue, and I understood them, that was the first vertigo, I understood every word and had no right to. Wren held my hands. She insisted. She planted herself in front of me cross-legged and gripped my fingers with both of hers, small and grimy and immovable, and said, "If you fall in, I'll pull you out," and there was no arguing with her, there never was.

The hearth opened.

Not like remembering. Remembering is a door you walk through. This was the door walking through *me*. The wall in my mind—the smooth glass wall I'd lived beside for fifteen years without once pressing my palm to it—cracked down the middle, and everything I had stored behind it came through at once, and I fell.

A kitchen. Flour to the wrists. Blue door.

A hillside terraced like staircases for giants, and a country breathing around it, and I mean *breathing*—the land itself alive, hedged and blessed and sung to, every stone named, every field holding its own small god the way a cup holds water.

A crowd. Smoke. Running. My mother's hand crushing mine hard enough to hurt, and her voice saying the song faster now, urgent, folding the whole language into me fold over fold like contraband sewn into a coat lining, *carry this, carry this, whatever happens carry this*—

And then the dark, and the cold, and a voice.

Not my mother's. Not human-sized. It came from everywhere the way cold comes from everywhere, patient and vast and so tired, and it said—or asked, it was asking, gods asking is the most frightening sound there is—

*Little door. Little last door. Everything else is shut. May I hide in you?*

And a child's voice—my voice, eight years old, already alone, already in Vell, already a thief of small things—answered:

*"Yes."*

I came back to the shrine floor on my hands and knees, water slopped across the stones, Wren's grip white-knuckled on my fingers, Anselm saying something in the old tongue that was probably a blessing and probably a warning and might have been both.

I knew. God help me, I knew, the way you know a bone is broken before you've seen it.

"They didn't erase Elodie," I said. My mouth tasted of smoke that was fifteen years old. "They erased everyone who could open it back. And they missed one. Because a god hid a country's last door inside a child, and the child grew up and built a life on top of the door and never once looked down." I started laughing, and it was awful, and I couldn't stop. "Fifteen years selling memories. Fifteen years keeping my head down, keeping my ledger clean, keeping *invisible*. And the whole time the biggest secret in the world was sleeping in my chest, waiting for someone to pray loud enough to wake it."

Anselm crouched in front of me. His eyes were wet and steady.

"Now you know what you are," he said. "Vessel. Door. Last living threshold of a nation. The Ministry has a word for what they'll do to you, and it isn't a kind one."

"Yes, thank you, I'd guessed."

"But that's not the question." He tapped the floor between us, once, hard. "The question is what *Elodie* is to you. Because a door doesn't just let things through, Cyrus. A door belongs to what's on both sides of it."

Wren hadn't let go of my hand. She was staring at me with an expression I'd never seen on her—not fear, not pity. Recognition. The look of a kid from the Stews finding out someone else had also been carrying something too big to name.

"So," she said. "What's on the other side?"

Outside, far off toward the city, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm. All the bells. Every tower in Vell, ringing at once, and under the ringing, faint but rising, a sound I had heard once already and would hear until I died: prayers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The whole city starting to whisper to gods that had just remembered how to listen.

Anselm stood, listening, and his face went gray.

"They're not all praying to be saved," he said quietly. "Some of them are praying to find the door. And the Ministry is praying loudest of all."

I looked down at my hands. Thief's hands. Keeper's hands. A door's hands.

"We leave tonight," I said.
