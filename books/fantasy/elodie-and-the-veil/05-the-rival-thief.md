## 5. The Rival Thief

*Cyrus*

The safehouse Anselm had given them was a former counting-house with a shrine in the cellar, which told Cyrus everything she needed to know about the priest's finances. Wren slept curled against the cold stove like a cat that had decided the house was hers. Cyrus sat on the stairs with a knife across her knees and did not sleep at all.

She heard the door at midnight. Not forced—picked, and picked well, because her own tripwire of ash and hair across the threshold was still intact when she checked it later. Whoever came in had lifted it, read it, and laid it back down like a courtesy.

She knew only one thief with manners.

"Sable," she said, not turning around. "You have about four seconds to explain why I shouldn't wake the whole street screaming."

"Because you'd be dead before the second scream." Sable stepped into the moonlight from the broken roof-glass, and fifteen years fell off her all at once. Same silver-streaked braid, same scar splitting one eyebrow, same way of standing as if every room were a market and everyone in it a customer. "Hello, Cyrus. You look terrible."

"You sold my name to the Ministry."

"I sold your *alias* to the Ministry." Sable spread her hands. "There's a difference, and the difference is you're still breathing."

Cyrus stood. The knife felt small and honest in her hand. "Six years ago. The Hollow Market raid. Three vessels burned because someone gave the Inquisitors a list, and my name was on it."

"And whose name was *first* on it?" Sable's voice didn't change, but something behind it did—the sound of a door being held shut. "Mine. I gave them yours so they'd stop looking for mine. That's the arithmetic of this city, Cyrus. You know it better than anyone. You've been doing sums like that since you were nine years old."

"Don't." The word came out rawer than she wanted. "Don't you dare inventory me."

Sable looked past her, down the stairwell, toward the cellar where the shrine to Elodie glowed faintly even now—Anselm kept a candle lit, always—and Cyrus watched her rival's face do something complicated.

"They say prayers are being answered again," Sable said quietly. "They say there's a vessel walking Vell with a country folded up inside her like a letter no one would deliver. They say the Ministry has a new Inquisitor, one who was a vessel once himself, and he doesn't burn them anymore." She met Cyrus's eyes. "He hollows them first. Keeps them alive. Uses them as hounds."

The knife suddenly weighed nothing at all, which was worse than any weight.

"Why are you here?"

"Because I'm offering you a deal." Sable smiled, and it was the old smile, the market smile, the one that had charmed a hundred memories out of a hundred strangers. "I can get you out of Vell. Past the checkpoints, past the hunters. I know roads that don't exist on any map the Ministry holds. I'll take you and the girl and the priest wherever you need to go."

"And the price."

"The god." Sable said it simply, the way you'd name a figure on an invoice. "When we reach whatever door it's hiding behind, I take it out of you. There are ways. Old ones. I didn't spend six years learning how the Ministry unmakes vessels for nothing. You walk away human. Empty. Alive." She tilted her head. "It's more than the Ministry's offering. It's more than the god is offering, if you ask me. What does a dead country care if you live or die? But I'm asking you to care. So: the god, in exchange for your life. Yes or no."

From the shadows at the bottom of the stairs, a small voice said, "No."

Wren stood there in her stolen coat three sizes too big, hair wild, eyes furious, and Cyrus's heart did something inconvenient.

"She hasn't answered yet," Sable said.

"She was going to say yes." Wren climbed two steps, planting herself between them like a fence post. "She always says yes to things that cost her everything. It's her whole personality."

"*Family meeting*," Cyrus said, before either of them could speak again, and the word *family* left her mouth and hung in the air like smoke, and she wished immediately she could steal it back.

* * *

*Sable*

The girl was a complication. Sable had planned for the priest—fanatics were predictable, they died on schedule—and for Cyrus, who was grief wearing a merchant's coat and could be steered by it. She had not planned for a pickpocket child who looked at Cyrus like she was a load-bearing wall.

Now the three of them sat in the cellar by the shrine while the priest, Anselm, boiled water over a spirit flame and pretended not to listen, which fooled no one.

"Say it plainly," Cyrus said. "All of it. No market talk."

Fine. Plainly, then. Sable set her cup down. "Six years ago I was caught. Not arrested—caught, by the Ministry's memory division, which means they opened my head like a drawer and went through everything. Do you know what they found?" She laughed, and hated the sound. "A hole. I was born near-vessel, Cyrus. Enough of the old blood in me to sense gods, not enough to hold one. A failed door. They tried to make me a hunter instead. I ran before the training finished, and I've been running since, and the only thing I've learned is that half a soul is worse than none. You spend your life standing outside windows."

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

"So when I heard the old gods were answering prayers—" she gestured at the shrine, at the light that shouldn't be light, "—I thought: there's a whole god, unclaimed, riding around inside a charm-seller who doesn't even want it. And I thought, I could be whole. One trade. Your burden, my freedom. Everyone walks away richer."

"That's not how burdens work," Anselm said mildly, pouring the water.

"Everything is how burdens work, priest. Burdens are the only currency that never inflates."

But Cyrus wasn't looking at her. Cyrus was looking at Wren, who had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-argument, her head tipping against Cyrus's shoulder with the total trust of the very young and the very tired. And Cyrus let her. Didn't shift away. Didn't calculate the tactical disadvantage of an anchored arm.

Oh, Sable thought. Oh, that's bad. That's the worst possible development.

Because she remembered Cyrus from before—from the years when they'd worked opposite ends of the same markets, two thieves circling each other like weather fronts. Cyrus had rules then, the same rules she still recited like scripture: small memories only, no names, no magic. Rules as armor. And Sable had broken her once already, sold her name, and Cyrus had rebuilt the armor thicker, and now here was a child dismantling it plate by plate without even trying.

"You're going to say yes," Sable said quietly. "To my deal. Eventually. Because you think you deserve to be empty. I know that look. I invented that look."

"I'm going to say yes," Cyrus agreed, "because everyone in this room lives if I do. Including you. Especially you—you're the only one who knows the roads."

"And the god?"

"The god," Cyrus said, and her voice went strange, distant, like a voice heard through water, "has been very patient with me. Fifteen years of patience. I think it might wait a little longer while I decide what it's actually owed."

The candle flame stood up straight, tall and white, and for one moment Sable felt it—the presence, vast and patient and unbearably sad, looking at her through the flame the way you look at a stray animal that keeps coming back to the same empty window.

Then it was gone, and Sable discovered she was shaking.

* * *

*Cyrus*

They left before dawn, because Sable said the Inquisitor's hounds worked the night shift and dawn belonged to ordinary people. Cyrus carried Wren on her back for the last stretch when the girl's legs gave out, and Wren slept against her neck, mumbling something in a language Cyrus almost recognized—a language outlawed before Wren was born, a language that lived in exactly one place now, folded up under Cyrus's ribs.

At the north gate checkpoint, Sable talked their way past a bored sergeant with forged pilgrim papers and a story about a sick aunt, and it should have been terrifying, how good she was, how easily lies moved through the world when you'd stopped caring what they cost.

Beyond the walls, Vell's smog thinned and the sky did something enormous.

"Family," Wren said sleepily, from her shoulder. Not a question. A statement of fact, the way children state facts: the sky is up, bread is food, you are family.

Cyrus's throat closed. Hunters she could handle. Hunters wanted her body, and her body was negotiable real estate. But *family*—family wanted the part of her she'd never put a price on, the part she hadn't known was for sale until a pickpocket claimed it without paying.

Behind them, a bell began to ring in Vell—not the hour, not an alarm. The Ministry's calling bell, the one that meant a hunt had gone city-wide.

Sable glanced back, then at Cyrus, and for once there was no market smile. "They know which gate. We need the road that isn't there. Pray, priest."

Anselm closed his eyes and began to pray over empty air—and the air, slowly, like a wound remembering how to close, began to open into a road.

Cyrus walked into it carrying a sleeping child, followed by a traitor she couldn't kill and a god she couldn't keep, and understood that whatever waited at the end of the road, she was already losing the argument with herself.

The price was the god. She had agreed to the price.

So why, she wondered, as Elodie's erased hills flickered green on the horizon like a promise breaking its silence—why did it feel less like selling a burden, and more like giving away the last piece of someone she was only just beginning to meet?
