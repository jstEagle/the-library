## 6. The Road That Isn't There

I've stolen a lot of things in my life, but I've never once been on the wrong side of a theft until now. Being hunted has a texture to it. A grain. It sits under the skin like a splinter you can't reach, and every hour outside Vell's walls it digs deeper.

We left through the tannery drains at dawn, which Anselm called fitting and I called disgusting. Four of us: me, the priest with his satchel of outlawed rites, Cyrus moving like a woman who'd spent fifteen years practicing how not to be seen, and Wren—the orphan—walking between us like she'd been assigned to our company by some committee I never met.

The first road appeared an hour out from the city, and I want to be honest about what that did to me, because honesty is the one thing I've never sold.

Anselm stopped at a field's edge where there was nothing but stubble and frost, knelt, and said words in a language that made my teeth ache. Not pain exactly. *Recognition.* Like hearing your own name spoken by someone who died before you were born.

And the ground answered.

A lane unrolled through the wheat, pale as old bone, hedged on both sides with hawthorn that had no business growing in this soil. It ran straight toward hills I knew weren't there—couldn't be there, because I'd memorized every map of this country by the age of nine, and maps are a thief's first scripture—and those hills flickered like heat-shimmer, solid, then suggestion, then solid again.

"That's Elodie," Cyrus said. Her voice was flat, but her hands weren't. They were shaking.

"A piece of it," Anselm said. "The roads remember. Pray over them, and they'll carry you." He looked at me when he said it. "All of you. Even you, Sable."

"I don't pray."

"You will," said Wren, with the terrible certainty of children. "You're the angriest one here. Angry people make the best prayers. All that noise has to go somewhere."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about erased countries: they don't leave cleanly. Erasure is a sloppy art. The Ministry burned the churches and salted the shrines and rewrote the maps, but the land itself kept its own records. Rivers still bent around villages that weren't there. Birds still migrated along lines that matched no border Vell recognized. And under all of it, thin as a coin worn to foil, lay the roads.

Anselm taught us the rites as we walked. Small ones, mostly. How to greet a threshold so it would let you pass. How to thank water before drinking it, because gratitude kept the old things sweet. How to walk a prayer into the ground with your feet—*left, right, left, right*—until the rhythm itself became the words and the words became unnecessary.

Cyrus learned fast, which frightened her. I could see it: every rite that came easily to her was another confirmation of what lived inside her, and she wore knowledge the way other people wore wet clothes. Wren learned faster, which frightened everyone else.

Because Wren didn't just learn the rites. She heard them back.

"Stop," she said on the second evening, holding up a hand at an empty crossroads. "There's someone waiting."

There was nothing waiting. I checked. Old habit—I check everything—but there was nothing in that crossroads but wind and a leaning milestone with its inscription chiseled off.

"The god of travelers," Wren said, frowning at the air like a woman reading a letter held too close to her eyes. "He's been waiting since before I was born. He says—" She tilted her head. "He says he'd forgotten he was waiting. He says we remembering him is the same as him arriving."

Anselm went very still. Then he knelt in the dust of the crossroads and wept, quietly, the way men weep who have practiced it for years so it wouldn't interfere with anything.

"He's answering," he said. "Fifteen years of silence, and now they're answering." He looked up at Wren with something that scared me more than any hunter: hope. Raw, unarmored, enormous hope. "Child, do you know what you are?"

"Yeah," Wren said. "Hungry. Can we eat after the god arrives?"

* * *

I should say something about Cyrus, since everyone assumes I'm watching her for the bounty. I am. But not only that.

She was changing on that road, and she hated it. The woman who'd built herself out of smallness—small charms, small rules, small life—was walking through a country that kept recognizing her. Hawthorn bowed when she passed. Wells cleared when she drank. On the third day a shrine that had been a heap of stones for a generation reassembled itself behind us, stone by stone, with a sound like slow applause, and Cyrus didn't look back at it once. Which is how I knew she wanted to.

"You could just ask me," I told her that night, while Wren slept against Anselm's shoulder and the priest pretended to sleep and listened to every word. "About the god. What it says to you."

"It doesn't talk to me."

"Everyone lies about their gods. It's practically a rite."

Cyrus looked at me across the dead fire. There was frost forming on her eyelashes, and behind her eyes something vast turned over in its sleep, and I thought: *there it is. The most valuable thing in the world, wearing a tired woman's face.*

"When it talks," she said finally, "it sounds like my mother. That's how I know it's not really talking to me. It's talking through me, to whoever's listening. I'm just the room it happens to be standing in."

"And if the room gets tired of being a room?"

"Then the room burns down." She pulled her coat tighter. "Go to sleep, Sable. You're worse company than usual when you're scheming."

I wasn't scheming. That's the part I keep having to explain, later, to people who weren't there. I was calculating, which is different. Scheming wants something for yourself. Calculating just wants everyone alive at the end, and if the arithmetic requires that the god end up in my hands instead of hers—

Well. Arithmetic isn't loyalty. But it isn't betrayal either. Not yet.

* * *

The ambush came on the fifth day, at a place where two remembered roads crossed and the world couldn't decide which country it belonged to.

I smelled them before I saw them—Ministry hunters smell like cold iron and ink, like paperwork given legs—and I got Wren down behind the milestone a half-second before the first bolt took the space where her head had been.

Four of them. Gray coats, gray faces, the leader carrying a censer that smoked black. They came down the false road—the real one, the Vell one, the one that existed on maps—and the moment their boots touched the crossing, the hawthorn hedges *leaned in*, and I understood that the land had opinions about who traveled here now.

"Cyrus!" I shouted. "The rite! The threshold rite!"

But Cyrus had gone somewhere else. She stood in the open, arms loose, and her voice came out doubled—one woman and one thing older than women speaking in unison—and the words she said weren't in any language I knew, and the frost on the ground bloomed into white flowers in a ring around her feet.

The hunters hesitated. Hunters always hesitate at miracles; it's the flaw in their training.

It should have ended there. Maybe it would have, if the leader hadn't done the smart thing, the thing I'd have done: ignored the miracle entirely and shot the priest.

The bolt caught Anselm high in the chest, spinning him, and his satchel of rites scattered across the crossing like spilled coins. And Wren—Wren did the thing I will be processing for the rest of my life. She stood up out of cover, walked into the open, looked at the four armed men of the Ministry of Silence, and said, in a voice that was not entirely her own:

"*You are trespassing in a country that remembers you.*"

Every bird within a mile went up at once. The hedge moved. The leader's censer guttered out like a snuffed candle, and the hunters broke—not routed, but *unsettled*, falling back the way soldiers fall back from something their doctrine has no page for.

Except the leader. He raised his crossbow at Wren.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the throwing knife leaving my hand, and I remember Anselm—wounded, dying-gray, already kneeling—getting his hand up between the bolt and the girl anyway, because priests are apparently incapable of letting anyone else pay first.

The bolt went through his palm and pinned it to the milestone. By the time we killed the hunters—all four, and I won't describe it, and Cyrus's flowers drank more than frost that day—it was clear the hand wouldn't save. The wound had gone black at the edges before we even cut him free. Whatever the Ministry coats their bolts with, it isn't meant to wound.

Anselm sat against the milestone, cradling his arm, and asked for his knife with the calm of a man ordering wine.

"Hold it steady," he told me. "You've the steadiest hands of any thief I've known. Use them for something clean, just once."

"Sable," Cyrus said. My name, but not warning. Asking.

I did it. I won't tell you what it cost him or what sound he made, because he made almost none, and that was worse. I bound the stump with strips of his own robe, and Wren pressed her small hands over the bandage and whispered the traveler's greeting, the threshold rite, and I swear on every theft I've ever committed—the bleeding slowed. Not stopped. Slowed. As if the land itself was leaning over him, breathing for him.

"We're close," Anselm said afterward, gray-lipped, smiling like a man who'd gotten good news at his own funeral. "The capital. Two days. I can feel the veil from here, like a draft under a door." His eyes found mine, and there was no accusation in them, which was somehow unbearable. "You carried us this far, Sable. Whatever you're planning to steal at the end of this road—"

"Don't," I said.

"—steal something worth keeping."

* * *

That night Wren sat beside me at the fire, uninvited, the way she does everything.

"You prayed today," she said. "When you threw the knife. You prayed."

"I swore."

"Same thing. Louder." She poked the fire. "The traveler god says you're going to matter. He says it like an apology."

"Gods should mind their own countries."

"He doesn't have one anymore. That's why he waits at crossings." She leaned her head against my arm, casual as breathing, and I let her, and I have been trying to steal that moment back ever since, from myself, and failing. "Sable? When we get to the capital. When the door opens. What are you going to do?"

I watched Cyrus across the fire, asleep sitting up, frost flowering and fading around her feet with each breath. The most valuable thing in the world. The door to a whole country. And me, a thief, with a knife-throwing arm that hadn't missed in eleven years.

"Ask me tomorrow," I said.

Wren nodded, satisfied, as if I'd answered honestly.

Two days to the capital. Two days to find out whether I was a schemer or a calculator, a traitor or the only one pragmatic enough to keep us all alive.

The road hummed under us in the dark, praying itself forward, and ahead—past the hills that flickered like a held breath—the ruined towers of Elodie's capital stood up against the stars, closer than any map allowed, waiting to see what we'd brought it.
