## 7. Where Duty Keeps Its House

The capital of Elodie had no name anymore. That was the first thing Cyrus understood when they crested the last hill at dusk—the city below wasn't ruined so much as *unwritten*, its towers collapsed into shapes that refused to be architecture, its streets grown over with grass that grew in straight lines, as if the land remembered the roads but not what had walked them. Where a name should have been, there was a silence shaped exactly like one.

"It was called Vessa," Anselm said quietly. He stood at the hill's edge with his bandaged stump cradled against his chest, his face lit orange by the last of the sun. "The City of Bells. When they erased it, they erased the bells first. Everything else went after."

Cyrus felt it before she heard it: a hum in her sternum, low and patient, like a struck bell still deciding whether to finish ringing. Fifteen years she'd carried that hum and called it nothing—called it hunger, called it nerves, called it the price of stolen sleep. Here, standing over the city's ghost, she could no longer pretend.

*You brought me home,* said the voice that lived behind her ribs. It didn't use her ears. It never had. But here, this close to the veil, it was louder than it had ever been, warm as a hand on the back of her neck. *Thank you.*

"Don't thank me," she muttered. "I haven't decided anything."

Wren looked up at her, small hand still fisted in Cyrus's coat hem the way it had been since the ambush, since Anselm's blood had soaked into the road dust. The girl's eyes were wide, but not afraid. They hadn't been afraid in days, and that frightened Cyrus more than anything the Ministry could do.

"I can hear it," Wren whispered. "It sounds like morning."

Sable made a sound in her throat—not quite a laugh. She'd been quiet for two days, which from Sable meant she was calculating. Her dark eyes moved from the city to Cyrus to the horizon behind them, where the Ministry's signal-fires would be lighting soon, if they weren't already. "Home," she repeated. "Charming. Can we discuss this somewhere the hunters can't see us silhouetted against the sky like an offering?"

They went down into Vessa as the light failed.

* * *

They made camp in what had been a shrine—Anselm identified it by touch alone, running his good hand along a wall until he found the groove where a votive niche had been chiseled out, then filled in, then half-forgotten. He built a fire anyway. After fifteen years of outlawed gods, the gesture seemed almost obscene, and Cyrus caught herself glancing at the treeline twice before she let herself sit.

Wren fell asleep almost immediately, curled against Cyrus's side like she'd been doing it all her life instead of three weeks. Cyrus should have moved her. She didn't.

"So," Sable said, across the fire. She'd taken off her boots, which Cyrus knew was a performance; Sable only went barefoot around people she wanted off-balance. "Are we going to talk about it, or are we going to keep pretending you haven't known since the border village?"

"Since the border village," Cyrus admitted.

"And?"

"And I don't know." She stared into the flames. The god stirred behind her ribs, gentle, expectant, and she pushed it down the way she'd push down a cough in a quiet room. "Fifteen years I've been careful. Small memories. No names. No magic. I built a whole life out of being invisible, and now—" She gestured at the ruined country around them, at the sleeping child against her side, at all of it. "Now I'm apparently a door."

"You're not a door," Sable said. "Doors don't get a vote. You're a woman with something inside her that can be cut out. I've seen it done. Badly, once, but it can be done."

"Sable—"

"There are rites. Anselm knows them, or parts of them. You take the god out, you burn it or bury it or whatever you do with a dead thing's last prayer, and you walk away. You lose maybe a year of your life. Some color. A few memories—the ones it touched deepest." Sable leaned forward, firelight catching the scar through her eyebrow. "You'd live, Cyrus. That's the whole pitch. You'd *live.*"

"She'd be hollow," Anselm said. He hadn't opened his eyes, but he spoke now with the flat certainty of a man reciting scripture. "A vessel emptied isn't healed. It's *hollowed*. I've seen that too. The Inquisitor who hunts us is what's left when you cut a god out and leave the shape of it behind."

"The Inquisitor is a fanatic who did it wrong."

"They all do it wrong," Anselm said. "There is no right way to amputate a soul."

Cyrus closed her eyes. Behind her ribs, the god was quiet now—not withdrawn, just waiting, the way it had waited fifteen years for her to stop being afraid long enough to listen.

"And the other way?" she asked. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "The other option. Tell me plainly, Anselm. You've been saving it since the road."

Anselm opened his eyes. In the firelight he looked older than he had in Vell, older than the exile and the missing hand had made him—old the way stone is old.

"The veil between Elodie and the world is held shut by erasure," he said. "Not force. Erasure. To open it, the veil needs a voice that remembers everything the country was—and a body willing to become the opening. The vessel speaks the country back into being. Every border. Every name. Every bell." His gaze didn't waver. "And the vessel does not survive the speaking. The prayer spends itself. That is what a vessel *is*, Cyrus. A prayer given flesh so it can be spent."

The fire popped. Wren stirred against Cyrus's side and settled again.

"That's it, then," Cyrus said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Die loudly or die quietly. Those are my choices."

"No," said Wren.

Cyrus looked down. The girl was awake—had perhaps been awake the whole time—sitting up now, hair tangled, eyes fierce and wet in the firelight.

"No," Wren said again. "Those aren't choices. Those are two ways of you leaving. I'm not picking either one."

"Wren—"

"You always say my name like that. Like I'm a candle you're worried about blowing out." She scrambled to her knees, facing Cyrus fully, and she was so small and so absolutely unmovable that Cyrus's chest hurt. "Sable wants to cut you open. Anselm wants to spend you. Nobody has asked *you*. Nobody has even asked what *you* want."

What she wanted. Cyrus turned the question over and found it rusted shut. What had she wanted, these fifteen years? To be left alone. To be invisible. To sell enough charms to eat, to stay under the Ministry's notice, to never owe anyone anything because everyone you owed could be taken away. She had built a life the size of a locked room and called it freedom.

And then a pickpocket had grabbed her coat in a burning square, and the room had gotten bigger, and bigger, and now it contained a priest with nine fingers and a thief with a traitor's history and a dead god's prayer humming in her bones like a bell that wouldn't finish ringing—and the room was so big now that losing it would be worse than dying.

That was the trap. She saw it clearly, sitting there in the ruin of a country that had died rather than stop praying. Duty said: open the veil, spend yourself, save millions. Love said: cut it out, survive, come back. And underneath both, quieter, more seductive than either: *just rest.* Just let go. Fifteen years of fear and hiding and holding other people's memories so they wouldn't have to grieve—she was so *tired*. Erasure wasn't death, exactly. It was sleep without dreams. It was the locked room again, but with the door finally open, and nothing left inside to guard.

"Cyrus." Sable's voice, sharp. "Your eyes just went somewhere. Where did they go?"

"Nowhere," Cyrus said.

"Liar. I've sold memories; I know the look. You were shopping for exits."

Behind her ribs, the god stirred—not pushing, never pushing, just present, the way it had been present in a child's memory of a mother singing in an outlawed tongue. *You may rest,* it said, gentle as candlelight. *If you choose it, I will make it gentle. I have never lied to you, little door.*

"That's the worst part," Cyrus whispered. "It's kind about it."

She put her face in her hands. The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, in the unwritten streets of Vessa, wind moved through grass growing in the shape of roads, and if she listened—she hated that she listened—it sounded almost like bells.

"Here's what I want," she said at last, and her own voice sounded strange to her, scraped thin. "I want it to not be my problem. I want someone else to carry it for one hour. One hour where I'm just a charm seller from Vell with bad knees and no country, and nobody needs me to be a door or a martyr or a mother."

Silence. Then Wren crawled back against her side, tucked herself under Cyrus's arm with the fierce efficiency of a cat claiming territory, and said, "Okay. One hour. We'll watch it for you."

"We'll watch *what* for you?" Sable asked.

"The god. The choice. All of it." Wren yawned, absurdly, in the middle of the apocalypse. "One hour. Then you have to come back and decide. But you have to decide *with* us. Not for us. Not despite us. With."

Cyrus laughed—a broken, wet sound she hadn't made in years. She kissed the top of the girl's head, tasting smoke and road dust, and felt the god settle behind her ribs like a bird folding its wings.

"One hour," she agreed.

She got perhaps twenty minutes before Anselm, who had been watching the dark beyond the fire with increasing stillness, said quietly:

"The Ministry is here."

Cyrus followed his gaze. On the far ridge above the dead city, torches were kindling—one, then ten, then dozens, descending toward Vessa in a slow, deliberate line, like a lit fuse being walked toward them.

At their head, she could feel him even at this distance: a hollow where a man should be, cold as a blown-out lamp.

The Inquisitor had found the door.
