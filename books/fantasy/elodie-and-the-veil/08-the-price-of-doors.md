## 8. The Price of Doors

The bells woke us before the birds did.

Not the market bells, not the chapel bells—these came from the ridge above the valley, deep and wrong, a sound like a struck bone. Anselm was on his feet before I'd finished sitting up, one hand pressed flat against the shrine's cold stone, his face gone gray in the pre-dawn dark.

"They found the road," he said.

Sable was already moving, rolling her kit with the efficiency of someone who had packed to run her whole life. "How many?"

"More than we can fight. Fewer than they sent for me." Anselm's voice was steady, but I saw his eyes go to Wren, asleep in the corner with her knees drawn up, and something in them broke a little. "They'll want the vessel alive. The rest of us are negotiable."

I looked at Wren and felt the old arithmetic start up—the one where you count exits, count bodies, count what you can carry. Fifteen years of that arithmetic. It had kept me alive. It had kept everyone at arm's length, which is another way of saying it had kept me alone.

"Then we split," Sable said. "Cyrus and I take the veil road. You two circle back toward Vell—"

"No." It came out of my mouth before I'd decided anything. Everyone looked at me. Even the god stirred under my ribs, attentive, like a dog hearing its name. "We don't split. That's what they're counting on. Four people are harder to burn than one."

"Four people are easier to catch," Sable said.

"Then we'd better be quick."

Wren opened her eyes. She didn't ask what was happening. She just got up, tied her boots, and stood beside me the way she'd been doing since Vell—like it was the most obvious place in the world to stand. It terrified me more than the bells did.

* * *

They came down through the mist an hour after sunrise, and they were not the ragged squads that had burned the market square. These were the Ministry's true shape: gray coats, silver masks, and at their center a man on foot who wore no mask at all.

He was young, or he had been once. Now his skin had the waxy look of candle tallow, and where his eyes should have been there were pale scars, smooth as river stones. A failed vessel. I knew it the way you know weather coming—not from evidence but from the ache in your own bones. Someone had tried to put a god in him, or pull one out, and neither had worked. What walked down the hillside wearing his coat was what remained.

Anselm made a sound low in his throat. "Inquisitor Hale," he said. "They gave him the ash of Saint Verren. It wouldn't take."

Hale stopped twenty paces from the shrine and looked at each of us in turn, unhurried, the way a man looks at items on a shelf. When his gaze reached me, it stayed.

"You're full," he said. His voice was soft and terribly even. "Do you know what that's like? Being empty? They cut it out of me when I was nine years old, because it wouldn't bloom. And now here you are, grown fat on a whole country's ghost, and they send *me* to fetch you." He smiled without any warmth reaching his ruined eyes. "I have wanted to meet a successful vessel since before you could walk."

"You could stop," Anselm said. "Walk away. Tell them the road was false."

"I tried walking away once." Hale tilted his head. "It left scars."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

Sable moved first—but not toward the Ministry. Her hand went to my coat, to the satchel where the charms rode against my hip, and she tore it free with a thief's clean motion and held it up over her head like a prize.

"The vessel," she called out, loud enough for the gray ranks to hear. "And every stolen memory she ever hoarded. That's what you want, isn't it? Proof. Leverage. I'm the one who sold her name to you fifteen years ago—I can sell the rest of her now."

The world went very quiet. Even the god went still inside me, listening.

Wren made a small sound, half fury, half grief, and started forward, and I caught her arm because if she ran at thirty silver masks she would die in the first three steps, and I could not—I could not—

"Sable," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You always said sentiment was a debt."

"It is," she said, and for one heartbeat something flickered across her face, too fast to name. "That's why I never took it on."

Hale raised one scarred hand, and the gray line began to advance.

* * *

The battle at the shrine lasted less time than it takes to tell it, and I will spend the rest of whatever I am trying to tell it anyway.

Anselm did not fight. He walked to the veil-stone behind the shrine—the cracked slab where Elodie's border still breathed, thin as paper—and he laid both hands on it, and he *prayed*. Not quietly. He prayed the way avalanches start, in a language the Ministry had outlawed so thoroughly that even the stones had forgotten it, and the air above the stone began to tear.

Silver light. The smell of rain on a country that no longer existed. Through the widening seam I could see it: hills, terraced fields, a road going home.

"GO!" Anselm roared, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word, and I understood—he was holding the door open with everything he had, and everything he had was being spent.

Gray coats closed in. Sable fought—of course she fought, she'd been fighting her whole life—but she fought *away* from us, toward the treeline, buying herself an exit while we bought ours. Hale walked through the melee untouched, his own soldiers giving him room, coming for me with the patient certainty of a debt collector.

Wren grabbed my hand. "Come on, come *on*—"

I looked back once. Anselm was on his knees now, blood at his mouth, hands still on the stone, still praying. Three soldiers had hold of him and he did not seem to notice them at all. The veil shuddered and held.

"Cyrus!" Wren screamed.

I ran. God forgive me—or rather, the god *in* me forgive me—I ran, dragging Wren through the seam of light while behind me Anselm's prayer rose to a note no human throat should hold, and then the light went white, and then it went out.

When I turned, the shrine was just a shrine again. Cracked stone. Cold air. No priest.

Hale stood on the far side of the closing seam, staring at the space where the door had been, and his ruined face did something I hadn't thought it capable of.

He looked *lost*.

* * *

The other side was Elodie. Or its bones. Hills the color of memory, a sky too blue to trust, grass that flickered like a bad signal. We had come through into the heart of it—the ruined capital, the place the god had been leading us all along—and the veil sealed itself behind us with a sound like a book closing.

Wren collapsed to her knees in the silver grass and was sick, and then cried, and then went silent in a way that frightened me more than either. I knelt beside her and did the only thing I knew how to do with memories: I held still and let her lean.

"He's gone," she said finally. "He held the door and they—" She couldn't finish it.

"I know."

"You always know. You never *feel* it where anyone can see."

The words went in deeper than any blade the Ministry carried. I opened my mouth to deny it and found nothing there to deny it with.

A sound behind us. Sable, stepping through a fold in the flickering hills as if the land itself had gotten tired of hiding from her. Alone. Empty-handed. My satchel wasn't on her anymore; she must have dropped it when she ran, or lost it, or—no. Sable didn't lose things.

She stopped ten feet away, breathing hard, and for a long moment none of us said anything.

"You sold us," Wren said. Flat. Final. She got to her feet and put herself between me and Sable, this scrap of a girl facing down a woman who had forgotten more about cruelty than Wren would ever learn. "You sold her name, and today you sold her again, and Anselm is dead because—"

"Because I made a deal eight years ago that I've been paying interest on ever since," Sable said. Her voice was stripped bare, all the silk gone out of it. "They owned me, girl. They owned me the way they own everyone who's ever been hungry in Vell. Today was the last payment." She looked past Wren, at me. "I meant to hand you over and walk out rich. That was the plan. It was always the plan."

"And?" I asked.

"And then I watched the old man die holding a door open for a child who calls a charm-seller *family*, and I watched you run back for her instead of running free, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again, angrier, at herself. "I thought, *there it is. The debt.*"

From beyond the veil, faint through the stone and the miles, came the grind of the Ministry regrouping. Hale would find another way through. There was always another way through; that was what doors were for, and what Inquisitors were for, and I was the largest door left standing in the world.

Wren was still glaring at Sable, but her hand had found mine without her noticing, the way it always did.

"So what now?" Sable asked. "Your god wants a door opened. Your priest is ash. The Ministry is coming through the walls of a country that doesn't exist yet. Choose, Cyrus. Cut it out and live, or open it and die. Those are the only—"

"No," Wren said.

We both looked at her. She was staring at the ruined capital below us—at the broken towers, the drowned streets, the vast silence where a country used to sing—and her eyes had gone bright in a way I'd seen exactly once before, in the night market, the moment a widow's candle lit itself.

"There's always a third door," Wren said. "There's *always* a third door. You just have to be small enough to find it."

Under my ribs, the god of Elodie turned over in its sleep—and for the first time in fifteen years, it felt less like a weight and more like a hand, reaching for something it had almost remembered how to hold.

Below us, in the heart of the dead capital, a single window lit with candlelight.
