## 9. Three Ways to Split a Soul

The veil's heart was a room with no walls, and it smelled like my mother's kitchen.

That was the first cruelty of it. Not fire, not light, not the vast indifferent hum I'd braced for—just bread and woodsmoke and the ghost of thyme, the exact smell of a house in a country that no longer existed, rendered so faithfully that my knees nearly went. Anselm had told me what to expect: *the god will offer you comfort first, because comfort is how gods learn what you cannot refuse.*

He was dying behind us, or already dead—I couldn't let myself know which. Sable stood at my left shoulder with her arms crossed and her jaw set like a woman watching someone walk into deep water. And Wren.

Wren had both hands fisted in my coat, the way she'd clung to me in the burning square, and she was looking up at me with that flat, furious certainty she wore instead of tears.

"Don't," she said.

"Wren—"

"I heard you thinking on the road. I hear everything now, you know that." Her voice cracked down the middle. "You're going to do the thing where you decide for everybody. You're going to stand there and be noble and die, and you think that's love, but it isn't. It's just another kind of leaving."

The god stirred beneath my ribs—not speaking, not yet, but turning over the way something enormous turns in shallow water. Fifteen years I'd carried it without knowing. A child's prayer tucked into the only hiding place small enough to keep it safe: me. *Refuge,* it had asked, and some terrified girl in an outlawed language had said yes, and neither of us had understood what we were agreeing to.

"Tell her the terms again," I said to the air, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be argued with.

The voice came from everywhere, from the bread smell, from the ground. It sounded like a border village singing. **THE VESSEL OPENS AND IS SPENT. THE COUNTRY RETURNS AND THE VESSEL DOES NOT. THIS IS THE SHAPE OF DOORS.**

"See?" I said to Wren. "It's simple."

"It's *stupid*," Wren said. "It's the stupidest door I ever heard of. What kind of country gets built out of somebody being gone?"

Sable laughed once, short and ugly. "All of them, kid. That's how countries work." But she stepped closer to me when she said it, close enough that I felt the warmth off her shoulder, and I knew what that cost her—the woman who'd sold my name to the Ministry for enough coin to vanish, standing at my back against a god.

"There's another way," I said.

Both of them looked at me.

"It's forbidden," I said. "Anselm told me about it once, late, when he thought I wasn't listening. The rite they burned priests for even *before* Elodie fell. Splitting an inheritance. The old law says a vessel is a vessel whole—a door is a door, all of it or nothing. But doors—" I put my hand flat against my own sternum, against the slow tidal weight under it. "Doors can be hung on more than one hinge."

**THAT RITE UNMAKES THE ONE WHO SPEAKS IT AS SURELY,** the god said, and there was something in the voice I hadn't heard before. Not anger. Fear. Fifteen years in a stolen body, and the last prayer of a dead country was afraid of the dark too.

"Doesn't matter," I said. "I'm not trying to survive this clean. I'm trying to survive it *crooked*."

* * *

Here is what fifteen years of stealing memories teaches you: nothing is as whole as it pretends to be. Every charm I ever sold was a splinter of a life, and the lives kept living around the gap. Grief survives amputation. Love does. I had watched a widow weep over a first kiss I'd sold her for three coppers, and I had learned that the soul is not a coin you spend once—it's a coat you can take apart at the seams and still wear.

So I took myself apart at the seams.

I knelt in the room with no walls and laid out the rite the way I'd once laid out charms in the night market: careful, ordered, each piece named. Anselm's voice walked me through it from memory—*three vessels, thief; one to hold, one to root, one to keep*—and his voice in my head was steady even though his hand, his remaining hand, was probably cold somewhere behind me.

"One part," I said, "goes home to the land. Not through me. Into the ground itself, the roads and the rivers and the fields that have been pretending to be empty for fifteen years. Elodie doesn't need a keeper to come back. It needs *roots*."

The ground under me shuddered, eager.

"One part goes to Wren."

"No," Wren said immediately.

"You hear the gods. You've heard them since the market, since before—you're not a vessel, you never were, you're a *hearth*. There's a difference. A vessel is a door. A hearth is where the fire actually lives." I took her face in my hands. She was crying now, finally, furiously, hating every second of it. "I'm not giving you a duty. I'm giving you a *home*, and you get to decide what it means. That's the whole point. That's what nobody ever gave me."

"And you?" she managed. "What do you keep?"

"The third part." I smiled, and it hurt. "A third of a soul. Enough to watch sunrises. Enough to remember my own name—most days. Enough to be *here*, Wren, not gone, not spent, not a story you tell. Half-erased. Crooked. Alive."

**IT WILL HURT,** the god said.

"Yes," I said. "**But not forever.**"

* * *

I began.

I won't pretend I remember it cleanly—that's rather the point. What I remember is seams: the sensation of my own life opening along lines I'd never known were there, the childhood I'd never examined peeling away into the light, the outlawed lullaby, the border village, the small terrified girl saying *yes* to something enormous. I remember the god moving through me like a river through a breached dam, and I remember making choices while it moved, fast and rough, a thief dividing a hoard by feel.

To the earth: the borders. The longing. The deep patient weight of a country refusing to stay forgotten. I pressed it downward and felt the land drink.

To Wren: the warmth. The hearth-fire, the answering, the terrible gift of hearing prayers and being *allowed* to answer them. She gasped like someone coming up out of cold water, and when she opened her eyes they had candlelight in them, the same candlelight that had answered a widow's prayer in a night market a lifetime ago.

And what stayed in me—

I held it up to the light before I let it settle. A third of a soul, thin as winter light. My name, most of it. My hands, my habits, the specific way I loved people badly and then tried again. Some of Elodie's songs, though I'd lose the words and keep the tunes. The memory of these two faces. Not everything. Never everything again.

Then it closed over me like water, and it did hurt, exactly as promised.

* * *

I woke on grass that had not existed yesterday.

Real grass, dew on it, growing in the shadow of walls that were remembering how to be walls. Above me the sky was doing something I had no word for—unbraiding, the great gray veil-thread of the Ministry's silence pulling apart strand by strand, and underneath it the country of Elodie was surfacing like a swimmer after a long dive. Roads unrolled themselves toward horizons that had been blank on maps my whole life. A river remembered its name somewhere to the east. Somewhere a shrine rebuilt itself stone by stone out of pure stubbornness.

Wren was kneeling beside me, and she was *glowing*—not metaphor, not poetry, actual low gold light under her skin like a banked stove—and she grabbed my hand and squeezed hard enough to hurt.

"You're faded," she said, furious and relieved. "You look like a bad copy of yourself."

"I've been called worse by better."

Behind her, Sable stood with her back to us, watching the horizon, and didn't turn around, and her shoulders were shaking, and I decided to let her have that privacy the way she'd never once given me mine.

And the god—or the third of it that remained in the land, in the grass, in the morning—spoke one last time, not in words but in the green pushing up through old ruins, in the borders blooming like ink dropped in clear water:

*A country is not a vessel. A country is everyone who refuses to leave it.*

Wren pulled me upright. My legs held, mostly. My name came when I reached for it—Cyrus, Cyrus, say it twice and it stays—which felt, for a half-erased woman, like unimaginable wealth.

"Come on," Wren said, hauling me toward the sunrise, toward the sound of a river remembering itself, toward whatever a country does on its first morning alive in fifteen years. "You have to see this. You *have* to see this."

"I'm seeing it," I said. And then, quieter, testing the words like a new charm: "Take me home?"

Wren stopped dead. Turned around. Grinned through the tear-tracks, gold light flickering at her throat.

"Yeah," she said. "Yeah, family. Come *home*."

And ahead of us, at the edge of the growing light, the road bent toward a horizon with a country's name written on it—our name, all of ours—and started walking toward us.
