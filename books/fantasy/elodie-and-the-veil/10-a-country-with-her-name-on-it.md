## 10. A Country With Her Name On It

Wren woke to birdsong in a language she didn't know and understood anyway.

The syllables came through the broken window of the shrine like water through a cracked cup, and she lay still on the pallet of folded cloaks, listening, letting them settle into her the way rain settles into ground that has been dry too long. *Orenneth, orenneth, the dawn-walkers say.* She could feel the words the way you feel a stone under your bare foot—solid, warm from yesterday's sun, unmistakably there.

She sat up. The shrine's rafters had been blackened by Ministry fire, but overnight something had happened to the char: pale shoots had pushed up through it, thin as thread, and where Anselm had died there was now a patch of white flowers she had no name for except that her chest ached looking at them.

*Anselm's flowers,* she thought, and then corrected herself. *Elodie's. He was just the one who kept watering.*

Outside, Cyrus was standing at the edge of the ruin with her back to the shrine, and Wren went to her the way she'd gone to her for months now—without deciding to, without asking permission of the part of herself that remembered what it cost to walk toward someone.

Cyrus looked different. That was the word Wren kept circling and never landing on, because every other word was worse. *Less* was unbearable. *Faded* was accurate and cruel. In the end Wren had settled on *thinner*, like a coin worn smooth by too many hands—not worth less, exactly. Just harder to read.

"You're up early," Cyrus said, not turning.

"The country woke me up."

That got a huff that was almost a laugh. "It does that."

They stood together while the light came up over the hills, and Wren watched the borders happen.

That was the only way she could describe it. There was no wall, no line drawn anywhere—but she could *feel* where Elodie ended, the way you can feel where your own skin ends even with your eyes shut. To the east, the land ran green and certain all the way to a river that hadn't been on any map since before she was born. To the west, past the ridge, Vell still sat in its bowl of smoke and law, and the border between them wasn't a thing you could see so much as a thing you could *mean*—and meaning it made it true, the way saying a name makes a person real.

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked. "Holding your part."

Cyrus considered the question honestly, which was one of the things Wren loved about her and had never once said out loud, because some things you keep like a charm you stole fair and square.

"It hurts like a scar hurts," Cyrus said finally. "Not when I'm still. Only when I stretch."

Down the slope, Sable was arguing with a fence post.

That was the truth of it, more or less. She'd spent three days trying to leave—packing her satchel at dawn, walking to the ridge by noon, being back by dark with her satchel unpacked and a face like a slammed door—and each day the road let her get a little farther before the land itself seemed to lose interest in her direction of travel and gently set her down again, closer to home than she'd started. Yesterday she'd shouted at the horizon for a full minute. Today she was negotiating with a fence post that had sprouted, overnight, into a boundary stone carved with the old script.

"*I don't consent to this,*" Wren heard her tell it, in the flat voice of a woman who knew exactly how ridiculous she sounded and had decided to be ridiculous anyway.

The stone did not answer. But the carving brightened slightly, the way a hearth brightens when someone sits down beside it, and Sable stared at it for a long moment with an expression Wren recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors once, years ago, before she'd learned better: the look of a person who has been alone so long that kindness feels like a trap.

Sable stayed. Nobody asked her to. That was, Wren suspected, the entire mechanism.

By midmorning people came.

Wren hadn't expected it, though maybe she should have—the way you don't expect spring and then feel foolish every year. They came up the old roads from the valleys, families mostly, carrying everything they owned on their backs, and they stopped at the boundary stone and touched it and wept without making noise about it, the way people weep who have practiced being quiet for a generation. Some of them spoke Vellish. Some spoke the other language, the waking one. Most spoke both, sliding between them like stepping between sun and shade.

A woman with grey braids knelt in front of Wren in the shrine yard and pressed her forehead to the ground.

"No," Wren said, horrified, pulling her up. "Get up. Don't do that."

"You're the keeper," the woman said. Her eyes were wet and fierce. "The hearth-child. The songs said—"

"I'm fourteen."

"You're the keeper," the woman repeated, as if age were a technicality, and maybe it was. Maybe that was the whole lesson of the last year: that the things that matter most are always handed to people who are far too young or far too tired or far too broken, and the handing itself is what heals the hands.

Wren took her duties seriously after that, in the small ways available to a fourteen-year-old keeper of a newborn country. She learned the prayers Anselm had left in his ledger-book, the ones he'd written in the margins during exile like a man smuggling seeds in his coat lining. She lit the lamps at dusk—all nine of them, one for each province the maps had erased. She listened. That was most of the job, she discovered. The god-part of her inheritance wasn't thunder; it was attention. When a farmer couldn't sleep for grief, Wren sat with him. When children argued, she arbitrated. When the land itself murmured—about water, about walls, about where the next shrine ought to stand—she heard it and wrote it down, and adults twice her age did what she wrote, and somehow nobody found this strange, because the land had chosen her and the land's judgment outranked everyone's.

Cyrus taught her the other half. Quietly, in the evenings: how to hold a memory so it doesn't bruise. How to give one away without giving yourself away. How to know when a charm is a gift and when it's a leash.

"Small memories only," Cyrus would say, tapping Wren's knuckles like a tutor. "No names. No magic."

"You're retired," Wren would answer. "You don't get rules anymore."

"I'll always get rules. Rules are how you stay *yourself*." And then Cyrus would go quiet, looking at her own hands, thinking about the third of herself she'd fed to the veil, and Wren would change the subject fast, because some rooms you don't follow your family into unless they invite you.

But here is what Wren noticed, and kept noticing, and treasured in secret the way she'd once treasured stolen coppers: Cyrus was *lighter*. Not less. Lighter. For fifteen years she'd carried a god and never once put it down, and now the weight was shared three ways—land, child, woman—and what remained of her was enough. Was, Wren thought fiercely, more than enough. A person didn't need to be whole to be complete. A door doesn't need to be a wall.

* * *

The morning of the first sunrise, Cyrus woke before the others and walked out alone, and for once that was all right. Solitude used to be her native country; now it was just a village she visited.

She stood where the shrine yard met the eastern slope and watched the sky do its slow work. Below her, Elodie was breathing. She could hear it—chimney-smoke rising from houses rebuilt in a week, hammering, a dog barking, someone singing the outlawed song at full unoutlawed volume, badly, joyfully. Fifteen years ago she had sold a widow a first kiss and told herself she was keeping the world safe by keeping it small. Now the world had ignored her advice entirely, grown borders and languages and shrines overnight like a garden left untended too long, and the strange thing, the thing she kept turning over like a coin, was that she felt no fear at all.

Invisible no longer. Noticed, thoroughly, permanently—a country had her name woven into its founding, for pity's sake—and alive anyway. Alive *because* of it, if she was honest, which she was trying to be lately. Belonging turned out not to be a debt you paid but a door, and doors, she had learned at great cost, open inward.

Footsteps behind her. Small ones. Wren came and stood at her shoulder without a word, and after a moment a second set joined them—heavier, reluctant, pretending to be passing through. Sable stopped three feet away and looked ostentatiously at the horizon, arms crossed, radiating the message *I am not part of this* with the conviction of a woman who had tried to leave four times.

"Morning," Cyrus said.

"Hm," said Sable, staying.

They watched the sun clear the ridge. Light poured down the new-green valley and caught the boundary stones one by one, west to east, each carved name kindling like a lamp being lit along a road, until the whole border of Elodie was a ring of small fires welcoming the day.

"Anselm would have hated missing this," Wren said softly.

"He'd have taken notes," Cyrus said. "Terrible ones. Then he'd have cried and blamed the wind." Her throat tightened around the laugh, but it came anyway, and Wren leaned into her side, and Sable did not move away, and that was its own kind of leaning.

The sun rose over a country that existed again. Somewhere below, a bell rang in a shrine that hadn't rung in fifteen years, and the sound rolled out across the fields like a name being called home.

Cyrus breathed it in—smoke, grass, wet stone, the particular smell of a world that had decided to continue—and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like the old god's voice but was entirely her own:

*I kept the door. I just never guessed it opened onto this.*

Then Wren tugged her sleeve and said, "Keeper's duties. There's a family at the west stone asking for you by name," and Cyrus Blackwood—charm-seller, vessel, half-erased, wholly found—turned and walked down into the country with her name on it, and did not look back, because everything she needed was ahead of her, asking for her by name.
