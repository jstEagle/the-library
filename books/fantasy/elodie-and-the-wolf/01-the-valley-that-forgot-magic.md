## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The ward-stone at Miller's Bend had cracked again, and Coral Ashgrove crouched beside it in the blue hour before dawn, running a thumb along the fissure the way a farrier checks a hoof.

"Third time this season," they muttered.

The stone didn't answer. None of them ever did. That was the whole arrangement, and had been for as long as anyone in Hollowmere could remember: the valley kept its magic out, the god kept its silence, and Coral Ashgrove kept the stones standing. Three duties, three silences, one long unbroken peace that everyone in town called a blessing and Coral privately called a truce nobody had bothered to read.

They pressed their palm flat against the stone's cold face. Beneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the ward hummed. It always hummed — a low, patient note that Coral had stopped consciously hearing years ago, the way you stop hearing your own pulse or the creak of your own house. Tonight, though, their hand found it and their hand did not like it. The note was thin. Off. Like a singer reaching for a pitch and landing just shy of it, over and over, too tired to correct.

Coral pulled back their sleeve and looked at the mark on their wrist.

It sat there as it always did: a coiled line of silver-gray, like a serpent drawn by someone who had only ever had serpents described to them. The god's mark. The oath-mark. Twelve years old now, same as Elodie, though the mark was older than that in every way that mattered. It had not moved, spoken, warmed, or so much as twitched since the day it settled into their skin, and Coral had long ago made peace with serving a god that communicated exclusively through absence. Some gods burned bushes. Theirs sent invoices.

"You could tell me if something's wrong," Coral said to it. "That's all I'm asking. One flicker. One itch."

The mark lay still, silver on brown skin, indifferent as a scar.

Coral exhaled through their nose, stood, and rolled their shoulder until it popped — an old injury from the border wars, from the life before the valley, from the person named Ashgrove who had been a mercenary instead of a warden. The title had changed. The work hadn't. You walked the line, you checked the stones, you fixed what was broken, and when something came down from the high passes looking for trouble, you killed it quietly and buried it deeper. Twenty-two years of soldiering had taught Coral exactly two things worth teaching: how to read a fight before it started, and how to tell the difference between quiet and *wrong*.

The valley tonight was wrong.

Not loud-wrong. Nothing so useful. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has moved a chair two inches and left everything else untouched. The wards hummed flat. The air had a pressure to it, like the whole valley was holding its breath. And the sky —

Coral looked up. The stars were all there. Every one of them, in their places, burning the way they'd burned every night of Coral's posting. But they looked *farther* tonight. Dimmer. As if the whole ceiling of the world had been raised a hand's width while no one watched.

"Hm," said Coral, which was what they said instead of the worse things they thought.

They finished the circuit anyway. That was the discipline. You didn't turn back early because the night felt strange; you turned back early because you were dead or because the job was done, and Coral was neither. So they walked the full line — Miller's Bend to the Tannery Rise, up past the orchard terraces where the trees stood black and patient against the dark, along the ridge where the last stone squatted like a tooth in the hillside. At each one they laid a palm, listened, frowned, and moved on. All twelve hummed. All twelve hummed flat.

At the ridge stone, the oldest one, worn smooth as river glass, Coral paused longer than usual. This was the stone the old-timers touched for luck, though luck in Hollowmere was a strange currency — the story went that the wards didn't keep danger *out*, they kept magic *out*, and that anything magical left outside the line would eventually lose interest and wander off, the way a cat loses interest in a door that never opens. The valley wasn't protected so much as it was boring. Deliberately, divinely boring. Whatever wanted in would find nothing here but mud, mutton, and mortals, and would move along.

It had worked for eleven generations.

Coral knocked twice on the ridge stone with a knuckle, an old habit, half salute and half apology, and started down toward home.

* * *

The house sat at the valley's edge, close enough to the ward-line that Coral sometimes joked they were less neighbors to Hollowmere than customs officers to it. It was a small house, stone-footed, timber-bodied, with a garden gone wild at the edges because Elodie kept planting things she'd read about in books — moonflowers, whisper-mint, a row of something she insisted was dragon's bane and Coral was fairly sure was a weed with ambitions.

Light in the window. Good. She was supposed to be asleep, but "supposed to be" had never once governed Elodie's behavior, and Coral had learned to count the small rebellions — candle after hours, boots on the table, questions at dinner that began with *why does* and ended somewhere Coral couldn't follow — as signs of health rather than defiance.

Inside smelled of bread and lamp oil. Elodie was at the table, cross-legged on the bench, a book open in front of her and her chin propped on both fists, dark hair escaping its braid in every direction like she'd lost a fight with a hedge. She looked up when the door opened, and her whole face did the thing it always did — the quick, searching brightness, the way she checked Coral's eyes first and hands second, cataloguing damage before greeting.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm exactly on time."

"You're late *and* you're lying about it." She slid off the bench. "There's bread. I made it. Well, I started it, and then Mrs. Halloway came by and finished it, because she said my loaves have 'structural ambitions.' Her word."

"That woman has been insulting your baking since you were seven."

"I know. I'm keeping a list." She poured tea without being asked, which she'd been doing since she was nine, and pushed the cup across the table. "Anything out there?"

Coral considered lying properly, then decided against it. They'd raised her on a simple rule: you don't have to tell a child everything, but you should never tell a child something false, because children build their houses on whatever ground you give them. "Wards are humming flat," they said. "All twelve. Can't find a break anywhere."

Elodie's brow creased. She had her mother's brow, whoever her mother had been — Coral knew the face only from memory and one stolen glance at a ledger page twelve years ago, and even that was more than the girl herself would ever be told. "Flat like sick-flat or flat like empty-flat?"

Coral blinked. "What's the difference?"

"Sick-flat is when something's wrong with the sound. Empty-flat is when something's wrong with the thing making the sound." She said it the way she said most things — plainly, without drama, as if reporting the weather. Then she went back to her book as if she hadn't just put her finger on the exact shape of Coral's unease better than Coral had managed all night.

"Where do you get this," Coral asked, "the sick-flat versus empty-flat?"

"Books." A page turned. "You should try them sometime. They're like patrols, but for facts."

Coral drank the tea. It was over-steeped and slightly too sweet, which meant Elodie had made it for herself and generously shared the error. Outside, the window showed a slice of valley and a slice of sky, and the stars in that slice seemed, if anything, farther away than they had on the ridge.

Twelve years ago, Coral had carried a bundle up from the low country in the dead of winter — a baby with a birthmark on her heel and a contract folded beneath her blankets, delivered into Coral's hands by a broker whose face Coral had never seen and whose voice Coral had never forgotten. *Raise her ordinary,* the voice had said. *Keep her inside the line. When the debt comes due, the court will send for her.* Coral had signed nothing, agreed to nothing aloud, and understood perfectly well that silence was itself a signature. They had taken the child anyway. Not because of the contract. Because the bundle had cried, and Coral — who had spent twenty years learning to ignore crying, to treat it as terrain, as noise, as weather — had picked her up, and something in their chest had closed around her like a fist and never opened again.

The court had never sent for her. Eleven generations of quiet, and the invoice had never come. Coral had begun, cautiously, treacherously, to believe it never would.

"You're doing the face," Elodie said, not looking up.

"What face?"

"The one where you're thinking about something you've decided not to think about." She turned a page. "You get a line right here." She tapped the space between her own eyebrows. "Mrs. Halloway says it's from squinting at the horizon. I say it's from hoarding."

"Hoarding."

"Thoughts. You hoard them. You think if you don't spend them, nobody can take them." Now she did look up, and her eyes were her own — gray-green, sharp, entirely unsold and unbargained, as far as Coral knew. "I'm not a child anymore, you know. I'm twelve. That's practically a mercenary."

"It's practically a schoolchild."

"In some cultures it's a mercenary."

"In no cultures."

She grinned, and the grin was the sun of the house, and Coral felt the day's wrongness recede from it the way frost recedes from a window with a fire behind it. This was the arithmetic Coral had done twelve years ago and re-did every morning since: whatever the contract said, whatever the court below the mountain wanted, whatever debt ticked in ledgers Coral had never seen — none of it outweighed this. Bread. Tea. A girl who insulted their reading habits and kept lists of Mrs. Halloway's crimes. If the price of keeping it was walking a ward-line every night and listening to a silent god, that was cheap. That was the bargain of a lifetime, and Coral had gotten away with it.

"Bed," Coral said. "School tomorrow. Master Fenwick's doing fractions, and he'll want you to demonstrate, because you're his favorite and he likes showing you off like a prize pig."

"A prize pig who can do fractions," Elodie said, gathering her book. "Fine. But if the wards are broken, you should fix them before bed, not after."

"They're not broken."

"They're *wrong*, though." She paused in the doorway, book hugged to her chest, silhouetted against the hall's dim lantern-light. And for a moment — just a moment — something in her posture was older than twelve. Something in her voice dropped below its usual register, into a plainness that wasn't quite hers. "You can hear it too. Everyone pretends they can't, but you can hear it. It sounds like it's waiting for permission."

Then she blinked, and she was twelve again, yawning, complaining about fractions, shuffling off to her room with the boneless grace of the young. Coral sat alone at the table with the cooling tea and the words *waiting for permission* sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone.

*Waiting for permission.* Waiting for what? Permission from whom? The wards didn't wait. The wards were walls; walls didn't ask. Unless —

Unless the walls weren't walls at all. Unless they were doors, and doors, unlike walls, had two sides, and hinges, and locks, and locks could be opened from either end if you had the key.

Coral looked at the mark on their wrist. Silver coil, dead still, same as ever.

"Don't," they told it quietly. "Whatever's coming, whatever this is — don't you dare make me choose."

The mark said nothing. It was very good at saying nothing. It had had twelve years of practice.

* * *

Coral slept badly and woke before dawn out of habit rather than rest, and the habit was the only reason they saw it.

They were at the window, splashing water on their face from the basin, when the light changed. Not brightened — *changed*. The pre-dawn gray took on a faint undertone, a color with no name, like the sky remembering a different sky. Coral froze, water dripping from their chin, and looked east, where the horizon should have been paling toward sunrise.

The stars were going out.

Not falling. Not fading. Going out — one by one, in no order Coral could track, each one snuffed with the clean finality of a pinched wick. And between one blink and the next, it wasn't one by one anymore. It was all of them, everywhere, the entire ceiling of the world going dark at once, and the darkness that rushed in behind them was not the darkness of night but the darkness of a held breath, of a lung emptied, of a room where someone has finally, after eleven generations of patience, blown out the candles.

Below the floorboards, beneath the hearthstone, under the roots of the mountain itself, Coral felt — did not hear, *felt* — something vast shift its weight.

And on their wrist, for the first time in twelve years, the god's mark began to burn.
