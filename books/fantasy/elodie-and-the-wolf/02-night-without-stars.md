## 2. Night Without Stars

The wards fell at the hour when even the dogs had given up barking.

Coral knew it first in their feet. Twelve years of walking the ward-line had taught them the stones' rhythm — a low hum through boot leather, steady as a heartbeat, so constant they'd stopped hearing it years ago and only felt its absence like a missing tooth. Tonight, halfway between the third stone and the fourth, that hum stuttered.

Then it stopped.

Coral stood very still in the dark and listened to the silence where a sound should have been. The valley breathed around them — wind in the pines, the creek muttering over its stones, some night bird working through its three-note question. All of it ordinary. None of it right.

They pressed two fingers to the nearest ward-stone. Cold. Not winter-cold, not stone-cold. Cold the way a dead man's hand is cold, cold with nothing left inside it. The faint silver script that lived in the rock's grain — the script no one in the valley could read anymore, inherited from grandmothers who'd inherited it from someone else entirely — was fading as Coral watched, letters going out one by one like windows in a house at dusk.

"No," Coral said, to the stone, to themselves, to whoever was listening. "Not tonight."

The mark on their wrist answered.

It had been quiet for six years — a pale scar in the shape of an open eye, inked into them by a god who never spoke, who communicated only in warmth and weight and the occasional dream of falling water. Six years of silence, long enough that Coral had begun to wonder if the god had simply moved on, the way landlords move on, the way employers do. Now the scar burned like a coal pressed to skin, and beneath the burn came something worse: a pulling. A direction. South and east, toward the mountain that anchored the valley's southern end like a fist closed around the horizon.

*Come here,* said the pull, without words, the way the god had always spoken. *Come here now.*

"Not yet," Coral told it, because Elodie was home, and home was north and west, and there had never been a moment in twelve years when the choice between those two directions had been anything but instant.

They ran.

* * *

The house sat where it always sat, squat and smoke-stained under its turf roof, the goat's pen leaning against the south wall where the goat had pushed it crooked every spring for a decade. Ordinary. Deliberately, stubbornly ordinary. Coral had built it that way on purpose — a house that looked like nothing, guarded nothing, promised nothing. A house you could disappear behind.

"Elodie." They were through the door without opening it properly, shoulder taking the frame, voice pitched low out of habit though every instinct screamed to shout. "Elodie, we're leaving."

She was already awake. Of course she was. She sat up in her loft bed with her hair a black riot and her eyes too wide, and Coral saw that she'd lit the stub of candle she wasn't supposed to have, the one she thought they didn't know about.

"I know," she said. "I felt it."

"Felt what."

"The valley changed." She said it simply, the way she said true things, which was always the worst way. "Like a door opened somewhere. Like something walked in."

Coral looked at this child — twelve years old, all elbows and questions, who had come to them as a squalling bundle wrapped in a blanket that cost more than the house — and felt the old fear rise, the one they kept buried under routine and rations and the daily small work of staying alive. The fear that had a name they never spoke aloud.

"Pack nothing," they said. "We go now."

"But—"

"Now."

To her credit, she moved. She'd been raised by a mercenary; she knew what *now* meant. She dropped from the loft, pulled her boots on without untying them, jammed her arms into the coat Coral held out. Only at the door did she stop, one hand on the latch, and look back at the little room with its carved toys and its window facing east.

"Coral," she said. "Look outside."

Coral looked.

The sky was empty.

Not clouded — clouds would have been a mercy, clouds would have been weather, explainable, survivable. The sky above the valley was clear and black and utterly bare, stripped of every star, every wandering light, everything that had hung overhead for as long as anyone had ever looked up. It was as if the whole night had been wiped clean by a great wet cloth, and what remained was not sky but a ceiling, close and smooth and absolute.

Coral had fought in three wars and survived things that still visited their dreams wearing old faces. They had never once been afraid of the sky.

They were afraid of it now.

"Back door," they heard themselves say, calm, mercenary-calm, the voice that meant *do exactly as I say and don't ask*. "Don't run ahead of me. Don't fall behind. If I let go of your hand, you keep moving anyway. Understood?"

Elodie's hand found theirs. Small fingers, strong grip. "Understood," she whispered.

They went out into a world gone strange.

* * *

Magic came back to the valley like floodwater.

Coral felt it in the first hundred steps — a pressure change, a taste on the air like copper and green things rotting sweetly. For generations the valley had been a place where magic simply did not function; that was why people like Coral lived here, people with marks and debts and histories, people who needed somewhere the old powers couldn't reach. The wards hadn't just kept things out. They'd kept the whole strangeness of the world at bay, and inside that kept space, life was small and hard and safe.

Now the wards were down, and the strangeness poured in.

Grass glowed faintly where Elodie's boots touched it, each footprint blooming soft blue and fading. The creek ran backward for a stretch of thirty yards, water climbing uphill with a sound like laughter played too slow. In the pastures, the sheep stood in perfect circles, heads turned toward the mountain, silent, attentive, waiting for something. And everywhere — everywhere — the air had a texture to it now, a thickness, as if the whole valley had been submerged in something that watched.

"What is it?" Elodie breathed. She wasn't scared, or she was scared but also something else, and that something else frightened Coral more than terror would have. She tilted her head at the glowing grass like a girl listening to music. "It's *loud*, Coral. Why is it so loud?"

"You can hear it?"

"Can't you?" She glanced up, puzzled, and in the starless dark her eyes caught light that wasn't there. "It's singing."

The mountain chose that moment to answer.

It started as a tremor in the ground, deep and slow, not an earthquake's violence but something more deliberate — a shifting of weight, vast and patient, the way a sleeper turns over. Dust sifted from the turf roof behind them. The goat began to scream, high and human, a sound Coral had never heard a goat make and hoped never to hear again.

Then, beneath the tremor, beneath the goat, beneath the singing Elodie still seemed to hear: a footstep. One. So large and so deep that it took four full seconds to pass through the earth under their feet, a slow rolling wave that set the pines swaying in the dark.

Something was walking inside the mountain. Or waking. Or coming up.

Coral did not wait to learn which.

They ran — north and west, away from the mountain, away from the pull on their wrist that burned hotter with every step, dragging at them like a hook set in meat. Elodie matched them stride for stride; she was fast, she'd always been fast, legs like a colt's, and Coral's hand around hers was less guidance than anchor, the one point of contact keeping them both tethered to the shape of the plan, which was simple: the old drover's track, the high pasture, the gap in the northern ridge where the ward-stones thinned and a body could slip out of the valley before whatever was happening finished happening.

Simple plans. Coral had learned long ago that the gods — any gods, even mute ones, even dead ones, even ones that apparently weren't done with you after all — loved to watch simple plans break.

At the fork by the millstone, where the track split toward the high pasture, the pull on Coral's wrist became unbearable. Fire laced up the forearm, across the shoulder, into the jaw. They staggered, dragged half a step sideways, southward, toward the mountain, and heard themselves make a sound they didn't recognize — a grunt, an animal noise of resistance.

"Coral!" Elodie pulled back against them, both hands on their arm now, planting herself like a fencepost. "Coral, look at me. Look at *me*."

They looked. Her face was pale and set in the lightless dark, and she was not afraid — that was the terrible thing, she was *not afraid*, she looked at the burning mark on Coral's wrist the way she looked at sums that wouldn't balance, with fierce concentration, as if the answer were owed to her personally.

"It wants you to go to the mountain," she said.

"Yes."

"Don't." Her grip tightened. "Whatever it is — don't listen to it. You always say the mark is just a scar. You always say the god went quiet and quiet means gone. So it's gone, and that thing pulling you isn't it, and we're not going."

For a moment — one long breath, two — Coral stood in the ruined dark with a child's hands on their arm and a god's hook in their flesh, and understood that both of them were right, and that being right was not going to save anybody.

Then the second footstep came, closer than the first, and the decision made itself.

"We go north," Coral said. "We go now. We don't stop until sunrise, and if there's no sunrise—" They stopped. Swallowed it. "There'll be a sunrise."

They ran.

* * *

They made the high pasture as the valley behind them began to glow.

Coral risked one look back, because a soldier always looks back, because knowing what follows you is worth the price of looking. What they saw would stay with them longer than the scars.

The mountain's southern face had opened. Not cracked — *opened*, a seam of darkness spreading down the rock like a wound parting lips, and out of that darkness rose a light that was not light, a black radiance, illumination made of absence, pouring upward into the starless sky and pooling there like smoke against glass. The sheep had abandoned their circles and were streaming downhill in a silent white flood, and among them, walking upright, unhurried, were figures. Dozens. Scores. Too tall, all of them, and too smooth in their movements, gliding rather than stepping, fanning out across the valley floor with the calm of owners inspecting land that had finally, finally come back to them.

One of them stopped. Turned. Even at half a mile, even in the dark, Coral felt its attention land on them like a physical weight — felt it find Elodie's hand in theirs, and linger there.

And then, carried across the distance on air that should not have been able to carry anything so softly, came a voice. It was not loud. It was courteous. That was the worst part — it was perfectly, exquisitely courteous, the voice of a host greeting guests who are very late.

"*Thirteenth payment,*" it said, and Coral knew, with a certainty older than thought, older than the wards, older than the twelve years of porridge and mended boots and bedtime stories, that it was not speaking to them.

It was speaking to the child whose hand they held.

Elodie's fingers tightened around theirs. When Coral looked down at her, she was staring back at the mountain, at the courteous dark, and her expression was not the expression of a frightened child.

It was the expression of a girl who has just remembered something she was made to forget.

"Coral," she said quietly. "I think I know what my name is."

The figures below began to walk toward them.
