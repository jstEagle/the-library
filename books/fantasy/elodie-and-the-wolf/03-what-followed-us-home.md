## 3. What Followed Us Home

The root cellar smelled like dirt and old apples and the particular cold of things that had been underground a long time. Elodie knew that smell better than she knew most smells. She'd hidden down here before — from thunderstorms when she was small, from the year of the fever, from Coral's bad nights when the shouting in their dreams got loud enough to wake the whole house.

She had never hidden like this.

Coral had shoved her through the cellar door so fast she'd barely gotten her feet under her, one hand clamped over her mouth, and said three words against her ear: *Don't. Make. Sound.* Then they'd pulled the door shut and the dark had come down on her like a lid.

That was before the sky ate itself.

She'd seen it happen. She'd been at the window when every star went out at once — not dimmed, not clouded over, *gone*, like someone had reached up with a wet cloth and wiped them off the black. And then the wards along the valley rim, which hummed so low and constant that Elodie had stopped hearing them years ago, had stopped humming all together. The silence where they'd been was worse than any sound. It was like standing next to a river your whole life and then one morning it simply wasn't there.

Then magic came in.

She didn't know how else to describe it. The air had changed texture. The lamp flame had leaned toward the window like a dog hearing its name. And far away, beneath the mountain that made the valley's northern wall, something had *moved* — she'd felt it in her teeth, in the floorboards, in the water glass on the table shivering into rings.

Now she sat in the dark between a barrel of turnips and the stone foundation wall, knees pulled to her chest, and listened to the thing walk through her house.

It didn't walk like a person. People had weight; they announced themselves even when they tried not to. This was more careful than that. Each step landed soft as snowfall, but the whole house heard it anyway — the floor above her head creaking in places Elodie hadn't known could creak, the hanging pans in the kitchen swaying without touching each other, giving off a faint bell-tone, once, twice, like a warning nobody wanted to say out loud.

*It came through the front door,* she thought. *We locked the front door.*

Then: *Where's Coral?*

That thought arrived and refused to leave. Coral had put her down here and gone back up. Gone back toward the door. Toward the thing. Because that was what Coral did — Coral stood between Elodie and everything, always, since before Elodie could remember. Bandits on the north road, the tax collectors, the fever winter, the boy who'd broken her arm falling out of the pear tree and the man who'd wanted to blame somebody for it. Coral stood in front of things. That was the shape of their love. She'd never had another word for it until now, sitting in the dark, and the word was *terrifying*.

Above her, the footsteps stopped.

The house went so quiet she could hear her own blood moving. She pressed both hands over her mouth and nose and breathed through her fingers, slow, the way Coral had taught her — *panic is loud, Elodie, panic is the first thing anything hunts* — and stared into the blackness where she knew the shapes of things were: the turnip barrel, the shelf of preserves, the ladder up to the trapdoor, the gap under the stairs where she used to hide her drawings.

Something spoke.

Not loudly. Not from directly overhead. From somewhere in the middle of the house, a voice like dry paper folding, patient as erosion:

"Elodie."

Her heart stopped. Actually stopped — she felt it stumble, trip, catch again. Because that was her name, yes, that was exactly her name, and no one was supposed to know she was here, and —

No. That wasn't what made her blood go strange.

It was the way the voice said it. Everyone said *Elodie*. Coral said it gruff and warm, like a coat being handed over. Vess said it bright, like a coin flipped. The schoolmistress said it tired. But this voice said it the way you read a word off a page you'd written yourself. Familiar. Certain. Like it had known the name longer than she had.

Like it had *given* her the name.

"No," she breathed into her hands. Barely air. "No, no."

The footsteps resumed. They moved toward the kitchen. A drawer slid open — the third drawer, the one with the good knives — and slid shut again, unhurried. Whatever it was, it wasn't rummaging. It was *browsing*. It was reading her house the way she read books, cover to cover, taking its time.

"Coral Ashgrove." The paper-voice again, closer now, drifting along the hall. "You have kept her well."

Elodie's stomach dropped through the floor.

*Kept her.* The way you'd say it about a goat. About a letter entrusted to you. About something that belonged to someone else.

"It isn't hunting Coral," she whispered, and the words tasted like ice water. "It's looking for me."

She should have cried. Some part of her registered that — the part that was still twelve, still a kid who slept with a blanket over her head during storms. That part was screaming somewhere far away, muffled, like shouting through a wall. But the rest of her had gone very still and very clear, the way the world had gone clear when the stars vanished, and in that clarity she noticed something she would spend the rest of her life trying to understand:

She wasn't surprised.

Some deep, buried room inside her had heard that voice say her name and had answered, quietly, *yes. Of course. Finally.* As if she'd been waiting twelve years for someone to come collect her. As if some part of her had always known she was borrowed.

The footsteps crossed the parlor. She heard the chair scrape — Coral's chair, the one with the wobbly leg — pushed aside gently, almost politely. Then the voice again, and this time it was speaking to someone else, or to itself, or to the house:

"She is below. Earth and stone. Good instincts, for one so young." A pause, and when it continued there was something almost like amusement folded into the dryness. "Or perhaps not instinct at all. Perhaps she remembers."

*Remembers what?*

"I was an infant," Elodie told herself furiously. "I don't remember anything. I don't remember my mother's face, I don't remember—" 

She stopped.

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. The thing she'd never said out loud to anyone, not even Coral, especially not Coral. Everyone in the valley had a story about before. The miller's daughter remembered her mother humming. The tanner's boy remembered firelight and a dog barking. Even Coral, who never talked about their own past, had fragments — Elodie had caught them over the years, dropped like coins: a barracks, a banner, a name spoken with hatred.

Elodie had nothing. Her life began at four years old, on Coral's doorstep, in the rain, wrapped in a blanket that was too fine for the valley — fine wool, silver-threaded, the kind of thing you'd see on a merchant's wife in the city, if the city still traded with the valley, which it didn't. Coral had said they found her. Abandoned. Left.

And Elodie had believed it because believing it was easier than asking why a mercenary with no family and no land and no reason would take in a stranger's baby and raise her for twelve years without ever once, not once, telling her where she came from.

The drawer opened again upstairs. Shut again.

"You were paid for," the voice said conversationally, as though continuing a thought she couldn't hear the beginning of. "Do you understand? Nothing in this world is given, child. Everything is bought, or owed, or taken. You were bought. Thoroughly. Formally. With witnesses." The footsteps came to the top of the cellar stairs. "And I have come to collect what was purchased."

Elodie's whole body shook now, silent shaking, jaw clenched so hard her teeth ached. Above her, the trapdoor handle turned. Slowly. Testing.

*Coral,* she thought. *Where are you. Where are you.*

And then, faintly, from outside — from beyond the walls, out in the dark where the stars weren't — she heard it. A sound she knew better than her own heartbeat. The low, grinding whistle Coral made when they walked, tuneless, half a breath, the sound Elodie had fallen asleep to through thin walls for twelve years. Coming around the side of the house. Getting closer.

Coming *back*.

The trapdoor handle stopped turning.

For a long moment the house held its breath. Elodie imagined the thing up there going still, its attention sliding sideways, toward the window, toward the yard, toward the small stubborn figure in the dark making that stupid brave whistle.

When the paper-voice spoke again, it was quieter. Almost gentle. Which was somehow the worst thing yet.

"Ah," it said. "The keeper returns. How devoted." A pause, and she could feel it thinking, feel the whole house leaning around its attention like grass around wind. "This complicates nothing. But it does make the evening less tidy."

The footsteps withdrew from the trapdoor. Crossed the kitchen. The front door opened — she heard the latch, the hinge, the night air rushing in, carrying a smell she'd never smelled before, green and metallic and old, the smell of magic finally coming home after a hundred years away.

"The mountain remembers you, Elodie," the voice said from the doorway, and it said her name again in that awful familiar way, like a key fitting a lock. "Sleep well tonight. It will be your last small sleep."

The door closed. The footsteps faded — not walking away, she realized. Walking *toward* the mountain. Toward the place where the ground had shivered. Unhurried. Patient. Things that had waited a hundred years knew how to wait a little longer.

Elodie stayed in the dark a long time after, hands pressed to her mouth, listening to the silence where the wards used to be.

Then the trapdoor above her creaked, and Coral's voice came down through the wood, rough and low and shaking in a way she'd never heard it shake: "Elodie. It's me. It's gone. Come up."

She climbed the ladder on legs that didn't feel like hers. Coral stood in the kitchen holding a lamp and a knife and looking like they'd aged ten years in ten minutes, and the relief on their face when he saw her was so naked and enormous that it broke something loose in her chest.

She meant to run to them. She meant to grab onto them the way she had when she was small, bury her face in their coat, let the whole night be fixed by the fact of them.

Instead she heard herself say, in a voice flat and strange and much older than twelve:

"What did you sell me for?"

Coral went white. The lamp flame leaned between them, toward her, like it was listening too.

"Elodie—"

"There was a contract," she said. "With witnesses. I was *paid for*, Coral. It knew my name before I did." Her hands were fists at her sides and she couldn't remember closing them. "What am I?"

Outside, far to the north, beneath the mountain, something vast exhaled — a long, subterranean sigh that rattled the cups in the cupboard and set the lamp swinging, shadows lurching across Coral's face, across the knife in their hand, across the door they'd need to bar against a night that had only just begun.

* * *

They didn't answer right away. That was the first wound — not the silence itself, but how long it lasted. Long enough for the lamp to stop swinging. Long enough for Elodie to count her own heartbeats and lose track past forty.

Coral set the knife on the table. Deliberately, the way you disarm yourself in front of someone you're afraid of. Then they sat down in the chair with the wobbly leg, and it rocked under them, and for a moment they looked so ordinary — just a tired mercenary in a lamplit kitchen — that Elodie almost let herself be lied to again.

Almost.

"Sit down," Coral said.

"I'll stand."

"Elodie." Their voice cracked on her name, and she hated that it still worked on her, that hearing it in Coral's mouth still felt like home even now, even tonight. "Please."

She sat. Across the table, the way you sit across from a stranger.

Coral rubbed their face with both hands. When they lowered them, they looked older than Elodie had ever seen them look, older than the fever winter, older than the night the bandits came. "I found you on the doorstep," they said. "That part's true. Rain, October, you were maybe two days old. Blanket too fine for this valley. There was no note."

"But."

"But there was a mark." Coral hesitated, then rolled up their left sleeve, and Elodie saw for the first time the brand on their wrist she'd grown up pretending not to notice — the coiled sigil of the god whose chapel stood empty at the valley's heart, the mute god, the silent one, the one all the mercenaries swore to and none of them talked about. Except the mark was wrong tonight. It had always been a dull gray scar, dead as a coin. Now it was raised and red and faintly, horribly, *glowing*.

"It woke up an hour ago," Coral said quietly. "First time in six years. When the stars went out." They turned the wrist slowly in the lamplight. "And when that thing was in the house, it burned so hot I nearly screamed. It's pulling me north. Toward the mountain." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and furious and terrified, and Elodie understood suddenly that whatever else was true, this was true too: Coral had not sold her. Coral looked like a person who had just learned they'd been standing guard over a debt their whole life without knowing it.

"I didn't sell you," Coral said. "But I think I was paid to keep you. And I think whoever paid me wasn't human, and I think the bill has just come due." They laughed, short and bitter, with no humor in it at all. "I swore an oath to a god who never spoke. Turns out maybe it wasn't mute, Elodie. Maybe it was just waiting for me to ask the right question."

Elodie sat very still. Outside, the darkness pressed against the windows like a tide against glass. Somewhere north, the mountain waited, singing a song she could almost — *almost* — hear the melody of, the way you can almost remember a dream.

"We're leaving," Coral said, already standing, already moving, pulling the pack from under the bed, hands steadying themselves with work. "Tonight. South road, past the ward-line, out of the valley before—"

"The wards are down," Elodie said. "There is no out of the valley anymore."

Coral stopped. Looked at her. And in the lamplight she watched the truth land on them the same way it had landed on her in the cellar: the ground cracking open, the old world sliding away beneath their feet.

"No," Coral agreed softly. "I don't suppose there is."

They barred the door anyway. Old habits. Then Coral banked the fire, took down the good knives after all, and began packing like a person racing a clock they couldn't see — while Elodie stood at the window with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the black northern sky where the stars used to be, listening to the mountain sing.

And underneath the fear, underneath the anger, underneath the crack running through everything she'd believed about herself, one thought kept surfacing, quiet and cold and certain, growing louder with every verse of that distant song:

*It called me by my real name. Which means Elodie was never my name at all.*
