## 4. The Mark That Answers

The mark woke at dawn.

Not gently. Not the way it had stirred in the early years, when the god still spoke through it — a warmth like a hand on the wrist, a pressure that might have been attention or might have been nothing at all. This was different. This was a hook set in flesh and hauled.

Coral came off the floor of the ruined kitchen with their knife already out, because twelve years of mercenary work didn't care what woke you; you woke armed. The house was dark. The stars were still gone. Outside the broken window the valley lay under a sky like a lid, and beneath the lid, something breathed.

The mark burned.

It sat on the inside of Coral's left wrist — a coiled line of silvered scar, the shape of a serpent eating its own tail, or a river turning back on itself, depending on who you asked. The god had never explained it. The god had never explained anything. It had pressed its mouth to Coral's wrist nineteen years ago in a chapel that smelled of cold iron and tallow, and it had never spoken since, and Coral had built an entire life inside that silence. A contract without terms. Service without orders. They had told themselves for two decades that this was what faith looked like: showing up anyway.

Now the silence was over, and Coral would have given anything to have it back.

The mark pulled. There was no other word for it. It pulled toward the mountain the way a fish is pulled toward the boat — not invited, *reeled*. Coral braced one hand against the doorframe and felt the tendons of their forearm stand up like bridge cables. The pain wasn't sharp. It was structural, deep, the ache of a thing being used for a purpose it was never consulted about.

"Elodie," they said, and then louder, because the house was big and the girl slept like the dead when she slept at all: "Elodie!"

A thump upstairs. Small feet on boards. Then her voice, thin with sleep and fear she was trying to hide: "I'm here. I'm coming."

"Don't come down. Stay by the window where I can hear you." Coral tested their weight against the pull. It eased when they faced the mountain, tightened when they turned away, which meant whoever — whatever — was doing the pulling wanted them walking north, and Coral had survived this long by making a religion of doing the opposite of what things wanted. "Are you hurt?"

"No." A pause. "Coral, there's a light under the mountain. I can see it from my room. It's like — it's like the mountain swallowed a lamp and it's shining through its teeth."

That was Elodie. Twelve years old and describing the end of the world in images nobody else would find until next week. Coral looked north. She was right. Along the mountain's eastern flank, in the seams between stone, a black light seeped upward — not glow exactly, more like the absence of glow given a place to live. It moved. It pooled and shifted and moved again, slow as honey, patient as debt.

The mark hauled harder.

Coral wrapped a strip of linen around the wrist, then a second strip, then cinched their bracer over both, and the pressure dulled to something a person could think through. Old trick. Vess had taught it to them, years ago, back when they'd both worn the mark and laughed about how the god never gave orders so they'd invented a whole theology of naps. Back before Vess left the valley. Back before everything.

"Listen to me," Coral called up the stairs. "We're leaving today. Pack one bag. Food, water, the flint, your mother's knife if you can find it. Nothing you love that you can't run with."

"You said we'd never leave the valley," Elodie said. "You said the wards—"

"The wards are down."

Silence from above. Then, very quietly: "I know. I felt them go. It was like the house exhaled."

*Like the house exhaled.* Coral closed their eyes for half a second. Somewhere in the last twelve hours their child had developed a vocabulary for catastrophe, and they hadn't been there to see it happen.

"We'll talk on the road," they said. "Move."

They went out the back into a morning that wasn't a morning. No sun rose behind the eastern ridge. The sky held a flat, sourceless grey, the color of the inside of a shell, and the valley wore it like a shroud. The ward-stones along the property line — Coral checked three of them as they circled the house — were cold and dead and split clean through, each crack running top to bottom as if the stones had been quarried rather than broken. Whatever had come through last night hadn't battered the wards. It had unmade them. There was a difference, and the difference frightened Coral more than the black light did.

Magic couldn't live in the valley. Everyone knew that. It was the valley's whole bargain, its whole identity: a bowl of honest dirt where the god's silence kept the strange things out. Farmers' grandparents had crawled here from wars fought with sorcery, and their grandchildren grew wheat and raised children and never once saw a door open onto somewhere else. For sixty years the wards had hummed a note too low to hear, and everyone had slept.

Last night the note stopped. And now magic was leaking back into the world like water into a hull, and Coral could feel it — actually feel it, in the mark, in the teeth, in the way the grass near the dead ward-stones had grown four inches overnight and leaned all in one direction, toward the mountain, like a congregation.

"Coral." Elodie stood in the doorway with a bag clutched to her chest, already dressed, already pale and steady. Her eyes went to Coral's wrist. To the bracer. "It's awake, isn't it. Your mark."

"How do you know that?"

"Because you're standing like someone's holding your arm up on a string." She set her jaw. "Is it taking you to the mountain?"

"It's trying."

"Then don't let it." She said it simply, the way children say true things. "Nothing good ever comes out of that mountain. You told me that myself. You said the only reason people don't dig into it is that the digging remembers."

"I did say that." Coral shouldered their own pack — knife, rope, dried meat, the small hoard of coin that was supposed to buy a quiet life someday. "Which is why we're going south. Away from it. As far and as fast as we can."

The mark seized.

Coral dropped to one knee in the yard. The pain went through the linen and the leather like they weren't there, a white bar of it from wrist to elbow, and for one lurching moment Coral saw — not with eyes, but with the mark — the mountain from underneath. Corridors of wet stone. A door tall as a cathedral. Something on the other side of the door turning its face toward them with the unhurried interest of a creditor.

Then it passed. Coral knelt in the frost-browned grass, breathing like a bellows, and Elodie was beside them with her hands hovering, not touching, because she knew better than anyone when Coral needed not to be touched.

"It showed me the door," Coral rasped.

"What door?"

"The one I'm going to spend the rest of my life pretending I didn't see. Up. We move. South."

They made two miles before the rider caught them.

* * *

Coral heard the horse first — a single set of hooves at a canter, unhidden, which in a valley full of panicking refugees meant either a fool or a friend. Then the whistle. Three notes, low-high-low, the old patrol signal, and Coral's body reacted before their mind did: relief first, hot and stupid, then suspicion climbing right behind it like a second pulse.

Nobody whistled the patrol signal anymore. The patrols were done. The valley was done.

The rider crested the field's far hedge and Coral's hand went to their knife and then stopped, because the rider was broad-shouldered and rode like a sack of laundry and swore fluently at the hedge the entire time she crossed it, and there was only one person in the world who fit that description.

"Vess," Coral said.

Vess Ondrey slid off the horse badly, landed worse, and stood there grinning at Coral across forty feet of dead ward-line with a face that was older than Coral remembered and thinner, marked along the jaw with the same silvered coil that Coral wore — except Vess's mark was black. Dead-looking. A scar where Coral's was a brand.

"You look terrible," Vess said.

"You look like you've been riding for days."

"Three. Through country that doesn't have roads anymore, because the roads—" She stopped. Her grin fell off her face like something dropped. Her eyes found Elodie, standing just behind Coral's shoulder, and something moved through Vess's expression too fast for Coral to name it. Grief, maybe. Or guilt wearing grief's coat. "She got big."

"She got twelve." Coral didn't move. Didn't soften. "You wrote twice in six years, Vess. Both times to ask if the wards were holding."

"And both times you wrote back one word." Vess walked her horse forward, tied it to a fencepost that had once bordered a neighbor's field and now bordered nothing. "Yes. So forgive me if my correspondence suffered. I've been busy learning why the wards wouldn't hold forever."

"Then learn fast and tell me, because last night every star in the sky went out, our border stones cracked like eggs, and my mark—" Coral pushed back the bracer, unwound the linen, and showed her. The silvered coil blazed. It had been blazing for hours, and up close Vess could not possibly mistake it for health.

Vess looked at it for a long time. When she spoke again, all the banter had drained out of her voice, and what was left was worse: flatness. The tone soldiers use for casualty reports.

"When did it start pulling?"

"Before dawn. Toward the mountain. Like a hook."

"Has it spoken? Words, dreams, anything?"

"Nothing. It hasn't spoken in nineteen years. Why would it start now?"

"Because it can't." Vess untied her pack, dug into it with the urgency of a woman who had rehearsed this speech across three hundred miles and hated every draft. "Coral. Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit *down*." And there it was — the old command voice, the sergeant voice, the one Vess had used in the passes during the border war when the fog-things came down the slopes and half the company froze and half ran and Vess's voice was the only fixed point in the world. Coral sat. It was humiliating and comforting in equal measure, and Vess knew it, and crouched in front of them so their eyes were level.

"Six months ago," Vess said, "I was stationed at the High Chapel. You remember the High Chapel."

"Where the god spoke to the founders. Nobody's heard it speak in living memory."

"That's what we thought. But the chapel keeps records, and the records keep dates, and I started noticing the dates didn't add up. The tithe ledgers. One soul per generation, entered as paid, year after year, generation after generation — but the entries stop being signed. Three hundred years ago the god's own seal stops appearing on the receipts. Do you understand? The payments kept getting *collected*, Coral. Somebody just stopped keeping track of who was collecting them."

The mark pulsed. Coral pressed it flat against their knee. "Vess. Say the thing you rode three days to say."

Vess's jaw worked. For a moment Coral watched her try to find a gentler version, and then watched her give up, because Vess had never in her life found a gentler version of anything.

"The god didn't go silent," she said. "The god defaulted. On its own bargain — the original one, the founding debt, whatever it owed and to whom, we don't know, the records are torn out, deliberately, pages razored away. And when it defaulted, the thing it owed came to collect. And it collected *everything*. The god isn't silent, Coral. The god is *eaten*. Has been for centuries. Every prayer you ever sent into that silence landed in the belly of whatever swallowed it. Every year of service, every ward you fed your strength into, every night you knelt in that chapel talking to something that loved you—" She stopped herself. Swallowed. "It never heard you. It was never there to hear you."

The field was very quiet. Somewhere behind them Elodie had gone still, and Coral was distantly aware of her listening, and distantly aware that he should send her away, and entirely unable to move.

"That's not true," Coral said.

"Coral—"

"The mark is *burning*, Vess. Right now. If the god is dead, what is this?" Coral thrust the wrist forward, the silvered coil blazing between them like a small captive star. "Dead gods don't pull. Dead gods don't want. Whatever's on the other end of this—"

"Is whatever ate it." Vess took Coral's wrist in both hands, gently, the way you handle a wound. Her thumb hovered over the burning mark without touching it. "This isn't a summons from your god. It's a claim. You're not being called home, old friend. You're being called *due*. Your oath didn't die with the god — it transferred. Everything the god owed, everything the god owned, everything the god had sworn to it — it all rolled over to the new holder. Including you." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "Including, I think, the newest entry in the ledger. The payment that came due this generation. The one sold before she could walk."

Cold moved through Coral that had nothing to do with the grey lightless morning.

They did not look at Elodie. They made themselves not look at Elodie, and knew, from the quality of the silence behind them, that Elodie heard every word.

"There's no ledger," Coral said. "There's no payment. There's a valley and a dead god and a girl I raised, and you're going to tell me exactly what you mean, Vess, or so help me—"

"The mountain's singing," Vess said quietly. "Has she started hearing it yet?"

And behind Coral, small and clear, Elodie answered for herself:

*"How did you know that?"*

* * *

They stood in a triangle in the dead grass, and the triangle was the whole world.

Elodie had come forward while Coral wasn't watching — of course she had, she always did, she moved like weather — and now she stood an arm's length away with her bag still clutched to her chest and her chin up and her eyes fixed on Vess with a ferocity that Coral recognized with a sick lurch, because it was Coral's own ferocity, learned at close range over twelve years.

"Start from the beginning," Coral said. Their voice came out level. They were proud of that, later. "All of it. Leave nothing out because you think I can't stand it. I've stood worse. I've stood you."

Vess almost smiled at that. Almost. Then she looked at Elodie, and whatever she saw in the girl's face made her choose, visibly, to stop lying by omission.

"Every generation," she said, "the valley pays. One soul, born inside the wards, given to the court beneath the mountain within the first year of life. In exchange, the valley gets its silence. Its safety. Sixty years of wheat and children and no doors opening onto anywhere else. That's the bargain. That's what the god administered, back when there was a god — it brokered the deal, kept the ledger, made the deliveries. And when it died, the deliveries didn't stop. Because the court doesn't need a god to collect. It just needs someone inside the valley willing to carry the child to the border."

"Stop," Coral said.

"Midwife's records, Coral. Chapel records. I cross-checked them for six months. Thirteen generations. Twelve souls delivered." Vess's voice cracked, just once, and she drove through it. "One outstanding."

The mark chose that moment to haul again — hard, sudden, a fisherman setting the hook — and Coral staggered, and Elodie caught their arm, twelve years old and steadying her parent in a dead field at the end of the world, and over Coral's bowed head she stared at Vess with tears standing in her eyes and her voice absolutely level, absolutely Coral's:

"Who carried me to the border?"

Vess didn't answer.

"Who *carried me*?" Elodie's voice went up and broke. "I was born here. You said — Coral always said — you said my mother left me at the chapel steps, you said—"

"Elodie." Coral straightened. Took her hand. Found that their own was shaking and let her see it, because the girl deserved one honest thing today. "Look at me. Not at her. Me. Whatever happened before you were old enough to remember — whoever did what — it was not you, and it will never be about what you're worth. Do you hear me? Debts are the debtor's shame. Never the collateral's."

"But—"

"*Never.*" And Coral turned to Vess, and the mark burned, and the black light seeped up the mountain's flank behind them all, and Coral said the hardest sentence of their life in the flattest voice they owned: "Tell me it wasn't you."

Vess stood very still. Her horse shifted at the fencepost. The grey sky pressed down like a hand.

"Coral," she said finally, "we need to get off this field. All three of us. Because I only know half of what's hunting us — but I know the other half is already walking, and it knows your name, and it knows hers, and it does not get tired."

And far to the north, beneath the mountain, something turned over in its sleep — and the mark on Coral's wrist flared white-hot, and for one instant, one clear ringing instant, Coral heard it speak.

Not words. Just a sound. The sound of a page turning in a very large book, and a dry voice, patient as dust, saying what sounded terribly like:

*Thirteen.*
