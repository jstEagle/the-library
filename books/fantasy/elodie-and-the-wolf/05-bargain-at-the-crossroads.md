## 5. Bargain at the Crossroads

The ward-line was a scar you could walk along, if you knew where to look. Coral had walked it a thousand times in twelve years of patrols — a seam of pale stone running the length of the valley floor, half-buried in heather, humming so faintly that most people mistook it for their own blood in their ears. Tonight there was no hum. Tonight the stones were just stones, and the silence where the song had been was louder than anything Coral had ever heard.

They kept Elodie behind them anyway. Twelve years of habit did not unlearn itself in one night.

"Stay on my left," Coral said. "If anything moves, you run back toward the house and you don't wait for me."

"You said that already."

"I'll say it again."

Elodie didn't argue. That was how Coral knew she was frightened. The girl argued about everything — about porridge, about practicing her letters, about whether it was fair that Coral got to carry a knife and she didn't. She had argued her way through the whole flight from the house, through the dark fields where magic now moved like weather, through the hedgerow that had reached for them with branches like fingers. She'd only gone quiet when the ground beneath the mountain had shuddered and something vast had turned over in its sleep.

Now she walked close enough that Coral could feel her breath on their shoulder blade, and said nothing at all.

The crossroads came up out of the dark like a wound. It was an old place — older than the valley's founding, older than the wards, a crossing of two roads that predated every map Coral had ever seen. Four worn tracks met at a ring of standing stones, and in the center of the ring stood a waymarker, a leaning pillar carved with faces so eroded by wind that they were more suggestion than feature. Travelers used to leave offerings there. Bread. Buttons. A copper coin pressed into a crack. Nobody remembered what for.

The valley's people called it a lucky place. Coral's god had called it nothing at all, because the god had never spoken, not once, not in all the years of Coral's service. But the mark on Coral's wrist — the coiled serpent-and-star that had been silent as its master since the day it was inked into their skin — had never once reacted to this place.

Tonight it was burning.

Not the dull ache of the last few hours. Burning. The mark had woken with the falling of the stars, and it pulled at Coral like a hook set behind the navel, dragging them toward the mountain with every step. Coral had spent the last mile fighting it, boots skidding on frost-slick grass, one hand fisted in Elodie's collar to keep her from being dragged ahead of them both.

"It hurts," Elodie said quietly. "Your arm. I can see it from here. It's glowing."

"I know."

"What is it doing?"

Coral looked down at their wrist. Under the skin, the mark moved. Not pulsed — *moved*, the serpent-shape sliding slowly around the star, patient as a thing that had all the time in the world. "I don't know," they said, which was the truth, and the worst kind of truth, because Coral Reyes did not say those words lightly. "But I'm going to find out."

* * *

The figure was already waiting at the waymarker when they reached the stone ring.

It stood very still, in the manner of things that do not need to breathe, wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of wet slate. Its face was a mask of pale porcelain, smooth and featureless except for two slits where eyes should be, and behind those slits there was no light at all. No lantern hung from its belt. No shadow pooled beneath its feet, though the moon — the only star left in the sky, the last small light the night had not swallowed — threw shadows from everything else. From the stones. From Elodie. From Coral.

From the figure, nothing.

Coral stopped at the edge of the ring and put out an arm. "Behind me."

"Coral—"

"*Behind me.*"

Elodie went. Coral heard her feet scuffing back over frozen grass, heard her breath going quick and shallow, and hated — briefly, furiously — that this was the second time tonight she'd had to be afraid of the dark, and that Coral had been the one who taught her the dark was survivable, and that none of those lessons seemed worth anything now.

"You're a long way from anywhere," Coral said to the figure. Their voice came out level. They were proud of that. "This road doesn't go anywhere anyone wants to be tonight."

The mask turned toward them. There was a pause, long enough that Coral began to wonder if the thing could speak at all, and then it said, in a voice like dry paper folding:

"The road goes wherever it is paid to go."

"Then we'll pay it ourselves."

"With what?" The mask tilted. "You have no coin that matters anymore, mercenary. Your god is dead. Your valley is open. Your wards are cut rope on the ground." It raised one gloved hand, unhurried, and gestured at the mark on Coral's wrist. "And your master's leash is pulling you toward the mountain whether you walk or crawl. You have perhaps a day before it drags you there on your knees. Two, if you fight it. You cannot fight it. I have watched men try."

Coral's jaw tightened. Everything the thing said landed like a stone dropped into still water, ripples spreading outward through every certainty Coral had built their life on. Dead god. Open valley. Leash. They had spent the whole flight refusing to think any of it through, and here was a stranger in a porcelain mask laying it all out in three sentences like a merchant displaying damaged goods.

"Who are you?"

"A broker." The word came out smooth, practiced, almost gentle. "Of roads. Of passages. Of certain arrangements between parties who find themselves... inconvenienced."

"Inconvenienced," Coral repeated flatly.

"The valley has been closed for a hundred years," the broker said. "Magic does not enter. Magic does not leave. Nothing crosses the ward-line but what the dead god permitted, and the dead god permitted almost nothing. Now the god is gone, and the wards are gone, and the mountain below us is waking up hungry after a century of fasting." The mask turned, slowly, toward Elodie. "And you are standing in the open with a child, on a night when everything that hunts does so freely, and the nearest safe shelter is four days' travel through country that will eat you."

Coral's hand found their knife. "Don't look at her."

"I am appraising," the broker said mildly. "It is what I do. And my appraisal says: you cannot outrun what is coming. You cannot hide from what is coming. You cannot kill what is coming. But I—" the gloved hand spread, magnanimous, "—can move you through the dark country unseen. Past the things that hunt by scent and sound. Past the faithful who will want answers about why the stars fell. Past the mountain's outer hunger, which is already tasting the air of your valley. I can put you somewhere the court below cannot reach. For a price."

"There's always a price."

"There is always a price," the broker agreed, and something in the tone made it sound less like a threat than a comfort. "Mine is simple. A promise. Signed and sealed. Passage and protection, in exchange for a promise to be redeemed at a time and place of my choosing."

"What kind of promise?"

"That," the broker said, "is the nature of a promise. If you knew what it would cost when you signed, it would be a purchase, not a promise."

The words settled over Coral like cold water. Somewhere behind them, Elodie shifted her weight, and the small sound of it went through Coral like a blade. Because here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: the thing in the dark behind them, the leash on their wrist, the four days of hunting country between here and anywhere safe, a twelve-year-old girl who had already lost everything once tonight and didn't know yet how much worse it would get. Against that: a blank check. A signature on a line whose other end was held by a faceless thing that cast no shadow.

Every instinct Coral had earned in twenty years of soldiering said *walk away*. Every one of those instincts had been trained into them by a god that was, according to this creature, dead. And the mark on their wrist burned and burned, and the mountain breathed beneath them, and Elodie was cold and frightened and looking at Coral like Coral was the last solid thing in a world come loose.

"Passage and protection," Coral said. "For both of us. Until we're somewhere safe. That's the deal. Both of us, or nothing."

The broker was quiet for a moment. Then: "Both. Agreed."

"And the promise comes due when? Where?"

"When and where I choose. After the debt is discharged, the promise redeems itself, and you are free of me forever." The broker drew something from inside its cloak — a strip of vellum, pale as the mask, and a stylus of black metal that drank the moonlight rather than reflecting it. "One name. One mark. That is all."

Coral took the vellum. It was warm. That was somehow worse than if it had been cold.

The contract was short. Shorter than any contract Coral had ever signed in their life — shorter than a mercenary's oath, shorter than a bill of sale. Most of it was written in a script that slid away from the eye, characters that refused to hold their shape, and at the bottom, above a single line, was a space for a name and a space for a mark. Above the line, in letters that did not slide, were six words:

*PROMISE TO BE REDEEMED LATER.*

"That's not terms," Coral said. "That's not anything. What am I actually agreeing to?"

"To be agreed to," the broker said. "Sign, and the details will become clear in time. Refuse, and the details will become clear much sooner, and much less gently, and without my protection." The mask tilted again, and though it had no eyes, Coral felt it looking past them, at the girl. "Time is short, mercenary. The mountain is waking. And the little one is tired."

*Love used as leverage.* The thought arrived whole, cold, and clear, the way the worst thoughts do. *It knows exactly what it's holding over me. It's known since it saw us coming.*

Coral looked down at the vellum. Looked at the six words. Looked at the space where their name would go, and thought about what a name meant, in a world where gods could be devoured and children sold and wards cut like rope. A name was a handle. A name was a hook. Everyone who had ever wanted to own a piece of Coral Reyes had started by learning their name, and Coral had spent twenty years giving theirs out carefully, sparingly, to oaths and employers and one silent god who had never once asked for anything until the asking started.

And now this.

"Coral." Elodie's voice, small, from behind. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing," Coral said, and lied to her for the first time in twelve years, and hated it. "Nothing, sweetheart. Just business."

They signed.

The stylus was heavier than it looked, and cold, and when the black metal touched the vellum it bit — not Coral's fingers, but something deeper, some thread that ran down through the wrist-mark and further, into places Coral hadn't known a person could be touched from. The name wrote itself in Coral's own handwriting, because of course it did; the stylus knew it already. And when the last letter closed, the vellum drank the ink, and the six words above the line glowed once, softly, like embers breathing, and went dark.

"Done," the broker said, and folded the contract away into its cloak, where it vanished without a wrinkle. "Passage and protection, for you and the child, until redemption. Keep walking, keep close, and do not stray from the roads I choose. Break faith with me, and the promise redeems early." A pause. "You would not enjoy the early redemption."

"I figured."

"One more thing." The broker extended the black stylus, handle-first, toward Elodie. "The seal requires a witness."

"She's a child."

"The seal requires a witness," the broker repeated, with the patience of a thing reciting law. "A touch. Nothing more. Her hand on the stylus while you hold it. It makes the promise *true*, mercenary. Unbroken promises are the only currency the dark country respects. Would you rather your word be worthless here?"

*No,* Coral thought. *No, no, no.* Every part of them screamed it. But the stylus was already in their hand, and the broker's glove waited, and Elodie was stepping forward before Coral could stop her — because she was brave, gods help them both, she was brave the way Coral had tried to teach her to be, and bravery in a twelve-year-old looks exactly like walking toward danger because someone you trust is standing in it.

Her small fingers closed over the stylus under Coral's hand.

Cold. That was all. Just cold, and a faint vibration like a struck bell fading, and then the broker withdrew the stylus, and it was done, and Coral's wrist-mark had gone quiet for the first time since the stars fell — not healed, not freed, but *held*, like a dog called to heel by a new hand.

"There," the broker said, and the paper voice sounded, for the first time, satisfied. "Now we are all bound together. How family-like."

* * *

They walked.

The broker led them off the crossroads onto a track Coral had never seen despite twenty years of patrolling this valley — a narrow path that hadn't existed yesterday, winding north through the heather, toward the pass. The moon rode low and red on the horizon, the last light in a starless sky, and the dark country beyond the ward-line opened up ahead of them like a mouth.

Elodie walked pressed against Coral's side. For a long time neither of them spoke. The frost crunched. Somewhere far behind them, the mountain exhaled, a long slow rumble that Coral felt through the soles of their boots.

"Coral?" Elodie said finally. Very quietly. "That thing. The broker. When it looked at me—" She stopped. Started again. "It looked at me like it knew me."

Coral's stomach dropped. "It looks at everyone like that."

"No." Elodie shook her head, and her voice went smaller. "It looked at me like it was checking. Like it was counting. Like—" She hesitated on the edge of whatever she was going to say next, and then stepped back from it, the way she'd learned to step back from saying things that got adults upset. "Never mind. I'm tired."

Coral's hand found her shoulder and squeezed. "When we're somewhere safe, you sleep. Real sleep. In a real bed. I swear it."

"You swore the same thing about breakfast," Elodie said, and there — there was a ghost of her, the argumentative, stubborn, impossible girl Coral had raised, surfacing through the fear for one second like a fish rising in dark water. "And we haven't eaten since yesterday."

"We'll fix that too."

Ahead of them, the broker walked without turning, without slowing, casting no shadow in the red moonlight. And Coral fell into step behind it with Elodie's hand in theirs, and told themselves the tightness in their chest was just the cold, and did not look too closely at the mark on their wrist, which had gone quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

Somewhere behind them, the mountain sang one low note to itself, like a thing tuning an instrument.

Somewhere ahead, in the dark country, something heard it and began to sing back.
