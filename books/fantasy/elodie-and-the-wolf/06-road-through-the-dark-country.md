## 6. Road Through the Dark Country

### CORAL

The ward-line was a scar you could walk along for miles — a seam of dead earth where nothing green would grow and nothing warm would stay. Coral kept to it because it was the only direction that made sense. Behind them lay the valley, empty now of stars and full of whatever had come up out of the mountain's roots. Ahead lay the dark country, which everyone in the valley had always described the way you describe the far side of death: reverently, briefly, and without any real expectation of going there.

Elodie walked between them, one small hand hooked into the strap of Coral's pack like she'd done since she was four years old and afraid of market crowds. She hadn't spoken in an hour.

Vess walked point, sword loose in its scabbard, moving with the easy rolling gait of someone who'd spent thirty years expecting ambush. She'd been quiet too. That was unlike her. Vess filled silence the way other people filled rooms — complaints, jokes, tactical observations nobody asked for. Her silence had a texture to it, and the texture was guilt.

"Talk," Coral said finally.

"About what?"

"About why you knew the wards were going to fall before they fell. About why you came to my door at midnight instead of dawn. About why you keep looking at Elodie when you think I'm not watching."

Vess's jaw worked. Ahead of them the road bent around a hillside of black stone, and beyond the bend there was light — not daylight, nothing so kind, but a smudge of orange against the unbroken dark, like a coal buried in ash.

"Not here," Vess said.

"You've been saying *not here* since the valley."

"And I'll keep saying it until there's a wall at my back and a fire between us and you." Vess glanced back at Elodie, softened by half a degree. "Some things shouldn't be said on open ground."

Coral let it go, because ahead of them the light resolved into something worth being careful about: a waystation, or what passed for one out here. A long low building of stacked stone with a roof of overlapping slate, windows glowing, smoke rising from a chimney into air so still the smoke just hung there, refusing to rise further, as if even it wasn't sure this world was real.

A sign hung over the door on chains. The letters had been painted and repainted so many times the wood beneath was barely holding:

**THE LAST LAMP — ALL TRAVELERS FED — NO QUESTIONS ASKED OR ANSWERED**

"Charming," Vess said.

"It's the first roof we've seen in six hours," Coral said. "We're taking it."

They were ten paces from the door when it opened and a woman stepped out to meet them. She was tall, gray-haired, dressed in the layered wool of someone who lived outdoors, and she moved like a fighter — weight forward, hands visible, eyes doing a fast professional count of their weapons before settling on Coral's face.

"You're from inside," she said. Not a question. "From the valley."

"How can you tell?"

"Because you keep looking up." She nodded at the starless sky. "People born under the dark stop checking. People who lost their stars never stop."

Coral realized she was right. They'd been glancing overhead every few minutes all night, some animal part of them convinced the sky might remember its job if watched closely enough.

"We're passing through," Coral said. "Toward the mountain."

Something flickered across the woman's face — pity, maybe, or its colder cousin, recognition. "Everyone passes through toward the mountain eventually. Come in. There's stew. There are beds if you have coin, floor space if you don't. And there's a rule." She held up one finger. "You hear singing tonight, you don't follow it. You don't hum along. You don't ask anyone else if they hear it too. Understood?"

Coral felt Elodie's grip tighten on the pack strap.

"Why?" Elodie asked. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour, and her voice came out smaller than usual.

The woman looked down at her, and her expression did something complicated — the look of someone seeing a debt come due on a face too young to owe it.

"Because the mountain doesn't sing for everyone," she said. "And the ones it sings for end up walking."

* * *

### ELODIE

The stew was good. That was the strange part. Elodie had expected the dark country to taste like ash and fear, but the stew was rich with barley and root vegetables and some smoked meat she didn't ask about, and the fire in the common room cracked and popped exactly like the fire at home, and for about twenty minutes she could almost pretend this was an adventure instead of the night her whole life broke open.

She sat wedged between Coral and the wall, which was where she always sat, which was where she was safest, except the woman at the door had said *the ones it sings for end up walking*, and ever since then Elodie had been aware of a sound at the edge of her hearing. Like a voice through a wall. Like someone humming in another room of a very large house.

She didn't tell Coral. She didn't know why she didn't tell Coral. She only knew that when she thought about saying it — *I hear something, Papa, I hear singing* — the words turned heavy in her mouth, and underneath the heaviness was a feeling she had no name for yet, a feeling like the song wanted to be a secret, like keeping it was already a kind of obedience.

Around the fire, the other travelers ate in silence. There were perhaps fifteen of them, and they were wrong in a way Elodie couldn't immediately name. Then she could. They didn't talk to each other. Not the way tired people sit quiet together — this was different. They sat apart even when they were close, each one sealed inside themselves like jars, and when one of them spoke it was slow and careful, as if words cost something.

An old man near the fire noticed her looking. He smiled, and the smile was kind, and it made everything worse.

"You're wondering what's missing," he said.

"I'm not—" Elodie started, then stopped, because lying to old men by fires seemed rude. "What is missing?"

"Bits." He tapped his temple. "We paid. To get through the dark country alive, you pay the tolls, and the tolls aren't coin. First toll is small — the name of your first dog, say, or how to whistle. Second toll is bigger. By the third crossing, most folk have traded away the middle of themselves. I gave up my wedding day." He said it without sadness, which was the most frightening thing Elodie had heard all night. "I know I had one. I know there was a woman. I kept the knowing and sold the day itself. Some mornings I reach for it and it's like reaching into a drawer that's been emptied. Just smooth wood."

"That's horrible," Elodie said.

"It's a price," the old man said. "Everything out here has one. The question isn't whether you'll pay. It's what you'll choose to lose." His eyes went past her, to Coral, and something in his face shifted — recognition, or the memory of recognition. "Your guardian's marked. Old mark. Deep one. You should know that marks like that draw attention in the dark country. Things notice a sworn soul the way moths notice a lamp."

"What things?"

He opened his mouth to answer, and then his face went blank — not confused, *blank*, like a page wiped clean — and after a moment he frowned slightly, apologized for losing his thread, and returned to his stew as though the conversation had never happened.

Elodie sat very still.

And beneath the crackle of the fire, beneath the wind worrying the eaves, beneath everything, the song got a little louder. It wasn't words. It was older than words — a rising and falling like breathing, like tide, like something enormous asleep and dreaming in a rhythm, and the rhythm fit somewhere inside her chest the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

*Come and see*, the song didn't say, because it didn't use saying. But she understood it anyway. *Come and see what you were. Come and see what was taken. We kept it safe for you. We kept everything.*

Her eyes stung. She blinked hard and stared into the fire and did not hum along.

Across the room, Coral was watching her. Coral always watched her — twelve years of watching, bandaged knees and fevered nights and every scraped secret — and Elodie saw the exact moment the watching sharpened into worry, saw Coral read something off her face that she hadn't meant to show.

She made herself smile. It was the hardest thing she'd done in her life, harder than running, harder than hiding in the root cellar while something called her name through the floorboards.

Coral's frown deepened. But the woman who ran the waystation came over then with bread, and the moment broke, and the song sang on, patient as stone, certain as winter.

*Soon*, it said, without saying it. *You were always ours. Soon.*

* * *

### CORAL

Coral waited until Elodie was actually asleep — truly asleep, breath slow, fists curled under her chin the way she'd slept since infancy — before pulling Vess outside onto the waystation's lean-to porch. The cold out there was total, a cold with no weather in it, no wind, no moisture, just absence shaped like temperature. Above them the sky was a solid lid of black. Coral's eyes kept climbing to it anyway. Lost-star habit.

"Now," Coral said. "Walls at your back. Fire between us and her. Talk."

Vess leaned on the rail. For a long moment she said nothing, and Coral let her, because twenty years of fighting beside someone teaches you which silences to break and which to wait out.

"The god didn't go silent," Vess said at last. "Twelve years ago."

The words landed like ice water. "That's impossible. The mark—"

"The mark went quiet, yes. We all felt it. The stillness, the flatness, the sense of something turned away. We were told it was the valley. The wards. Magic can't cross the line, so the god's voice couldn't reach us either. That's what we were told." Vess's knuckles were white on the rail. "It's what I told you. It's what the elders told everyone. But I was on the border company, Coral. I saw the ledgers. Once a generation, a child of the valley is entered into the record and never entered again. Thirteen times now. Thirteen names. And every time, the year after, the harvests come in fat and the fevers pass us by and the god goes quiet for a season — like something feeding."

Coral's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "Thirteen children."

"Thirteen payments. That's what the ledger calls them. Payments." Vess turned, and her face in the lamplight spilling through the window was terrible with honesty. "And twelve years ago, when the thirteenth payment was due, the elders couldn't find a family desperate enough. So they bought one. Or made one. A foundling, newborn, no kin to raise hell. Sold to the court below the mountain for the valley's safety, and the god stood surety on the bargain, and the god—" Vess's voice caught. "The god defaulted, Coral. Or was made to default. Either way, something ate it. Something's been wearing its silence ever since, and the court's been waiting to collect, and the collection date was last night."

The porch tilted. Or Coral did. They gripped the rail.

"Say her name," Coral said quietly. "Say whose child."

"You know whose child."

"*Say it.*"

"Elodie." Vess said it like setting down something heavy. "Elodie was the thirteenth payment. And whoever sold her — the broker, the elders, I don't know the whole chain — the sale was registered under your household. Your name, Coral. Because you were the one who took her in. On paper, in whatever law governs that place, she was sold *from you*. Which means the debt runs through you. Which means when the wards fell, the court didn't send something to hunt a mercenary who happened to be in the way." Vess met their eyes. "It sent something to collect what was already owed."

Coral stood very still. Inside the waystation, through the window, they could see Elodie asleep by the fire, small and breathing, and the sight of her hit differently now — not just the child they'd raised but a line in a ledger, a coin in a vault, a payment twelve years deferred and now due with interest.

"They'll have to go through me," Coral said.

"Coral—"

"They will *have to go through me.*"

"I know." Vess's voice was rough. "I know that's what you'll do. I've known you twenty years; I could have written this speech for you. That's why I'm telling you the rest." She hesitated. "There's a broker works the fallen ward-lines. Masked. Buys promises from desperate people and sells passage to the mountain. Everyone out here knows the shape of the deal: protection on the road in exchange for a signature. Nobody knows what the signature says, because nobody who signs comes back to tell. If you're set on this road — and you are, I can see it, it's written on you like the mark is — then you'll meet that broker within a day. And you'll want to sign, because you'll be tired and scared and Elodie will be hungry, and the broker will be *kind*, Coral. That's the trap. It's kind."

"Then what do I do?"

"I don't know." Vess laughed, short and bitter. "Twenty years a soldier of a dead god and I don't know. But I know this: out here, a promise is a physical thing. It has weight. It can be owned. So whatever you sign — read it. Read it twice. And if you can't read it, don't sign it, no matter what it costs, because the things out here count better than we do, and they never round in your favor."

From inside the waystation came a sound — small, muffled, half-swallowed by walls. Singing. A child's voice, humming something tuneless and sweet, the way children hum in their sleep.

Coral was through the door before the second note.

* * *

### ELODIE

She woke inside the song.

That was the only way to describe it — not hearing it anymore but being *in* it, the way you're inside a dream without noticing the door you came through. She was standing in the common room, barefoot on cold flagstones, facing the banked fire, and her own mouth was open and the humming was coming out of it, and the melody was not hers but it fit her throat perfectly, the way a well-fitted coat fits.

Behind her, footsteps, fast. Arms around her, lifting, turning — Coral's face above hers, pale as bone in the ember-light.

"Elodie. Hey. Hey, look at me."

She looked. It was hard to look away from the fire. The fire was where the song pooled.

"You were humming," Coral said. "In your sleep. Then you got up."

"Was I?" Her voice sounded far away to herself. "I don't remember getting up."

Coral crouched to her level, hands on her shoulders, firm and warm and shaking very slightly — shaking, Elodie realized, with fear, and the realization frightened her more than the song did, because Coral was never afraid, Coral was the person fear happened *around*.

"Papa, I'm fine. It's just a dream."

"It's not a dream." Coral's eyes searched hers. "Is it? Tell me the truth. What do you hear?"

And there it was again — the heaviness in her mouth, the words turning to stones. The song curled around the truth and held it shut like a hand. *Ours*, it murmured. *Keep our secrets. Keep our song. They wouldn't understand. They'd only be sad.*

"I hear the fire," Elodie said. "And the wind. That's all."

For a moment — a long, terrible moment — Coral just looked at her, and Elodie understood with sick clarity that Coral didn't believe her, and that Coral was choosing, right there in the ember-light, to accept the lie because the truth was a door neither of them could afford to open on a dark road in a dark country with no stars.

"Okay," Coral said softly. "Bed. My bedroll. Right next to me."

"Yes, Papa."

She went. She lay down. Coral lay beside her, one arm over her like a bar across a gate, and Vess sat up against the wall with her sword across her knees, watching the windows.

And in the space between sleeping and waking, in the warm dark behind her closed eyes, the song sang on — gentle now, almost tender, a lullaby with teeth hidden somewhere far beneath it.

*Sleep*, it sang. *Grow strong. Walk well. Every step brings you home.*

And far away, beneath the earth, beneath the roots of the world, something vast turned over in its sleep — and smiled, if mountains can be said to smile, and began, patiently, to count the days.

Outside the waystation's darkened windows, on the road they'd have to take in the morning, a figure stood at the edge of the lamplight. Tall. Still. Wearing a mask that caught the glow and gave nothing back.

Waiting, with the courtesy of one who knows exactly when the debtors will come to him.
