## 7. The Inn of Small Mercies

The inn had no name on its sign, only a symbol Vess recognized and wished she didn't: a hand with the fingers closed, thumb tucked in. The old sign for *nothing watches you here*. She'd seen it twice before in her life, both times in places where the air felt thick enough to chew.

She left her horse with a boy who didn't ask for coin, which was the first wrong thing. Nothing in the dark country was free. Everything just hadn't named its price yet.

Inside, the common room was low-ceilinged and lit by lamps that burned without oil — small mercies, someone had called them once, and the name had stuck like a burr. A dozen people sat at scattered tables, and Vess read them the way she read any room: threat first, story second. A woman with ash-grey hands turning a cup she never drank from. Two brothers who shared a single shadow that moved a half-second behind them both. An old man whose eyes were the flat grey of river stones, watching the door without seeming to watch it.

Servants of dead gods. Every one of them. You could always tell, once you knew to look. They had the look of people whose leash had been cut but whose neck still remembered the weight.

Vess's own god had died eleven years ago, quietly, in the middle of a blessing. One moment the warmth of it in her chest; the next, nothing, like a hearth going out while you slept. She'd spent years telling herself he'd merely gone silent, gone elsewhere, gone deep. It was easier than the truth.

The truth had found her three days ago, in the mouth of a dying priest on the far side of the pass: *They didn't go silent, girl. They were eaten. All of them. And the thing that ate them is collecting.*

So she'd ridden hard, following the trail of a friend she'd betrayed so thoroughly it looked like love, toward an inn where the godless gathered, because if anyone knew how to survive what was coming, it would be the people already orphaned by it.

The keeper was a broad woman with a face like a closed book and a bar towel over one shoulder. "Room?"

"Information," Vess said.

"Different price."

"I assumed."

The keeper studied her, then jerked her chin toward a table near the back, half in shadow. "Sit. I'll bring stew. Eat it or don't."

Vess sat. The old man with stone eyes drifted over uninvited and settled across from her like fog rolling in. Up close she could see the faded brand at his throat — a circle with a line through it. The Quiet Hand. A god of silence and borders. Dead, then. Another one.

"You're Reyes," he said.

"And you know that how?"

"The valley sends people out maybe once a decade. You walk like valley-trained." He smiled without warmth. "Also, your sword's stamped with the Ashgrove contract mark. I did three winters under that seal myself, before my god stopped answering."

"Before," Vess said carefully, "or after?"

The stone eyes didn't blink. "That's the question everyone at this table is asking themselves every morning, isn't it? Before or after. Silence or devouring." He extended a hand. "Marrow."

She shook it. His palm was cold as a cellar floor.

* * *

The stew arrived and it was good, which annoyed her. She ate anyway. Around them the common room murmured in the low tones of people comparing wounds.

Marrow talked while she ate, quiet and unhurried, laying out the shape of things the way a scout lays out terrain. The gods of the borderlands — the mute ones, the silent ones, the ones who marked their servants and asked nothing aloud — had been dying for a generation, not fading but *taken*. Eaten by something beneath the mountains that had been owed more than it could collect and had decided to collect from its creditors instead.

"The court below," Vess said. "I've heard the name whispered since I was a girl. Thought it was a story to keep children from wandering."

"It's a story to keep *adults* from wandering. Children were never the point." Marrow turned his cup. "Children were the *currency*."

Something went cold down Vess's spine. "Say that plainer."

"One soul per generation. That was the original bargain — the valley's founders bought their safety from the court with a promise: one child's soul, freely sold by the parents, delivered each generation. In exchange, the court pushed back the dark country, held the wards, kept the magic out. A valley where nothing supernatural could follow you. Paradise, if you didn't ask what paid for it." He glanced at her brand-hand. "Your god was the middleman. The collector. It took the payments north and kept the ledger."

"And when the payments stopped—"

"They didn't stop. That's the thing." Marrow leaned forward. "The payments never stopped. Thirteen generations, one soul apiece. Your valley has been shipping children under the mountain since before it had a name, and everyone up top agreed to forget because forgetting was part of the terms. But twelve generations ago, something went wrong with the thirteenth payment. A default. A shortfall. The court doesn't forgive shortfalls." He tapped the table once. "It ate the guarantor."

Vess set down her spoon. Her mouth had gone dry. "The god."

"The god. Devoured for debt. And now the court holds the paper on everything the god used to hold — including the marks." He reached over, almost gently, and turned her wrist so the faded scar of her own oath showed in the lamplight. "You feel that ever itch? Like something reading over your shoulder?"

She had. For years. She'd told herself it was weather.

"Every sworn servant is collateral now," Marrow said. "And the ones who signed anything new since the stars went out—" He stopped. Looked at her face. "You've seen them recently. The ones you're following."

"Coral Ashgrove. And a girl."

"How old?"

"Twelve."

Marrow exhaled slowly through his nose, and around them, Vess noticed, the room had gotten quieter. Not all at once — the way a forest goes quiet when something walks through it. Tables nearby had stopped pretending not to listen.

"What?" she said. "What am I missing?"

The keeper came over with a fresh lamp and set it down between them, and her face, which had been a closed book, opened just long enough to show pity. "The broker," the keeper said. "The masked one at the crossroads. What did your Ashgrove sign?"

"A promise. Passage and protection. Coral said it was a formality, a toll. Signed with a mark, blood-pressed." Vess heard her own voice flatten as the pieces began to find each other. "Why?"

"Because the broker doesn't sell passage," the keeper said. "The broker sells *deferment*. Passage and protection against a promise unnamed means the promise gets filled in later — by whoever holds the paper. And since the stars went out, there's exactly one party holding paper in this country." She straightened. "Your friend didn't buy a road. Your friend bought time. And the interest is collected in flesh."

"Meaning what, exactly? Say it."

Marrow answered, soft as snowfall. "Meaning Coral Ashgrove is property now. Legally, absolutely, in the oldest law there is — the law underneath everything else. The court doesn't send soldiers for what it owns. It sends *invoices*. And invoices get delivered however they need to be delivered." He paused. "Through whoever's closest."

The lamp flame bent sideways though there was no draft.

Vess sat very still. Twelve years of friendship ran through her head like water through a cracked dam — training drills, bad wine, the night Elodie was born screaming in a birthing house two valleys over, the night Vess had stood witness while—

While.

Oh.

* * *

She made it to the yard before her legs got the message to stop. Cold air, horse-smell, the black sky with its terrible absence of stars pressing down like a lid. She put her hand on the fence rail and breathed until the shaking stopped, which took longer than she liked.

Because here was the thing she'd been carrying for twelve years, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her chest, never once unpacked:

Elodie had not been born to Coral. Everyone in the valley knew Coral had "taken in" a foundling; almost no one knew the arrangement behind it. Vess knew. Vess had *made* the arrangement. Twelve years ago, when the ward-stones had hummed wrong for a full season and the god's silence had grown teeth, the elders had come to her — not to Coral, never to Coral, Coral was too honest, too stubborn, too likely to burn the whole valley down rather than sign anything — and they'd said: *we owe a payment this generation and we have no child to give. Find another way.*

And Vess, who loved Coral like a brother, who knew Coral had come home from the last contract hollow-eyed and hungry for something to keep alive, had said: *there's a family in the next valley. Poor. Too many mouths. They'd take silver for the newborn.*

She'd told herself it was mercy. A child saved from poverty, a soldier saved from emptiness, a valley saved from the dark. Three salvations, one signature. She'd even forged the lineage papers herself, good enough that Coral never questioned why the foundling's eyes were the wrong color for either dead parent listed.

She had never told herself the rest of it: that the child was the payment. That Coral hadn't adopted a daughter so much as *carried the debt* without knowing, loving it, feeding it, teaching it to whistle — twelve years of raising the invoice and calling it a family.

And now the court below had begun collection, and Coral had walked into the dark country carrying the payment in one hand and a signed promise in the other, and every mile brought them closer to the mountain, and Vess had followed.

Why had she followed?

She'd told herself: warning. Loyalty. Making it right.

Standing in the cold yard of the Inn of Small Mercies, she finally let herself look at the other answer, the one crouched underneath loyalty like a second rider on a horse.

Self-preservation.

Because her mark itched. Because she was collateral too. Because if Coral reached the court and the court learned what Vess had done — that a servant of the dead god had brokered the sale of the thirteenth payment, had *facilitated the default*, had handed the court its grievance twelve years early — then Vess wouldn't be an invoice. She'd be evidence. And courts disposed of evidence.

But if Coral never reached the court —

If Coral turned back. If Coral was stopped. If word reached the mountain that the Ashgrove servant had perished in the dark country, along with the child, along with the debt —

Then the ledger stayed closed. Then Vess lived.

The thought arrived whole and horrible, and she let it stand there in the yard with her, breathing the same cold air, because lying to yourself was the one luxury a mercenary couldn't afford.

*Use them,* the thought said. *Or warn them. There's no third door.*

There was a noise behind her. The keeper, arms crossed in the doorway, watching with the patience of someone who'd watched a hundred people have this exact conversation with themselves.

"We get a lot of the godsworn through here," the keeper said. "They all end up in that yard eventually, having that look. You want to know what separates the ones who walk back out from the ones who don't?"

"Tell me."

"The ones who walk back out decided fast. The ones who don't decided slow, and the country decided for them." She came down the steps. "Your friends are two days behind you, heading for the mountain road. Word travels faster than horses out here — the court's servants already know they're coming. There are things in the dark wearing familiar faces, and they'll offer your Ashgrove anything to keep them walking north. Anything except the truth."

"So warn them," Vess said. "That's what you'd do."

"I didn't say that." The keeper's voice was flat as slate. "I said decide fast. Warning them costs you everything — the court will know who spoke, and it collects from speakers. Using them costs you something worse, but you don't pay it till later, and mercenaries are very good at later." She shrugged. "Me, I'm just the innkeeper. I serve stew. What you serve is your own business."

She went back inside. The door shut. The dark pressed close.

Vess stood a long time at the rail. Somewhere east, two days off, Coral was probably sitting at some cold fire telling Elodie everything would be fine, believing it the way only truly honest people can believe lies. And somewhere south of them, the mountain waited with its cracked door and its patient teeth, holding a piece of paper with Coral's blood-mark on it and a twelve-year-old girl's name written in a hand older than kingdoms.

Two doors.

Vess thought about Elodie learning to whistle. About Coral teaching her to hold a wooden sword, laughing, before the world taught the girl what swords were actually for. About the forged papers in a locked box under her floorboards, and how easy it would be, tonight, to burn them.

About how burning them wouldn't unmake anything. Paper wasn't the debt. She was.

She went back inside, climbed to the rented room, and did the hardest thing she'd done since the god died: she took out ink and paper and wrote the warning she should have given at the crossroads — *the promise you signed makes you owned; turn back; I'm sorry; I'll explain everything if we live* — and sealed it with her mark.

Then she called the inn's fastest rider, paid triple, and gave him one instruction: *find them before the mountain does.*

And only after the hoofbeats faded did she sit down on the edge of the bed, put her face in her hands, and admit what the ink had already known:

She'd sent the letter. But she was still riding north herself. Still following. Still deciding nothing — because some part of her, the part that had signed the first papers twelve years ago, wanted to see how it ended before choosing which side of it to stand on.

Outside, in the starless dark, something passed over the inn — a pressure, a hush, the feeling of being read. The lamp flames all bent east at once, toward the mountain.

Toward Coral.

Toward the invoice, walking itself to delivery.

Vess blew out her light and started packing.
