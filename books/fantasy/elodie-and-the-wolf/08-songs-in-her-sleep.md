## 8. Songs in Her Sleep

The song started three nights after the crossroads, and at first Elodie thought it was the wind.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of a hundred such ruins scattered through the dark country like teeth knocked out of some enormous jaw. Coral had built a fire from wood that burned green and slow, giving off more smoke than heat, and was cleaning their sword with the methodical patience of someone who needed their hands busy. Vess sat apart, as she always did now, chewing on a strip of dried meat that had probably been someone's boot leather in a former life.

Elodie lay on her bedroll with her eyes closed, listening.

It wasn't the wind. The wind in this country came in gusts, ragged and directionless, carrying smells of ash and cold stone. The song was different. It rose out of the ground itself, or maybe out of the air, low and patient, a melody with no beginning that she could find. It sounded the way deep water looked — dark, layered, going down further than you could follow without your chest hurting.

*Come along,* it seemed to say, though it didn't use words. *Come along, little one. You're nearly home.*

She opened her eyes. Coral glanced over, caught her looking, and gave her the small crooked smile that meant *you all right?*

"Fine," she said.

And that was the first lie.

* * *

By the fifth night she could hear it even while walking, even while talking, a thread of sound woven underneath everything else like a second heartbeat. It didn't frighten her anymore. That was the strange part. She'd expected fear — the dark country was full of things to be afraid of, things she'd seen from the corner of her eye and pretended not to: shapes standing motionless in distant fields, watching them pass; villages where the doors had been bricked up from the outside; once, a river that flowed uphill, its surface crawling with pale fish that swam with their bellies toward the sky.

But the song wasn't those things. The song felt like it knew her.

On the sixth night, it showed her her mother's face.

She was asleep by the fire when it happened — properly asleep, dropped down into it like a stone into a well — and suddenly she was somewhere else. A room she had never seen but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face in an old portrait. Small. Warm. A window with real sunlight coming through it, yellow and thick as honey, falling across a wooden floor. She hadn't seen true sunlight since the stars went out. In the dream it made her want to weep.

A woman stood at the window with her back turned. She was young. She had dark hair pinned up carelessly, strands escaping around her ears, and she was humming — humming the same melody that lived under the world now, but soft, unbroken, a lullaby instead of a summons.

"Mama?" Elodie said, and her voice in the dream was small, much smaller than she was, the voice of a baby or maybe of nothing at all.

The woman turned.

Her face was Elodie's face. Older, softer, worn at the edges the way a well-loved book is worn, but the same — the same narrow chin, the same dark eyes with that faint ring of amber around the pupil that Coral had once told her was *unusual, very unusual, don't let anyone tell you different.* She was crying. She was smiling and crying at the same time, and she held out her arms, and Elodie ran to her, and just before they touched —

— the dream folded.

Not ended. Folded, like a letter being tucked away, and in the space of the fold she saw other things, quick and terrible: a table. A contract on the table, written on skin that wasn't paper, the letters raised like scars. A man's hand signing it — not Coral's hand, broader, wearing a ring she didn't know. And beneath the contract, listed in a column like goods in a merchant's inventory:

*One soul. Female. Newborn. Price: remission of debt, one generation.*

Price paid.

She woke with the fire burned down to embers and Coral sitting up sharply, hand already on their sword, because she must have cried out. "Elodie." Their voice was rough with sleep and worry. "What is it? What did you see?"

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the road, and she stood at it for a long time — a breath, two — looking at Coral's face lit orange by the coals, that face that had been the only fixed point in her life for twelve years, scarred and tired and so fiercely hers.

"I dreamed about the sun," she said.

Second lie. Easier than the first.

Coral's shoulders came down. They reached over and pulled the blanket higher around her, an awkward, rough-gentle gesture, and said, "We'll get there. I promise," and lay back down, and Elodie stared at the smoke-dark sky where no stars were, and understood something that settled into her chest like a swallowed stone:

She couldn't tell them. Not because they wouldn't believe her. Because they would. And then Coral would do what Coral always did, which was put themselves between her and the thing, whatever the thing was, and the contract in her dream had been signed before she was born, and there was nothing Coral's sword could do about a price that had already been paid.

Some debts you can't fight. You can only carry them.

* * *

After that, she stopped fighting the song.

This was the part she kept locked away most carefully of all — not the dreams, but how much she wanted them. Every night the mountain showed her more, and every morning she woke knowing things she hadn't known at sundown, and it felt less like being hunted and more like being *remembered.* Like there was a version of herself waiting somewhere below the earth, complete, finished, and the song was simply calling her home to become it.

She learned to listen with her eyes open, half-listening, the way you carry a conversation and a secret at once. She learned which silences in the day belonged to the song and which were ordinary. She learned that if she pressed her palm flat against the ground, the melody sharpened until it was almost language.

*Thirteenth,* the ground sang, when she did this on the ninth night, while Coral argued quietly with Vess about the road ahead. *Thirteenth of twelve-and-one. The last leaf. The closing sum.*

"What are you doing?"

Vess. Standing over her, arms crossed, her shadow long in the firelight. Elodie snatched her hand back from the dirt.

"Nothing. Checking the ground. Coral said to watch for tremors."

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Vess had eyes like a hawk that had gotten old and tired of hawking — flat, patient, missing nothing. Elodie had the sudden, horrible certainty that Vess knew exactly what she'd been doing, and worse, that Vess knew what it meant, and worst of all, that Vess was deciding right now whether it mattered.

"You should sleep," Vess said finally.

"Yeah," Elodie said. "I should."

She slept. And dreamed. And in the dream the room with the honeyed light came again, and this time her mother spoke.

"They told me it was the only way," her mother said. She was holding a bundle now, wrapped in white cloth, and the bundle was small and warm and breathing, and Elodie understood with a lurch that she was looking at herself. "They told me the valley starved without the bargain. They told me one child, one generation, and the rest go free. I signed it. God forgive me, I signed it, and I have regretted it every single day of your life, which is to say every day of mine."

"Did you love me?" Elodie asked. It was the only question that mattered. Everything else — the contract, the price, the court — she could survive not understanding. This one she couldn't.

Her mother knelt. Pressed her forehead against the bundle's forehead. And said, "Every day. Which is why it broke me."

Then the dream shifted again, crueler, faster: the white bundle being carried away in arms that were too long; a door of black stone opening without hinges; a voice, vast and courteous, saying *we accept payment, the account stands, thirteen generations, the thirteenth shall close the ledger* — and Elodie woke with tears frozen on her cheeks in the night cold, and Coral asleep beside her, trusting, and the song rising all around them now, unmistakable, joyful almost, the way a trap is joyful to the trapper when the animal finally turns toward it.

*Soon,* it sang. *Soon, soon, soon.*

* * *

On the eleventh day, they passed a shrine.

It was a small thing, half-swallowed by the dark earth — a stone basin on a pillar, cracked down the middle, with dead offerings still curled inside it: flowers gone to gray lace, a child's shoe, coins nobody had come back for. Coral stopped and studied it the way they studied everything, like it might bite.

"Old god-road marker," Vess said. "Prayer stations. People used to leave things when they traveled. Asking protection."

"Did it work?"

Vess was quiet a moment. "For some gods. Ours went quiet a long time ago. Maybe it was never listening. Maybe it was listening and couldn't answer." She shrugged, and the shrug didn't match her face. "Doesn't matter now. Whatever heard prayers in this country is dead or eaten."

Elodie touched the basin as they passed. Just brushed it with her fingertips.

The song flared.

It came up through the stone, through her bones, loud enough that she staggered, and for one blinding instant she wasn't standing on a road in the dark country at all — she was *below*, in a hall of black glass, and things were turning to look at her, beautiful things, patient things, faces like her mother's face and faces like nothing human at all, and they were smiling, and one of them said, in a voice like honey poured over a blade:

*There she is. There's our girl. We've kept your place, little thirteenth. Your chair is warm. Your name is written. Come and close the book.*

Then Coral's hand closed on her shoulder and the vision snapped like a thread, and she was on her knees on the road, and Coral was crouched in front of her, and their face was the face she'd lied to twice now, drawn tight with a fear she'd never seen there before.

"Elodie. Look at me. What happened."

"The stone," she managed. "It was — cold. I got dizzy."

Third lie. They were getting easier. That frightened her more than the court did.

Coral didn't let go of her shoulder. They searched her face the way they searched a treeline, thorough and grim, and Elodie made herself meet their eyes and keep them, because she'd learned lying from the best — she'd grown up watching Coral lie to landlords and border clerks and anyone else who asked questions about a mercenary raising a child alone — and Coral believed her, or decided to, and helped her up, and said, "No more touching things. Anything. Promise me."

"I promise."

Fourth lie. She was getting good at this. She hated how good.

That night she lay awake listening to Coral breathe, and the song hummed beneath it, and she thought: *this is what it costs.* Not the court, not the contract, not the souls in glass she sometimes glimpsed sleeping far below, row upon row of them, dreaming. This. Lying three feet from the person who loved her most in the world and building a wall out of silence, brick by brick, because the truth would hurt them worse than any enemy could.

Coral stirred. "You awake?"

"Yeah."

"Scared?"

She thought about lying again. Then she said, honestly, "Yeah."

"Me too," Coral said. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we make the foothills."

Below the foothills was the mountain. Below the mountain was the door. Elodie closed her eyes and let the song pour over her like dark water, and in it, faint and patient, she heard the sound of a page turning.

The ledger was almost open. And she was the only one who knew — the only one who knew what her mother's face looked like, what the contract said, what waited below with her name already written in a book bound in something that used to be alive.

She rolled toward the fire, toward Coral's sleeping shape, and whispered into the dark, so quietly that even the song might miss it:

"When we get there, I'm not going to be the payment. I'm going to be the problem."

The song paused.

Just for a heartbeat — just long enough for her to know it had heard.

Then it began again, sweeter than ever, and somewhere far beneath the earth, Elodie would have sworn she heard something laugh.
