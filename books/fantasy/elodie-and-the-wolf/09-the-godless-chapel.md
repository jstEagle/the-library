## 9. The Godless Chapel

The chapel sat at the valley's heart like a tooth that had never been pulled.

Coral had walked past it a thousand times in twelve years of patrol routes. Grey stone, squat tower, door of black oak gone silver with weather. Children dared each other to touch it. Old women left bread on the step out of habit older than memory. Nobody went inside, because there was nothing inside — no altar, no icon, no god. The god had spoken from that place once, generations ago, and then gone silent, and silence was all the valley ever got back.

Tonight the chapel's windows glowed faintly blue, and the glow moved.

Coral crouched at the tree line with Elodie's weight warm against their hip — she'd refused to be left at Vess's camp, and after the river ambush two nights ago, Coral hadn't had the heart to argue. The broker's mark sat under their glove like a coin pressed into the skin, cold and patient. The god-mark above it still burned its slow coal-red, dragging them north whenever they stopped paying attention to their own feet.

"You said nobody goes in there," Elodie whispered.

"Nobody does."

"Then why is it lit?"

Coral didn't have an answer that wasn't a lie, so they gave her none. They watched the chapel breathe its blue light for a long moment, then checked the knife at their belt out of pure habit, knowing it would do nothing. The wards were down. Magic had come back into the world like floodwater into a cellar, and everything Coral knew about surviving this valley had expired with the stars.

"We go in quiet," they said. "We look. We leave. If anything speaks to you — *anything* — you don't answer. Not even to say no. Understand?"

Elodie nodded. Her jaw was set the way Coral's used to be, before the valley wore it soft. She'd learned that face somewhere, and Coral tried not to think about where.

They crossed the graveyard in a low run, boots finding the gaps between stones by feel. The grass was frost-burnt black. Every grave marker they passed had been scrubbed clean of lichen, which was wrong, because no one had tended these graves in living memory — and now Coral understood, with a lurch, who might have been doing the tending, and what kind of hands.

The chapel door wasn't locked. It wasn't even closed. It stood open six inches, and the hinges made no sound at all, as if the wood had been oiled recently, or as if sound simply declined to happen near it.

Coral pushed through first.

* * *

Inside, the blue light came from the walls themselves — mortar between the stones glowing like drowned moonlight, brightening and dimming in a rhythm almost too slow to catch. The nave was bare except for dust and a scatter of old bread on the threshold, gone green with mold. No pews. No altar. Just an empty room shaped like a question.

Except for the floor.

Where the altar should have been, the flagstones had been pulled up sometime in the last three days — edges sharp, dirt fresh — revealing a stairwell descending into the hill. Cold air rose out of it, smelling of ink and iron and something older underneath, something like a cellar where meat had once been kept.

"They dug," Elodie said behind Coral. "Somebody dug."

"Stay behind me."

"Coral." Her voice had changed. Heavier. "I heard the mountain again last night. While I was sleeping. It was singing the way you sing when you're counting." She swallowed. "It counted to twelve and then it stopped, and then it started over, and the twelfth time it said my name instead of a number."

Coral turned and looked at her. Twelve years old, wrapped in a borrowed coat too big for her, chin up because she'd learned early that crying got you nothing. Their whole chest felt like the moment after a bad landing, everything compressed and waiting to break.

"Then we're going down," Coral said, "and we're going to find out what it's counting toward. And whatever it says, whatever we find — you are mine. Not theirs. Not the mountain's. Mine. That doesn't change. Say it back."

"You're mine," she said, because she'd never once said it the other direction, and Coral let her have it.

They descended.

* * *

The stairs went further than the chapel was deep. Coral counted forty steps, then sixty, then lost count around ninety, the walls sweating that same blue light, the air growing warmer instead of cooler, which was wrong in every way a cellar could be wrong. Somewhere below, water dripped in a pattern too regular to be water.

At the bottom was a scriptorium.

That was the only word for it. A long vaulted room carved straight out of the hill's bones, lined with desks, and the desks lined with books — hundreds of them, thousands, their spines cracked and their pages furred with damp. Candles burned on every surface without melting. Ink pots sat full beside pens worn down to stubs, as if the writers had stepped away mid-sentence and simply never come back.

One desk nearest the stairs held a ledger open, a pen lying across it, the last line still wet.

Coral touched nothing. They read.

The entry was dated four days ago. Before the stars went out. The hand was careful, clerkly, unhurried:

*Tally confirmed. Thirteen payments contracted, twelve received. The thirteenth matures upon the fall of the ward-line. Collection authorized.*

Below it, a second line in a different hand — shakier, human:

*Forgive us. There was no other price it would take.*

Elodie made a small sound. Coral put an arm across her without looking away from the page.

"Thirteen payments," she read aloud, because she could read faster than fear could stop her. "Coral, what payments? What did they sell?"

"I don't know yet." Which was a lie, or half of one, because some animal part of Coral had known since the thing in the root cellar called her by a name she'd never been told. Since the word *sold*. You can know a thing in your body years before your mind consents to hold it.

They moved deeper into the archive, reading spines as they went. Most were parish records: baptisms, harvests, quarrels arbitrated, the ordinary machinery of a valley that had once talked to its god and gotten answers. Then the records changed character, the way a road changes from gravel to mud to no road at all.

A slim volume bound in grey leather, its title stamped plain as a butcher's bill: *Terms of Settlement, Being the Agreement Between the Court Beneath the Mountain and the People of the Valley, Recorded in Perpetuity.*

Coral opened it standing up, because sitting felt too much like settling in.

The first page was a preamble in language so old it took a moment to parse — clauses about boundaries, about the ward-stones, about magic being *withheld from the valley as a mercy, for so long as the debt stands current.* The valley without magic wasn't a curse. It was a payment plan. Generations of people had lived in a place drained dry on purpose, and called it home, and thanked the silent god for its protection, and the protection and the draining were the same act.

The second page listed the price.

*One soul per generation, freely surrendered by contract of birth, payable to the Court Beneath the Mountain, in perpetuity until the principal be discharged. The soul shall be taken in infancy and raised among its own until collection. This arrangement secures the ward-line, the famine-sparing, and the peace of both parties.*

*In infancy.* Raised among its own until collection. Coral's mouth went dry. They hadn't found Elodie abandoned on a roadside, however much the story they'd built — that they'd built themselves, that everyone had helped them build, that they'd needed so badly to believe — insisted otherwise. She'd been placed. Delivered. Left where a mercenary with a soft spot and a sworn mark would find her, raise her, keep her fed and loved and *ripe.*

Twelve years of ripening.

"Coral?" Elodie's voice, very small. "Why does it say 'raised among its own'? Raised by who?"

Coral couldn't answer. So they kept turning pages, because motion was the only thing holding them together, and the ledger obliged with a list.

*Record of Payments Received.*

The handwriting changed with each entry. Different clerks, different centuries, but the format never varied: a name, a birth-date, a collection-date, and a single word of disposition.

*Received: Maren, daughter of Aldous. Collected in the winter of the long fever.*
*Received: Tam, child of the ferry-house. Collected at the turn of the year.*
*Received: Isbet...*

Twelve entries. Coral counted them twice, hoping to be wrong, and was not. Twelve names spanning more than three hundred years. One per generation. Each one born in this valley, each one given up by contract before they could walk, each one raised in a family that may or may not have known what it was raising, and then collected — taken beneath the mountain, into whatever court sang counting songs to sleeping children.

Twelve souls dreaming in glass, if the fragments Elodie described meant what Coral feared they meant. And a thirteenth line, waiting, the ink of the name already laid down in anticipation:

*Contracted: Elodie.*

No parent listed. No family name. Just the name itself, written in that careful clerkly hand, and beside it a notation that made Coral's blood stop moving:

*Surrendered by proxy, per original terms. Proxy signature: R. Ashgrove.*

The room tilted. Coral gripped the desk edge hard enough to hurt.

Their own name. Their own mark — they knew that abbreviation, had signed contracts with it for twenty years, the R for the name they'd shed along with the life that came before it. But they had never signed this. Never seen this book, never stood in this chapel, never —

And then they remembered the night Elodie appeared. Twelve years ago. The storm. The stranger who'd knocked at the door wearing a traveling cloak with the hood up, who'd asked Coral — sworn servant of the valley's god, trusted, bonded, *bound* — to witness a document "for the god's own files." Who'd guided Coral's hand to the signature line with the gentle insistence of someone helping a drunk to bed. Who'd left before dawn, leaving a bundle in the cradle they'd brought with them, and a note saying only *her name is Elodie now; see that she believes it.*

Coral had believed they were witnessing a formality. A filing. An administrative kindness.

They had signed their daughter over to the court beneath the mountain with their own hand, and spent twelve years calling it rescue.

"No," Coral said aloud. It came out flat, stripped of everything. "No, that's not — I didn't know. I didn't—"

"Coral." Elodie had both hands on their arm now, shaking it. "Coral, look at me. What's my name say? What does the book say about me?"

"That you're contracted." The words tasted like copper. "That I signed it. That I'm the reason they can collect you."

"That's not true."

"It's written down, Elodie. In the god's own house, in the god's own hand—"

"The god's dead." She said it simply, terribly, the way children say true things. "You told me. Vess told me. Whatever wrote this, it isn't protecting anybody anymore. And if you signed something you didn't understand, then somebody *made* you sign it, and that's not the same thing." Her grip tightened. "You taught me that. Contracts need consent on both sides or they're just traps. You said it when the wool-trader tried to cheat us. You *said* it."

Coral looked down at her — at this child the world had priced before she drew breath, sold by a stranger's trick and their own trusting hand, and somehow still standing there arguing law on their behalf because that was what family did. Something in Coral's chest cracked along a fault line that had been forming since the stars went out.

"Then we find the trapdoor in it," Coral said. "Every contract has one. We read every word in this room until we find theirs."

* * *

They searched until Candlemark — Coral tracked time the soldier's way, by candle-lengths, though the candles here refused to shorten, which unsettled them more than anything else in the hill. Elodie worked the far shelves, pulling volumes and reading aloud anything that mentioned *payment*, *collection*, or *court*, her voice steady in the way of someone doing a job to keep from screaming. Coral worked the ledgers chronologically, hunting for the original instrument itself — not the summary terms, but the founding contract, the actual paper the court had drawn up, because summaries lie and originals bleed.

They found it in a locked case at the room's far end, the lock long rusted through, the case's glass starred with a single old crack.

The founding contract was written on vellum gone amber, in ink gone brown, and it named names.

The court beneath the mountain — its true name was written in letters that squirmed when read directly, so Coral read them slantwise, catching fragments: *the Quiet Ones*, *the Patient Teeth*, *they who lent the light and wait for the repayment.* Against those names stood the valley's counterparty, and Coral's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Not the ancestors. Not the elders. Not any human hand.

*The god of the valley,* the contract said. *Sworn debtor. First party to the bargain.*

The god had borrowed. That was what the preamble had buried under clause after clause of legal fog: the ward-line, the magic-withholding, the famine-sparing — all of it was collateral on a loan the god itself had taken out, ages ago, from things that lived under the mountain. And the interest schedule was souls. One per generation, harvested from the god's own worshippers, because the court respected nothing else. The god had sold its people's children to service its own debt, and built the valley's whole quiet, safe, magic-less existence as the fence around the payment plan.

And when Coral reached the final annotation, appended in a later hand — newer ink, hurried strokes — the bottom fell out entirely:

*Note, added upon the default: the debtor having failed to render payment in full at term, the creditor has executed seizure of the debtor's substance entire. The god of the valley is hereby declared consumed. All obligations transfer to the estate. Collection continues.*

The god hadn't gone silent.

Vess had said it first, on the frozen road — *it didn't go quiet, it was eaten* — and Coral had filed it away as grief-talk, as one more madness in a season of them. Now it sat in front of them in brown ink on amber vellum: devoured for default. Eaten by its creditors. And the court had kept collecting anyway, generation after generation, harvesting children against a debt whose original debtor no longer existed — collecting against an *estate*, against a hollowed-out claim, against nothing but momentum and the fact that no one had ever come downstairs to read the fine print and object.

"Elodie," Coral said. Their voice sounded like someone else's. "Come look at this."

She came. She read. Coral watched her lips move through the note about the default, watched her arrive at the same arithmetic, watched her understand that the thing singing to her in her sleep was singing to a debtor's asset, that her whole life had been an installment plan serviced by a dead god's ghost.

"So it's not even real," she said finally. "The debt. The god that owed it is *gone*. They're collecting on a dead man's loan and there's nobody left to pay it off, so it just — goes forever?"

"Debts like that don't die," Coral said slowly, thinking of the broker's mark cold under their glove, of the promise they'd signed at the crossroads without reading, of love used as leverage twice now in one lifetime. "They get inherited. Down and down, until somebody breaks the chain or the chain breaks them." They looked at their daughter. "There's a clause here — 'all obligations transfer to the estate.' An estate has heirs, Elodie. If we're all heirs to the god's debt, then maybe—"

Maybe what? Maybe the reverse was also true: heirs could inherit more than debt. Heirs could inherit the right to contest it. To challenge the terms. To stand in a courtroom made of patient teeth and argue.

The thought was still forming when the blue light in the walls flickered — once, twice — and steadied into a color it hadn't been before. Red. Deep, arterial red, pulsing now in time with something that felt horribly like a heartbeat coming up through the stone.

Every candle in the scriptorium guttered at once.

On the open ledger by the stairs, where no wind could reach, new ink began to move across the page. Slow. Careful. Clerkly. Writing itself in that same patient hand, adding a line beneath the tally Coral had read hours ago:

*Proxy noted present within the archives. Asset noted present within the archives. Collection may proceed at creditor's convenience.*

Beneath it, after a pause, one more line:

*The stairs are longer going up than they were.*

Coral took Elodie's hand and ran for the dark.
