## 10. Hunted by the Faithful

The river froze the night the stars went out, and it never thawed.

Coral felt it through their boots before they saw it — a wrongness in the ice, a stillness too complete. Real ice creaked. Real ice groaned and settled and muttered to itself in the dark. This ice said nothing. It lay across the valley's river like a held breath, smooth as glass, black as the sky above it, and when Coral knelt and pressed a palm flat against its surface, the cold that came up through their glove was not the cold of water. It was the cold of something waiting.

"Stay back from the edge," they said, without turning around.

Elodie stopped. Twelve years old, wrapped in two cloaks and Vess's old scarf wound three times around her neck, she had learned on this road to read the changes in Coral's voice the way sailors read weather. She backed up three steps and stood very still among the dead reeds.

They had been walking for six days through the dark country — six days of a night that did not lift, of villages where people smiled with borrowed memories, of Elodie waking with the mountain's song caught between her teeth like a fishhook. Six days since the crossroads. Six days since Coral had signed whatever it was they'd signed, and felt the broker's mark settle onto their skin beside the god's dead one like a second leash.

The mark on Coral's wrist had been quiet since the chapel. Quiet, but not asleep. It sat under their skin like a stone at the bottom of a well, and every hour it grew heavier, as if it were slowly filling with something.

*You owe,* it seemed to say. *You owe. You owe.*

"I know," Coral murmured, and then realized they'd spoken aloud, and pressed their lips together.

Ahead, the frozen river ran straight as a road between banks of frost-killed willows. And that was exactly what bothered them about it. In six days of broken country, they had found no road so clean, no path so convenient. The dark country did not offer straight lines. It made you earn every mile twice.

A road this easy was an invitation.

And invitations, in Coral's long experience, came from one of two kinds of hosts: those who wanted to feed you, and those who wanted to eat you.

They rose from the ice and turned to Elodie, who was watching them with that new stillness she'd developed — the stillness of a child learning that the world was larger and stranger than the woman who raised her had ever let on. It broke something in Coral a little, every time they saw it. That look used to be wonder. Now it was wariness.

"We'll cut east," Coral said. "Through the willows. The river's too—"

The arrow took them in the shoulder.

No warning whistle. No challenge shouted across the ice. Just the flat crack of a bowstring somewhere upriver and then the impact, a fist of wood and iron punching through cloak, through leather, through muscle, deep into the meat below Coral's collarbone. The world tilted. Coral went down to one knee on the bank, and the cold came flooding into the wound like water into a boot, and somewhere very far away Elodie screamed their name.

*Ambush.* The word arrived with strange calm, the old mercenary arithmetic clicking into place even as the pain arrived behind it, white and total. *Upstream. Elevated. At least two archers, probably more. They let us walk past the first position. They want us on the ice.*

On the ice. Where footing was treacherous. Where there was no cover. Where a body could be pinned down and picked apart at leisure, or simply waited out until the cold did the work.

Godsworn tactics. Coral knew them because Coral had taught half of them.

"Elodie." Their voice came out rougher than they intended. "Down. Behind me. *Now.*"

She dropped. Coral heard her small body hit the frozen mud, heard her scramble back against the willows, and the sound of it — the fear in it, the trust in it, the absolute faith that Coral could fix this — landed in Coral's chest harder than any arrow could have.

Three more arrows came out of the dark. Coral threw themselves sideways off the bank and onto the ice, and the impact wrenched a grunt out of them, and the wounded shoulder screamed, and the arrows hissed past overhead and shattered against the far bank like the bones of small birds.

Then Coral rolled onto their back on the black glass of the river, looked up at the starless sky, and did the only thing left to do.

They laughed.

It hurt to laugh. It felt like swallowing knives. But the absurdity of it — lying gut-shot-adjacent on a haunted river while children of the same god they'd served for twenty years tried to put arrows in them — the sheer stupid symmetry of it demanded acknowledgment.

"CORAL ASHGROVE!" The voice rolled down the river, amplified by more than lungs. Godsworn carried speaking-horns on long patrols; Coral remembered the weight of one on their own shoulder, the way it made your voice feel like someone else's. "YOU ARE ACCUSED OF THE THEFT OF THE VALLEY'S MAGIC. YOU ARE ACCUSED OF WAKING WHAT SLEPT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN. SURRENDER, AND RECEIVE THE GOD'S MERCY."

*The god is dead,* Coral thought, and could not stop the bitter twist of their mouth. *Your god is a carcass in a court beneath the mountain, and I've seen the ledger.*

But saying that would change nothing. These weren't thinkers. These were believers — the kind the valley bred best, the kind Coral had once been, before the silence of the mark had taught them doubt. They had spent their whole lives being told that magic was locked away, that the wards kept the world safe, that the god watched over the faithful. And now the stars were gone and the wards were dust and something vast moved beneath the mountain, and they needed someone to blame.

Coral Ashgrove was right here. Convenient.

"I HAVE A DAUGHTER WITH ME," Coral called back, and hated how their voice shook. Not from fear. From the effort of keeping it level. "SHE'S TWELVE YEARS OLD. SHE HAS NOTHING TO DO WITH THIS."

Silence, for a moment. Then, closer — boots on ice, many boots, the particular crunch-and-slide of trained fighters moving on frozen ground. "THE CHILD IS WITNESS TO YOUR CRIMES. SHE WILL BE RETURNED TO THE VALLEY AND GIVEN TO THE CARE OF THE FAITHFUL."

*Given to the care of the faithful.* Coral had seen what happened to orphans given to the care of the faithful. Half of them ended up scrubbing chapel floors until their hands bled. The other half—

The other half ended up sold at birth to courts beneath mountains.

Something in Coral went very quiet and very cold, colder than the river, colder than the arrow-wound, colder than the starless sky. It was the cold place they went when the fighting started, the place they'd built brick by brick over twenty years of mercenary work, the place where feeling things got you killed. Most days they hated that place. Tonight they were grateful for it.

"All right," Coral said softly, to no one. To themselves. To the dead god whose mark sat heavy on their wrist. "All right, then."

They pulled the arrow from their shoulder.

It came free with a wet sound and a sheet of pain that whited out the world for two full seconds, and Coral bit down on the scream and swallowed it, and pressed their gloved hand hard against the hole in their shoulder, and stood up.

There were seven of them.

Seven godsworn, fanned out across the ice in a crescent formation, closing slow. Coral recognized three faces from the valley garrisons — Bren of the East Stones, with his red beard crusted with frost; the twins Aldith and Marren, who'd been children in the training yards when Coral left; and old Hesk, gray-haired, gray-bearded, who had taught Coral the spear when Coral was younger than Elodie. All of them wore the god's mark on their wrists, dull and dark as Coral's own. All of them carried steel.

None of them looked like monsters. That was the worst part. They looked tired, and scared, and certain. Certain the way only frightened people can be certain.

"Coral." Hesk's voice was quiet, almost gentle. The speaking-horn was down now; this was just an old man talking to a younger one. "Put down your blade. Come home. Face the judgment. Whatever you've done, the rites can cleanse it. You know they can."

"The rites need a god," Coral said. "Yours is dead. I've seen what ate it."

Hesk's face didn't change, but something behind his eyes flickered — a door opening a crack onto a room he couldn't afford to look inside. He shut it. Coral watched him shut it. Twenty years ago, Coral would have done the same.

"You're lying," Hesk said. "Or mad. Or bought." He lifted his spear. "Last chance, Ashgrove. Kneel."

Coral knelt.

Hesk blinked. The crescent faltered, just slightly, just for a heartbeat — surprise rippling through trained men and women who had expected a fight and gotten surrender. Bren actually lowered his sword an inch.

And in that heartbeat, Coral moved.

Not toward the godsworn. Toward the ice itself — toward the thin seam where river current still ran fast beneath the surface near the eastern bank, the seam Coral had been mapping with their eyes since the moment they knelt. They drove their dagger down into the black glass and *pulled*, carving a line, and the river — the real river, the living water underneath all that waiting stillness — heard the invitation and answered.

The ice split.

It opened along Coral's carved seam with a crack like the sky breaking, and the crescent of godsworn, standing in perfect formation on what had been solid ground thirty seconds ago, suddenly wasn't. Bren went down first, bellowing, arms windmilling. One of the twins — Aldith, Coral thought, Aldith always was the quicker — leapt clear and came on fast, low, spear leveled, and Coral met her charge with the flat of their blade and a shoulder-check that put her skidding across the ice on her back.

Then it was close work. Then it was ugly.

Coral fought the way they'd been taught to fight, which was to say: to end things. A pommel strike to Bren's temple as he clawed his way out of the split ice, dropping him senseless into the shallows. An elbow that broke Marren's nose with a sound like a green branch snapping. When one of the others — young, faceless, a scarred jaw and terrified eyes — came at Coral with a hatchet and more courage than skill, Coral disarmed him in two moves and put him down with a knee to the sternum and a dagger resting against the soft of his throat, and felt the boy's pulse hammering against the blade like a trapped bird.

"Yield," Coral rasped. Blood ran warm down their arm, steaming faintly in the freezing air. "Yield and live."

The boy yielded. Most of them did. Hesk did not.

The old man came through the steam and chaos like the last piece of the old world falling, spear driving for Coral's chest with all the weight of everything he believed behind it, and Coral — bleeding, exhausted, grieving, furious — met him with killing intent because there was no time left for anything gentler. Blade turned spear. Boot swept feet. Hesk went down hard on the black ice, and Coral was on him before he finished falling, knee on chest, dagger high.

"Coral—" Hesk said. His eyes were wide and clear and unafraid, and that was somehow worse than fear would have been. "Coral, I knew you when—"

"I know," Coral said.

He brought the dagger down anyway.

Not into the throat. Into the shoulder joint of Hesk's spear arm, deep, twisting, severing tendon, and Hesk's scream cracked across the frozen river like the ice had cracked, and Coral struck him once across the temple with the pommel and left him unconscious and bleeding and alive, alive, *alive*, and knelt there in the spreading heat of the old man's blood shaking like a leaf in wind.

Around them, the godsworn lay groaning or still. None dead. Coral counted heartbeats with a professional's grim attention and found them all still beating, and did not know whether to be proud of that or ashamed, because the only reason none of them were dead was that Coral had been lucky, and luck was not a plan, and next time—

Next time there might not be a choice.

"Coral?"

Elodie's voice. Small. Coming from the bank.

Coral looked up.

She was standing at the edge of the willows, and she had seen all of it. Every moment. The arrow. The blood. The breaking of Hesk's arm. The kneeling figure with the knife, and the screaming, and the shaking afterward. She had come out of hiding because hiding had become unbearable, and now she stood in the frost-dead reeds looking at Coral the way you look at a door you thought led to one room and discovered led somewhere else entirely.

Her face wasn't afraid of Coral. That was the thing that undid them. Her face was afraid *for* Coral — for the blood still seeping between their fingers, for the tremor in their hands, for the person kneeling in the wreckage of other human beings who was also, impossibly, the person who had tucked her in every night for twelve years.

Both of those people were real. Both of them were standing in front of her. And she was twelve years old, and she was seeing it for the first time, and no amount of love could make the seeing gentle.

"Elodie," Coral said. Their voice came out wrecked. "I—"

"It's okay," she said.

It wasn't. They both knew it wasn't. But she said it anyway, the way Coral had said *it's okay* to her after nightmares, after fevers, after the night the stars went out — the small lie that holds a larger truth at bay, the family language of people who take care of each other. She picked her way carefully across the ice, stepping around the groaning godsworn without looking at them, and knelt beside Coral in the blood-flecked snow, and began, with small competent hands, tearing strips from the hem of her second cloak to bind the wound.

Neither of them spoke while she worked. The river groaned and settled around them, real ice sounds now, honest ones. Somewhere downstream, water chuckled through the split Coral had made, and the sound was almost like laughter, almost like weeping.

When the bandage was tied, Elodie sat back on her heels and looked at Coral with an expression far too old for her face.

"They think you stole the magic," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And the mountain took the stars."

"Yes."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "The song says the mountain didn't take anything." Her voice dropped, went strange and distant, the voice she'd been using in her sleep for days now. "The song says the mountain was *paid*. It says the payments are almost done. It says—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Looked away.

"Says what?" Coral asked gently.

Elodie's jaw worked. For a moment Coral thought she wouldn't answer — and there it was again, that new distance, that small locked door that hadn't existed a week ago. Then she turned back, and her eyes were bright and fierce and terrified.

"It says the thirteenth payment is walking toward it," she whispered. "Right now. On its own feet."

The words landed in Coral's stomach like ice water.

Because Coral had signed something at a crossroads six days ago. Because the broker's promise had been passage and protection, and the price unread. Because the mark on their wrist had grown heavier every hour, filling with something, pulling them onward — and they had told themselves it was pulling them toward answers, toward rescue, toward a way to save her.

Toward. Not away.

Every mile Coral had walked since the crossroads, they had walked *toward the mountain*. And they had brought Elodie with them.

"Oh," Coral said. Very quietly. "Oh, you clever, cruel thing."

The broker's mark pulsed once beneath their skin, warm and satisfied, like a hand patting a well-behaved animal.

Downriver, in the direction of the valley they had fled, a horn sounded — long, low, answered by another, and another. More godsworn. A hunting party becoming a hunt.

Elodie stood and pulled Coral up by their good hand, small fingers gripping hard.

"Come on," she said. "We have to keep moving."

And Coral, bleeding and hollow and more afraid than they had ever been in twenty years of soldiery, let a twelve-year-old lead them off the ice and into the dark.
