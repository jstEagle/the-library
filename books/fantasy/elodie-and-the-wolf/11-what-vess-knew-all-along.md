## 11. What Vess Knew All Along

Vess had killed men for less than the look Coral was giving her now.

They stood in the lee of a fallen watchtower, three days out from the frozen river, and the dark country pressed close around them like an audience. The broker's mark on Coral's wrist had stopped burning and started *pulling*, and Elodie slept against a pack with her hands curled under her chin like she was nine instead of twelve, and none of it — none of it — was why Vess's mouth had gone dry.

It was the ledger page. Coral held it out flat between them, torn from the godless chapel, and Vess's own name was written on it in ink that hadn't dried in forty years.

*Vessa Marun, sworn. Witness to the twelfth payment.*

"You were there," Coral said. Not a question. Their voice was very quiet, which was worse than shouting. Vess had heard Coral shout at dying men, at broken contracts, at the sky itself during the worst years of the border wars. She had never heard them this quiet. "Twelve years ago. You stood witness when they took a baby out of Aldera Reyes's arms, and you came back to my house and ate my bread."

"Coral—"

"Did you eat my bread?"

"Yes." The word came out of her like a splinter. "Yes, I did."

Elodie stirred in her sleep. Both of them went still until she settled again, and in that stillness Vess understood exactly how far she'd already fallen, because her first thought wasn't *I'm sorry*. It was *don't wake the girl, don't let her hear this, not yet, not here.* Twelve years of protecting a child by lying to everyone around her, and the habit had grown teeth.

"Sit down," Vess said.

"I'll stand."

"Sit *down*, Coral. You'll want to be sitting."

Coral didn't sit. Of course they didn't. So Vess stayed standing too, because some conversations you don't get to have sitting down, and told them everything.

* * *

"It was the year the valley started forgetting," she said. "You remember. You were twenty-three and full of knives and you thought the ward-stones humming wrong was a maintenance problem."

"It was a maintenance problem."

"It was a *symptom*." Vess wrapped her arms around herself. The cold in the dark country got into old wounds; hers ached from hip to shoulder. "The wards weren't failing. They were being *paid out*. Every year the hum got thinner because there was less behind it. The god was spending itself keeping the valley closed, and gods can't spend what they don't have. It needed collateral. Something the court beneath the mountain would accept as interest while the principal came due."

"And that something was a child."

"That something was a child," Vess said. "One soul per generation. That was the original bargain — before either of us was born, before the valley even had a name anyone remembered. The court lends its patience, the valley keeps its magic out, and every generation the debt is serviced. One soul. Sold willingly by the parents, freely accepted by the court, witnessed by one of the godsworn so the terms hold."

"Aldera sold her daughter."

"Aldera saved her *valley*." Vess heard her own voice harden and hated it, but she couldn't stop it; she'd rehearsed this defense for twelve years and it came out polished as armor. "There were two hundred people in that valley, Coral. Two hundred. And the choice was one baby or all of us going magic-mad when the wards finally snapped — you've seen what happens to unwarded places, you've seen the villages east of the pass, the things the people became. Aldera wept for three days and then she signed, and I stood witness because someone godsworn had to, and I would do it again."

"You would do it again."

"In a heartbeat."

Coral moved then — one step, fast, and Vess's hand went to her knife on pure reflex before she registered that Coral had only stepped closer, fists at their sides, shaking. "She was *three days old*. You let me raise her. You sat at my table every winter solstice and watched her blow out candles and you *knew*."

"I knew."

"Why?" The quiet cracked at last, and what came through it was worse than rage — it was bewilderment, raw as an open wound. "Why me? Why give her to *me*? If she was already sold, already paid, why put her in the home of a mercenary with a dead god's mark and enemies on three borders? Why not some farm family who'd never ask questions?"

And there it was. The part Vess had sworn she'd take to her grave, the part that made everything else unforgivable, and she found — standing in the dark with her oldest friend's eyes on her — that she could not lie about it anymore. The dark country didn't allow it. She felt the truth rise in her like water finding a crack.

"Because the terms required it," she said. "A payment has to be raised well. Loved. The court doesn't want damaged goods — a soul taken unwanted comes through thin, sour, half-formed. A soul raised in a good home, secure, cherished—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "A soul raised loved comes through *rich*. The contract specified a foster placement. Someone strong enough to protect her, kind enough to make her feel safe. Someone whose affection would season the asset."

"*Asset.*"

"The word in the contract is 'asset,' yes."

Coral turned away. Walked three paces toward the sleeping girl and stopped, and Vess watched their shoulders heave once, twice, and understood that they were not crying — they were doing arithmetic. She'd seen them do it before battles. Counting costs. Counting what they'd been made complicit in without ever signing anything.

"Every bedtime story," Coral said. "Every meal I cooked. Every time I taught her to hold a knife, to read the sky, to stand up straight when men tried to make her small. None of it was mine. It was *husbandry*."

"Don't you dare." Vess crossed to them and grabbed their arm and spun them around, and this close she could see the god-mark crawling faintly under their skin, restless, hungry, answering the mountain's pull. "Don't you dare turn your love into a crime just because someone else planned to profit from it. You loved that child. Nobody contracted that. Nobody *made* you carry her through fever-nights or spend your whole bounty on a coat she'd grow out of in a season. The court wrote the terms, but you — you were the one thing in her life that was never in any ledger, and if you cheapen that now you're letting them win twice."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Then, very softly: "You could have told me. Any year. Any single year, Vess. You could have told me and we'd have run — you know we'd have run. I'd have burned my mark and carried her over the pass and we'd have lived poor and free and *never come back*."

"And the wards would have snapped within the month, and two hundred people would have gone mad, and the court would have collected her anyway plus penalties besides." Vess released their arm. Her hand was trembling; she made a fist of it. "There was no running, Coral. There never was. That's what you've never understood about debts like these — you don't escape them, you can only decide who pays. I chose. I chose the version where she got twelve years of being loved first. If that makes me a monster, fine. But she's alive, and she's whole, and she knows what it feels like to have a family, and when the court takes her she'll go knowing it was real. I bought her a childhood with my silence. I'd buy it again."

"You keep saying that," Coral whispered. "You keep saying you'd do it again, like it's a virtue."

Behind them, in the dark, a small voice said: "Do what again?"

* * *

Elodie was sitting up.

Neither of them had heard her wake — no rustle, no breath, nothing, as though the dark country had swallowed the sound of her rising just to deliver her to this moment. She stood with the blanket still around her shoulders like a cloak, and her eyes went from Vess to Coral and back, and Vess saw the exact instant the child in her finished the arithmetic adults had been doing for three days.

"Witness to the twelfth payment," Elodie read off the ledger page in Coral's hand. She'd always been able to read upside down; Coral had taught her that, thinking it was a game. "That's me. Isn't it. I'm the twelfth payment."

"Elodie—"

"Is that my name, even?" Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst part, Vess thought. The child had learned steadiness from somewhere, and God help them both, it was from Coral. "Or did you pick it out of the contract too? Was there a section on naming? Did you two sit down and choose something soft so I'd come through *rich*?"

"Your mother named you," Vess said. "Aldera. Before she signed. It's the one thing in the whole arrangement that was free."

Elodie looked at her for a long time. Then at Coral, whose face had gone gray and still as the ward-stones. Then down at her own hands, turning them over, as if checking whether she could see the ledger written on her skin.

"Say the rest," she said to Vess. "All of it. I'm not a child tonight."

So Vess said the rest. About the bargain older than the valley. About the eleven souls before, and the god that had gone silent — devoured, she'd learned, though she'd only confirmed it last week, and the shame of that confirmation still sat in her stomach like a stone. About how the payments kept the wards fed and the valley sane, and how every adult in Coral's household had eaten, drunk, and slept inside the shelter of a child's stolen soul for twelve years without one of them asking where the warmth came from.

When she finished, the silence was total. Even the dark country seemed to lean in.

"You should have told me," Elodie said at last, and her voice cracked after all, twelve years old again, and Vess felt it go through her like a blade through wet leather. "Not Coral. *Me*. It's my soul. Everyone decided things about my soul and nobody asked me, not even once, not even to say goodbye to myself."

"I know."

"Do you?" Elodie's chin came up, and there was something in her face Vess had never seen before — something old moving behind the young eyes, something that had been listening to the mountain sing for days. "You keep saying you'd do it again. Would you? Right now? Knowing what I am, what they're waiting for, what I hear at night when the mountain sings to me?" She took one step forward, blanket sliding off her shoulders. "Would you sell me *again*, Vess?"

And Vess — honest for the first time in twelve years, hollowed out by it, standing in a country with no stars above a child she had helped deliver to a court of quiet teeth — opened her mouth to say *no*, and found that the dark country was listening, and that lies had weight here, and that the truth, whatever else it was, was heavier still.

"No," she said. "Not again. Now that I know her — now that I know *you* — no. I'd burn the valley down first."

Something in Elodie's shoulders loosened a fraction. Not forgiveness. Vess hadn't earned forgiveness, and knew it. But maybe acknowledgment. Maybe the difference between those two things was all she'd get, and all she deserved.

Coral spoke without turning around. "How far to the door?"

"Two days. Maybe three, with the river thawed wrong."

"Then walk ahead of us," Coral said. "You lead. You carry the watches. And every night you look me in the eye and tell me one more thing you never told me, until there's nothing left in you but truth. That's your penance. That's what you owe *me* — and believe me, Vess, I'm keeping a ledger of my own now."

Vess nodded. It was more mercy than she'd expected, and less than she needed, and exactly what she deserved.

She picked up her pack. Ahead, past the black trees, the mountain had begun to show itself — a vast absence against the absent stars, a shape darker than the dark, and from its slopes came a sound that all three of them heard at once: singing, low and patient, in a voice like silk drawn over teeth.

Elodie's head tilted toward it, the way a compass needle finds north.

"It knows we're coming," she said. "It's been setting a place for me."

And far below the mountain's roots, something ancient turned a page in its ledger, and waited.
