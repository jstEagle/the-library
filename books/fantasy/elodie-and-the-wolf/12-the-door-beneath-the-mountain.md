## 12. The Door Beneath the Mountain

The mark woke before dawn, and it did not burn this time. It *pulled*.

Coral had felt the god's mark do many things over fifteen years of service — ache before ambushes, warm when Elodie slept against their shoulder, go cold as river stone on the day the stars went out. But it had never pulled. Now it tugged at the meat of their wrist like a fishhook set deep, steady and patient, a rhythm that said *this way, this way, this way* whether Coral wanted to follow or not.

They followed.

There wasn't really a question anymore. The broker's promise sat signed somewhere in the dark architecture of the world, and every mile they walked toward the mountain made the shape of that promise clearer and worse. Protection. Passage. A debt deferred but not forgiven. Coral had read enough contracts in the chapel ledger to know what "deferred" meant in the language of things beneath mountains. It meant *later*. It meant *interest*.

Elodie walked beside them without being asked to. That was the part that frightened Coral most, some mornings — not the dark country, not the singing mountain, but how naturally the girl had taken to walking through a world with no sun. She'd stopped flinching at the shapes between the trees. She'd started humming back at the mountain when it sang, low and absent, the way you hum along to a song you don't remember learning.

"Stop that," Coral said, for maybe the hundredth time.

Elodie blinked at them, caught. "I wasn't—"

"You were."

She pressed her lips together and looked away, and Coral hated the smallness of their own voice. Twelve years old. Twelve years of raising her on oatmeal and sword-drills and stories about the valley, and now she was tuning herself to the thing that wanted her, note by note, because nobody had told her she shouldn't. Because Coral hadn't known to tell her.

Vess ranged ahead, as she always did now, moving through the black country like a woman reading familiar handwriting. She'd been quiet since the frozen river. Since the confession. Some betrayals didn't get resolved; they just got carried, and both of them carried hers now, one on each end of the same rope.

"The road bends east," Vess called back, not turning. "Mark's pulling you east?"

Coral checked their wrist. The pull had shifted, subtle as a current. "East."

"Then we're close." Something in her voice Coral couldn't name. Not fear exactly. Recognition. "I've been here before," Vess said, when Coral didn't answer. "Years ago. When we sold—" She stopped. Started again. "When the bargain was paid last time. I came down with the collectors. I saw the door."

"And you didn't think to mention that."

"I'm mentioning it now."

* * *

They came down off the ridge as the dark country's eternal night thickened toward something almost solid, and there it was.

The door stood alone in a bowl of bare stone, no wall around it, no structure, nothing to be a door *of*. Just a slab of grey rock taller than three men, rising out of the earth like a tooth, carved from crown to base with a face.

Coral knew the face. Had known it since childhood, since the first oath-swearing, since the priest had held their wrist to the stone and let the mark bite. The Silent God: eyes closed, mouth closed, hands open and empty, the whole expression one of terrible patience. Every ward-stone in the valley bore a miniature of it. Coral had touched it a thousand times without thinking.

But the face on the door was wrong now. A crack ran from the crown of the head down through the closed eyes, splitting the face into halves that no longer quite matched, and from the crack seeped something that was not water and not smoke — a slow black light, if light could be a kind of bleeding. It pooled at the door's base and sank into the stone, and where it sank, the stone was smooth and glassy and faintly warm.

Elodie's hand tightened in theirs. Coral realized they'd reached for her without deciding to.

"That's him," she whispered. "That's who sings."

"No." Coral's voice came out flat and hard. "That's what's left where he used to be. There's a difference, and you're going to remember it."

Even as they said it, they weren't sure they believed it. The pull from their wrist had become a roar of wanting, and standing this close to the door, Coral could feel the mark straining toward the crack like a dog toward its master's corpse. Fifteen years of service. Fifteen years of believing the silence was discipline, was faith, was the god choosing stillness. And all of it had been a dead thing's reflex, a puppet twitching on strings cut long ago.

Vess hung back at the rim of the bowl. "It opens for those who owe," she said. "That's what the collectors told me. You don't knock. You don't call. You stand in front of it and it weighs you. If you owe nothing, it's just rock. If you owe—" She shrugged, a small ugly motion. "You know what you signed."

Coral stepped forward.

The black light rose to meet them. Not fast — nothing about the door was fast — but steadily, climbing the carved robes like a tide coming up a beach, and where it touched Coral's boots they felt it take an inventory. They could feel it happening, item by item, the way a pawnbroker's fingers move across goods laid on a counter. The oath. The mark. The years of patrol and vigil and silence. The signed promise at the crossroads. And underneath all of it, older than any of it, a debt Coral had never known they were carrying, threaded through them like dye through cloth.

*Inherited*, the chapel ledger had said. *One soul per generation. The debt runs with the blood.*

The light reached their knees. Their chest. Their throat.

And then it stopped, recoiled slightly, and turned — Coral felt it turn, the way you feel attention on the back of your neck — toward Elodie.

"No," Coral said aloud, to a door, which was absurd, and meant it completely.

The black light hung between them, quivering, tasting the air where the girl stood. Elodie didn't step back. That was the worst part. She stood very straight, chin up, the way she did when she was scared and refusing to show it, and the mountain's song was audible now even to Coral, threading up through the stone under their feet, and Elodie's lips were moving faintly with it.

"It wants me to come in," she said. Not a question.

"It wants a lot of things." Coral crouched to her height, took both her hands. Her fingers were ice. "Listen to me. Whatever happens in there, whatever you see, whatever offers get made — you hold onto my hand and you do not let go. Not for anything. Not for anyone wearing my face. Not for me telling you to. You hear me? Nothing in there is going to ask nicely twice, and everything in there is going to sound like someone you love."

Elodie swallowed. Her eyes were wet and furious, twelve years old and ancient at once. "What if you let go of *me*?"

The question went through Coral like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. That was what the court below would offer, eventually, in one form or another — a version of events where letting go was mercy, where release was love, where the right thing to do was the easy thing dressed in better clothes.

"I won't," Coral said. "Whatever it costs. I won't."

Behind them, Vess said quietly, "It's weighing you. Both of you. Decide."

Coral stood. Faced the cracked face of the god they'd served, the god that had been devoured while they kept its vigils, the god whose silence they'd mistaken for trust. Black light ran down its cheeks like tears it could not feel.

"We owe," Coral said. "We've always owed. Open."

The door did not swing. Doors swing. This one simply stopped being closed — the stone forgot itself, the carved face dimming like a lamp guttering out, and where the door had been there was a passage sloping down into warmth and darkness and the smell of deep places, old candle-smoke and honey and something underneath both that smelled the way a held breath sounds.

The song swelled. Elodie's grip went slack for half a heartbeat, her body leaning toward it like a plant toward light, and Coral clamped down on her hand hard enough to hurt and felt her come back to herself with a small sharp gasp.

"Ow," she whispered.

"Good. Pain means you're still you."

They looked back once. Vess stood at the rim of the bowl, arms crossed, face unreadable in the black light's glow. She didn't say good luck. She didn't say wait for me. She said, "If you find the archive down there — the real one, not the copies — look for the first entry. The original terms. Everything else is commentary."

"Why?" Coral asked.

"Because I only ever saw the summary," Vess said, "and the summary is why I did what I did. Maybe the full text reads different." She paused. "Or maybe it doesn't, and I'll deserve what's coming either way. Go."

Coral went.

The passage swallowed them by degrees — first their feet, then their shadows, then the last grey edge of the dark country's night. Elodie's hand burned cold in Coral's grip, and the mark on Coral's wrist sang silent harmony with the mountain's song, and behind them the door became stone again, patient and blind, already forgetting them.

Halfway down the slope, where the walls began to sweat black light instead of water, Elodie spoke without stopping, her voice very small in the vastness.

"Coral? What if the door doesn't let us back out?"

Coral thought about lying. Considered it seriously, the way you consider any kindness that costs nothing now and everything later.

"Then we make it," they said, and squeezed her hand, and walked down together into the court of quiet teeth.
