## 13. Court of Quiet Teeth

The door closed behind them like a mouth.

Elodie felt it in her teeth first — a soft, enormous click, the sound of something deciding to keep what it had swallowed. Then the dark of the passage was gone and they were standing in a hall so vast that her eyes kept trying to find its ceiling and failing, sliding upward into a glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, pale as moonlight through milk.

"Coral," she whispered.

"I see it." Coral's hand found hers. Their palm was slick with sweat, but their grip was steady, the way it always was when everything else wasn't. Twelve years of that grip. She knew every version of it: the grip on a fence rail while she learned to climb, the grip on a splintered door during the storm two winters ago, the grip now, tight enough to hurt, which meant *I have you, I have you, I have you.*

The hall stretched out before them in long colonnades of black stone veined with silver, like the inside of a geode the size of a cathedral. The floor was smooth as still water and gave back their reflections — except the reflections were wrong. Elodie's showed her standing straighter, older, wearing something white. Coral's showed them empty-handed, though both their hands were full.

"Don't look down," Coral said quietly.

"It's looking up."

"I know. Don't look down anyway."

They walked. There didn't seem to be anywhere else to walk. The hall simply went on, and the light went with them, and after a while Elodie understood that the light *was* going with them — that the court had lit this place for their benefit, the way you'd set candles for a guest whose comfort mattered very much to you.

That was the first thing she noticed about the court, and she would remember it later as the most frightening thing: they were *hospitable*.

They came out of the colonnades one at a time, or maybe they'd been there all along and only became visible as they chose to be. Tall figures in robes the color of deep water, faces smooth and lovely and slightly unfinished, like sculptures someone had abandoned at the moment of perfection because anything more would be showing off. They did not crowd. They arranged themselves along the walls at a respectful distance, the way courtiers do, and they inclined their heads as Elodie passed, each one, in sequence, like wheat bending under wind.

One of them spoke without opening any mouth Elodie could see.

"The thirteenth payment."

The voice was warm. That was the horror of it. It was warm the way a bath is warm, the way a bed is warm on a morning you don't have to get up. It wrapped around her name — not *Elodie*, some other name, older, heavier — and held it up to the light like a jeweler examining a stone.

"Thirteen generations," said another voice, from the other side, equally warm. "And here is the last. The closing of the account. The final grace note."

"We are so pleased," said a third, "that you came whole."

Elodie stopped walking. Coral stopped with her, and she heard them breathe in — the sharp breath of someone about to put their body between a child and a threat, which was a thing Coral had done maybe a hundred times over twelve years, mostly against dogs and drunk men and once, memorably, a falling chimney.

"She's not a payment," Coral said. "She's a girl. She's my daughter."

The nearest figure turned its lovely unfinished face toward Coral, and Elodie watched something happen in it — not anger, nothing so crude. Interest. The mild, patient interest of a banker hearing an amusing claim.

"Of course she is," the figure said gently. "You raised her beautifully. We've watched. You taught her to read, to swim, to lie convincingly when it matters. You fed her the bitter tea in winter and let her think you didn't hate it yourself. All of this was noted. All of this was *appreciated*." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "You were excellent custodians of our asset."

The word landed in Elodie's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for the splash and never heard it hit bottom.

"Asset," Coral repeated. Their voice had gone flat, which Elodie knew was worse than loud.

"That is what a payment is," the figure said, with the tone of a teacher delighted by a student's question. "A thing held in trust until due. Her mother signed. Her mother wept, if it helps — they usually do — and then her mother slept well that night for the first time in a year, because the bargain was made and the valley would stand and the winter would pass and the magic would stay outside where it belonged. Thirteen times this has happened. Thirteen times a family has been made safer by a single signature." The face tilted. "You benefited too, custodian. Every quiet year. Every ward-stone that hummed instead of screamed. You were paid in peace. You simply weren't told your wages."

Elodie felt Coral's hand tighten around hers and then, slowly, deliberately, loosen — not letting go, but softening, the way you unclench a fist before you can do fine work. She'd seen Coral do that before fights. It scared her more than shouting would have.

"We're leaving," Coral said. "Both of us. Now. And if anyone in this room tries to stop us—"

"No one will stop you," the figure said, and it sounded genuinely puzzled that Coral would think otherwise. "You may walk out that door whenever you wish. It will open for you. It opened for you coming in; it is not a trap." Another pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "But she will remain. And you may return tomorrow, and the day after, and every day of your life, and knock, and ask for her, and we will receive you courteously every time, and the answer will be the same, forever, because it is written in a hand older than the language you're speaking. This is not cruelty, custodian. This is arithmetic."

*Arithmetic.* Elodie turned the word over. Somewhere behind her fear, a cold little part of her mind — the part Coral had trained, the part that counted exits and weighed strangers — noticed that the court hadn't denied anything Coral said. Hadn't argued. Arguing was for things that could lose.

* * *

They were given rooms.

That was the second thing about the court, and it was somehow worse than the first: they were given *rooms*. Beautiful ones. A chamber for Coral with a bed of dark wood and linens softer than anything Elodie had ever touched, and a window that looked out on — she checked three times — a garden that could not exist underground, all roses and rainlight and a sky that was blue in a way no sky had been since the stars went out.

Her own room was next door. It was, she realized within ten steps of crossing the threshold, built for a girl who had lived here her whole life. The shelves held books at exactly her reading level, including three she'd wanted and never told anyone about. The desk had a chair adjusted to her height. On the nightstand sat a wooden horse, carved small and rough, the kind of toy a child makes for themselves, and when she picked it up her thumb fit perfectly into a worn groove on its neck, as if she'd been holding it for years.

She had never seen it before in her life.

She put it down carefully, the way you'd put down something sleeping.

There was a knock — soft, three taps, polite — and one of the tall figures stood in her doorway with a tray. Bread still warm. Honey the color of amber held up to fire. Fruit she didn't recognize, cut into neat crescents. The figure set it on the desk and stepped back and did not leave, exactly, but became less present, the way furniture is present.

"You should eat," it said kindly. "Traveling is hard on the body. Growing is harder. We have watched you grow for thirteen years, little ledger-line. We would not have you arrive at your due date hungry."

"My name is Elodie."

"Yes," it agreed, warmly, agreeably, in the tone of someone humoring a beloved child's game. "That is what they call you. It's a good name. It fits you the way a coat fits — chosen with care, worn with love." Its unfinished face did something that was almost a smile. "We call you by what you are. Both things can be true. Down here, almost everything is true twice."

"What am I?" she asked. "Twice?"

The figure considered her for a long moment. Around it, through the open door, she could see others of its kind moving in the corridor, unhurried, graceful, attending to tasks she couldn't identify. Nothing hurried down here. Nothing needed to. Everything down here had already won.

"You are a daughter," it said. "That is the first true thing. You are loved by a mercenary with a dead god on their wrist who would burn this court down if they could find a match, and that love is real, and we do not dispute it, and we think it has made you strong." It folded its hands. "And you are the thirteenth payment on a contract signed before you could focus your own eyes. That is the second true thing. The first does not cancel the second. It never has. Twelve girls stood in this room before you, and every one of them was somebody's daughter too."

The words went through her like cold water finding every crack.

"There were twelve?"

"Twelve souls, dreaming safe in glass, waiting for the account to close. They are well cared for. We are not monsters, little ledger-line. We honor our debts — that is the entire point of us. Ask your god what happens to those who don't." Something flickered across the smooth face then, fast as a fish under ice, gone before she could name it. "Eat. Rest. Tomorrow we will show you everything, because you deserve to understand what you are, and understanding is a courtesy we extend even to property."

It bowed — actually bowed, low and formal — and withdrew, and the door stayed open, because doors down here didn't need to be locked.

* * *

Coral found her an hour later sitting on the bed with the untouched tray beside her, holding the wooden horse.

They took one look at her face and crossed the room in four strides and sat down next to her, and for a while neither of them said anything. Below them, faintly, through floors of stone, Elodie could hear it again — the singing, the mountain's song she'd been hearing for days, except now she understood it wasn't the mountain at all. It was the court. Humming to itself while it worked. Contented.

"They gave me a room," she said finally. "With my books in it. Books I wanted. How do they know what books I wanted?"

"Because they've been watching." Coral's voice was scraped raw. "Probably since before you were born. Probably the whole time." They rubbed their face with both hands. "Elodie. Listen to me. Whatever they offer you, whatever they show you, however kind they seem — none of it changes what they are. Kindness is just a tool they're better at than most."

"I know."

"Do you? Because they're very good, sweetheart. They've had a long time to get good. They'll make it feel like home in here. They'll make it feel like you were always supposed to—"

"I said I know." It came out sharper than she meant, and she saw Coral flinch, and immediately hated herself for it. She leaned into their shoulder. "Sorry. I'm sorry. I just—" She looked down at the horse in her hands. "Coral. My thumb fits in this groove. Like I've held it a thousand times. Like it's *mine*."

Coral was quiet for a long moment.

"Then that's how they'll get you," they said at last, very softly. "Not with chains. With *already*."

* * *

That night — if it was night; there was no way to tell down here — Elodie lay in the bed that fit her perfectly and did not sleep.

The song was louder now. Close. And underneath it, threaded through it like a darker thread in bright cloth, she could hear the other voices. Twelve of them. Faint and far away and unmistakably *girls*, humming fragments of the same tune, the way children hum in their sleep.

*Tonight,* the song said, or seemed to say, in its warm and endless patience. *Rest tonight, thirteenth payment. Tomorrow we will show you your sisters.*

She stared into the soft milk-light of a ceiling she couldn't find, and her hand crept to the nightstand and closed around the wooden horse, and its groove fit her thumb like an answer to a question she'd never asked.

Tomorrow, then. Let them show her. Let them show her everything — the glass, the sisters, the whole shining courteous machine of them.

Because Coral had taught her that too, along with swimming and lying convincingly: *anything you can see clearly, you can find the crack in.*

Somewhere below her, patient as arithmetic, the court began to sing her to sleep.
