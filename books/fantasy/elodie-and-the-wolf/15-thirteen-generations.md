## 15. Thirteen Generations

The court did not lock her anywhere.

That was the worst part, Elodie decided. She had expected chains, or a cell, or at least a door with a bar across it. Instead they gave her a room with a bed of grey silk and a window that looked out on nothing — not darkness, exactly, but the memory of darkness, the way your eyes kept seeing a candle after it went out. The walls were warm. The floor was soft. Everything in the court beneath the mountain was soft, and patient, and absolutely certain she would stay.

A servant had brought her food that tasted like every good meal she'd ever eaten, all at once, which should have been wonderful and instead made her want to cry. Then it had left — bowed, actually bowed, low and long, the way you'd bow to someone important — and Elodie had stood alone in her beautiful room listening to the mountain sing.

It sang through the stone. Not with sound. With *knowing*. A pressure behind her ears, a tune she could almost name, and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: twelve small lights, waiting.

She didn't know how she knew there were twelve. She just knew, the way she knew her own hands.

*Come see us,* the knowing said. Or maybe she only wanted it to say that. It was getting harder to tell the difference.

So when the door opened — no knock, because nothing here needed to knock — and the tall one stood in it with its face like moonlight poured over water, Elodie was already standing. Already dressed. Already holding her chin up the way Coral had taught her, back when Coral still smiled.

"You wish to see them," the tall one said. Not asked. It read it off her the way you'd read weather off a sky.

"I want to know what I'm the thirteenth *of*."

Something moved beneath the surface of its face. She chose to call it a smile.

"Then come. We are proud of our collection."

* * *

They walked for a long time. Down stairs that hadn't been carved so much as grown, the steps smooth as knucklebones. Through halls where the ceiling dripped slow silver light that never reached the floor. Past doorways she didn't look at, because looking felt like being looked at back.

Elodie counted her breaths the way Coral had taught her, four in, four hold, four out, for fear. *You can't panic and count at the same time,* Coral said once, cleaning blood off a knife by their campfire, entirely matter-of-fact. *Your body only has room for one lie at a time.* She'd been nine. She'd thought it was funny then.

She was thirteen now, and it wasn't funny, and she counted anyway.

"They dream," the tall one said as they descended. "That is what we keep. Not souls in the abstract — souls are difficult to hold, all teeth and direction. We keep the dreaming part. The part that wants." It glanced down at her. "Yours is very loud, little payment. We heard it from the surface. That is rare."

"My name isn't *payment*."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "Your name was sold before you had one. What remains is ours to assign."

Her chest went tight and hot. She counted. Four in. Four hold. Four out. *Coral will come. Coral always comes. Coral traded everything for me and Coral does not lose.*

Except Coral wasn't here, and the last time Elodie had seen them, they were signing something at a crossroads while she watched, and their face had gone strange and far away afterward, like a house with all its shutters closed.

Then the corridor opened, and Elodie stopped counting.

* * *

The room was round and vast and quiet in a way that pressed against her eardrums. And there — floating, each in its own globe of glass no bigger than a lantern — hung twelve small lights.

Not flames. Not stars. They moved like things asleep move: slow turns, small flutters, a light that brightened and dimmed with something like breathing. Each glass sphere hung from nothing, at the height of a person's heart, arranged in a wide ring around the center of the chamber. Twelve lanterns. Thirteen spaces, if you counted the empty place nearest the door, polished and waiting, its pedestal freshly cut.

Hers.

Elodie's legs nearly quit. She locked them. She walked into the middle of the ring instead, because running toward a thing was braver than running from it, and Coral had raised her on that too.

Up close she could see faces in the lights. Faint ones. The way you see a face in smoke if you look too long. A man with his mouth open mid-laugh. A woman whose brow was furrowed like she was working a hard problem even in her sleep. A child — a boy, small, younger than her, curled up tight as a seed.

"Oh," Elodie said. Very quietly. "Oh, you're *people*."

"We are what people leave behind," the tall one said from the doorway, content as a cat. "Look at the first, if you like. She is the oldest. She remembers things the others have forgotten."

"The first?"

"The first payment. Thirteen generations ago." It gestured with one long hand toward the globe at the ring's far side, where the light burned steadier than the rest, older-colored, like candlelight versus daylight. "We do not usually permit introductions. But the collection enjoys visitors, and you will be joining it soon enough. Speak, if you wish. Dreams hear more than the waking ever suspect."

And then it was gone, and the door with it, and Elodie stood alone with twelve sleeping souls and her own hammering heart.

She approached the oldest globe slowly, the way you'd approach a horse you didn't know. Up close, the face inside was a girl's face. Younger than Coral. Maybe sixteen, seventeen when they took her — though *took* wasn't right either, because Coral's ledger had said *sold*, and sold meant someone had chosen it, and Elodie's stomach turned over thinking about who had chosen, thirteen generations ago, to give away a daughter.

Inside the glass, the girl's eyes opened.

Elodie stumbled back a step. The light didn't flare or fade. The girl simply looked at her — brown eyes, ordinary, human, *awake* — and said, in a voice like wind through dry grass:

"You're the new one."

"You're—" Elodie's voice cracked. She started over. "You're awake? They said you dream."

"I dream," the girl said. "I also wake. They don't check anymore. After the fourth generation they stopped checking." Her eyes crinkled, and there — buried under thirteen generations of glass and silence — was humor. Actual humor. "Takes about a hundred years to learn how. You get very patient in here."

"What's your name?"

The girl was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had changed, gone careful, like someone handling something breakable. "Sera. My mother called me Sera. I haven't said it out loud in a hundred and thirty years."

"Sera." Elodie pressed her palm flat against the glass. It was cool, and hummed faintly, like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. "I'm Elodie."

"I know. The mountain told us. It tells us everything; it thinks we're furniture." Sera's gaze drifted past Elodie's shoulder, toward the dark. "You came from the valley."

"Yes. Above. The real world."

"Is the sun still there?"

The question landed wrong somehow — too hungry for how casual it tried to sound. Elodie blinked. "Yes? Of course it—"

"Don't be sorry." Sera's voice gentled. "It's just — I remember it. That's my whole treasure now, one memory they couldn't file away properly because I hid it too deep. The sun. On the morning they took me, I stood in the doorway and the light came across the floor and touched my feet, and I made myself memorize it. Every detail. The color of dust in sunbeams. The warmth on the tops of my feet. I was so angry I forgot to be scared." Her laugh was small and cracked. "Thirteen generations of payments, and I'm the only one who kept the sun."

The other globes had begun to glow brighter. Slowly, one by one, they drifted closer — not fast, dream-slow, but unmistakably closer. Faces turning toward her. Toward the conversation. Toward the word *sun*.

"They can hear me?" Elodie whispered.

"They always hear you. They just can't answer." Sera's expression flickered with something old and sad. "The court takes the wanting first. That's why we dream. A soul without wanting doesn't fight. Doesn't grieve. Just... drifts, and glows, and keeps the bargain warm." Her eyes fixed on Elodie again, and there was iron in them now, sudden and total. "But you. You came down here *wanting*. I can feel it from in here. It's pouring off you like heat off a stone. Do you know how long it's been since anything new wanted anything in this room?"

"How long?"

"Since they put me in."

* * *

Elodie sat down on the cold floor, cross-legged, in the middle of the ring of lights, and Sera told her things.

She told her about the first bargain — not the version in Coral's ledger, but the true one, passed soul to soul down thirteen generations of dreaming. About the valley dying by inches, crops failing, children sickening, and the thing beneath the mountain offering terms. About a god who agreed to stand surety for the debt — *one soul per generation, freely surrendered, and the valley lives* — and about how the god had grown old and tired and finally, quietly, stopped answering prayers, and how the court had eaten it for defaulting, and how nobody above had even noticed, because gods die the way banks fail: slowly, then all at once, and always with paperwork.

"It's still here," Sera said. "What's left of it. Did you meet the broker? Masked thing, smells like ozone and old incense?"

"Coral signed with it. At the crossroads."

"That's it. That's the god's remnant, wearing a broker's mask. It survived its own death by hiding inside the debt." Sera's voice dropped. "It will offer your mercenary a way out, eventually. It always does. It offered me one, once. All I had to do was stop asking questions and let the thirteenth generation arrive on schedule." Her jaw tightened inside the glass. "I said no."

"Why?"

"Because I was the first. Because I remember the sun. And because—" Sera paused, and Elodie watched her gather something, the way you'd gather a dress hem before stepping into deep water. "Because the contract has a flaw. There's always a flaw; courts love their rules more than they love their power. The original terms named a debtor. One specific soul, named in ink, thirteen generations ago. The whole edifice stands on that signature."

"And if the name were... wrong? Spoken wrong? Unspoken?"

"If the true first name were spoken aloud in this room, by a voice the contract doesn't recognize—" Sera's light flickered, quick as a skipped heartbeat, "—the terms void. All of them. Every soul returns. But the name was struck from every record the moment the ink dried. The court made certain no one living remembered it. Even I've lost most of it. I remember *that* there was a name. I remember it began with an *A*. And I remember—" her voice went thin, straining against thirteen generations of filing-away, "—I remember it sounded like *my own name, sung backward*."

Elodie sat very still.

"Ashen," she said, without deciding to.

The room changed.

Every globe flared at once — twelve lights blazing white-hot, and the mountain's song *stuttered*, a missed note, a caught breath, somewhere far above them in the dark. The floor shuddered once, gently, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

When the light settled, Sera was staring at her with her mouth open.

"What did you just say?"

"I don't—" Elodie's own voice sounded far away. "It just came out. Like I already knew it. Like I've *always* known it—"

"The song," Sera breathed. "Oh, clever, cruel mountain. It's been singing to you since the border. Teaching you. Feeding you pieces." Her eyes were wide and burning. "Don't you understand? The court didn't sell you a name at birth, child. They sold you *before* you were born. Before your mother ever held you. They shaped the bargain around you — around what you'd become. You weren't the thirteenth payment. You were the *thirteenth key*."

The words went through Elodie like cold water. Key. Payment. Sold twice — once by whoever signed for her, once by whatever wrote her into the world.

But underneath the fear, something else was kindling. Small. Hot. Stubborn as a coal.

She looked around the ring — at the laughing man, the frowning woman, the boy curled like a seed. At Sera, who had hidden the sun in the deepest room of herself and kept it lit for a hundred and thirty years.

"I'm going to get you out," Elodie said. "All of you. Every single one. I don't know how yet, but the contract has a flaw, and I'm the flaw, and I promise—" her voice shook and she let it, "—I promise I'll free you or die trying."

The twelve lights rose.

Not drifting this time — *rising*, all together, the whole ring lifting a handspan, and the hum in the glass shifted pitch, and for the first time in thirteen generations, the dreaming souls made a sound.

It wasn't words. It was better than words. It was twelve voices saying *yes* in the only language they had left.

Sera pressed her palm to the inside of her globe, matching Elodie's hand through the glass, and her smile was the first sunrise Elodie had ever seen underground.

"Now," Sera said softly, as the far door began to open, and the tall one's silhouette spilled long and patient across the floor, "they'll have felt that. Whatever happens next, little sister — *don't tell them what you know*."

The door opened. The tall one smiled its moonlit smile.

"Dinner is served," it said. "And afterwards — the court wishes to discuss the schedule. Your collection date has been... moved up."
