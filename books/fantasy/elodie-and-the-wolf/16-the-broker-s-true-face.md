## 16. The Broker's True Face

The broker came to Coral in the dark between the archive and the throne room, in a corridor where the walls were carved from something that might have been bone or might have been patience.

Coral had been walking for hours. Or minutes. Time below the mountain moved like water finding its level — it went where it was needed and pooled where it wasn't. The broker's mark on their palm had grown warm as they left the ledger hall, warm enough to feel like guidance, and Coral had learned by now that warmth in this place was never accidental.

The corridor opened into a chamber that had no business existing inside a mountain. It was small — smaller than Coral's first rented room in Ashfall, back when they were nineteen and still believed mercenary work would be temporary. A table. Two chairs. A single candle burning in a dish of black glass. Through a window that shouldn't have been there, the valley was visible far below: dark, wardless, snow-covered, impossibly distant.

"Sit," the broker said.

Coral sat. Not because they wanted to. Because their legs made the decision without consulting them, and because some animal part of them recognized what this room was. They'd seen its like before — in the tents of generals negotiating surrender terms, in the offices of magistrates who decided which villages burned and which were merely emptied. A room built for one purpose: to make the person sitting across from you feel like the negotiation was already over.

"You've read the ledger," the broker said. The mask caught the candlelight — porcelain-white, featureless except for two slits of darkness where eyes should be. "All of it."

"The parts I could stomach."

"And the part about yourself?"

Coral said nothing. Their name had been in that book, inked in the same patient hand as every other entry. *Coral Ashgrove, sworn servant, collateral against default.* They'd known, in the abstract way you know your own death, that the mark on their wrist bound them somewhere. They'd just never imagined *here* — never imagined that twelve years of faithful service to a silent god had been service to a creditor all along.

"I want to know what you are," Coral said. "Not what you do. What you *are*. You knew my name before I signed. You knew Elodie's face when you found us at the ward-line. Nobody knows those things unless they've been watching for a very long time."

The broker's head tilted. For a moment the mask seemed less like a mask and more like a face that had forgotten how to move.

"You served it," Coral pressed. "You speak like someone who loved it once. Who is under that porcelain?"

Silence stretched. The candle flame didn't flicker, which was somehow worse than if it had.

Then the broker reached up, gripped the edge of the mask, and lifted it away.

* * *

There was no face beneath. There was a *wound*.

That was the closest language Coral had for it — a hollow shaped like everything the god's chapel had once been. Coral saw it whole for a half-second: the vast gray presence that had spoken into their wrist at seventeen, the warmth of the oath, the sense of being held by something enormous and indifferent and, in its way, kind. And threaded through that shape, everywhere, ran cracks filled with debt. Black script crawling through the wound like veins through marble. The god hadn't died cleanly. It had been *unmade into owed money*.

Coral's chair scraped back. Their hand went to a knife that wasn't there — they'd surrendered their weapons at the door, along with their pride and most of their assumptions.

"Don't," the remnant said. Its voice, unmasked, was the voice from the wrist-mark. Coral's whole body reacted before their mind could — twelve years of obedience flooding up like bile. "I am not going to hurt you, Coral. I am the only thing in this entire mountain that won't."

"You're dead." Coral's voice came out flat. "Vess said you were devoured. The ledger said you defaulted. Which—"

"Both true. Sit down. Please." The remnant set the mask on the table with the care of someone handling an old injury. Without it, the candlelight bent strangely around the hollow where its face should have been. "The court does not kill its debtors. Killing ends the account. Instead it devours — takes everything a soul is and converts it to principal. When I defaulted, thirteen generations ago, that was my punishment. I became part of the sum owed. Everything I was, spread across the balance sheet."

"But you're here."

"A rounding error." Something that might have been humor moved through the wound-voice, dry and awful. "A fraction of me was too entangled with the original contract to be consumed. The contract needed an administrator. Someone who understood its terms. So the smallest piece of what I was was peeled off and given work." The remnant gestured at itself, at the mask, at the little room with its impossible window. "For thirteen generations I have served the court that ate me. I find debtors. I offer bargains. I collect. And every year I remember slightly less of why any of it mattered."

Coral stared. The mark on their wrist felt suddenly obscene — twelve years of prayers sent down a line to a thing that no longer existed, devotion poured into a grave. "The valley worshipped you. I *worshipped* you. Children were named after you."

"Yes."

"People died keeping your wards lit."

"Yes."

"And the whole time—" Coral's hands curled into fists on the table. "The whole time you were the reason the wards existed at all. The bargain was yours. The debt was yours. We built our whole lives inside the shadow of what you *owed*."

"We did." No deflection. No excuse. Somehow that was worse. "And now I am going to offer you the only honest bargain anyone has offered you in this mountain, and you are going to hate me more for it than for everything else combined. So let me say the rest first."

The remnant leaned forward. Where its face should have reflected light, there was only depth.

"Elodie will be collected tomorrow night, at the rising of the last star's remnant. That is fixed. The ritual has begun; the court has waited thirteen generations and will not wait longer. She will sit on a chair not unlike this one, and she will be devoured as I was, and her soul will keep the court fed for another hundred years, and the stars will stay dark, and the world above will grow stranger and colder until there is nothing left worth ruling. That is the future as it stands. I have watched it happen twelve times. I know every detail. Do not ask me for details. You will imagine them anyway, and your imagination will be kinder than the truth."

"Then tell me the alternative," Coral said. "Because I know you didn't bring me here to describe her death. You brought me here because you have something to sell."

* * *

The remnant was quiet for a long moment. When it spoke again, the voice had changed — lower, rougher, stripped of the broker's polish. Closer, Coral realized with a sick lurch, to the voice that had first spoken the oath into their seventeen-year-old wrist. The voice they'd trusted.

"When the court devoured me, it made an error. Debts require a debtor. It took everything I was except the part that *owed* — and the part that owes cannot be consumed while the obligation stands. So long as the contract lives, so long as the court holds accounts, there must exist a fragment of me to administer them. I am immortal the way a stain is permanent." The wound-shape shifted, restless. "But obligations can be *transferred*. Freely assumed, freely released — that is the oldest law in this place, older than the court, older than the stars. If another soul willingly takes up my debt — all of it, the whole thirteen-generation principal, the administrative burden, the binding — then I am released. And the taker becomes the court's instrument in my place."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"*No.*" Coral was standing now, palms flat on the table, close enough to see the black script crawling through the remnant's hollow like insects through rot. "You're asking me to become you. To put on the mask. To spend eternity finding children and selling them to the thing that ate you."

"I'm asking you to survive."

"I don't *want*—"

"Listen to me." The voice cracked open, and underneath it Coral heard something vast and grieving, the sound a mountain would make if it wept. "Tomorrow night the court collects Elodie. Nothing stops that. Nothing *can* stop that — not Vess and her suicidal mercenaries, not your knives, not the girl's own courage, which I grant is considerable. The collection happens. The only question is what happens after. Option one: you watch. You live — the court has no further use for you once the payment clears — and you carry her absence out of this mountain and up into the valley and you live with it for whatever years remain to you, which will feel like exactly as long as they need to feel. Option two: you die trying to stop it, and she dies anyway, and the last thing she sees is your failure. Option three—"

"There is no option three."

"Option three." The remnant rose. Without the mask, without the shape of a person, its movement was wrong — like watching a hole in the world stand up. It drifted around the table until it stood beside Coral's chair, close enough that the cold of it touched Coral's skin. "Option three, you take my place. The debt transfers. The court gains its new administrator and loses its appetite for the old arrangement — a willing soul freely given is worth more than a sold child's, yes, but a *bound* soul, contracted, permanent, is worth more than both. They will release Elodie. They will release the twelve. Every glass soul in that vault walks out of this mountain free, because the account that held them closes, and a new one opens — yours. And you spend forever down here in the dark, wearing the mask, making the bargains, handing the court its payments generation after generation, remembering just enough of what you lost to understand what you did."

The candle flame finally, softly, guttered.

"And Elodie?" Coral's voice came out very small.

"Goes free." The remnant's tone gentled into something almost cruel in its kindness. "Grows up. Marries or doesn't, fights or doesn't, forgets you slowly or remembers you always — that part isn't in the contract. She goes home to a valley with no god and no wards and no one left who sold her, and she lives the life that was stolen from her. Isn't that what you wanted? From the first night, at the root cellar, with the thing calling her name — hasn't every choice you've made since been about her survival?"

"Yes." Coral's throat ached. "But not like this."

"There is no version of this that isn't like this." The remnant crouched, bringing the wound of its face level with Coral's eyes. This close, Coral could see movement in the black script — letters, endless columns of them, the whole architecture of a debt written in flesh-that-wasn't-flesh. "That is the lesson of this place, Coral. I have had thirteen generations to learn it. Survival is never clean. Someone pays, always. The only mercy available is choosing *who*. I chose the valley. Thirteen generations of children bought that valley its quiet centuries, and I told myself it was worth it, right up until the day the court called in my portion and I learned exactly how the arithmetic felt from the other side." A pause. "You are better than I was. I have watched you for twelve years. I chose you for the oath because you were better. Which means you may succeed where I failed — you may hold the debt without becoming it. Or you may not. But Elodie will be alive either way, and you will be the one who decides whether that's enough."

Coral closed their eyes.

And there it was — the temptation, laid out with surgical precision, sharpened to a knife and held against everything soft in them. Because the terrible thing, the unforgivable thing, the thing Coral would never say aloud in any room with witnesses, was that some part of them *wanted it*. Wanted a bargain where Elodie lived. Wanted a price they alone could pay. Twelve years of loving a child who was never legally theirs, and here at last was the transaction that fit the shape of their heart: everything for her, nothing held back, no one else bleeding on the frozen river for their mistakes. No Vess spending old debts in blood. No Elodie standing in a throne room speaking names she shouldn't know. Just Coral, and the dark, and forever.

It would even be easy. That was the horror of it. The remnant had built this room — the smallness, the candle, the window onto the valley — to make eternity feel negotiable.

"Coral." The wound-voice, very quietly. "The transfer requires only your word. Freely given. Say *I take the debt*, and it is done, and she sleeps tonight in a bed instead of a vault."

Coral opened their eyes.

"I need to see her," they said. "Before I answer. I need—" Their voice broke and they hated it and kept going anyway. "I need to look at her one time and know what I'm buying. Is she awake? Can I see her?"

Something moved behind the hollow of the remnant's face — regret, maybe, or envy, or the memory of a love that had also once been asked to weigh itself against arithmetic.

"She's in the vault with the others," it said. "The court allows visitors to the payment, in these final hours. They find it... improves the flavor."

Coral pushed back from the table so fast the chair fell.

"One condition." They were at the doorway before they turned, and when they turned, they pointed at the mask lying on the table like a shed skin. "If I take your debt — *if* — I burn this first. Whatever you were under it, whatever the court made you into, I'm not inheriting your face."

The remnant was silent. Then, softly, in the voice of something that had once been worthy of worship:

"That, Coral Ashgrove, is exactly why I chose you."

They walked. Down corridors of bone and patience, past doors that breathed, toward the vault where twelve dreaming souls hung in glass and the thirteenth waited — and behind them, faint as a dying star, Coral heard the mountain begin, very softly, to sing.

The collection had started early.

Somewhere above, the last star's remnant was guttering out ahead of schedule, and every clock in the court began to run.
