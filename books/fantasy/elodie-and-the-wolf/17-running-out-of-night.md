## 17. Running Out of Night

### Coral

The ritual began without warning, the way all the worst things in Coral's life had begun.

One moment they were running through corridors of black stone that breathed like a sleeping animal; the next, every vein of light in the walls guttered at once — a whole city's worth of candles caught in one long inhale. The dark didn't fall so much as lean in.

Elodie's hand tightened around theirs. "What was that?"

"The collection," Coral said. "It's starting early."

They had come down through the door beneath the mountain three days ago, or three years — time below moved like water finding its level — and every hour since, the court had been patient with them in a way that made Coral's skin crawl. Courteous things offering them rooms. Offering them food. Offering Elodie gowns cut for a girl twice her age and asking, so gently, whether she wouldn't like to see where she'd have lived, if her mother hadn't been poor.

Now the patience was over.

The corridor ahead opened into a vaulted hall where twelve glass vessels sat on twelve pedestals arranged in a ring, each holding a curl of light that might once have been a soul. Thirteenth place stood empty. Waiting. Above it, suspended in nothing, hung a shard of sky — the last remnant of stars the court had hoarded like a miser's coin, and even now it was dimming, its pinpricks going out one by one like windows going dark in a dying town.

Coral counted the lights inside it. Eleven. When they'd first seen the shard there had been more. They couldn't remember how many more, and that frightened them worse than anything else.

"It's eating the sky to pay for itself," they said. "The bargain's failing, and it's spending everything it has left."

"Then we run." Elodie's voice was steady. Twelve years old, sold before she could walk, raised by a mercenary who never told her what she was — and her voice was steadier than Coral's own heartbeat. "You said the broker's mark opens doors."

"The broker's mark opens *its* doors." Coral pressed their wrist, where the god-mark had burned itself into something new since they'd signed at the crossroads: two brands overlaid, the old silent god's hook and the broker's seal, scar tissue arguing with scar tissue. "The court's doors are another matter."

Behind them, far up the throat of the mountain, came a sound like a bell being struck underwater. Then another. Then many, many more.

Elodie turned. "That's not them."

"No," Coral said, because they knew those bells. They'd marched to them for twenty years. Ward-bells. Valley bells. Bells that had no business ringing a hundred miles under the earth.

### Vess

Vess had led eleven people into hell, and she'd done it by telling them it was a rescue.

Which, to be fair, it was. It just wasn't only that.

The godsworn had come to her in the valley's ruins — Ashka with her river-frozen hands still bandaged from where Coral had broken them on the ice, Brannoc who'd lost two brothers to the wards' collapse, the twins who spoke only to each other now. They'd believed Coral stole the magic. Some of them still did. But the mountain had swallowed the valley's children's names out of the baptismal rolls, and the dead god's chapels had gone cold as hearths in summer, and when Vess told them the truth — *the god is devoured, the debt is real, and the thirteenth payment is a child we all watched grow up* — hatred curdled into something more useful.

Purpose. And underneath it, guilt. She could work with guilt. Guilt was just loyalty with nowhere to put itself.

"Say it again," Ashka said as they descended the last stair, the one the broker-god's remnant had shown Vess in a dream — because the broken god still owed Vess a debt from a winter neither of them talked about, and debts were the only currency down here.

"The court collects souls on contract," Vess said. "Twelve taken, one per generation. The thirteenth is due tonight, early, because the bargain's collapsing. While the collection runs, the court's attention is on the ritual. Everything it has goes into holding the sky-shard together and pulling the girl's soul loose."

"And us?"

"We are a diversion." Vess checked the edge of her blade. Godsworn steel, quenched in chapel-water that no longer held any god — which meant it was just steel now, but steel sharpened on grief cuts deeper than most blessed things. "We make noise. We break things. We give Coral and the girl a road that isn't guarded."

Brannoc spat. "And how many of us walk back out?"

Vess looked at him honestly, because he deserved that much. "Some. Maybe. If we're fast and lucky and the court decides we're cheaper to ignore than to kill."

"You've done the arithmetic," Ashka said quietly.

"I always do the arithmetic." Vess touched the old scar on her forearm, where she'd sworn herself to a god that turned out to be a debtor wearing divinity like a borrowed coat. "I did it twelve years ago too, when I signed the papers that sold Elodie Reyes to this place. I bought the valley forty years of quiet with a baby I helped deliver. Tonight I'm spending the change."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part. They all understood debts here.

The bells rang louder as they came down into the mountain's root-halls, and Vess felt the sound in her teeth, in the oath-scar, in the place behind her ribs where loyalty and self-preservation had been fighting a slow war for twenty years and loyalty had finally, finally won on points.

"Godsworn," she said, and eleven faces turned to her. "Last chance to be sensible."

Ashka smiled with her split lip. "Sensible died with the stars."

They went down ringing like their own funeral.

* * *

### Elodie

Elodie heard the bells before anyone told her what they meant, and she knew — the way she knew the mountain's song, the way she knew the taste of her own name in the court's mouth — that these were bells from home.

"Someone's coming," she said. "From the valley."

Coral's face did something complicated. "Who?"

"I don't know. But they're singing the ward-march." Elodie had heard it a thousand times through the shutters of their house, the mercenaries' low cadence on patrol nights, the song that meant *someone is watching over you while you sleep.* "They came for us."

"They came to die," Coral said, but their voice cracked on it, and Elodie understood suddenly that her parent — because that's what Coral was, whatever the ledgers said about blood and contracts — was doing the arithmetic too, and hating every number.

The court felt it too. The beautiful things materialized along the edges of the ritual hall like frost forming on glass, and their courtesy had changed texture. Still gentle. No longer patient.

"The thirteenth payment should rest," said the one called Lady Meridian, whose face was everyone's face a half-second after you looked away from it. "The working requires calm."

"The working requires my soul," Elodie said. "I know. You've been very clear."

She had stopped being afraid somewhere around the twelfth glass vessel, when the first debtor — the girl who remembered the sun — had pressed her light against the glass and whispered *your name isn't theirs, it's only borrowed, names can be given back.* Since then Elodie had been carrying something new under her ribs, small and hard as a seed. She hadn't told Coral. She hadn't told anyone. Some things you plant in secret or they don't take.

But the bells were getting closer, and the seed stirred.

"Coral," she said. "When they get here — whoever they are — don't try to save me first."

Coral looked at her like she'd spoken in a language they'd forgotten. "What?"

"Save the twelve." She nodded at the ring of glass. "I mean it. I've talked to them. They've been dreaming in there for six hundred years, and I promised—" Her voice caught. She hadn't meant to say that part. "I promised the first one."

"Promised her what?"

"That either we all walk out, or none of us do."

Above the empty thirteenth pedestal, the sky-shard flickered. Another star went out. Ten now. The court's Lady Meridian turned her borrowed face toward the sound of the bells, and for the first time since Elodie had met her, something crossed it that wasn't serenity.

It looked almost like hunger. Or fear. From the outside, Elodie thought, they were probably the same thing.

* * *

### Coral

They came through the far wall of the hall the way an avalanche comes through a village — Vess first, blade drawn, eleven godsworn behind her in a wedge formation Coral had drilled beside her for a decade, back when the world had stars and rules.

"Vess." Coral's voice came out flat. The last time they'd stood face to face, Vess had confessed to selling Elodie, and Coral had put a knife to her throat and meant it, and the only reason neither of them bled was Elodie's hand on Coral's wrist. "You brought them *here*."

"I brought them where they chose to go." Vess backed toward the ring of pedestals, buying ground, buying seconds. "Court's busy. Collection's live. Every servant it has is pulling the girl's soul loose right now, which means nobody's watching the doors. Go. Now. Take her and run."

"And you?"

"We'll hold the hall." Ashka stepped up beside Vess, bandaged hands wrapped around a chapel-cold sword. "As long as it takes."

"It'll take forever," Coral said. "You know that. There's no *out* that doesn't go through the throne."

"Then we hold forever," Brannoc said, "or until forever gets bored."

The court's servants poured in from the side galleries — tall, courteous shapes with too many joints, moving with the unhurried grace of things that had never once needed to hurry. The first exchange of blades sounded like a bell breaking. One of the twins went down screaming, and the other made a sound Coral would hear for the rest of their life.

"Vess!" Coral shoved Elodie behind them and waded in, because twenty years of muscle memory doesn't care about arithmetic. Back to back with the woman who'd betrayed them, cutting down things that apologized politely as they died. "Why? After what you did — why spend yourself on *this*?"

Vess parried, pivoted, took a grey hand across the shoulder and didn't even grunt. "Because I owed her!" She jerked her chin at Elodie, who was — Coral realized with a lurch — not hiding. She was walking the ring of pedestals, laying her palm flat against each vessel, whispering to the lights inside. "Twelve years I owed her! You think I slept? You think a single night went by that I didn't do the sums? Forty years of quiet for one child — I told myself it was good math, Coral, I told myself until I believed it, and then the stars went out and the math came due and it turns out *there is no number*, there was never a number, I just wanted there to be!"

A servant of the court closed its hands around her sword-arm. Vess twisted, drove her dagger up under its jaw, and it came apart like smoke apologizing.

"So yes," she panted, bleeding, grinning like the mercenary Coral had loved before either of them knew what their oaths cost. "Old wounds don't heal. But old debts can be spent. Consider this account settled."

"It's not settled," Coral snarled, cutting down the thing reaching for Vess's blind side. "You don't get to settle it by *dying*—"

"Everyone settles eventually." Vess slammed her shoulder into the next attacker. "Question is what you buy with the payment. Go! The shard's nearly spent — look at it!"

Coral looked. The sky-shard above the thirteenth pedestal was down to nine stars, eight, seven — and with each star that died, the court's servants faltered, flickered, like puppets whose strings ran up into a failing sky. The collection was consuming the court's own power. The ritual was eating itself.

And Elodie stood in the center of the ring with both arms raised, and the twelve lights in the twelve vessels were leaning toward her like flowers toward a window.

* * *

### Elodie

The song was deafening now. Not the mountain's song — *hers*. The one the first debtor had taught her through the glass, note by note across three days of whispers: the name of the first soul ever taken, the true name, the one struck from every ledger because a court cannot be bound by a debtor it refuses to remember.

She wasn't supposed to use it yet. The first girl had been clear. *Wait until the contract is in your hands. Wait until you can speak it against the paper.*

But the bells were ringing, and Vess was bleeding, and one of the twins wasn't moving anymore, and Coral was fighting with everything they had and it wasn't going to be enough, because the court was turning from its failing ritual toward the intruders with the slow certainty of a tide.

Lady Meridian appeared in front of Elodie, blocking the ring, her borrowed face wearing Coral's expression the way a thief wears a coat.

"You should rest, little payment," she said.

"I'm not a payment." Elodie's voice didn't shake. The seed under her ribs had become a coal. "I'm a creditor. You took twelve souls on a contract signed by a god who defaulted, and defaulted gods void their own terms — you just never let anyone read the fine print. Well." She looked past the lady, at the archive doors across the hall, at the ledger that Coral had found once and would find again. "I've read it."

Meridian's smile thinned. "Child. The collection is beginning. Your soul is already half-loosed. You feel it, don't you? Like a thread being drawn."

Elodie did feel it — a soft tugging behind her navel, gentle as sleep. And she was tired. Gods, she was tired, and the court was warm, and the thread pulled toward a place where nothing would ever hurt again.

Then she heard Coral shout her name, and heard Vess laugh wildly somewhere in the smoke, and heard the twin who still lived begin to sing the ward-march through her tears, off-key and defiant, and Elodie planted her feet.

"When my — when *Coral* gets here," she said, "you're going to wish you'd collected me while I was still polite."

Above them, the seventh star went out. Six remained. And down the shattered length of the hall, cutting through the court's servants like a debt coming due, Coral and Vess fought their way toward her side by side — not forgiven, not yet, maybe not ever — but together, which tonight was worth more than forgiveness.

The thread around Elodie's soul pulled tighter.

The last stars began to die.

And in the archive, unattended, the great ledger of the court fell open by itself to the first page — to a name written in a dead god's hand, waiting six hundred years for someone brave enough, angry enough, young enough to say it out loud.
