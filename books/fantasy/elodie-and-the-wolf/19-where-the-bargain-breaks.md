## 19. Where the Bargain Breaks

Elodie had never seen a soul leave a body before.

She had imagined it would look like smoke, or light, or something gentle — the way the stories said it happened when old people died in their beds with their hands folded. It was none of those things. What came out of Coral came out like thread being pulled from cloth, slow and silver and *unwilling*, and it made a sound she felt through her teeth more than her ears: a long, low note that bent downward as it stretched, the way a rope groans before it parts.

Coral knelt on the black glass floor of the court's great hall, and they were not fighting it. That was the worst part. They had offered. They had knelt down on purpose, in front of the throne of quiet teeth, and said the words, and now the court was collecting, and Coral was letting them, because the alternative was Elodie.

*Don't watch,* Coral had told her. *Look at me instead.*

So Elodie looked at them. At the gray creeping into their face from the edges inward, like frost forming on a window. At their hands, flat against the floor, fingers spread, holding on to nothing. At their eyes, which stayed open and stayed fixed on hers because Coral Reyes did not look away from things, not ever, not even from this.

The thirteenth payment sat untouched in its cradle of bone-white silk beside the throne. The court had accepted Coral's soul in its place — a willing soul, freely given, worth more than a sold child's — and the ritual was already half done. Around the hall, the beautiful patient things stood in their perfect rows, watching with expressions that were almost kindness. Almost.

And above everything, through the wound in the ceiling where the mountain opened onto the sky, the last remnants of the stars guttered like candles in wind.

Elodie stood six feet away and could do nothing.

That was what she kept coming back to, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Six feet. She could have crossed it in two steps. She could have thrown herself between Coral and the thing drawing them out, and it would have changed nothing, because you cannot block a debt with your body. She had learned that in this hall. Debts went through flesh. Debts went through love. Debts went through twelve generations of girls who had each been told, in one way or another, that there was nothing to be done.

But debts were written down. And written things could be read. And read things —

She didn't finish the thought yet. She held it, small and hot, in the place behind her ribs where the mountain's song used to live.

* * *

The contract hung in the air above the throne.

She had seen it first when they'd come down into the court, days ago or hours ago — time worked strangely here, where night never lifted — a scroll of pale skin-colored vellum, unrolled and suspended in nothing, turning slowly so that all the court could admire it. Thirteen names in thirteen different hands, each entry older than the last, each sealed with a drop of something dark. The bargain that kept the valley safe and the stars fed and the court beneath the mountain alive. One soul per generation, owed and paid, owed and paid, for longer than anyone living could remember.

The court kept it displayed the way other courts kept trophies. Openly. Proudly. Because no one had ever challenged it, and because things that are never challenged forget they can be broken.

Elodie had spent the whole journey here learning to stop telling Coral what she saw. The song in her sleep had shown her fragments — her mother's face, worn smooth by the same valley wind; a table; a pen; a price — and she had swallowed every one of them, because saying them out loud would have broken Coral open, and Coral needed to be whole to walk them through the dark country alive. She had hated the silence. She had carried it like a stone. But she had also learned something in the carrying: that she could hold terrible knowledge without shattering, and that knowledge held quietly was still knowledge, and that it waited.

It was waiting now.

The first debtor's name was on the scroll. Not the name the court used — the court called her *the First Payment*, the way you'd call a coin by its denomination — but her true name, the one her mother gave her before the ledger took her, the one that had been struck through in ink so old it had gone brown. Struck through, but not erased. Ink lies over writing. It doesn't remove it.

The girl in the glass had given it to her. Three nights ago, in the vault where the twelve souls slept in their vessels of dreaming crystal, Elodie had pressed her palm to the oldest sphere and the girl inside had woken just enough to speak. *I remember the sun,* she'd said. *I remember my name.* And then she had said it, slowly, in a language older than the valley, older than the wards, older than the god whose face was carved on the door a thousand feet above them — and Elodie, who had grown up in a valley where magic couldn't follow, who had never been taught a single word of the old tongue, had understood it anyway. Some names don't need translating. They go straight into the bone.

*Ilsabet.*

She had carried it up out of the vault in silence, past the courteous guards, past the broker with its cracked mask, back to Coral's side, and she had said nothing, because there had been nothing yet to say. A name was just a name until you knew what it was for.

Now she knew what it was for.

* * *

The pulling continued. Coral's outline had begun to blur at the shoulders, the way a candle flame smears when someone moves too fast past it. Their breathing had gone shallow and strange, in-and-out at the wrong rhythm, as if the body was forgetting what it was for without the soul to remind it.

One of the court stepped forward — the tall one, the one who had greeted Elodie as *the thirteenth payment* with a bow so graceful it looked like an apology — and spoke in its voice like water over stones.

"It is nearly complete," it said. "You have witnessed a rare mercy, child. Few debtors are permitted to see a substitution. Your guardian's soul will keep the court another lifetime. Yours will keep it the one after — unless, of course, another offers. The mathematics are elegant. You need not fear them."

"Elegant," Elodie repeated. Her voice came out flat and young and twelve years old, and she let it. "You think this is elegant."

"I think it is *order*," the court-thing said, and around it, the others inclined their heads in perfect unison, a field of flowers bending in a wind that wasn't there.

Order. She turned the word over. Order was what the valley had been, before the stars went out — the wards humming, the patrols walking, everyone safe inside a circle drawn by someone else's suffering. Order was twelve girls in twelve spheres. Order was a contract hanging in the air like a decoration, admired by monsters.

And order, she thought, looking at the scroll, was *written*. Written by hands. Hands that could be shaken. Pens that could run dry. Ink that could fade.

The court had one law older than all its courtesies, one rule it had never had occasion to break because no one had ever found the crack in it: a bargain stands while its terms stand. The terms were the words. The words were on the scroll. The scroll was right there.

Right there, above the throne, turning slowly, close enough to touch if you stood on the dais steps, which no one had ever done, because the throne's presence forbade it — but the throne was busy. The throne was drinking. She could feel it, the vast slow pull of it, attention narrowed to a point, all that ancient patience focused down to a single silver thread and the kneeling body it came from. For the first time since she'd arrived below the mountain, the court was not watching her.

They were watching Coral.

*Don't watch,* Coral had said. *Look at me instead.*

"I'm sorry," Elodie whispered, and she wasn't sure if she was saying it to Coral or to the twelve sleeping souls or to herself. "I'm going to watch everything."

* * *

She moved on the fourth step.

Not running — running would have pulled eyes. Walking, quick and small, the way she'd walked to the well in the valley a thousand mornings, unremarkable, a child doing a child's errand. The tall court-thing turned its head slightly, tracking her, and she felt its attention like a hand between her shoulder blades, and then Coral's soul-thread pulsed, brightened, demanded the throne's whole focus again, and the attention slid off her like rain off slate.

The dais steps were black glass and cold as river ice through her boots. One. Two. Three. The scroll turned above her, and she reached up —

— and her fingers passed through it.

Not through it like mist. Through it like it wasn't there, like her hand was the unreal thing. She grabbed again, both hands, and the vellum shimmered and stayed exactly where it was, and somewhere behind her the court-thing made a sound that might have been laughter filtered through deep water.

"The record is not for mortal hands," it said gently. "Nothing below is. You should have understood that by now, thirteenth payment. You belong to us. Everything you touch belongs to us. But you cannot touch *this*."

Her hands hung in the empty air where the contract turned, and the cold of the hall climbed her wrists, and for one long moment Elodie Reyes stood on the steps of a monster's throne with nothing in her grip at all.

Then she remembered the song.

The mountain had been singing to her since the dark country — fragments at first, then verses, then whole movements of it, a music made of pressure and stone and patience. She had thought it was the court calling her home. She had thought it was hunger. But lying awake in the inn of small mercies, listening, she had noticed something she hadn't told anyone, not even Coral, not even in the end when the secrets had gotten heavy enough to bend her spine: the song was older than the court. The court sang *along* with it, the way a choir sings along with a river. The mountain had been singing before there was anything down here to sing to. The mountain had been singing when Ilsabet was alive.

And songs, unlike contracts, lived in the air. In the walls. In the bones of the world. No one owned them. No one had ever thought to strike them through.

Ilsabet's name was in the song. It had been in the song all along, buried under ten thousand years of the court's harmonies, one small syllable-thread woven into the deepest verse, the way a single gray hair stays black at the root. The first debtor's name had never left this place. It had gone into the stone the day she was taken, and the stone still held it, the way stone holds heat after the fire is out.

Elodie let go of the air. She put her palm flat against the dais instead — against the black glass, against the mountain itself, the living root of it — and she listened the way she'd learned to listen in the dark country, with her whole body, with the place behind her ribs.

There. Under everything. Under the throne's slow drinking, under the court's held breath, under the groan of the silver thread:

*Ilsabet. Ilsabet. Ilsabet.*

Saying it aloud would cost her. She understood that the way you understand a stove is hot — totally, instantly, without argument. Names spoken against their owners' chains do not come free. The court would hear it. The court would know what she was doing before she finished the second syllable. Whatever came after would come fast.

But Coral was graying at the edges, and the stars were guttering, and a girl who remembered the sun had waited ten thousand years in a glass ball for someone to say her name out loud.

Elodie filled her lungs with the court's cold air.

* * *

"Ilsabet."

The first word dropped into the hall like a stone into a well, and the well answered.

Every surface rang. The black glass under her feet hummed. The walls of the cavern flexed, once, like the sides of a breathing animal. The court-things flinched — all of them, all at once, the perfect rows breaking for the first time in living memory — and their faces, those beautiful patient faces, went through something Elodie had no word for. Not fear. Something older than fear. The expression of creatures hearing a sound they had buried so deep they'd convinced themselves it had never existed.

"WHAT ARE YOU DOING," the tall one said, and its voice broke, water over stones becoming stones over stones. "CHILD. WHAT ARE YOU —"

"Ilsabet," Elodie said again, louder, pressing her palm harder into the mountain, pulling the name up through the stone like drawing water from a deep spring. "Ilsabet, first daughter of the valley, first payment of the bargain — I speak your name. I speak it back into the world. I speak it *against the terms*."

The silver thread trembled.

The throne stopped drinking.

She felt it happen — felt the vast attention snap upward, away from Coral, toward her, all of it, the whole weight of the court's focus swinging like a searchlight — and she did not stop. Coral's body sagged forward onto its hands, half-empty, half-here, alive in the way a banked fire is alive, and Elodie spared it one glance, one, because Coral had told her to look, and then she turned back to the mountain and said the name a third time.

"Ilsabet."

And the mountain said it with her.

That was the moment the foundation cracked. Not the walls — the *foundation*, the thing underneath the walls, the original agreement, the first yes that all the other yeses had been built on. Because a contract requires consent, and consent requires a person, and a person requires a name, and Ilsabet's name had been struck through rather than erased, which meant the court had always known, in whatever way such things know, that the first debtor had never truly been removed from her own bargain — only silenced. Silenced things can be unsilenced. The mountain had kept her name the way a river keeps a drowned bell, and now the bell was ringing, and every term of the bargain that rested on her voice was resting on a voice that had just come back from the dead.

The scroll above the throne screamed.

There was no other word for it. The vellum, which had turned serenely for centuries, whipped taut and began to tear along the struck-through line, along the brown ink, along the oldest entry, and as it tore, the darkness that leaked from it was not ink but something else — the stored weight of thirteen generations, pouring out, rising.

The glass spheres woke.

All of them. In the vault a hundred feet below, through the stone, Elodie felt it more than heard it: twelve hearts starting in twelve bodies that had forgotten they had bodies, twelve dreams ending at once, twelve souls pressing outward against crystal like seedlings against frost. The nearest vessel — the one the court kept closest, the second payment, a boy who'd been taken so long ago his mother's language had died with everyone who spoke it — shattered first, and the light that came out of it was warm. Warm, in a hall that had been cold for ten thousand years.

The court's foundation cracked, and the crack ran upward through everything built on it. The throne split from crown to base with a sound like a glacier calving. The beautiful patient things began to come apart at their seams, unraveling into shapes less certain than the ones they'd worn, because their forms had been sustained by the bargain, and the bargain was dying, and they knew it, and for the first time since Elodie had come below the mountain, they were afraid of a twelve-year-old girl standing on their dais with her hand on the stone.

"CORAL," she shouted, because the thread was still attached, still anchored in her guardian's chest, and dying bargains do not release their holdings gracefully. "CORAL, HOLD ON —"

Coral lifted their head. Their eyes found hers across the ruined hall — blurred, gray-rimmed, but finding hers, the way they always had, the way they had on the night the stars went out, the way they had every morning of twelve years of breakfasts and patrol reports and bad jokes over the dishwater — and Elodie saw, in the wreck of everything, in the falling dark and the rising light, the exact moment her guardian decided to live.

The last thing the contract said, as it tore free of the air and burned to nothing in the warmth of twelve waking souls, was Ilsabet's name, unstruck, whole, legible at last.

The first debtor rose out of the floor of the world.

And the mountain began, very slowly, to sing the sun back in.

* * *

Above them, through the wound in the ceiling, the sky was changing.

Elodie stood on the cracked dais with her hand still pressed to the stone, and she watched the first star come back. It came back the way everything true comes back — not all at once, but honestly, a pinprick of white in the black, then a steadying, then a burn. Then another. Then three. The court's remnant-things were dissolving around her, folding down into shadows, into dust, into the occasional grateful whisper, and the hall that had been a throne room was becoming a cave, and the cave was becoming just a place inside a mountain, and the mountain was shaking off ten thousand years like a dog shaking off rain.

Twelve lights rose past her, upward, unhurried, the freed souls climbing toward the rekindling sky. The oldest of them paused beside Elodie — a girl-shape of warm light, small, the size she had been when they took her — and touched what remained of her hand to Elodie's shoulder.

"You said it," the light said, in the old tongue, in the voice of someone remembering the sun. "You said my name."

"Somebody had to," Elodie said.

"No." The light shook, which was how light smiled. "*No one* had to. That's the whole point. That's what they never understood. That's why we're free."

Then it rose, and joined the others, and the twelve of them went up through the wound in the world together, and Elodie turned around —

— and Coral was lying on the black glass, cold, hollowed, barely breathing, and the last of the silver thread was fading out of their chest like breath fading off a window, and Elodie did not know yet whether what she had saved was a person or only the shape of one.

She went to them anyway. Of course she went to them anyway.

The stars were coming back overhead, one by one, and the mountain was singing, and somewhere far above, past all that returning light, Vess and the godsworn were still fighting their diversion in the dark country, and the broker-god's mask lay cracked on the floor with nothing behind it anymore, and Elodie knelt in the wreckage of the oldest cruelty in the world and put her hands on Coral's sternum and pressed down, once, twice, and did not know yet — could not know yet — whether the hollow thing under her hands could be refilled, or whether she had traded a sold soul for an emptied one, or whether there was any law left anywhere, in heaven or below the mountain, that could give back what had been freely given away.

The twelfth star reignited.

Elodie kept pressing.
