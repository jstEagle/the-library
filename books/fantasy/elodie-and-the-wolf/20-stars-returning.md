## 20. Stars Returning

**Coral**

The cold came first.

Not the cold of the mountain, which had been a living thing all this while — a cold with appetite in it, a cold that watched. This was simpler. Older. The cold of a body that had stopped being argued over.

Coral lay on their back on the shattered floor of the court and felt their soul go out of them like a tide going out of a bay: not torn, not stolen, but *drawn*, gently, the way you'd draw thread from a spool. It didn't even hurt. That was the cruelest part. All those years of imagining what it would cost to give up the mark, the oath, the name the god had burned into their wrist at nineteen — and in the end it was painless. Like setting down a pack after a long march. Like sleep.

Above them — above everything — the twelfth star reignited.

Coral saw it through the crack Elodie's voice had split into the ceiling of the world, that ragged wound of open sky where the court's dark had been peeled back like skin from fruit. Through the wound poured something Coral hadn't seen in twelve years and had stopped believing in somewhere around year six.

Starlight.

It fell into the court the way water falls into an empty cup, and everywhere it touched, the beautiful things began to come apart.

* * *

**Elodie**

She did not stop pressing because she understood, in the way children understand things adults have forgotten, that the contract was not stone or paper or law. It was a *story*. And stories end when someone says so.

The glass souls rose around her as she spoke — all twelve of them, waking one by one as her voice found each of their names in the ledger's ruined pages. The first girl, the one who remembered the sun, rose last and highest, and when she passed Elodie she reached down and touched her hair, once, the way a mother does, and Elodie felt the touch land somewhere deeper than skin.

Then they were rising past the broken throne, past the weeping door, up through the crack in the sky, and the court screamed.

It wasn't a sound. It was the absence of one — every courteous, patient, generous thing in that vast hall going silent at once, the way a room goes silent when a debt is forgiven. The pillars leaned inward. The floor tilted toward a center that no longer existed. The whole architecture of the bargain, which had stood for thirteen generations on the weight of one small soul at a time, discovered all at once that it had nothing left to stand on.

Elodie turned to find Coral.

They were lying very still on the black glass, and they were *cold*. She knew it before she touched them, knew it the way she'd known the mountain's song, the way she'd known her own name before anyone told it to her. She knelt and put her hands on their chest and their heart was there but slow, so slow, a drum at the bottom of a well.

"Coral," she said. "Coral, wake up. You're not done. You're not allowed to be done."

Nothing. Just that slow, stubborn heartbeat, keeping time with something far away.

Above her, the thirteenth star reignited.

* * *

**Coral**

Dying, it turned out, was a room.

A small one. Warm. There was a chair in it, and a window, and through the window a valley Coral recognized down to its bones — the ward-stones, the frozen river, the crooked chimney of the house where a child had waited for them every night for twelve years.

Someone sat in the chair.

It wore the broker's mask, but the mask was cracked now, split down the middle like river ice in spring, and behind it there was no face at all. Only light. Thin, exhausted, grateful light.

"You came," the god said. Its voice was the song from the mountain, unbraided at last, all its threads loose. "After everything I cost you. After Vess. After the ledger with your name in it. You came down here and you paid my debt."

"Your debt," Coral said. Their voice came from very far away too. "Thirteen generations of children. That was your debt."

"Yes." No flinching. No excuse. The god had been devoured for defaulting; it knew exactly what it was. "I borrowed against souls I never owned, and when the note came due I let the court collect from people who'd never signed anything. And then I hid inside my own debt for five hundred years, wearing masks, selling passages, telling myself I was waiting for a way out." The light guttered. "There was never a way out. There was only ever a way *down*. And you took it. For her."

Coral looked at the valley through the window. Somewhere in it, a house stood empty. Somewhere in it, wards were learning to hum again.

"I didn't do it for you," Coral said.

"No," the god agreed. "That's why it worked."

It stood. The mask fell from its face entirely, and Coral saw — just for a moment — what a god looks like when it stops pretending to be anything at all. Not vast. Not terrible. Small. Tired. Almost human-shaped, almost familiar, the way a memory is shaped like the person you miss.

"The court is coming apart," the god said. "When it goes, everything it held goes free. Including me. I won't survive it. I don't want to." A pause, and then, quieter, with something that in any other mouth would have been called humility: "But I wanted to say thank you. Before. To someone. Anyone. You'll do."

"Don't thank me," Coral said. "Fix what's left. If anything of you is left."

"It will be," the god said, and smiled without a face. "Seeds grow."

The room went white.

* * *

**Elodie**

The court collapsed inward the way a held breath collapses out.

One moment the pillars were leaning, groaning, the black light bleeding from the cracks in everything — and the next, the whole vast hall simply *folded*, walls meeting ceilings meeting floors, the architecture of the bargain folding itself up like a letter no one would ever read again. Elodie threw herself over Coral's body as the ceiling came down, and the ceiling came down as starlight, and starlight does not crush.

When she could see again, they were standing — she was standing, Coral was still lying — in a ruin under open sky.

Real sky. Whole sky. And it was *burning*.

Stars were reigniting overhead one by one, each ignition a soft silent bloom, like lamps being lit in windows down a long dark street. Twelve stars for twelve souls — Elodie counted them as they came, and the freed souls themselves were rising past her now, streaming upward through the ruin like sparks off a bonfire, each one a person-shaped shimmer of light. As each soul passed the star that was its own, the two met, and the star burned brighter, and Elodie heard — faintly, impossibly — singing.

The first girl paused beside her on her way up. She looked older than she had in the glass, or maybe just more *there*, more real, a person instead of a payment.

"They'll tell stories about you," the girl said.

"I don't want stories," Elodie said. "I want Coral."

The girl looked down at Coral's still body, then back at Elodie, and her expression was old beyond her years and gentle beyond her lifetime. "Then keep talking to them," she said. "Souls hear longer than hearts do." And she rose, and her star caught fire, and the singing swelled.

Elodie knelt back down. She took Coral's cold hand in both of hers.

"You heard that," she whispered. "So listen. You promised me the valley. You promised me the river in summer. You promised me you'd teach me to fight with the short blade and you only ever taught me the first three moves because you said the rest could wait until I was taller. I'm going to be taller, Coral. I'm going to be taller and you're going to be *furious* about it."

Overhead, the fifteenth star bloomed.

"So keep listening," Elodie said, "because I'm not done either."

* * *

**Coral**

Somewhere very far away, someone was counting stars aloud.

The voice came through the warm dark like light through shutters, in slats, in pieces, and Coral drifted toward it the way a drowning thing drifts toward a hole in the ice — not because it wants the cold, but because the cold means *up*, and up means air.

*—sixteen, seventeen — that one's mine, I think, the bright one, obviously — eighteen —*

*Coral.*

*I know you can hear me. The god told me souls hear longer than hearts do. Well. Something like a god. It dissolved, by the way. Gratefully. I think you'd have liked it at the end. Or hated it. One of those.*

*You set down your pack. I understand that. But you picked it up for twelve years, and you don't get to put it down until I say the march is over, because I'm the one who was sold, and I'm the one who says when the price is paid, and I say it's paid, do you hear me? I say it's PAID—*

The dark shuddered.

*—and besides,* the voice went on, softer now, wetter, *besides, who's going to check the ward-stones? Who's going to sit with me when the magic gets strange? Who's going to be my family? Because I decided, down here, in the dark, that you're it. You and maybe Vess, if she earns it back, which she's going to have to work very hard at. You're my family, Coral Ashgrove, and family doesn't get to die on a floor while I'm still talking—*

Coral opened their eyes.

The first thing they saw was a star — new, fierce, close, as if it had come down specifically to be the first thing they saw. The second thing was Elodie's face above theirs, tear-streaked and furious and lit from above by a sky full of burning constellations that had not existed an hour ago.

"Twenty-two," she said, sniffling, glaring, radiant. "There are twenty-two stars now. There used to be none. You missed twenty-two stars."

"Did I," Coral rasped.

"You did." She grabbed their shoulders and hauled, and Coral — hollowed, emptied, lighter than they had been since they were nineteen years old and a god pressed a mark into their wrist — sat up in the ruins of the court beneath the mountain, under the open, mending sky.

Something was missing. Coral felt it the way you feel a missing tooth: a smooth absence where the mark had been. They turned their wrist over. Bare skin. Clean. For the first time in thirty years, no god lived in their arm.

"Weird," Coral said.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah." They looked up. Starlight fell on both of them, warm as noon. "Feels quiet."

Elodie laughed — cracked, sobbing, real laughter, the kind that comes after. "Get used to it."

* * *

**Vess**

She came over the ridge at dawn with forty godsworn mercenaries and a plan she'd expected to die carrying, and found the mountain open like a split loaf, the court gone to dust and glitter, and a sky so full of new stars that half her soldiers dropped to their knees and the other half started arguing about whether it was an omen.

At the mouth of the ruin sat Coral Ashgrove, wrapped in a child's cloak, drinking from a waterskin, looking like a man who had been through a threshing machine and come out the other side as flour.

Vess walked down to them alone. Her hand shook. She made herself stop it.

"The diversion's not needed," she said.

"No," Coral agreed.

"The mercenaries are going to want a story."

"Tell them the truth. They won't believe it. That's fine. Nobody believes the truth anymore; it's the safest place to hide it."

Vess sat down heavily on a fallen pillar. Above them the new stars were fading gently into the dawn, which did not diminish them at all. "Twelve souls," she said. "Free. Thirteen generations of payments, ended in one night. And the god?"

"Dissolved. Thanked me first. It was embarrassing for both of us."

"And you gave your soul to buy the girl's."

"I offered it." Coral looked at their bare wrist. "Turns out offering isn't the same as paying. Someone refused the trade."

"Who?"

"That," Coral said, "is a very good question. And I think—" they nodded toward Elodie, who was standing at the edge of the ruin watching the sunrise with her chin lifted, her eyes narrowed, wearing the expression of someone who has recently argued with something ancient and intends to argue with it again, "—she knows."

Vess followed their gaze. Whatever she saw in the girl's face made her go pale, then thoughtful, then — slowly, reluctantly — proud.

"She's going to be trouble," Vess said.

"She's going to be *magnificent*," Coral said. "There's a difference."

Below them, in the valley, the wards began — softly, tentatively, like a struck bell remembering its note — to hum.
