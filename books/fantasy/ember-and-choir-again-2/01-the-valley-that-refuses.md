## 1. The Valley That Refuses

The dead of Hollowmere did not haunt the valley. They lived in it, politely, the way old aunts live in a spare room—present, murmuring, occasionally demanding tea.

Jude Thackeray had spent thirty years learning to listen to them, and thirty years learning when not to. There was a difference, and it was the whole art.

This morning the dead were chatty. They always were after rain, when the ground softened and let their voices rise like steam from a kettle. Jude sat at the workbench in the lean-to behind their cottage, a drowned farmer's shinbone braced in a vice of ash wood, and listened while they talked over one another the way sparrows argue over a feeder.

*Cold water,* said the bone under Jude's hands, in the voice of a man who had been cold water's guest for three days now. *Cold, and then not-cold, and then nothing, and it wasn't so bad, the nothing. Tell Marta the nothing wasn't so bad.*

"I'll tell her," Jude murmured, and set the first groove with the smallest knife.

Bone-carving was not, strictly speaking, necessary. Hollowmere could have buried its dead the way other places did—under dirt, under stone, under silence—and gotten on with things. But the valley had never been a place that got on with things. The valley was a place that refused. It refused the king's tax collectors (they rode in twice a year and rode out again having forgotten why they'd come). It refused the weather beyond a certain temperament (storms broke apart at the ridgeline like arguments abandoned mid-sentence). And most of all it refused magic, which slid off the boundary stones like rain off oiled wool, so that no witch's hex, no hedge-wizard's charm, no court enchanter's grand and expensive working had ever taken root inside the stones in living memory.

Including, mostly, Jude's own gift.

That was the joke of it—the valley's private joke, played on Jude alone. The gift of hearing the dead was magic, or near enough that no one had ever successfully argued otherwise. Inside the boundary stones, it worked only through carved bone. Ivory held what flesh could not: a whisper caught in the grain, a murmur pressed into the marrow like flowers into a book. Outside the stones—well. Jude had never gone outside the stones. Thirty years, and never once past the humming gray monoliths that ringed the valley like a jaw of old teeth. So Jude didn't know what the gift did out there, whether it roared or starved or simply stopped, and had made a life's discipline of not wondering.

The knife moved. The bone warmed under Jude's hands, the way it always did, as if the dead inside it leaned toward the touch.

*Tell her I didn't suffer. Well. Tell her I suffered a little, but briefly, and mostly about my boots.*

"Your boots?"

*I loved those boots. She'll want to know where they are. Third fencepost past the mill, I wedged them on a stake so they'd dry. She always said I'd lose them to the river and by god I showed her.*

A wet laugh came through the ivory, thin as breath on winter glass. Jude laughed too, because you learned to. Grief in Hollowmere was a shared meal; everyone brought something, and nobody left hungry, and somehow everyone went home fed.

Outside the lean-to, the morning was doing its best impression of an ordinary day. Mist lay in the hollows like spilled milk. Somewhere upslope, Tam Broadfoot was already cursing at his father's mule, which had opinions about Mondays. The village of Hollowmere Proper—eleven houses, a smithy, a shrine nobody remembered building, and a tavern whose sign read THE DROWNED GOOSE for reasons lost to time and possibly alcohol—smoked gently from its chimneys, minding its own business, as it had for longer than anyone could count.

And around it all, at the valley's rim, the boundary stones hummed.

You stopped hearing them after a while, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. But some mornings—cold mornings, moon-turn mornings—they came back to awareness like a tooth remembering it ached. A low note, felt in the sternum more than the ears. *We are here. Nothing crosses. Nothing leaves.* Jude had asked Warden Ferris once, at fifteen, what would happen if someone tried. Ferris had looked at Jude for a long moment with those gray eyes that seemed to be counting something behind them, and said, "Nothing has tried," in a voice that made it clear this was both an answer and a warning.

Jude had not tried. That was the shape of the life: small, warm, deliberate. A cottage with good hinges. A trade that mattered. Dead men's boots delivered to grieving widows. It was, Jude told themselves most mornings, enough.

Most mornings the telling worked.

* * *

Marta Hale received the news of her husband's boots with the particular composure of a woman who has already cried everything she has and found, underneath, a stubborn practical core.

"The third fencepost," she repeated. "Past the mill."

"He was very proud of them."

"He was proud of everything he owned. He once complimented our door hinge for ten minutes." Her mouth trembled toward a smile and won. "Is there—do the bones say anything else? You don't have to tell me. People say I shouldn't ask. People say a lot of things."

"People say a lot of things," Jude agreed. "He says the nothing wasn't so bad."

Marta went still. Then she sat down hard on her own doorstep, which was exactly where a person wants to be sitting when the world rearranges itself, and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook in a way that had nothing to do with drowning and everything to do with being allowed, finally, to stop holding herself up.

Jude stood in the yard and gave her the privacy of company. This, too, was part of the trade. The bones carried the words, but the carver carried the weight, and after thirty years Jude had learned that the weight was not evenly distributed—it pooled in the ones who stayed behind, and someone had to help carry it out of the house and into the light, where it could evaporate.

After a while Marta wiped her face and looked up, red-eyed and fierce. "He's still in there, isn't he? In the bone. Not—not all of him. But a piece."

"A piece that remembers loving you. Yes."

"And it doesn't hurt him? Being carved? Being—" she gestured vaguely, "—listened to?"

"It's the opposite of hurting, most days. Think of it as being remembered out loud." Jude hesitated. "Though he does want you to know the boots were a triumph."

Another laugh, wetter than the last. "Of course he does." She stood, steadied herself on the doorframe, and became abruptly, briskly practical, which Jude suspected was how she survived most things. "You'll take eggs. Don't argue. And come to the wake tonight—the proper one, not the grim one. He'd hate a grim wake. He hated grimness the way other men hate debt."

So Jude went home for the eggs' sake and returned at dusk for the wake's sake, and if anyone in the village thought it strange that the bone-carver who lived alone at the valley's edge was welcome at every table in Hollowmere Proper, no one said so. Loneliness, in Hollowmere, was understood to be a craft like any other—one you could practice well or badly, and Jude practiced it well, with good hinges and regular visits and a standing invitation to the Drowned Goose on moon-turn nights that they accepted roughly half the time.

Half the time. That was the arithmetic of a deliberately small life. Enough company to stay human, enough solitude to stay unexamined.

* * *

The memorial took four days, which was fast, but Aldous Hale had been a beloved man and the dead hurried for their favorites.

By custom, the finished piece went to the shrine nobody remembered building—a low stone structure at the village center with a roof of overlapping slates and, inside, shelves upon shelves of carved bone. Birds and boats and bootjacks. Spoons and shepherd's crooks and one ambitious attempt at a fiddle that had been, according to the dead man who commissioned it, "a fair likeness of a turnip, but heartfelt." Each piece held its whisper. Generations of Hollowmere's dead murmured together on those shelves, a congregation of ivory, and on quiet evenings the shrine hummed with all of them at once, soft as bees in a wall.

Jude carried Aldous's memorial—a small carved river, waves frozen mid-curl, a pair of tiny boots riding the biggest wave like a ship—up the shrine path on the fourth evening, and paused at the door because the dead inside were loud today.

Not grief-loud. Argument-loud.

*—telling you the stones sang different last night—*

*Stones don't sing different. Stones sing same. That's the entire point of stones—*

*Same is the point, yes, but it wasn't same. It was—thinner. Like a song losing its nerve—*

Jude stood in the doorway with the river-and-boots in their arms and waited for the argument to resolve, the way you wait for weather. It didn't. The murmur kept circling, uneasy, an itch in the air of the shrine.

"Evening, all," Jude said aloud, because politeness cost nothing, and set the carving on the empty shelf between a toy horse and a woman's portrait-brooch.

The moment the bone touched wood, the whispers nearest it went quiet—then swarmed, eager, pressing close the way cats press against the one person who feeds them.

*New arrival! Ask him about the boots—*

*Ask him about the singing—*

*There was no singing—*

*There WAS singing, I heard it through the shelf, through the WALL, the stones at the rim went thin as paper and something on the other side of them was—*

"Was what?" Jude asked.

Silence. The particular, loaded silence of people who have just realized they've said too much.

Then, from deeper in the shrine, from the oldest shelf, where the carvings were black with age and the voices had gone soft and strange with centuries: a single word, in a voice like dry leaves.

*Listening.*

The word settled over the shrine like frost. Even the arguers hushed.

Jude straightened slowly. "Listening to what?"

No answer. The dead of Hollowmere were many things—gossipy, sentimental, obsessed with boots—but they were not liars, and when they declined to speak it was because speaking would cost them something. Jude had seen that only twice before in thirty years, and both times had involved the boundary stones, and both times Warden Ferris had appeared within the hour with questions shaped like kindness and edges like knives.

Which was, of course, exactly what happened.

* * *

Ferris did not knock. The Warden of Hollowmere knocked for no one, on the theory—Jude supposed—that a warden who knocked admitted the valley belonged to someone else.

They stood in Jude's kitchen while the kettle worked itself up to a boil, and Ferris examined the room with those counting eyes: the tools on their pegs, the half-finished cradle rocking-horse for the Weaver baby, the single chair, the single cup, the single everything. Jude had long ago stopped feeling judged by it. Mostly.

"The shrine was restless tonight," Ferris said.

"The shrine is often restless. Old dead are like old pipes."

"These weren't old dead complaining about the weather. These were old dead going quiet when you asked a direct question." Ferris turned. Gray eyes, gray hair cropped short, gray coat without a single wrinkle—as though wrinkles were a form of disorder the coat had refused, like everything else the valley refused. "What did they say, Thackeray?"

"One word. *Listening.*"

"Listening to what?"

"They wouldn't say. Which is why I'm telling you instead of them."

Something flickered across Ferris's face—approval, quickly suppressed, the way the valley suppressed storms. The Warden crossed to the window and looked out at the dark, toward the rim of the valley, where the boundary stones were invisible at this distance and yet somehow the first thing your eye found anyway.

"When I was a girl," Ferris said, and it was such an unprecedented opening that Jude actually set down the kettle, "the stones hummed one note. One. You could stand at the rim on a cold night and feel it in your teeth. My father said it had been one note since his grandfather's time. Since the founding." A pause. "Last month, I counted two."

"Two notes."

"A second note. Faint. Underneath. Like something answering the first one." Ferris's reflection in the dark glass was unreadable. "I have served this valley twenty-two years, Thackeray. I know every sound it makes. And I am telling you, as plainly as I know how, that the valley has begun to make a new one."

The kettle screamed. Jude took it off the heat and poured two cups, because even wardens drank tea, and handed one over. Ferris wrapped gray fingers around it and did not drink.

"My mother used to say the valley keeps us the way a shell keeps the sea," Jude offered. "Held, and heard, and safe."

"Your mother," Ferris said, "said a great many things." And there it was again—that flicker, that counting look, aimed now at Jude rather than the room. "She also left. Nobody leaves, Thackeray. That's the covenant. That's the whole arrangement. We refuse the world, and the world, in its vast indifference, agrees to refuse us back. It is a good bargain. It is the only bargain. And bargains like that—" the gray eyes lifted, "—have terms."

"Terms."

"Conditions. Clauses. Fine print written by somebody, somewhere, for reasons nobody remembers." Ferris drank the tea at last, one precise sip. "The dead are listening because something has changed in the fine print. I don't know what. I don't like not knowing. Keep your ears open, carve your bones, and Thackeray—if anything comes up the road to your door, anything at all, you send for me before you do anything else. Before you feed it. Before you name it." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "*Especially* before you name it."

"That's oddly specific advice."

"I'm an oddly specific woman." Ferris set down the cup, untouched thereafter, and moved to the door. On the threshold the Warden paused, backlit by the moon, and for one unguarded moment looked less like an institution and more like a tired person standing between a valley and the dark.

"Moon-turn tomorrow," Ferris said. "First of the season. Sleep lightly."

And then the Warden was gone down the path, gray coat fading into gray dusk, leaving Jude alone with two cups of tea and a silence that felt, for the first time in thirty years, less like peace and more like held breath.

Jude washed the cups. Banked the fire. Stood a moment at the window, looking north toward the rim, where the boundary stones hummed their one ancient note—or their one ancient note and whatever new thing answered beneath it.

*Sleep lightly,* the Warden had said.

Jude slept the way they always slept: deeply, quietly, alone, in a life deliberately made small.

Which is why, when the knock came at the door near midnight—small, patient, unmistakably human—Jude woke all at once, heart hammering, with Ferris's voice still hanging in the dark:

*Before you feed it. Before you name it.*

The knock came again.

Jude reached for the lamp.
