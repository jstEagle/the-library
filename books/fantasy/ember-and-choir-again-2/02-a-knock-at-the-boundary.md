## 2. A Knock at the Boundary

The lamp caught on the first try, which Jude would later think of as the night's only mercy. They lifted it and stood in their own doorway with the flame cupped behind one hand, listening.

The knock came a third time. Small. Knuckles, not a fist. The knock of someone who had been taught to wait.

"Who's there?" Jude asked, and hated how thin their voice came out. Thirty years in Hollowmere and they had forgotten how to sound like anything but an apology.

No answer. Only the wind coming down off the rim, carrying its usual freight of pine and cold stone, and beneath it—the hum of the boundary stones, that single ancient note the valley had sung for as long as anyone could remember. Except tonight it sounded wrong. Tonight it sounded like a note being held by someone whose arm was tired.

Jude opened the door.

The child stood on the third step, because the first two were rotten and everyone in Hollowmere knew to skip them, which meant she had either been told or had simply understood. She was perhaps seven years old, small for it, wrapped in a cloak that had been fine once—dyed a deep river-blue, hemmed in silver thread now gone gray—and was now so travel-worn it looked like something the road had coughed up. Barefoot. Her feet were filthy but not cut, not bleeding, which was its own small impossibility; the mountain path from the north pass ate grown men's boots in a day.

She looked up at Jude without fear. That was the thing Jude noticed second. First was her eyes, which were dark as the water in the old quarry, and second was that she did not look afraid, and no seven-year-old standing barefoot on a stranger's step at midnight should be unafraid.

"Hello," Jude said.

The child tilted her head. Then she spoke.

The syllables came out of her like water over stones—quick, liquid, layered, three words braided into one the way a chord is three notes pretending to be a single sound. Jude knew every language spoken within two hundred miles of Hollowmere, or rather, knew of them: valley common, the lowland drawl, the high shepherds' whistled tongue, even the formal liturgy-words the Warden used for oaths. This was none of them. This was older than any of them. It was the kind of language you might expect to come out of a cave, if caves could talk, or out of the ground itself, if the ground had been waiting a very long time to say something.

And when she said it—

The dead went silent.

Jude did not know, at first, what the strange new feeling was. It took them several heartbeats to recognize it, the way a man who has lived his whole life beside a river takes a moment to notice the silence when the river stops. Because there was always the murmur. Always. Through the bones on the shelf, through the memorial pieces hanging in the workshop, through the little carved tokens in every pocket and every lintel in the valley—the dead murmured, all of them, all the time, a tide of whispering that Jude had heard so long they had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. It was the sound of Hollowmere. It was the sound of Jude's whole life.

It stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, clean, like a door closing. The bone beads at the window went quiet. The memorial rack in the workshop went quiet. Even the drowned farmer's finger-bone, which Jude had been carving that morning and which had spent the entire day sighing about the cold, went silent as river stone.

Jude stood in their own doorway and heard—for the first time in thirty years of living—nothing at all.

It should have been a relief. They had imagined, once or twice, what quiet might be like. They had thought it would feel like rest.

It felt like being buried.

"Oh," Jude said, very softly.

The child watched them with those quarry-water eyes. She said something else—a shorter phrase this time, almost gentle, the way you'd speak to a startled animal—and the silence deepened around her voice like a footprint in snow.

"You'd better come in," Jude heard themselves say, "before I decide I dreamed you."

* * *

She crossed the threshold the way water crosses sand: everywhere at once, gently, leaving no line. She looked at everything. The banked fire. The cups drying on their rack. The shelf of finished carvings above the hearth—small memorials waiting to be claimed, each one humming faintly with whatever was left of whoever it remembered. She looked longest at those.

When she reached toward the shelf, Jude said, "Careful," and then, because the silence was making them reckless, "they're people."

She paused. Looked back at Jude. And then she did something that made the hair rise along Jude's arms: she bowed to the shelf. A small, precise bow, hand over heart, the gesture of one courtier greeting another. As though she recognized them. As though she knew exactly who was sleeping in each piece of ivory, and considered them worth respect.

Then she turned back to the fire and held her hands toward it, and Jude saw that they were shaking—not from cold, he thought, or not only from cold. From something else. From effort, maybe. From holding herself together the way you hold a full cup while walking.

Jude fed the fire. Put water on to heat. Got down the bread and the honey, because Ferris's voice was in their head saying *before you feed it* and Jude had spent thirty years learning exactly which orders to ignore.

"Eat," they said, setting the plate before her.

She ate the way she did everything: carefully, completely, wasting nothing, licking honey from her thumb with a gravity that would have been funny if the whole night weren't so deeply strange. Jude sat across from her and studied her face. Round-cheeked. A smudge of ash on one jaw. Hair black as the inside of a bell, cropped short in a way that suggested scissors applied by someone in a hurry. There was nothing about her that said *mountain child*, nothing that said *lowland*, nothing that said *anywhere*. She looked, Jude thought uneasily, like a word written in a language you couldn't read—you could see every stroke of her perfectly, and understand nothing.

"What do I call you?" Jude asked.

She looked up. Considered. Then she touched her own chest, and said a word in that layered, liquid speech—two syllables braided into one—and the dead stayed silent, and the fire popped, and outside the boundary stones hummed their tired note.

"Ash," Jude tried, because the syllable had a shape like hers, quick and pale and burning at the center.

Something flickered across her face. Not recognition exactly. More like the way a lock feels a key that is nearly right. She repeated the word after him—"Ash"—in careful valley common, her first word in any tongue Jude could answer, and then she nodded once, decisively, as though a bargain had been struck.

Which, Jude would think later, it had.

* * *

They did not sleep, either of them. Ash curled in the chair by the fire with her bare feet tucked under her, eyes half-closed but never fully shut, and Jude sat across from her and did the thing they always did when the world refused to make sense: they worked.

There was comfort in the tools. Bone-saw, gravers, the little hooked knife their mother had left them, worn smooth where a thumb goes. Jude took up the drowned farmer's finger-bone and set it on the cloth and began, mechanically, to finish the border pattern—and realized halfway through the first curve that they were doing it wrong. Not badly. Wrong *deliberately*. Their hands were carving the mourning-knot, the pattern reserved for the newly dead, the one that says *we have lost you and we are still speaking*.

For a child who wasn't dead.

Jude put the knife down.

Across the room, Ash's eyes were open. Watching the bone. Watching the hands. And as Jude watched her watching, she began, very quietly, to hum.

It was not a tune from anywhere. It moved in intervals that made Jude's teeth ache pleasantly, the way a high wind does, and it had no beginning that Jude could find—it seemed to have been going on forever somewhere, and she had simply let a little of it out. And here was the thing, the thing Jude would lie awake turning over for years afterward: the humming did not break the silence. It *was* the silence. It filled the room the way the dead's murmur usually filled it, same weight, same texture, but empty instead of crowded. A choir with all the singers removed, still singing.

Jude's eyes stung. They wiped them with the heel of a hand and pretended it was the woodsmoke.

*You are thirty years old,* they told themselves sternly. *You have survived blizzards, a flash flood, and the Warden's opinion of your fence posts. You will not weep in front of a child because the voices in your head took a holiday.*

But oh, it was quiet. It was quiet the way the world must have been quiet before people started dying in it. And some traitor part of Jude, some part that had been tired for thirty years without admitting it, leaned into that quiet like a cat into sun.

That was the wonder. That was the part that felt like grace.

The dread came later, sitting with it in the dark, when Jude finally let themselves ask the question they had been refusing to ask since the knock:

*How did she get in?*

Because the boundary stones did not allow things in. That was the entire point of them. Magic could not cross—they could not cross, curses could not cross, the hungry dead of the outer world could not cross, and nothing living found the valley unless the valley wanted it, which it never did. Pilgrims had walked the rim for centuries and wandered home again with no memory of why they'd come. Armies had marched at it and woken up three days later in their own beds, embarrassed. Whatever kept Hollowmere invisible and inviolate, it was not a wall. It was a refusal. The valley simply declined, with tremendous politeness, to be part of the world.

And yet here was a child. Barefoot. Unafraid. Speaking a language older than kingdoms, silencing the dead with her voice, bowing to bones like a diplomat.

Here was a child who had walked through a refusal.

Jude got up, quietly, and went to the window. North, past the dark shapes of the workshops and the meeting hall, the rim of the valley showed against the stars, and along it the boundary stones stood in their ancient circle, and they were humming. Jude could feel it through the floor, through the soles of their feet, a note pitched too low for ears and just right for bones. But tonight the note had a frayed edge to it. Tonight it sounded less like a song and more like a sentence being held under great strain.

*Something answered beneath it.* That was what the Warden had said last night, in her office that smelled of ink and iron, with her ledger open between them. Jude had assumed she meant weather. Warden Ferris assumed everything meant weather until proven otherwise.

Now, looking at the frayed note, at the sleeping-not-sleeping child in their chair, Jude suspected otherwise.

Behind them, soft as breath: "Ash."

He turned. She was sitting up, watching him, and she pointed—one small finger, steady now—at the window, at the north rim. Then she pressed that finger flat against her lips.

*Quiet,* the gesture said. Or maybe *hush*. Or maybe, in whatever older tongue her body spoke when her mouth couldn't, *they are listening.*

"For whom?" Jude whispered.

Ash shook her head slowly. She didn't know. Or she wouldn't say. She pointed at the window again, and then at the floor, and then she drew a shape in the air with one finger: a triangle, tall and narrow.

A mountain.

Jude's mouth went dry. There was no mountain inside Hollowmere. There was no mountain within a week's walk except—

Except the one on the far side of the rim. The one the valley maps marked with a blank space and the words *do not survey*. The one the old songs gave thirteen different names, all of them warnings.

Ash folded her small hands in her lap, and waited, patient as midnight, for Jude to understand what she already knew.

Outside, the boundary stones hummed their strained note, and beneath it—faint, far, and getting nearer—something answered.

Jude blew out the lamp, and sat down in the dark with a stranger's child, and listened to the first true silence of their life, and understood that it had not come to stay.

It had come to warn them.
