## 3. What the Bones Remember

The child slept in my bed.

I had given her the bed because she was small and I was not, and because the alternative—letting her sleep on the floor like a stray cat—felt like the kind of decision a person regrets at three in the morning. She had curled up without argument, pulled the quilt to her chin with both hands as though she'd been waiting all her life for someone to offer her one, and gone under so completely that for a while I stood there just watching her breathe, half convinced that breathing was something she'd have to remember to do.

She did not snore. She hummed. Very faintly, on the out-breath, a thread of sound that wasn't quite a tune and wasn't quite speech—the same syllables she'd spoken at my door, worn down to almost nothing, like a river running under ice.

And the dead were silent.

I want to be clear about what that meant, because if you've never lived in Hollowmere you cannot imagine it. The dead are always here. Not as ghosts, exactly—they don't rattle chains or drift through walls, nothing so theatrical—but as murmurs. They live in the bones we carve. Every memorial in every house in this valley holds its dead the way a loom holds thread: quietly, constantly, a whisper of *here, here, still here*. I had heard it every day of my thirty years. It was the sound of home. It was also, I realized standing over that sleeping child in the blue hour before dawn, the sound I had never once been free of.

Her humming hushed them. All of them. The drowned farmer's bone on my workbench, the grandmothers in the lintel, the little carved sparrow on my shelf that held a child dead forty years before I was born—all silent, all at once, as if the whole valley had drawn one long breath and forgotten to let it go.

It should have been peaceful. Instead it felt like the moment a clock stops: you never notice how loud it was until it isn't.

I lit a lamp, sat down at my workbench, and looked at the drowned farmer's femur, which I had not yet finished carving, and which now sat mute as an ordinary bone. Then I looked at the child. Then back at the bone.

"Well," I said to no one, "that's not ominous at all."

* * *

By full light I had made tea, eaten half a loaf, and talked myself into and out of four different theories, none of which survived contact with the evidence. The evidence being: a barefoot girl of perhaps seven years, who had appeared at my door on the eve of the first moon-turn, speaking a language older than any kingdom, unbothered by cold, unafraid of me, and capable of silencing the dead by humming.

There was exactly one way to learn more, and I had been avoiding it since sunrise, which is a long time to avoid anything when you live alone and your workshop is twelve feet from your kitchen.

Divination through carved bone is the oldest craft in Hollowmere, older than the boundary stones, or so Warden Ferris claims in the tone of a woman who considers everything older than herself to be mildly disappointing. You take a bone—traditionally one already holding a name, because a named bone has somewhere to look—and you carve a question into it. Not words. Words are too blunt. You carve the *shape* of a question, a spiral or a keyhole or whatever form the question takes in your hands, and then you listen, and the dead who live in the bone answer by moving the grain, shifting the marrow-shadow, showing you what they know.

Most readings come back mundane. *He died of fever. He hid coins under the hearthstone. He forgave his brother, mostly.* The dead are people, after all, and most people, even dead, are mostly ordinary.

But I had a gift—or a curse; the distinction depended heavily on the day—that went past the ordinary. Where other carvers heard whispers, I heard whole conversations. Where they saw shadows in the grain, I saw lives laid open like books. My mother used to say I heard too much for one pair of ears, and she said it strangely, with a tightness around her mouth I'd never understood until—

Well. Until recently. We'll get there.

The point is: if anyone in Hollowmere could pull a truth out of a bone, it was me. And I did not want to do it. Because some part of me, the part that had spent thirty years building a life deliberately, carefully small, understood that once I knew where this child came from, I would have to *do* something about it. Knowledge in Hollowmere is not a passive thing. The valley repels magic; it does not repel obligation.

I washed my hands. I sharpened my smallest knife. I chose the femur of the drowned farmer, partly because he was fresh, partly because he had been a patient man in life—I'd learned that much from him already—and partly because he owed me nothing and therefore couldn't complain about being woken up twice in two days.

"Master Aldous," I said formally, because the dead appreciate courtesy, "I'm going to ask you a question, and I'd be grateful for whatever you can see from where you're lying."

The bone stayed silent, of course. Silent as stone. The child's humming still filled the house, thin as spider silk.

I frowned. A named bone that wouldn't answer was nearly unheard of. Unless—

Unless it wasn't refusing. Unless it was *muffled*. As if her humming weren't a wall between me and the dead but a blanket thrown over their heads.

I tried anyway. I set the bone in its cradle of river clay, took up the fine knife, and carved the shape of my question: *where did you come from?* rendered as a road running backward, each milestone a small notch, ending in a door.

The grain shifted.

Not gently. The whole surface of the bone seemed to shudder, the way water shudders when something large passes beneath it, and then the marrow-shadow bloomed up through the ivory like ink through paper, and I saw—

A door. Black stone, taller than any door has a right to be, set into rock that curved overhead like the inside of a throat. Cold light behind it, the color of deep water. And beneath the door, filling the bottom of the vision like a signature fills the bottom of a letter, a woman's hand—pale, steady, ringless—pressing a mark into a contract whose letters moved as I watched, rearranging themselves the way letters do in dreams.

The image held for three heartbeats. Then it collapsed, and reformed, and it was the same image again. The door. The hand. The contract.

I broke the reading, wiped my hands on my apron, and started over with a different bone—an old rib from the memorial wall, holding a weaver dead sixty years. Same question. Same answer. Door, mountain-dark, hand, contract.

Third bone. Fourth. By the sixth I was sweating, and every reading came back identical, which does not happen. Readings vary the way memories vary. Identical readings mean one of two things: either the truth is so heavy it displaces everything else, or something very large and very deliberate is pressing on the other side of the bone, making sure I see.

I set down the knife. My hands were not entirely steady.

"Thank you," I told the bones collectively. None of them answered. The child hummed on.

* * *

She woke near noon, sat up in my bed, surveyed my workshop with the frank appraisal of a tax collector, and pointed at the half-carved sparrow on my shelf.

"That's Hester," I said. "She died when she was six. Her mother asked me to make her something with wings."

The child looked at me. Looked at the sparrow. Made a sound—not language, not quite music—a soft descending note that managed, somehow, to convey *oh*, and *how sad*, and *but it's a good bird*, all at once. Then she slid off the bed, padded across the floor, and stood on tiptoe to lay one finger, feather-light, against the sparrow's wing.

Hester's bone, silent these two days along with every other bone in the valley, gave the faintest possible creak.

The child smiled at it. Then she turned to me and pointed at herself, then at the floor, then made a gesture I eventually understood to mean *name*: she wanted one. Or she was offering one. With her it was hard to tell the difference, and I suspected it always would be.

"You don't have one?" I asked, knowing she couldn't answer in any way I'd understand, and understanding her anyway, which was becoming a habit I didn't trust.

She shook her head. Then, after a moment, she touched her own hair—dark, coarse, shot through with a single streak of white at the temple, like ash worked into charcoal—and looked at me with an expression of such matter-of-fact expectation that I laughed for the first time in what felt like a season.

"Ash," I said. "We'll call you Ash."

She considered this. She repeated it—not in my tongue, but in hers, transforming it into something older and rounder, *ashen-eth* or something near it—and then she nodded once, gravely, the way a queen accepts a treaty, and the deal was done. Ash she was. Ash she had perhaps always been, waiting for someone to say it out loud.

She helped me with lunch. I say *helped*; she mainly supervised, arranging my carrots by length with an air of quiet disappointment at my previous system. When I burned the onions she patted my arm. When I got it right the second time she applauded, briefly and precisely, three claps, as though grading me *adequate*. Within four hours of having a name she had developed opinions about my kitchen. I found I didn't mind. I found, rather alarmingly, that I minded how little I minded.

Thirty years I had kept my life small. One room for living, one for working, a garden, a lane, the same neighbors, the same rituals, the dead murmuring their constant *here, here, still here*. Small was safe. Small was the whole point of Hollowmere—magic can't cross the boundary stones, and neither, in practice, did trouble. I had chosen smallness the way other people choose a profession: deliberately, young, and with the sense of closing a very large door behind me.

Ash ate her soup with intense concentration and then fell asleep sitting up, mid-supervision of my dishwashing, and I carried her back to bed and stood there again watching her breathe, and thought about doors.

* * *

I read until dark. Not divination—books. My mother's books, the ones I'd inherited along with the house and the tools and the silence where her voice used to be. She had died eleven years ago, and in eleven years I had never once heard her among the dead. I'd told myself it was grief playing tricks, that the dead she would have joined were elsewhere, that bones only hold those buried under carved stone. But standing in her old study with a sleeping soul-child down the hall and a vision of a signed contract still burning behind my eyes, I noticed for the first time what I should have noticed years ago:

No one in Hollowmere ever spoke of her. Not the dead, not the living. Warden Ferris mentioned her only in the flat voice reserved for closed matters. The bones in this house—her house—held farmers and weavers and children, but not her. As if she had been subtracted from the record. As if the valley itself had agreed to forget her, thoroughly and on purpose.

I found what I was looking for in a geography folio she'd kept at the back of the shelf, behind a false spine, which should have told me enough on its own. A map of the northern ranges, hand-drawn, annotated in her writing. And there, west of everything, a mountain drawn larger than the page strictly allowed, its peak running off the edge as if the cartographer had lost their nerve.

Beneath it, in her small precise hand: *Serel. Do not sing near it. Do not sign anything.*

Do not sign anything.

I sat with that for a long time. Outside, the first stars came up over the boundary stones, and I could feel them humming their low warding note the way you feel a hearth from the next room. Safe. Everything inside the stones was safe. That was the bargain the valley had made, everyone said so—safety purchased long ago at a price nobody discussed.

A price paid by somebody. Signed by somebody. By a woman's pale, steady, ringless hand.

"No," I said aloud, to the map, to the mountain, to my mother wherever she was and whatever she'd done. "You didn't. Tell me you didn't."

The study offered no opinion. Down the hall, Ash hummed in her sleep, and every bone in the house kept its silence, and underneath her humming—so faint I might have imagined it, except I have never once imagined the voices of the dead—I heard them. Not silenced after all. Pressed close against the far side of her song like faces at a window. And they were not murmuring *here, here, still here.*

They were screaming warnings.

I caught only fragments—dead voices outside a carving are always fragments—but the fragments were enough. *Thirteenth turn.* Over and over, in a dozen different mouths, some of them dead five hundred years: *the thirteenth turn, the thirteenth turn, before the thirteenth turn—*

The moon turns thirteen times between midwinter and midsummer. Tonight had been the first.

I closed the folio. I banked the fire. I stood in the doorway of my bedroom and watched Ash sleep, this impossible, borrowed, bargained-for child, and I felt the fear in my chest do a strange thing: stop sloshing around and set, like cooling wax. Take a shape. Become something I could hold.

I had sworn, thirty years ago, never to leave the valley. The stones keep magic out; they also keep people like me sane. Beyond them the dead's voices ran wild and enormous, and I had spent my whole life grateful for the dampening, grateful for the smallness, grateful for the door I'd closed.

Somewhere under that mountain was another door, black stone, taller than any door has a right to be. And somewhere in this valley, tomorrow probably, a stern woman with a ledger was going to come knocking and tell me what the covenant cost and what was due.

"Sleep," I told Ash, though she couldn't hear me, or perhaps because she couldn't. "Whatever's coming, it doesn't get you tonight."

In the morning, I would carve a new question into the oldest bone I owned. But I already knew what the bones remembered. The door. The hand. The name on the contract, which I had not yet let myself read, and which I suspected, with a certainty that settled over me like snowfall, I had known my whole life.

The knock, when it came the next evening, was not Ash's knock—small, polite, three precise raps she'd invented within a day of learning doors. It was hard, official, and impatient, and it belonged, I knew before I opened it, to Warden Odile Ferris.
