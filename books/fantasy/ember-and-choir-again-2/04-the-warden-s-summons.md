## 4. The Warden's Summons

Warden Odile Ferris had not slept well, and she resented the child for it.

She resented many things before breakfast, as a matter of principle. The mist that crept up from the mere and dampened her boots. The rooks that nested in her eaves and argued like lawyers. The way Jude Thackeray always looked at her—politely, patiently, with that particular stillness of a person who has already decided what they will say and is merely waiting for you to finish being wrong. But this morning, standing on the Thackeray doorstep with her grey coat buttoned to the chin and her official staff planted on the flagstone with a crack that startled crows three fields away, Odile Ferris resented the child most of all.

Because the child was the reason the stones were singing wrong.

She had walked the boundary at dawn, as she did every dawn, thirty years without fail. She knew the hum of each stone the way other folk knew the faces of their children. The eastern stone sang low and steady, like a grandmother humming over bread. The western stone had a slight rasp in its note, an old injury from the year the lightning struck. And the northern stone—the one nearest the road out of the world—had always carried a faint sweetness, a high thread of melody woven through its drone.

This morning, all eleven stones had gone quiet. Not silent—Odile Ferris would have preferred silent. Silent meant broken, and broken could be mended. No: they had gone *attentive*. They hummed with their mouths closed, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it. The whole valley, for the first time in her long tenure, was listening to something.

And then there was the matter of the drowned farmer's memorial, which she had inspected personally, because inspecting things personally was half of what a warden was for. The bone carving itself was fine work—Jude Thackeray never did less—but the dead man's voice inside the ivory had been strange. Muffled. Frightened. And every few minutes he had said, quite clearly, in the flat tone the dead used when they said anything at all:

*She is coming.*

Not *who is coming*. Not *when*. Just *she*, over and over, until Odile had wrapped the memorial in oilcloth and put it in her cellar, because a warden who left frightened bones lying about deserved whatever happened next.

So here she stood, on the doorstep, knocking for the third time.

Jude opened the door looking exactly as Odile had known they would: sleeves rolled, bone dust on one cheek, an expression of mild and infuriating calm.

"Warden Ferris," Jude said. "You're up early."

"The valley doesn't keep itself." She stepped past them into the house without asking, because asking implied you might be told no, and Odile Ferris had not been told no in Hollowmere since before some of her current residents were born. "Where is it?"

"Where is what?"

"Don't." She held up one finger. "I am sixty-one years old, Thackeray, I have buried four wardens and outlasted two bishops' inquiries, and I do not have the patience this morning for games of *where is what*. The child. The small barefoot person who walked through eleven warding stones as though they were puddles."

Jude's calm flickered. Only a little—a tightening around the eyes—but Odile had spent thirty years reading faces across official desks, and she saw it land. Good. Fear was appropriate. Fear was the correct response to nearly everything, in Odile's experience, and it saved an enormous amount of time.

"She's asleep," Jude said finally.

"Wake her."

"I'd rather not."

"I'd rather the valley didn't get eaten by whatever owns that child's soul," Odile said, "but here we both are, wanting things."

That landed too. Jude went very still, and then very carefully moved so that their body stood between the interior doorway and the warden—not aggressively, nothing so useful as aggression, just a door becoming a door. Odile almost admired it. Almost.

"You know something," Jude said quietly. "About where she came from."

"I know what I've read." Odile set her staff against the wall and lowered herself into Jude's best chair, the one by the hearth, because if you were going to ruin someone's morning you ought to do it comfortably seated. "Which is more than you do, apparently, or you'd have sent word to me the moment she appeared instead of hiding her for two days and hoping I'd grown sentimental in my dotage."

"Have you?"

"Terribly. Now fetch me tea and the child, or neither, and we'll see how far your hospitality stretches against my jurisdiction."

* * *

The tea was bitter and over-steeped, which Odile drank without comment because commenting would have been gratitude. The child sat on Jude's knee by the fire, blinking sleepily, small feet tucked under the hem of a borrowed nightshirt that had been, until recently, a flour sack. Up close, the strangeness of her was worse than at a distance. Her skin had a faint luminosity, like the moon seen through thin cloud. When she yawned, the firelight bent toward her mouth.

And the bones on Jude's shelves—all those years of carved memorials, each one murmuring its small eternal murmur—had gone utterly, perfectly silent. Odile could feel the silence like a weight behind her ears. Thirty years in this valley, and she had forgotten what true quiet sounded like. It sounded like this: like held breath. Like the moment before lightning.

"That's not natural," she said.

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't."

"Do you understand what I'm about to tell you?" Odile set down her cup. "I'm going to tell you it once. I don't repeat myself; repetition breeds familiarity, familiarity breeds carelessness, and carelessness is how valleys die. So listen.

"Hollowmere is not hidden by accident. Every child here learns that the boundary stones repel magic, that no spell crosses them, that we are safe. What they don't learn—and what the wardens are taught, and what has been passed from warden to warden since before my grandmother's grandmother—is *why* the stones work. Magic doesn't simply bounce off them, Thackeray. Magic is *paid for*. Someone paid, long ago, for this valley's invisibility. Bought its silence the way you'd buy a horse: outright, with a signature, no refunds."

Jude's arms had tightened around the child. "Paid with what?"

"There's the question, isn't it." Odile gazed into the fire. "My predecessor's predecessor kept records. Most of them burned—conveniently, in my opinion, though I was young and suspicious then and I remain old and suspicious now. But fragments survived. A covenant, sealed beneath Mount Serel, in the court that lives under it. The Undercourt, the old texts call it. The Magistrate of Debts. Names that sound like stories to frighten children, except our valley is the story, and somebody signed us into it.

"And here is the part you must hold onto." She leaned forward. "A bargain like that doesn't end. It matures. It sits under the mountain like a debt in a ledger, gathering interest, waiting for its term. For thirty years—my whole tenure, Thackeray, every dawn I've walked those stones—Hollowmere has sat quiet inside its purchase. Nothing new. Nothing anomalous. We were invisible because we were *unremarkable*, and unremarkable is a kind of armor.

"Then this child walks through wards that have turned back everything from plague-spirits to a tax collector with a grudge, and the stones start *listening*, and the dead in your shelves start whispering *she is coming*, and you tell me, Thackeray—no. Don't tell me. You can't tell me, because you don't know, and not knowing is precisely the problem."

"She's just a child," Jude said. Their voice had gone rough. "Look at her. Seven years old, maybe eight. Barefoot in autumn. Whoever sold her, whoever made her, whatever's owed—she didn't sign anything."

"No," Odile agreed, and found, to her irritation, that saying it cost her something. "She didn't. That is the entire tragedy of contracts, and why I despise them professionally. The signature belongs to someone else; the payment comes due in the smallest hands available." She rose, retrieved her staff, and stood a moment with her back to the fire. "But understand me. If the Undercourt sends anything to reclaim her—if so much as one of its clerks sets foot past my stones—then the covenant is breached. The purchase voids. And everything Hollowmere bought with whatever price was paid long ago—every ward, every silence, every safe night—evaporates. Not just for her. For everyone. The miller. The Broadfoots. Old Marta and her bees. Three hundred souls who never asked to be collateral."

"So what are you telling me to do?" Jude asked. "Send her out? Hand her to the road, alone, seven years old, in the dark?"

"I'm telling you I don't know yet." It came out harsher than intended, and Odile, who prided herself on never saying anything unintended, let it stand anyway. "I'm telling you I need three days. There's a record—a ledger fragment—in the warden's vault, sealed, older than my office. My predecessors sealed it for a reason, and I have spent thirty years respecting seals, but thirty years may be about to come due alongside everything else. I'm going to open it. And when I know what was paid for this valley, and when, and by whom—" she stopped herself. Something in Jude's face. Something in the way the child had curled smaller against their chest, awake now and watching Odile with those too-luminous eyes, unblinking, unafraid.

Unafraid. Of *her*. Children feared Odile Ferris; it was one of the few consistent pleasures of her office. This child looked at her the way one looks at weather.

"One condition," Odile said. "Until I return, she stays indoors after dusk. No boundary walks. No visits to the mere. And Thackeray—" she paused at the door, hand on the latch, not turning around, "—if you decide to run, do me the courtesy of doing it badly enough that I catch you. Because whatever's coming for that child, it will follow her trail straight through my valley, and I would rather it followed her out than in."

The door closed behind her. Outside, the mist was lifting off the mere, and the eleven stones hummed their closed-mouthed hum, attentive, patient, listening.

In the cellar below the warden's house, wrapped in oilcloth beside forty years of confiscated curiosities, the drowned farmer's memorial whispered into the dark:

*She is coming.*

And for the first time in thirty years, Warden Odile Ferris found herself wondering whether the dead ever meant anyone good.
