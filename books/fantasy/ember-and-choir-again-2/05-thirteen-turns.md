## 5. Thirteen Turns

The elder's house smelled of beeswax and dried yarrow and the particular sweetness of a life being slowly put away—drawers emptied a little at a time, linens folded for whoever would come after. Jude had known Maren Ostwick their whole thirty years in Hollowmere. She had taught them the first grip of the carving knife when their hands were too small to close around it properly, laughing through her nose the way she did, saying, *You'll hold it like that until you learn better, and then you'll hold it like that forever.*

Now her hands lay on the quilt like two pale roots, too thin to hold anything.

"You're staring," she said without opening her eyes.

"I'm visiting."

"You're staring with your mouth doing that thing it does when you're worried but want everyone to think you've come for the honey cakes." Her eyes opened then, milky-bright, finding Jude's face with an accuracy that never failed to unsettle people who assumed blindness was deafness. "Sit down before you wear a groove in my floorboards. The child can sit too."

Ash sat. Ash always sat when offered a chair, with the grave courtesy of someone who had perhaps been a duchess in some other arrangement of bones, or a cat. She folded her hands in her lap and looked around the room with those dark, patient eyes, and Maren looked back at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that Jude could not read and did not like.

"Well," Maren said. "So it's true. The valley's gone quiet as a held breath since she came, and every bone I own is humming like a struck bell." She turned her head on the pillow toward Jude. "You came to ask me about the covenant."

"I came for the honey cakes."

"Jude Thackeray. I am ninety-one years old and my heart is quitting its post like a soldier at the end of a long war. Do not insult us both."

Jude sat down. Outside the small window, the boundary stones on the far ridge caught the afternoon light, and if one listened—and Jude always listened—one could hear them humming their low, patient note, the sound that meant *safe*, the sound that meant *nothing gets in, nothing gets out*. For thirty years that hum had been the floor beneath everything. Now it felt like a lid.

"The bones won't tell me more than they have," Jude said. "A door under a mountain. A woman's hand signing. That's all thirteen readings gave me, and the last three just showed the door again, like the dead were tired of repeating themselves."

"They are tired." Maren's breath rattled faintly, a sound like wind through reeds. "They've been shouting about this for days, haven't they? Through everything you carve. You've got the look of a person who hasn't slept in a week and has heard nothing good in any of it."

The dead had indeed been shouting. Every piece of ivory Jude touched now carried the same urgent chorus: *the turn, the turn, count the turns.* They had said it through a spoon. They had said it through the memorial beam for old Hollis the cooper, which was meant to be solemn and dignified and instead had practically vibrated off the workbench. Jude had begun wearing mittens indoors, which fooled no one.

"Count the turns," Jude repeated now. "That's what they keep saying. I don't know what it means."

Maren was quiet for so long that Jude thought she'd drifted off. Then she said, "Bring me the box on the mantel. The little one with the cracked lid. Mind the crack; it bites."

The box was old horn, worn smooth by generations of thumbs, and inside it lay a single sliver of bone—not carved, not yet, just a raw pale curl of it, kept wrapped in cloth gone soft with age. Jude knew what it was. Everyone in Hollowmere over a certain age knew what it was, though no one spoke of it: a piece of the first warding, from the year the founders had bought the valley its silence.

"Hold it," Maren said. "Just hold it. Don't carve. Some things only speak unworked."

Jude held it.

And the dead spoke—but not the local dead, not the murmuring chorus of Hollowmere's familiar departed, the drowned farmers and failed brewers and one memorably dramatic miller. This voice was older. It came up through the bone like water coming up through stone, cold and slow and absolutely certain, and it said:

*Thirteen turns of the moon from the crossing. On the thirteenth, the debt comes due, and what was sold is gathered.*

The voice faded. The room came back—the beeswax, the yarrow, the small snoring weight of Ash now curled sideways in her chair, asleep as easily as a cat in sun. Jude realized their hands were shaking and set the bone down very carefully, the way one sets down a sleeping animal.

"That's how long?" Their voice came out strange. "How many turns have passed?"

"She crossed the stones—" Maren counted silently, lips moving, "—nine nights ago. So you have four turns left. Four moons, Jude. Then whatever holds that child together stops holding, and the Undercourt reaches in and takes what it was promised."

"But she's *here*." Jude heard their own voice climb and hated it. "She's inside the stones. Nothing crosses the stones. That's the entire point of the valley—that's what the warding *is*. Magic can't reach in here. No scrying, no summoning, no—"

"No," Maren agreed mildly. "It can't reach in."

"Then she's safe. We wait four turns and the contract—what, expires? Fails? A debt unpaid is a debt forgiven, isn't that how it works?"

"Oh, Jude." Maren reached out and took their hand, and her grip was still stronger than it had any right to be, the grip of a woman who had pulled calves into the world and coffins into the ground and once, famously, dragged the Warden herself out of a flooded culvert by the collar. "You sweet, stubborn thing. Think about what you just heard. *What was sold is gathered.* Not *the debt fails*. Not *the bargain breaks*. Gathered. The contract doesn't need to cross the stones, because the contract isn't outside. It's in her. It's wound through whatever she is, thread by thread, and on the thirteenth turn it pulls tight from the inside."

Jude looked at Ash, asleep in the chair, her small chest rising and falling, one hand open against her knee. Perfectly ordinary. Perfectly, impossibly alive.

"There must be a way to break it," Jude said. "There's always a way. Contracts have terms. Loopholes. There's a whole shelf of law-books in the Warden's office about—"

"And who wrote those law-books? The founders. The same people who made the bargain." Maren closed her eyes again. "I was a girl when the last elder tried to explain the covenant to a frightened valley. I'll tell you what she told us, and then you'll let an old woman nap, because dying is tiring work and I intend to do it properly.

"A long time ago—before the stones were stones, when they were just ideas in the minds of desperate people—Hollowmere was going to be destroyed. Not by armies. Armies we could have survived; there's nothing here worth burning. By magic. By the courts below and the powers above both deciding that a valley full of people who could hear the dead was a valley full of loose ends. Your gift, Jude. Our gift. Everyone born here with the hearing—it wasn't a blessing to the world. It was evidence. Proof that the wall between things was thinner than anyone wanted admitted. And thin walls get torn down, or filled in, or the people standing near them get disappeared.

"So the founders went looking for a buyer. And they found one."

"The Undercourt," Jude said quietly.

"The Undercourt. Beneath Mount Serel, where debts are currency and souls are collateral and everything is written down in ledgers that cannot lie. The founders sold something enormous—I've never learned exactly what, and the ones who knew took it to their graves rather than carve it—to buy the warding. The silence. The stones that say *this valley does not exist, please move along*. And it worked. It worked for two hundred years." Maren opened her eyes. "But bargains like that aren't paid once, child. They're paid in installments. And somewhere along the way, someone started making payments on the family account."

Jude's mouth had gone dry. "My mother."

"I don't know. The dead here won't speak of her—you've noticed. Thirty years and not one whisper, not even through the freshest bone. That's not grief, that's *terms*. Someone bought her silence out of the record, and the only power I know that buys things out of records lives under a mountain." Maren's hand tightened. "Whatever she did, she did it before you were old enough to remember, and she did it believing she was protecting you. People under mountains rarely mean harm, Jude. They mean *profit*. It's worse."

* * *

Jude walked home the long way, along the stream path, because the long way took forty minutes and they needed all forty.

Four turns. Four moons. The arithmetic of it sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. Nine nights ago a barefoot child had appeared at their door speaking a language older than kingdoms, and the dead had gone silent for the first time in Jude's remembered life, and Jude had thought—foolishly, shamefully—they had thought *finally, quiet, rest*. They had named her Ash because of the pale streak in her hair and because names were the first thing you gave a person and the last thing you could take back.

Now the name felt like a promise Jude didn't know how to keep.

Because here was the shape of it, the ugly geometry: the contract lived in Ash, and the contract would pull tight on the thirteenth turn whether she stood inside the stones or outside them. The valley couldn't protect her. The valley had never been able to protect her; the valley could barely protect itself, and only by pretending the rest of the world wasn't there.

Which meant leaving.

Jude stopped at the footbridge and gripped the rail and made themselves think the word properly, all the way through, the way you make yourself look at a wound.

*Leaving.* Crossing the boundary stones. Walking out of Hollowmere for the first time in thirty years.

They had come here at nineteen with a knapsack and a letter of introduction from a master carver who had been glad to see the back of them, and they had sworn—had actually spoken the vow aloud, standing between the two great gate-stones with the Warden watching—that they would never leave. Because outside, the hearing was unbearable. Inside the valley, the dead murmured: gentle, muffled, a conversation held through several walls. Outside, it was a flood. Every battlefield, every plague pit, every lonely crossroads where somebody had died badly and unfinished—all of it pouring into a mind with nowhere to set it down. Jude had lasted eleven days in the lowlands at nineteen, and had come home with their hands over their ears and blood in one nostril, and had sworn the vow with real gratitude and never once regretted it.

Thirty years of comfortable loneliness. That was the phrase they used, even to themselves, especially to themselves. A small life, deliberately made small: the workshop, the stream path, the memorial beams and cradle-posts and the occasional spoon. Neighbors who were fond of them in the distant, respectful way one is fond of a well. The dead for company, because the dead required nothing and asked nothing and never left.

And now the arithmetic said: four turns, and then either you carry that child across the stones into the flood, or you stand here and watch her unravel into whatever the Undercourt gathers souls into.

"It's not fair," Jude said aloud to the stream, which had heard worse. "I did everything right. I stayed. I kept the vow. I made myself small and quiet and useful, and I was *content*, damn it, I was actually content—"

The stream said nothing, being a stream. But downstream, half-sunk in the shallows, an old cow's jawbone that Tam Broadfoot had tossed in years ago and Jude had never gotten around to retrieving began, faintly, to hum. Jude could feel it from twenty feet away, a buzz against the teeth: the dead, agreeing with them, or mocking them, or possibly just singing along. With the dead it was genuinely hard to tell.

* * *

That evening, Ash sat on the workshop floor sorting bone scraps by size with tremendous seriousness while Jude pretended to work on a butter stamp and actually watched her.

She had grown, in nine days. Not taller—something else. More *there*. When she'd arrived she'd seemed almost translucent at the edges, a sketch of a child, and Jude had told themselves it was exhaustion, road-dust, imagination. But tonight, firelight went through her fingertips if you looked at the right angle, and her shadow on the wall flickered slightly out of rhythm with her body, a half-beat behind, like a dancer counting a different song.

On the thirteenth turn, the contract pulls tight from the inside.

"Ash," Jude said. She looked up. She always looked up when they said her name, quick and total, as if the word were a physical thing being handed to her. "Do you dream?"

Ash considered this. Communication between them remained mostly gesture and tone, though she had begun, lately, to mimic words—shaping them silently, tasting them. Now she nodded, then paused, then tilted her hand back and forth in the air. *Yes and no.* Then she pointed at her own chest, and then downward, and made a slow closing motion with both fists, like something being gathered in.

Jude's stomach dropped. "Something pulls you down."

Ash nodded. Unbothered. She picked up another scrap of bone, held it to the light, put it in the small pile.

"And when the moon finishes turning—" Jude stopped. Started again, gentler. "You know what happens. Don't you. You've known the whole time."

Ash set down the bone. She looked at Jude with those dark patient eyes, and then she did something new: she reached out and touched the back of Jude's hand, once, lightly, the way you touch a thing you're afraid of breaking. And her mouth moved around silent syllables that Jude's gift translated without permission, the way it translated everything, the dead's meaning rising up through living flesh for the first time ever—

*It's all right. I was borrowed anyway.*

"No," Jude said, too loudly. Ash blinked. "No, it is not all right, you are not *borrowed*, you are—" A child. Mine, went the thought, arriving unbidden and enormous, mine the way the valley was mine, the way the vow was mine, the way every deliberately small day had been mine. "You're staying. Whatever it costs."

Ash smiled then—a real smile, crooked and sudden, the first one—and went back to sorting her bones, satisfied, as if the matter were settled.

Outside, the moon was already a fat silver coin above the ridge, three nights from full. First turn of the remaining four. Jude stood in the doorway a long time, listening to the boundary stones hum their safe, useless lullaby, and understood with perfect clarity that the vow they'd made at nineteen was about to be broken, and that the person who'd made it—the small, careful, contented person they'd spent thirty years building—was not going to survive the crossing.

Only Jude would. Whoever Jude became on the other side.

Behind them, Ash hummed three notes of a lullaby Jude's mother used to sing, and did not know why it made the carver go white as fresh ivory.

Three days later, a stranger arrived at the boundary asking questions—a woman with a singer's posture and a prisoner's eyes—and Warden Ferris sent for Jude before the woman had even finished giving her name.
