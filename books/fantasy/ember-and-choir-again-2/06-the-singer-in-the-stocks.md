## 6. The Singer in the Stocks

The trouble with being famous, Wren Alaire had decided somewhere around her third week in the stocks, was that everyone who passed felt entitled to an opinion.

"You're the one who cracked the glass," said a baker's boy, mouth full of bun, as though she were a curiosity at a fair rather than a woman slowly learning the exact shape of her own spine.

"Cracked it clean through," Wren said. "Fourteen panes. You could hear it sing for a mile."

"They say the note was forbidden."

"They say correctly."

"Why'd you sing it, then?"

Wren considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, which was none. "Because I was asked not to," she said, and the boy laughed, wiped his hands on his apron, and went on his way, and she settled back against the wooden collar as well as a person can settle back against anything designed by someone who hates necks.

Three weeks in the stocks outside the courthouse at Vell. Not the dungeon—she'd earned the dungeon, honestly, and part of her had been almost flattered when the Undercourt's bailiffs came for her—but the Magistrate's people had a flair for public punishment. Why lock a voice away where no one could enjoy its ruin when you could display it? The stocks were positioned precisely where the morning market traffic crossed the evening market traffic, so that every soul in Vell had two daily opportunities to look at her and feel better about their own lives.

It was, she had to admit, effective.

Her hair, once the celebrated black waterfall that had made three duchies weep, hung in greasy ropes. Her throat was fine—that was the joke of it; they hadn't touched her voice, because a Voice of the Undercourt couldn't be unmade, only misused—but everything else ached. Her shoulders had developed opinions. Her knees had filed formal complaints. And the sun, that great indifferent lamp, had burned her nose to a shade she privately called *humiliated*.

Still. There were worse fates than the stocks. She knew several intimately. She had, in fact, been present at the creation of a few.

"Wren Alaire." A shadow fell across her, and she opened one eye.

The man standing over her wore the gray livery of the Court of Debts, which meant he was either a bailiff or a debt-collector, and in Vell those professions overlapped like a Venn diagram drawn by a drunk. He had the face of a man who counted other people's sorrows for a living and found the arithmetic satisfying.

"Bailiff Corven," she said. "Come to gloat? You'll have to wait your turn, there's a queue."

"I've come with an offer."

That got both eyes open. Offers, in her experience, arrived dressed as threats wearing their good clothes.

"The court is prepared to commute your sentence," Corven said, "in exchange for services rendered."

"Ah." She let her head loll back against the wood. "You want me to sing again."

"I want you to *listen*. There is a difference, and it is the difference between what you were and what you are now."

Charming. She smiled anyway, because smiling was free and everything else cost. "Go on, then. What am I listening for?"

Corven glanced left, then right, then leaned down as if sharing a confidence with a particularly disappointing niece. "A child has gone missing from the ledgers. A soul-child, woven, thirteen threads. It walked out of Mount Serel's underways eleven days ago and has not been recovered."

Something cold slid down Wren's spine and pooled at the base of it, where all her worst knowledge lived.

Soul-children were not supposed to walk anywhere. They were not supposed to do much of anything except exist in their alcoves beneath Serel, humming quietly, keeping the deep fires lit. A woven soul was a tool, the way a bellows was a tool, and nobody worried about the feelings of a bellows.

Eleven days loose. That wasn't an escape. Escapes were loud, and this would have been louder than most. Eleven days without recovery meant either the child was very clever or someone was hiding it, and Wren had met exactly one person clever enough and no one brave enough, so: clever child, hidden, somewhere in the world above, carrying thirteen souls' worth of weight in a body the size of a bread loaf.

"And why," she said carefully, "does the court need my ears?"

"Because the child speaks in the Old Tongue, and there are perhaps four living throats that know it, and yours is one. Because it will leave traces—candles guttering, ghosts stirring, small wrongnesses—and you spent six years reading such signs for us before you disgraced yourself. Because the Magistrate wants it found *quietly*, before the thirteenth turn, and quiet work requires a quiet instrument." He straightened. "Find it, deliver word, and your sentence ends. Refuse, and you rot here until the crows file their own reports."

He produced a key, unlocked the stocks' wrist-pin—not freeing her, merely loosening her enough to sign—and held out a contract on paper thin enough to read a lie through.

Wren looked at it for a long moment.

Here was the thing about Wren Alaire that the ballads never got right: the ballads said she sang the forbidden note out of passion, out of principle, out of some grand artist's fury. Nonsense. She sang it because she had been ordered to sing something else, something worse—a soul out of a body, a specific soul, a girl of nineteen who had owed the court eleven crowns—and Wren had stood in the throne room with the glass chandeliers trembling and thought, *no*, and put that refusal into a note instead of words, because notes were the only language she'd ever trusted.

Passion, principle. Fine. But underneath it, always, was the arithmetic of a coward: she had refused once, at tremendous cost, and had sworn never to be in a position to refuse again. Six years since. Six years of small songs, safe songs, songs that opened nothing and closed nothing and changed nothing.

And now the court wanted her back. Wanted her ears, her craft, her particular talent for finding things that did not wish to be found.

They wanted her to hunt a child.

"Terms," Wren said.

Corven blinked. "The terms are as stated—"

"The terms are amended. I work alone. No handlers. No tail. And I keep whatever I find until I choose to report it." She held out her hand for the pen. "I'm told I crack things when I'm annoyed. Glass. Contracts. Hearts, allegedly. You don't want me annoyed."

He hesitated—the hesitation of a man doing sums—and then set the paper on her knee and gave her the pen.

She signed *W. Alaire* in a hand far too elegant for a woman in stocks, blew on the ink, and said, sweetly: "Now untie me properly. I have a reputation to rebuild and it can't be done sitting down."

* * *

Freedom, it turned out, smelled like horse, river mud, and her own unwashed self, and Wren loved every note of it.

She took a room at the Drowned Bell—cheap, because the innkeeper owed her from the old days and knew better than to collect—and spent her first free hour buying a bath, a meal, and information, in ascending order of expense.

The information came from Sefa, who ran the pawnshop on Tallow Row and knew everything that happened within thirty miles of Vell, provided it was illegal, embarrassing, or both.

"A soul-child," Sefa repeated, turning her pipe in her fingers. "Funny thing to ask after."

"Funny season generally."

"Mm. There's talk." Sefa leaned forward, lowering her voice into the register reserved for merchandise. "Talk out of the lowland villages, west and south. Candles guttering where no draft runs. An old ghost in Marrowfen churchyard sat up in his grave and asked for breakfast—first time in forty years anyone's heard him. And a tinker swears he saw a little girl walking the river road barefoot, alone, singing in a tongue that made his teeth ache. Headed west, he said. West and south."

West and south. Wren turned that over. West of Vell lay farmland, river country, and then—past the hills nobody farmed because the hills didn't permit it—the valley. Hollowmere. The place that repelled magic the way oiled cloth repels rain, the place even the Undercourt's ledgers marked with a blank page and a note in the margin: *do not pursue*.

A soul-child walking toward the one place in the world the Undercourt couldn't follow.

Either the child was lucky, or the child was smart, or somebody inside that silent valley had opened a door that should have stayed shut. Any of the three made an interesting story. All three together made a fortune.

Because that was the plan. Let's be honest with ourselves, Wren told her reflection in Sefa's smudged window. The plan was never rescue. The plan was: find the child, confirm the child, and then sell the finding to whichever party paid best. The court wanted it quietly; but the court's enemies wanted it loudly, and there were collectors in this world who paid in gold for a woven soul with thirteen threads, and Wren had been poor for six years and tired of it for five and a half.

She was a coward, yes. But she was a coward with excellent taste in exits, and she intended to exit this situation rich.

She bought a horse—half-dead, cheap, honest about both qualities—and rode west.

* * *

She caught up with them on the fourth day, at a crossroads shrine where travelers left offerings to saints whose names had worn off the stone.

She had expected to find a fugitive. A smuggler, maybe, or a desperate parent, someone moving fast and looking over their shoulder. She had rehearsed the approach: dismount at a distance, hands visible, voice pleasant, the whole soft-shoe routine she'd learned in six years of court work.

What she found instead was a picnic.

There was a big man sitting on a milestone, built like a door, holding a small girl on his knee while she solemnly examined a wildflower he'd picked for her. Beside them, a second figure—tall, lean, travel-worn, with the careful hands of a craftsman—was laying out bread and hard cheese on a cloth with the ceremony of a state dinner. And the girl—

The girl was laughing. Silent laughter, Wren noticed, her whole body shaking with it, no sound coming out, and yet the sound of it reached Wren anyway, somehow, the way a struck bell reaches you through a floor.

Then the girl looked up and saw her, and stopped.

And the world went quiet.

Not metaphorically. Wren had spent her life inside sound the way fish spend their lives inside water; she heard the hum of the earth's own deep note, the choir beneath everything, the sound the Undercourt had trained her to hear and to use. It had been with her every moment since she was four years old. It was with her now, faintly, under the wind.

When the child looked at her, it stopped.

Total silence. The first Wren had ever known. It was like being suddenly unable to see, or breathe, or remember her own name. It was the most terrifying and the most beautiful thing that had ever happened to her, and she sat her half-dead horse in the middle of the road with her mouth open like an idiot while a seven-year-old unmade the foundation of her entire existence.

The tall one rose slowly, putting a hand on the girl's shoulder, stepping slightly in front of her. Protective. Good instincts. The hands said carver; the stance said someone who'd learned recently and thoroughly that the world required defending against.

"You're a long way from any court," the tall one said. Voice low, level, watchful. "We don't want trouble."

"Neither do I," Wren said, and was disturbed to find she mostly meant it. "I heard there was a child traveling these roads. Alone. Barefoot. I—" She stopped. Reorganized. Tried honesty, which was unfamiliar territory and the ground was uneven. "I came to find you. I was going to sell you."

Silence, still. The big man had gone very still, the way large men do when they're deciding whether to be gentle.

"Sell," the tall one repeated flatly.

"To the court. To collectors. Whoever paid." Wren swung down off the horse, because it seemed important, suddenly, to be lower than them, closer to the ground, less of a tower. "I signed a contract three days ago. Find the soul-child, report it, earn my freedom. That was the whole shape of my ambition. I want you to understand that going in, because I'm told trust works better when it starts with the ugly parts."

The girl tilted her head. Studied Wren with eyes that were too old and too young at once, thirteen someones looking out through one small face. Then she did something extraordinary.

She walked past her guardian's leg, right up to Wren, and held out the wildflower.

Wren looked at it. A common wayside bloom, slightly crushed from a small fist, petals gone crooked.

"For me?" she said stupidly.

The girl nodded once, gravely, and pressed it into Wren's palm, and closed Wren's fingers over it with both her small hands, and patted them twice, as if sealing a bargain far more binding than any Wren had signed in her life.

And the silence broke—not all at once, but gently, the deep hum of the world returning like tide coming back over sand—and Wren Alaire, disgraced Voice of the Undercourt, breaker of throne-room glass, coward of six years' standing, discovered that her eyes were wet and had absolutely no professional explanation for it.

"Well," she managed. "That's that, then."

"That's what?" the tall one asked.

"I'm not selling you." She tucked the flower into her breast pocket with the absurd care of a woman pocketing a crown jewel. "I'm apparently—" She gestured vaguely at the picnic, the milestone, the big man already making room on the cloth without having discussed it with anyone. "I'm apparently having lunch with you. And after that, I suppose, I'm taking you to Mount Serel, because that's where your contract lives, and I'm the only person alive who knows both the way in and the song that opens it. And I'll probably die doing it. But first, cheese."

The tall one studied her for a long moment. "You realize we have no reason to trust you."

"None whatsoever," Wren agreed cheerfully, sitting down on the grass and accepting a heel of bread. "But I cracked fourteen panes of enchanted glass with one note, I know the mountain's doors by heart, and I make excellent company. Weigh it how you like."

The girl climbed into her lap as if it were furniture that had always belonged to her, and pointed at Wren's throat, and then at the air, and made a small expectant motion with her hand.

"She wants a song," the big man rumbled, with the first smile Wren had seen on him.

"Of course she does." Wren looked down at the child—at the thirteen souls wearing one face, at the flower-crushed fist of her, at the whole impossible miracle of her—and felt the last plank of her cynicism go out from under her feet like a pier in a storm.

"One song," she said. "And then we ride, because the moon's counting down and I know exactly how many turns it has left."

She sang. And far to the west, under stone older than kingdoms, something began—very softly—to listen.
