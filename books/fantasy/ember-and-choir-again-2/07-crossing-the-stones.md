## 7. Crossing the Stones

The moon was a fingernail of bone in the sky, and Jude Thackeray had spent the whole day lying to their neighbors.

They had told Maren Cottle they were taking Ash on a walking holiday to see the lowland orchards. They had told the miller's boy that Tam Broadfoot was coming along to carry the packs, because Tam was broad and honest and could be trusted with luggage, which everyone agreed was the truest thing ever said about him. And they had told themselves, for most of the afternoon, that this was not the worst idea they had ever had, though by sundown even that lie had begun to fray.

"You're doing it wrong," Ash said.

Jude paused with one boot half-laced. "Doing what?"

"Everything." The girl sat on the edge of the bed, feet swinging—bare feet still, because she had refused shoes for three days running and Jude had finally decided to lose that particular battle at a more convenient time. She was watching Jude pack with the grave attention of a small magistrate. "You fold like you're saying goodbye."

"I am folding a shirt."

"You fold *like*—" Ash frowned, hunting for words. She had been learning common speech for two weeks now, word by hard-won word, and when she couldn't find one she made her hands do the talking instead. Now her fingers walked up into the air and then drooped, wilting, mournful. "*Good-bye*-shaped."

Jude looked down at the shirt. It was, admittedly, folded goodbye-shaped.

"It's a long trip," they said, which was true, and also a lie, because the truth was that Jude had never once left the valley in thirty years, and had sworn—at fifteen, at twenty, at thirty-five, swearing being something Jude did often and solemnly and always with an audience of murmuring bones—that no power under sky or stone would drag them past the boundary stones.

And here they were, dragged. By a seven-year-old in bare feet.

Ash watched them a moment longer, then hopped off the bed and went to the window, where she liked to sit in the evenings. Outside, Hollowmere lay quiet under the thin moon: the crooked lanes, the smoke-smudge of chimneys, the far pale gleam of the boundary stones ringing the valley like a necklace of teeth. Somewhere out there a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The dead murmured in their usual way through every carved bone in the house—the lintel humming an old harvest song, the shelf whispering about weather, the little memorial figures on the mantel gossiping gently among themselves like old women at a well.

It was, Jude thought, the sound of safety. It had been the sound of their whole life.

Then Ash hummed—one note, soft, in that language older than kingdoms—and every bone in the house went silent at once, the way birds go silent before a storm.

"There," Ash said, satisfied. "Better."

"Ash, we've talked about doing that indoors."

She looked over her shoulder, unrepentant. Her eyes were dark and enormous and slightly translucent at the edges now, if Jude let themselves really look, which mostly they didn't. Two weeks since she'd appeared on the doorstep. Two weeks of divinations that all showed the same thing: a door beneath a mountain, a woman's hand signing a contract, thirteen moons turning toward a date that could not be missed. Eleven turns gone already. The moon above them was the twelfth.

Outside, someone knocked softly on the door, three times, apologetic.

"That'll be Tam," Jude said, grateful for anything that wasn't the future.

* * *

Tam Broadfoot stood in the lane with a pack the size of a coffin and the expression of a man who had been talked into something against his better judgment, his judgment, and the judgment of several ancestors he respected.

"Evening, Jude," he said. "I've come to say I'm not coming."

"Hello, Tam."

"I mean it. I lay awake all night composing my refusal. Very firm. My mother helped."

"How is your mother?"

"She says I'm a fool, but she packed me sausage anyway, so interpret that as you will." He shifted the enormous pack higher on his shoulders. He was a big man, Tam, gravedigger's son and part-time fence-mender, with a face built for patient sorrow and hands built for holding things together. He looked at the pack on his own back as though it had betrayed him personally. "The point is, I'm not coming. I've thought it over very carefully, and leaving the valley is mad, and I won't do it."

"Of course," Jude said. "Would you help us as far as the stones, then? Just to the edge. Ash walks slowly."

Tam opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked down, where Ash had appeared beside him without making a sound—she did that, moved like a draft under a door—and taken hold of two of his fingers with one small hand.

"I walk slow," she agreed, looking up at him.

Tam's face did the thing it always did around Ash: the whole architecture of reluctance collapsing quietly, like a snowbank in March. "...Just to the stones," he said, in the voice of a man negotiating with himself and losing. "And then I'm turning round. I want that understood. I have responsibilities. I have a *fence*. The Hendersons' goats have been eyeing it all season."

"The Hendersons' goats," Jude said gravely, "are a serious matter."

"They are, actually."

They went down the lane together under the fingernail moon, three shapes and a coffin-sized pack, past shuttered windows and the well and the meeting hall where Warden Odile Ferris's notice still hung, freshly inked, its message plain: *The child is an anomaly. The child must be given over. The covenant holds only so long as we keep our bargains.* Ferris had come to the door herself two days ago, stern as a boundary stone, and spoken of the many weighed against the one, and Jude had heard her out and then shut the door very politely and begun packing goodbye-shaped shirts.

The dead murmured along the lane as they passed—through the gateposts, the grave-fences, the little carved markers folk kept on their sills. They were uneasy tonight. Jude could feel it the way you feel a change in weather: the whispers running quick and thin, like water before frost.

"They know," Tam said quietly.

"Everyone knows. Everyone just doesn't know they know yet."

At the last bend of the lane Jude stopped, turned, and looked back once at the cottage—the lit window, the mantel of memorial figures, thirty years of a life deliberately made small and comfortable as a cupped hand. Ash squeezed their fingers.

"Bye-shaped," she said softly.

"Yes," Jude said. "All right. Yes."

They went on.

* * *

The boundary stones stood in a ring on the valley's rim, and up close they were less like teeth than like sleeping animals—great rounded monoliths taller than two men, furred with moss, humming. That was the thing visitors never believed and neighbors stopped hearing after a while: the stones hummed. A low, continuous note, felt in the fillings of your teeth more than heard, steady as a heartbeat, older than the village, older than the covenant, older probably than most of the gods currently in business.

Every child in Hollowmere learned the rule before they learned letters: *the stones keep magic out, and keep it in, and nothing crosses that isn't invited.*

Nobody, in thirty years, had explained to Jude how one went about inviting oneself out.

"We go between the tall one and the split one," Tam said. He had stopped a dozen paces off and was hanging back with the air of a man at the exact limit of his commitment. "That's the shepherd's gap. Old Hew used to drive his flock through there when the low passes flooded. Sheep cross fine."

"And people?"

"People," Tam said, "have historically stayed on this side of it. That's rather the entire arrangement."

Ash was already walking toward the gap. Bare feet silent on the frost-stiff grass, dark hair drifting, edges faintly luminous in the moonlight—and the nearer she came to the stones, the louder they hummed, the note climbing from a heartbeat toward something with an opinion.

Jude's mouth had gone dry. Thirty years. Every oath, every careful year, every night falling asleep to the sweet murmur of the dead in the walls. Beyond those stones was everything the valley existed to exclude: wild magic, hungry ghosts, courts beneath mountains, a world where souls were sold and songs could crack glass. Beyond those stones, Jude would be—

"Jude." Ash held out her hand.

They took it. Her palm was cool and faintly buzzing, like touching a struck bell.

Between the stones, the humming became a wall. Jude felt it press against them physically, a pressure on the chest, on the ears, on whatever part of a person knows itself—and within that pressure, for one vertiginous moment, Jude heard the dead of Hollowmere all at once, every whisper in every bone in the valley rising together like a congregation standing for a hymn, and the sound they made was not a warning or a lament but simply, terribly, *farewell*—

and then the wall broke, and they were through.

* * *

Afterward, Jude could never describe it except as drowning in reverse.

Magic hit them like cold water—like the plunge pool below Serel's meltwater falls, the one the daring boys dared each other toward and never quite touched. It came up through the ground and down through the sky and sideways out of the very grass, and it filled every hollow place in Jude that thirty years inside the stones had left empty, and the hollow places, it turned out, were nearly all of them.

Jude dropped to their knees in the wet grass beyond the gap.

Because the voices came back.

Not the valley's voices—those were left behind behind the stones, murmuring their polite domestic murmur through lintels and grave-fences. These were *other*. These were everywhere. The lowland dead rose around Jude like fish surfacing in a pond at feeding time: the shepherd Hew, dead forty years and still fussing about his flock; a Roman soldier whose name had worn away but whose homesickness hadn't; children of some plague-year playing some plague-year game in the ruins of a sheepfold; an entire churchyard's worth of the modest departed two hills over, all of them talking at once, none of them quiet, none of them dampened, none of them filtered through ivory and courtesy. Inside Hollowmere the dead whispered. Out here they *spoke*, they *shouted*, they *sang off-key and argued about fences and recited debts and asked who was going to feed the dog*—

"Jude?" Tam's voice, alarmed, very far away. "Jude, you've gone white as the stones. Should I fetch—"

"*Quiet,*" Jude breathed, and then, because the dead were asking too, "*please*."

The world roared. The world *chattered*. A thousand years of the unquiet ordinary pressed in on Jude from every direction, and terror and exhilaration hit them simultaneously and fused into something that had no name, and Jude knelt in the frost grass outside the valley of Hollowmere and laughed until it turned into something else, and Ash crouched beside them and put her arms around their neck and patted their back in the brisk manner of a much older person comforting a child.

"Loud," she observed.

"So loud," Jude managed.

"You get used." She considered. "No. You don't. But loud is alive."

Somewhere in the roar, Jude registered, distantly, that Tam Broadfoot had crossed the stones after all—that he was standing on the outside too, pack and all, staring back at the gap with an expression of profound grievance.

"Well," Tam said. "Well, that's that, then. I only meant to see you across. This is—someone pushed me. I want it noted that someone pushed me."

"No one pushed you, Tam."

"My legs pushed me. It counts." He sat down heavily on a rock, produced his mother's sausage, and began eating it with the grim determination of a man refusing to enjoy anything ever again. After a moment he tore off a third and handed it to Ash, who accepted it with ceremony. "Right. Lowlands. Which way, since apparently I'm coming."

Jude got to their feet, still ringing like a bell, still deafened by the living noise of the dead, and looked out over the country they had sworn never to see. Hills rolled away silver under the fingernail moon, roadless, unfamiliar, enormous. Somewhere out there was a city called Vell, a singer named Wren Alaire whose reputation had reached even Hollowmere's ears—*the Voice that cracked the court's glass*, smuggled rumors said—and a mountain named Serel with a door in its roots and thirteen turns closing like a fist.

The dead pressed close, curious, chattering, offering contradictory directions in four dead languages.

For the first time in thirty years, Jude Thackeray was entirely unable to hear themselves think.

"North," they said, picking a direction out of the roar mostly at random, "and may every god who's listening have mercy on us."

"Oh, they're listening," said the ghost of old Hew the shepherd, right at Jude's ear, cheerful as sin. "Everyone out here is listening, lad. That's rather the trouble."
