## 8. Road of Small Mercies

Tam Broadfoot had dug four hundred and eleven graves before he'd ever left Hollowmere, and he had learned one thing from all of them: the dead were patient, but the living were not. So when Wren Alaire said they had to make the lowlands in six days, Tam believed her, and when she said they had to walk through villages without stopping, without looking at anyone too long, without letting anyone see the child's face — well. He believed that too, though it went against every grain of his broad, honest body.

He was a gravedigger's son. He knew how to keep his head down.

The trouble was that Ash made keeping your head down impossible.

It started the first night out, in a village called Marrow's End — which Tam thought was an ugly name for a place with such good bread. They'd taken a room above the tavern because Jude insisted the child needed a bed and a hot meal, and Wren had grumbled that beds left records and records left questions, but even she hadn't argued with the meal. Ash sat on the bench between them, feet swinging, eating her stew with the solemn concentration of someone who had only recently discovered that food could be more than fuel.

And then the candles guttered.

All of them. At once. The whole room dimmed like a held breath, and an old woman by the fire looked up from her knitting and said, clear as a bell across the sudden hush:

"That's my mother's song."

There was no music playing. Tam was sure of it. But the old woman's eyes had gone soft and far away, and she was humming something under her breath — something slow and sweet and old — and around her, three other people stopped talking and tilted their heads like dogs hearing a whistle no one else could catch.

"Out," Wren said quietly. "Now. Back door."

They went out through the kitchen, past a scullery boy who didn't look up from his pots, and into the cold. Behind them, through the window, Tam saw the old woman weeping into her yarn, and two men embracing awkwardly as if remembering, all at once, that they loved each other.

"What did she do?" Tam whispered.

"Nothing," said Jude. Their voice had a strange texture to it, tight and wondering at once. "That's what frightens me. She doesn't *do* anything. She just — is. And things that have been quiet a long time remember they weren't always quiet."

Ash looked up at them both, stew still on her chin, entirely unbothered, and tugged Jude's sleeve. She pointed back at the tavern, then pressed her small hand flat over her own chest, and made a sound — not a word, nothing so useful as a word, just a low hum, questioning.

"She wants to know if she hurt them," Jude translated. It wasn't really translation. Tam had noticed that already, three days into knowing this child: Jude read her the way he read weather, or the way his father had read soil. Some people simply paid attention better than others, and Jude Thackeray paid attention the way other people breathed.

"No," Tam said, and crouched down so he was at her eye level. He wasn't good with children. He'd never been good with anyone, really, except holes and the people who went into them. But he held out his hand, palm up, the way you'd offer it to a horse. "You didn't hurt anybody. Sometimes being near a thing is enough to wake it up. That's not the same as breaking it."

Ash studied his hand. Then she put her small fingers in his palm, very seriously, as if accepting some formal office, and Tam felt something in his chest go strange and warm, like a coal wrapped in wool.

"Well," said Wren, from somewhere above them, dry as chalk. "Aren't we all going to die horribly. Let's keep moving."

* * *

Wren taught them the road the way Tam's father had taught him graves: practically, unsentimentally, and with a running commentary about everything you were doing wrong.

"Never sleep in a churchyard," she said, on the second morning, as they walked the rutted road between hedgerows white with frost. "I know it seems safe. Dead ground, consecrated, nobody bothers you. That's exactly why soulsellers stake them out. A churchyard is where grief goes to be alone, and grief is the easiest door there is."

"Where do we sleep, then?" Tam asked.

"Barns. Haylofts. Anywhere that smells of living animals. Grief doesn't like the smell of livestock." She glanced back at Ash, riding Jude's shoulders with her fists knotted in their hair. "And never let her be the last one awake. Things notice a light burning after everyone else has gone out."

Tam wanted to ask what kind of things. He decided he didn't want to know badly enough to ruin the day.

She taught them which villages had a toll and which had a beggar at the gate who was really a scout for somebody. She taught them that if a stranger asked where they were headed, you named a town two days behind you, never ahead. She taught them that Wren Alaire could talk a toll-collector out of a toll, a farmer out of eggs, and — once, memorably — a suspicious constable out of his own hat, which she wore for the rest of the afternoon purely out of spite.

"You were a criminal," Tam said, watching her charm a bridge-keeper into waiving passage for "a widower, his ward, and his mute sister."

"I was a *professional*," Wren corrected. "Criminal implies I broke rules. I mostly followed rules that other people hadn't noticed yet."

"And now?"

Wren's mouth did something complicated. "Now I'm following a gravedigger and a ghost-whisperer toward the mountain that owns me," she said lightly. "Ask me again when we're closer and I'll probably lie."

But Tam noticed — because he was a man who noticed things slowly, but thoroughly — that Wren always walked nearest to Ash. Not touching. Never touching. Just near, the way you'd walk near a candle flame in wind, ready to cup your hands around it.

* * *

The third night they slept in a hayloft over a byre full of sleeping cows, and Tam woke in the black hours to find Ash sitting at the loft's open edge, legs dangling, looking out at the moonlit fields.

His heart did the thing hearts do. He crossed the straw quietly and sat down beside her, leaving a careful arm's length between them, the way you'd sit with anyone at a grave's edge.

For a long time neither of them said anything. This was, Tam reflected, the most comfortable conversation he'd ever had.

After a while, Ash reached into the collar of her shirt and drew out a cord. On it hung a chip of bone — Jude's work, Tam recognized the fine spiral carving, a little token Jude had made her back in the valley. She turned it over in her fingers. Then she held it out to him.

Tam took it. The bone was warm. Warmer than it should have been, on a frost night in a drafty loft, and beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse through a wrist, something moved. Not words. Not exactly. More like the feeling you got standing in a churchyard at dusk — a sense of company, of many small attentions turned toward you.

"She talks to them," Tam said softly. "Even out here. Even with all that noise Jude hears outside the stones. She found a way to make it quiet enough to listen."

Ash nodded. Then she tapped the bone, and tapped her own ear, and shook her head — and Tam understood, with a lurch, what she was telling him. She couldn't hear them herself. Whatever she was, whatever she was made of, the voices came *through* her, but never *to* her. She carried a hundred whispers and heard none of them.

"That's the loneliest thing I ever heard," Tam said honestly. "And I grew up in a graveyard."

Ash leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against his arm. She weighed almost nothing. She was warm as a stone kept by a fire.

"My da used to say," Tam told the dark fields, because it was easier than telling her, "that silence is just listening you haven't earned yet. He'd stand over a fresh-cut grave and say the dead aren't gone, they're just waiting their turn to talk, and our job is to dig deep enough and neat enough that when their turn comes, they've got a comfortable place to say it." He huffed. "Ma thought he was mad. Maybe he was. But he was never lonely, and neither am I, most days. You get used to company you can't see. It's the company you can see that's hard."

Ash patted his arm twice, solemnly, like a tiny magistrate approving a petition.

From across the loft came a rustle, and Wren's voice, low and sleep-roughened: "If you two are plotting, plot quieter."

"We're not plotting," Tam said. "We're communing."

"One of these days," Wren murmured, settling back down, "your plainness is going to stop fooling people, Broadfoot." But there was no edge on it. There was something underneath it instead, soft and surprised, like a person finding a coin in an old coat.

* * *

On the fifth day they passed through a village where the ghosts had not stirred gently.

Tam knew something was wrong before they reached the well at its center, because the dogs were barking at nothing, and the shutters were closed at midday, and an old man stood in his doorway holding an iron poker like a spear.

"The bell," the man said, when Wren tried to pass with a nod. "Our bell rang last night. Nobody pulled it. Rang thirteen times and cracked." His eyes went past Wren, past Jude, and fixed on Ash with the flat certainty of fear. "Rang for *her*, whatever she is. We know what comes after a cracked bell. We buried folk after the last cracked bell."

"We're passing through," Jude said carefully. "We mean no harm."

"You're bringing it whether you mean it or not."

What happened next, Tam would turn over in his mind for years afterward. Wren stepped forward, planted herself between the poker and the child, and began — very quietly, barely more than breath — to sing.

It was nothing Tam had a name for. Three notes, rising, falling, rising again, like water finding its way downhill. And the terror in the village went with it. Not vanished — Tam wasn't a fool; he knew a performance when he saw one, and this was partly that, a spell worked on living nerves rather than dead ones. But underneath the performance, the dogs stopped barking. The shuttered houses seemed to exhale. Somewhere a child laughed, suddenly, as if released.

When Wren finished, the old man lowered his poker, looking confused about why he'd raised it.

"Thirteen rings means a debt come due," Wren told him gently. "Not a death. Debts can be paid. Go put your kettle on."

They walked out the far side of the village fast, and nobody spoke until the hedges gave way to open moor.

"You felt it," Jude said finally. "Didn't you. When you sang. Something answered."

Wren walked several paces before answering. "I felt the road ahead getting shorter," she said. "That's what I felt."

But that night, at the fire, she sat closer to the rest of them than she ever had.

* * *

The campfire on the sixth night was the best hour of Tam's whole life, though he couldn't have said why at the time and would spend years trying to say it afterward.

They had made the lowlands' edge. Tomorrow the ground began to rise, and the moor gave way to foothills, and somewhere beyond the foothills Mount Serel stood in the cloud with its doors of teeth and its court of debts. Tonight there was only a ring of stones Wren had chosen, a fire built small and bright, rabbits skewered on green sticks, and the four of them.

Jude had produced, from their pack, a lump of honeycomb bought that morning with the last of the valley coin, and the ceremony with which they presented it to Ash suggested a coronation. Ash ate it with both hands and entire devotion, and got it in her hair, and laughed — her first real laugh since the boundary stones, high and startled, as if it had escaped before she could decide whether she was allowed.

Nobody spoke for a moment. The fire crackled. Somewhere an owl asked its question of the dark.

"There," Wren said at last, and her voice was odd. "That's the note. That's the one I cracked the glass with, or near enough." She was smiling, which on Wren's face looked like a ship righting itself in rough water. "All those years training, and it turns out the truest note in the world is a child with honey in her hair."

"You could teach that," Jude said quietly. "When this is done. Music. Here, or anywhere. People would come a long way to learn how a voice does what yours does."

"People come a long way to learn lots of things that don't fix anything." But Wren looked at the fire, and Tam watched her turn the idea over the way you'd turn a stone to see what lived under it.

"And you," Jude said, turning to Tam, and there was something in their face Tam had never seen aimed at him before — respect, plain and undecorated. "You've been carrying half her weight on the climbs and all of the watch on the nights. Why?"

Tam shrugged, deeply uncomfortable. "Somebody had to. You don't leave a job half-dug. My da's rule."

"Your da sounds like he'd have liked this fire," Jude said.

"He'd have said we were digging our own graves, walking toward that mountain." Tam turned his stick, and the rabbit dripped fat into the flames, hissing. "Then he'd have sat down and had some rabbit. He always said the dead are patient. Might as well eat first."

Ash, sticky and drowsy, climbed into the space between Tam and Jude as if it had been measured for her, and was asleep before her head found Jude's shoulder. Above them the moon rode clear and round, one night short of full — and Tam, watching it, felt the old gravedigger's arithmetic turn over in him uneasily. Thirteen turns. The elder's deadline. The moon was nearly whole, and the child in Jude's arms was nearly due.

"Wren," he said. "How far to Serel?"

Wren looked east, where the foothills made a black seam against the stars, and where — if you knew to look, and Tam was beginning to wish he didn't — a shape stood against them, vast and pale-topped, like a tooth breaking gum.

"Two days' hard walking," she said. "One, if the mountain's impatient."

"And if we're late?"

Wren fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. When she answered, all the performance had gone out of her voice, and what was left was worse than any joke.

"Then the Undercourt doesn't send for her," she said. "It sends *everything else*. And we find out what a soul weighs, all four of us, on a set of scales none of us chose."

The fire popped. Ash stirred in her sleep and murmured something — and this time, for the first time, it wasn't a hum, and it wasn't a language older than kingdoms.

It was a word. One small, clear, common word, spoken in a child's sleepy voice:

"*Home.*"

Nobody moved. The owl called again, far off. And Tam Broadfoot, gravedigger's son, keeper of four hundred and eleven comfortable resting places, looked at the sleeping child and the fire and the two stubborn, frightened, brave people beside him, and understood that whatever the mountain took or didn't take in the next two days, the debt he'd pay gladly —

— was the one that kept this fire burning.

Above the black seam of the hills, the moon slid a hair's breadth nearer to full, and far away, faint as a crack in glass, something beneath Mount Serel began — very softly — to sing back.
