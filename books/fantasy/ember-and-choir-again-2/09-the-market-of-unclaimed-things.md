## 9. The Market of Unclaimed Things

Wren had been to markets where they sold knives, markets where they sold secrets, and once, memorably, a market in the Salt Reaches where a man sold weather by the jar. But Vell's night market was the only one she knew that sold *people*—or pieces of them, which was worse, because pieces could be pawned and redeemed and pawned again until nothing whole remained.

She smelled it before she saw it: tallow smoke and wet stone and, underneath, something sweeter and more wrong, like fruit going bad in a locked room. The entrance was a fishmonger's stall on the Canal Row, shuttered for the night. The fishmonger himself sat dozing behind it, and Wren dropped two coppers into his apron pocket without waking him. Old habit. You paid the door, or the door remembered you.

"Stay close," she murmured. "And whatever you see, don't stare. Staring is how people end up *inventory*."

Tam Broadfoot shifted the pack on his shoulders and looked like a man who very much wanted to be anywhere else—a gravedigger's son, broad as a door, with the kind of honest face that got honest people robbed. Ash had her hand fisted in the back of Tam's coat. Jude walked beside Wren, pale in the lamplight, carrying their carved bones wrapped in oilcloth against their chest like a mother carries an infant.

"You've been here before," Jude said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I've been lots of places." Wren kept her eyes forward. "Walk."

The stall swung open onto stairs going down—of course down; everything worth hiding in Vell went down—and the market opened beneath them like a throat.

Lanterns hung from chains in tiers, hundreds of them, each one burning a different color, and Wren knew what burned in them and hated that she knew. Soul-light. Not the imprisoned fireflies of the Undercourt itself—that was grand theft, cathedral-grade—but the leavings, the shavings, the little pawns and pledges that came through this market like river water through a sieve. A green lantern here might hold a farmer's luck with dice. A red one there, a widow's grief, sold by the widow herself when grief grew too heavy to carry.

Stalls lined the walls in crooked rows. A woman with silver teeth appraised a young man's shadow, holding it up to a lamp while he stood blinking and strangely flat in the gloom. Two brothers argued over a jar containing, if the label was honest, someone's talent for whistling. A child no older than Ash sat cross-legged on a counter, being weighed—not her body, but something else, some scale Wren couldn't see working—and the child swung her feet and hummed, bored, while the scale-woman frowned at numbers only she could read.

Ash stopped humming the moment they entered. She pressed closer to Tam and made a sound Wren had never heard from her: a low, thin note of distress, like a struck string refusing to die.

"She doesn't like it here," Tam said.

"No," Wren said. "She wouldn't."

Because Ash was made of thirteen souls, and this market traded in exactly such things, and somewhere in that small translucent-edged body every one of those thirteen was remembering what it was to be weighed and priced and shelved.

"We get the map," Jude said. Their voice was steady, but Wren heard the effort in it, the way you hear ice bearing weight. "We pay. We leave."

"That's the plan." Wren touched the knife at her hip out of habit. "Plans are lovely. I've sung plans."

They moved into the crowd. And that was when Wren saw the purple coat.

It was a fine coat, violet wool with bone buttons, worn by a small round man with a waxed mustache and spectacles tinted amber, who stood at a stall of bottled memories conducting three conversations at once and winning all of them. His name was Master Quillon Vesh, and for six years he had been Wren's handler in the Undercourt's service, and he was currently laughing at a joke told by a soul-broker whose collar was worth more than Tam's house.

Wren did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat. Exit through the fish stall: too far, too crowded, and Quillon faced the stairs. Hide among the stalls: possible, but Quillon had spent thirty years spotting hidden things in crowds; it was practically his trade. Or—

Or walk past him like anyone else. He'd never seen her face since she'd fled. He'd seen her *voice*, though. He'd know her voice anywhere, the way a man knows the knife that cut him.

"Don't talk," she breathed. "Any of you. Especially not me. Walk past the purple coat and keep walking."

"What—" Tam started.

"*Don't talk.*"

They walked. Wren fixed her gaze on the middle distance, adjusted her posture—shoulders up, chin down, the slouch of a tired laborer instead of a trained singer's carriage—and steered them along the wall, past a stall selling secondhand courage, past a man auctioning the last hour of a condemned thief's life (going cheap; nobody wanted it), past the purple coat itself, close enough that she caught his scent, sandalwood and ink, and heard him say, mid-sentence:

"—and I tell you, the Undercourt pays triple for anything out of Hollowmere these days. Triple. There's a contract maturing, my dear, a big one, thirteenth turn, and the Magistrate wants eyes everywhere—"

Wren's blood went cold and stayed there.

She kept walking. Ten steps. Twenty. Around a corner of stacked crates, into the smell of frying onions from a food stall that somehow existed even here, because hunger was the one law no market ever escaped.

"Triple," she said softly, when they were safely around the corner. "They're looking for us. They're looking for *her*. Which means we have maybe an hour before someone connects a mute child to a bounty."

"The map first," Jude said. "If we can't find the underways, none of the rest matters."

"The map first," Wren agreed. "There's a cartographer at the far end. Doss. He owes me."

"He owes *you*?" Tam said. "How does a court singer make a mapmaker owe her?"

Wren smiled without any warmth in it. "I sang at his daughter's wedding after he'd drunk away the fee. Some debts are pleasant." She paused. "Most aren't."

* * *

Doss's stall was a cave of rolled vellum and hanging weights, run by a man shaped rather like his own merchandise—tall, cylindrical, and slightly yellowed. He took one look at Wren and his face performed a complicated maneuver involving guilt, relief, and indigestion.

"Wren Alaire," he said. "Word was you were dead."

"Word was right. Keep it that way." She leaned on his counter. "I need the Serel underways. All of them. The old ones especially—the ones the Court pretends don't exist."

Doss's eyes flicked to Ash, then to Jude, then to the bounty-hunters' notice board nailed to his own stall post—he had the grace to look embarrassed about that—and back to Wren. "You know what you're asking. The underways maps are Court property. If I sell you one and anyone learns—"

"Nobody will learn, because you're going to sell it to me quietly, and I'm going to consider your daughter's wedding debt settled, and we'll both sleep well." She let a thread of her real voice into the words, just a thread, the resonance that made grown men sign contracts they later regretted. "Unless you'd prefer I settle it *loudly*, here, in front of your customers."

Doss wilted. From beneath his counter he drew a tube of oiled leather, checked over both shoulders twice, and slid it across. "The deep ways. Drawn from a runner's memory forty years back, so forgive the gaps. And Wren—" he lowered his voice, "—the third level, the Gallery of Ledgers? Don't stop there. Whatever you need, don't stop there."

"Why not?"

"Because that's where they keep the contracts," Doss said. "All of them. Signed ones. And the Gallery has a way of... showing you yours."

Jude had gone very still. Wren felt them looking at her and didn't turn to meet it. Later. Everything could be dealt with later; that was the whole philosophy of her life, and it had worked *magnificently* so far.

"Passage," she said briskly. "We need passage east to Serel's foot. River or road, fast and quiet. What's the toll at the gate?"

"Gate takes coin. Ferryman takes more." Doss named a figure that made Tam inhale through his teeth.

Wren turned out her pockets with theatrical despair and produced, in total, eleven coppers and a button. She looked at Jude.

Jude unwrapped the oilcloth with careful hands. Among the carved bones—each one a voice, each one a name, thirty years of the valley's dead held in ivory—Jude selected a piece the length of a finger: a memorial bead, dense with fine work, a drowned farmer's name spiraling down its length in letters no living hand could carve.

"This one," Jude said quietly. To the bead, not to Wren. "You carried his name thirty years. Carry it a little farther. Buy a child's road home."

The market seemed to lean in. Even the lanterns guttered, all colors at once.

Doss took the bead between two fingers and held it to his lamp, and his professional appraisal curdled into something like awe. "This is grave-work. True grave-work. I can't price this."

"Then don't," Jude said. "Price the passage. The rest is a gift."

Doss named a number. It was generous—more than enough for the ferry and then some—and he counted the coins out slowly, like a man handling relics. Then he wrapped the bead in silk, separately, and put it somewhere beneath the counter rather than in the till.

"It'll go to a good buyer," he said gruffly. "Someone who'll listen to it."

"They usually do," Jude said, and rewrapped the remaining bones, and Wren watched them do it and thought: *thirty years of listening to the dead, and the dead never sounded like that. Like gratitude.*

Which was when the bell rang.

Not a market bell. A high, sweet, terrible note, one clear tone rolling down through the tiered lanterns, and every light in the cavern flared at once—every soul-lantern turning toward the sound like flowers toward sun. Wren knew that bell. She had *been* that bell, once. The Court's summons-tone, sent out through any vessel that would carry it, and it meant one thing:

*A claim has been called. Present all pledged souls for inspection.*

The market changed instantly. Stall-keepers began pulling down shutters. The scale-woman swept her counter clean. Somewhere a child started crying, and someone hushed her with a hand over her mouth, gently, urgently.

"That's not routine," Wren said. "Inspections are quarterly. This isn't quarter-day."

"No," said a new voice, warm and round and horribly familiar. "It isn't."

Master Quillon Vesh stepped around the crate-stack, amber spectacles catching the lantern-light, smiling his smile that had closed a thousand bargains. His gaze passed over Tam, dismissed him. Passed over Jude, lingered a moment on the oilcloth bundle. Came to rest on Ash.

And stopped.

"Well," he said softly. "Now *there's* a face I've seen in a ledger."

Wren moved before she decided to move—stepped between Quillon and Ash, and made the mistake of opening her mouth. "She's pledged already. Full claim. Move along."

Quillon's smile widened by one tooth. And Wren heard herself—heard the voice come out of her own throat, the trained resonance, the Court's own instrument—and watched him hear it too.

His head tilted, birdlike. Behind the amber glass, his eyes went bright.

"My dear," he said, with dawning delight, "do I know you? Have we met? Because I would swear on the Magistrate's own ledger that I have heard that voice sing before—and the last time I heard it, it cracked the throne-room glass, and the girl who owned it has been worth a fortune to whoever finds her ever since."

He raised one gloved hand, and above the stalls, every lantern in the market turned its flame toward them like an eye.

"Now then," said Quillon Vesh pleasantly. "Let's talk about redemption."
