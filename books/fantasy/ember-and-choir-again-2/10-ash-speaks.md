## 10. Ash Speaks

The trouble with a child who does not speak your language, Jude had learned over the long road from Hollowmere to Vell, is that you spend all your words guessing.

They guessed at breakfast. They guessed on the road. They guessed when Ash tugged Tam's sleeve and pointed at a hedgehog with an expression of such profound moral outrage that Tam spent ten minutes explaining, through elaborate pantomime, that the hedgehog was fine, actually, that was just its whole deal. Ash had remained unconvinced. She kept one eye on the hedgehog until it ambled off into the bracken, as though filing it away for future judgment.

"She'll make a fine magistrate someday," Wren said dryly, shouldering her pack. "Judgmental and barefoot."

Jude laughed — and then felt the laugh catch in their chest, because that was the other thing about Ash. When she was near, the dead went quiet. Not silent the way they were inside Hollowmere's boundary stones, muffled behind some ancient warding, but quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in. Respectful. Watchful. In three weeks of travel, Jude had not heard a single whisper from the ivory beads at their wrist, and they were beginning to understand that this was not absence. It was attention.

Something in the world was listening to this child.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, half a day out of Vell, where the road bent toward the grey shoulder of Mount Serel. Tam built the fire with his usual methodical fussiness — kindling sorted by thickness, flint struck exactly four times, a small grunt of satisfaction when it caught. He had been a gravedigger's son for twenty-two years and he approached every fire as though it might need burying later.

Wren was teaching Ash a clapping game. Or rather, Wren was attempting to teach Ash a clapping game, and Ash was teaching Wren a much more complicated version involving three hands' worth of claps that Wren could not follow, which delighted them both beyond reason. The sound of it — Wren's startled laughter, Ash's breathless huffing giggle — settled over the camp like a blanket pulled up to the chin.

Jude sat apart, as they always did, turning a scrap of bone between their fingers. It was a practice rib from a sheep Tam had bought in the last village, boiled clean, waiting for a knife. Jude carved when they could not sleep, and they could not sleep most nights now. The bone was supposed to be nothing — a practice piece, a curling vine, maybe a bird if the vine went wrong. But their knife kept finding its own way, and what emerged under the firelight was a door. Small, arched, set into the curve of the bone like a doorway cut into a hillside.

Beneath a mountain.

Every reading since Hollowmere had shown them the same thing. A door beneath a mountain, and a woman's hand, signing.

"Jude."

They looked up. Tam had gone still by the fire, poker halfway to the coals, his broad face wearing the particular expression he reserved for things that frightened him but that he refused to admit frightened him. He was looking at Ash.

Ash was looking at the fire. And she was humming.

It was nothing, at first. A child's tuneless wandering, the kind of sound children make when they think no one is counting the notes. But then it found its shape. Found its rhythm. Rose and fell in a pattern that was not wandering at all, that was old and worn smooth as river stone, and Jude's whole body went cold from the scalp down.

They knew that song.

Not from books. Not from the valley's rituals, which had their own solemn chants, or from the road-songs Wren muttered under her breath. They knew it from before memory had proper edges — from a kitchen with a crooked door, from arms that smelled of soap and woodsmoke, from a voice that had hummed it low while kneading bread, while mending shirts, while sitting very still on the step at dusk looking east toward mountains she never spoke of.

Their mother's lullaby.

The knife slipped. Jude didn't notice. The bone door lay forgotten in the dirt, and Jude was seven years old again in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere in the world, hearing a melody that no one else alive could possibly know, coming out of a child who had crossed the impossible stones barefoot and unafraid.

"Ash," Jude said. Their voice came out strange. Thin. "Where did you learn that?"

Ash stopped humming. She looked at Jude across the fire, head tilted, those dark eyes catching the light like a bird considering something. Then she said — in her own tongue, the old syllables that no living mouth should know — a short string of words, soft and apologetic, and spread her hands as if to say: *it is only a song.*

"No." Jude was on their feet without deciding to stand. "No, sing it again. Please. Sing it again."

"Jude," Tam said carefully. "Maybe—"

"*Sing it again.*"

The camp went still. Even the fire seemed to lean back. And Ash, who feared nothing, who had walked out of nowhere and into their lives like water finding a crack, looked at Jude for a long moment — and then, slowly, she began to hum again.

And this time, as she hummed, she spoke.

It came haltingly, in fragments, her mouth shaping the common tongue the way a hand shapes itself around a tool it has never held. But the words came. One at a time. As though the song were loosening something in her, as though the melody were a key turning in a lock that had been rusted shut since the day she appeared at Jude's door.

"Made," she said. "I was... made."

Tam's poker clattered against a stone. Wren had stopped breathing entirely; Jude could see it, the stillness of a woman trained to be invisible, every muscle locked.

"Not born," Ash said. Her brow furrowed with effort. "Woven." She pressed her fingertips together, wove them, unwove them. "Thirteen." She held up both hands, then folded one finger down. "Twelve... and me."

"Woven from thirteen souls," Wren whispered. She had gone pale in the firelight. "That's Undercourt work. That's deep work. That's—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Jude saw her swallow whatever else she'd been about to say. Another secret for the pile. Another lock on another door. Later. Everything was later.

But Ash wasn't finished. She rocked slightly as she hummed, the way children rock, the way people rock when they are holding something heavy, and her eyes had gone somewhere far away — or perhaps somewhere very close, somewhere inside herself where twelve other lives lived tangled together like roots under one stone.

"One of them," she said, and her voice cracked on the word, small and wondering, "sings. Inside. All the time. She sings..." Ash touched her own chest, over her heart, and then pointed — hesitantly, apologetically — at Jude.

"She sings *your* song."

The world narrowed to the size of a fire.

Jude sat down. They didn't choose to; their legs simply ended their employment. The ground was cold through their trousers and very far away and very close, all at once. Somewhere above them the moon was climbing toward its thirteenth turn, and somewhere beneath Mount Serel a Magistrate kept a ledger with their mother's name in it, and here beside a dying fire a child woven from thirteen stolen souls was humming a lullaby that only two people in all the world had ever known — and one of them was dead, and the dead never spoke of her, never, not once in thirty years, as though the valley itself had agreed to forget her —

As though the valley had been *paid* to forget her.

"Oh," Jude said. It was a very small word for a very large falling. "Oh, Mother. What did you do?"

"Jude?" Tam's hand landed warm and clumsy on their shoulder. He crouched beside them, big and solid and worried, the way he always was, the gravedigger's son who knew exactly how much a body weighed and how little that told you about a person. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"That song." Jude heard their own voice as if from across a river. "Nobody knows that song. Nobody living. My mother used to sing it. She died when I was nine, and I—" Their throat closed. They tried again. "I thought I was the only one left who remembered it. I thought when I forgot it, it would be gone. And now it's—" They gestured helplessly at Ash, at the small humming shape of her. "It's *in her*. Someone whose soul is in her knew my mother. Sang with her. Was *loved* by her, maybe, or loved her, because you don't learn a song like that unless—"

Unless someone sang it to you. Unless someone held you in a kitchen with a crooked door and hummed it low while the bread rose.

Unless you were family.

The thought arrived whole and terrible, and Jude could not unthink it. Thirteen souls. Woven together. One of them carrying a lullaby from a woman who had sold her firstborn's future to buy a valley's silence. Who had paid, somehow, in ways Jude was only beginning to see the shape of — and what if the payment hadn't been coin? What if the payment had been *people*? What if the ledger beneath the mountain held thirteen names, and one of them belonged to someone who used to sing?

"Ask her," Wren said quietly. She had moved around the fire without Jude noticing, and she knelt now on Ash's other side, her cynical courtier's face stripped down to something rawer. "Ask her about the singer. Ask her name."

"I don't know how to ask. We don't share enough words."

"Then ask with this." Wren reached into her collar and drew out a thin silver chain, and from it a small pendant — a bird, folded wings, worn nearly smooth. "Songs travel farther than language. Everyone knows that. Sing the question, Jude. You've got the hearing; you must have something of the voice."

"I haven't sung since I was a child."

"Then you're overdue."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by nerves. Ash watched them all with her grave, unhurried eyes, still humming, patient as stone, as though she had all the time in the world — except that she didn't, none of them did, the moon was waxing and her edges had begun, on certain nights, to look faintly translucent, like paper held up to a candle.

Jude closed their eyes.

They found the lullaby the way you find a stair in the dark — by memory of the body, not the mind. The first note came out cracked and uncertain, a stranger's voice in their own throat. The second was better. By the fourth, the kitchen had come back: the crooked door, the smell of rising bread, the last gold light on the floorboards, and a woman sitting very still on the step looking east, humming, always humming, as though the song were the only thing keeping some door of her own shut.

Jude sang the verse through. Then, hardly knowing they were doing it, they changed the ending — lifted the last phrase into a question, the way Wren had taught them without teaching them, the way music asks what words cannot. *Who?* the melody said. *Who are you? Where did you go?*

Silence.

The fire popped. Tam shifted. Far off in the dark, some night bird called once and thought better of it.

Then Ash opened her mouth, and answered in harmony.

Her voice threaded through Jude's like a second strand of thread through a needle's eye, and where the two melodies crossed, something happened that Jude had no name for. The dead-quiet that followed Ash everywhere deepened — became not silence but *presence*, became a held breath, became the feeling of a full room in the dark. And in that fullness Jude heard, or almost heard, or dreamed they heard, a third voice underneath: faint, frayed, familiar in the way a face in a dream is familiar, singing the same lullaby from somewhere very far below the earth.

The note broke. Ash swayed. Tam caught her before Jude could move, gathered her up with the easy competence of a man who had carried coffins and knew how to hold weight gently, and laid her down on his bedroll, where she curled up at once, already asleep, one hand closed loosely around the hem of his sleeve.

For a long time nobody spoke.

Finally Wren sat back on her heels. She was looking at Ash, but Jude could tell she was seeing something else — a throne room, perhaps, and glass cracking under a forbidden note; a voice that had once belonged to a court beneath a mountain.

"The thirteenth soul," Wren said softly. "The one that sings. Whoever she was, she's still in there. Still whole enough to remember a lullaby. Which means she can be brought back out." She turned to Jude, and her eyes were bright and hard and afraid. "Contracts can be read, Jude. Souls that were woven can sometimes be unwoven — if you know the song. If you know the *terms*."

"And the terms are written in a ledger," Jude said, "under a mountain, guarded by a Magistrate, signed by my mother." They looked down at their hands — carver's hands, coffin-nail hands, hands that had spent thirty years making small beautiful things for the dead because the dead were safe to love. "She didn't just buy the valley's silence, Wren. I think she paid with people. I think one of them was someone who loved her. Maybe—" The word came out barely voiced. "Maybe someone who sang to me too. When I was small. Before."

Before the valley. Before the stones. Before a childhood that had a hole in it shaped like a woman's face, because the dead of Hollowmere would never speak of her, and Jude had always believed it was grief.

It had never been grief. It had been a contract clause.

Tam cleared his throat. His arm was still curled protectively around sleeping Ash, and his voice, when it came, was rough and plain and steadying as a spade in soil. "Then we know where we're going," he said. "And we know why. That's more than we had this morning." He glanced at the dark eastern horizon, where Mount Serel waited invisible behind the miles. "We get to Vell tomorrow. We get the map. We get up that mountain. And we ask your mother's friend — whoever she is, wherever she is in that poor kid — if she remembers anything else worth knowing."

Jude looked at him. Plain, stubborn, terrified Tam Broadfoot, who had crossed the boundary stones for no reason except that his neighbors needed doing it with.

"Yes," Jude said. "That's exactly what we do."

They banked the fire and took the first watch, because sleep was a country they couldn't reach tonight. Ash slept on, breathing slow, her translucent edges catching the starlight. And just before dawn, half-dreaming, she smiled and murmured one more fragment of common speech — new words, carefully assembled, as though she had spent the whole night translating:

*"The singer says... tell the carver... the door was never locked from outside."*

Jude sat bolt upright in the cold grey light, heart hammering, and looked east — where Mount Serel stood revealed against the paling sky, closer than it had ever been.

Waiting to be sung open.
