## 11. Letters Never Burned

The chest had been in the corner of my mother's house for as long as I could remember, and for just as long I had treated it the way one treats a sleeping dog of uncertain temper: with respect, from a distance, and never on purpose.

It was cedar, iron-banded, older than the house itself. My mother had kept it locked, and she had kept the key on a cord around her neck, and when she died—quietly, in her sleep, eleven years ago now—the key had gone into the ground with her because Warden Ferris said that was the custom, and because none of us thought to argue.

So the chest sat. Through floods and one memorable chimney fire and Tam Broadfoot's adolescence, which was its own kind of disaster. It sat through my whole life, really, patient as a stone, while I carved bones and buried farmers and told myself the past was a country I had no passport for.

Now there was a child asleep in my bed who spoke in syllables that made the dead go quiet, and every divination I attempted pointed at a door beneath a mountain, and a woman's hand signing her name to something terrible.

I wanted very much not to know whose hand it was.

I knew anyway. That's the cruelty of knowing: you don't get to choose the moment. You only get to choose how long you pretend.

* * *

*She is kneeling at the hearth, and I am six, and I have asked the question children ask.*

*"Mama, why won't the dead talk about you?"*

*I remember the poker stopping mid-stir. The coals ticking. Her back very straight under her shawl.*

*"What do you mean, love?"*

*"Old Marta's bone says your name and then goes quiet. All of them do. They say everyone's names but yours."*

*A long silence. Long enough that I counted the ticks of the cooling fire—one, two, three, four—and then she laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm as bread, and she said, "Perhaps they're being polite," and ruffled my hair, and I let it go, because I was six and mothers are allowed their mysteries.*

*I have thought of that laugh ten thousand times since. I have never once believed it.*

* * *

The chest's lock had rusted shut sometime in the last decade, which meant the choice had been made for me, in a way. I brought a chisel and a mallet and stood over it in my mother's cold front room like a man at a graveside who has come to say something he should have said years ago.

"You could knock," said Tam from the doorway. He'd followed me over with an armful of split ash-wood, ostensibly for the stove, actually because Tam cannot bear to let anyone do anything difficult alone. He is a gravedigger's son and he has the gravedigger's instinct: show up, stand quietly, be useful when the crying starts.

"It's locked."

"I can see it's locked. I'm saying you could still knock. Politely. Before you break it open like a starving badger."

"She's dead, Tam."

"Dead people appreciate manners most of all. Nobody interrupts them otherwise." But he set down the wood and came to stand beside me, and after a moment he put his big blunt hand on the chest lid, the way you'd steady a horse. "Do you want me to hit it?"

"No."

"Because I will. I've been practicing."

"On what?"

"The Warden's door. Don't tell her." And despite everything—despite Ash asleep three houses down with her edges faintly wrong, despite the thirteenth turn coming on like weather—I laughed. That's Tam's whole gift, really. He makes the unbearable briefly ridiculous, and you breathe again, and then the unbearable comes back but you're braced for it.

I swung the mallet. The hasp gave on the third blow with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the smell of cedar rose up out of thirty years, and inside—

Not treasure. Not horror. Paper.

Letters. Bundles of them, tied with string gone soft as cloth. A christening gown folded small. A wooden rattle I half-remembered from someone else's babyhood. And beneath everything, flat and heavy as a paving stone, a ledger bound in grey leather with no title at all.

I knew the handwriting on the first page before I knew anything else. I'd seen it on recipe cards and margin notes and one furious letter to the miller about short weights. My mother's hand: slanted, impatient, beautiful the way weather is beautiful.

Tam read over my shoulder, because of course he did, and for once he didn't joke.

* * *

*The ledger opens on a date forty-one years gone. The ink is brown with age but the strokes are hard enough to have gone through the paper in places.*

*There is a column of figures. Weights, I think at first—grain, tallow, the dull arithmetic of a valley farm. Then I see what the figures weigh.*

*One hearing of the unquiet dead. Firstborn issue of Maren Thackeray. To commence at birth, to endure until surrendered or seized.*

*And beside it, in a different hand—a hand like frost forming on glass, each letter a little too symmetrical, a little too pleased with itself—the counterweight:*

*Warding of the vale called Hollowmere, in perpetuity. Silence within the stones. No magic crosses; no power finds; no debt collector calls.*

*No debt collector calls.*

*I read that line four times. Somewhere in the house a floorboard settles, and I flinch like a thief.*

*Below the terms, signatures. The frost-hand signs itself in letters I cannot pronounce but somehow understand anyway: a name like a door closing. And beneath it, smaller, pressed so hard the nib nearly tore through:*

*Maren Thackeray.*

*My mother sold my ears before I was born. She sold them to buy a wall around a valley, and she never told me, and she raised me inside that wall teaching me to carve the dead's words into bone as if it were a kindness, as if it were a craft, as if it were mine.*

*It was never mine. It was collateral.*

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that the light moved. Long enough that Tam took the ledger gently out of my hands, read it through twice, and set it down on the table with the care of a man handling something that might go off.

"Jude."

"Don't."

"All right." He didn't leave, though. He got the stove going, because Tam's answer to every crisis is fuel, and put the kettle on, because his mother had raised him right, and then he sat across from me and waited the way gravestones wait.

I turned to the back pages. There were more entries—not bargains but records. Payments received. Small ones. Over years.

*Received: one memory of a wedding song, from the widow Hale, against one winter without fever in her house.*

*Received: one grief, unnamed, from the cooper Aldous, against the mending of his daughter's hip.*

*Received: one first kiss, freely given, from —*

The entry stopped there. No name. Just a blot where the ink had been wept on, or dripped, or both.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, Jude. She wasn't just buying the warding."

"No." My voice came out strange, flat, like a bone that hadn't been carved yet. "She was making payments. Keeping it fed. Little trades, little coins, all these years. Everyone's small sorrows—" and I thought of the widow Hale who had hummed no tune at her husband's burial, of old Aldous who had stood at his daughter's wedding looking puzzled, searching his own face for the joy that should have been there. "She was paying the mountain out of the valley's pockets. And they let her. Because the alternative was letting it collect from the whole vale at once."

"That's not villainy," Tam said slowly. "That's—" He rubbed his face. "That's taxes."

That startled a sound out of me that was almost a laugh and mostly not. "Yes. Thank you. My mother was a tax collector for hell."

"Undercourt."

"Undercourt," I corrected, and hated how easily the word came now.

* * *

*She is dying. I am twenty-six and I am holding her hand and the room smells of lavender and the particular staleness of a life ending, and she keeps trying to say something.*

*"Mama, hush, save your breath."*

*"No. Listen. Listen, love, I need—" Her eyes are bright and frightened and fixed on me. "If they ever come asking. If a door ever opens. You pay nothing. Do you hear me? You promise me. Whatever they ask, whatever it seems worth—you pay nothing, because once you start paying them they never stop counting."*

*"Who's they? Mama, who would come asking?"*

*But she has gone somewhere behind her eyes, somewhere the pain can't follow, and her hand tightens on mine once—once, hard, like a signature—and relaxes.*

*I promised. Of course I promised. She was dying; you promise dying people things; it's practically the definition of the deathbed.*

*I have spent eleven years keeping a promise I never understood.*

* * *

"Why did the dead never speak of her?" I asked the room, or the ledger, or possibly the ceiling. "All those years. Every soul in this valley passed through my hands, near enough, and every one of them would give up their name, their sins, their last words—but ask them about Maren Thackeray and it was like talking to river stones. I thought—" I had thought she was beloved. That grief had made her sacred beyond even the dead's reach. That silence was a kind of honor.

"They're afraid of her," Tam said. Not unkindly. "Or of what she owed. If you were a ghost and some great pale clerk came round asking after the woman who'd been selling bits of you lot for forty years, would you chat?"

I hadn't thought of it that way. I had thought of my mother as the wall's keeper. I had never thought of her as the wall's debtor—or worse, its agent. Its friendly local face. The one who knocked politely on doors and left with a memory or a grief tucked under her arm like eggs borrowed from a neighbor.

And the worst part—the part that sat in my chest like swallowed river ice—was that I understood her. Completely. Utterly. Give me a mountain full of hungry clerks and a valley full of neighbors and a child not yet born, and I would make the same trade. I might already be making it. Wasn't that what this was? Ash asleep down the lane, and me turning over every awful arithmetic that might save her?

"She'd have liked Ash," I said, because if I didn't say something ordinary I was going to break something. "You know. Stubborn. Bad at sleeping."

"She'd have terrified Ash."

"She'd have terrified Ash and then taught her to swear in three languages and fed her until she burst."

"Yes," Tam agreed. "Same thing."

* * *

I went through the letters that evening, by lamplight, alone. Tam had offered to stay; I'd told him someone should sit with Ash, which was true, and also a lie, because what I needed was to do this badly in private.

Most of the letters were mundane. Accounts with the miller. A recipe for plum cordial annotated with the word *cowardly* next to the suggested sugar quantity. One long correspondence with a stonemason in Vell about boundary markers, dry as dust until the third letter, where between discussions of granite grades my mother wrote:

*You asked once why I do this work. Here is your answer and you'll keep it behind your teeth: I did not build the wall. I inherited the bill. My mother signed, and hers before her, all the way back to the founding, and the debt does not die, it only changes hands. I intend that it change hands exactly never. I have a child. The child pays nothing. Find another quarryman.*

Find another quarryman. As if the Undercourt were a trade dispute. As if fury were a strategy. I loved her so much in that moment that it hurt in my teeth, and I hated her so much in the same moment that it hurt in the same teeth, and I sat with both hurts like a man holding two ends of a rope in a tug-of-war against himself.

Near the bottom of the bundle, wrapped separately in oilcloth, was a letter that had never been sent. The address line was blank. The wax unbroken around a fold of paper that began:

*To whoever holds my hearing after me—*

And stopped. There were three false starts beneath it, crossed out so violently the pen had torn the page. In the margin of the last one, in tiny letters, almost hidden:

*Tell them it was love. Tell them it was love and it was not enough and I chose them anyway. Let them hate me for the choosing, not for the hiding.*

I put the letter down. I looked at the fire for a long time.

Then I did not burn it. That's the thing people always assume—that rage burns letters. Rage doesn't burn anything. Rage sits very still in a cold kitchen holding a piece of paper, reading the same sentence over and over: *let them hate me for the choosing, not for the hiding.* Because the hiding was the crime. The choosing I could forgive. Mothers choose; it's the whole wretched job description. But she had let me grow up loving the silence, thinking it holy, thinking it mine, and she had died with the truth locked in a box under her feet.

*Let them hate me for the choosing, not for the hiding.*

"Well," I said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to her, to nobody. "Half succeeded."

* * *

Ash woke near midnight. I heard her—heard the absence of her, rather, the peculiar hole in the world's whispering where she slept—and went and sat on the edge of the bed, and she blinked up at me with eyes the color of deep water over slate.

She looked at my face. Children do that; they read you like weather. Then she reached out and touched the ledger, which I hadn't realized I was still carrying, clutched against my ribs like a splint for a broken thing.

She opened it. Small fingers, sure of themselves, turning pages she could not possibly read in any tongue I knew. She stopped at the first page—at the frost-hand, at the terms, at my mother's signature pressed hard enough to tear—and she made a sound.

Not a word. A hum. Three notes, rising, falling, rising again.

A lullaby. My mother's lullaby, the one she sang with the words worn off, the one I had not heard in eleven years and would have known through stone, through earth, through the floor of the world itself.

The dead outside my window went silent. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"Ash," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "Where did you learn that?"

She looked at me with those impossible eyes, and patted the ledger—patted my mother's name—and said, in that language older than kingdoms, in a voice like a small bell rung underwater, something I could not understand and understood completely anyway.

*From her. From the piece of her that's in me. From the piece of her they took.*

I sat very still while the ice in my chest finished cracking, and outside, far off toward the mountains, something that was not thunder rolled along the ridgeline of Serel, patient as a clerk, patient as a debt, and I understood that we had less time than I'd thought—and that I was going to need help I did not yet know how to ask for.
