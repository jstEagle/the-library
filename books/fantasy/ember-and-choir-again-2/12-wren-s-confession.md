## 12. Wren's Confession

The fire had burned down to that stage Wren liked best—the stage where it stopped being a tool and became an audience. Embers clicked and settled like a polite crowd waiting for her to begin. She had spent eleven years of her life in front of audiences. She knew the difference between the ones who wanted music and the ones who wanted blood.

This one wanted neither. That was the problem.

Jude sat across the flames with their hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea they weren't drinking, watching her with those patient valley eyes. Ash was asleep against Tam's shoulder, and Tam was pretending not to listen while absolutely listening, the way gravediggers' sons do—heads down, ears up, missing nothing.

Wren turned her cup slowly on her knee. The tea had gone cold hours ago. She'd been holding it as a prop, which was, she reflected, exactly the sort of cowardice she was about to confess to.

"You're going to ask me," she said, "why I know the mountain."

"I wasn't going to ask tonight," Jude said.

"You were going to ask tomorrow. Politely. Over breakfast. With that look you do." She gestured at them with the cup. "That look. The one where you wait for people to tell you things because you've learned that waiting works better than asking. It's very annoying. It works on everyone. I hate it."

Tam snorted into his own cup. Jude had the grace to look faintly unrepentant.

"Fine." Wren set the tea down before she could use it to hide behind again. "Fine. You want to know why a disgraced court singer knows the underways of Mount Serel better than the miners who dig its edges? Here's your answer, and then you can decide whether you still want my help, because I'll warn you now—it changes the price."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called, and Ash stirred but didn't wake.

"I wasn't just a court singer," Wren said. "I was the Voice."

* * *

She had told the story so many ways in her head over the years—in taverns, to no one, in the dark of various cells—that she almost had it polished smooth. Almost pleasant. A little tragedy with a good exit line. But looking at Jude's face across the fire, she found she couldn't give them the polished version. They'd hear the seams anyway. People who lived with the dead learned to hear what was underneath.

"The Undercourt keeps a Voice," she said. "One singer, trained from childhood, whose job is—" She stopped. Started again. "You've heard what song does to souls. Everyone has, a little. A lullaby settles a fretful child. A dirge helps a grieving family let go. The Undercourt took that truth and made a profession of it. The Voice sings souls *out*, Jude. Out of bodies. Out of hiding places. Out of bargains gone wrong. When someone sells a soul to the Court and the soul won't come quietly—when it clings, when it weeps, when it runs down the years inside some poor borrowed body trying to be a person—someone has to sing it loose. Someone has to stand in that cathedral of teeth and stone and make leaving sound beautiful."

Her voice had gone very steady, which was how she knew it was bad. Steady was the performance. Steady was the trick she'd been taught at eight years old: never let them see the cost, or they'll charge you less, and then what are you worth?

"They started training me young," she said. "They like them young. Young voices haven't learned to flinch. I stood in the choir galleries under Serel for nineteen years, and I sang—" She counted, because counting was easier than feeling. "Two hundred and six souls out of their bodies. Maybe more. I stopped keeping track after two hundred because after two hundred the numbers stop meaning anything and start meaning everything, if you follow me."

Nobody followed her. Nobody said anything at all. Tam had gone very still, and Ash slept on against his shoulder with the terrible innocence of the truly exhausted, and Jude watched her with an expression she couldn't read, which frightened her more than condemnation would have.

"Go on," Jude said quietly. "Please."

* * *

"The last one," Wren said, "was a girl of about twelve."

There it was. The seam. She'd known it would tear open; it always did.

"Not a sold soul. Not a debt. The Court had decided her family owed them—a gambling debt, generations old, compounded by interest the way only the Undercourt compounds interest—and the Magistrate ruled that the girl herself would settle it. Her soul, weighed and taken. And the order came down to me the way orders always did, written in that silver script on grey vellum, and I read it, and I—" She laughed, short and ugly. "I read it twice, because the first time I thought surely I'd misread. Surely even the Court wouldn't. But the Court does everything, that's the whole point of the Court. It does everything the law allows, and the law allows anything if the paperwork is right."

"So you refused," Tam said. His voice was rough. He hadn't moved his arm from around Ash.

"I refused," Wren agreed. "And here is the part I'm not proud of, Broadfoot, so pay attention, because this is the confession inside the confession: I didn't refuse loudly. I didn't throw down my music and denounce them from the gallery. I refused *quietly*. I went to the rehearsal for the extraction and I sang one note wrong. One note. A quarter-tone off, buried in the third phrase, small enough that the Magistrate himself might have missed it." She looked at her hands. Singer's hands, long and scarred at the fingertips from decades of strings and cold. "But the doors heard it. The doors are carved from the jaws of kings and they hear *everything*. The resonance cracked the throne-room glass. Forty panes. It rained diamond dust on the courtiers and the Magistrate knew instantly—I saw him know it, saw him understand that his Voice had chosen, deliberately, to fail. You cannot accidentally crack glass with a quarter-tone. It takes intent. It takes *aim*."

"And they disgraced you for it," Jude said.

"They disgraced me for it," Wren said. "Stripped my title, broke my name from the rolls, put me in the stocks in Vell and let the city decide what I was worth. Which, as it turned out, was rotten fruit and a rumor. But here's the thing, Thackeray, the thing I need you to understand before you trust me with that child's soul: I ran. That's my whole moral history in four words. A twelve-year-old girl went down into the dark singing, and I cracked some glass, and I *ran*. I don't know what happened to her. I have spent eleven years not knowing, and I built a whole clever cynical life on top of that not-knowing like a roof over a hole. Selling information. Bargaining my way out of stocks. Keeping my voice pretty and my heart locked. Because if I ever stopped moving, I'd have to find out whether my one wrong note saved her or just made her extraction slower and crueler."

The nightjar called again. Closer this time, or maybe that was just the silence pressing in.

"That's why I came after you," Wren said. "In Vell, at the market, when I first heard about a soul-child loose in the world—my plan was to sell the information. I want that on the record too. I was going to sell a seven-year-old to whoever paid best, and I got as far as pricing it before I saw her." She nodded at Ash, small and breathing in the firelight. "And something in me that I'd carefully killed for eleven years sat up and said, oh, *there* you are."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. Wren found she was shaking slightly—not visibly, she was too well-trained for visible—but internally, the way a struck bell shakes after the sound stops.

Then Jude said, "What was her name? The girl's."

Wren blinked. Of all the responses she'd rehearsed for—disgust, pity, the door of the camp closing against her—that wasn't any of them. "I—what?"

"The girl. The one who went down singing. What was her name?"

"They don't—" Wren's throat closed. She made herself push through it; she'd pushed through worse. "They don't let the Voice know names. Names are attachments. Attachments ruin the performance. We were given numbers. She was Two-Thirty-Eight." She heard how it sounded the moment it left her mouth. "Gods. Eleven years, and I still remember the number faster than I remember that she must have had a name. Somebody named her. Somebody rocked her and named her and then lost her to a ledger."

"Two hundred and six," Jude said softly. "You counted."

"I counted."

"And you remember the number of the last one."

"I remember all of them," Wren said, and it came out rawer than she intended, stripped of all the polish, all the tavern-versions. "That's the curse of a trained memory, Thackeray. They taught me to hold a song after hearing it once, and it turns out the teaching doesn't discriminate. Every face. Every note. Every soul I sang out, filed away in perfect pitch forever. Do you know what it's like to have a memory that won't let you forget a single thing you've done?" She laughed again, and this time it wobbled. "No. Of course you don't. You're about to lose yours—the dead's voices, the hearing—and you think that will be the grief of your life. Let me tell you the opposite grief. The grief of remembering everything, and having done nothing."

Tam shifted. Ash murmured in her sleep and burrowed closer to his shoulder, and he looked down at her with an expression Wren recognized despite herself, because she'd seen it in her own mirror for eleven years: the look of someone guarding something they were terrified to love.

"So," Jude said. "The counter-song."

Wren startled. "What?"

"You said knowing why you know the mountain changes the price. So name it. What's the price, Wren?"

* * *

Here it was, then. The part she'd been circling all night like a moth around a lamp it both wanted and feared.

She stood up. Pacing helped; pacing had always helped; the choir galleries under Serel had been long enough to pace the length of a whole fugue, and she'd worn grooves in the stone.

"The counter-song exists," she said. "It's real. It's not legend and it's not hope—it's theory, and I am possibly the only living person who knows the theory, because I was trained in everything the Court's contracts rest on, including the flaws. A soul-contract is a sung thing, Thackeray. Written in ink, yes, signed in blood or whatever dramatic fluid they prefer, but the binding itself—the thing that makes it hold—is a chord. A specific chord, sustained, renewed at every turn of the moon. Break the chord and the contract is paper. Burnable paper."

"But breaking it requires the Voice," Jude said. "Requires you."

"It requires a Voice, and there's exactly one person alive who was trained to sing it, and she's standing in front of you wearing boots with a hole in the left toe." She held up a hand before Jude could speak. "Yes. I can teach it. No, I won't pretend it's simple. The counter-song isn't a weapon, it's a completion—you don't shatter the contract, you *finish* it, you sing the debt resolved, and the Court's own law has to honor a completed resolution because the law is the only god they worship. But it has to be sung *in* the Court, in the cathedral, under the Magistrate's nose, at the moment of maturity. Which means—"

"Which means you have to go back," Tam said.

"Which means I have to go back," Wren agreed. Her pacing had brought her to the edge of the firelight, and she stopped there, half in warmth and half in dark, which felt about right. "Into the mountain I fled. Into the gallery where I sang two hundred and six souls out of their bodies. In front of the court that owned my voice for nineteen years and the Magistrate who watched me choose failure and marked me for it. If they catch me, they don't kill Voices. They're too expensive to train. They *keep* us." She let that sit. "So that's the price, Thackeray. My teaching costs you a promise: when we go up Serel—and we go up Serel, because you're going, I've watched you sharpen yourself into going for days now—you carry me back in. You don't let me run. Whatever I say at the doors, whatever I bargain, whatever clever exit I improvise—and I will improvise one, I promise you, I'm very good—you hold me to it. You make me walk into that cathedral. Because I have been a coward for eleven years and I have discovered, recently, at inconveniently close range, that cowardice is just fear that's been allowed to make all the decisions."

She turned to face Jude fully. The fire was at her back now, and she knew what that did to a singer's face—threw every line into shadow, made honesty look like performance. She hated it. She stepped sideways until the light hit her properly. Let them see all of it.

"Two cowards," she said. "That's what this expedition actually is. You, who swore never to leave your valley and are leaving it. Me, who swore never to go back and is going back. Neither of us brave. Both of us doing the frightening thing anyway, because a child is asleep ten feet away and her edges are going translucent, and courage turns out not to be a quality you have. It's a thing you do with your knees shaking."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Then they said, "Why now? Why her? You've walked past suffering for eleven years, Wren. Sold information about it, probably. What makes Ash different?"

And Wren opened her mouth to give the clever answer, the deflecting answer, the one about pragmatism and debts and the market value of a clear conscience—

—and found she didn't have it.

"Because she hums," she said instead. "When she thinks no one's listening. Little scraps of melody, half-finished, like someone started teaching her songs and never finished. And one of them—" Her voice caught. She let it. For once, she let it. "One of them is a lullaby I wrote. Nineteen years in that place, and the only thing I ever made that wasn't a weapon was a lullaby, for the children in the lower galleries, to help them sleep before the tests. I thought it died with me when I ran. And she's humming it. Which means somewhere in whatever she's woven from, there's a thread that remembers my song. There's a piece of that place that remembers something *kind*." She swiped at her eyes, furious at them. "I can't sell that. I tried. Believe me, I priced it, and the market wouldn't take it."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed by respect. Ash slept on.

"All right," Jude said.

Wren blinked. "All right, what?"

"All right. I promise. I'll carry you back in, and I won't let you run, and you'll hate me for it, and I'll do it anyway." Jude's mouth curved, tired and true. "Provided you teach me the counter-song well enough that I can sing it even if something happens to you. Because I've noticed something about our group, Wren. Everyone in it keeps volunteering to be the one who gets sacrificed. I'd like to break the pattern."

"A noble sentiment," Wren said, "from someone who plans to trade their hearing for the child."

"Different kind of sacrifice."

"How?"

"Mine's already decided. Yours, we're negotiating." Jude lifted the cold tea at last and drank it, grimacing, entirely unbothered. "Lessons start at dawn?"

"Lessons start at dawn," Wren agreed, and then, because the bell in her chest was still ringing and she couldn't leave it there, she added: "Thackeray. The thing I said about the girl. Two-Thirty-Eight. If we get Ash out—if the counter-song works and the contract burns—there will be other numbers after her. Other contracts maturing. Other voices in that dark."

"I know," Jude said.

"You can't save all of them."

"No," Jude agreed. "But we can save one, and then we'll know how. And knowing how is a dangerous thing to carry back down a mountain." They looked at her across the embers, and for the first time since Vell, Wren felt something in her chest that wasn't dread. It was smaller than hope and harder to kill. "Get some sleep, Wren. Tomorrow you start teaching a carver to sing."

Wren lay back on her bedroll and stared up at unfamiliar stars—the valley folk's stars, strange constellations, a sky she didn't know yet—and found that her hands had stopped shaking.

Above her, faint as breath, she could have sworn she heard Ash humming in her sleep.

Three notes of the lullaby. Then a fourth, new, unfinished.

Learning it. Growing it. Carrying it somewhere she'd never been.

Wren closed her eyes and, for the first time in eleven years, began composing toward morning.
