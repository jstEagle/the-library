## 13. The Ascent of Serel

Tam Broadfoot had dug graves for a living, so he knew a thing or two about ground. Good ground held together. Bad ground slid. Ground that had been dead a long time and wanted to stay that way had a particular smell to it—cold and mineral, like a coin left in the rain.

Mount Serel smelled like all three at once.

"Tell me again," he said, hauling himself up a ledge of black rock while sleet needled the back of his neck, "why we couldn't have taken the road."

"There is no road," Wren called down from above him, not even breathing hard. She climbed like she'd been born on a cliff face, which Tam suspected was one of those things she'd never explain and he'd never stop wondering about. "There's a pilgrim track for the first thousand feet, and then there's what the mountain allows."

"The mountain *allows*," Tam repeated. He got his boot into a crack and pushed. "Right. And I suppose it disallows plenty too."

"It disallowed a whole expedition of soul-brokers in my grandmother's day," Wren said cheerfully. "Avalanche. Nobody found so much as a boot."

"That was supposed to be comforting?"

"It was supposed to be interesting."

Tam decided to save his breath for climbing. Behind him, Jude came up the switchback in their steady, stubborn way—not fast, never fast, but never stopping either, which Tam had learned over the weeks of travel was its own kind of speed. And behind Jude, small hands finding holds Tam would have sworn didn't exist, came Ash.

She was climbing well. That was the strange part. She'd been growing quieter for days now—the words she'd learned coming slower, her questions fewer—and Tam had braced himself for her to slow down too, to become one more weight they carried. Instead she moved like the mountain was something she remembered. Like her feet knew the way before her head did.

He tried not to think about why that might be. He was getting good at not thinking about things. It was practically a skill.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of an outcropping that curved overhead like a cupped hand. Tam built the fire while Wren rigged their oilcloth into a lean-to, and Jude sat with Ash wrapped in both blankets, feeding her strips of dried apple because chewing seemed to be easier than talking, some days.

The moon hung above them, fat and pale, two turns shy of full.

"Thirteenth turn," Jude said quietly, when Ash's eyes had finally closed. "Eleven days."

"I can count," Tam said. Then, because he felt bad about the sharpness: "Sorry. Sleet makes me rude."

"Sleet makes everyone rude," Wren said. "It's why mountain folk are famous for honesty." She fed a twist of dry heather to the fire. The flames burned a strange color up here, Tam had noticed—greener than they should be, like they were burning something other than wood. "We'll make the gates by the eleventh turn if the weather holds. Twelfth if it doesn't. That leaves a day to spare."

"And if we're late?"

Wren looked at him across the fire. For once she didn't dress the answer up. "Then we're late," she said. "And Ash goes where souls go when the debt comes due, and we spend the rest of our lives being people who were eleven days short."

Tam nodded slowly. He looked at the sleeping child, at the way the firelight went through her a little easier than it should—her edges soft, like a figure seen through fogged glass. When they'd started this journey she'd been solid as any child. Now, in certain lights, he could see the rock behind her.

He poked the fire harder than it needed poking.

* * *

The second day brought the haunted switchbacks.

Tam had heard Wren use the word "haunted" back in the lowlands and had assumed it was singer's flourish, the way she said "legendary" about an inn that had merely been adequate. But somewhere past the tree line, where the path bent back on itself again and again up the mountain's shoulder, he began to understand she'd just been accurate.

The voices started as wind. Wind said a lot of things if you listened wrong, and Tam had spent enough nights in churchyards to know how to listen right, which meant knowing when *not* to. But these weren't wind. These were footsteps. Boots on gravel, keeping pace with them on the switchback below—a whole line of climbers, by the sound, though every time Tam leaned over the edge to look, there was nothing but scree and cloud.

"Don't look," Wren said, without turning around. "They climb faster when you watch. They want you to see where they're going."

"Where are they going?"

"Nowhere. They've been going nowhere for four hundred years. Pilgrims who came up asking the mountain for things it doesn't give." She stepped over a gap in the path and reached back for Ash, who took her hand and swung across like it was a game. "The Undercourt used to let supplicants climb. Let them wear themselves out hoping. By the time they reached the doors, they'd agree to anything."

"What happened to them?"

"They agreed to anything."

Tam kept his eyes on his boots after that. But the footsteps stayed with him, patient as weather, and once—just once—he heard, beneath the boots, a voice humming. A little tuneless thread of melody, rising and falling, rising and falling, like someone trying to remember a song they'd only half learned.

Ash stopped walking.

She turned her face toward the drop-off, toward the cloud, and her mouth opened, and for one horrible second Tam thought she was going to answer it.

"Ash." Jude crouched beside her, took her hand. Their voice was calm the way a frozen lake is calm. "That song isn't yours. Whatever's singing it—it isn't yours."

Ash looked up at Jude. Her eyes were doing the translucent thing again, the gray of them gone thin as ice on a puddle. Slowly, deliberately, she shook her head. Then she pointed at herself, and then at Jude, and then she made the sign she'd invented weeks ago on the road, the one that meant *us*, the one that meant *together*—two fists pressed knuckle to knuckle over her heart.

"No," Jude said, and their voice cracked clean through the middle of the word. "No, sweetheart. You don't get to say goodbye yet. We haven't even gotten to the hard part."

Ash smiled. It was a small smile, and it was the worst thing Tam had seen in thirty years of digging holes for people who hadn't wanted to go either.

He cleared his throat. "Right," he said loudly, to the mountain in general. "I don't know who's down there, and I don't much care. But we've got a schedule, and my mum raised me to be punctual, so if you could keep your humming to yourself, we'd all appreciate it."

The footsteps paused. The humming stopped.

Then, from somewhere below the cloud, distinctly, came a single word in a voice like dry leaves: *"Punctual."*

"Well," said Wren, after a moment. "That's new. They usually don't have opinions."

"Everyone's got opinions," Tam said. "Most of them just don't say 'em out loud." He picked up his pack. "Come on. Daylight's spending itself whether we walk or not."

* * *

On the fourth day the sleet turned to snow, and the snow turned sideways, and they lost the path twice and found it once by accident when Ash simply walked to it out of the white, unerring, like a needle finding north.

"She knows the way," Wren murmured that night, watching the girl sleep. The fire was small; they'd run low on anything that burned. "Jude. She knows the way better than I do."

"I know."

"That should worry us."

"I know that too." Jude rubbed their eyes. Up here, away from Hollowmere's stones, Tam could see what the valley had hidden: the way the dead clung to Jude like burrs, the way their shoulders hunched against voices only they could hear. Outside the boundary, Jude heard everything. Tam had asked once what it was like, and Jude had thought about it a long time and finally said, "Like living in a house where every room is full of people talking and none of them will look at you." Tam hadn't asked again.

"You should sleep," Tam said now. "I'll take first watch."

"You took last watch."

"And I'm taking this one. I sleep like a fence post anyway. Ask anyone in Hollowmere. Best watchman the burial society ever had, and that's including the year Marta Kellingsworth did it sober."

Jude almost smiled. Almost. They lay down next to Ash and were asleep inside a minute, the deep felled-tree sleep of the truly spent.

Tam sat with his back to the rock and watched the snow come down and listened to the mountain breathe.

Here was the thing about Tam Broadfoot that nobody had ever bothered to notice, least of all Tam: he was ordinary. Not modest-ordinary, not secret-gifted-ordinary, just plain, workaday, dig-the-hole-straight-and-deep ordinary. Jude heard the dead. Wren's voice could open doors and had cracked throne-room glass. Even Ash, seven years old and fading like breath off a window, was made of thirteen souls and older languages. And Tam could carry sixty pounds uphill for ten hours and complain about it the entire time.

For most of his life he'd figured that was the whole of him. Then a mute child had shown up at his neighbor's door, and he'd packed a bag, and nobody—including himself—had been more surprised than when he'd walked out past the boundary stones with them.

And somewhere on this mountain it had dawned on him, slow as spring thaw, what ordinary was actually *for*. Jude and Wren were the ones who'd fight the court, sing the songs, break the bargain. Fine. Good. But somebody had to make sure everybody ate. Somebody had to count the days until the thirteenth turn, mend the oilcloth, carry the extra water, notice when Ash went quiet, notice when Jude went quiet, stand in the gap between the miracle-workers and the weather and just—hold. Ordinary was a load-bearing wall. Nobody sang about it, but take it out and the whole house came down.

So Tam counted the days. Eleven. He mended the oilcloth. He melted snow for water. And when the moon rose, fat and white and horribly nearly-full, he looked at it the way you look at a hole you've dug and know is the right size, and he thought: *not yet. We're not done yet.*

* * *

On the sixth day they crossed the Weeping Stair, a stretch of the cliff where meltwater ran down the rock face in a hundred thin streams, freezing as it fell, so that the whole mountainside glittered and chimed like a chandelier. It was beautiful. It was also, Wren explained, the loudest place on the mountain, and sound up here traveled to places sound shouldn't go.

"We cross quiet," she said. "No singing. No talking if we can manage it. The Court listens through the ice."

They crossed quiet. Or mostly quiet. Halfway along, Ash stumbled, and Tam caught her arm, and she looked up at him and mouthed something—no sound, just the shape of it—and it took him a moment to read her lips, because she'd shaped it carefully, a word she'd been saving:

*Thank you.*

Tam's chest did something complicated. "Don't mention it," he whispered. "Literally. Don't. Wren'll have my ears."

But Ash was looking past him now, up the stair, at the falling water, and her expression changed. Tam followed her gaze and saw nothing but ice and light. But she raised one small hand, and for a moment—just a moment—the nearest stream of meltwater bent, gently, impossibly, away from the rock, arcing out over the path like a bowing servant, before falling back into its course.

Nobody spoke about it. There wasn't time, and besides, the ice was listening.

But that night, in the shelter of a cave whose entrance they'd had to dig open, Ash sat between Jude and Tam with her hands folded in her lap, and she was more transparent than Tam had ever seen her. Firelight passed through her fingers and printed the rock behind them in wavering orange. She was fading. Everyone could see it; nobody said it; saying it wouldn't carve away a single hour of the eleven that remained.

Instead, Ash did something else. She took Jude's hand, and she took Tam's, and she pressed them together, palm to palm, over her own small head, like a roof. Then she closed her eyes and leaned into the space beneath.

"Sleep, love," Jude whispered. "We've got you."

And Tam, holding the hand of a woman who talked to the dead, under the joined hands of a child made of ghosts, on a mountain that ate pilgrims, discovered that he wasn't frightened. Not for himself. He'd known for six days now what he was prepared to do if it came to it—had felt the idea settle into him the way a spade settles into soil, quiet and inevitable—and the strange thing about carrying your own sacrifice was that it weighed nothing at all. It was the lightest thing he owned.

The heavy things were the ones he meant to keep.

* * *

On the ninth day, the weather broke, and the mountain showed them where they were going.

They came around a shoulder of rock into sudden blazing sunlight—snow-blind bright, the sky a blue so hard it rang—and there, across a saddle of white, stood the upper slopes of Mount Serel proper, and near its peak, catching the sun, a gleam like a row of teeth.

"The gates," Wren said. Her voice came out strange. Tam glanced at her and saw her face had gone the color of the snow. "Doors of Teeth. I told you they were real. I don't think I mentioned they could see you coming from three days off."

"Three days?" Jude shaded their eyes. "You said twelve if the weather turned."

"The weather turned kind." Wren was quiet a moment. "Nothing on this mountain is kind. Which means—" She stopped. Started again. "Which means it wants us to arrive early. And I have been to that court before, and I am telling you, whatever it wants, we should be afraid of wanting it too."

Tam looked at the distant gleam of teeth. He looked at Ash, standing at the edge of the light with the sun going straight through her, thin as a window, her face turned toward the doors with an expression he'd last seen on the old ones back home, in the week before the end: not fear. Recognition.

Below them, faint on the wind, from the switchbacks far beneath, came the sound of footsteps. Hundreds of them. Climbing.

Keeping pace.

"Then we go early," Tam said, and shouldered his pack, "and we knock, and we're polite, and we ask for our girl back. And if they say no—" he shrugged, the shrug of a man who has dug too many holes, "—well. I've argued with stonier company than a magistrate."

Wren laughed—one short, startled bark, gone as quick as it came. Jude put a hand on each of their shoulders.

"Day ten," Jude said. "Let's not waste it."

They started down toward the saddle, three figures and a fading child, walking into the teeth of the mountain—and behind them, patient as weather, the dead of Serel climbed on.
