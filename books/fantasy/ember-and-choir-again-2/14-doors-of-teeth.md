## 14. Doors of Teeth

The mountain heard us coming.

I don't mean that in a poetic way, though I've had thirty years of practice making poetry out of things I'd rather not say plainly. I mean that as we climbed the last switchback of Mount Serel, sleet turning to a fine cold rain that found every gap in my coat, the dead beneath the stone began to murmur—and for once, I wished they wouldn't have.

"They're singing," Ash said. She had been saying very little for three days; her edges had gone faintly translucent at the wrists, like paper held up to a window, and speech seemed to cost her something now. But she stopped on the trail, tilted her head, and said it clearly: "They're singing *down*."

Tam, hauling our pack like a man who had been promised this would be a short errand and was beginning to suspect otherwise, looked at me. "Is that bad?"

"Everything under a mountain is bad," Wren said. "The question is only whether it's bad in an interesting way."

"It's a hymn," I said. The voices threaded together through the rock, dozens of them, hundreds, worn smooth by repetition. Not a warning. An invitation. That was somehow worse. "They're welcoming someone home."

"Her," Wren said quietly, and didn't look at Ash, and we all didn't look at Ash together, which is its own kind of looking.

* * *

The mouth of Mount Serel was not a cave opening. I want to be clear about that, because when Wren first described it to me—"gates carved from the jaws of ancient kings"—I had imagined something crude and dreadful, a skull-face bolted onto rock by people who thought dread was a design philosophy. What we found instead was worse because it was beautiful.

The mountainside simply ended, cut clean as a tombstone face, and set into that cut were two doors each forty feet high, hinged at the center, wrought from what could only be jawbone—the lower jaws of kings, fused and polished until they gleamed like wet ivory, teeth still intact, rows upon rows of them running up the doors like the keys of some impossible instrument. Between the teeth ran channels and grooves, and in the grooves moved light, slow and golden, like honey poured through bone.

Carved above the doors, in letters older than any kingdom I could name:

**NOTHING ENTERS UNPAID. NOTHING LEAVES UNWEPT.**

"Cheerful," said Tam.

"They're not joking," Wren said. "That's the thing people never understand about the Undercourt. They are absolutely, scrupulously honest. It's the most frightening thing about them." She set down her pack, rolled her shoulders, and I watched her do the thing she did before performing: touch her throat once, lightly, as if checking that a door was locked.

"You've done this before," I said.

"I opened these doors eleven years ago," she said. "Coming out. They let you out free if you run fast enough and cry properly. Going in is the expensive direction." She glanced back at us. "When I sing, the doors will wake up. And then they'll take their toll. One memory each. From everyone who passes."

"Everyone?" Tam's voice went up half an octave. "Even—"

"The child pays too," Wren said. Her voice was gentle but flat, the voice of someone delivering news she'd rehearsed being gentle about. "The Undercourt doesn't make exceptions. Especially not for her. If anything, they'll want a bigger piece."

Ash touched her own translucent wrist and said nothing, which from Ash was a kind of agreement.

"What kind of memories?" I asked. "Can we choose?"

"No. Yes. Both." Wren made a frustrated gesture. "You choose what you *offer*. The doors choose what they *take*. You offer your mother's apple pie and they might reach past it and take the smell of your mother's kitchen instead, and you won't know what's gone until you go looking for it and find the hole." She looked at me then, directly, with those performer's eyes that miss nothing. "Jude. Whatever you offer, offer something you can live without. Don't get clever. Don't try to trick them. Everyone who has ever tried to trick these doors is still in there, trying."

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of the mountain's shoulder, close enough to the gates that the golden light between the teeth lit our fire instead of the other way around. Tam cooked the last of the lowland sausage and pretended the meal wasn't a funeral dinner. Ash sat closest to the doors, cross-legged, watching the light move.

I took out my carving knife and a length of birch and worked without deciding what I was making, which is how my best pieces start. After a while I realized it was a small door. A little wooden door with a latch, sized for a dollhouse, sized for a hand to open.

"That's sweet," Tam said. "What's it for?"

"I don't know yet."

Wren sat beside me. For a long time neither of us spoke, and the dead murmured their hymn under the stone, and the golden light crawled between the teeth.

"When my mother died," Wren said, "I sang at the funeral. Of course I did. I was already the Voice by then; the Court lent me out for occasions, weddings and coronations and the occasional rich man's grief. And afterward I couldn't remember her laugh. I remembered that she had one. I remembered where it sat in her chest, here—" she touched her sternum, "—and how it started, always, with a little cough, like it was clearing customs. But the sound itself was gone. I assumed grief had taken it. Grief takes things." She turned the little wooden door over in her hands. "It wasn't grief. It was the doors. I paid them twice that year and forgot I'd paid at all."

"Wren," I said, "what did you offer them?"

"A memory of the sea," she said. "I offered the sea and they took my mother laughing. That's the game. That's always the game." She gave the door back to me. "So whatever you're planning to offer, Jude Thackeray, offer it with your eyes open, and then let me check your eyes after, because I know what missing looks like now."

I hadn't told anyone what I meant to offer. I hadn't quite admitted it to myself. But the plan had been sitting in my chest since Vell, since the ledger, since I read my mother's signature in ink gone brown as old blood: *the hearing of my firstborn, in perpetuity, against the warding of Hollowmere.* Since I understood that everything I was—every whispered conversation with the drowned farmer, every comfort given to the grieving through carved bone, every quiet morning of my deliberately small life—had been purchased with a contract I never signed.

And I understood something else, something I hadn't said aloud even to myself: that if I was going to walk into a court that traded in souls, carrying the very gift that contract had bought, I was carrying contraband. Leverage. A hook they could sink into me. Better to arrive lighter.

Better, even, to arrive *less*.

* * *

In the morning the sleet had stopped and the sky was the color of a blade, and Wren stood before the Doors of Teeth and sang.

I will not describe the song, because I can't. Not truly. I can tell you it began with a single note held so long it stopped seeming like sound and became a fact about the world, like gravity. I can tell you that the golden light in the grooves quickened, flowing faster, and that the teeth—each one taller than Tam—began very faintly to ring, each tooth a different pitch, so that the whole gate became a chord. I can tell you that Ash stood with her hands pressed flat against the air in front of her, translucent fingers spread, and hummed along without knowing she was doing it, and that the doors' chord bent slightly toward her humming, like a plant toward sun.

And I can tell you that when Wren finished, a voice came out of the doors—not from behind them, but *from* them, out of the bone itself, dry and vast and patient as sediment.

**WHAT DO YOU BRING.**

"Four travelers," Wren said. Her voice didn't shake. I don't know how it didn't shake. "One guide. One gravedigger. One singer returned. One soul come home."

**THE SOUL IS EXPECTED,** said the doors. **THE OTHERS ARE NOT. THE TOLL STANDS FOR ALL WHO PASS. A MEMORY EACH, WEIGHED AND KEPT. OFFER NOW.**

"I'll go first," Wren said, before anyone could argue. She stepped forward, lifted her chin, and closed her eyes. "I offer the memory of the first time I ever sang for money. A tavern in Corrow's Bend. I was fourteen. The crowd threw a cabbage."

The light in the grooves gathered at the base of the doors, pooled, and rose up in a thin bright line—and touched her forehead, feather-light.

For a moment Wren smiled. Then the smile flickered, confused, and settled into something smaller and more puzzled, the way a face looks when it reaches for a word on the tip of the tongue and finds the whole sentence gone.

**ACCEPTED,** said the doors. **AND IN EXCHANGE FOR THE CABBAGE STORY, WE KEEP THE SOUND OF YOUR FATHER WHISTLING.**

"*What?*" Wren's eyes flew open. "I didn't offer—that wasn't—"

**YOU OFFERED YOUR FIRST PAID SONG. HE WAS IN THE CROWD. IT BELONGS TO THE SAME MEMORY. WE TAKE WHAT TOUCHES WHAT WE TAKE.** A pause, and I would have sworn the vast voice sounded almost apologetic, in the way a landslide might apologize. **THIS IS WHY THEY CALL US CRUEL. WE PREFER THOROUGH.**

Tam grabbed my arm. "Jude. Jude, I want to go home."

"You can't," I said gently. "Not without her."

"I know." He blew out a long breath. "I know. That's the problem with liking people." He stepped forward before his courage could reconsider. "I offer—I offer the memory of the worst day of my life. Digging my father's grave. All of it. Take the whole day."

The light touched him. He flinched, waited, blinked.

**ACCEPTED,** said the doors. **WE TAKE THE DAY YOU NAMED. AND WITH IT, THE REASON YOU BECAME A GRAVEDIGGER.**

Tam stood very still. "But that's—" His brow furrowed, working at it like a man pushing a cart uphill. "I wanted to follow him. Didn't I? That was why. There was a reason. There was—" He shook his head slowly, like a dog shaking off rain. "Huh. There's just... digging. Turns out I'm good at it." He looked back at me, and his face was calm, almost pleasantly blank, and that calm frightened me more than anything the mountain had done yet. "It's fine, Jude. Honestly. Feels like setting down a heavy pack. Maybe they did me a kindness."

"They didn't," I said, and hated how unsteady my voice was. "They took the shape of your grief. You're allowed to grieve him, Tam."

"Am I?" he asked, genuinely curious, and walked toward the doors.

Then it was Ash.

She approached the gate the way she approached everything—without fear, without hurry—and the golden light bent toward her eagerly, hungrily, a dog straining its leash. She didn't speak. She simply stood, and offered herself, palms open.

**NO,** said the doors.

The word dropped like a stone into water.

**SHE HAS ALREADY BEEN PAID FOR,** the doors said. **HER MEMORIES ARE COURT PROPERTY BY CONTRACT. SHE MAY PASS WITHOUT TOLL, OWNING NOTHING WORTH OUR CLAIM.**

Something in me cracked at that—cleanly, like ice underfoot. A seven-year-old girl so thoroughly owned that even the doors of the deep couldn't be bothered to rob her.

Ash looked up at me. She pointed at the gap between the doors. Then at herself. Then she smiled—small, translucent at the edges, certain—and stepped through into the dark beyond, unpaid and unowned by anything except, for thirteen more turns, everyone who loved her.

Which left me.

I stepped forward. The light gathered, waiting. Behind me Wren said softly, "Jude. Eyes open."

Eyes open.

I thought about the ledger. My mother's careful handwriting. *Item: the hearing of my firstborn.* I thought about how, in thirty years, the dead of Hollowmere had never once spoken her name. How the bones went quiet around her grave like men taking off their hats. I had thought it was reverence. It was shame. The dead knew what she'd sold, and they were too polite—or too pained—to say.

If I walked in there carrying her bargain in my ears, the Magistrate would find it. He would hold it up. He would turn it in the light and show me exactly what I was made of. So.

"I offer," I said, and my voice carried strangely well in that stone-cut air, "the memory of my mother's face."

Behind me, Wren made a small sound, like a gasp bitten in half.

The light rose. It hovered at my forehead, considering, weighing, and I braced for the theft, for the reaching past, for whatever adjacent thing they'd strip away—the sound of her voice, the warmth of her hand—

**THAT IS ALL?** the doors asked. **A FACE?**

"That's all."

**MOST WHO COME HERE OFFER THEIR WORST DAYS,** the doors said. **YOU OFFER YOUR BEST ONE.** A pause. The golden light pulsed, almost gently. **WE ACCEPT.**

It touched me.

I felt it go—not pain, not pulling, just a soft subtraction, a page lifted cleanly from a book. Her face. The brown eyes, the crooked smile, the way her nose wrinkled when she laughed, the gray starting at her temples the last year, all of it, gathered up and gone as neatly as breath off a mirror. I knew she had had a face. I knew I had loved it. I knew the shape of the love and the absence where the face used to be, the way you know a room is empty by the echo.

And underneath the loss, quieter than the loss, something else. Relief. Terrible, shameful relief. Because whoever she had been, whatever she had done in that ledger, her face could no longer make me forgive it.

**THE TOLL IS PAID,** said the Doors of Teeth. **ENTER.**

And the gates, with a groan like a choir clearing its throat, swung inward on hinges forty feet tall, and the dark of Mount Serel breathed out over us—cold, mineral, and full, far below, of a sound like ten thousand pages turning.

Wren caught my sleeve as I passed her. Her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce.

"What did they take from you?" she demanded. "Tell me now. Tell me exactly."

"My mother's face," I said. "Just what I offered. Just her face."

"And how does it feel?"

I searched myself honestly, the way she'd taught me. "Like I finally put down a pack I forgot I was carrying," I said, "and I hate that it feels that way."

Wren nodded slowly. "Good," she said. "That's the truest answer anyone's given these doors in a century. Come on. The Magistrate hates to be kept waiting, and he takes it out on the furniture."

We stepped through together, four shadows swallowed by the mountain's throat—and far below us, in the cathedral dark, something enormous closed a book, and said, in a voice like settling debt:

*"The heir arrives."*
