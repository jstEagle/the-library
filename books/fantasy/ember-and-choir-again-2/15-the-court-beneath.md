## 15. The Court Beneath

The doors of teeth closed behind them with a sound like a book snapping shut on a finger.

Wren had told herself she would not flinch. She had rehearsed not-flinching the whole climb up Serel, through sleet and haunted switchbacks and Tam Broadfoot's cheerful insistence on counting his steps aloud to keep his nerves quiet. She had rehearsed it in the dark of the underways, where the walls sweated cold and something far below sang in a register no throat should own. She had rehearsed it at the threshold of the great gates while they drank her memory of a rainy afternoon in Vell — a small price, an afternoon of rain, though she still caught herself reaching for it like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

She flinched anyway. Just once. Just a little.

Nobody saw. That was the art of it.

"Cozy," Tam said behind her, in the voice he used when it was absolutely not cozy. His hand found Ash's shoulder. The child stood very still, translucent as sea-glass now, the light of the passage showing faintly through her palms.

"It isn't," Wren said. "But keep your voice down. Sound has weight down here. Everything you say, someone will try to collect."

They walked into the cathedral of stalactites.

Wren had been here before — eleven years ago, a girl of nineteen with a voice like a drawn blade and a contract signed in her own blood because she'd thought blood was romantic then. She had told herself the memory would have dulled. It had not. It had only grown more precise, the way grief does.

The cavern opened upward forever. Stalactites hung in their thousands, pale as choir throats, some thick as towers, some thin as needles, all of them weeping slow beads of mineral water that rang against the stone below — plink, plink, plink — a metronome older than any kingdom. And threaded through them, drifting between the stone teeth like motes in a sunbeam, were lights.

Soul-lights. Fireflies, if fireflies had once been people.

They moved in slow constellations, hundreds of them, thousands, each one pulsing gently with its own rhythm, and Wren knew — because she had been trained to know, because knowing was the cruelest part of her education — that each pulse was a debt being counted. Somewhere in the dark above, a ledger turned its pages, and every flicker of light was a line item.

"Oh," Ash said softly. It was the longest word she'd managed in days, and it came out round with wonder rather than fear, which broke Wren's heart in a way she hadn't budgeted for.

"Don't look up too long," Wren murmured. "They notice being noticed."

* * *

The court assembled itself around them the way weather assembles — no heralds, no trumpets, just a gradual gathering of presence until you realized you had been surrounded for some time.

Benches of black stone rose in tiers along the cavern walls, and upon them sat the Undercourt: figures robed in colors that did not quite commit to existing, grey-violet, dusk-brown, the green of deep water. Some wore shapes that were almost human. Some wore shapes that had heard about humans secondhand. Their faces were smooth as river stones, and where features should have been there were only suggestions — the idea of eyes, the rumor of a mouth.

At the center, on a dais of fused bone, stood a desk. Not a throne. A desk. Because the Undercourt did not rule; it accounted.

And behind the desk, sorting papers by lantern-light with the unhurried contentment of a man who loved his work, sat the Magistrate of Debts.

He looked, infuriatingly, kind. Round-shouldered, spectacles of smoked glass, ink-stained fingers, the face of a favorite uncle who always remembered your birthday. Wren knew better. She had watched that face sign away a miller's daughter's laughter for three coppers' worth of favor, and it had worn the same gentle expression throughout, the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

"Well," the Magistrate said, looking up, and his voice was warm bread and hearth-smoke and every lie Wren had ever believed. "Visitors. How unusual. We so rarely get visitors who haven't already signed something."

"We're here about a signature," Jude said. Wren heard the tremor under their steadiness and respected it enormously. "One made thirty years ago. By my mother."

"Mm." The Magistrate removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, replaced them. "Ledgers are kept in the inner office. Appointments are required."

"We don't have time for appointments," Jude said. "The moon turns thirteen days from tonight. Thirteen turns since the bargain matured—"

"Twelve," said the Magistrate pleasantly. "You lost a day on the switchbacks. The mountain keeps its own calendar; I'd have thought the dead taught you that much." He folded his hands. "But of course. Of course you may wait. Everyone waits, in the end. Do make yourselves comfortable."

The benches shifted. Stone ground against stone, and a space cleared for them in the front row, close enough to the dais that Wren could smell the ink — and under the ink, faintly, the smell of the soul-lights, which smelled like the moment just before waking from a good dream.

Tam sat down heavily beside her. "I hate this place," he whispered.

"I know," Wren whispered back.

"No, I really hate it. There's no sky. A man needs a sky."

"A man also needed to stay home planting turnips," Wren said, "and yet."

He elbowed her. It was, absurdly, comforting.

Ash did not sit. Ash stood in the aisle, looking up at the drifting lights, and one of them — a small gold one, quick-pulsed, restless — drifted down toward her. It circled her twice. Ash raised one translucent hand, and the light settled on her palm like a bird deciding to trust her, and for a moment the whole court went silent, even the stalactites seeming to hold their drip.

Then a clerk-thing with too many elbows scurried up and shooed the light away, muttering about unscheduled contact, and the moment broke, and the court resumed its murmur, and Wren's hands were shaking.

Because she recognized that gold light. Eleven years ago, she had sung it out of a body on this very floor. She had been ordered to, and she had done it, and the body it left had been a boy of six who had owed a debt incurred before his birth, and she had sung him loose like drawing a splinter, and the sound he made —

She had refused the next order. That was the whole story of Wren Alaire, compressed: she sang one soul free and refused the next, and the note she loosed instead cracked forty panes of court glass and cost her everything, and she had spent eleven years telling herself the first song didn't count because she'd been young, because she'd been owned, because — because.

The gold light pulsed across the cavern, quick and restless, and Wren looked at her hands until it stopped hurting.

* * *

They waited four hours. Perhaps five. Time down here was a courtesy the court extended or withheld, and it withheld it generously.

It was during the fourth hour that a clerk-thing sidled up to Wren — the many-elbowed one, its robe smelling of dust and sealing wax — and leaned close to what might have been its ear.

"The Voice," it hissed. "We had a Voice once. She ran."

"Ran where?" Wren said. "Sounds careless."

"It is asked whether the Voice remembers her scales."

"It is answered that the Voice remembers nothing and is nobody, a traveling musician of no account, poor as a church mouse and twice as pious."

The clerk-thing made a sound like wet paper tearing, which Wren remembered was laughter. "The court requests a demonstration. Unannounced performances are traditional. It would be a shame if the visitor could not... participate."

And there it was. Eleven years gone, and the court still knew exactly where to put the knife.

"I'm afraid I've lost my voice," Wren said. "Tragic condition. Very sudden."

"The court is patient," said the clerk-thing, and oozed away.

Tam leaned in. "What was that?"

"They want me to sing."

"Is that bad? You're good at singing. You're insufferable about how good at singing."

"Yes," Wren said. "That's the problem." She flexed her fingers, felt the old calluses, the ghost of the training that lived in them deeper than any scar. "Down here, singing isn't performing, Tam. It's transacting. Every note is an offer. Every phrase is a contract. They trained me to sing souls out of bodies, and if I open my mouth in this room, they'll be listening for exactly how much of that training is still in me. And how much of it wants to be."

"And is it?" Tam asked. He wasn't mocking now. He was looking at her with those grave, turnip-farmer's eyes. "Still in you?"

Wren thought about lying. She was, historically, excellent at lying.

"Some of it," she said. "The way a knife stays sharp in a drawer."

* * *

The Magistrate called for music at the turning of some hour only he could see.

"Entertainment," he announced, to the court at large, spreading his arms like a host unveiling dinner. "Our guests have traveled far, and hospitality is a debt like any other. We pay our debts here." A ripple of stone-dry amusement through the tiers. "The lady in the grey cloak. You have the look of a singer. Singers always do — it's in the posture, the way you hold your chin. Come. One song. A small one. Nothing binding."

*Nothing binding.* The oldest lie in the Undercourt. Nothing down here was unbinding. Breath was a loan; silence was collateral.

Wren stood. Her knees behaved, which surprised her. She walked to the open floor beneath the stalactite-cathedral, and the dripping metronome counted her steps — plink, plink, plink — and above her the soul-lights drifted and pulsed, and among them, somewhere, a small gold light was watching, and behind the desk a kind-faced man waited with his pen already moving toward fresh paper.

She had planned, on the climb, what she would sing if this happened. Something harmless. A road ballad, a drinking song, anything with no power in it, a musical shrug.

But then she looked up at the lights. At the boy-shaped gold one, quick-pulsed, restless. At Ash standing at the bench, watching her with those eyes going more transparent by the hour, and humming — barely audible, barely there — the lullaby fragment that had undone Jude in the night, the lullaby of the soul woven inside her.

And Wren understood, with the terrible clarity that only ever arrives onstage, that the court was not testing whether she could still sing.

They were testing whether she would sing *for* them. Whether the old instrument still played itself.

So she chose the counter-song.

Not all of it. Not the true melody — that was for the thirteenth turn, that was the whole desperate plan, and she would die before she handed it over early. But the scale beneath it. The exercises. The first thing they had ever taught her, repurposed: the same intervals, inverted. The notes that had been used to loosen souls from bodies, run backwards, so that instead of pulling a soul out, they settled a soul in. Grounded it. Gave it weight and walls and a door that locked from the inside.

A lullaby, if lullabies had been designed by traitors.

She began low. The cavern took her voice and multiplied it, stalactite to stalactite, until it seemed the whole mountain hummed. The drips fell into rhythm with her. The soul-lights began, slowly, to drift downward — not summoned, not commanded, but *comforted*, settling like birds at dusk, dimming toward rest.

And the gold light — the boy-shaped one, the one she had pulled from a six-year-old's chest eleven years ago — sank through the air and came to rest, glowing soft and slow, just above her head. Like a crown. Like forgiveness.

The court had gone utterly still.

On the dais, the Magistrate's pen had stopped moving. He regarded her over his smoked glasses with an expression that had finally, briefly, stopped being kind — and became, instead, interested. Which was infinitely worse.

"That song," he said, when the last note had died among the stones, "is not in any repertoire I know."

"I improvise," Wren said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Road habit. You pick things up."

"Mm." He wrote something. One line. Then he set down the pen, folded his hands, and smiled at her with all the warmth of a bank vault. "You know, we never did close your file, Mistress Alaire. Accounts have such long memories. And yours—" he tapped the ledger once, lightly, "—has a page remaining. Half a page. Interesting, that. Most pages fill completely."

Behind her, she heard Jude go very still.

"What page," Jude said. "What's on her page?"

"Now, now." The Magistrate spread his hands, beneficent, awful. "Client confidentiality. Though — as it happens, since we are discussing accounts, and since your appointment has finally, formally begun—" he drew a ledger toward himself, an old one, its cover dark with age, and set it on the desk before him with the tenderness of a man laying out a child for blessing, "—perhaps we should discuss yours. The Thackeray account. Opened thirty years ago. One signature. One purchase." He looked up, and his smoked glasses caught the soul-light, and for a moment his eyes were two small fires. "Seller present. Buyer deceased. And one item of merchandise standing right there, wearing out of her own skin."

He turned the ledger around to face them.

"Shall we review the terms?"
