## 16. The Magistrate's Ledger

The Magistrate of Debts received us in a gallery of hanging scales.

That is not a figure of speech. I mean it literally. The chamber beyond the throne-cathedral was long and dim and lined on both sides with brass balances suspended from chains that vanished into the dark overhead, and each scale held its small cargo: a moth wing, a child's tooth, a knot of hair gone grey, a single dried cherry. Some pans hung level. Some dipped low as if their contents were heavier than anything in the world. None of them moved. There was no wind down there, and no breath either, except ours.

Wren walked ahead of me with her chin up and her hands very still at her sides, the way she did when she was terrified and wanted it to be nobody's business but her own. Ash had my hand in a grip that had stopped being a child's grip somewhere on the switchbacks and become something more like a drowning sailor's. Tam brought up the rear, carrying our packs and his own stubbornness in equal measure.

"He'll try to be polite," Wren murmured without turning her head. "That's the worst part. He's terribly polite."

"Is he cruel?" I asked.

"Oh, he never has to be." She said it lightly, the way she said most true things. "He just opens the ledger."

* * *

The Magistrate sat at a desk.

I had expected a throne. I had expected, after the cathedral of stalactites and the soul-lights drifting in their cages like slow green snow, some vast and terrible figure enthroned above us all. Instead there was a modest desk of black wood, an inkwell, two chairs for guests — actual chairs, cushioned, which struck me as obscene — and behind the desk a person who might have been anybody's uncle. He had a soft face, spectacles perched on a soft nose, ink stains on three fingers, and the patient expression of a man who has spent centuries doing arithmetic and has made his peace with it.

"Thackeray," he said, before I had spoken a word. "Jude Thackeray. Do sit down."

"How do you know my name?"

"You are written here." He touched the ledger on his desk with one stained finger, almost fondly. "You have been written here for thirty years. Longer, actually. Since before you were born, which is a sentence I rarely get to say to the person concerned. Most of them are dead by the time they arrive." He smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was somehow worse. "You must be tired. The ascent is brutal this time of year. Would you like tea?"

"We would like Ash's contract," Wren said, "nullified."

"Yes. I imagine you would." He rang a small bell, and from somewhere in the dark a servant-thing with too many elbows brought tea in real cups, real porcelain, painted with little wrens. I looked at Wren. Wren looked at the ceiling. "Drink," the Magistrate advised us all generally. "Bargaining is thirsty work, and you are about to hear terms. It goes better warm."

Ash took a cup because Ash took everything offered — she had been made, I think, out of thirteen souls' worth of hunger, and hunger does not learn manners quickly. She drank. Her edges had gone translucent again since we'd entered the mountain, faint as breath on glass, and when the lamplight passed through her shoulder I could see the wall behind it. My stomach turned over every time.

The Magistrate opened the ledger.

* * *

"There are several ways this can go," he said, "and I will be honest with you about all of them, because honesty costs me nothing and confusion costs everyone time." He turned pages. The paper whispered. Somewhere in the hanging scales, something shifted — I could not see what — and settled. "First: the child leaves with you tonight, free and whole, and the debt remains unpaid. This is the option you came hoping for, so let me save us all the suspense. It is not available. Contracts beneath Mount Serel are not enforced by me, Master Thackeray. They are enforced by the terms themselves. I am merely the bookkeeper. You might as well ask the river to unspill."

"The counter-song—" Wren began.

"Is real," he agreed pleasantly. "And difficult. And I notice you haven't taught it yet, Mistress Alaire, which suggests you're still deciding whether you can bear to watch him pay the price. That's your business. But we'll come back to what the counter-song requires, because I find people often misunderstand it, and misunderstandings make such messes on my clean floor."

He turned another page and then, with a small ceremonial air that I hated immediately, he rotated the ledger to face us.

"There," he said. "Your mother's hand."

I had prepared myself for many things in that chamber. Scales. Stalactites. Tea. What I had not prepared for was handwriting I knew.

My mother wrote the way she did everything — firmly, quickly, slightly slanted leftward, as though leaning into a wind only she could feel. I had seen that script on flour sacks and seed packets and one letter to my father, folded into a coat pocket after she died, which I had read so many times the creases went to fur. *Meet me at the stones at moonrise. Bring nothing. Tell no one.* That same hand now filled half a page of Undercourt vellum:

*A hearing of the dead, firstborn of Maren Thackeray, sold in perpetuity against warding of the valley called Hollowmere, its boundary fixed, its silence guaranteed unto the third generation.*

Below it, in different ink: *Witnessed. Weighed. Accepted.*

And below that, in a column I hadn't noticed at first because it was so small, so ordinary, so much like a grocer's tally: *Maturity: thirteenth turn of the moon following the arrival of the vessel.*

The vessel. Ash.

"I don't understand," I heard myself say. My voice sounded far away, like it was coming through one of those hanging scales. "She sold my hearing. Not me. Not my — why does the contract care about a child I've known for twelve turns?"

"Because debts mature into their consequences, Master Thackeray, and consequences travel." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried. "Your mother bought silence for a valley. Silence has to come from somewhere — nothing is ever simply absent down here; absence is a material, like timber. The quiet of Hollowmere was cut from thirteen voices, and woven — you know this already, I think, or you've guessed — into a vessel. A holding-shape. Something that could carry the debt until it came due. Your mother did not create the vessel. She merely signed for it. The Court does the weaving."

"Made," I said. The word came out flat. "Not born. Made."

"Made," he confirmed gently. "Of thirteen voices that once belonged to living people. One of whom, incidentally, sang rather beautifully. You'd have liked her. Everyone did."

Wren made a small sound beside me, like a string being tuned too tight.

"But here is where your evening becomes interesting." He drew the ledger back and laid one finger along the maturity clause. "The debt comes due at the thirteenth turn. The vessel — the child — passes wholly into the Court's possession. Soul, voice, everything she is. That is the letter of it. However." He looked up, and behind the spectacles his eyes were old and brown and terribly reasonable. "Contracts admit substitution. Always have. It is the oldest mercy in accounting. If a soul of equal weight presents itself, voluntarily, knowingly, in full possession of its faculties — the vessel may be released, and the substitute takes up the debt instead. Clean transfer. No interest accrued. I would even waive the processing fee, as a courtesy, given the circumstances of your journey."

The chamber was very quiet. In their hundreds, the hanging scales hung motionless.

"And what," I asked, and my mouth had gone dry around the words, "would equal weight be?"

"That depends on the soul." He turned back a page, and there — I saw it — was a table of weights, entries in that same tidy grocer's hand. "A life weighs differently according to what it carries. Regret adds mass. So does love, though people are always surprised by that; they assume love makes you lighter. It doesn't. Love is ballast." He glanced at Ash, translucent in the lamplight, watching him with those eyes that had seen thirteen lifetimes. "The vessel weighs, at current estimate, the equivalent of a long grief borne without complaint. Or a talent surrendered rather than used. Or—"

"Or a hearing of the dead," I said. "Sold willingly. By the heir."

His pen stopped moving.

For the first time since we had entered, the Magistrate of Debts looked at me — really looked, over the rims of his spectacles, with something that in anyone else I would have called surprise.

"Well," he said. "You *have* been talking to someone."

"Wren told me the shape of it. On the road. Not the details." I kept my voice steady. It cost more than I can easily describe. "The counter-song. The heir surrenders what the contract purchased, and the debt dissolves. That's one door."

"It is the elegant door," he admitted. "Elegant doors are expensive. You would lose it forever — the whispers, the bones, the whole conversation you've had with your valley's dead since you were small enough to fit inside a flour barrel. Every carved memorial you ever made, mute thereafter. Thirty years of voices, ended in one song. People tell me it feels like going deaf twice." He folded his hands. "Whereas substitution is simpler. Someone else pays. The child walks out whole. You keep your gift. Everyone gets what they want except the substitute, who is, presumably, past caring."

"And who," said Wren, very quietly, "would that be?"

The Magistrate spread his hands, all innocence, all ink stains. "Whoever volunteers. That is rather the point of volunteering."

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened inside me in that moment, because later — in the shaking dark, on the run, in the year after — I needed to remember it exactly, and memory blurs whatever shames it.

For the length of perhaps ten heartbeats, I wanted someone else to pay.

There. It is written now, in whatever ink these pages use, and I won't scratch it out. The Magistrate had barely finished speaking before a voice in me — smooth, reasonable, wearing my mother's leftward-slanting hand — began its arithmetic. *Tam,* it said. *Tam Broadfoot, gravedigger's son, kind and plain and heavy with regret he's never once spoken aloud. He'd weigh enough. He'd volunteer, if asked rightly. He thinks in terms of fair trades; it's how he was raised. And he'd live — that's the thing, isn't it? He wouldn't die. He'd just stay. Down here, among the scales, keeping accounts. People live decades in the deep places. And Ash gets to grow up. And you get to keep the voices, keep the bones singing, keep the one part of yourself that was ever truly yours —*

Ten heartbeats. Maybe eleven.

Then I looked at Ash's hand around mine — nearly transparent now, the lamp shining through it like through horn — and at Tam standing behind us with his jaw set in that way that meant he had already done the same arithmetic I had, and arrived at the same answer, and was *proud* of it — and the voice in me shut up as if slapped.

"No," I said aloud, to the room, to the Magistrate, mostly to myself.

"No?" The Magistrate tilted his head.

"No substitutions. Nobody in this company is inventory." I stood, and my knees complained, and I ignored them. "We didn't climb your miserable mountain to trade one hostage for another. You can close that page."

"A pity." He did not sound as though it were a pity. He sounded as though a fisherman feels watching a trout decline the fly — patient, amused, certain the river is long. "But the offer stands until the turn. It always does. Substitution never expires; that's its charm." He closed the ledger softly. "You should rest. The thirteenth turn arrives tomorrow at moonset, and counter-songs want strong lungs. Oh — and Master Thackeray." I paused at the door. He was re-inking his pen, not looking at me. "Your mother's signature is the finest I've handled in a century. Firm. Unhesitating. Whatever else she was, she meant it. People find that either comforting or unbearable. I'm curious which it will be for you."

Neither, I wanted to tell him. Both at once, forever. But the elbow-servant was already showing us out, and the scales swung faintly in the draft of the closing doors, and somewhere behind us a page turned.

* * *

They gave us a cell — "guest quarters," the servant insisted, and it even had beds — high in the mountain's ribs, with a window slit that showed a smear of cloud and, lower down, the distant glimmer of the lowlands where fires burned in villages that had names and mornings.

Wren sat cross-legged on the floor with her back to the wall, teaching herself the counter-song in whispers, stopping every few bars to press her knuckles against her mouth. Ash slept — actually slept, curled small, her translucence fading a little in honest rest, and I tucked the blanket around her and felt the ghost-weight of her, hardly more than a cat.

Tam stood at the window slit, looking out at the fires.

"You should sleep," I told him.

"Aye. In a bit." He didn't turn around. His voice was easy, easy as a Sunday. "Go on, Jude. You've got a song to learn."

I went. I lay down beside Ash in the dark and listened to Wren's whisper rise and fall like weather, and I told myself the tightness in my chest was only fear of losing the voices, only grief rehearsing itself.

It was only much later — when the whispering stopped, and Wren slept, and the mountain ticked and settled around us like a cooling stove — that I realized the cell was short one Broadfoot.

And that the scales, in their hundreds, had all seemed to lean, very slightly, toward the door.
