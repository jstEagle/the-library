## 17. What Tam Carried Down

Tam Broadfoot had never been good at secrets. In Hollowmere, where the dead could hear a lie forming in your chest before your mouth got around to it, he'd learned early that the only safe secret was a small one — like the time he'd eaten an entire funeral pie meant for the mourners of old Marta Kell, and had confessed within the hour because his stomach growled guiltily every time a ghost drifted past.

This secret was not small.

It sat in him now like a swallowed stone as he stood watch outside the guest alcoves of the Undercourt's antechamber, listening to the drip of water in the dark and the faint, imprisoned glow of soul-lights caught in the stalactites overhead. They looked like fireflies in jars, Wren had said, and then she hadn't said anything else for a long while, which for Wren was its own kind of confession.

Tam checked that Ash was asleep. She was — curled on her side in the crook of Jude's arm, her edges still faintly translucent, so that when she breathed you could see the pale shimmer of the stone wall through her shoulder. Thirteen turns. The moon was nearly full above the mountain, and up here beneath it, in this cathedral of debts, she was thinning like smoke deciding whether to stay in the room.

*His plain life weighs least.* That was the arithmetic Tam had done, back on the road, in the quiet hours while the others slept and he kept watch because watching was what he was for. He wasn't clever like Wren or strange like Jude or miraculous like Ash. He was a gravedigger's son from a valley that refused the world. He could dig a straight hole, carry a heavy load, and sit with dying people without flinching, which his father had always said was the whole trade in the end. Nobody would write songs about him. Nobody *had* written songs about him, and Wren had once told him, with terrible kindness, that this was actually a compliment.

A soul of equal weight, the Magistrate had said. A substitution freely offered.

And Tam had lain awake doing sums, the way he did when the numbers were too simple to say out loud. What weighed more: a child woven from thirteen souls, or a man whose greatest accomplishment was knowing how deep to dig before the frost line? He knew what the ledger would say. Ledgers always said the same thing.

So he'd waited until Jude fell asleep holding Ash, until Wren's breathing went slow and even in the far alcove, and then he'd taken the little knife he used for trimming candlewicks and cut his palm, because the Magistrate's clerk — a gray, apologetic creature made mostly of paperwork and regret — had explained that oaths required blood, and blood required a blade, and everything in this place required something.

The corridor to the Magistrate's hall was not hard to find. That was the trap of it, Tam realized as he walked. Nothing down here was hard to find. The Undercourt *wanted* you to come. It was a market, and markets left their doors open.

* * *

The hall was smaller than he expected, and colder. The stalactite chandeliers burned low, their captive lights dimmed to embers, as though the room itself were trying not to wake anyone. The Magistrate of Debts sat behind a desk that seemed grown rather than built, all smooth dark wood and slow grain, and he looked up at Tam with the expression of a man who has been interrupted during the least interesting part of his day.

"The gravedigger," the Magistrate said. "Broadfoot. Third of that name, if my records are accurate." He turned a page in a ledger that had no business being legible by that light, but was. "You've come about the child."

"I've come to make a trade," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was a mercy. His hand, wrapped in a rag he'd torn from his shirt, did not feel steady at all. "Me for her."

The Magistrate set down his pen. It was a long moment before he spoke, and when he did, there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened Tam more than cruelty would have.

"You understand," the Magistrate said, "that I am obligated to tell you what this costs."

"You're obligated to take it, is what you are."

"I am obligated to *weigh* it." The Magistrate rose, and his shadow moved wrong across the walls, lagging half a step behind him like a man reluctant to follow. "That is the whole of my office, Master Broadfoot. I do not choose what comes into my court. I weigh it. And I must tell you —" he opened the ledger again, turned pages with a dry whisper, "— that your life does not weigh what you believe it weighs."

"Then weigh it," Tam said. "Plain life. Plain man. Gravedigger's son. No magic, no songs, no prophecies. I can't read worth a damn and I've never been further from Hollowmere than Hollowmere's edge. There's folk who'd miss me — three of them, counting the girl — but they'd get over it, because they're the kind that gets over things. That's what makes them good." He heard his own voice crack on the last part and hated it. "She's seven. She's thirteen people in one small body and she hasn't gotten to be any of them yet. Weigh that against me. Go on."

The Magistrate regarded him for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Do you know why your valley is silent?"

Tam blinked. "What?"

"Hollowmere. The warding your mother's generation purchased — no, forgive me, *Jude's* mother. You are not of that line." He turned another page. "I mention it because silence is also a weight, and you have carried it your whole life without knowing. Men who keep watch over graves for thirty years, who sit with the dying because someone must — these lives do not tip scales the way you imagine. But proceed. If you wish to be weighed, I will weigh you. Place your hand upon the ledger and speak your oath, and we shall see what a plain life comes to."

Tam stepped forward. The desk was cold under his unwounded palm. The ledger lay open to a page headed in silver script: *Substitution, Voluntary, Irrevocable Upon Completion of Oath.*

He took a breath. He thought about his father's hands, earth to the wrist. About Ash laughing at his terrible whittling. About Jude's face when she'd first held the girl, like someone handed a lantern after thirty years in the dark.

"In place of the soul called Ash," Tam began, "freely given, fully meant, I offer—"

"*Don't you dare finish that sentence.*"

Jude's voice hit the hall like a thrown stone.

* * *

She came out of the corridor fast, barefoot — she'd run from the alcove without her boots, Tam saw, and her hair was loose and wild and her eyes were wet and furious, which was a combination he had never once seen on Jude Thackeray in thirty years of knowing them, and it was aimed entirely at him.

Wren was behind her, moving slower, arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone who has seen a play before and knows how the second act goes.

"How," Tam said stupidly, "did you—"

"You bled on the rag and left it by the door," Jude said. "The dead used to talk to me, Tam. Blood talks louder." Their voice broke and reassembled itself. "You were going to do it while we slept. You were going to *die* while we slept, and let us wake up to a note."

"There wasn't going to be a note."

"That's worse!"

"It's tidier!" Tam heard his own voice rise to meet theirs, and some distant part of him noted that they were shouting in the Undercourt, which Wren had specifically and at length instructed them never to do. "Look — Jude — it's arithmetic. It's just arithmetic. One plain life against hers. Any fool can do that sum."

"Then let me show you where your sum is wrong," Jude said, and crossed the hall, and took Tam's wounded hand — gently, the way they handled bone, the way they handled everything that had once been alive — and held it up between them. "Because I have spent thirty years weighing lives. That was my whole work. Every carving I ever made, I asked the dead what a person was worth, and do you know what they said? Every single one? They said *you cannot know from the outside*. The drowned farmer I carved in the first chapter of my life here — everyone in the valley thought he was nothing, a drunk, a waste of soil. The bones remembered a man who pulled three children out of the river the winter before he went in himself. Plain lives." Their grip tightened. "*Plain lives are the ones that hold everything else up, Tam. You're not the lightest thing on the scale. You're the thing the scale stands on.*"

Tam's throat had gone tight. "That's not fair. You can't say things like that when I'm trying to be brave."

"I know," Jude said, and their voice gentled all at once, which was somehow worse. "Bravery that costs other people isn't bravery, love. It's just spending someone else's grief."

"He's right, you know," Wren said from the doorway, and Tam turned to see her leaning against the stone with her arms folded, and behind her, small and sleep-rumpled and heartbreakingly solid-edged tonight, Ash.

Ash padded forward, looked at Tam's bandaged hand, looked at the ledger, and then did something none of them expected. She reached up and took Tam's wounded hand in both of hers, and she hummed.

It was the lullaby. The one from the thirteenth soul, the one that had undone Jude in the ruins of their certainty weeks ago. But she bent it, changed it, made it rounder and sillier, the way children ruin songs into something better, and she hummed it directly into Tam's palm as though she could pour it into the cut.

"She says," Wren translated softly, "that if you go, she'll have learned her first word from a coward. And she doesn't think you're a coward. So she'd have to unlearn it, and words are heavy."

Tam laughed, or sobbed, or produced some sound that was legally both. "When did she learn to joke?"

"Roads teach fast," Wren said. Then her face changed, and the humor drained out of it, and she looked past Tam at the Magistrate, who had watched the entire scene with the stillness of furniture. "And you. You were going to *weigh him*. You were going to let him swear."

"I am bound to accept any oath freely offered," the Magistrate said. "I am not bound to approve of them. I will note, Singer, that you of all people should understand the difference between a debt and a choice."

"Don't." Wren's voice went flat and cold, a key turning. "Don't you use my name for things."

But the damage was done; Tam saw it land. He saw Wren's shoulders draw in, saw the singer's famous poise crack along an old seam, and he understood suddenly — the way you understand things in bad light, all at once and uselessly — that Wren had stood in this exact spot once, before this exact desk, and that whatever she'd sworn or refused to swear down here was the wound she'd been carrying since before any of them met her.

"We're leaving," Jude said. "All four of us. Together. That was the whole plan. That was the *only* plan."

"And yet the contract matures in two days," the Magistrate said mildly, "and you have no substitution, no counter-song you can perform, and no legal standing in my court. Forgive me. I do not say it to wound. I say it because I am, above all things, accurate." He closed the ledger. "Take your gravedigger home, Thackeray. Grieve efficiently. Some bargains cannot be broken."

"Some bargains," Wren said quietly, and there was something new in her voice, something Tam had never heard from her — not performance, not armor, but the raw timber underneath, "can be *completed*. Check your own laws, Magistrate. You taught them to me. You should remember what's written in them."

For the first time since they'd entered the mountain, the Magistrate of Debts went still in a way that suggested he had just remembered something he had very much hoped to forget.

"Get out of my hall," he said.

"Gladly," said Wren, and took Ash's hand, and led them all out into the dark.

* * *

They didn't speak until they were back in the antechamber, and then they spoke all at once, which is what families do.

"You cut your *hand*, Tam, there are a hundred ways to—"

"You left the rag *by the door*, honestly, I've seen you bury people with more subtlety—"

"I was being *practical*—"

"You were being a *martyr*, which is practical the way drowning is swimming—"

"Enough." Wren raised one hand, and — habit older than her disgrace — the room quieted, because a Voice is a Voice even stripped of rank. She looked around at the three of them: Jude with their fists still clenched, Tam cradling his bandaged palm, Ash sitting cross-legged on the floor watching them all with the grave attention of someone taking notes for later.

"Here is what happens now," Wren said. "Nobody offers anybody. Nobody swears anything without the rest of us in the room. We have two days, and I have a song, and it requires a seller's heir willing to give up the gift the bargain bought." Her eyes went to Jude, and softened, and Tam saw her decide to say the next part plainly, because plainness was the only currency left. "It means the dead go silent for you forever, Jude. Not dampened. Gone. If we do this, you lose the voices you've had since you were born."

The silence that followed was the truest silence Tam had ever heard — deeper than Hollowmere's, heavier than the mountain's. Jude looked at Ash. Ash looked at Jude. And the child reached out and took hold of Jude's sleeve with one small translucent hand, and held on.

"All right," Jude said at last, hoarse. "Teach me the song."

"But first," Tam added, because somebody had to, and because his hand hurt and his heart hurt and gentle humor was the only tool he'd ever trusted, "somebody tie me to a chair at night. Apparently I make deals in my sleep."

Ash giggled — her first laugh in the mountain, bright and impossible as a struck bell — and somewhere far below them, in the dark under the world, something ancient shifted in its sleep and began, very slowly, to count down.
