## 18. The Counter-Song

Wren Alaire had spent eleven years telling herself she was a coward, and she had gotten very good at it. It was, in its way, her finest composition—more practiced than any aria, more convincing than the lie she'd told the Magistrate's clerks to slip out of Vell, more durable than the voice itself. A coward did not go back. A coward knew which doors stayed shut.

So when the answer came to her at three in the morning, in the cold antechamber of the Undercourt where they'd been permitted to sleep on benches like luggage, her first instinct was not triumph.

Her first instinct was to keep her mouth shut and let Jude Thackeray die of old age inside a bargain they didn't understand.

She sat up on the stone bench. The soul-lights in the cavern above drifted past the doorway like slow amber fish, and their glow caught the edges of everything—the sleeping child curled against Tam Broadfoot's side, the ledger pages Jude had copied by candlelight spread across their knees even in sleep, ink-stained fingers still holding the quill.

Wren looked at those pages for a long time.

She had read them too, of course. She'd read them with a singer's eye rather than a scholar's, and that was the difference. Scholars read contracts for clauses. Singers read them for rests—for the places where the music stopped and something had to fill the silence.

And there it was, in the fourth clause, written in the cramped hand of some Undercourt scrivener who had probably been dead for two centuries: *the hearing purchased shall pass wholly to the buyer and her heirs, until such time as the heir of the seller relinquishes it freely, wherein the consideration is deemed returned entire.*

*Deemed returned entire.*

The debt dissolved. Not transferred. Not substituted. *Dissolved.*

Wren pressed her palms against her eyes and laughed, once, quietly, the way you laugh when you find out the exit has been behind you the whole time and it opens onto a cliff.

Because clause four wasn't a mercy. It was a trapdoor dressed as one. It meant that Jude Thackeray—the person who had spent thirty years listening to the dead whisper through carved bone, who had built an entire life around that listening, who had crossed the boundary stones knowing what would come flooding back into them, who had paid a memory at the Doors of Teeth without flinching—it meant that Jude could save Ash only by giving up the one thing that had ever made them feel useful in the world.

The dead's voices. Forever.

Not muted. Not dampened by valley stones. Gone. The counter-song would unmake the gift at its root, and Wren knew exactly how it worked, because she had been trained in the making of such songs. The Undercourt did not teach its Voice how to free souls out of any particular fondness for freedom. It taught her the architecture of binding so that she could build bindings—and every binding, every contract, every chain sung into a soul had a seam. One note, placed precisely, and the whole garment came apart.

The problem was that the seam ran through Jude.

* * *

"You're awake," Jude said, when Wren gave up pretending to sleep and went to sit by the antechamber's single guttering lamp.

"I'm thinking."

"That's worse." Jude didn't look up from the ledger copy. "You think loudly. You always have. You hum when you're working something out, and right now you're humming the third movement of the Lamentation of Serel, which is either very bad news or very good news, and given that we're sitting under a mountain full of creatures who eat debts, I'm betting bad."

"Your mother," Wren said, "had terrible taste in bargains."

Jude's quill stopped.

"She also," Wren went on, before the silence could set like mortar, "had excellent taste in loopholes. Or her scrivener did. Clause four, Thackeray. Read it again. Read it the way I taught you to read the door-songs—not what it says, what it *doesn't* say."

Jude read it. Wren watched them read it, watched the understanding arrive the way weather arrives over a flat field—first the far-off darkening, then the wind, then the whole sky coming down at once.

"No," Jude said.

"It dissolves the debt."

"*No.*"

"It doesn't move Ash's soul to someone else. It doesn't weigh anyone against anyone. It returns the payment, entire, and the contract comes apart like wet paper, and the Magistrate—bound by his own laws, which is the only thing in this mountain that binds him—has to open his hands and let her walk out."

"I said no." Jude's voice cracked on the second repetition, and Wren heard what lived underneath it: not anger. Terror. "You don't get to decide that. You don't get to sit there with your beautiful ruined voice and your eleven years of running and decide what I give up."

"I'm not deciding anything." Wren kept her voice level, the way she'd learned to keep it in the throne room, where any tremor was noted and priced. "I'm telling you what the song does. What you do with that information is yours."

"And if I say yes? If I sing this thing with you?" Jude finally looked up, and their eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened, and Wren thought: *thirty years alone in a valley, and this is the face they make when someone offers them a way out.* "What happens to me? Tell me honestly. You owe me that much."

"The gift unravels," Wren said. "All of it. Everywhere. You won't hear the dead in Hollowmere or out of it. You won't hear them through bone or stone or water. It isn't deafness—it's worse than deafness, because deafness leaves silence, and silence can be listened *into*. This will be nothing. A room with no walls. You'll carve a memorial for some drowned farmer and the ivory will be just ivory, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering if you got the likeness wrong, because no one will ever tell you."

Jude flinched as though struck.

"There," Wren said softly. "That's the price. That's the whole price. No substitutions, no clever trades, no plain gravedigger's sons weighing themselves on scales that were never meant for them." She thought of Tam, huddled asleep with a child tucked against his ribs, and her chest did something complicated. "Which is why I waited until he was unconscious to tell you. He'd have tried to sing it himself, and his voice would crack the note, and we'd all be turned into interest payments."

A sound from across the room. Tam, not asleep after all, sitting up with Ash blinking beside him. His ears had gone pink.

"How long have you been awake?" Wren asked.

"Long enough," Tam said, "to know you two are going to argue about me like I'm a sack of turnips again, and long enough to hear the word *dissolves*, which I liked very much, and then the part about Jude losing—" He stopped. Looked at Jude. Looked at Wren. "Forever?"

"Forever," Wren said.

Tam was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone of a man laying a plank across a gap: "Well. It's a better trade than mine was."

"It's not a trade," Jude said. "It's an amputation."

"Ash isn't a limb," Tam said. "Ash is Ash." And the child, hearing her name, looked up at all of them with those eyes that had gone translucent at the edges now, faint as breath on glass, and smiled, and none of them could speak for a while after that.

* * *

They rehearsed through the night, because there was nothing else to do with the hours, and because the moon would reach its thirteenth turn tomorrow whether they slept or not.

Wren taught the way she had been taught—cruelly efficient, then, guiltily gentle now. The counter-song had seven phrases. Six of them were ordinary enough; any trained voice could carry them. The seventh was the seam-note, and it could not be sung alone. It required two voices in unison, pitched exactly, held exactly, because a binding woven by two parties—a seller and a court—could only be unwoven by two voices standing in for them. Wren would carry the court's line. Jude would carry their mother's.

"You'll be singing as her," Wren explained, positioning them facing each other across the lamp. "Not impersonating. Standing in. The contract doesn't care whose throat the note comes from, only whose *debt* it answers. When you hit the seventh phrase, you need to mean the surrender. Not perform it. *Mean* it. The song knows the difference. Everything down here knows the difference."

"What if I can't mean it?" Jude asked. "What if, at the last moment, my voice shakes?"

"Then we find out what the Magistrate does to defaulters, and I'd rather not find out. Again."

They began.

The first phrase was simple, almost lullaby-like, and Wren felt the old machinery of performance wake in her chest—the breath support, the placement, the cold bright attention. But singing it toward another person, rather than at a throne, changed the weight of it entirely. She kept catching herself watching Jude's face instead of the notes.

Second phrase. Third. On the fifth, Jude stumbled, and swore, and started over, and Wren said, "Again," in the tone of every merciless tutor she had ever survived, and Jude shot her a look that would have curdled milk, and obeyed.

Somewhere in the sixth hour, something shifted. Wren couldn't name the phrase where it happened. But the song stopped being a lesson and became a conversation—two lines braiding, answering, the court's cold harmony threading through the seller's warmer one, and when they reached the sixth phrase cleanly for the first time, the lamp flame stood straight up without flickering, and the soul-lights beyond the doorway drifted closer, gathering like moths, and Wren felt her arms prickle.

"They're listening," Tam whispered from the corner, where he sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. "The lights. They like it."

"They remember being sung to," Wren said quietly. "Everyone down here remembers being sung to. That's the whole horror of the place, if you want to know. Every creature in this mountain was somebody's treasure first."

On the next run-through, they reached the seventh phrase.

They stopped just short of it, as agreed—no sense spending the note in a rehearsal—but Wren sang up to its threshold and held there, and watched Jude hold there, both voices suspended a hair's breadth above the seam, and the silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

"It's real," Jude said. Their voice was hoarse. "It actually works. I can feel it waiting."

"Yes."

"And tomorrow I lose everything."

Wren sat back. Her throat ached; her hands, folded in her lap, were not quite steady, and she was glad the dark hid them. "May I tell you something cowardly?"

"You've never needed permission before."

"When I fled the court, I told myself I'd escaped. Eleven years I've told myself that. But I didn't escape, Thackeray. I *left*. There's a difference. Escaping means the thing stops having power over you. Leaving just means you've put distance between yourself and the power, and you spend the rest of your life checking over your shoulder to see how much is left." She looked at the lamp. "Tomorrow, when you sing that seventh phrase—if you mean it—you'll be doing the thing I never managed. Not leaving. Letting go. The dead's voices aren't a cage, I know that. They're the opposite. They're the thing that made you *you*. And you're going to open your hand and drop it, on purpose, for a child you've known half a moon-turn." She shook her head. "I rehearse courage for a living. I've never once seen it performed that well."

Jude was silent a long time. Then: "My mother sold my hearing to buy a valley's silence. Thirty years I thought that made me the price of something. Tomorrow I get to decide what I'm worth instead." A pause. "Besides. It's not as though I'm losing the only voice I'll ever hear."

"Oh?"

"I'll still hear yours. Terrible news for me, but there it is."

Wren laughed—really laughed, the unguarded kind, the kind she hadn't produced since before the glass cracked—and Tam snorted from his corner, and even Ash stirred and made a small sound that might, in a language older than kingdoms, have been laughter too.

They sang it through twice more, stopping short each time, and by the gray false-dawn that seeped into the antechamber from vents no one could explain, Jude's voice had gone smooth as river stone over every phrase, and Wren's had gone quiet in the way it only went quiet when she stopped performing and simply *sang*.

"One more," she said, "and then we rest. Because tomorrow night, when the moon turns, we stand in front of the Magistrate of Debts and we sing the seventh phrase and we *mean* it—and Thackeray?"

"Mm?"

"When you open your hand and let it fall—I want you to know that what you're buying with it is worth more than what you're paying. I've priced a great many things in my life. I know value when I see it."

Jude looked at her across the lamp, at the child asleep against Tam's shoulder, at the amber lights drifting in the dark beyond the door like patient stars.

"I know," Jude said. "That's the only reason I can sing it."

Far above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the mountain gave a low groan—as if it, too, had heard the rehearsal, and knew what was coming, and was already grieving the shape of things to come.
