## 19. Thirteenth Turn

The Undercourt did not have a sky, so it had built one.

Above the cathedral of stalactites hung a dome of grey stone painted—or perhaps grown—with a slow procession of moons, thirteen of them, each carved in a different phase, drifting on some mechanism older than the kingdom above. Tonight the thirteenth moon was nearly full, and its light fell through the cavern like milk poured into water.

Jude stood at the edge of the Court's great floor and tried to remember how to breathe.

They had rehearsed the counter-song all night in a side gallery that smelled of wet chalk and old incense, Wren's voice low and patient, correcting Jude's intervals again and again. "You're rushing," she'd said, for the fortieth time. "You always rush when you're frightened. It's the Thackeray curse. Your mother rushed too—I've heard the recordings in the ledger's margins."

"You've heard my mother sing?"

"I've heard her *hesitate*," Wren had said. "It's in the contract itself. Every bargain leaves a residue. Hers left a hesitation, right before she signed. Listen for it. When you feel yourself rushing—stop. Let the silence do the work."

Now, standing before the assembled court, Jude let the silence do the work.

* * *

The Magistrate of Debts sat upon a throne of stacked ledgers—actual ledgers, thousands of them, bound in leather gone soft with age, arranged so that their spines formed a seat and their pages fanned out behind him like the tail of a preening bird. He was not large. He was not small. He was exactly as large as the debt he was currently considering, which tonight made him very tall indeed.

Around him, tiered into the cavern walls, sat the rest of the Undercourt: clerks with ink-stained fingers, advocates with faces like folded paper, and here and there the soul-lights—imprisoned fireflies of pale gold, each one caged in a lantern of black glass, each one humming faintly. Ash had flinched at every hum on the way in. Now she stood between Tam and Wren, gripping Tam's hand hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and her edges had gone translucent as sea glass. Through her shoulder Jude could see the far wall of the cavern, dimly, like a candle behind parchment.

"Thirteenth turn," said the Magistrate, and his voice was the sound of a quill scratching across vellum, amplified past all decency. "The moon is full. The account matures. Present the collateral."

"She has a name," Jude said. "Her name is Ash."

"Ash." The Magistrate turned the word over like a coin of doubtful minting. "A name given by strangers. A name without provenance. Collateral does not require a name, heir of Thackeray. Collateral requires only weight." He opened a ledger across his knees—the original, Jude knew it at once, though they had never seen it before; they felt it the way you feel a storm coming in an old scar. The page bore thirteen signatures woven together in a braid of ink, and beneath them, alone, larger, written in a hand Jude knew better than their own heartbeat: **Maren Thackeray**.

Their mother's name. Their mother's hesitation, visible even now—a tremor in the M, a pause before the pen came down, thirty years old and still unfinished.

"I have not come to contest the signature," Jude said.

The court rustled. Somewhere a clerk dropped his quill and scrambled after it.

"Oh?" The Magistrate's eyebrows rose by a precise sixteenth of an inch. "Then you have come to pay. How refreshing. Most heirs weep. Some bargain. One gentleman attempted to flee up the switchbacks with the collateral under his arm, which I confess I found almost charming." His gaze slid to Tam, who went red to the ears. "But payment, payment is rare. What do you offer? Another soul of equal weight? We discussed this. There are candidates present, I believe. Several volunteers, in fact, who have been most tiresome about it—"

"No substitutes."

"Then what?"

Jude's mouth was dry. Thirty years of whispers pressed against the inside of their skull—the drowned farmer from chapter one of their life, the grandmother bones, the memorial stones, every voice they had ever carried, all of them murmuring at once now, as if they knew, as if the dead on the far side of the boundary stones had leaned close to listen.

*I don't want to lose you,* Jude told them silently. *I never wanted anything but to carry you.*

And then, because Wren had taught them this too: *Thank you.*

They looked at Wren.

Wren Alaire, disgraced Voice of the Undercourt, stood in the place where she had once been owned, wearing a traveling coat with a burnt hem and an expression she probably thought was neutral. Her hands were shaking. She saw Jude notice, and deliberately laced her fingers together to stop it, and lifted her chin in the smallest, most defiant nod Jude had ever received.

*Cowards together,* she'd said last night. *That's twice as much courage per coward. Arithmetic.*

Jude turned back to the throne of ledgers.

"I offer completion," they said. "Not substitution. Completion."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for three separate clerks to consult each other in urgent whispers and for one advocate to unfold himself slightly, like a letter being reread.

"Explain," said the Magistrate.

"The contract my mother signed purchased two things. You wrote them both down—you write everything down; it's your whole personality." A ripple went through the tiers; somewhere, incredibly, someone laughed and then strangled it. "She sold my hearing of the dead. And with the price, she bought the valley's warding—Hollowmere's silence. Two silences, one signature. But a contract is a living thing, Magistrate. Wren taught me that. It breathes. It can be *completed*—not broken, not cheated, not substituted. Completed. If the seller's heir voluntarily surrenders the gift the contract purchased—if I give up the hearing of the dead, freely, before this court, with full knowledge and no coercion—then the purchase dissolves. There is nothing left to buy. The debt returns to zero. And your own laws—" Jude nodded at the fanned pages behind the throne, "—which you are bound by more tightly than any of us will ever be bound by anything, require you to release what no longer carries debt."

"And if I decline?"

"Then you are holding a null contract," Wren said, stepping forward, her voice ringing off the stalactites, "and every clerk in this room knows what happens to a Magistrate who keeps a null contract on his books. The audit comes. The audit *always* comes." She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "I used to sit behind you, sir. Third tier, second row. I remember where you keep the audits."

For the first time, the Magistrate's quill-scratch voice hesitated.

Jude thought of their mother's hesitation, right there in the ink. *Stop,* Wren had said. *Let the silence do the work.* So Jude stopped. They let the whole court wait. They let the thirteenth moon drift another degree toward full.

"We will hear the counter-song," the Magistrate said at last, heavily, like a man agreeing to surgery. "If it fails, the collateral is forfeit, and the volunteer souls in the third row will be held in reserve. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Jude.

"Don't agree to the reserve part," Tam whispered.

"It's fine," Jude whispered back.

"It is extremely not fine—"

"TAM." This from Ash—and it was the first word she had spoken in common tongue above a whisper, cracked and rusty and furious, and it startled everyone including herself. She clapped a translucent hand over her mouth. Tam stared at her. Wren stared at her. Even the Magistrate's quill paused mid-stroke.

"That counts," Tam said thickly. "Whatever happens tonight. That counts as her first word and it was at me and I'm going to be insufferable about it forever."

"Wren," Jude said, because if they didn't start singing soon they were going to cry, and the counter-song required a dry throat. "Shall we?"

* * *

They sang.

Wren began, as she always began, with the note that had once cracked the throne-room glass—but softened now, bent downward, made gentle, a door opening instead of breaking. The sound moved through the cavern and the soul-lights leaned toward it in their black-glass cages, hundreds of small flames tilting like grass in wind.

Jude came in on the second phrase, the way they'd rehearsed, and immediately rushed.

*Stop.*

They stopped. The silence spread out around them, enormous, and in that silence Jude heard—for the last time in their life, though they did not fully know it yet—every voice they had ever carried. Not screaming. Not warning. Just *present*, the way a room is present around a lamp. The drowned farmer, whose memorial they had carved in the first week of their thirtieth year. The elder who had given them the deadline, already fading. The grandmother-bones in the valley, humming. And underneath all of it, so faint it might have been memory: a woman singing a lullaby, hesitating just before the final line, the way she had hesitated just before the signature.

*Mum,* Jude thought. *I'm paying your bill.*

They sang the surrender.

It is difficult to describe in words what it is to give away a sense. The counter-song did not take the hearing of the dead the way a thief takes a purse. It took it the way a river takes a boat—gently, inevitably, with the full consent of everything floating. Jude sang each voice loose, one by one, naming them silently as they went: the farmer, the elder, the grandmothers, the unnamed thousands the valley had muffled for thirty years. Each named voice rose out of them like heat off stone and dissolved upward into the dark of the false sky, and the thirteenth moon drank the light, and grew full.

On the throne, the contract caught fire.

It burned without smoke, green-gold, the braid of thirteen signatures unraveling thread by thread, and then Maren Thackeray's name burning last, longest, the hesitation in the M finally, finally finishing itself. The Magistrate watched it burn with an expression that on a human face would have been called grief and on his was merely arithmetic reaching its sum.

"The account," he announced, "is closed."

Ash solidified.

There was no other word for it. The translucency drained out of her like water out of a bath, color flooding in from the center outward—warm brown skin, scuffed knees, the ink stain on her left palm from Tam's lessons. She gasped, a full-bodied, astonished gasp, the gasp of someone surfacing, and then she looked at her own hands and turned them over and over, and started to laugh, and the laugh was ordinary, mortal, seven years old, and it rang through the Undercourt louder than any forbidden note Wren had ever sung.

The mountain began to shake.

* * *

Dust sifted from the ceiling of the false sky. The thirteen carved moons juddered in their orbits. Somewhere deep below, something vast turned over in its sleep, annoyed at being woken by bookkeeping.

"The mountain objects to balanced accounts," the Magistrate observed, rising from his throne of ledgers as the ledgers themselves began to slide. "It prefers debt. Debt is structural." He looked at Jude across the shaking floor, and inclined his head precisely one degree. "Heir of Thackeray. You have cost me a very good evening."

"Sorry," said Jude, who could not think what else to say.

"Don't be. It was a well-made payment. I respect craftsmanship." He was already turning away, gathering falling ledgers out of the air with little flicks of his fingers, saving his library first and his dignity second. Over his shoulder he added: "The singer may keep her voice. Consider it a bad investment I am choosing not to pursue. And the gravedigger's son—" a pause, the ghost of something almost warm, "—tell him the reserve list is cancelled. He was going to be tedious about it either way, but less so alive."

Tam made a noise like a kettle being thanked.

"RUN," said Wren.

They ran.

Behind them the cathedral of stalactites groaned, and the black-glass lanterns burst open one after another—not shattering, but *opening*, hinges that had waited centuries swinging wide—and the soul-lights poured upward through the cracking stone like reversed snow, thousands of small gold flames rising toward a surface none of them had seen in longer than kingdoms. One of them, passing, brushed against Jude's cheek, warm as a hand on a fevered forehead, and though Jude could no longer hear the dead, they felt it anyway, plain as sunlight:

*Thank you.*

Jude ran harder.

* * *

The last thing Jude saw of the Undercourt, looking back from the mouth of the ascending passage, was the Magistrate standing amid his sliding ledgers, restoring order to chaos with both hands, pausing only to watch the freed lights rise past him. He raised one finger and traced a single line of light in the air—a closing entry, a final notation—and whether it was farewell or simply good accounting, Jude chose, for the rest of their life, to believe it was farewell.

Then the passage turned, and the mountain roared, and there was nothing ahead but sleet, switchbacks, and three people and a child running for the moonlight—

—and behind them, from the depths, a sound Jude would spend years trying to describe: the whole Undercourt exhaling, at last, like a held breath released.

Ahead, Ash laughed again, breathless, terrified, alive, and tugged them onward into the dark.
