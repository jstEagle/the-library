## 20. Running Out of the Mountain

The mountain started screaming before we reached the first door.

Not a scream like a person screams—more like the whole of Serel clearing its throat after a thousand years of holding its breath. The floor shivered under my boots. Dust came down in ropes from the ceiling, and somewhere behind us, deep in the dark where the Magistrate's court had been, something enormous gave way with a sound like a cathedral kneeling.

"Run," Wren said, very calmly, which was how I knew she was terrified.

We ran.

I want it written down somewhere—I want it carved into something, since that's how this valley keeps its records—that I, Tam Broadfoot, gravedigger's son, carried a seven-year-old girl up a collapsing mountain at a dead sprint, and I did not once drop her, though I did once drop myself, twice, and Jude hauled me up by the collar both times without so much as breaking stride.

Ash rode on my back with her arms around my neck and her chin hooked over my shoulder, and she was laughing.

That's the part I keep coming back to. The whole Undercourt coming down around our ears, soul-light streaming past us in rivers, stone the size of farmhouses cracking loose overhead—and this child, this impossible child who'd been going translucent as candle-glass for thirteen turns, was laughing into my ear like we were sledding down Garrow's Hill in a good snow year.

"It tickles," she said, when a gust of freed souls went through us on its way up. And it did, a little. Like standing in a flock of starlings.

* * *

You have to understand what it looked like, going up through those galleries while everything behind us let go.

The Undercourt had been lit by imprisoned fireflies—soul-lights, Wren called them, caught and caged and hung in the stalactites like lanterns in an orchard. When the contract burned, every cage opened at once. They came off the ceiling in their thousands, tens of thousands, a slow bright snowfall running backward, rising instead of falling, pouring up the shafts and stairways ahead of us and lighting our way better than any torch ever could.

It should have been beautiful and it was, God help me, it was beautiful, but it was also terrible, because every one of those lights had been somebody once. You could feel them passing. Some brushed against me and left little impressions, half-thoughts: *the smell of bread, a dog's name, someone waiting.* A whole harvest of small human things, finally going home.

Jude ran beside me with their face turned up into it, eyes wet, mouth moving—not singing anymore, the counter-song was spent, but maybe just saying goodbye. They couldn't hear the dead anymore. That was the price they'd paid not an hour ago, willingly, cheerfully almost, the way you'd hand over a coat. But watching them now, I understood that losing the voices wasn't like handing over a coat. It was like handing over a limb and then having to run on the stump of it.

They didn't complain. Not once. Not even when the way forked and Wren shouted a direction and Jude flinched because for thirty years they'd have known which way to go by listening to the bones in the walls, and now there was nothing. Just rock. Just choice.

"This way!" Wren bellowed, taking the left fork. "The singers' stair—it's steep but it goes straight to the teeth!"

"The what?" I shouted back.

"Doors! Doors of teeth! Move your legs, Broadfoot!"

* * *

Here is a thing about me, and I'll own it plainly: I am not brave. Never have been. My da used to say I had a coward's gift for noticing danger early, which he meant as an insult but which has kept me alive in ditches and flooded gravesites more times than I can count. So when the mountain began to come apart, my whole body wanted one thing, and it wasn't to run toward the noise. It was to find a nice deep hole and be in it.

But there was Ash on my back, warm and solid and *opaque* again—fully, wonderfully opaque, her heels drumming my ribs—and there was Jude ahead of me choosing stairs blind, and there was Wren Alaire, disgraced court singer, professional coward by her own cheerful admission, leading us deeper into the one place on earth she'd sworn never to return to.

So I ran toward the noise. First time for everything.

The singers' stair was exactly as advertised: steep as a ladder laid against the sky, cut into the living rock, winding up through the dark with no rail and no mercy. Freed souls streamed up alongside us, patient as migrating birds, and their light showed the steps just well enough to kill yourself on.

Halfway up, the stair behind us went.

I heard it before I saw it—a groan, a crack like the world's largest knee, and then forty feet of staircase simply folded away into blackness. The wind of it hit our backs. Ash whooped.

"Don't look down," I told her.

"Why?"

"Because I'm looking down and one of us should be sensible."

She looked down, obviously. She was seven. "Tam," she said thoughtfully, "the bottom isn't there anymore."

"I know."

"You're still running up anyway."

"That's the trick of it, yes."

* * *

Wren stopped at the top of the stair, and I nearly bowled her over, and then I saw why she'd stopped, and I wished I hadn't.

The doors of teeth stood open.

Of course they did—the counter-song had unmade the contracts, and contracts were all those gates had ever been made of—but seeing them open was worse than seeing them shut. Shut, they'd been a monster's grin. Open, with the pale light of the surface spilling through the gap between those enormous jaws, they looked like something else entirely. They looked like a mouth saying yes.

Beyond them: sleet. Grey daylight. The smell of snow and pine and cold stone, the ordinary smell of the top of the world, and I have never in my life been so glad to smell anything.

"Go," Wren said. "Go, go—all of you, through, don't wait on me."

And that's when it happened. The thing I'll tell my grandchildren, if I ever get any, if Ash doesn't scare them all off first.

Wren didn't move.

* * *

I saw it happen, and I think only I did, because Jude was hauling me through the gates and Ash had gone ahead to stand in the snow with her arms out like she was greeting an old friend. Wren hung back on the threshold, one foot in the light, and her face—

Her face did something I'd never seen it do. All that road, all those miles, she'd been wit and armor and sharp edges, the woman who could talk her way out of a hanging and probably had. Now the edges fell off her all at once, and underneath was just a woman looking back into the dark she'd crawled out of, and wanting it.

Because I knew what she was hearing. Even I could hear it, faint and far below: the court's great hall was collapsing, but its acoustics were dying grandly, and her old throne of song—the Voice-seat, the place where they'd trained her to sing souls out of bodies—was still standing in the ruin, and the mountain's last echoes were pooling around it like water around a stone. To a singer, that sound must have been a whole religion. It must have sounded like being young again, and believed-in, and owned.

"Wren," I said.

"I'm coming." She didn't come.

The floor between her and the gates split with a crack like ice going out on the mere in spring. Dust sheeted down. Somewhere far below, the Magistrate's ledger-hall—the heart of the whole rotten place—finally let go, and the boom of it rolled up the shaft like thunder learning to climb stairs.

"WREN."

"I said I'm coming, Tam, stop shouting—" But her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she took one step backward, back toward the dark, back toward the sound, and I swear on my da's grave her eyes had gone glassy with it, homesick for a prison.

Then a small hand found hers.

Ash had come back inside. Nobody asked her to; nobody even noticed until she was already there, standing in the falling dust with her fingers laced through Wren's, tugging, gentle as anything, the way you'd lead a horse that's spooked.

"Come on," Ash said. In common speech. Clear as a bell. One of her first sentences in our tongue, and she spent it on this.

Wren looked down at her. Something went out of her shoulders—something heavy, some old weight she'd been carrying since the day she fled this mountain—and she laughed, a wet, broken, wonderful laugh.

"You're supposed to be afraid of me," Wren told her. "Everyone says I'm terrifying."

"You are," Ash agreed solemnly. "Come be terrifying in the snow."

And Wren Alaire, former Voice of the Undercourt, let a seven-year-old lead her out of hell by the hand.

* * *

The rest was mostly running, and I won't dress it up as anything nobler than that.

The passage beyond the doors was groaning like a ship in a storm. Chunks of ceiling came down behind us in time with our footsteps, as if the mountain were keeping count. The freed souls had thinned out by then—most of them already gone up, out through cracks and chimneys we'd never have fit through, streaming into the sky above Serel like reversed snow, like sparks off a bonfire blown straight upward. We passed the last few in the final gallery: a knot of dim lights hovering around a fallen block of stone, and as we splashed through the meltwater, they rose together and followed us, close as a flock of hens, all the way to the daylight.

Maybe they wanted to see the sun. Maybe they wanted to make sure we made it. I choose to believe the second, because it's kinder, and because after everything, I've decided to spend my belief on kind things.

The last hundred yards, the mountain gave up pretending to be a mountain and became an avalanche with opinions. Wren shoved us ahead of her—shoved, actually shoved, Jude first, then me with Ash still on my back—and the three of us went sprawling out through the gates' narrowing gap onto the scree slope beyond as the jaws ground shut behind us with a sound I will hear in my dreams until I die.

Dust boiled out of the gap like breath. Then silence. Then, high above, a long rumble as the peak settled itself, shrugged, and went back to being a mountain.

For a long moment none of us moved. We lay on the frozen scree in the grey afternoon light, four people and a flock of departing souls, breathing like bellows.

Then Ash slid off my back, stood up, stuck both arms straight out from her sides, and spun in a circle in the falling sleet.

"I'm solid," she announced, to the sky, to the mountain, to us. "I'm SOLID."

"You're going to fall down," I said.

"I'm solid AND I'm dizzy," she corrected, and fell down, and laughed, and lay there in the snow making a snow-angel with tremendous commitment, and I looked at Jude and Jude looked at me, and neither of us said anything, because there wasn't anything that needed saying.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a boulder halfway down the mountain's shoulder, because none of us had the legs to go farther and because Wren, examining the sky, pronounced weather coming that would "kill anyone foolish enough to descend in the dark, and I include myself in that category, which pains me."

Jude built a fire with hands that shook—not from fear, I think, but from the sheer animal relief of being alive and cold and tired in the ordinary way. Wren produced, from somewhere in her ragged cloak, a lump of hard cheese and half a loaf of bread that had survived the entire Undercourt, and divided it with the gravity of a queen distributing honors.

"Where did you even get that?" I asked.

"A singer never reveals her provisions."

"You stole it from the market in Vell."

"I liberated it from the market in Vell. There's a difference and it's spelled *legally*."

Ash ate her share sitting as close to the fire as safety allowed, and then, having finished, held her hands out to the flames and watched them with an expression I couldn't read. Watching her watch fire, I remembered what she was—or what she had been. Thirteen souls woven into one small body, and every one of them free now, gone up through the mountain's bones into the sky. What remained, what sat warming her hands at our fire, was whatever was left when the debt was paid. Herself, I supposed. Whatever a self is, once you take away everything that was owed.

"Tam," she said.

"Mm?"

"When the lights went up. Did you see? Some of them waved."

I hadn't seen. But I nodded. "Course they did. They liked you."

"They were *me*," she said, matter-of-fact, the way she'd say *it's Tuesday*, and then she yawned enormously, lay down against my side like I was furniture she'd personally purchased, and was asleep before I could think of anything to say to that.

Which was probably just as well.

* * *

Jude sat up longest, as I knew they would. I woke once in the small hours and found them awake, wrapped in the threadbare blanket, facing down the mountain toward the dark bulk of Serel. Toward the valley beyond it, far off, invisible: Hollowmere, the place that refused the world, the place they'd sworn never to leave and had left and would now return to, changed.

"Can't sleep either?" I whispered.

"I can't hear them," Jude said quietly. Not bitter. Just stating it, the way you'd state the weather. "The dead. Out here, past the stones, they should be everywhere—louder than ever, Wren said. And there's nothing. Just wind." A pause. "I keep reaching for them. Like a tongue finding a missing tooth."

"That sounds awful."

"It is," Jude said, and then, surprising me, they smiled—a real one, tired and crooked. "And it isn't. Do you know what I heard instead, all day? Ash laughing. Your terrible singing. Wren arguing with a goat we passed." They pulled the blanket tighter. "Thirty years I listened to the dead, Tam. Turns out the living make a better noise."

I didn't say anything to that either. Some things you just let sit, like bread proving.

Above us, through a tear in the clouds, the moon was waning at last—past her thirteenth turn, on the long slide down to dark and beginning again. And rising all around her, scattered across the whole bowl of the night sky, were lights that hadn't been there yesterday. Faint, drifting, unhurried. Souls, thousands of them, released from under the mountain, making their slow way wherever souls go when they're finally, finally free.

One of them, I'd swear on anything you like, paused directly over our camp. Hung there a moment. Then drifted on, upward, homeward.

Ash slept on, solid as a stone, warm as bread.

And far below us, at the foot of the mountain, the road wound south toward the boundary stones of Hollowmere—toward home, or whatever we'd decide home was now that we'd gone and chosen each other instead of safety. Warden Ferris was going to have opinions about that. Warden Ferris always had opinions.

But that, I figured, was tomorrow's problem.

Tonight, the mountain was done with us, the sky was full of the freed, and for the first time in my plain, ditch-loving, coward's life, Tam Broadfoot fell asleep in the open air smiling.
