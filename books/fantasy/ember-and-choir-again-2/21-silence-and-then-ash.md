## 21. Silence, and Then Ash

The first thing Jude did upon crossing the boundary stones was stop.

They stood in the tall grass just inside Hollowmere, Ash's small hand in theirs, Tam wheezing behind them with a pack that had doubled in weight somewhere on the road home, and they waited. They waited the way one waits for a wound to announce itself—for the grief to arrive, the great hollowing they had been promised by their own heart ever since the counter-song left their throat in the dark beneath Mount Serel.

Nothing came.

The valley hummed its old hum around them. The boundary stones murmured at their backs like drowsy sheep. Somewhere downhill, the mill turned, and the miller's dog barked at nothing, and all of it arrived at Jude's ears as simply sound—sound without commentary, without the dead threading their whispers through it. The wind moved the grass and said only *wind*. The stones hummed and meant only *stone*.

It was the quietest moment of Jude Thackeray's life.

And it was not empty.

That was the thing no one had warned them about—not Wren, who had sung the counter-song into their mouth note by patient note; not the dying elder who had named the price; not even Jude themselves, who had rehearsed this loss every night for thirteen turns until they could recite it like a prayer. Everyone had described the silence as an absence. A severing. A door closed forever on thirty years of murmuring ivory and bone-borne counsel.

No one had thought to mention that silence could be a room.

Jude stood in that room now, and found it larger than expected.

"Jude." Ash tugged their hand. She was squinting up at them with the fierce, imperious concern of a seven-year-old who has decided someone requires supervision. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you go somewhere else." She tapped her own temple. "In there."

"I'm not going anywhere," Jude said. "I'm right here."

"Good." She nodded once, satisfied, and then—because she was Ash, and because stillness was a garment she wore badly—she spun away toward the path, arms out, walking the low stone wall like a tightrope. "Tam! Tam, is Hollowmere your valley or Jude's valley?"

"Jude's," Tam called back, huffing up the hill. "I just dig the holes."

"He digs the *best* holes," Ash informed Jude gravely. "He told me. He dug one for his father and one for his mother and one for a horse named Buttercup, and Buttercup was the saddest one but he doesn't want to talk about it."

"Ash," Tam said, ears going red.

"You don't have to talk about it," Ash said kindly. "I'll remember for you."

Jude felt something move through them then—not grief, not yet, maybe never—but something adjacent to it, something that lived in the same neighborhood: the recognition that for thirty years they had filled their silences with the dead, and the dead had never once offered to remember anything *for* them.

* * *

The valley, of course, had questions.

Word traveled ahead of them the way word always did in Hollowmere, which was to say slowly, by foot, and with embellishment. By the time the three of them reached the village green, half of Hollowmere had arranged itself into a loose, pretending-not-to-stare crescent near the well. Old Marta stood at the front with her arms folded. Behind her, children peeked from between their mothers' skirts at the strange child who had appeared out of nowhere and vanished again, and come back walking, talking, and—this was the detail that would circulate for weeks—*solid*.

Warden Odile Ferris stood apart from the crescent, as she always did, a grey straight line against the softer shapes of the villagers. Her hands were clasped behind her back. Her face was the face of a woman who had prepared seventeen objections and was determined to use every one of them.

"Warden," Jude said.

"Thackeray." Her eyes went over them—the road-worn clothes, the healed scrapes, the absence of whatever it was that had left Jude's shoulders when they sang in the dark—and then to Ash, and then, last and longest, to Wren Alaire, standing slightly behind the group with the wary posture of a cat returned to a house it had once been thrown out of. "You left the valley."

"Yes."

"The valley you were born sworn to keep. You crossed the boundary stones in the night, in defiance of every covenant this community rests upon, taking with you a neighbor's son and returning with—" her gaze flicked to Wren again, "—a court singer. A *disgraced* court singer."

"That's me," Wren said cheerfully. "Disgraced twice now, if the mountain counts."

"It counts," Ferris said, without looking at her. "Everything counts. That is rather the entire nature of debts." She drew a breath, and Jude braced for the ledger of charges, the summons, the long cold arithmetic of consequence.

What came out instead was quieter. "The dead have been loud since you left."

Jude blinked. "Loud?"

"Restless. The bones in the ossuary chattered for thirteen nights straight. Old Hollis's memorial would not hold its carving—three times the letters faded before dawn. The valley felt—" For a moment, the warden's composure thinned, and underneath it Jude saw something almost human, almost tired: a woman who had spent her whole life holding a door shut and had felt the door rattle. "It felt like a held breath. Like the valley itself knew something of it had walked out and might not come back."

"And now?"

Ferris looked at Ash. It was not a soft look, exactly. But it was not the look of a warden assessing an anomaly either. It was the look of a woman looking at a child who had been sold before she could walk, and who had climbed out of a mountain anyway.

"Now the bones are quiet," she said. "Quieter than I have known them in forty years of keeping. Whatever debt hung over this valley—" her jaw worked, "—it seems it has been paid. By a child. And by you."

"Not paid," Jude said. "Unmade. There's a difference. Wren can tell you—it's a song."

"I don't want to know the song." Ferris said it quickly, and Jude understood: some doors, once opened, could not be re-shut, and Odile Ferris had made a life's discipline of knowing which doors to leave alone. She straightened. "Very well. Here is what will happen. The child stays. The singer stays—under observation, and if she sings so much as a scale that rattles my windows, she will learn what our stocks are for. Broadfoot goes back to digging holes and stops telling children about them." Tam made a small wounded noise. "And you, Thackeray—you will resume your work. The valley has dead who deserve remembering, and you are the only carver we have."

She turned to go, then paused. When she spoke again, her back still to them, her voice had dropped into something that was almost, in its own armored way, gentle.

"My mother knew yours. Did you know that? They kept the covenant together, in the early years." A pause. "Your mother would have done what you did. I think that is why she bought the silence in the first place. Not for the valley." She resumed walking. "For whoever came after her."

Then she was gone, a grey line retreating up the lane, and the crescent by the well exhaled as one body, and Old Marta said, "Well. I suppose we're feeding them, then," as though the party had already been planned, which, knowing Marta, it had.

* * *

The house took some getting used to.

Jude had left it expecting never to return; they had not bothered to bank the fire properly, and there was a fine grey civilization of dust across everything, and a mouse had opinions about the pantry. But it was theirs, and when Ash walked through the door for the first time, she stopped in the middle of the single room and turned around very slowly, taking inventory with the seriousness of a general surveying territory.

"This is small," she announced.

"It's a good size for one person."

"There are four of us now."

"There are not four of us now. Wren has the loft over the smithy. Tam has his own cottage."

Ash considered this. "But you eat together."

"We... eat together sometimes, yes."

"Then it's four of us," she said, with the iron logic of the very young, "and the house is too small, and we should build another room." She pointed at the wall. "There."

"That's where the woodpile goes."

"Move the woodpile."

"We are not moving the woodpile."

"We are moving the woodpile," Ash said serenely, and went to inspect the hearth, and Jude stood in their too-small house in the valley of Hollowmere and realized, with a sensation like sunlight arriving late, that this was what it was going to be like. Forever. This was the silence now: not the dead murmuring through ivory, but a child narrating a woodpile into a future room.

They found they did not miss the whispers even a little.

* * *

Grief, when it finally came, came sideways.

It arrived on the ninth evening home, while Jude sat at their workbench finishing a cradle for the miller's daughter—a cradle, carved for the living, which still caught strangely in the chest each time the chisel bit. Ash was supposed to be asleep. Instead she lay curled on her pallet watching the lamplight, and when Jude glanced over, she was looking at them with that unnerving directness she had never unlearned from the mountain.

"Does it hurt?" she asked. "The quiet."

Jude set down the chisel. Outside, the night pressed gently at the window—wind, crickets, the far-off mill, and nothing else, nothing else, nothing else.

"Sometimes I reach for it," Jude admitted. "Out of habit. Like reaching for a step that isn't there. Thirty years is a long time to hear a voice, even if the voices weren't mine."

"Do you hear me?" Ash asked. "When I'm quiet. In the other room. Do you know I'm there?"

"Always."

"Then it's not empty," Ash said, as though settling a matter of considerable public importance, and rolled over, and was asleep within the minute, because she was seven, and the great metaphysical crises of adults were, to her, things to be resolved before bedtime.

Jude sat a while longer. Then they picked up the chisel and finished the cradle, and if their eyes stung a little, the lamplight was dim, and no one saw, and the silence kept their secret the way it now kept everything: softly, and with room inside it.

* * *

Spring came early that year, or perhaps it only seemed to, because everyone was watching for it.

Wren lasted eleven days under "observation" before the village discovered that a disgraced court singer could teach even the tone-deaf sons of farmers to hold a round, and after that the warden's restrictions became, in practice, a formality observed mainly by the warden herself, who was seen on more than one occasion lingering at the edge of the square during lessons with an expression that suggested she was cataloguing crimes but might, under extreme duress, be persuaded to tap her foot.

Tam planted his garden on the boundary line, half in and half out of the valley, because—as he explained to anyone who asked—"the soil doesn't care about covenants, and neither do turnips." He dug Ash a hole of her own, a proper one, two feet deep, on the strict understanding that it was for planting things and not for anything else, and Ash visited it daily with the gravity of a high priestess, reporting on the progress of a single bean sprout as though it were a matter of state.

And Ash learned words.

She learned them the way rain fills a dry streambed—all at once and everywhere. She hoarded them, tested them, broke them open to see what was inside. She learned that *yesterday* was a word and was outraged by it, demanding to know where yesterday went and whether it could be gotten back. She learned that *enough* existed and deployed it strategically at meals. She asked Jude what *orphan* meant, and Jude told her the truth—that it meant a child whose parents were gone—and Ash thought about this for a long moment and said, "But I have you," in the tone of someone correcting a minor error in a ledger, and Jude had to go stand outside for a while and look at the hills.

Some evenings she told stories. They were enormous, rambling, magnificent things, stitched from the mountain and the road and the market of Vell and the bean sprout, and they always featured the same cast: a brave gravedigger (Tam), a singer with a voice like weather (Wren), a warden who was secretly kind but must never be told so (Ferris), and a carver who could make anything out of bone except a promise (Jude, whom she regarded with a mixture of love and pity). She told these stories aloud, at full volume, filling the house and the yard and the lane, and the neighbors complained, and the neighbors' children wouldn't miss a single installment.

One evening in late spring, she came running up from the garden with dirt on her knees and something cupped in her hands, and thrust it at Jude.

"It bloomed," she said. "The bean. It has flowers. Little ones. White ones."

"So it does."

"Tam says beans do that. But I think—" she lowered her voice, confiding the great secret, "—I think it did it for me."

"I think you're probably right."

She beamed, and ran off to tell Tam, who would pretend to be embarrassed and then tell everyone at the tavern, and Jude stayed by the gate a moment longer, in the gold light, in the humming valley, in the wide and furnished silence.

Thirty years, they had believed the whispers were the only voice the world would ever offer them. They had built a life the size of a whisper and called it safety, and called the safety duty, and called the loneliness peace.

Now the valley said only *valley*, and the wind said only *wind*, and a child's laughter came up the garden path saying *look, look, look*—

and it was enough. It was more than enough. It was, Jude thought, turning back toward the house where supper waited and the woodpile stood condemned to make way for a new room, the loudest silence they had ever heard.

Inside, Ash was already asking what came next.
