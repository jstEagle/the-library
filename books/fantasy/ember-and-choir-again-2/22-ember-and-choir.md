## 22. Ember and Choir

### Jude

The cradle took three days.

Jude had carved memorials for thirty years — bones for the dead, names for the drowned and the fallen and the merely old — and their hands knew grief's geometry the way a riverbed knows water. But a cradle was different. A cradle was hope with edges. You had to sand it smooth enough that no splinter would ever find soft skin, and rockers curved just so, and somewhere in the middle of the second day Jude realized they were smiling at a piece of alder and could not stop.

"Who's it for?" Ash asked, chin hooked over the workbench edge, her dark eyes tracking the knife.

"The Broadfoots' new grandchild. Due at harvest."

"You're making a bed for someone who isn't born yet."

"I am." Jude blew shavings off the curve of the headboard. "That's rather the point of most things worth making."

Ash considered this with the gravity she brought to everything now, though her silences had changed shape entirely over the year. Once her quiet had been a locked door in a language Jude couldn't read. Now it was just Ash thinking, which was its own kind of music.

"They'll grow out of it," she said.

"Everything grows out of everything. That's not a tragedy, little ember. That's the whole trick."

She climbed onto the stool beside them and put her hand flat on the alder, feeling the grain the way Wren had taught her to feel a melody — palm first, then fingers, then heart. "Will you carve something in it? Underneath? Where nobody sees?"

Jude looked at her. A year ago she had arrived barefoot at their door speaking syllables older than kingdoms, and the dead had gone silent around her, and Jude had thought the silence would kill them. Now the silence was just the room they lived in, and Ash filled it constantly: questions, arguments, songs half-learned and entirely off-key.

"What should it say?" Jude asked.

Ash thought. Then she said, in her careful, deliberate way, the words she'd learned from Tam first because Tam never rushed her: *"You are wanted before you arrive."*

Jude's throat did something inconvenient. They turned back to the wood so she wouldn't see, though of course she saw; she always saw; children who had been made from thirteen stolen souls developed an unerring eye for the truth in people.

"That," Jude said, when they could speak evenly, "is exactly what goes underneath."

### Wren

Wren Alaire had performed for dukes. She had sung in the Court Beneath the Mountain, before the Magistrate of Debts himself, in a cathedral of stalactites lit by imprisoned fireflies — and she had been magnificent there, and it had cost her everything, and she had sworn on the cracked glass of the throne room that she would never again sing for anyone who owned her.

Now she taught music to eleven children in the Hollowmere village square, on a bench, for bread and eggs and the occasional jar of honey, and it was the finest engagement of her career.

"Again," she said, "and this time *breathe*, Milo, you're not being chased."

Milo Ferris — the Warden's nephew, gods help them all — puffed out his cheeks and tried the scale again. He got four notes up before wandering off into territory no scale had ever visited. The other children giggled. Wren held up a hand and they quieted, because they had learned that when Wren raised her hand it meant something was about to be interesting.

"Do you know what you just did?" she asked Milo.

"Sang it wrong."

"You sang something *new*. Wrong is just new that hasn't been practiced yet. Every song anyone has ever loved was wrong once." She crouched to his height. "I once sang a note so wrong it broke a window in the most powerful court in the world, and I have never regretted it for one single day."

"You broke a window?" breathed a small girl named Petal.

"A very large window. In a very bad place. And the sound it made was the first honest thing I'd ever done." Wren straightened, brushed off her skirts. "Which is why we practice scales. So that when your wrong note comes — and it will come, each of you, the one only you can sing — you'll have the strength to hold it."

Tam Broadfoot, passing with a basket of seed potatoes, called from the lane: "Are you filling their heads with philosophy or teaching them actual notes?"

"Both. Philosophy is free. Notes cost honey."

"I'll bring honey Thursday." Tam went on toward the boundary line, where his garden was, and Wren watched him go with the particular fondness she reserved for people who had once tried to sacrifice themselves to an underworld court and lived to plant turnips anyway.

Her hands ached some mornings still. Old habit, reaching for the Voice the Undercourt had trained into her — the voice that could sing souls loose from bodies, that had been sharpened into a tool and pointed at people. Some mornings she woke and didn't know whose throat she had.

But then the children arrived, off-key and eager, wanting nothing from her but songs, and her hands remembered what they were for.

### Tam

The garden grew right up against the third boundary stone, close enough that Tam could lean his trowel against it while he worked.

People said he was mad. The stones hummed, faint and constant, the sound of the valley refusing the world — magic slid off them like rain off slate — and everyone knew nothing planted near them would thrive. Except Tam had spent a lifetime digging graves beside his father and had learned the one lesson graves teach: things grow best where somebody bothers to stay.

So he'd cleared the ground along the boundary line in early spring, hauled manure nobody would thank him for, and planted. Beans along the stones themselves. Squash sprawling toward the meadow. A row of sunflowers standing sentry like tall awkward guards.

And the beans came up. The squash flowered. The sunflowers turned their faces east every morning like a congregation, and if they grew a little strangely — leaning toward the stones as if listening — well, Tam leaned toward them too sometimes, and neither of them heard anything, and that was fine.

"You talk to them," Ash said, appearing at his elbow the way she did, silent as her own name. She crouched by the bean rows and inspected a pod with the seriousness of a magistrate.

"I talk to everything. It's a gravedigger's son's habit. Dead folks don't answer much, so you get in the practice talking anyway."

"What do you say?"

"Oh — weather, mostly. Complaints about the weeds. Telling them they're doing better than last week." He shrugged. "Same as I'd say to you."

Ash smiled. Her smiles had come slowly, that first season, like frost melting off a window — but they came all the way through now, and when she smiled the whole garden seemed to lean in. "When I couldn't talk," she said, "you didn't make me. You just sat."

"You were busy. I could tell. Busy people don't need another person yammering."

"I wasn't busy. I was *locked*. There's a difference." She picked a bean pod, opened it with her thumbnail, counted the seeds inside — five — and showed him solemnly, the way she showed him everything, because Tam was the one who understood that counting things was how she kept the world from dissolving. Thirteen souls had been woven into her once. She counted everything now. Seeds, stairs, stars, the beats between Wren's drumbeats.

"Five," Tam agreed. "Good pod. That one goes to seed, then. We'll have ten pods next year, and twenty after, and eventually the whole valley will be up to its knees in beans and it'll be entirely your fault."

"My fault," she repeated, delighted, filing the word away. Faults, debts, blame — she collected the vocabulary of consequence like other children collected river stones, turning each one over, learning what it weighed. But she held this one lightly. *My fault,* she said again, tasting it, and laughed.

Tam decided right then that the sunflowers could hear it too.

### Odile

Warden Odile Ferris had spent forty years keeping the valley safe, and she had believed, until quite recently, that safety was a wall: high, humming, unbroken, maintained by vigilance and rules and the occasional terrifying glare at travelers.

Then a soul-child had walked through her wall as if it were morning mist, an ex-court singer had moved in above the bakery, and her own nephew was taking music lessons.

She stood at the edge of the square on market day, arms folded, watching Wren Alaire conduct eleven children through something that was either a round or a riot, and waited for the old anger. It didn't come. That was the trouble. She kept reaching for it and finding the shelf empty.

"Milo's flat," she said, when Wren paused for breath.

"He's *learning*."

"He's my sister's boy. He's flat."

"And you're sharp, Warden, but here we are, both of us, making a choir." Wren wiped her brow. "Stay. Stand in the back. Nobody will see."

"I am the Warden of Hollowmere. Everyone sees."

"Yes," Wren said, "that's rather what I've noticed about you," and there was such gentle mockery in it, such affectionate insolence, that Odile found herself — to her lasting horror — sitting down on the bench at the back of the square.

The children sang. Badly, mostly, and then suddenly, briefly, wonderfully, not badly at all — a bar or two where the parts caught each other and the harmony stood up and walked. Odile felt something in her chest shift like furniture being moved in a room she'd long since stopped redecorating.

Afterward, Milo ran to her. "Did you hear? Did you hear my part?"

"I heard it," Odile said gravely. "It was flat."

"But did you *hear* it?"

Odile looked down at her nephew's shining face, and at the boundary stones beyond, humming their ancient refusal, and at the garden growing shamelessly along the line where nothing should grow, and at the cradle-maker's workshop with its door propped open and its shavings drifting gold in the afternoon light.

"I heard it," she said. "We'll work on it."

### Ash

Ash kept her treasures in a box under her bed: five river stones, a broken harp string Wren had given her, a pressed flower from the mountain pass, a scrap of the burned contract that Tam had pocketed from the Magistrate's floor without asking anyone (they had all agreed this was very Tam). And at the bottom, wrapped in cloth, a flute of pale alder wood.

Jude had made it in midwinter, without being asked, and left it on her pillow the night of the thirteenth-turn anniversary — the night she'd been sold, the night she'd been saved, the night the mountain shook and let them go. No carving on it, no decoration. Just six holes and a mouthpiece and a note scratched underneath where nobody sees:

*You are wanted before you arrive.*

She played it badly. Wren said she played it honestly, which Ash had learned was the same thing said kindly. She practiced on the hillside above the village where nobody could object, and she played the lullaby — the one that had hummed inside her before she had words, the fragment of a soul woven into hers, the melody Jude's mother used to sing. Jude had gone white as bone-shaving the first time Ash hummed it. Then Jude had sat down on the floor, and Ash had sat down too, and they had stayed there a long while, and afterward Jude taught her the rest of the verses from memory, note by note, voice cracking and continuing anyway.

The dead had sung that lullaby once, Jude said. Through the bones. Before the valley went quiet for good.

Ash couldn't hear the dead. Nobody in Hollowmere could anymore, not even Jude, not anywhere. But Ash had been made of thirteen voices, and she liked to think that somewhere in her weaving, in whatever deep grain of her ran under everything like woodgrain, those voices were still there — not whispering warnings, not screaming contracts, just singing. Just keeping the tune warm until she was ready for it.

She played the lullaby on the hillside. Below, the valley went about its evening: lamplight kindling, smoke rising straight in the still air, Wren's last students trailing home, Tam covering his seedlings against the frost, Jude's workshop window glowing.

Ash finished the song, lowered the flute, and listened to the ordinary, miraculous silence.

It was full.

### Jude

On the evening of the anniversary, Jude walked the boundary line alone, as they did every year now — not as penance, they'd decided, but as thanks.

The stones hummed. They always had. Thirty years ago Jude had heard them as a wall, a refusal, the valley saying *no* to everything beyond — and behind that refusal, the whispers of the dead, threading through every carved bone in Hollowmere, murmuring names and grievances and the occasional unsolicited opinion about Jude's technique. Their gift. Their burden. Their inheritance, bought with a child's future by a mother who had traded her firstborn's hearing to purchase silence for a valley.

Jude had given it back. All of it. Standing in the Court Beneath, singing Wren's counter-song with a voice that shook and did not stop, surrendering the dead's voices forever so that a girl woven from thirteen stolen souls could keep her own.

They had expected grief. They had braced for it the way you brace for winter. And the first weeks had been strange — reaching for whispers that weren't there, turning toward voices that no longer came, the habit of thirty years running its hand over emptiness.

But emptiness, it turned out, was a matter of what you carried into it.

Jude stopped at the third stone, where Tam's sunflowers stood taller than a man, and rested a palm on the warm granite. Nothing answered. Nothing ever would again. And the silence where the dead had been was not hollow — it was simply *room*. Room that Ash filled with questions. Room that Wren filled with scales and philosophy. Room that the village filled, gradually, season by season, with the sounds of the living: hammering, planting, arguing about fences, children being flat on purpose.

Jude's mother had bought silence with her firstborn's future. Jude had paid silence away to buy a child's present. Somewhere in the arithmetic of it, duty had quietly become choice, and the ledger had balanced itself in a hand Jude finally recognized as their own.

From the village below came laughter — bright, unmistakable, Ash's laugh, the one that started low and broke upward like a bird taking off. Jude turned. She was running up the path with the flute in one fist and her other hand outstretched toward them, hair wild, face lit from inside.

"Jude! Jude, Wren says if I learn the third verse by autumn she'll teach me the *window-breaking note* — Tam says absolutely not — Wren says that's why it's *interesting* —"

Jude listened. Not to the dead. To this.

Thirty years of whispers, and not one of them had ever sounded like this.

"Enough," Jude said softly, to no one, to the stone, to the whole wide quiet world — and took their daughter's hand, and walked home.
