## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The salt came down from the grey coast in sacks stitched with lies—*flour*, said the stencils; *grain for the garrisons*—and Vesper Fairweather carried it up from the tidelands on a mule named Obstinate, through the black pines of Thornfell, toward the free city that had once been hers and was no longer anything of hers at all.

She had learned to love the road in exile. That was the trick of it, the small mercy nobody warned you about: after enough years, the thing they took from you becomes the thing you build instead. She had a route now, three fences who trusted her, a mule with opinions, and a name she wore like a borrowed coat. Nobody on the salt road called her storm-caller. Nobody looked at her hands when thunder rolled far off over the water and flinched. She was just Vess, the smuggler, reliable, unremarkable, safe.

The night it all ended began like any other. Cold, but that was Thornfell in autumn; the cold came early and stayed late, and she had dressed for it. Wool to the wrists, oilskin over that, her hood up against a wind that smelled of pine resin and coming frost. The mule's breath steamed. Her own did too. Somewhere behind her, miles back along the switchbacks, the sea muttered against the cliffs like an old man complaining in his sleep.

She noticed the silence first.

Thornfell's border wards had hummed her whole life—a sound below hearing, more a pressure than a noise, the way you feel a storm before the clouds arrive. Every child of the free cities grew up inside that hum the way fish grow up inside water. It kept out the things that crawled down from the northern wastes; it kept out worse things besides. On the worst nights of her childhood, when the storms she'd called had frightened even her, Vesper had pressed her palms flat against the ground and felt the wards answering beneath her, steady as a second heartbeat.

Now the road was just a road. The air was just air. And the quiet where the hum should have been rang louder than any bell.

Obstinate felt it too. The mule stopped dead in the middle of the track, ears swiveling forward, nostrils wide, and would not be persuaded by kick or curse or the dried apple Vesper fished from her coat. Mules were cowards, but their cowardice had never once lied to her.

"All right," Vesper murmured. "All right. I hear it."

What she heard was nothing, and then what she saw was worse.

The stars went out.

Not dimmed—not veiled by cloud, because there was no cloud, the sky above the black pines was hard and clear as kiln glass—but *out*, one by one, in a slow wave rolling south across the heavens like a shutter being drawn. She stood in the middle of the road with her mouth open and watched constellations she had navigated by for eleven years simply cease. The Sisters. The Salt Crown. The great red eye of the Wanderer, which sailors swore had burned since before the first wall was raised. Gone. The dark that came down to replace them was not ordinary night. Ordinary night had depth to it, texture, the suggestion of more stars beyond the ones you could see. This dark was flat. Final. A lid.

Cold poured down out of that lid like water through a torn roof.

Vesper had called storms since she was six years old. She knew every mood weather owned—the wet green breath of spring squalls, the dry crackle of heat lightning, the deep animal patience of a winter front waiting over the mountains. This was none of those. This was a cold with intent behind it, a cold that fell rather than gathered, and when it touched her face it felt less like temperature than like attention. Something vast had turned its gaze through the hole where the wards used to be, and it was looking at Thornfell, and it found the land wanting.

Her hands shook. Not from fear—or not only. The old call rose in her blood, answering the wrongness the way a struck string answers its twin: *there is a storm owed here, and I am the one who owes it.* Eleven years she had starved that part of herself, fed it nothing, let it go thin and quiet as a caged bird grown patient. Now it beat against her ribs and demanded the sky.

"Not here," she told it, through her teeth. "Not yet."

She got the mule moving again—off the open road, off the ridgeline, down into a fold of the hills where a smuggler's bothy sat tucked under a shelf of granite, invisible unless you already knew it was there. She knew. She'd sheltered there twice before, and left coin under the hearthstone both times, which was why the door wasn't locked against her.

Inside, she lit no lamp. She built a small fire instead, low and screened, and put the kettle on, and tried very hard not to look at the sky through the smoke-hole, and failed, and looked anyway. The stars stayed dead. The cold kept falling. Somewhere north, faint as memory, she thought she heard something howl that was not wolf and not wind, and she decided, firmly, that she hadn't.

That was when the knocking started.

Three blows, spaced wrong—too long between the second and third, the rhythm of someone fighting to stay upright. Then a body sliding down the outside of the door, and the wet heavy sound of a person landing in snow.

Vesper stood with her hand on the poker for a ten-count, listening. One set of lungs out there, ragged. No second set. No voices. In Thornfell these days, that passed for reassurance.

She opened the door on a courier half-buried in the drift, a young woman in the mud-brown leathers of the free cities' relay service, one arm cradled against her chest at an angle arms don't go, blood frozen black down her side from a wound she'd bound with a scarf and sheer refusal. Snow had gotten into her collar and melted and frozen again. Her lips were the color of slate. But her eyes, when they found Vesper's, were awake and desperate and burning.

"Fairweather," the courier rasped. "I need—I was told—Fairweather."

The word landed in Vesper's chest like a dropped stone. Eleven years since anyone had spoken her family name to her face. She'd buried it so thoroughly she sometimes forgot it was hers.

"You've got the wrong smuggler," she said, which was true, and a lie, and both of them knew it.

"Salt road," the woman managed. "Northbound. They said if the stars—" A cough tore through her, wet and awful. "If the stars went out, find the salt road, find Fairweather. Please. Before I stop."

Vesper looked at the dying stars. She looked at the girl bleeding into her snowdrift. She thought about the bothy's other occupants, real and imagined: the fence who trusted her, the mule, the careful small life built out of eleven years of not mattering.

Then she hauled the courier inside by the collar, kicked the door shut against the falling cold, and got to work.

* * *

The wound was a spear-point thrust, angled up under the ribs, missed the lung by what Vesper judged to be the width of a prayer. She cleaned it with boiled water and the last of her spirit, packed it with the moss she carried for exactly this kind of work—smuggling paid well, but it collected its fees—and splinted the arm. The courier endured all of it without screaming, which told Vesper two things: the girl was brave, and the girl had been trained by someone crueler than Vesper.

By the time the fire had done what fire could do, color had crept back into the courier's face, the bad kind of color, fever's herald. Vesper propped her against the wall, got half a cup of willow-bark tea into her, and held out her hand.

"The writ."

The courier's good hand went to her chest—to the oilskin wallet strapped there, worn smooth by years of riding, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. She didn't give it over. Instead she looked at Vesper with those burning eyes and said, "You're her. You're the sister."

The kettle had gone cold on the hearth. The fire popped. Outside, the flat dark pressed against the shutters like a palm.

"I don't have a sister," Vesper said.

"Everyone says that." The courier's voice was slurring now, fever pulling her under even as she fought it. "Every Fairweather says that. Like the name's a debt you can disown." Her fingers worked at the wallet's buckle, clumsy. "Chancellor's office. Sealed writ. Priority of blood—it has to go to a Fairweather, no one else can break the seal, no one else can even read it, the wax knows." She laughed, a thin cracked sound with no humor in it anywhere. "The wax knows, do you understand? It'll open for your hand or it won't open at all. I rode four days with this thing against my heart. Four days. They came over the pass behind me and I don't think they were chasing me. I think they were just... following the same dark."

"Who came?"

But the courier was going, sinking, her head lolling, her grip on the wallet loosening by degrees. Vesper caught her, eased her down onto the bedroll, and the girl's eyes fluttered open one last time.

"Wren," she whispered. "It's about Wren Fairweather. Solstice. You have until solstice."

Then she was asleep, or past sleep, breathing in shallow fevered pulls while the tea cooled beside her and the wind found its voice again outside—except it wasn't wind, Veper thought, it was that same not-wolf, not-wind howl from earlier, closer now, circling the fold of hills like something reading a map.

She knelt there a long moment with the wallet in her lap, not opening it. The seal gleamed dully in the firelight, blood-colored wax stamped with the sigil of the free cities' chancellery—and beneath the sigil, smaller, pressed deep, a mark she knew better than her own heartbeat. A wren in flight. The stamp her family had used for three generations to seal letters, contracts, bargains.

Bargains most of all.

Her mother had taught her that seal. Her mother had made her practice it on scrap wax until her lines were clean. *A Fairweather seal is a promise,* her mother used to say, warming the spoon over the candle in the kitchen that no longer existed, in the house that no longer existed. *And a promise, little storm, is just a debt that hasn't come due.*

Vesper pressed her thumb to the wax.

It warmed under her skin—actually warmed, a pulse of heat like recognition, like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep—and split cleanly along the wren's flight-path, folding open without tearing, and the writ inside was written in a hand she also knew, a hand that had written her name in a hundred ledgers before the exile, and the first line read:

*To the surviving heir of House Fairweather, regarding the soul of Wren Fairweather, sold and held in pledge beneath the mountain, payment of principal and interest due upon the solstice binding—*

The fire guttered. The courier breathed. Far overhead, through the smoke-hole, the sky stayed empty of everything but the dark, and somewhere beneath Vesper's feet—deep, deep, deeper than roots, deeper than the oldest roads—she felt, for the first time in eleven years, something answer the storm in her blood.

It felt like her sister calling her name.
