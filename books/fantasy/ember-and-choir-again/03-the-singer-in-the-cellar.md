## 3. The Singer in the Cellar

The trail began with a song.

Vesper had followed it for three days, down from the salt flats where Bramble's last coin had bought her a mule and a warning, through the drowned orchards of the Marchlands where the frost had come too early and too hungry. The song was a smuggler's mark, hummed in taverns by men who thought they were only keeping an old tune alive: a descending line of seven notes that ended on a held breath, like someone leaning over the edge of a stair into darkness. Bramble had heard her humming it once, years ago, and gone pale as unbleached linen.

*That's a door-song,* he'd said. *Undercourt music. You don't want to know how it got up here.*

But she did want to know. Because Wren used to hum it too — Wren, who had never been below the mountain in her life, who learned it somewhere, from someone, and never said who. And because the writ folded against Vesper's heart named *Wren Fairweather* in ink that still smelled faintly of cold iron, and every road Vesper walked now was a road toward her sister, even when she could not see the way.

The trail ended at a salt-house outside the village of Marrow's Rest, if you could call three leaning houses and a shrine to a forgotten river god a village. The salt-house was built into the hillside like a tooth in a jaw, its doors brine-stained, its windows shuttered against a wind that carried the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but intended to. The smuggler who owned it — a wide woman named Hesk with forearms like mooring rope — took one look at Vesper's face and one look at the writ's seal showing through her coat, and jerked her chin toward the cellar stairs without a word being spent between them.

"Third door," Hesk said. "He'll hear you coming. He always hears."

"He?"

Hesk almost smiled. "You'll see. Or rather." She tapped her ear. "You'll hear."

* * *

The cellar stairs went down farther than any salt-house needed. Vesper counted forty steps, then fifty, then lost count as the walls changed from cut stone to something older, something fitted without mortar, the blocks so large and seamless they might have been grown rather than laid. The air cooled. Her skin prickled with the old familiar static of deep places, of stone remembering pressure, and beneath it she felt something else — a vibration, faint and rhythmic, like a heartbeat kept by the mountain itself.

The third door was made of black wood banded in tarnished silver, and it stood slightly ajar. Through the gap came light — not lamplight, which flickers, but a steady, patient glow, the color of honey held up to the sun. And through the gap came music.

Vesper stopped with her hand on the door.

It was a viola da gamba, or something shaped like one, played low and slow, a melody that wound downward through registers until it seemed less like sound than like descent — each phrase a step deeper, each rest a landing. She knew the tune. Of course she knew the tune. It was the seven-note door-song, but unfolded now, opened up like a hand revealing what it had been hiding, and hearing it whole made her chest ache in a way she couldn't name, the way homesickness aches for a place you've never been.

She pushed the door open.

The room beyond was small and round, carved from the living rock, lit by a single lantern whose flame burned without wavering — and whose glow was wrong, somehow, too warm, too gold, the light of a soul-lantern, though Vesper didn't have a name for that yet. Shelves lined the curved walls, crowded with instruments: lutes with cracked bellies, a harp missing half its strings, pipes bundled in oilcloth, a hurdy-gurdy sleeping under dust. And in the center, on a stool worn smooth by years of sitting, sat a man playing a long-necked stringed instrument older than all the rest.

He was younger than his hands. That was the first thing she noticed. His face had the soft, unfinished quality of someone not yet thirty, but his fingers moved over the strings with the patience of decades, callused and precise, each note placed like a stone in a wall. He wore a musician's coat, once fine, now patched at the elbows with cloth that didn't match, and his hair was dark and long and tied back with what looked suspiciously like a bootlace. When the door opened he didn't stop playing. He simply turned his head, eyes closed, and finished the phrase as though Vesper's entrance were merely another note in it.

Then he set down the bow, opened his eyes, and looked at her.

"You're the sister," he said.

Not a question. Vesper kept her hand near the knife at her belt and let the silence do her talking. It was a habit from the smuggling years: silence made people fill it, and filled silences gave up more than questions ever did.

The man waited her out, which annoyed her immediately. Then he sighed and gestured at the lantern. "You came down the salt road behind a courier who carried a writ with your family's seal. You walk like someone who calls weather — weight on the balls of your feet, ready to move if the ground argues. And you flinched at the seventh note." He picked up the instrument again, cradling it, not playing. "Nobody flinches at the seventh note unless they've heard it sung properly. Down there." He nodded, as if the mountain's roots were visible through the floor. "Or from someone who has."

"My sister," Vesper said. "Wren."

"Ilex," he said. "Though down there I was Ilex Vane, second chair of the Regent's own consorts, before—" He stopped, and something moved across his face too quickly to read. "Before. You're Vesper. The exiled one."

"The exiled one," Vesper repeated flatly. "Is that what they call me?"

"What everyone calls everyone, down below. Titles are cheaper than souls there, and both are traded daily." He rose, and up close she saw that his fine coat hid more patches, and that beneath the collar a thin silver chain held a small ring — a woman's ring, plain band, worn thin. "You want to go under the mountain. To the court. Before solstice."

"How do you know that?"

"Because the writ is a summons, not a sentence. It names Wren Fairweather as debtor, and debtors are called home to pay. Solstice binding. Everyone below knows what that means; nobody above does, which is how the court likes it." He set the instrument down with exaggerated care, the way a man handles a thing he cannot afford to break. "And because Wren told me you would come."

The words landed like a dropped lantern. "When."

"Three years ago." Ilex's voice went quiet, and for the first time the performance fell away and left something raw underneath. "The night before she signed. She came to my rooms in the court — yes, she'd been below, twice, no one ever knew — and she sat where you're standing and she said, *when my sister comes, and she will come, tell her about the roads.* Not whether. When." He laughed, short and bitter. "I told her no one would come. I told her the Fairweathers looked after themselves. She hit me. First time anyone in the court had ever struck me, and I deserved it."

Vesper's hand had closed around the knife hilt without her deciding anything. "You loved her."

It wasn't a question either, and they both knew it, and Ilex didn't insult her by denying it. He touched the ring at his throat instead. "I did. I do. Which is why I'm in a cellar under Marrow's Rest instead of a tower under the mountain. I spoke against the binding at court. Openly. A musician, objecting to a contract — you can imagine. They didn't kill me. Killing is expensive. They *disgraced* me. Struck my name from the rolls, broke my commission, and let it be known that Ilex Vane owes the court a debt of silence, payable whenever they choose to collect. So I ran, and I've been running for three years, and every smuggler road I know ends at a border that died four nights ago." He met her eyes. "There is nowhere left for me to run *up*. Only down."

"So you want passage out."

"I want passage *through*. There's a difference. I know the Undercourt's roads — the true ones, the ones that remember names. I can get us inside the mountain past every ward and toll. In exchange, when this is done, whatever's done — you take me with you. Out. Anywhere. I don't care where. Somewhere the word 'debt' means what it meant before the court got hold of it."

Vesper looked at him for a long moment. At the ring on its chain. At the instrument he'd set down like a heart on a table. At the soul-lantern burning steady and gold, and she wondered — with a cold twist in her stomach — whose light that was, and whether he knew, and whether it mattered to him.

Here was the shape of her distrust, and she examined it honestly, the way she'd been taught to examine a storm front: Ilex had loved Wren. Ilex had known about the sale — maybe not the terms, but the fact of it — and had stayed below for three years while Wren's soul sat in a vault, singing for the court that owned her. Love that stayed behind a locked door wasn't love; it was sentiment wearing love's coat. Vesper had crossed a dead ward-line, buried a courier, bribed a fence, and walked three frozen days on the strength of a hummed tune. What had Ilex sacrificed? A chair. A title. Comforts.

And yet.

And yet he had spoken against the binding *openly*, in a court where speaking cost souls. And yet Wren had trusted him with the one message that mattered. And yet the seventh note had made him close his eyes just now, when he'd thought she wasn't looking, the way people do when a sound reaches into them and finds the old wound.

"You could be leading me into a trap," she said. "The regent knows your name. Debts get collected."

"They do." He smiled, and it was a tired, crooked thing. "Which is why I'm offering the only currency I have left. My knowledge, my music, and my ruin. If I betray you, I lose the only exit I'll ever be offered. If I help you, I might die — but I might also stand in the vault where her lantern hangs, and I have wanted that for three years the way the drowning want air." He picked up the instrument again, slung its strap across his back like a sword. "So. Do we have a bargain, storm-caller? Passage through the dark, for passage out of it?"

Vesper should have said no. Every instinct honed by exile said no: trust no one who loved what you lost, no one who stayed comfortable while you froze. But the writ against her heart seemed to warm, and somewhere far above, clouds were gathering that she could feel through sixty feet of stone, restless, unanswered, hers.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When we find her lantern — if we find it — I speak to her first. Alone. Whatever you were to her, whatever you are, that's mine before it's yours."

Something flickered across Ilex's face — hurt, maybe, or respect, or the two tangled past telling. "Fair," he said finally. "More than fair." He turned to the shelves and began pulling down oilcloth bundles, checking strings, tucking a tuning key into his belt. "We leave tonight, before the frost closes the passes. There's a door in the mountain's roots that opens for Fairweather blood — Wren told me of it, once, when she was drunk on court wine and grief. Doors that remember names." He glanced back at her, and the lamplight caught the ring at his throat. "Do you know your mother's songs, Vesper Fairweather? All of them?"

The question slid into her like a splinter. "Why?"

"Because the door will ask for a toll," Ilex said, "and it doesn't take coin."

Outside, far overhead, the first snow of the dead winter began to fall through a sky with no stars in it, and down in the cellar the soul-lantern burned on, patient and gold, waiting to learn whose light would be spent next.
