## 4. Armies on the High Road

The smoke reached them before the town did.

Ilex smelled it first — not woodsmoke, which he knew from a hundred hearths and a hundred hiding places, but something sweeter and fouler underneath, the reek of burned thatch and rendered fat and things he refused to name. He had spent eleven years in the Undercourt learning to read the air the way other men read faces, and this air had a face like an open wound.

"Vesper." He said it quietly, because the high road bent ahead through gorse and granite, and sound carried strangely in the cold where the stars should have been. "Do you smell it?"

She did. He watched her stop, one hand going to the salt-stained satchel at her hip as if contraband could protect her, her chin lifting the way it must have lifted when she called storms down over the free cities — before Thornfell's council decided a storm-caller was worth more exiled than employed. She was younger than her reputation. Everyone was.

"The wind's wrong," she said. "It's coming *from* the road."

"It's coming from Ashmill," Ilex said, and hated how steady his voice came out. He had rehearsed steadiness for years in courts where a tremor meant death. "Or what's left of it."

They crested the rise together, and there it was.

Ashmill had been a free-town of perhaps four hundred souls — a market town, famous for its apple brandy and its stubbornness, both of which it had displayed to the wrong people. Now it was a black sketch of itself against a colorless sky. The granary still stood, one wall upright like a man refusing to kneel. Everything else was char and cinder, roofless rooms open to the dead stars like mouths mid-scream. Smoke rose from it in slow gray ropes, unhurried, because whatever had burned here had finished burning days ago and no one remained to put it out.

No one remained at all. That was the worst part. No crows, even. Crows always came.

Vesper walked down into it. He should have stopped her — every instinct honed in the Undercourt said *do not walk toward the ruin, walk around it, walk away* — but his feet followed hers down the rutted high road, past a waystone whose carved blessing had been chiseled out with deliberate, patient strokes. Not defaced in rage. Erased in policy.

"They took their time," Vesper murmured. She crouched by the town well and drew up a bucket with hands that didn't shake, though he saw the effort it cost her. The water came up gray. She drank anyway. "Chiseled the wards off the stones. That's not looting. That's *unmaking*."

"That's the usurper's masons," Ilex said. "They travel with the vanguard. Before the army takes a town, they take its names."

He hadn't meant to say so much. But she turned and looked at him with those storm-gray eyes, and he understood suddenly why Wren Fairweather had loved her sister enough to sell herself — and why Vesper would burn the world rather than learn it too late.

"Tell me," she said. "All of it. You've been circling it since the cellar."

So he told her, standing among the ashes of a town that had trusted the border wards to keep such armies out, because the wards were dead now, and secrets kept in living bodies were all anyone had left.

* * *

"The man they call the usurper has a true name," Ilex said. "That matters more than you'd think. Names are currency below, and he's been spending his for twenty years. He was a ward-mason once. A good one. He helped raise the Thornfell wards himself, back when your family's bargain was young and the payments were small."

"My family's bargain," Vesper repeated flatly.

"Your family's bargain." He let it sit between them, ugly and undeniable. "You thought the wards ran on weather and prayer. So does everyone above ground. But wards are engines, Vesper, and engines need fuel. The fuel is souls. Banked, bound, and bled slowly beneath the mountain, in the court your kind pretends isn't there. Your grandmother signed the first contract. Your mother renewed it. And when the renewal came due last winter—" He stopped.

"When Wren paid it," Vesper said.

"When Wren paid it." He looked at the ruined granary rather than at her face. "The usurper knows. That's the whole shape of the war, if you want to call it that. He doesn't want Thornfell. Thornfell is a doorstop. He wants the throne beneath the mountain — the Hollow Regency, the seat that holds the ledger of every soul ever banked under the wards. Whoever sits that throne commands the fuel. Whoever commands the fuel can light the wards for their own cities or snuff them for everyone else's. He means to be king of a dark country with everyone else kneeling in the dark."

"And the souls already banked?"

"Assets," Ilex said simply. "He'll spend them. Including—"

"Including my sister." Vesper's voice didn't rise. It went somewhere worse: quiet, and cold, and deep, like water finding the crack in a dam. "The solstice binding makes the sale permanent. You told me that in the cellar. Tell me again what happens at solstice."

"The keystone soul gets set into the binding permanently. Woven into the ward-lattice itself. After that, she can't be freed without unmaking the wards entirely — and the wards are the only thing standing between the free cities and winters like the one that killed the old kingdoms." He finally made himself look at her. "That's the trap, Vesper. Her freedom and everyone else's warmth are the same coin. Spend one, you lose the other."

For a long moment she said nothing. Above them the starless sky pressed down like a lid. Somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, faint as a headache, Ilex could hear it — the vanguard. Wheels. Iron-shod hooves. The low rhythmic clatter of a mason's chisel eating another waystone.

"We should go," he said. "South, down to the coast roads. There are smuggler coves at Gullwrack, ships that don't ask names. We could be at sea in three days. The usurper's reach ends at the tide line — for now."

Vesper was looking north. North, where the land climbed into the Spine, and the Spine climbed into the mountain, and the mountain went down and down into the dark where a girl named Wren Fairweather hung in a lantern, waiting to become architecture.

"No," she said.

"Vesper—"

"I said *no*." She turned, and for one heartbeat he saw the storm-caller whole — not the smuggler, not the exile, but the thing the free cities had chained and shipped away because it was too much weather for one small border town to hold. Frost feathered across the well-bucket rim. The gray water shivered. "You want to run for the sea. Run. I'm done running from debts other people signed in my name. My sister is *below*. Solstice is nine days away. And every hour we spend walking south is an hour the usurper's masons spend chiseling the world down to nothing."

"The mountain will kill you," Ilex said. "Or worse — the court will *keep* you. They know your bloodline. They'll find a use for a storm-caller's soul, and it won't be a gentle one."

"Then I'll be in good company." She shouldered her satchel. "You said you knew the court's roads. You said you owed me passage. Was that true, musician? Or was it just a song to get yourself out of a cellar?"

He wanted to lie. Eleven years of survival said lie, smile, sing something pretty, slip out the back while she marched into the dark alone. But he had already failed one Fairweather by staying behind and staying safe, and the ash of Ashmill was settling on his shoulders like a debt of its own.

"It was true," he said. "God help me, it was true."

"Then lead me down." She started north along the high road, past the erased waystone, and did not look back to see whether he followed.

He followed.

* * *

They left the high road at dusk, or what passed for dusk under a sky with no stars to mark it — only a bruised dimming, like a lamp guttering in a far room. Ilex took them onto a drover's track that wound up through the gorse toward the Spine's foothills, a path so old it predated the wards, predated the free cities, maybe predated the naming of things. His knees remembered it. His nightmares certainly did.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen standing stone, and Vesper built no fire, and neither did he, because fires drew eyes and eyes drew armies. They ate hard bread and salt fish and drank the gray-tasting water, and the silence between them filled up with everything neither of them would say about Wren.

"She used to sing," Vesper said at last, to the darkness rather than to him. "Badly. Off-key, always. Mother said the Fairweather gift skipped her — all the storm, none of the voice. She sang anyway. All the time. Drove the neighbors mad."

"She wasn't off-key," Ilex said.

Vesper's head turned slowly. "You heard her."

"Once." The word cost him. He let it. "At the solstice fair, six years ago. Before your exile. Before everything. She was buying honey cakes and arguing with the seller about the price, and she started humming — some nonsense song about a fox and a ferryman — and I stopped in the middle of the square like an idiot with my mouth open. She caught me staring." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "She said, 'You look like a man who's about to offer unsolicited advice.' And I said, 'You're flat on the third line.' And she said, 'I know. It's better that way. Anyone can hit a note.'"

Ilex stared at the starless sky and laughed — a short, broken sound with grief inside it, the laugh of a man who has found something funny in a burning house.

"Anyone can hit a note," he repeated. "She said that to me too. Different words, same song. That was the day I asked her to run away with me. Down below. I had a place in the court orchestra then. I thought—" He shook his head. "She said no. She said someone had to stay above and mind the sky. I thought she meant the family. The wards. Duty." His voice dropped. "I didn't know she meant *you*. I didn't know what staying would cost her until it was already paid."

Vesper said nothing for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was stripped down to bare timber.

"Then you loved her, and she stayed, and I left, and she sold herself, and now there's ash where Ashmill was. That's the whole song, isn't it. Four verses, all of them mine."

"Not all yours," Ilex said. "Some of it's mine. Some of it's theirs." He nodded east, toward the sound of wheels he could no longer quite hear but knew was there, patient as rot. "And some of it hasn't been written yet. That part's still ours."

She lay back against the standing stone, arms crossed, eyes closed — but he had seen enough courtiers feigning sleep to know she wasn't sleeping. Neither was he. Above them the empty heavens wheeled, blind and black, and somewhere beneath their backs the mountain breathed its long slow breath, and somewhere beneath that, behind doors older than kingdoms, a lantern burned with half a girl inside it.

"Nine days," Vesper said into the dark. "Solstice."

"Nine days," Ilex agreed. "And three of them to reach the roots of the Spine, if the roads hold and the vanguard stays east."

"And if they don't stay east?"

He was quiet a moment. Then, because she deserved the truth and he was done rationing it:

"Then we won't need to find the mountain's doors, Vesper. The army will have opened them for us. The usurper doesn't march on Thornfell — he marches *through* it, straight to the crown of the Spine, where the old descent stairs begin. Wherever he breaks ground, the ways below break open with him." He paused. "There's a door in the mountain's roots that answers to your name. Your family sealed it when the bargain was struck. Fairweather-made, Fairweather-locked. If any key to the Undercourt still turns for your blood... it's that one."

In the darkness beside him, he heard Vesper Fairweather exhale — slow and deliberate, like a storm deciding where to strike.

"Then that's the door we knock on," she said.
