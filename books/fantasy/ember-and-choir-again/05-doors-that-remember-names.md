## 5. Doors That Remember Names

The smuggler roads did not appear on any map, and that was the first mercy of them. The second was the warmth. After three days of climbing through passes where the usurper's cold had settled like a sickness, Vesper Fairweather walked into a tunnel that breathed.

She felt it before Ilex said anything — a faint exhalation from the stone, carrying the smell of deep earth and old iron and something sweeter beneath, like apples rotting in a cellar no one had emptied. The mountain's roots. They were inside them now, or close enough that the mountain knew they were coming.

"Here," Ilex said quietly, and set down his lantern.

The tunnel widened into a hollow where the walls curved like the inside of a throat. Roots thicker than her waist crossed the ceiling in braided arches, silver-grey and smooth as driftwood, and among them hung things she did not want to look at too long: small glass shapes, dull and dark, swaying without wind. Soul-lanterns, empty ones. Shells. Whatever had lived in them had been poured out long ago, or never been poured in at all.

"Smugglers call this the Vestibule," Ilex said. "Every road into the Undercourt passes through it eventually. But there's only one road that opens for a Fairweather."

Vesper's hand went to the writ folded against her heart before she could stop herself. Three days of walking beside him and she still flinched from every mention of her name, as if the word itself were a debt collector's knock.

"You've used it," she said. "This door."

"Once." He crouched by the wall, brushing grit away from the stone with two fingers, gentle as a man touching a sleeping animal. "Years ago. When I still thought love was a thing you could carry across a border." He glanced up at her, and the lantern light made his face look older than his voice. "It let me through because Wren had spoken my name to it. It closed behind me when I ran. Doors here remember everything, Vesper. That's not poetry. It's how they work. A door is just a memory with hinges."

* * *

They cleared the wall together for an hour, Ilex directing and Vesper digging, until the shape of it emerged: a slab of grey stone taller than either of them, seamless except for a seam so fine she might have taken it for a crack. No handle. No keyhole. Only a shallow basin carved into its face at the height of her chest, dry as an old tear-duct.

"The toll is paid here?" she asked.

"The toll is *asked* here." Ilex stepped back. "What it takes, it chooses. You don't get to pick what a door remembers of you. You only get to decide whether to pay."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we walk back down the smuggler roads and hope the usurper's outriders are feeling merciful." He almost smiled. "They aren't. I've met them."

Vesper pressed her palm flat against the stone. It was warm — blood-warm, skin-warm, warmer than anything had a right to be this far under a frozen sky — and beneath her hand she felt a pulse, slow and vast, like something turning over in its sleep.

She called the storm.

Not the way she'd done it above ground, all thunder and desperation, lightning dragged out of clouds that weren't there anymore. This was quieter. She reached into the place inside herself where she hoarded weather the way misers hoarded coin — the squall off the Saltreach coast she'd caught at fourteen, the white gale that had carried her out of Thornfell in disgrace, the small hot thunders of every night since that she'd swallowed rather than spent — and she let a thread of it run down her arm and into the stone.

The door drank.

Light moved under the surface of the slab like fish under ice, tracing lines she recognized with a jolt that went through her worse than the cold ever had: the Fairweather knotwork, the same pattern her mother had stitched into the hems of their cloaks, the same pattern burned into the lintel of the house in Thornfell that no one had lived in for six years. Her family's mark, grown enormous, grown patient, waiting.

A voice came out of the door. Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words — her mother's cadence, worn soft, saying something Vesper couldn't quite hold on to, the way you can't quite hold a dream after waking. The basin filled with shadow that wasn't shadow: images, half-formed, rising like silt stirred from a riverbed.

And then the asking began.

* * *

It took her childhood first.

She saw the kitchen of the Thornfell house with such clarity that her knees nearly buckled — flour on the table, Wren's small hands printmaking in it, the window fogged with steam. She smelled bread. She heard her own laugh, high and unbroken, a child's laugh from before exile, before wards, before any of it. The door held these up to her one by one, tenderly, horribly, the way a merchant holds up cloth, and waited.

"No," Vesper whispered. "Take anything else—"

But the door didn't bargain. It simply kept showing, and each image dimmed slightly as it passed, like coals going grey, and she understood with dawning horror that it wasn't showing her memories. It was *weighing* them. And somewhere in that great slow pulse beneath her palm, it was deciding which one was worth a road into the mountain's roots.

Ilex's hand hovered near her shoulder but didn't land. "You can step back," he said softly. "There's no shame—"

"There's every shame." Her voice came out cracked. "Everything I have left of them is in here. If it takes—"

The kitchen dissolved. The flour, the fogged window, Wren's laughing ghost — gone, and she felt the absence the way you feel a tooth missing, tongue going again and again to the gap. Grief, she realized, had a texture. It had weight and grain and temperature, and the door was tasting all of it.

Then came the lullaby.

Her mother had sung it badly — that was the truth no one else alive knew, the secret heart of it. Off-key, always, wandering off the melody and finding her way back by some private route, and Vesper had loved it *because* it wandered, because it meant even her mother's songs needed forgiving, because forgiveness could be sung. Six years of exile, six years of frozen stars, and Vesper had kept that tune folded inside her storm-hoard like a coal wrapped in ash, taking it out on the worst nights, humming it to herself in smugglers' cellars and stolen beds, telling herself that as long as she remembered it wrong in exactly her mother's way, her mother wasn't entirely gone.

The door found it. Of course it did. It was the warmest thing in her.

"No," she said again, and this time it came out as a sob. "No, take the storms, take all of them, take every gale I've ever—"

But the storms were currency too, and cheap ones. The door wanted what she loved. That was the whole terrible arithmetic of it. Inheritance, she was beginning to understand, was not what your family left you. It was what your family had already spent, and what the world would keep charging you for, generation after generation, until someone finally paid the principal.

Wren had paid with her whole soul. The least the door could do was leave Vesper her sister's name.

She opened her hands. She let go.

The lullaby rose out of her like heat off summer stone — she felt it leave, felt the melody unraveling note by note, the wandering line, the off-key grace of it, her mother's voice thinning as it went until it was only the tune, only the shape, and then not even that. What remained was a hole shaped exactly like a song. She would spend the rest of her life reaching for it, she knew, humming near-melodies that collapsed before they resolved, wondering why her throat closed at certain intervals of sound.

The basin drained. The seam in the stone brightened.

* * *

The door opened inward, onto darkness that breathed.

Warm air rolled over them, thick with mineral smell and the distant, impossible sound of music — strings, very far below, playing something in a minor key that made the empty soul-lanterns overhead tremble in their brackets.

"That's the court," Ilex murmured. He had gone pale. "We're in its lungs now. Everything past this point hears us coming."

Vesper stood in the threshold a moment longer. Behind her, the door was already forgetting to be a door, the knotwork fading, the seam closing like a scar. Somewhere in the stone, her mother's lullaby was being filed away among six years of Fairweather payments, added to a ledger older than the wards, older than the sale, older perhaps than the mountain itself.

She had come to steal her sister back. She was beginning to understand she would leave with far less than she'd brought.

"Which way?" she asked.

Ilex lifted the lantern. Far below, the music changed — the same minor key, but slower now, and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, she heard singing. One voice. Thin and clear and achingly familiar, though it couldn't be, though it was impossible, though Wren's soul was banked in a vault leagues beneath them—

The song stopped. Then, very distinctly, it began again — the opening phrase repeated, patient, insistent, like someone knocking.

"Vesper," Ilex said slowly, "that's not possible."

"I know," she said, and stepped into the dark.
