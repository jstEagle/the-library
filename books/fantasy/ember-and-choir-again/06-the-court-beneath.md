## 6. The Court Beneath

The last smuggler stair let them out through the mouth of a dead fountain, and Ilex felt the Undercourt before he saw it — a vibration in the teeth, a hum in the hollow of the throat, like standing inside the body of an instrument someone else was playing.

He had told himself he would not be moved. He had rehearsed it on the road, in the burned town's ash-smell, in Vesper's cold silence: *I am done with this place. I am a man who left. I am a man they struck from the rolls.* He had imagined the court as a wound he could walk past without bleeding.

He was wrong.

The cavern opened around them like a held breath. Rivers of pale water threaded between columns of living stone, and the columns had been carved — or had grown, Ilex had never learned which — into figures with their faces turned upward, toward a ceiling that imitated a night sky. That was the first obscenity of the place, and the one he had forgotten: the Undercourt had its own stars. Cold white lamps hung on invisible wire in the dark overhead, arranged in constellations that had never existed in any sky men had slept beneath, and their light fell without warmth, without shadow, without mercy.

And beneath that false heaven: the lanterns.

They lined the processional way by the hundreds, glass vessels on iron crooks, and each one held a light that moved. Not flickered — *moved*, the way a trapped moth moves, the way a drowning man moves. Some pressed against the glass in slow, patient pulses. Some circled, restless, tracing the same path over and over as if the shape of the path were the last thing they remembered. As Ilex came down the way with Vesper at his shoulder, the nearest lanterns turned. He felt them turn. Felt a hundred small attentions swing toward him like the heads of flowers following a sun that would never rise for them.

"Don't look at them," he said quietly.

Vesper was looking. Of course she was looking. Her face in the cold light was the face of a woman counting, and he understood with a sick lurch that she was counting lanterns, as if one of them might be Wren, as if she could pick her sister out of a congregation of the sold the way one picks a face from a crowd.

"How many?" she asked.

"I don't know. No one knows. The court doesn't publish its accounts." He kept his voice flat, a musician's flat, the tone he used for tuning an instrument in a noisy room. "Walk like you belong here. That's the only armor that works."

They belonged, or they performed belonging well enough. The processional emptied into the great gallery where the court held its perpetual evening, and there the masks began.

Ilex had forgotten the masks too, or had buried the memory of them. Every noble wore one — porcelain, lacquer, beaten silver, carved bone — and not one of them covered the whole face. A woman with a heron's beak of white porcelain left her mouth bare. A man whose mask was a single silver eye left everything else naked, and his remaining eye wept steadily, elegantly, as though grief here were a fashion and he wore it better than most. They drifted through the gallery in slow constellations of their own, drinking wine the color of garnets, and the sound they made was the worst thing: not silence, but *murmur*, a low continuous wash of voices that never resolved into words, like a choir heard through a wall.

"They're not talking to each other," Vesper said.

"No." Ilex's mouth had gone dry. "They're talking to the lanterns. It's considered intimate. It's considered *artistic*." He had done it himself once. He had stood at a gathering very like this one and improvised a melody for a lantern that had once belonged to a miller's daughter from the free cities, and the court had applauded, and the lantern had pressed itself against the glass toward the sound, and he had told himself that was a kindness. That the soul remembered music, and he was giving it music. He had needed eleven years and a disgrace to understand what he had actually been: a man playing lullabies to the imprisoned.

A string of musicians played on a terrace above the gallery — his old colleagues, or their successors, playing the court's endless pavane on instruments of black wood and silver wire. The music was beautiful. That was the trap of the whole place; everything here was beautiful, and the beauty was the mechanism. He felt it working on him even now, felt his feet wanting the pavane's rhythm, felt his hands remembering fingerings, felt the terrible homesick ease of returning to a cage whose bars you had memorized.

Vesper's hand closed on his arm. "The regent," she said. "Which one is the regent?"

And Ilex, who had been avoiding looking directly at the dais at the gallery's heart, made himself look.

The regent sat beneath a canopy of woven silver wire, and wore a mask of plain, unpolished iron — the only humble mask in the room, which was its own kind of ostentation. It was small and slight and had been the regent for as long as Ilex had served the court, and no one knew what was beneath the iron, and no one asked, because the regent's voice did not come from behind the mask. It came from everywhere at once, softly, from the walls and the water and the wire of the canopy, the way sound comes from a well-strung instrument touched in the dark.

The regent was looking at him.

Ilex knew it the way he knew a wrong note. The iron mask had not moved, had not turned, and yet the attention of the whole court bent toward them like light through a lens, and the murmur of the masked nobles faltered, and the pavane upstairs stumbled and recovered.

"Ilex Vane," said the regent, from the walls, from the water, from everywhere. "The court's lost voice comes home."

Every masked face turned. Ilex felt Vesper go rigid beside him and put his hand over hers on his arm — *still, still, we are nothing, we are guests* — and bowed, because there was nothing else to do. The bow his body had been trained into, low and graceful, hands open.

"Your Radiance honors a disgraced man," he said. "I'm only passing through."

"Everyone is only passing through." The regent rose. The mask did not catch the light; it drank it. "Passing through, passing down, passing over. The court remembers all its instruments, even the cracked ones." The slight figure descended the dais steps, and the crowd of masks parted, and the regent came to stand before them, near enough that Ilex could see that the iron was rusted in places, that beneath it a throat moved when the everywhere-voice spoke. "You left in the night, Ilex Vane. You took nothing but your hands. And now you return with a Fairweather. The court does not believe in coincidence. The court believes in *accounts*."

"We've come about an account," Vesper said.

Ilex could have kissed her or strangled her. Her voice carried — that was the storm in her, he realized, it lived in her voice the way music lived in his hands — and it cut through the gallery's murmur like a blade through silk. A hundred masks turned back toward them. Somewhere above, the pavane stopped altogether.

"Then let us speak of accounts," the regent said, and the everywhere-voice softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register it owned. "Walk with me. The gallery is drafty, and the free cities have taught their children such ugly words for what we do here."

* * *

The regent led them along a colonnade above the pale river, and the soul-lanterns followed at a respectful distance, drifting on their crooks like a congregation that had learned not to hope.

"Your sister's lantern is the keystone," the regent said to Vesper, without preamble, without cruelty, the way one reports weather. "The solstice binding requires an anchor. The wards your family built were anchored to a soul freely given, and freely given souls are rare, and your sister's was — is — exceptional. When the binding renews at solstice, her soul becomes the court's in perpetuity, and the wards of Thornfell burn on her light for another hundred years. It is a tidy arrangement. Your family has always appreciated tidiness."

"I've come to break the arrangement," Vesper said.

"Yes." The regent walked on. The river murmured below, and in the murmur Ilex thought he heard voices, or the memory of voices. "And here the court must disappoint you, storm-caller, because the court has had an offer. A generous one. There is a man above us — you will have seen his work on the high road — who wants the throne beneath the mountain, and with it the bank of souls, and with it the binding. His emissaries arrived three days ago. They offer the court the free cities, delivered, in exchange for the vault and the regency's continuation. War is expensive. Peace, purchased, is cheap."

"And Wren?"

"Would burn on his wards instead of yours. A lantern does not care whose hand carries it." The regent stopped at a balcony overlooking the vault road — a descending spiral, far below, lined with lanterns so dense they made a river of light. "But the court is sentimental. It keeps old instruments in tune. So I offer you what I offered no emissary, Fairweather. Walk away. Return to the free cities. I will have the writ of your exile annulled. Your name restored, your property returned, your storms relegalized. The usurper will be dealt with; the court has survived worse men. And your sister's soul will keep the lights burning, which is, when one thinks of it coldly, the use she was always meant for."

The words landed in the silence like stones in still water.

Ilex watched Vesper's face and saw the offer do exactly what it was designed to do. Not tempt her — he had known her eleven days, and he knew she was not built for temptation — but *wound* her. Because it was her old life, offered back to her whole, on the condition that she let Wren burn for it. Because it was the family bargain restated in the regent's gentle voice: *one Fairweather pays, the other walks free.* It was the debt that had exiled her, wearing a kinder mask.

"That's the same deal," Vesper said. Her voice was very quiet. "That's exactly the same deal my parents made."

"Yes," said the regent. "It is a good deal. It has kept the stars lit for a hundred years. Sentiment is the only thing that has ever threatened it." The iron mask tilted. "And you, Ilex Vane. Your debts are also on the court's books. Eleven years of interest. I could burn them tonight, as a parting gift to a lost voice. Both of you could walk out of this mountain free, whole, and forgiven. The court asks so little. It asks only that you stop struggling against an arrangement older and wiser than either of you."

The gallery's murmur had resumed somewhere behind them, soft as tide. The lanterns on their crooks had drifted close, and Ilex saw that they were pressed against the glass, all of them, every soul in every vessel turned toward Vesper Fairweather like an audience toward the one note that might finally resolve.

And then, from the vault road below, came a sound that was not music.

Horns. Deep, brassy, ugly — surface horns, army horns, the signal-calls of the usurper's emissaries, echoing up the spiral with the flat confidence of men who believed the bargaining was already over. The regent's iron mask turned toward the sound, and the whole cavern seemed to turn with it, false stars shivering on their wires.

"Ah," said the regent, from everywhere. "The other buyers. Come. If you must argue about accounts, storm-caller, you may argue in front of the vault — and see for yourself what your sister's light is worth."

Below them, down the spiral road, the lantern-river began to tremble.
