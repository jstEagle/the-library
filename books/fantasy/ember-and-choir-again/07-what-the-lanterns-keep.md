## 7. What the Lanterns Keep

The vault road descended in a spiral so long and so patient that Vesper lost count of the turns. The regent did not walk beside them — it glided, or seemed to, its iron mask catching the light of the lantern-river below and giving nothing back. Ilex walked on Vesper's left, one hand trailing the wall of black stone as though he needed to remember what solid things felt like, and he did not speak. He had not spoken since the horns.

*They are here already,* Vesper thought, and the thought had teeth. *While I was learning doors and debts, they were already at the table.*

Below them, the river of souls widened into a lake. That was the only word for it — a lake of lanterns, thousands upon thousands of them, drifting on a slow dark current that had no wind to drive it and no shore to hold it. Each lantern held a light that was not firelight. Some burned white and steady. Some guttered like candles in a draft. One, far out, pulsed a slow sullen red, and Vesper found she could not look away from it until Ilex touched her elbow.

"Don't count them," he murmured. "It's a way of going mad that feels like diligence."

"I wasn't counting."

"You were. You do it when you're frightened. You counted the knots in the cellar beams for three days before you'd speak to me."

She almost smiled. Almost. The lake swallowed the smile whole.

At the water's edge stood a gallery of niches cut into the living rock, each one cradling a single lantern apart from the current — the banked souls, the ones with accounts still open against them. The regent stopped before the gallery and gestured with one long grey hand, and the gesture was almost courteous, which was the most frightening thing it had done all night.

"The Fairweather holding," it said. Its voice came from the mask and from the walls and from somewhere behind Vesper's own ribs. "You may look. Looking is free. Everything else has a price."

Vesper stepped forward.

There were three lanterns in the niche. She had expected one. The first was old, its glass smoked nearly opaque, its light down to an ember's worth of stubborn gold — her grandmother, she understood without being told, or some Fairweather older than names. The second was dimmer still, barely a breath of blue. Her mother. *Her mother's soul,* and no one had ever told her, and the lullaby she had already paid at the door rose up in her throat like bile. They had been paying this debt for generations, in installments, quietly, the way farmers pay tithes.

The third lantern was new. Its glass was clear as mountain water, and inside it burned a light the color of a storm-lit evening — violet-grey, shot through with sudden silver, flickering the way Wren used to flicker between laughter and fury, quicksilver and then thunder.

"Wren," Vesper said aloud, and the lake went quiet around the word, as if the water itself were listening.

*Don't count them,* Ilex had said. But this was one. This was the one.

She reached for the lantern, and the regent's hand closed over hers — not gripping, merely resting, cold as a ledger's cover.

"Touching costs," it said. "Speaking costs more. Are you prepared to open an account?"

"I've been opening accounts since the night the stars went out." She pulled her hand free. "Name the price for speaking."

The iron mask tilted. Somewhere in it, something considered her, the way a moneylender considers a farm that will fail but not today.

"Curious," the regent said. "Most kin weep at the glass and bargain for the whole soul at once. You ask for a conversation." A pause. "A conversation, then. Five minutes of true speech with what remains of your sister. The price: one memory of her, freely given, that she herself does not know you keep."

Vesper's breath went out of her slowly. *That she does not know you keep.* Every memory she had of Wren was shared; that was what memories between sisters were. Except —

Except the ones taken alone. The ones stolen through doorways and keyholes and the small cruelties of watching someone who did not know they were watched.

"Yes," she said, before she could think better of it, because thinking better of it was a luxury the Fairweathers had never once afforded themselves.

She lifted the lantern from its niche. It weighed nothing and everything. When her fingers closed around the glass, warmth bloomed against her palms — real warmth, the specific warmth of a hand she had held crossing a frozen ford when they were girls, and Vesper made the sound of someone taking an arrow, a short punched breath, and did not let go.

The light inside stirred. Rose toward her hands like a moth toward a window. And then Wren's voice came out of the lantern, thin and silvered and unmistakably, devastatingly alive:

"Ves? Ves, is that — oh, you *idiot.*"

Vesper laughed and sobbed in the same breath. "Hello to you too."

"They said someone might come. They say that to everyone, it's part of the cruelty, they let you hope so the hoping hurts. I didn't believe — " The light flared, steadied. "Your hair. I can't see your hair. Tell me about your hair, tell me something stupid, tell me anything."

"It's shorter. There's grey in it. I fell off a smuggler's cart in the Rainwards and landed in a ditch full of eels."

"*Eels.*" The laugh that came out of the lantern cracked in the middle, and Vesper felt it crack in her too. "Oh, that's — I have missed you so much it doesn't fit inside me anymore."

Five minutes. She had five minutes, and she was spending them on eels, and it was right, it was exactly right, because underneath the silliness ran the other thing, the thing they both knew was coming.

"Wren." Vesper crouched, bringing the lantern level with her heart. "Why didn't you tell me? The debt. The sale. Why did I have to find it on a dead courier's writ?"

The light dimmed, drew inward. When Wren spoke again, the quicksilver was gone, and what remained was older, wearier, and terribly familiar — it was their mother's voice, worn like a coat.

"Because it was never supposed to be you." A pause. "Because the wards were dying anyway, Ves. Grandmother's bargain bought them sixty years and the sixty years were up. Someone had to renew the payment or the border falls and the usurper walks into the free cities over our graves. Mother was going to do it. Then Mother got sick, and the court doesn't take sick souls — too thin, they said, not enough light in them. So there was me." The light pulsed once, hard, like a heartbeat deciding. "And there was you. Exiled. Storm-caller. The strongest light in the family by triple. Do you understand? If I'd told you, you'd have come home. And if you'd come home, they'd have taken *you.*"

"They wouldn't have — "

"They would have. The regent showed me the scales, Ves. It showed me both our lights weighed against each other. Yours outweighed mine like a cathedral outweighs a bell. One of us could buy sixty more years. It was always going to be whichever of us loved the other one more." The voice gentled. "I won the coin toss nobody else got to see."

Vesper knelt there on the cold vault stone with her sister's soul cupped in her hands, and the truth went through her the way the storm-calling went through her — total, blinding, remaking everything it touched. All those years of exile she had spent nursing resentment like a hidden wound — *they cast me out, they let me go, they chose the cities over me* — and all along Wren had engineered the exile itself, had pushed her out of the burning house and stood in the doorway so the fire would take the one who stayed.

"You protected me," Vesper whispered. "I hated you for leaving me free and you were *protecting* me."

"Somebody in this family had to learn how," Wren said, and laughed, wet and broken. "Mother sacrificed. Grandmother sacrificed. We are a dynasty of altars, Ves, and I am so tired of being good at it."

Behind her, Vesper heard Ilex make a small sound — grief, or recognition, or both. She did not turn. There would be time to fall apart later, or there would not, and either way the minutes were bleeding out of her five.

"Then hear me," she said, and her voice dropped into the register she used for storms, low and certain and carrying. "I am not here to rescue you. I finally understand that rescuing is just another word for what this family does — trading one altar for another. I'm here to *repay* you. To end the account. No more Fairweather sacrifices, Wren. Not yours. Not mine. Not ever again. Whatever it costs, it ends at solstice, and it ends with us both walking out."

The light in the lantern flared so bright that the niche behind it lit up, grandmother's ember and mother's blue breath kindling in answer, three generations of banked Fairweather souls leaning toward her voice like flowers toward a sun that had finally come.

"That's not how the court works," Wren said softly. "That's not how *anything* works. Everything balances, Ves. Something pays."

"Then let it be the storm." Vesper stood, lantern in her hands, and felt beneath her ribs the hoarded weather of twenty exiled years — every squall she'd swallowed, every lightning strike she'd caught and kept, the whole private armory of a woman who had been saving up for a war no one knew she was fighting. "Let it be everything I've been carrying. I'll split my own soul down the seam if that's the price. Half of me for all of you. Do you hear me? *Half of me for all of you.*"

For a long moment the lantern said nothing. Then, very quietly, in the smallest voice Vesper had heard since childhood — the voice Wren used during fevers, during funerals, during the night their father's boat didn't come home:

"You always did think love meant paying double."

"No," Vesper said. "I learned it from you."

The light reached for her one last time, pressing against the glass like a palm against a window, and the warmth of it spread up her arms and into her chest and settled there beside the storm, two fires in one house.

"Then hurry," Wren whispered. "The binding is in two days. And Ves — the usurper's people aren't here to buy. They're here to *take.* If they reach the vault before solstice, they don't need my consent, they'll just — "

The lantern shuddered. Not from within. From without.

Vesper turned.

Far across the lake of souls, where the spiral road met the vault gallery, torchlight had appeared — surface torchlight, ugly orange, dozens of them, descending in ranks. And above the water the drifting lanterns were beginning to pull sideways, all together, all in the same direction, like fish sensing a net.

The regent's iron mask reflected ten thousand small lights, and none of them were its own.

"Ah," it said again, from everywhere. "It seems the conversation has run over its allotted time. Storm-caller — you may wish to decide quickly whose side of the scales you intend to stand on. Because the men with the torches have brought their own weights."

Down in the dark, the first soldier's boot touched the shore of the soul-lake, and every lantern on the water flinched at once.
