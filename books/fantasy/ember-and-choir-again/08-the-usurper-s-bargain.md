## 8. The Usurper's Bargain

The mountain began to scream at the hour the court called dusk, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

Ilex felt it first through the floor of the regent's gallery—a shudder in the stone like a plucked string played too hard, a note that went on and on and would not resolve. The soul-lanterns guttered in their niches. Somewhere far above, iron was meeting rock, and the mountain's roots were carrying the sound down to every hall and corridor, so that even the masked nobles paused mid-reverie and turned their porcelain faces toward the ceiling as if expecting rain.

He had been in courts before—had played for them, flattered them, starved quietly in their antechambers while they feasted—but he had never heard a court go silent all at once. It was worse than any noise. Three hundred masks turning together. Three hundred pairs of gloved hands going still over wine that did not ripple because nothing here obeyed the ordinary laws of liquid or light.

Beside him, Vesper stood with her spine straight and her jaw set, wearing a borrowed gown of grey silk that made her look like weather about to break. She had refused the mask. That refusal had cost them three hours of negotiation and one courtier's dignity, but the regent had finally laughed—a sound like coins spilling across marble—and said the storm-caller could come bare-faced, since she would not be staying long enough to be remembered anyway.

Ilex had not liked the laugh. He liked it less now.

"The crown," Vesper said quietly. "That's the crown they're breaking."

"Yes." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "The old mineshaft. The one the smugglers call the Throat."

"You've been through it."

"Twice. Once coming, once running." He kept his eyes on the lanterns rather than on her face. "It's how I meant to get us out. After."

She looked at him then, and he felt it like a hand on his neck. "After what?"

But there was no time to answer, because the doors of the gallery opened without being opened—the Undercourt's doors did such things when they wished to make a point—and the regent came through them trailing cold like a bride trailing veil.

* * *

The regent wore a mask of black glass fitted so closely to the face beneath that Ilex sometimes wondered if there was still a face, or only the mask, worn so long it had grown into the bone. Tonight the mask caught the lantern-light and threw it back broken, and when the regent spoke, every noble in the gallery leaned in despite themselves, the way flowers lean toward a lamp that is also a fire.

"Storm-caller." The regent stopped an arm's length from Vesper, which no one did; courtiers gave the regent room the way sailors give a lee shore room. "You have heard the knocking above. Shall we discuss what your kind has brought to my door?"

"My kind," Vesper repeated.

"Surface-folk. Soldiers. Men who burn towns and call it governance." The regent's voice was mild, almost bored, and that mildness was the most frightening thing in the room. "They have sent emissaries ahead, you know. Charming creatures. They sit in my lesser halls even now, offering me treaties written on skin. They want what is banked below—every lantern in my vaults, every soul your free cities sold me across four hundred years of comfortable forgetting. And they want the keystone most of all."

"Wren," Vesper said.

"Your sister. Yes." The regent drifted past her toward the great window of the gallery—not glass, but a sheet of polished obsidian that showed the vault-fires burning far below, hundreds of small flames each holding a name. "And here is where I confess something, storm-caller, because I find confession makes bargains cleaner. Your sister's binding is not merely a lock upon her. It is a lock upon everything. The wards above, the stars, the salt-roads, the borders—all of it draws from the solstice binding. Cut the thread, free the girl, and—" the regent spread white-gloved hands, a gesture of terrible helpfulness— "the wards stay dead. Forever. The usurper's armies walk into Thornfell unopposed. The free cities burn by spring. You will have saved one soul and spent ten thousand."

Ilex watched Vesper's face do something small and catastrophic. He knew that look. He had worn it himself, years ago, standing in this same gallery while the court took his music from him note by note until he signed away his name to buy passage out. Duty pointing one way. Love pointing the other. And the road between them paved with the court's patient, ancient cruelty.

"There's another way," Vesper said.

"Is there?" The regent sounded genuinely curious. "Do tell."

"The solstice binding can be *paid*, not cut. A soul given freely against a soul taken. That's the old law. Even you keep the old law."

Something moved behind the black glass of the mask—too fast to be called an expression. "The old law," the regent murmured, "is very expensive. Are you certain you know its price?"

"I'm learning."

"Then learn faster." The regent turned, robes whispering like snow sliding off a roof. "Because the usurper's men have offered me a simpler trade: give them the keystone lantern, and they leave my vaults untouched. I am, you understand, inclined to take it. Unless someone gives me a reason not to. You have until the armies reach the second gate. After that, sentiment becomes unaffordable—even mine."

The regent swept toward the doors, then paused, and the mask turned just enough to catch Ilex in its reflection.

"And the musician. Still lurking. Still owing." A pause like a knife laid flat on a table. "Your debts have grown interest, Ilex of the Ninth Choir. Remember which of us holds the paper."

Then the doors closed, and the gallery exhaled, and the nobles resumed their murmuring as though nothing had happened, because for them nothing ever did.

* * *

They walked back along the Lantern Way in silence, past alcoves where soul-flames burned behind veined glass, each one humming faintly—the sound Ilex had spent his life chasing and could never quite write down. Wren's lantern was somewhere ahead of them, deeper in, behind the vault-door that only opened at solstice or at payment.

Vesper stopped beneath a lantern shaped like a teardrop and pressed her palm flat against the cold wall.

"He told the truth," she said. "That's the worst part. He didn't lie about any of it. Cut the binding, kill the wards, doom everyone. Free her honestly, and I pay with—" Her hand curled into a fist against the stone. "With whatever's left of me after."

"I know."

"Do you?" She rounded on him, and the nearest lantern flared with her temper, shadows leaping up the corridor walls. "Because you've been counting exits since we got here, Ilex. I've watched you do it. Every hall, every door, you're measuring it for two people walking fast. So tell me the truth for once: what's the way out?"

And there it was. The question he had been dreading since the Throat, since the cellar, since the first night he'd played a dead girl's favorite song and watched her sister flinch.

"The Throat," he said. "The old mineshaft under the crown. It comes out above the tree line, north of the salt-roads. The armies are breaching the crown right now—which means the shaft's about to be full of soldiers. But it's wide enough to climb, and dark enough to hide in, and I know the turns. We could be on the surface by dawn. Away. Both of us. Alive."

Vesper stared at him. "And Wren?"

The silence stretched. Above them, faintly, the mountain screamed again—another blow against the crown, closer now, dust sifting down from the seams in the ceiling like flour from a torn sack.

"That's what I thought," Vesper said softly.

"Vesper—"

"No. No, you don't get to say my name like that. Like you're sorry." Her voice cracked down the middle, storm-light flickering under her skin where the borrowed gown gapped at the wrist. "You loved her. You told me. You sat in that cellar and played for her ghost and told me you loved her, and the whole time you were keeping a map of how to leave without her."

"I was keeping a map of how to keep *you* alive!" It tore out of him louder than he'd meant, echoing down the Lantern Way, and a dozen lanterns flickered in answer as if the vault itself were listening. He lowered his voice. "Wren chose the sale. You told me that yourself, in the vault, with her own voice in the flame. She chose it to protect you. Do you think she'd want you to spend yourself buying her back? Do you think she'd want either of us to die in this hole?"

"She'd want us to stop deciding for her," Vesper said. "That's what Fairweathers do, Ilex. We decide for the people we love, and we call it protection, and we hand them the bill." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, furious at them. "Not this time. Not again. I'm paying the debt myself, at the binding, at solstice—and you're going to help me get there, or you're going to get out of my way."

Above them, the mountain shuddered. Dust became gravel. Somewhere in the upper galleries, glass broke, and voices rose—not court voices, but the flat barking cadence of soldiers, and beneath it the thin sweet tone of the usurper's emissaries giving orders in a language the court pretended not to understand.

The second gate had fallen early.

* * *

What happened next, Ilex would remember afterward in fragments, the way musicians remember a performance that went wrong: in bars, in beats, in the spaces where the melody should have been.

They were running for the vault approach when the soldiers found them—six of them, torchlight and steel, come down the breached galleries ahead of their officers. Vesper raised her hands and the storm inside her answered; lightning crawled the walls, and two men went down smoking, and the others fell back around a corner screaming about witchcraft.

But behind them, more boots. Always more boots. And ahead, blocking the vault stair, stood one of the emissaries—tall, grey-robed, faceless under its hood except for a mouth that smiled too widely, holding a lantern of its own: Wren's lantern, stolen already from its niche, the flame inside bent sideways like a candle in wind.

"Return it," Vesper said, and thunder rolled through solid rock.

The emissary's mouth widened further. "The regent has sold it," it said, in a voice like paper tearing. "Sold to the winning side. Come and take it, storm-caller, if you can carry what it costs."

And Ilex saw it clearly then, the whole shape of the trap: the emissary wanted Vesper to lunge, to spend her storm recklessly in these lantern-lined halls where one stray bolt could crack a hundred vessels and spill a hundred souls into the usurper's waiting hands. It wanted her desperate. Desperate people were cheap.

So he did the thing he had sworn he wouldn't do. He stepped between them.

"Emissary," he said, and bowed, the way he'd been taught in the Ninth Choir, low and graceful and full of old court manners. "Before you bargain with her—you should hear what the regent actually promised. There's a clause. About the vaults. About *your* master's share." He let the lie bloom slowly, watching the hooded head tilt. "Surely you weren't told everything? They never tell the new allies everything. Ask me. I sang in this court for eleven years. I know where the bodies of treaties are buried."

The emissary hesitated. It was only a hesitation—a half-breath, a flicker of that too-wide mouth—but in the Undercourt a half-breath was a fortune, and Ilex spent it all.

"*Run,*" he breathed to Vesper, not turning his head. "The binding chamber. Now. Don't look back."

"Ilex—"

"*Now.*"

He heard her go—boots on stone, receding, the storm-light of her fading down the stair—and he kept talking, kept bowing, spinning clauses and counter-clauses out of thin air and eleven years of watching liars work, while the emissary's smile grew narrower and narrower and the boots behind them grew louder and louder.

When the soldiers finally rounded the corner, the emissary lifted one grey hand—not at Vesper's retreating back, but at him.

"The musician knows too much," it said pleasantly. "Hold him."

They held him. That was the phrase his mind chose later, absurdly, as the first blow took him across the mouth and the second took his knees and the third—he lost count somewhere in the marble floor, cheek against cold stone, blood filling the seam between tiles like ink filling an engraving. Through the ringing in his skull he could hear the fighting above, feel it through the floor: the armies pouring down, the court's ancient defenses waking, the whole mountain groaning toward solstice.

And beneath all of it, faint and steady, Wren's lantern singing as they carried it away from him—carrying it toward the binding chamber, where Vesper was running, where everything would be decided.

He laughed. It hurt to laugh. He did it anyway, blood on his teeth, because after eleven years of playing other people's songs, he had finally, at the end, written one true note of his own.

"Hold him," the emissary had said.

So they held him. But they could not hold the hour. Solstice was coming, and it was coming fast, and somewhere below, a storm-caller with half a plan and all of her heart was walking toward the binding with her hands open and the sky waiting inside her.

Ilex spat red onto the marble, grinned up at the hooded thing above him, and said the last defiant words he had in him:

"You're late. She's already won."
