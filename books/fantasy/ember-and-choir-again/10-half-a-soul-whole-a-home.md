## 10. Half a Soul, Whole a Home

The mountain let them go the way a fist lets go of something held too long — slowly, joint by joint, with an ache that outlasted the opening.

Vesper climbed. That was all there was to climbing: one handhold, then the next, then breath, then again. The smuggler road wound upward through the mountain's roots like a vein of old silver, and somewhere behind her Wren's boots scraped stone in the same rhythm, half a beat off, the way their footsteps had never matched even as children. She had missed that rhythm without knowing she'd missed it. Half a soul apparently still had room for such small hungers.

Her hands were wrong. She noticed it at every rest stop — the way they trembled not from cold but from *absence*, reaching for things that were no longer there. When she flexed her fingers she felt the storm inside her and felt, too, the clean-edged hollow where half of it used to live. It didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. Grief hurt. This was more like a room in her house she could no longer enter, whose door stood open forever onto darkness.

*You're staring again,* Wren signed, because Wren's voice had gone down into the vault with everything else, and Vesper had learned on the third day of climbing that if she looked at her sister's mouth when Wren tried to speak, something behind her ribs would crack in a way that had nothing to do with souls.

*I'm allowed,* Vesper signed back. *You're new.*

Wren laughed — a silent laugh, shoulders shaking, head thrown back against the dark stone — and the sound of nothing filled the passage better than any music Ilex had ever played.

They slept in a niche above the flooded galleries, and Vesper dreamed of the lullaby.

She had given it to the door. She knew that. She had pressed her mother's voice into the Fairweather door's hungry seam along with her palm, and the door had drunk it and remembered her name and opened. But dreams kept no ledger. In the dream her mother sat on the edge of a bed in the old house above Thornfell harbor, singing the four descending notes that meant *sleep now, the storm will keep watch*, and Vesper lay there listening and could not for the life of her recall whether she was hearing a memory or stealing one.

She woke with salt on her face and did not examine which sea it came from.

* * *

They came out through the crown of the mountain at dusk.

Not the usurper's breach — that gash had been sealed by the court itself after the binding broke, stone knitting over stone with a slowness that suggested offense rather than repair. The smugglers' exit was humbler: a chimney of broken slate that opened behind a shrine to some forgotten shepherd god, its niche long empty, its view anything but.

Vesper pulled herself over the last lip of rock and stopped.

Stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The whole spilled treasury of them, burning hard and close over the free cities' hills, each one a ward-flame rekindled, each one a debt discharged. The sky that had been dead black glass since the night the wards shattered now wheeled and glittered like something alive, and beneath it the land lay lit in silver-blue, and beneath *that*, far below and eastward, Thornfell's harbor lamps pricked out in their familiar crooked constellation.

Beside her, Wren went very still.

Then her sister raised one hand to the sky — not signing, just reaching, the gesture older than language — and Vesper understood that Wren was counting them. Checking. The way you check a thing you sold once and bought back at terrible cost, turning it in your hands, making sure it's real.

It was real. They had made it real. Half of Vester's soul burned up there now, or so the regent had claimed before the lanterns blazed and the court bowed its masked heads — half her storm scattered into the high wards, singing in the dark where stars should be. Some nights, Vesper suspected, she would look up and try to find herself.

"Which one are you?" she asked aloud, forgetting for a moment that Wren couldn't answer, and Wren pointed — deliberately, gravely, with great ceremony — at the brightest star low over Thornfell's harbor, and then at Vesper, and then shrugged elaborately, as if to say: *you tell me.*

Vesper laughed until the laughing turned into something else, and Wren held her while she shook, and the stars kept watch the way the lullaby always promised they would.

* * *

The free cities had rallied. That word arrived with the first travelers on the high road three days later — refugees going home, militia columns marching under the old banners the usurper had outlawed, salt-wives and smugglers and hedge-mages all moving with the particular fierce purpose of people who have seen the sky die and watched it come back. A carter gave them a ride as far as the river and wouldn't take coin. "Fairweather," he kept saying, squinting at Vesper's face, at the storm-gray eyes that half the countryside had heard described by now. "You're a Fairweather. My gran said the Fairweathers sold us all. Gran owed your family money, though." He spat thoughtfully over the wheel. "Debts cut both ways, seems."

Vesper said nothing. Wren, beside her, wrote in the dirt of the cart bed with one finger: *TELL HER GRAN WE'RE EVEN.*

The carter read it upside down, snorted, and drove on.

But the story ran ahead of them anyway, the way stories do, growing stranger at every telling: the storm-caller who split herself in the deep places, who paid the Undercourt in her own blood and lightning, who walked out of the mountain carrying her sister's ghost and left it lighter than she found it. By the time they reached the salt-roads above Thornfell, children were already playing at it, calling storms at the gulls with cupped hands and falling down dramatically in the shingle.

Vesper listened to a version told by a fishwife in which she had died, gloriously, and felt oddly light about it. Let the dead version have the glory. The living one just wanted her sister fed.

* * *

Thornfell had changed and hadn't.

The harbor smelled the same — brine and tar and the sweet rot of the kelp-rows — but the town wore its scars openly now: the burned quarter where the usurper's vanguard had made its example, the boarded customhouse, the ward-stone in the market square cracked clean through and left unpatched, a monument. People moved differently. Freer, Vesper thought, and then corrected herself: *decidedly*. As if everyone in Thornfell had spent the dark months learning exactly what they'd lose, and now carried the knowledge like ballast.

The Fairweather house stood above the harbor on its old foundations, shuttered, gull-haunted, the door swollen shut with nine years of weather. Vesper put her hand against the wood and waited to feel something — the door's hunger, the family debt coiled behind it, the weight of inheritance.

Nothing. Just wood. Just a house.

"It's only ours," she said, surprised. "That's all it ever was. We just kept paying for other people's versions of it."

Wren touched the doorframe where generations of Fairweather children had been measured in knife-scratches, found her own marks, her own height at seven years old, and pressed two fingers to them for a long moment. Then she stepped back and gestured at the whole sagging, salt-bitten edifice with the air of a queen inspecting troops: *well? Are we keeping it?*

"We're keeping it," Vesper said.

And meant it, and knew what it cost, and paid gladly. Because the letter had come up from the coast with the refugee boats, waiting for her at the harbormaster's — three letters, actually, sealed with wax from Saltmarsh, from the Grey Council at Corveil, from a university she'd once dreamed of attending before exile taught her what dreaming safely cost. All of them offering the same thing: the old life restored. Pardons, positions, a chair at Saltmarsh with her name on the door, honors, lectures, the free cities' gratitude formalized into comfort.

The old exile's bargain, inverted. Come back; be somebody again.

She burned the first two letters in the kitchen hearth that evening, and read the third twice, and burned it too, and watched the ash rise up the flue toward the star she'd decided was hers.

"I'm staying," she told Wren across the small fire. "Here. Not Saltmarsh. Not Corveil. Here, where the wards run thinnest and the debts ran deepest. Somebody has to teach this coast its own storms again." She turned her hand over and watched faint gray light gather in her palm, the remnant storm answering her mood like a dog answering a whistle. "Half is enough for that."

Wren reached across the table and took her hand — the storm-lit one — and held it steady until the light settled. Then, slowly, deliberately, she drew on the tabletop in spilled flour: *HALF OF YOU IS ENOUGH FOR ANYTHING.*

* * *

Ilex did not climb out with them.

He had stood at the foot of the last stair, in the blue glow of the relit lantern-corridors, with his ruined lute slung across his back and the court's masks watching from the shadows above, and he had smiled the sad, crooked smile of a man finally paying a bill he'd been dodging for years.

"Somebody has to teach this court its own songs again," he'd said, echoing words Vesper hadn't yet spoken — or perhaps she'd already spoken them, in the cellar, weeks ago, and he was only returning them with interest. "The regent's offering me the conservatory. The whole archive. Every song the Undercourt ever swallowed." He'd looked at Wren then, and whatever he saw in his sister-in-grief's face had seemed to settle him, the way ballast settles a boat. "I owe her a requiem. I owe this place a hundred years of stolen music. And you—" he'd touched Vesper's shoulder, lightly, a musician's touch, precise and brief— "you owe yourself a life. Go be half of something whole."

Now, on clear nights above Thornfell, Vesper sometimes felt it: a thread of melody rising out of the mountain's roots, thin and strange and beautiful, winding up through the stone the way the smuggler roads did. The town heard it too. Children danced to it without knowing why. Old women wept. And once — only once — Vesper caught a phrase in it she recognized, four notes descending, a lullaby the door was supposed to have eaten forever.

She wrote to him about it. He wrote back on paper the color of lantern-light: *Doors remember names. So do I. Keep listening.*

* * *

Winter came early that year, and kind.

On the solstice night — the first anniversary, though no one called it that — Vesper and Wren climbed the headland above the harbor where the old ward-stones stood in their crumbling ring, and Vesper did what she had begun doing every solstice, every equinox, every night the sky burned especially hard: she raised her arms and called the storm.

It came willingly, off the cold sea, full-throated, and she did not hoard it. That was the lesson the mountain had carved into what remained of her: storms are not for keeping. She let it break over the headland and through her, let the lightning find the old ward-stones and run the ring, let her half-soul pour itself out into the lattice the way it was designed to, generous, replenishing, *hers* — and overhead the stars flared brighter, all of them, the whole burning ledger balanced.

When it passed, Wren was sitting in the wet grass laughing her silent laugh, hair standing on end, alive, alive, alive.

And Vesper sat down beside her, soaked and emptied and more complete than she had ever been whole, and looked north along the coast road, where torches were coming — a column of them, a delegation, banners she could not yet make out, riding hard through the solstice dark toward Thornfell bearing, according to the breathless boy sent ahead on a lathered horse, *a summons, a warning, and a sealed writ with the Fairweather name on it again.*

Wren read her face and signed: *Again?*

Vesper stood, and the last rain ran off her like a blessing, and somewhere beneath their feet the mountain sang.

"Again," she said, and started down toward the lights.
