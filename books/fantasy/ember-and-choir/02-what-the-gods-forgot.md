## 2. What the Gods Forgot

The dead, in my experience, are terrible conversationalists. They repeat themselves. They fixate on the moment of their leaving the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. And they almost never—*never*—address me directly. I am, to them, a well-carved piece of furniture with an unfortunate talent for eavesdropping.

So when the drowned girl's voice said, "You can hear me," on my third night of carving her memorial bone, I did what any sensible craftsman would do.

I dropped the chisel on my foot and swore.

"Language," she said, prim as a schoolmistress, which was rich coming from someone who had apparently spent her final moments doing something reckless near water. Ghosts always know exactly how they died, even when they lie about everything else.

"I'm working," I told the bone. It was a fine piece of river-calf femur, pale as candlewax, and I was three days into the border work—a running pattern of reeds and small fish, because her mother had asked for something gentle, and grief will pay handsomely for gentle. "You're not supposed to talk to me."

"You're not supposed to hear me," she agreed. "Yet here we both are, breaking rules."

I set down the chisel properly this time and looked at her. She stood by the window of my workshop the way the dead do—not quite casting light, not quite casting shadow—with her hair dripping though there was no water anywhere near her, and her dress hem dark with it. Eight years old, maybe nine. The kind of face that makes you understand why people once invented gods.

"What's your name?" I asked, because the memorial commission had only said *a child lost to the river*, and names matter. Ask any ledger-keeper.

"Maren."

"Maren," I repeated, and reached for my logbook out of pure habit—the small one, the private one, where I record the dead who speak to me, dates and durations, like weather observations. "And what do you want from me?"

She considered this with the gravity of a child who has had time to think about very few things. "I want to go home. But I can't." Her dripping head tilted. "Someone sold the way home."

Now. I have kept the Vellum Guild's ledgers for eleven years. I have transcribed sales of bottled last breaths, of shadows cut free from their owners at a fair price, of years harvested from the dying and sold to the young and rich. I have recorded, in my neat clerk's hand, the purchase of a man's name so thoroughly that his own mother walked past him in the street. Nothing in that archive should have surprised me anymore.

But my hands went cold anyway. Because I knew—I *knew*, in the way you know a thing before you know you know it—that I was going to have to look up Maren Hale, drowned, age eight, and find out which entry carried her.

"The Guild doesn't sell souls," I said. "Souls aren't collateral. It's the first rule in the charter."

"It's the first rule in the *public* charter," Maren said, with the mild contempt children reserve for adults pretending things. "There's another book."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Aldous Crane's private ledger: officially, it does not exist. Unofficially, every senior clerk knows it exists, and none of us has ever seen it, and all of us have entered transactions into it—or rather, into the courier slips that vanish into Crane's tower office and never come back. It is the Guild's one true book of accounts, and it is kept the way dragons keep gold: absolutely, and for no one.

The morning after Maren spoke to me, I forged a requisition slip. This is the sort of sentence that would have made my younger self faint. Eleven years of careful invisibility, of being the man who fetches ink and aligns columns, of being so reliably dull that three separate Guildmasters have forgotten my face between visits—and now I was forging documents to access the restricted archive under the pretext of a routine audit of pre-Consolidation holdings.

My hand didn't even shake. That frightened me more than anything else.

The restricted archive smells of tallow and cold stone. The archivist, a woman named Pell who has been old since before I was born, looked at my slip, looked at me, and looked at the slip again.

"Winterbourne," she said. "Didn't know you could write anything but columns."

"Columns are just words that gave up on ambition," I said, and she almost smiled, and let me through.

Crane's private ledger is not shelved like other books. It lives in a locked case, and the lock is not a lock but a *promise*—an old god's seal, from back when gods still answered, worked into the iron. You don't pick it. You either have the right to open it or you don't.

But I am a bone-carver, and bones remember. And the case was made of pressed horn and glue over a frame of—yes—bone. Old bone. I put my palm flat against it, the way I do when listening to a skull for the shape of the life it held, and I asked, silently, the way I ask the dead, *who sealed you?*

The bone answered the way bone answers: not in words, but in weight. A name. A date. And beneath it, faint as a watermark, the smell of a river in summer.

Maren Hale. Sold. Not her body, not her memory, not her ghost—her *soul*. Entered into Crane's private ledger fourteen months ago, transaction type: *collateral, long-term*. Counterparty: the Court Beneath the Mountain.

Counterparty: a place that appears in our records exactly four times in two hundred years, each entry written in a different clerk's hand, each clerk, I realized with a slow roll of nausea, subsequently retired to the country and never heard from again.

I copied the entry reference number onto my cuff in pencil, because I am a coward even in rebellion, and I got out of the archive before my legs finished understanding what they'd learned.

* * *

The city, meanwhile, had gone mad. Politely, at first, then entirely.

It started, I think, with the cobbler on Tanner's Row whose prayers to Vessa-of-the-Threshold were answered with a door that opened onto his dead wife's kitchen. Then the widow on Candle Street who asked Saint Ammen for rain and received a flood localized entirely to her enemy's roof. Small miracles, badly aimed, like a giant waking up and trying to be gentle.

By the third day, the temples couldn't hold the crowds. People who hadn't prayed since childhood knelt in gutters and shouted upward. Street preachers who'd been ignored for decades suddenly drew hundreds, their voices cracking with vindication. Someone burned an effigy of the Consistory's chief rationalist in Coppermarket, and the crowd sang while it caught.

You could smell hope everywhere, and hope, when it's been starved for generations, smells like panic.

I walked through it with my collar up, feeling the dead press close around me—not the quiet ambient murmur I've lived with all my life, but *urgent*, dozens of voices overlapping, the dead leaning toward the living like spectators at a fire. Because if the gods were answering, the dead wanted to know: what about us? What about the ones already sold, already filed, already entered?

"They're waking up," Maren said, walking beside me through the crowd, invisible to everyone but me, dripping on the cobblestones. "The old ones. Under the mountain too. That's why he sold me *now*. Souls are worth more when the court's doors are about to open."

"Doors open when?"

"Equinox." She said it simply, the way children say terrible things. "Then they shut for a hundred years. He's trading fast."

I stopped in the middle of Coppermarket, in the crush of desperate hopefuls, and felt something crack cleanly inside me—the sound, I imagine, of a ledger spine breaking.

Eleven years. Eleven years of telling myself that transcription is not complicity, that keeping accounts is not making them, that a man who writes down a sale did not make the sale. Eleven years of invisibility as a moral strategy, as if evil done neatly in columns somehow stays contained within them.

A child's soul. In a book. With my Guild's seal on the contract, and my handwriting somewhere in the supporting documentation, I'd bet my life on it—it usually is.

"You're angry," Maren observed.

"I'm recalculating," I said.

"That's what angry looks like on clerks?"

"*Yes,*" I said, with more force than I intended, and a flower-seller nearby gave me a look of profound concern.

That night I went back to the workshop and did not sleep. I sat with Maren's half-finished memorial bone under the lamp and turned the problem over like a carving gone wrong, looking for the line where I could split it clean.

Steal the file. Warn someone. Burn the archive. Every option ended the same way: seen. Known. Hunted. Eleven years of safety traded for a dead girl's way home, and no guarantee at the end of it except that I would never again be able to pretend the ledgers were someone else's sin.

"There's a priestess," Maren said, watching me carve. "Defrocked. Sera Voss. Her temple's god went silent when she was young. She comes to the Guild sometimes, asking after records of the old rites. She believes things. Even now. Even after."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you'll need her," Maren said. "And because—" she hesitated, and for a moment she sounded less like a schoolmistress and more like an eight-year-old who had been alone in the dark for fourteen months, "—because I'm scared, and you're the first person who's heard me since the river, and I don't want to do this part alone either."

The chisel stopped in my hand.

I am not a brave man. Let the record show—I'm a clerk; records are rather my whole profession—that Edmund Winterbourne, bone-carver and keeper of impossible debts, chose his next step not out of courage but because a drowned child asked him not to leave her alone with it.

"Tomorrow," I said. "We find the priestess."

"And after that?"

"After that," I said, reaching for my logbook to enter the first honest entry of my career—*Maren Hale, age eight, sold; recovery pending*—"we audit a god."

Outside my window, far off toward the mountain, something vast exhaled, and every candle in the city guttered at once, and the praying crowd roared like a tide coming in.
