## 3. A Priestess Without a God

I had not set foot inside the Vellum Guild in thirty-one years, and I want it noted that I did not tremble on the threshold. My hands were steady. My voice, when I gave my name to the door-warden, was level as a ruled line. If my heart was doing something undignified in my chest, that was between me and whatever god was listening—and for most of my life, the answer to that question had been *no one*.

The warden consulted a ledger of his own. Everything in this building was a ledger; I suspected the drains were itemized.

"State your business," he said.

"I've come to read records."

"Records of what?"

"Prayers," I said. "Answered ones."

He looked at me the way men look at women who mention gods in public—pity with a garnish of suspicion. Thirty years ago, when I wore the grey-and-gold of the Choir of the Quiet Flame, that look would have been deference. Now it was the look you give a beggar who claims to be a general. I let him look. I'd had practice.

"The Guild does not deal in prayers."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "You deal in what people will trade for them. Which is the same market, only better dressed."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the small alarm of a man who has just realized the madwoman at the gate is quoting Guild doctrine back at him. He went to fetch someone with more authority and less sense.

While I waited, I looked up at the façade, because some habits survive defrocking. The Guild's motto was carved over the doors in letters meant to be read from horseback: *ALL THINGS HAVE A PRICE.* Beneath it, older and half-effaced, someone had chiseled something else, centuries ago, before the Guild grew polite: *AND SOME THINGS KNOW IT.* I had always liked that second line. It had the honesty of graffiti.

The signs had begun three weeks ago. I want to be clear about this: I am not a woman who mistakes coincidence for miracle. I spent twenty years watching a congregation mistake both, and then twenty more watching nothing at all. When the well-water in the Tanner's Quarter turned sweet overnight, I noted it and moved on. When old Widow Casselin's prayer for rain was answered within the hour—a thing she told everyone, loudly, in the market—I assumed weather. Weather happens. But then the fevered child on Candle Street woke singing a hymn no one had taught her, one from the rite of the Quiet Flame itself, a hymn whose last living singer was standing in the market buying turnips and trying not to weep into them.

That was when I stopped moving on.

Gods do not come back all at once. They come back the way feeling returns to a frostbitten hand—painfully, and starting at the extremities. The extremities of divinity are the desperate: the widow, the fevered child, the drunk who swears the gutter spoke to him. And where answered prayers pool, the Guild arrives with contracts. I knew their methods from the inside; my temple had sold its reliquaries to them in the lean years, one saint's fingerbone at a time, until there was nothing left to hock but the silence itself.

So here I was. Asking to see the books.

* * *

The man they sent was not what I expected, which is to say he was exactly what I should have expected and wasn't: a clerk. Grey, quiet, ink-stained at the second finger of his right hand, with the particular stillness of a man who has made himself furniture so thoroughly that even he sometimes forgets he's a person. He introduced himself as Edmund Winterbourne, keeper of minor accounts, and led me through corridors lined with shelves, and I watched him watch me, and neither of us said anything true for the length of three hallways.

It was in the reading room that he finally slipped.

I asked for transaction records touching on votive offerings, and he pulled them without checking an index—which meant he already knew where they were, which meant he had already been looking. I said as much, mildly, the way you'd note a stain on a collar.

His pen stopped moving.

"You'll forgive me," I said, "but I recognize the posture. That's the posture of a man reading something he isn't supposed to have found yet."

"Madam—" he began.

"Sera," I said. "Formerly Sister Sera of the Quiet Flame, formerly of anything at all. You needn't perform discretion at me. I was defrocked by a council of nine men who couldn't agree on whether silence was a message or an absence, so I promise you my standards for institutional honesty are subterranean."

And there it was—not a smile, exactly, but the loosening around the mouth that precedes one, quickly suppressed, like a man catching himself about to laugh at a funeral. He glanced at the doorway. Then he turned the ledger on the table toward me, and tapped one entry with an ink-dark finger.

"Three weeks ago," he said quietly, "the Guild recorded a sale. A votive flame, bottled. Buyer anonymous. The seller claimed it had 'rekindled itself.' The Guild paid four hundred crowns for what any priest could have told them was impossible, because the Guild pays for impossibilities and files them under anomalies." He paused. "There are eleven such entries now. Eleven. In three weeks. In sixty years prior, there were none."

I looked at the entries. My hands were steady again, which surprised me, because inside I was a bell being struck.

"They're coming back," I said. It came out flat, which is how I say the largest things.

"It appears so."

"And your masters are pricing them."

"The Guild prices everything." A pause, and then, with the air of a man stepping off a ledge: "I don't think they understand what they're pricing. I don't think *anyone* understands it yet. Prayer hasn't worked in generations. The theology of it is—" he stopped, embarrassed.

"Rust," I supplied. "Yes. I'm aware. I'm what's left of the theology."

He looked at me properly then, and I saw him make the calculation I'd seen priests make a hundred times: *this woman knows things I need, and needs things I know.* An alliance of the abandoned. I'd have laughed if it weren't so precisely true.

"What were you looking for," I asked, "before I arrived? In these records. Don't insult us both by denying it."

The clerk—Edmund—was quiet long enough that I heard the building breathing around us, the rustle of pages in distant rooms, the Guild digesting its inventory. Then he said, "A girl. A dead girl. She—" and stopped, and I watched a man decide, visibly, painfully, whether to be a person or a filing system.

"She speaks to you," I said.

His head came up sharply. "How could you possibly—"

"Because you flinched at the word *girl* the way only the haunted flinch. And because I spent twenty years listening to a god who wouldn't answer, and I know what it does to a person when something dead finally talks back." I folded my hands. "Start at the beginning, clerk. I have nowhere else to be, which is either a tragedy or a great professional advantage, depending on the day."

* * *

He told me. Not all of it—I could see the shape of what he withheld, the way you can see the shape of furniture under a sheet—but enough. A drowned child named Maren. A voice in his workshop. A trail of entries leading upward through the Guild's hierarchy toward a name he said the way you'd say the name of a disease: *Crane.*

"Aldous Crane," I repeated. "The Guildmaster himself sells a child's soul beneath the mountain, in the same season the gods wake? That's not greed, clerk. That's timing. That's a man who knew the tide was coming and built his boat early."

"You believe that? You believe he *knew*?"

"I believe powerful men always know the tide before the rest of us. It's the one prayer they never stop receiving." I stood, joints announcing themselves, and paced the length of the reading room. "Here's what matters. If Crane sold a soul to the court under the mountain, he didn't do it for money. Souls are currency down there, yes—but up here they're leverage. He's bought himself a seat at a table where the guests are about to include gods. And the equinox—"

"—seals the court for a century," he finished. "I know. I've been reading the old accounts. They're inconsistent, but the date recurs."

"Then we have weeks." I turned to face him. "Understand what I'm proposing, because I'm about to propose it and I'd rather you refuse with full information. I am a disgraced priestess with no temple, no order, and no god—or none that admits to knowing me. You are a clerk who hears the dead and has spent his life pretending otherwise. Between us we have one working skill each: I remember how worship works, and you remember where the bodies are filed. I am suggesting we commit crimes together."

For a moment he simply looked at me, and I thought: *he'll refuse. They always refuse. Safety is a religion too, and he's devout.*

But what he said was: "The ledgers. If we move against Crane, the ledgers are the battlefield. Every claim the Guild holds—including, I suspect, whatever contract binds that girl—is written there. Ink is the whole architecture."

"Ink burns," I said.

Something moved behind his eyes at that—fear, or its cousin, hope. Before he could answer, the reading room door opened without a knock, and the temperature of the world changed.

* * *

I had never met Aldous Crane. I knew him the way everyone knew him: silver-haired, silver-tongued, the man who had doubled the Guild's holdings in a decade and smiled in every portrait as though the painter owed him money. What the portraits failed to convey was the quality of his attention. When Crane looked at you, you felt *appraised*—weighed, valued, entered into some inner book with a price assigned. It was like being measured for a coffin by a very friendly undertaker.

"Lady Okonkwo." He pronounced my full name correctly, including the parts I'd stopped using. "Or shall I say Sister? Forgive me—I've read your file. Fascinating reading. The council's findings against you were, I've always thought, unjust. Silence is not proof of absence. Any honest theologian knows that."

"Any honest theologian also knows the Guild doesn't employ them," I said. "Master Crane. You keep remarkable files on the unimportant."

"On the *potentially* important," he corrected gently. "There is so little difference, these days." His gaze slid past me to Edmund, and warmed by a degree that somehow made it colder. "Winterbourne. Showing our guest the anomaly ledgers? How industrious. Do give her the full picture—transparency is the soul of good business." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Of course, some accounts remain private. House policy. I trust you've respected it."

Edmund said, "Of course, Master Crane," in a voice so smooth I nearly applauded, and I understood in that instant that the mild clerk had survived decades in this building by being underestimated, and that Crane—damn him—might be the one man who had never quite believed it.

"Well." Crane clasped his hands, radiating benevolence. "Lady Okonkwo, the Guild is always pleased to assist students of the divine. Whatever records you require—within reason—are at your disposal. Consider it an act of faith on my part." His smile deepened by a millimeter. "After all, with the gods returning, faith may soon become our most valuable commodity. I'd hate to see yours wasted on the wrong investments."

He wished us a productive afternoon and withdrew, and the room exhaled.

"He knows," Edmund said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"He suspects. There's a difference, and it's the only difference we have left." I gathered my coat, my mind already running ahead of me down dark streets and darker stairways. "Clerk—Edmund. That file you're hiding. The girl's. How long do you think it stays hidden, now that he's looked at you twice?"

I watched him arrive at the answer, and I watched the safe, invisible life he'd built close around him like a door.

"Not long," he said.

"Then finish telling me about Maren," I said, "and tell it fast. Because I think, my friend, that we are about to become very visible indeed."
