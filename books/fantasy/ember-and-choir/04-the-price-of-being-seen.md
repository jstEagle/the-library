## 4. The Price of Being Seen

There is a particular quality to the silence in Aldous Crane's private office, and I had spent eleven years learning to read it the way sailors read weather. Most days it was the silence of a man who trusted his ledgers more than his staff, which suited me fine. A clerk who is not watched is a clerk who can breathe.

That evening, the silence was different. It was the silence of a trap that has already been sprung, and is merely waiting for you to notice.

"Edmund." Crane did not look up from the letter he was sealing. Wax the color of dried blood, his signet pressed into it with the unhurried precision of a man who has never once needed to redo anything. "Close the door."

I closed the door. There are certain instructions one does not weigh, and an order from the Guildmaster is chief among them, right after *don't lick the ledger-ink* and *never count aloud in front of the appraisers*.

"You've been with us eleven years," he said. "Four months. Sixteen days."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

"Do you know why I remember the exact figure?"

Because you are a man who keeps accounts of everything, including people, I thought. Aloud: "I couldn't say, sir."

"I remember because you were the only applicant who didn't lie to me." He set down the seal and finally looked at me, and I felt the way a specimen must feel when the lepidopterist's pin goes through it—pinned, displayed, and somehow still expected to be flattered about it. "Everyone else told me they wanted to serve wonder. You told me you wanted quiet work and no questions. It was the most honest thing anyone said to me that year, so I hired you on the spot."

"I'm glad my cowardice was useful, sir."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat checked. "And yet lately you've been asking questions. Quietly. In your careful little hand. The file for lot forty-one, the Vellum girl. Pulled twice. Cross-referenced against the deep vaults. And here's what interests me, Edmund: you did it all correctly. Every form. Every authorization. Which means either you're the most scrupulous thief in the city's history, or you genuinely believe paperwork will protect you."

"It usually does," I said.

"Not from me."

The lamp between us guttered, though there was no draft. I want to state for the record that my hands did not shake. My hands have carved memorial bones for drowned children without shaking. They shook anyway, somewhere deeper than the hands, in whatever part of a man does the actual living.

"You knew," I said. "About me. About what I hear."

"I've known since your second week." He rose and came around the desk, and up close he smelled of wax and cold iron and something older underneath, like a cellar that had been a grave first. "You transcribed the sale of a storm-bottled breath, and while you copied the buyer's name your lips moved. Not reading-mouthing. Talking. To someone standing just left of your shoulder, where nobody stood. And you apologized to them. For the price."

"The dead complain about the invoices," I said. "It seemed polite to agree."

"And instead of reporting it—" he spread his hands, magnanimous as a cat presenting a dead bird, "—I gave you eleven years of quiet. No questions. No assignments near the necropoli. Your own room above the counting-house. Because a man who hears the dead is worth ten auditors, Edmund, and I am nothing if not patient with my investments."

*Investment.* Of course. Even my invisibility had been an entry in his books. Somewhere in some private ledger of his there was presumably a column headed *Winterbourne, E.* with figures beside it, and tonight he had come to collect.

"The gods are answering again," Crane said. He said it the way other men announce a war or a wedding—as though the world had simply been waiting on his schedule. "Do you understand what that means? For centuries worship was a dead market. Faith sat in vaults like spoiled grain. But now—" his eyes had a light in them I recognized, because I'd seen it in buyers' eyes over impossible things, "—now every prayer is a transaction waiting for a broker. And I intend to be that broker. But the old contracts need auditing first. Every soul ever sold. Every debt ever filed." He paused, delicately. "Including lot forty-one."

"Maren Vellum," I said. "She was nine years old."

"She was an asset."

"She was a girl who liked to skip stones and hum off-key, and her father sold her soul to pay a debt of four crowns and a goat, and your Guild took it, and she has been standing behind me for three days asking me to bring her home." My voice stayed level. Eleven years of practice. Inside, something was coming apart with a sound like ice on a thawing river. "So no, Guildmaster. I won't be surrendering her file."

Crane sighed—the sigh of a reasonable man confronted with unreasonable sentiment—and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives too late to be useful, that I had just answered the only question that mattered.

"I had hoped," he said, "to offer you partnership. A seat at the table when the new age opens. Authorship, even. Men like us, Edmund—we don't get offered authorship often." He straightened his cuffs. "But I find partnerships require a certain alignment of interests, and yours appear to have wandered off with a dead child." He rang a small bell on his desk, once. "You'll want to leave by the back stairs. The front ones have appraisers on them, and appraisers gossip."

"Why let me go?"

"Because I'm curious what you'll do," he said simply. "And because, Edmund—" and here he smiled at last, fully, warmly, and it was the worst thing I saw all night, "—everything you take from these offices is already mine. You're carrying my property out of my building. I know exactly where my property goes."

---

I did not go out the back stairs. I went out the window, which is a sentence I never expected to write about myself, and down the drainpipe, which I will deny to my grave if my grave gives me the chance, and through the counting-house yard where the night-soil cart made its rounds, because dignity is a luxury for men whose Guildmasters haven't just smiled at them.

Sera was waiting where I'd told her, in the shadow of the tannery wall, wrapped in a defrocked priestess's idea of anonymity, which is to say a hood and an expression of permanent disapproval.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've just climbed out a window."

"That would explain the smell." She fell into step beside me, and I handed her the file—Maren's file, pulled properly, authorized, every stamp in its place, because if the world was going to end I intended to end it procedurally. She read it by the light of a guttering lamp some drunk had abandoned, and her face went through several weathers. "Four crowns and a goat."

"The goat appreciated in value afterward. They billed the estate for the difference."

"Your Guild," Sera said, "is going to burn."

"That's the plan," I said. "Eventually. First we run."

We ran. Or rather, we walked very fast in the manner of people who have learned that running attracts eyes, down through the tanners' rows where the air itself seemed pickled, past the chandlers' quarter where the candles burned green for the newly dead, and down, always down, into the bone-market.

You will have heard of the bone-market, or you will have heard lies about it, which amount to the same thing. It lives beneath the Shambles like a pearl inside an oyster that hates it: stalls of yellowed ivory and polished femur, carvers hawking memorial beads, whisper-merchants selling fingerbones of saints whose sainthood is disputed by everyone who ever met them. The dead come here too—or their echoes do, the way heat lingers in a stone after sunset. It is the one place in the city where I am not strange. Here, everyone talks to someone who isn't visibly present.

I bought passage from a stall-keeper who owed me—for years I'd carved her mother's memorial plate at cost, because grief should not carry a surcharge—and she looked at Sera, and at Maren's file under my arm, and at my face, and asked no questions, which in the bone-market is the highest form of courtesy.

"There's a god in the gutters," she said, sliding two tokens of knucklebone across the counter. "Under the aqueduct, where the prayers wash down. If you're running from the Vellum man, you'll want roads he doesn't own."

"Is there such a thing?" Sera asked.

"No," the stall-keeper said. "But there are roads he hasn't gotten around to."

We went down past the last stalls, where the market frays into tunnels that predate the city, predate perhaps the idea of cities, and the dark came down around us like a held breath. And I want to tell you honestly what I felt, because this account deserves honesty even if nothing else in my life has managed it:

I was terrified. Properly terrified, teeth-and-kidneys terrified, the terror of a man who has spent eleven years being furniture and has just discovered furniture can be broken. Crane knew. Crane had always known. Every quiet year had been a leash measured out slowly, and I had mistaken the slack for freedom, and now the leash had run out and somewhere behind us a patient man was checking his watch.

And underneath the terror—God forgive me, underneath it—was something bright and reckless and entirely new.

I was seen. Truly seen, catalogued and valued and hunted, and it was the most alive I had felt since childhood. The dead see me, but the dead forgive easily; they've larger concerns. Crane's gaze had weight and appetite, and I had stepped into it deliberately, and some traitor part of me sang about it.

"Stop smiling," Sera said. "It's unsettling."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're absolutely smiling. We're fugitives in a sewer and you look like a man who's just been promoted."

"Perhaps I have," I said. "From invisible to hunted. It comes with better benefits."

"What benefits?"

"None whatsoever. That's what makes it a promotion."

She snorted—a defrocked priestess, laughing in the dark—and the sound bounced along the tunnel walls and came back changed, the way sounds do down there, the way everything does.

Then the laughter died in both our throats at once, because ahead of us, where the aqueduct's drip fed a channel of black water, something small was sitting in the wet stone. Small and stooped and ancient, with eyes like banked coals that had been waiting a very long time for someone to bring them wood.

It raised its head. And it said, in a voice like the last ember of a fire that had once warmed a civilization:

"*You came looking. Nobody has come looking in three hundred years.*"

* * *

Behind us, far above, I heard—or imagined I heard, though with me the distinction has never meant much—a bell ringing in the counting-house. Once. Twice.

Crane, calling in his debts.

I tightened my grip on Maren's file and stepped toward the ember-eyed dark, taking the first true step of a life I had not planned, could not audit, and would not, whatever it cost me, surrender.
