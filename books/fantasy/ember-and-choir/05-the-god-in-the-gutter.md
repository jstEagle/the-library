## 5. The God in the Gutter

I had been a priestess for eleven years and a defrocked one for thirty-two, and I will tell you which of those professions taught me more about the divine: the second, by a country mile.

The temple taught me that gods are tidy. They live in marble, they eat incense, they answer prayers on a schedule posted by the acolytes' guild. The street taught me otherwise. The street taught me that divinity is like water — it goes where it's needed, pools in the low places, and smells terrible when people dump things into it.

Which is why, three nights after fleeing the Vellum Guild with Edmund Winterbourne and a stolen file full of one dead girl's worth of paperwork, I found myself kneeling in a gutter in the Saltmarket district, talking to what appeared to be a pile of rags with opinions.

"You're sure this is him," I said. Not to the pile. To Edmund, who stood behind me holding a candle stub like it was a holy relic, which, given our circumstances, it nearly was.

"The dead say so." Edmund's voice was quiet as always, careful as always, the voice of a man who has spent his whole life trying not to be noticed by anything, living or otherwise. "They call him the Old Man of the Crossroads. They leave him buttons."

"Buttons?"

"Dead children used to pray with buttons. Before coins. He keeps them." A pause. "He has a lot of buttons."

I looked at the pile of rags again. Up close — or as close as I was willing to get, which was perhaps four feet, because I am devout but not stupid — it resolved into a man. Or the shape of a man, the way a footprint resolves into a foot long after the walker has gone. He was thin the way winter is thin. His skin had the grey patience of old stone. And his eyes, when they opened, were the eyes of something that had once been enormous and had learned, through long practice, to fold itself small enough to survive.

"Well," he said. His voice was dry leaves and door hinges. "A priestess. It's been a while since one of you knelt in my gutter. Usually you're too busy telling people I don't exist."

"I don't tell people anything," I said. "I'm not licensed anymore."

"No," he agreed, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than mockery. "You wouldn't be. They took your god and then they took your title, and left you standing in an empty building wondering if any of it was ever real." He smiled, and his teeth were the color of old ivory — bone-carver's ivory, I thought, and glanced at Edmund, who had gone very still. "I remember your temple, Sera of the Choir of Ashes. You sang well. Your god liked you best of all of them, you know. That's why it hurt so much when he stopped answering."

The gutter was suddenly very cold against my knees. I hadn't realized I'd knelt. Old habits.

"We need the roads under the mountain," I said, because grief is no excuse for poor negotiation. "The corpse-roads. The ones only the dead remember."

"And why would I show you those?"

"Because we're going to steal a soul back from the court beneath, and because—" I hesitated, because the next part was true and true things are expensive. "Because nobody else remembers you either. And I think being forgotten is its own kind of death, and I think you'd rather not finish dying."

The rag-shape was silent for a long moment. Somewhere down the street, a drunk was singing a hymn to a god who had answered his prayer about a sick cow last week and had said nothing since about his drinking, which told you everything about how the returning gods chose their miracles: selectively, and with an eye toward publicity.

"You want the roads," the old god said at last. "Fine. I know them. I knew them before the court beneath the mountain had a name, back when the dead walked their own way home and didn't need tolls and queens and contracts." His face darkened, if a face made of weather could darken. "But nothing is free. Not even from me. *Especially* not from me, because unlike the Guild, I can't print my own currency."

"Name your price," I said. "We haven't got much. Edmund has a candle and a guilty conscience. I have a heresy conviction and forty years of resentment."

"I don't want your money." Those stone-grey eyes fixed on me, and I felt them go through me the way water goes through cloth. "I want devotion. True devotion. Freely given. One worshipper, one prayer, meant honestly, from the bottom of whatever's left of your faith." He held up a hand before I could speak. "And before you offer to fake it — don't. I've been starving for six hundred years, priestess. I know the difference between bread and a picture of bread. A false prayer would pass through me like light through glass, and I'd be hungrier after than before. It has to be real. It has to cost you something."

* * *

Edmund and I argued about it in the room above the tannery where we were hiding, which smelled the way you'd expect, and which I had privately decided to think of as a monastery, for morale purposes.

"He's asking you to believe in him again," Edmund said. He sat on the floor with Maren's file open across his knees, though he wasn't reading it. He read it the way other men touch wounds. "After everything. After your god went silent. After they stripped your orders. After thirty years of—"

"Of what? Go on. Say it."

"Of being wrong," he said gently. "That's what you're afraid of, isn't it? Not that he'll refuse us. That if you kneel and mean it, and he takes it, you'll have proven that faith was worth the price you paid for losing it."

I threw a boot at him. He caught it, which annoyed me further; I hadn't taken him for a man with quick hands, but bone-carvers are precise by trade.

"It's not that simple."

"It never is. But Sera—" He set the boot down and looked at me with those ledger-keeper's eyes, the ones that saw entries everywhere, debts and credits, costs and balances. "You've spent thirty years being angry at a god who stopped answering. That's still a relationship. Anger is just love with the receipts attached. I would know. I keep books."

"That is the most depressing thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once heard a funeral sermon delivered by a man who was actively embezzling from the widow."

"And yet." He shrugged. "You talk about him constantly. The god who left. You know his silence better than you know your own heartbeat. Maybe the question isn't whether you can believe in something that small and broken and gutter-dwelling." He nodded at the window, toward the Saltmarket, toward the crossroads where the Old Man kept his buttons. "Maybe the question is whether you ever stopped."

I wanted to hit him again. Instead I did what I always do when cornered by truth: I went to bed early and lay awake until dawn composing rebuttals.

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, not the inquisitors, not the tribunal that defrocked me, not the kind landlady in Coppersmith Row who left me soup during the bad winter:

I never stopped praying.

Not properly. Oh, I told myself I had. I told everyone I had. I gave eloquent interviews about the death of faith and the honesty of unbelief, and I meant every word the way you mean a eulogy — sincerely, and from a safe distance. But every night, lying in whatever bed I had, I would find my lips moving. The old litanies. The dawn-blessing. The prayer for travelers, which is the one I said for my mother, and the prayer for the dead, which is the one I said for my brother, and the small wordless one at the end that isn't in any liturgy and doesn't have a name, the one that is just *are you there* repeated until sleep took pity on me.

Thirty-two years of *are you there*, addressed to a silence.

People assume unanswered prayer breaks faith like a bone. It doesn't. It breaks it like a branch — bent and bent and bent, until you forget it was ever straight, and then someone asks you to trust it with weight and you discover, to your horror, that it never snapped at all. It just grew crooked around the waiting.

So on the third night, I went back to the gutter alone, because some negotiations are not committee work, and I knelt down in the wet and the muck beside a god wearing six hundred years of other people's discarded clothing, and I said:

"I'm not doing this because it's strategic."

"I know," he said. "Strategic prayer tastes like vinegar. Get on with it."

"I'm doing it because—" And here my voice did something humiliating, cracked like a girl's, and I let it, because he had asked for true devotion freely given and apparently that included the undignified parts. "Because I am so tired of talking to nothing. Because I sang to your kind for eleven years and I have been singing to an empty chair for thirty-two and I cannot stop singing, it's the only part of me they couldn't take, and if you are anything at all — if there is one spark left in all that ruin — then hear me now: I see you. Small and filthy and forgotten. I see you. And I would rather worship a beggar-god who answers than a golden god who doesn't, and I would rather be wrong with company than right alone, and *please*. Please. My friend needs the roads under the mountain, and a child needs her soul back, and I need—" 

My throat closed.

"I need to know the singing wasn't wasted."

Silence. The long, awful, familiar silence, the one I knew better than my own name.

And then the old god moved, unfolded, rose up out of the rags like smoke rising out of a doused fire, and he was not large — that was the thing, he was still so terribly small, a stooped grey man with button-bright eyes — but when he put his hand on my head, I felt it. Warmth. Faint as an ember in a dead hearth, faint as the last note of a hymn in an empty nave, but *warm*, and aimed at me, and real.

"Oh," I said. Stupidly. Tears running into the gutter-muck. "Oh, you're *there*."

"Barely," he said, and his dry-leaf voice had gone soft. "Barely. But yes. Thank you, priestess. Six hundred years, and you're the first one to ask instead of demand." He drew his hand back and looked at it as though surprised it still worked. "The roads are yours. All three of you. And Sera of the Choir of Ashes — when this is done, if any of us survives it, come find me again. I find I'd rather like being sung to. Even badly."

"I sing beautifully."

"You sing like a woman arguing with heaven," he said, "which is the only kind of hymn I ever trusted."

* * *

We gathered at the crossroads at moonset — Edmund pale and clutching his candle, myself wrung out and strangely light, like a house after the furniture's been sold but before the emptiness sets in. The old god stood among his buttons in the gutter-water and began, without ceremony, to teach us the way down.

"There are three roads beneath the mountain," he said. "The Road of Regret, which shows you what you lost. The Road of Accounts, which shows you what you owe. And the Road of Names, which shows you who you are — and mind, that last one is the dangerous one, because most people have never actually met themselves and come away pleased." His button-eyes glinted. "The court sits where all three meet. And you should know, before we take a single step, that the queen below has recently had a visitor. A pleasant gentleman. Very well-dressed. Smelled of vellum and ink."

Edmund went white. "Crane."

"He struck a bargain," the old god said, watching Edmund with something like pity. "Days ago. And whatever he promised them, he paid for it with something he had no right to sell." He turned, and the shadows of the crossroads seemed to lean toward him, listening, the way they never lean toward the living. "Now. Hold your candles high, keep your regrets close, and whatever you see on the Road of Accounts — do not stop to balance it. We walk. Are you ready?"

I looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at me. Somewhere beneath our feet, miles down, a dead girl was waiting to be carried home.

"As we'll ever be," I said.

And the gutter opened like a mouth, and we went down.
