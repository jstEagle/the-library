## 6. Descent by Candlelight

The corpse-road began behind a wall.

Not through it—*behind* it, in the way that a word has a meaning behind its spelling. Vessel pressed his palm against the mortared stones of a tannery's back fence and said, "Knock," and when none of us did, he sighed the sigh of a god who had spent three centuries explaining things to people who thought they already knew better, and knocked himself. The wall opened like a mouth that had been waiting politely to be asked.

"Only the dead remember this road," Vessel said. "Fortunately for you two, I am extremely old and have an excellent memory for grudges."

"That's not reassuring," Sera said.

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be accurate. Reassurance is a different product entirely, and I no longer stock it." He ducked through the gap, his beggar's coat brushing stone, and the candle he carried—a stub of tallow he'd produced from somewhere about his person with the shameless sleight of hand common to deities—bent its flame toward the dark as if greeting a relative.

I went in last, because I had spent eleven years going in last, entering rooms after Crane, leaving them before him, arranging my life so that doors were always between me and whatever was about to happen. Old habits. But Maren's voice was at my shoulder, thin as thread: *It's cold down there, Mr. Winterbourne.* And I thought: she is twelve years old and dead and walking into the dark ahead of me, so perhaps I could manage to walk into it too.

The road itself was paved with something I chose not to examine too closely. It sloped down at the exact angle of a held breath. Behind us, the city's noise thinned to a rumor, then to nothing, and what replaced it was worse: the sound of a great many people underground being very quiet on purpose.

"Rules," Vessel said, without turning around. He held up fingers. "One: eat nothing offered. Two: give nothing asked for outright—trade only, always trade, even if you're trading insults. Three—" here he paused, and the candle flame shivered, "—when they ask you to account for yourself, and they will, do not lie. They can smell a false entry the way I can smell incense. Which is to say, from a very long way off, and with complicated feelings."

"And if we do lie?" Sera asked.

"Then the road keeps you. There are sections of this tunnel that used to be people. I try not to step on them. It feels rude."

Sera walked a little straighter after that, which I noted with the detached professional interest of a man cataloguing his own doom. She had been defrocked for twenty-nine years and still carried herself like a woman whose spine had been issued by a temple. I had been invisible for eleven and carried myself like a man whose spine had been issued by nobody, which was, I reflected, rather the point.

*Mr. Winterbourne,* Maren said, as the dark deepened. *Why do they call it a corpse-road?*

"Because corpses made it," I murmured. "Or because corpses travel it. The records disagree."

*Which records?*

"Mine, mostly."

*Are yours right?*

"Almost never," I said. "But they're consistent, which in my profession passes for truth."

She laughed at that—a small, startled sound, like a bird landing somewhere it hadn't expected to land—and then stopped laughing, because the first court appeared out of the dark, and there was nothing funny about it at all.

* * *

The courts of the buried dead were lit by their own regret, which glows, apparently, a color I can only describe as the yellow of old paper. Each one sat in a hollow off the main road like a tumor of architecture: a few pillars, a dais, a throne of stacked things. This first one's throne was built of shoes. Hundreds of them, thousands, laced together into something almost majestic, and atop it sat a figure in burial clothes so fine they had rotted into lace.

"Travelers," it said, in a voice like a drawer opening. "The toll of this court is an accounting. Present your regrets, or present yourselves, for keeping."

Vessel stepped forward with the weary air of a man who had done this many times and hated it slightly more each century. "Passage for three, plus one passenger of the immaterial sort. We'll pay standard rates."

"There are no standard rates on this road," said the shoe-throned thing. "There never were. That was always the lie the living told themselves about death—that it could be standardized. Priced. Filed." Its attention slid past Vessel like water finding a crack, and settled on me. "You smell of ledgers, little clerk. Come forward. You will enjoy this part least."

I want it on the record that I considered running. I want it further on the record that running was impossible, because the road behind us had quietly become a wall, the way roads do down there when they've decided something about you.

The court extended a hand, and into it bloomed a book. Not any book. *My* book—or the shape of my book, the ledger I kept in my head where I filed everything I'd ever done wrong under headings designed to make it look like administration. *Discretion. Prudence. Necessary measures.*

"Account," the court said.

And because rule three was rule three, I did.

"I entered four hundred and eleven souls into Guild inventory over eleven years," I heard myself say, in the flat voice I used for figures. "I transcribed each sale. I knew what the sales were. I told myself I was only keeping records, that the record is not the knife, that the man who writes *sold* is not the man who sells. Four hundred and eleven entries. I remember every name. I recited them to myself at night like a rosary, which I believed made it penance, and which was actually just more filing." My voice cracked on the next part, and I let it, because lying was forbidden and the truth was already out and wearing my face. "Entry four hundred and twelve was a girl named Maren Colm. Age twelve. Cause of death, fever. Disposition of soul: transferred to the court beneath the mountain, consideration received, terms confidential. I copied that line in my best hand. I was proud of my best hand."

The silence afterward had texture. Sera had gone white. Vessel was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse—pity, maybe, or recognition, or the particular grief gods feel when humans do exactly what gods always feared they would.

The shoe-king leaned forward. "And your regret?"

"That I was good at it," I said. "That being good at hiding inside the machine felt like safety, and I mistook it for innocence."

The court was quiet a long moment. Then it reached down among its shoes, selected one—small, worn, a child's boot with a patched sole—and set it on the road before me.

"For passage," it said. "A debt acknowledged weighs less than a debt denied. Walk on, clerk. Your books keep you; see that you start keeping them back."

I picked up the boot. It weighed almost nothing. I carried it anyway.

* * *

We passed three more courts before the descent grew serious. The second demanded Sera's regrets and got them—twenty-nine years of praying into a silence that never answered, and the shameful relief she'd felt the day she stopped, and the worse shame of missing it. The third court wanted Vessel, and he gave it something in a language that predated language, and the whole hollow shuddered, and I decided as a matter of policy never to ask.

The fourth court was different. It had no throne. It had a table, and chairs, and sitting at the table was a figure I recognized, because I shave his face every morning.

"Ah," I said. "This again."

"You've met him?" Sera asked.

"Every night for eleven years. He's very punctual."

The Edmund at the table looked up. He had my hands, ink-stained, steady. He had my voice, when he spoke, which was the worst part. "You could still turn back," he said. "Crane will forgive you. Crane forgives anything that's useful. Go back, surrender the file, take the partnership. You'd be safe. You'd be *nothing*, which has always fit you so well."

"That's the test?" I said to the empty chair at the head of the table. "A temptation?"

"The test," said the room, in the voice of all four courts at once, "is whether you know the difference between prudence and cowardice. Many men file them under the same heading."

I stood there a while. Maren's presence hovered at my elbow, patient as winter. Then I took out my traveling kit—the bone-carver's kit, knives and rasps and the little folding vise—and laid it on the table in front of my double.

"I'm not turning back," I said. "But I'm not pretending I'm brave, either. I'm terrified. Write that down. Enter it. *Edmund Winterbourne, afraid, proceeding anyway.* If your books are going to keep me, let them keep an honest version."

The double smiled—my smile, rueful, crooked—and slid the kit back across the table. "Passage," said the room, and the walls exhaled, and the road continued downward.

Sera touched my arm as we walked on. "That was well done."

"It was barely done at all."

"Yes," she said. "That's usually what well done looks like."

* * *

Near the bottom, where the candlelight turned from yellow to a blue like drowned starlight, Vessel finally stopped and leaned against the tunnel wall, and for the first time since the gutter he looked genuinely tired—not beggar-tired but *god*-tired, the exhaustion of a very large thing folded into a very small shape for too long.

"Ahead is the antechamber," he said. "Past it, the Court Beneath proper. Souls as currency, memory as collateral, and a queen who hasn't said a true word in nine hundred years because true words have prices down there and she pays her debts." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I should tell you now, while it's cheap to tell you: I don't know if I can hold the gates open when we leave. The toll they'll demand for exit is devotion, freely given, and I've been running on fumes and one priestess's stubbornness since the gutter. So when we get in—and we'll get in, I'm good at doors—understand that getting out may cost more than any of us brought."

"Then we'll find something worth spending," Sera said simply, and Vessel looked at her the way starving men look at bread.

Maren tugged at my attention, fainter now, the mountain's weight pressing on even the dead. *Mr. Winterbourne. I'm scared.*

"So am I," I said aloud, and Sera and Vessel both glanced over, having long since learned that I sometimes answered voices they couldn't hear. I took out the child's boot from the first court and set it on the ground between us, a small absurd anchor. "But I've started a new ledger, and the first entry says we go on."

Far below us—so far it might have been imagination, except that nothing down here was ever imagination—came the sound of music. Court music. Strings and glass and something underneath it like counting.

The equinox was three days away, and the doors of the Court Beneath were already beginning to think about closing.
