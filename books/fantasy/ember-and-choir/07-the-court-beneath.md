## 7. The Court Beneath

I had officiated at funerals for thirty years before my god went quiet, and I thought I knew what the dead looked like. They looked like absence. Like a coat still on its hook. Like a name spoken too loudly in an empty room.

The dead beneath the mountain do not look like absence. They look like *aristocracy*.

That was my first mistake, walking in: I kept flinching as though I'd stumbled into a sickroom, when really I had stumbled into a salon. The court of the buried dead is lit by veins of pale mineral light that run through the cavern walls like frost through a windowpane, and under that light everyone is beautiful in the way of things that have been dead so long they've forgotten to be embarrassed about it. Their skin has the sheen of old ivory. Their eyes are lamps turned down low. They wear their shrouds the way our nobility wear silk—draped, deliberate, with an air of having chosen the fabric personally.

They were also, all of them, staring at us.

Well. At Vessel, mostly. A god, even a beggar-shaped one, even one carrying his divinity in a tin cup like a beggar's takings, is still a scandal at a dinner party. The courtiers parted around him the way water parts around a stone, and then closed behind him and pretended he hadn't happened, which is the most refined cruelty I've ever witnessed and I once attended a synod.

"Smile," Vessel murmured to me, without moving his mouth. "They can smell fear, but they can smell *sincerity* worse."

"I don't know how to be insincere," I said. "It was trained out of me. Along with everything else."

"Then lie with your posture. You're a priestess. Stand like one."

So I did. Spine straight, chin level, hands folded—the whole architecture of authority, built over a foundation of pure terror. And it worked, more or less, because here is the secret I learned in my years at the temple and never once got credit for: ceremony is not belief. Ceremony is what you do instead of screaming. I could perform reverence in my sleep, and frequently had.

Edmund walked beside me with his ledger-clerk's hunch half-erased, wearing the Guild's gray seal on a chain around his neck like a man wearing someone else's wedding ring. He had argued for this plan in the corpse-road, quietly, in the way he argues—by stating facts until the facts become a wall you cannot climb.

"The court respects paper," he'd said. "They respect nothing else. Not prayer, not grief, not love. Paper. If I walk in as an auditor from the Vellum Guild, they will show me the vaults themselves, because the Guild's claims are the only law down here older than the queen."

"And if they ask you questions?"

"Then I'll answer them the way I answer everything." He almost smiled. "Accurately."

I should have known better than to find that comforting. Accuracy, it turns out, is its own kind of danger. But we'll come to that.

* * *

The throne room—or the *accounting hall*, which is what these people call their throne room, because of course they do—was vast and cold and hung with chandeliers made of fused memory-glass, each pane holding somebody's best day, refracted endlessly. I recognized none of the days. That's the thing about other people's happiness; it looks like weather happening to someone else.

The Queen of the Buried Court sat on a dais of black bone—not carved, *grown*, which told me something about how long her family had been down here—and she received us the way a spider receives flies: with excellent manners and no movement whatsoever.

She was old in a way that made Crane seem a schoolboy. Her voice, when she finally used it, came out layered, three voices braided together, as though she'd swallowed two ancestors and found them useful.

"Auditor," she said to Edmund. "And a priestess of the silent temples. And—" her lamp-dim eyes settled on Vessel, and every chandelier in the room flickered, "—*that*. What are you calling yourself these days, little god?"

"Vessel," said Vessel. "It seemed honest."

"It seemed *defeated*."

"There's a difference," Vessel said, "but it's narrower than I'd like."

The Queen laughed. It was not a kind sound. It sounded like ice settling on a lake, like something structural giving way somewhere far off. Then she waved us toward the vaults with one long ivory finger, and I understood that we had just been permitted, the way you permit a moth to circle your lamp.

Edmund went with two dead stewards toward the soul-vaults, and I stayed in the great hall to do my part, which was the part I had dreaded and, privately, the part I had volunteered for, because it was the only thing left I knew how to do.

I gave them a liturgy.

A false one. Composed on the corpse-road out of scraps—half-remembered canticles from my temple, phrases from funeral rites, a hymn structure I bent until it fit a god who wasn't there. I stood in the center of that cold hall and I sang to the returning gods, to the answering sky far above us, to the *idea* of the divine, and the dead nobility gathered around me like crows around a plow, because they had been underground for centuries and the surface world's new fervor was the only news they had.

Here is what I did not expect: it worked too well.

They didn't just listen. They *joined*. An old count whose name had been eaten by time picked up the descant in a voice like wind through a keyhole. A duchess made entirely of shroud and posture harmonized. Somewhere in the third verse I stopped conducting and simply held on, because there were four hundred dead voices in that hall and they were singing my false liturgy back to me with a conviction I hadn't felt since I was twenty-two years old and believed my god was listening.

I want to be honest with you, whoever reads this, whoever I eventually confess this to: for about ninety seconds, standing in the dark among the beautiful dead, I forgot it was false. I felt the old current move up through my feet and into my chest, the current I'd spent thirty years mourning, and I thought—*this is what it was. This is what I lost.* And then I remembered that I was performing distraction while a clerk committed burglary one floor below, and the current curdled, and I sang on anyway, because that is also what ceremony is. Doing the sacred thing with your hands while your heart files a dissent.

* * *

Edmund found me at the end of the fourth verse, pale as the courtiers, moving fast.

"I have it," he said. "Or—I know where it is. Come on."

We slipped out during the doxology, which the dead sustained enthusiastically without us, and took a stair of black spiral bone down into the vault levels, where the air changed. Warmer. Wrongly warm, like a body's warmth, like standing too close to someone living in a room full of the dead. The corridors here were lined with niches, and each niche held a flame behind glass, and each flame was a soul—small, various, some guttering, some steady, some the color of a struck match and some the deep amber of a hearth fire tended for decades.

Souls as currency. I had heard the phrase and understood the words and comprehended nothing. Standing in that corridor, I understood it completely, and it made me want to break every pane of glass in the mountain.

"She's here," Edmund said. His voice had gone thin. "Third corridor. She's—Sera, she's small. She's been burning down here for six years."

Maren's flame sat in a niche at child-height, which I suspect was not an accident but a courtesy of the cataloguing system, souls filed by age the way a library files by subject. It was a match-flame color, trembling, barely taller than a thumb. Six years of being currency does that. Six years of being *spent*—because that's what happens down here, Edmund explained later, in the flat voice he uses for unbearable facts; souls aren't just stored, they're spent, burned a little at a time to power bargains, to seal oaths, to light the Queen's memory-glass.

Edmund reached for the niche with both hands, and I watched his fingers shake, and I thought: this man who has spent his whole life keeping accounts of other people's losses is finally holding one he intends to return.

And then he stopped. Went very still. Read the ledger plate mounted beside the niche.

"Sera," he said. "There's a withdrawal record."

"What does that mean?"

"It means someone accessed this soul nine days ago." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Not spent. *Withdrawn.* Full balance. And re-deposited yesterday—with an attached contract."

He pulled the plate's record free—it came away like a leaf, like the vault itself wanted to be read—and handed it to me, and I read it twice because the first time I assumed I had misread it in the bad light.

The signature line bore the Vellum Guild's seal.

The counter-signature bore the Queen's.

And between them, in Aldous Crane's elegant, unhurried hand, the terms: the soul of Maren Ilves, tendered as keystone collateral against a master bargain yet unsealed, due at equinox. Nine days ago. Three days before we ever set foot on the corpse-road.

"He was already here," I said. The words came out very calm, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "He came down ahead of us. He knew we were coming. He—" I looked at the little flame, trembling in its glass, and the dread I'd been carrying since the Guild house sharpened all at once into something with an edge. "He didn't steal her back, Edmund. He *re-invested* her. We're not running ahead of him. We're running inside his plan."

Behind us, somewhere up the spiral stair, the false liturgy ended, and the silence that followed had footsteps in it.

Edmund took Maren's flame out of the niche anyway, cupping the glass like a bird with a broken wing, and said the truest thing I've heard him say:

"Then we stop running."
