## 9. The Burning of Accounts

I have attended a great many ceremonies in my life, and I can report with confidence that they are all, at bottom, the same: someone important stands somewhere elevated, everyone else kneels or pretends to, and the whole apparatus exists to make an exchange look like a mystery. Weddings. Funerals. Guild audits. The equinox sealing beneath the mountain was simply the largest and oldest example — the court's entire nobility arranged in tiers of bone-white galleries around a vault door the size of a cathedral, while their queen presided over the closing of accounts for the next hundred years.

The dead do not sleep, but they do, apparently, do paperwork.

Sera stood beside me in the press of courtiers, wearing borrowed mourning silks and the expression of a woman who had spent thirty years defrocked and had recently rediscovered what spite could accomplish when properly laundered. Vessel was a beggar-shaped shadow at my heel, so diminished now that most of the court looked straight through him, which was either our great advantage or a very bad omen about how much god he had left.

And three tiers up, in a gallery reserved for honored guests of the crown, stood Aldous Crane in his grey coat, holding Maren's file under one arm like a man holding a hymnal at a service he did not believe in but intended to profit from anyway.

*You remember the plan,* Sera murmured, not moving her lips. She'd learned that trick from a puppeteer in the bone-market, along with several other skills she refused to discuss.

*I remember the plan.*

*Recite it.*

*You distract the court with a false liturgy. I get to the vault. Vessel holds the door. Nobody sets anything on fire until I say so.*

*And?*

*And then everything goes wrong in a new and interesting way.*

Her mouth twitched. *Good enough.*

Above us, the queen rose. The court went silent the way only the dead can go silent — not hushed but *emptied*, as if every soul present had briefly stepped out of its body to listen better. Her voice came down through the galleries like water finding cracks.

"The equinox turns," she said. "The doors close. All debts are sealed; all balances stand. Let the ledger be read."

They brought out the master ledger.

I want you to understand what I saw, because I have kept ledgers all my life and I know what they are. This one was the size of a coffin lid, bound in something I chose not to identify, chained to two attendants who walked like men carrying a relic. Every contract ever struck in this court was written in it — every soul sold, every memory pawned, every bargain between the buried dead and the living world above. And because Crane had spent decades buying his way into this court's confidence, trading in prayers and answered hopes, the newest pages were thick with his hand. Including, somewhere in that vast book, the clause by which he claimed the returning gods themselves as collateral.

Including, on a page near the end, a girl named Maren, drowned at nine years old, sold for a debt her father never knew he owed.

The queen began to read the seals aloud. Each name spoken closed a little further. Somewhere above us, a hundred feet of mountain shifted toward locked.

"Now," Sera breathed, and stepped into the aisle, and began to lie magnificently.

I will not reproduce her liturgy here; some blasphemies deserve privacy. But I will say that she used the old forms — the true ones, from before her temple went silent — and bent them just enough sideways that they sounded like reverence while meaning nothing at all. She called the court to witness a rite of *unbinding*, a pre-sealing purification that no one present had heard of and no one dared admit it. The dead are terrified of looking ignorant. It is their one great weakness, and Sera played it like a fiddle strung with wire.

Every eye in the galleries turned to her. Even the queen paused, head tilting, intrigued despite herself.

I walked.

The vault door stood open behind the dais — open *now*, because a sealing requires the thing being sealed to be visible first, and the court liked its treasures displayed before the century shut over them. Maren's soul hung in a niche of black glass at the center of the display: a small light, the color of candle-flame seen through honey, flickering in a rhythm I recognized.

She was singing. Faintly. The song she'd hummed through my workshop wall for three years, the one about the ferryman's dog.

"Edmund Winterbourne." The queen's voice found me across the floor without effort. She had not stopped watching Sera, exactly — but part of her attention had come down to me like a dropped glove. "The Guild's auditor. Come to verify the accounts?"

"Yes," I said, which was even true, depending on your definition of audit.

"Then verify quickly. The doors close at the last bell."

I reached into my coat and produced the forgery Sera and I had built from Crane's own private ledger — his handwriting, his seals, his claim of transfer. *All assets of A. Crane reassigned to the crown, in perpetuity, effective immediately.* It was beautiful work. I had cried a little making it.

The queen took it. Read it. And smiled, which is the single most frightening thing a dead monarch can do.

"Clever," she said. "But the master ledger records the truth of all contracts. Paper above is paper. The book below is law."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm not here for the paper."

And I took out the lamp.

Not a lamp, really. Vessel had called it an ember, and he had carried it inside himself for four thousand years — the last unspent scrap of his divinity, hoarded through centuries of silence the way a starving man hoards a single seed. He had pressed it into my hands at the corpse-road's mouth and said, *When the moment comes, don't be gentle. Gentleness wastes fire.* Then he had walked beside me ever since, growing smaller with every step, until now he was barely a boy-shaped absence at my ankle, and I understood what the toll had truly been. Not devotion. Everything else.

I opened my hands over the niche where Maren's soul sang.

The ember left me like a bird leaving a fist.

It touched the black glass and the glass did not shatter — it *remembered being sand*, and fell. Maren's light dropped into my palms, warm as a sleeping bird, and the whole court inhaled at once, ten thousand dead throats, a sound like a tide going out wrong.

"STOP HIM," said the queen, and the ceremony stopped being a ceremony.

What happened next happened fast and badly, which is how true things usually happen. Courtiers moved — not running, the dead don't run, but *arriving* unpleasantly quickly, like weather. Sera abandoned her liturgy mid-verse and started throwing the real kind, the old words with weight in them, and where her voice landed, dead things flinched as if remembering what worship used to cost them. Vessel — small, gutter-small, emberless Vessel — planted himself in the vault doorway and *grew*, just slightly, just enough, a beggar becoming a doorframe becoming a wall, spending whatever remained of himself to hold the passage open.

"HOLD THE GATES," he said, in a voice that made the mountain sit up. "LITTLE CLERK. THE BOOK. NOW."

I ran for the master ledger.

The attendants fought me, and I am ashamed to report that I fought back like a clerk: badly, desperately, with elbows. One of them caught my collar. I felt Maren's soul shift in my coat pocket, warm against my ribs, and I thought — absurdly, clearly — of the memorial bones I carve for children. Of the names I cut into them so someone, someday, would know they had been here. That was always the whole job. Not the ledgers. The names.

I got the ledger's chain loose. I got it off the dais. And I got it to the brazier.

Every sealing ceremony has a brazier, because every ceremony needs a place to burn the previous year's records — even the dead keep archives, and archives need pruning. The flame in it was cold white, funerary, hungry. I heaved the master ledger up over the rim, and the whole court screamed at once, and Aldous Crane came down the gallery stairs three at a time like a man who had finally run out of patience for pretending to be respectable.

"Winterbourne!" His voice cracked across the chaos. "Think about what you're doing! Every contract — the gods' return, the city's debts, *your own indenture papers* — burn that and none of it ever legally existed! You'll unmake the Guild! You'll unmake *yourself!*"

I looked at him. Grey coat, grey eyes, grey arithmetic. And I understood, finally and completely, what I should have understood years ago in his office: that he had never been wicked in the way stories mean it. He had simply believed, with his whole heart, that anything could be an entry. That a soul was a line item. That a god was an asset awaiting valuation. He had committed the oldest sin there is, which is bookkeeping applied to things that are not books.

"You're right," I told him. "It will unmake me too. My contracts. My protections. Twenty years of being untouchable." I smiled, and meant it, which frightened me more than the court did. "Good."

I let the ledger fall.

The burning of accounts, as experienced from inside, feels less like fire than like *amnesia*. The flames took the pages and the pages took their contents and the contents took the world's agreement with them, and I felt it happen in my own chest — twenty years of careful entries dissolving, the Guild's claims on me unraveling thread by thread, until I was, for the first time since I signed my indenture at nineteen, entirely and dangerously free. Around me the court convulsed. Debts dissolved. Souls that had been collateral woke up confused and ownerless. Dead lords clutched at memories that were suddenly theirs again, unpawned, and wept or laughed or both. The queen staggered on her throne as a century of carefully balanced power turned, all at once, into ash and equity.

Crane screamed — not in pain, in *arithmetic*. Because his creditors, suddenly unbound by any contract, remembered that they were creditors anyway, and that gratitude is not the same as forgiveness, and that a man who burns the books still owes what the books recorded. They came for him out of the smoke — the souls he'd brokered, the bargains he'd stacked, the whole architecture of other people's desperation he'd lived in — and they bore him down toward the closing dark, and the last I saw of Aldous Crane was his hand reaching upward, not for help, but for a pen that wasn't there.

The bells began. The doors began to close.

Vessel held them.

I will not describe it fully; some things belong to him. But a god with nothing left gave the last of it anyway — stretched himself across a hundred feet of stone threshold and became, for eleven seconds, the oldest kind of miracle: simply *in the way*. Long enough for Sera to reach me. Long enough for me to feel Maren's soul singing against my heart. Long enough for us to run up the corpse-road while the mountain shut its mouth behind us, and the last sound from below was not a scream but a sigh — ten thousand freed debts exhaling at once.

We came out into starlight and snow on the mountainside, and Sera collapsed laughing, and I sat down hard in the drifts, and Vessel —

Vessel was gone. Or nearly. A smudge of warmth in the snow, an ember with nowhere left to spend itself.

Except.

In my pocket, Maren's light pulsed once, bright, and I heard her voice — clear, close, nine years old and done waiting — say:

"Mister Winterbourne? I know the way home from here. But I think somebody small needs carrying."

And in the snow between us, the ember flickered, and waited to see if anyone would pick it up.
