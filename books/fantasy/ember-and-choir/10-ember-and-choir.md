## 10. Ember and Choir

*Edmund*

The world did not end when the ledgers burned. I had half expected it to. A man who spends his life keeping accounts assumes, somewhere in his marrow, that the books are holding up the sky.

Instead, the morning after the equinox, the sky held itself up and the baker on Coriander Street gave away his first forty loaves because a god he'd never heard of told him, in a dream, that hunger was an old debt and debts were done. By noon there was a queue. By evening there was a fistfight over who'd been waiting longer, which I suppose proves that divinity can only do so much against human nature.

The Guild's survivors scattered like ash off a struck match. Some fled to coastal towns where nobody asked questions about impossible trade. One junior factor turned himself in to the magistrates, weeping so hard they could barely understand his confession. Old Mistress Halloway, who had sold a widow's grief to a collector of rare sorrows, simply locked her shop and walked into the sea, which even now I cannot decide was cowardice or the only honest entry she ever made.

And Crane—Crane is beneath the mountain still, dragged down by creditors whose names he'd written in his own hand. The court's doors sealed at equinox dusk with him inside them. A century of compound interest. I try not to think of him often. I fail more than I succeed, which is, I've decided, its own kind of penance.

I stayed in the city. That surprised everyone, including me. The bone-carver's shop on Tanner's Lane still has my name on the door, though the sign has faded to where it reads more or less *Edmund Wint rbou e*, which feels accurate. I carve memorials again. Only memorials. No ledgers, no inventories, no entries. When someone dies, their family brings me a bone—a rib, usually, or a knuckle from the left hand—and I carve their name into it and whatever small true thing deserves remembering. *She made the best plum jam in three parishes.* *He forgave his brother on a Tuesday.* *She was afraid of moths and unafraid of anything else.*

The dead don't speak to me much anymore. I used to think that was my gift failing. Now I suspect it was the Guild all along—the constant hum of bought and sold souls drowning out the ordinary quiet of the ordinary dead. With the contracts voided, the dead have gone back to being dead: patient, distant, at peace. It's better this way. A man shouldn't be a postbox for eternity.

But Maren spoke to me one last time.

Her soul came home with me in a candle jar, wrapped in Vessel's last borrowed warmth, and for six days it sat on my workbench while I decided what shape to give it. Not a ledger line. Never again a ledger line. In the end I chose a songbird's wishbone—not hers, nothing of hers remained but the soul itself, but a wishbone seemed right for a girl who had been wished upon and wished back and never once granted anything.

I carved for four days without stopping. Her name along the curve. The date she drowned. And then, because the bone demanded it, the thing I'd learned about her in the dark places under the mountain: *she sang to the other lost ones so they wouldn't be afraid.*

On the sixth night, as I set down the graver, the shop filled with singing.

It wasn't loud. It was the kind of singing you hear in dreams, or just before waking—small and clear and slightly out of tune in the way only children are out of tune, earnestly, gloriously wrong. Maren's voice. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. She sang something without words, a rising-and-falling thing like water finding its level, and every joint of bone in my shop hummed sympathy, and I sat very still with my hands flat on the bench and let myself weep like a man with no reputation to lose.

When the song ended, the silence afterward was not empty. It was full, the way a finished sentence is full. She was home. Wherever home was—her mother's kitchen, some green field beyond the mountain's memory—it had her now, and no contract, no court, no clerk could touch her.

I buried the wishbone in the churchyard on Bellwether Hill, under a rowan tree, because Sera said rowans keep off ill luck and I wanted the girl overdetermined in her safety. I said no prayers. I didn't need to. Somewhere, I am certain, she was already singing to somebody else.

*Sera*

You cannot rebuild a temple in a day. You cannot rebuild one in a year, either, if the god you're building it around is the size of a coal scuttle.

Vessel sits on my windowsill most mornings, in the shape of a grey-feathered bird that may or may not be a wren, watching me sweep the floor of what used to be the Temple of the Quiet Flame and is now, officially, nothing at all. The defrocking was never rescinded. The hierarchy that defrocked me dissolved into argument within a month of the gods' return—half of them insisting the old liturgies must resume exactly as written, the other half insisting the old liturgies were precisely what had driven the gods silent in the first place. They are still arguing. They will argue until the sun burns out. Meanwhile the actual faithful have gone elsewhere, to bakeries and street corners and hilltops, praying to whoever answers, and mostly getting answers, and mostly not knowing what to do with them.

That, I decided, was my work. Not doctrine. Not hierarchy. The awkward practical business of teaching people how to receive a miracle without dropping it.

"Tell them again," Vessel said yesterday, in a voice like dry leaves negotiating with wind. "Tell them the part about price."

"There is no price," I told the congregation—eleven people, sitting on crates in the gutted temple, because pews burn and crates do not. "That's the whole of it. The old bargains are voided. You ask. If it is given, it is given freely. If it isn't, it isn't, and you will survive that too."

An old man raised his hand like a schoolboy. "Then what do we *owe*?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's what terrifies you. I know. It terrified me for thirty years."

Vessel ruffled his feathers, which is his version of applause.

What nobody tells you about a god grown small is that smallness clarifies. The great gods of my childhood had been vast and vague, thunderheads of requirement, demanding sacrifice and silence and perfect posture. Vessel, reduced to an ember, has become almost conversational. He wants company. He wants stories. He wants, above all, to be *chosen*—not bound by contract, not purchased, but chosen daily, the way you choose a friend. Devotion freely given was his toll for guiding us under the mountain, and I paid it expecting to feel robbed. Instead I felt, for the first time since my ordination, honest.

So we are building worship out of almost nothing. Morning gathering, no liturgy, just whoever comes. An evening meal shared with anyone hungry. A practice I invented myself, which scandalizes exactly no one anymore: each person names aloud one thing they received that day that they did not earn. Bread. Rain. Forgiveness. A stranger's smile. We call it the counting of gifts, and yes, Edmund winced when I named it, and yes, I chose the name specifically to watch him wince, and I regret nothing.

Because here is what I learned in the dark, holding a false liturgy together with spit and nerve while the dead nobility circled: faith was never the transaction. Faith was the *choir*. All those voices, imperfect, out of tune, making something none of them could make alone. The Guild turned that into bookkeeping. My old temple turned it into bureaucracy. But underneath both, it was always just people, singing badly, together, on purpose.

We are going to sing badly together for a very long time. I have never been more certain of anything.

There remains only the matter of Edmund Winterbourne, who attends the morning gatherings and stands at the back and does not sing, and who has, in two months, repaired our roof, rehung our door, carved memorial bones for nine of our dead, and fixed the leaking cistern, all while maintaining the fiction that he is merely passing through.

Tonight I intend to end that fiction.

*Edmund*

She ambushed me with soup. I should have expected it. Sera Okonkwo's tactical instincts were wasted on the priesthood; she'd have made a magnificent general.

"You've replaced the roof joists," she said, setting the bowl in front of me in the temple kitchen. "Re-leaded the windows. Carved bones for half the parish. You've been 'passing through' for sixty-three days."

"I'm thorough."

"You alphabetized my herb shelf."

"It was chaos."

"Edmund." She sat across from me, and the lamplight caught the grey at her temples, and I understood with sudden terrible clarity that I was about to be audited by someone with far better moral standing than I ever possessed. "I'm not asking you to join the temple. I'm not asking for vows or liturgy or any of the machinery we've both spent our lives watching rot people. I'm asking why a man who has nowhere else to be keeps behaving like a guest."

I looked at the soup. Barley and root vegetables, the humblest possible food, made with obvious care. Everything in this room was humble and cared-for. The mended roof overhead. The ember-small god asleep in a bird's shape on the sill outside, dreaming whatever tiny vast dreams remain to him. The counting-of-gifts list chalked on the wall in Sera's blunt hand: *bread, rain, a second chance, a friend who fixes roofs.*

"I spent twenty years being invisible," I said finally. "It worked. That's the awful part—it *worked*. Nobody saw me, so nobody took anything from me. Then I found out what my invisibility was *for*. Every entry I transcribed, every sale I filed clean and neat—I was the mechanism. Crane couldn't have moved a single soul without a clerk willing not to look."

"You burned the ledgers."

"After. After a girl died twice. Being seen after the fact isn't virtue, Sera. It's just arithmetic."

"No," she said, with the mild immovability of a woman who has argued with gods and won on points. "Being seen *at all* is the virtue. Before, during, after—it doesn't matter when. Most people manage it never." She pushed the soup closer. "Stay. Not as a clerk. Not as a penitent doing penance-chores. As family. The kind you choose. The kind that sees you on a Tuesday when you haven't done anything impressive, and stays anyway."

The dead girl's song was still somewhere in my chest, faint as a pressed flower. *She sang to the other lost ones so they wouldn't be afraid.* I thought about all the years I'd sung to no one, quietly, invisibly, keeping my books balanced and my heart the same.

"All right," I said.

Sera blinked. I suspect she'd prepared three further arguments and was disappointed to waste them.

"But I'm alphabetizing the hymnals," I added. "They're a disgrace."

"They're in *piles*."

"Piles are how it starts."

She laughed—really laughed, head back, the sound bouncing off the mended roof—and outside the window the little grey god opened one eye, considered us both, and closed it again with what I can only describe as satisfaction. Somewhere beyond the walls the city went on adjusting to mercy, badly and beautifully, and the rowan on Bellwether Hill kept its small watch over a small grave, and for the first time in my careful, invisible life, I was exactly where I belonged, seen, accountable, and entirely unwilling to file a single thing.

The soup, for the record, was excellent. Some miracles you don't have to pray for. They just set a bowl in front of you and wait.
