## 6. The Village That Isn't

The rain found them an hour past dusk, and the hollow found them an hour after that.

Sefa felt it before she saw it—the way you feel a hearth through a wall in winter. A warmth in the air that had no business existing on a moor where nothing burned. She stopped at the lip of a shallow valley and put out her arm to halt Noor behind her.

"Do you smell that?" she asked.

"Bread," Noor said. His voice did something strange on the word, as if it had caught on a splinter in his throat. "Bread baked with ash."

"You know that smell?"

"I know *of* it." He said nothing else, and Sefa had learned over three days of forced travel that pressing Noor Vasquez was like pressing a locked door: you only bruised your shoulder.

She pressed anyway, because she was warden-trained and suspicion was her mother tongue. "The map led us here. Off every road. Into a hollow that shouldn't be warm. Either your living ledger is guiding us or it's herding us, and I'd like to know which before we walk into whatever's baking."

Noor unslung his pack and drew out the map of Vhalcrest by feel, not light—he'd stopped needing light for it days ago, which unsettled her more than anything else about him. He held it flat against his forearm, and even in the dark she could see the faint shimmer along its western border, like heat-haze over a forge.

"The river waymark is behind us," he said. "The next one is ahead." He turned the map slowly, and Sefa watched his face in the ghost-light of its shimmering ink. "It's pointing down. Into the ground."

"Into the *ground*."

"Some doors go sideways," Noor said mildly. "Some go down. The guild has a cellar full of doors that go Tuesday."

"And this one?"

"This one goes home, if Maro told me true." He rolled the map away before she could study it further. She noticed he did that often—gave her glimpses, never views. A careful man rationing his careful life. But the rationing had cracks in it now, and through the cracks she kept seeing something raw and young, like meat under armor.

They went down into the hollow single file, because the path allowed no more, and because Sefa wanted Noor behind her where she could hear him. Old habit. In the vigil they called it walking point; her grandmother—no. Not grandmother. She had been carefully not-thinking about the name in the sealed file for three days now, the way you don't touch a burn.

*Vasquez-Reyes, Amara. Warden, Third Circle. Exiled under the Ash Edict.*

Her grandmother had been a warden. Her grandmother had been *exiled by wardens*. And Sefa had spent her whole life polishing the badge those same wardens had pinned on her, never once asking why the family stories stopped at her mother's childhood like a road washed out by flood.

The path opened without warning into a bowl of land cupped between two hills, and Sefa forgot how to breathe for a moment.

There were houses. Perhaps forty of them, low and stone-roofed, hunched into the hillside as if ashamed to be seen—and every single door faced west. She counted without meaning to; counting was what she did instead of feeling. Forty-one doors, all of them hung facing west, all of them closed against the night, and from beneath several of them came firelight, and voices, and the smell of bread baked with ash.

"There's no village marked here," she whispered. "I've surveyed this moor. Twice. There is *nothing* here."

"That," said Noor, "is rather the point."

A dog barked once, somewhere—a deep, old bark, the kind of bark that comes from dogs bred for guarding rather than petting. Then a shutter cracked open, lamplight spilled yellow across the wet grass, and a voice called out in a language Sefa did not know and yet understood the shape of perfectly, the way you understand the shape of a face seen once in infancy.

It said: *Who walks the ash-road after dark?*

And Noor answered—in the same language, halting but correct, like a man reciting words learned from a book he'd loved as a child: *"Two travelers. One bound by oath, one by blood. We ask bread and a wall."*

Silence. Long enough that Sefa's hand drifted toward her knife. Then the voice again, closer now, from a figure emerging from behind the nearest house: an old woman wrapped in a shawl the color of rust, holding a lamp high enough to show a face seamed like dry clay.

*"Bound by oath,"* the woman repeated, and her eyes found Noor's palm—the oath-mark there, the fine silver scarring shaped like a sword laid flat. She made a sound low in her chest, half reverence, half grief. *"We were told the last speaker died in the burning."*

"People tell themselves a lot of things," Noor said, back in Common Tongue, "to survive the telling."

The old woman's gaze slid to Sefa then, and everything changed.

Sefa had been looked at many ways in her life. As a recruit, as a rival, as a weapon, as a woman, as a problem. She knew all those looks the way a soldier knows drill. This was none of them. The old woman stared at her the way people stare at a door they'd mourned shut, opening on its own.

Then the lamp trembled, and the woman said, in careful Common Tongue, "Child. Show me your left wrist."

Every instinct Sefa owned stood up at once. *Warden. Vigil-trained. Never show anyone anything.* Her hand actually moved to cover her wrist—the left one, the one with the birthmark she'd hidden under bracers and cuffs and warden's leathers since she was six years old, when a vigil examiner had frowned at it and written *unclassified marking* in her file, and her mother had gone very quiet and never spoken of it again.

"No," Sefa said.

The old woman didn't press. She simply turned her own wrist up to the lamplight, peeling back the shawl's edge.

The mark there was small and dark, a whorled shape like a flame folded around a key. Sefa's stomach dropped through the wet grass, through the moor, through the world. Because she had that mark. Same size. Same place. Same impossible little flame-key that three vigil physicians had called a stain and one, the kind one, had called *a thing worth asking your family about*—and her mother had wept and said there was no one left to ask.

"Where did you get that?" Sefa heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away.

"It was given to my grandmother," the old woman said, "the night before the burning. Everyone born of the exile lines carries it. It is how we would know each other, if we ever found each other again." She smiled, and it broke Sefa's heart, because it was the smile of someone who had waited sixty years to smile it. "We were told no one carrying the mark had ever come back west. And here you are, walking out of the dark with the last swordspeaker, wearing a warden's coat over our oldest sign."

The rain chose that moment to redouble, cold and needling, and the decision was taken out of all their hands. The old woman—*Maren*, she said her name was, Maren Vasquez-Reyes, and Sefa's knees nearly buckled at the second name—led them through the rain to a house with a west-facing door, and fed them, and asked nothing else until morning.

* * *

Sefa did not sleep.

She sat by the peat fire in the low stone room while Noor slept the sleep of the genuinely exhausted, one hand curled near his sword hilt even in dreams, and she turned her left wrist over and over in the firelight like a woman reading a letter she couldn't finish.

Around her, the house breathed. The family who owned it—three generations, plus a great-uncle who snored like a landslide—had given up their main room without hesitation, spread straw pallets, and vanished to the loft. On the walls hung things that made her chest ache in ways she had no vocabulary for: a woven banner with a country's sigil she'd only ever seen in confiscated documents, rendered lovingly in thread. A child's drawing of a dragon, wings spread, done in charcoal and ash-water. A shelf of small clay lamps, each one labeled in that language she almost-understood, each one facing west.

Everything faced west. She'd noticed it in the first hour and hadn't stopped noticing since. The doors. The lamps. Even the graves in the little yard behind the houses—all the headstones turned so their faces pointed west, toward a country that had been cinders for a hundred years.

At midnight, Maren came back with bread.

It was dark, dense, gray-crusted, and it smelled of woodsmoke and something bitter underneath, and when Sefa bit into it she tasted ash the way you taste salt tears—not as flavor but as fact. She ate it sitting up, and halfway through the loaf she realized she was crying, silently, the way she'd trained herself to cry during warden assessments: no sound, no shaking, just water leaving the body as efficiently as possible.

Maren sat down beside her and said nothing, which was the kindest possible thing.

"My grandmother's name was Amara," Sefa finally said. "Amara Vasquez-Reyes. I found it in a sealed file four days ago. Warden, Third Circle. Exiled under the Ash Edict." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I reported a courier's death to the vigil and they ordered me to burn the evidence and kill the man holding it, and instead I read a file I wasn't cleared for, and it turns out my whole life has been standing on top of a buried room."

"We knew some stayed," Maren said. "After the burning. Some took the Crown's pardon, changed their names, raised children who never knew. It was survival. No one blames the ones who hid." She paused. "Though I will admit we wondered, sometimes, whether any of the hidden ones remembered."

"My mother remembered *something*," Sefa said. "She cried when the physician asked about my wrist. She told me it was nothing. She told me—" Her voice failed, restarted. "She told me never to let anyone in a uniform look at it twice. I thought she meant bandits."

"She meant us," Maren said gently. "Or she meant what we'd cost her. Both are heavy things for a mother to carry alone."

Across the room, Noor stirred but didn't wake. Sefa watched him a long moment—the oath-marked hand, the face younger in sleep than he ever let it be awake. Three days ago she'd been hunting him. Two days ago they'd been back to back against ash-priests in the reeds while his swordsong tore the ambush apart like paper, and she'd felt the song in her teeth, in her sternum, in the scar tissue of her old warden training, and understood in one lurching instant that everything the vigil taught her about swordspeakers was a lie built to make killing them easier.

Now she sat in her ancestors' village eating her ancestors' bread, and the ache of it was worse than any wound she'd taken. Because belonging, she was discovering, was not a warm thing. Belonging was a hook. It went in easy and it did not come out clean, and every minute she sat in this room the barb worked deeper.

"I can't stay," she said.

"I know," Maren said.

"If the vigil finds this place—"

"They won't. It hasn't been found in a hundred years. The hollow keeps itself." Maren's eyes glinted. "You think it's chance that no survey ever marks us? That maps slide off this valley like rain off oilcloth? Vhalcrest hid its children well. Some of the hiding soaked into the land."

"Nothing soaks forever."

"No," Maren agreed. "Nothing does. Which is why the bread is ash-baked, and the doors face west, and why we have waited a century for a swordspeaker to walk down that path." She rose, stiff-backed, and gathered the empty plate. At the doorway she paused. "Your grandmother's hands kneaded this dough, girl. The recipe came west in her memory when nothing else could. You carry her mark. When you're ready to ask what it means, I'll be in the bake-house at dawn."

She left. Sefa sat by the dying fire, wrist turned up, flame-key whorl dark against her skin, and let herself want something for the first time in fifteen disciplined years.

She wanted to go to the bake-house at dawn.

That was exactly what frightened her.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of smoke, and Sefa was already awake when the shouting started.

It came from the eastern rim of the hollow—one voice first, then three, then the deep old dog barking like a war drum. Sefa was on her feet with her knife out before her mind caught up, crossing the room, shouldering open the west-facing door—

—and stopping dead, because the entire village was already in the lane, and no one was panicking.

They were *watching*. Forty-one households stood in the wet grass in the early light, faces turned east, calm as congregation, watching a rider pick his way down the ash-road into the hollow. He was gaunt, mud-splattered, leading a lathered horse, and he wore no colors she recognized—but slung across his back was a courier's satchel, and courier satchels meant the guild, and the guild meant Maro, and Maro's bargains had a smell she'd already learned to distrust.

Noor appeared beside her, sword drawn, oath-mark pale on his grip-hand. "Friend or ash-priest?" he murmured.

"Neither. Guild." She watched the rider's eyes sweep the crowd, searching, and settle—with visible relief—on Noor. "Looking for you specifically. That's either very good or very bad."

The rider reached them, swung down, and pressed a sealed letter into Noor's hands with the desperate efficiency of a man who'd ridden through two nights to do exactly one thing. "From the guildmaster," he gasped. "He said—burn it after reading. He said the priests already know about the hollow."

The lane went silent. Forty-one west-facing doors, and every soul in the village listening.

Noor broke the seal. Sefa read over his shoulder, because the right to privacy had ended somewhere back in the reeds, and she watched his face as he read, and what she saw there was not surprise.

It was confirmation. Cold, arriving on schedule.

"What does it say?" Maren demanded, coming forward through the crowd.

Noor lowered the letter. When he spoke, his voice was level in the way Sefa had learned meant the opposite of level underneath.

"It says the ash-priests bought our route from Maro three days ago. It says they know about the village." He looked up, and his eyes found Sefa's, and she saw in them the same arithmetic she was doing herself: one moon's turn, one ember failing, one hollow full of people whose hiding had just soaked through. "And it says we have until tomorrow's dark to leave—because the priests are coming to burn it."

Behind them, all down the lane, forty-one west-facing doors stood open in the wind, facing the country that had died, while the riders of the country that killed it rode closer with every hour.

Sefa put her hand on her knife and made the only promise left to make.

"Then we don't waste the day."
