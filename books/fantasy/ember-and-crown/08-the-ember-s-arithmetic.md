## 8. The Ember's Arithmetic

The vigil-tower of Karthen Fell had no stairs. It had a spiral of iron rungs bolted into the stone, and Sefa climbed them the way she had climbed them since she was nine years old: hands remembering before her mind did, body moving through the dark on the muscle-memory of duty.

Three hundred and six rungs. She counted them every time, because counting was what wardens did instead of praying, and because the number never changed, and in a life where so much else had begun to shift beneath her like river gravel, she needed one thing that stayed put.

At the top, the ember room opened around her like the inside of a bell.

It was round, walled in black glass that the first wardens had poured from dragon-breath itself, and at its center, suspended in a cradle of silver chains, hung the last ember of Vhalcrest.

Sefa stopped on the threshold, as she always did, and let it look at her.

That was how the old texts described it—not *observed*, not *monitored*, but *looked upon by*. The ember was the size of a fist and the color of a coal three days dead: a dull orange heart wrapped in skin of grey ash, pulsing so slowly that between one beat and the next you could convince yourself it had stopped. When she was a novitiate, she had asked High Warden Ilset why they bothered guarding something so small, so nearly gone, and Ilset had looked at her with an expression Sefa had spent fifteen years trying to forget—pity wearing the mask of patience—and said:

*Because everything the Crown owns that cannot be replaced is guarded most fiercely when it is smallest.*

She hadn't understood then. She understood now, standing in the bell-shaped silence with her ledger under her arm, exactly what kind of thing could not be replaced, and exactly who wanted to keep it small.

* * *

The ember room had one window, a slit of east-facing glass, and through it the pre-dawn sky was going the color of unpolished pewter. Sefa set her instruments on the reading table—the brass astrolabe, the fire-gauge with its coiled mercury thread, the box of calibrated lenses—and began the morning's measurement before her hands could decide whether they were still steady enough for this work.

They were not. She made herself do it anyway.

First the gauge. She touched its probe to the glass housing around the ember and watched the mercury thread climb, shiver, and settle. She wrote the number down. Then the lenses, stacked in their brass frame, through which the ember's pulse resolved into discrete bands of light, each band a register of heat the naked eye couldn't separate. She wrote those numbers too. Then the astrolabe, for the drift—the slow, fractional wandering of the ember's position within its chains, which the manuals called *settling* and which Sefa had privately, for two years now, been calling by its true name.

*Falling.*

She finished the third measurement, sat back, and looked at the column of figures marching down her ledger page, and felt the floor tilt under her even though the tower stood on bedrock.

For eleven years, the ember's decline had followed a curve so gentle it was almost a straight line. The vigil's own projections—inked into the official registers, copied into the Crown's ledgers, recited at every investiture—gave the last ember of Vhalcrest another forty years of slow fading. Forty years. Long enough for everyone currently serving the vigil to retire, die, and be replaced by people who would inherit the same slow arithmetic and never have to think about it.

The numbers in front of her gave it twenty-eight days.

She checked the gauge again. Same number. She checked the lenses. The bands had shifted again since yesterday—not by the fraction of a hair-width the models predicted, but by a full width, the outermost register dimming as if someone had drawn a curtain partway across it. She checked the astrolabe. The drift had doubled.

Twenty-eight days. Perhaps thirty, if the weather turned cold and the world drew less heat from the air. Perhaps fewer, if whatever was eating the ember from inside kept accelerating the way it had accelerated every day this month.

And she knew—it was not suspicion anymore, it was arithmetic—*why.*

The confiscated things.

Every impossible thing the vigil seized came up these rungs eventually: the bottled storms, the second shadows, the singing coins, the doors that opened onto nowhere. Official doctrine said they were stored here for study. Sefa had done the studying. They were not studied. They were fed to the housing—slid, one by one, into the feeding-chutes around the ember's cradle, where the black glass drank them and the ember flared briefly, gorgeously, and the registers spiked, and the Crown's monopoly on miracle burned a little brighter for a night.

But the flare was a loan, not a gift. She'd proven it with three years of figures the vigil had refused to read. Every impossible thing fed to the ember made it burn hotter *and die faster*—the way a candle drowned in its own wax gutters bright before it goes out. The ash-priests knew. The High Registry knew. They were spending the last ember of a murdered country to keep their lights on, and the official projections—the forty comfortable years—were fiction, maintained the way all convenient fictions are maintained: by shooting the messengers.

Two messengers before her had raised the discrepancy. One had been reassigned to the salt-roads. One had simply stopped appearing in the rolls.

Sefa closed the ledger and pressed her palm flat against its cover, and thought about a courier who had staggered into a guildhall burned from the inside out, and a map that only opened for an oath-marked hand, and a careful man with tired eyes who had looked at her across a backward-flowing river and sung a song that should not have been possible.

Twenty-eight days.

Noor Vasquez was somewhere north of the hollow village with a map that moved and a door that sang, and he had no idea he was racing a clock that had already lost most of its hours.

* * *

She heard the rungs before she heard the voice—always did; Ilset climbed like a woman half her age and announced herself like a siege engine—and by the time the High Warden pulled herself over the lip of the hatch, Sefa had the ledger closed, the instruments cased, and her face arranged into the smooth professional blankness that had gotten her through four years of increasingly unbearable mornings.

Ilset didn't buy it. Ilset had never bought it. That was the cruelty of mentors: they taught you your tells and then used them against you forever.

"You've been up here since the middle watch," Ilset said. She was a broad, grey-templed woman built like the tower itself, in a warden's coat gone soft at the elbows, and she crossed the ember room the way other people crossed their own kitchens. "I know because I read the sign-in roll, and I know because I know you. Show me the numbers."

"Ma'am—"

"Sefa." Ilset stopped beside the ember's cradle, and for a moment both of them watched the dull coal pulse in its chains, slow as a dying man's breath. "I have known you since you were nine years old and afraid of the dark, and you joined a vigil whose whole purpose is sitting alone in the dark with a dying fire. I signed your first oath. I will not stand here and watch you lie to me about arithmetic. Show me."

So Sefa showed her.

She opened the ledger flat on the reading table and turned it to face her mentor, and watched Ilset's eyes move down the columns—gauge, bands, drift, gauge, bands, drift, thirty days of entries in her tight warden's script—and saw the exact moment the numbers stopped being numbers and became a sentence.

Ilset read it twice. Then she did something Sefa had never seen her do in fifteen years.

She sat down.

Not gracefully. She lowered herself onto the reading bench like a woman whose knees had been cut out from under her, and she stared at the ledger, and the tower's silence stretched so long that Sefa could hear the ember pulse—could *feel* it, a pressure behind the ears like the moment before thunder.

"The projections say forty years," Ilset said finally.

"The projections are wrong. They've been wrong for at least three years. Maybe longer. The feeding—" Sefa stopped. Made herself go on. "You know about the feeding, ma'am. You've always known. Every confiscated thing goes into the chutes, and every feeding spikes the registers, and every spike shortens the curve. I can show you the correlation. I *have* shown you the correlation. Twice."

"I remember." Ilset's voice was very quiet. "I also remember what happened to Warden Corvane."

"I remember too."

They sat with that. Through the eastern slit, the sky had begun to turn the grey-pink of old scars, and the first light caught the black glass walls and made the whole room glow faintly, like the inside of a lantern, like the inside of a throat.

"Twenty-eight days," Ilset said. "What happens in twenty-nine?"

Sefa had run that calculation too, alone, at night, in a room that smelled of lamp-oil and cold iron, and she had hated every line of it. "Nobody knows. The texts say the ember is the anchor-point. That everything bound to Vhalcrest's fire—every relic, every working, every door—is held open by it. When it fails..." She spread her hands. "The doors close. Permanently. Whatever's on the far side stays there. Forever. And every impossible thing in the world stops being possible. Bottled storms go flat. Second shadows fade. The guild's whole inventory becomes junk."

"And the people on the far side of the doors?"

"There are no people on the far side," Sefa said, in the voice the vigil used for novitiates. "Vhalcrest burned a hundred years ago. There's nothing left to burn."

She heard herself say it and heard, underneath it, her grandmother's name in a sealed file, and a hollow village full of ash-bread and west-facing doors, and a birth-mark recognized by strangers, and she watched Ilset hear it too—or something like it—because the old woman's face did something complicated and settled into grief.

"No," Ilset said. "I suppose there isn't."

* * *

It was Ilset who broke the silence, and when she spoke again, her voice had changed—cooled, hardened, put back on like armor—and Sefa felt the shift the way you feel a change in wind direction before a storm.

"Here is what happens next," Ilset said. "In four days, the Registry sends an audit team. Three priests, two senior wardens, a full inspection of the registers and the feeding schedule. Standard procedure."

"Standard procedure doesn't include me showing anyone my private ledger."

"No. It doesn't." Ilset met her eyes. "Which brings us to the part I came up three hundred and six rungs to say, and which I will say once, plainly, because you deserve plainness and I am apparently not yet too old to give it."

Sefa's mouth went dry.

"The Registry already knows the ember is failing faster than the books say. They have known for two years. The audit is not coming to fix anything. The audit is coming because a courier died in a guildhall carrying a map of a country that does not exist, and the ash-priests have traced that map to a swordspeaker, and the swordspeaker has acquired a partner—a disgraced warden named Sefa Amari, whose file I personally sealed four years ago and whose grandmother appears, by extraordinary coincidence, in the exile rolls of the very country in question." Ilset's voice never rose. It got quieter and heavier instead, like stone settling. "They believe you are helping him find the last door."

Sefa said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't confession.

"They are wrong about one thing," Ilset went on. "They think you're helping him out of sentiment. Heritage. Blood. They think you want Vhalcrest back." A pause, and something flickered behind her eyes—something old and tired and, horribly, sad. "I told them that was nonsense. I told them you were loyal. I told them you were mine."

"Ma'am—"

"So here is your choice, and I will not dress it." Ilset stood, and standing, she seemed to fill the ember room, casting a long shadow across the ledger with its terrible honest numbers. "Deliver him. The swordspeaker, the map, the route. Bring them to the audit team in four days, and the Registry restores you—full rank, clean file, your grandmother's name struck from the exile rolls and buried so deep no one ever finds it again. You keep your coat. You keep your oaths. You keep—" her voice caught, barely, a hairline fracture in stone, "—you keep me.

"Or you don't. And when the audit comes, they find what they find, and I will not be able to protect you, because I will be standing in that room with them, and either I arrest you myself or I burn with you, and I have spent fifty years building the only thing standing between the ash-priests and every warden under my command, and I am telling you honestly, child, I do not know which I will choose until the moment comes."

The ember pulsed. Between beats, the silence rang.

"That's not a choice," Sefa said. Her voice came out low and rough. "That's a knife with two edges."

"It's the only kind the Crown issues." Ilset picked up her own copy of the sign-in roll, tucked it under her arm, and moved toward the hatch. At the lip she paused, back turned, and said, without looking around:

"For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much—I hope you choose the way that lets me keep respecting you. Whichever way that is."

Then she was gone, down the rungs, boots ringing on iron, and Sefa was alone with the ember and the ledger and the arithmetic.

* * *

She stayed until full sunrise.

She did the afternoon measurements early, though there was no one to report them to, because the ritual of it steadied her hands: probe, lens, astrolabe, write. The outermost band had dimmed again. The drift had worsened. Twenty-seven days now, maybe, if you were generous, and generosity had never once changed a number.

She thought about Noor Vasquez. About the way he'd looked at the backward river—not greedy, not hungry, just *careful*, the way a man looks at water when he's spent his whole life mending leaks. About the swordsong waking out of him like something that had been asleep in his bones since before he was born, and the expression on his face afterward: terror and wonder in equal measure, a man discovering his own house had rooms he'd never entered.

He was paying for that song. She'd seen it in the way he'd paused, mid-sentence, hunting for a word that should have been easy—small losses, little gaps, a man quietly bleeding years he didn't know he'd spent. He didn't know the price yet. Or he knew and hadn't told her. Both possibilities made her want to shake him.

Twenty-seven days. Four until the audit. Two choices, and neither edge of the knife.

She thought about her grandmother's name in the sealed file. *Amari, Yeva. Hollow of West-Facing Doors. Exiled, age six.* A child, marched out of a burning country with ash in her hair, who grew up, married, bore children, died, and never once told her granddaughter what the mark on her wrist meant—because some inheritances are too heavy to hand to someone you love.

Sefa looked at her own wrist. The mark sat there under the skin like a word waiting to be read, and for the first time in her life she let herself read it all the way through.

*I belong to a place that burned. And the place is not done burning. And I am the only one doing the arithmetic.*

She closed the ledger. She took out a fresh sheet, and after a long moment, she did not write a report. She wrote a letter she would never send, addressed to a woman six years old in a column of exiles, and it said only: *I found the numbers. I'm sorry it took me so long. I'm coming.*

Then she burned the letter in the ember's slit-window sunlight, using the vigil's own fire-glass, and watched the ash drift down through the light, and understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only at the end of a sleepless night—that she had already chosen. Somewhere between the river and the hollow village and the rungs of this tower, she had chosen, and everything Ilset had offered her was just the sound of a door closing behind a decision she'd already made.

Which left only the question of what to do with the four days.

She packed the ledger. She packed the fire-gauge, against regulations. She took one last look at the ember—dull, guttering, patient, a whole country's worth of dying compressed into the size of a fist—and she bowed to it, the way wardens bowed at investiture, the way you bow to something you have guarded and failed.

"Hold on," she told it. "We're coming. We're slow, but we're coming."

Then she went down the three hundred and six rungs, past the sleeping barracks, past the sealed archive where her grandmother's name waited in the dark, out into the grey morning where the roads ran north toward a hollow village, a lying guildmaster, and a man who was spending his memories like coins and didn't know the purse was nearly empty.

Four days. Twenty-six, if the numbers were kind.

They would not be kind. Numbers never were.

But she had a horse in the stable yard, a stolen gauge in her pack, and a decision no one on earth could share or soften, carried alone like a coal in cupped hands—

—and by nightfall, she intended to be riding.
