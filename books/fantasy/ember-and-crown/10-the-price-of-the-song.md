## 10. The Price of the Song

The hollow village kept its secrets the way other places kept grain: in jars, in cellars, in the hands of old women who had nothing left to lose by telling.

Sefa found hers at the well at dusk, drawing water because her arms needed something to do that wasn't shaking the ash-priest's blood out of them. The ambush at the river was two days behind them, and Noor's swordsong still rang in her teeth when she thought about it—that impossible chord of steel and voice that had unmade six armed men like a breath through a candleflame. She'd been a dragon-warden for eleven years. She had seen fire do terrible things and beautiful ones. She had never seen a man *sing* a battle into ending.

"Girl." The old woman's name was Hesta; she baked bread with ash in it and hung her door facing west like everyone else in the hollow. She took the bucket from Sefa's hands with the casual authority of someone who had been taking things from younger people for eighty years. "You keep looking at your swordspeaker like he's a puzzle you mean to solve."

"I'm looking at him like he's a liability I'm chained to."

"Mm." Hesta lowered the bucket again, unhurried. "Then why do you count his breaths when he sleeps?"

Sefa said nothing, which was answer enough, and Hesta smiled with three teeth and began, as if continuing a conversation they'd been having for years: "You want to know what the song costs. Everyone does, eventually. The exiles used to say it plain, back when there were swordspeakers to say it about." She hauled the bucket up, water sloshing silver in the last light. "A year, girl. Every verse costs a year of memory. Not strength. Not years of *life*—the Crown's priests would love it if it were only that, easy to tax. Memory. The singer chooses nothing about what burns. The song takes what it takes, oldest first, closest first. It eats from the middle outward, like rot in an apple."

The bucket stopped halfway to the well's lip.

Sefa heard herself say, very evenly, "That's a story for children."

"It's an oath," Hesta said. "Oaths are always stories for children, until someone pays one." She set the bucket down between them and looked at Sefa with eyes gone pale as river ice. "Your grandmother paid it once. Did you know that? Before the burning. She sang the door shut over a village full of children so the priests couldn't follow. They wrote her name in the exile rolls for it—*Sefa, daughter-line of Vhalcrest, marked for the vigil.* The Crown has been raising her blood to guard their fire ever since. Poets couldn't invent anything crueler."

The world went narrow and ringing. Sefa gripped the well's stone lip and did not fall into it.

"My grandmother," she said, "was a laundress in Corren's Ford."

"On paper," Hesta agreed. "Everything is on paper. That's rather the point of paper."

* * *

She didn't confront him that night. She wanted to—her hands actually shook with wanting to—but eleven years of warden discipline is not nothing, and some part of her, the part that had read the sealed file on Vhalcrest by candlelight until dawn, understood that this information was a blade and she should know exactly where she meant to cut before she drew it.

So instead she watched.

They lodged in the loft above the bakehouse, Noor below her on a pallet near the door, because he always took the position nearest the entrance and she had stopped arguing about it somewhere around the second ambush. He slept badly. She had noticed that before, filed it under *guilt* or *fear*, the ordinary insomnia of hunted men. But now she watched with new eyes, and what she saw rearranged itself.

He woke twice in the night and lay staring at the rafters, moving his lips. Counting? Naming? On the third waking he sat up, fumbled in his coat, and produced a small battered notebook—the guild ledger habit worn down to pocket size—and wrote by the light of the banked oven-glow below. He wrote for a long time. His shoulders were wrong while he did it. She knew that shape of shoulders. She'd seen it on wardens copying out reports about dragons they loved and would have to cull.

That's a man taking inventory, she thought. That's a man checking what's still in the storehouse.

In the morning she tested him. Casually, over bread, the way you test any suspect.

"What did you think of Corren's Ford?" she asked. "You said you passed through it once, trading."

He chewed. Frowned slightly—not the frown of a lie being built, which she knew intimately, but the frown of a man reaching for a shelf and finding it empty. "Did I say that?"

"You did. The ford town. River crossing, wool market, a shrine to the drowned."

"Noor," he said slowly, tasting the name like a word in a language he was relearning. "I know my name. I know the guildhall—I can smell it if I try, tallow and dust and Maro's terrible tea. I know—" His hand went, without his seeming to decide it, to the scarred palm where the oath-mark sat. "I know I had a sister. I don't know her face anymore. I've known that since the courier died. I assumed—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I assumed grief had done it. Grief does that. Wears things smooth."

"And the ford?"

"The ford is just a word you gave me." He looked at her then, directly, and there was no evasion in it, only a kind of exhausted honesty that made her chest hurt worse than any lie would have. "Why are you asking me about towns, Sefa?"

Because I watched you write yourself down in the dark like a man bailing a leaking boat, she did not say. Because an old woman at a well told me the song eats you from the middle outward, and I have been doing arithmetic all night, and the arithmetic says you've sung four times since the courier died, and four years is gone from you, and you *knew*, you must have known the price when you swore—

"Nothing," she said. "Forget it."

He almost smiled. "That's the one thing I can't seem to do."

* * *

She cornered him properly at midday, in the strip of scrubland behind the village where the map's second waymark pulled at them like a hook in the flesh. He was practicing—or trying to. Standing alone with his sword drawn, mouth opening around the first syllable of the hinge-song and closing again, over and over, like a man testing ice that keeps cracking.

"Don't," she said.

He didn't jump; she'd give him that. He just lowered the sword and waited, which was somehow more infuriating.

"A year," she said. "Per verse. That's the oath. That's what the exiles' records say, and Hesta says, and probably what your own guild ledger says if anyone bothered to read the front matter instead of the prices." She crossed the scrub grass until she was close enough to see the fine lines of exhaustion around his eyes that she'd been cataloguing for days and mislabeling. "How many times, Noor. Since the courier. How many verses."

His jaw worked. For a moment she thought he'd lie, and she felt something ugly rise in her throat ready for it—and then he simply said, "Four," and the ugliness had nowhere to go, so it turned inward and became something worse.

"Four *years*." Her voice came out low and flat, warden-flat, the voice she used on people about to do something fatal. "Four years of your life, burned off the inside, and you didn't tell me. You let me plan routes with you. You let me stand watch assuming we were two whole people watching each other's backs, and every time you opened your mouth and saved us, you were quietly amputating yourself, and you said *nothing*—"

"There wasn't time at the river."

"There's time now! There was time after the first one!" She was shouting; she could hear it bouncing off the hills and hated it and couldn't stop. "Do you understand what you've done? Do you understand what I could have—if I'd known, I could have planned around it, I could have kept you from singing unless it was the last resort, I could have—"

"Could have what?" And now his voice rose too, and there was something underneath it she hadn't heard before, something cracked open and bleeding. "Could have fought the priests for me? Died in my place? Sefa, listen to what you're saying. You're a disgraced warden with a price on your head and a birthmark you learned about two days ago. I'm a ledger-keeper who happens to be the only key to a dead country. Neither of us gets to be careful. The song is the only weapon I have, and every time I reach for it, I get to choose: us or me. Four times I've chosen us. I'd choose it forty more. What exactly did you want me to tell you—that I'm spending myself down to the wick to keep you alive? Would that have changed anything except how it felt?"

"Yes!" It tore out of her before she could dress it up. "Yes, it would have changed—it would have—" 

She stopped. Breathed. The hills were quiet again, embarrassed by them both.

"You don't get to decide alone," she said finally, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "That's the whole point of—of whatever this is. Two people walking the same road. You don't get to be a martyr in secret and call it protection. I've *read* what the Crown does with martyrs. They make monuments out of them and weapons out of the monuments. If you're going to burn, you burn in the open, where I can see the flame, or so help me I will hog-tie you and carry you to the door myself and let the country sing its own damned way out."

He stared at her. Wind moved the scrub grass. Somewhere behind them, in the village, Hesta's oven breathed its ash-smell into the afternoon.

"That's the most words I've ever heard you spend on anything," he said at last.

"I'm furious. It loosens the tongue."

"Furious at me."

"Furious at you," she confirmed. "Furious at the oath. Furious at whoever designed a magic that bills a man's own past as ammunition. Mostly furious that you've been carrying this since the courier died, alone, writing yourself down in a notebook at night like—" Her voice caught, traitorous. She pushed through it. "Like inventory. Like stock that's running out."

He looked down at the notebook in his hand, and something in his face folded. "It helps," he said quietly. "Writing it. When I wake up and something's missing—a gap where a thing used to be—I write down that it's missing. So at least the absence is recorded. Guild training." A ghost of humor, gray as ash. "We keep meticulous books."

"Show me."

"Sefa—"

"*Show me.*"

He hesitated. Then, because he was, whatever else he was, an honest man in the ways that mattered, he handed it over.

She read standing there in the wind, and each page hit her like a step down into colder water.

*Day 3. Lost: the name of the street I grew up on. I remember the house but not how to reach it.*

*Day 9. Lost: my mother's voice. I know she sang. I cannot find the tune.*

*Day 16. Lost: the taste of Maro's terrible tea. This one I mind less.*

*Day 22. Lost: —*

And there the entry stopped, a blank line followed by a single shaky sentence: *Something else is gone. I can't tell what. That's the worst one yet.*

Sefa closed the book with great care, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

"Twenty-two days," she said. "You've been paying for twenty-two days, and the gaps are already starting to hide from you." She looked up at him—at this man she'd met as a target, a mark, a job, and who had become, somewhere between the river and the well, the person whose breathing she counted in the dark. "Noor. How much of you is left? If the door needs a dozen more verses. If getting home means singing the whole road. What's left at the end?"

"I don't know," he said. "Nobody knows. The last swordspeaker who tried the full song didn't leave records." He paused. "Or maybe he did, and nobody could remember whose handwriting it was."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's *not*—" She pressed the notebook against her sternum like a shield, and heard her own voice drop to something raw. "I spent my whole life serving a fire that belongs to the Crown. Guarding an ember they hoard in a tower while a country's people scatter like seed. And the first true thing I've ever been given to protect is a man who spends himself like small change and calls it duty." She stepped closer. Close enough that he had to look up at her, close enough that he couldn't pretend to misunderstand. "So here's how it's going to be, swordspeaker. From today, I hold the book. Every verse you sing, I record it—you don't lift a finger. Every gap you find, you tell me, and I write it down, and I keep it safe, because apparently someone has to keep *you*. And when we reach the next waymark, we decide together whether the song is worth the price. Together. Am I understood?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, slowly, he reached out and took the notebook back from her—not pulling it away, but folding her fingers around it, closing her grip over the cover.

"Keep it," he said. "But hear me, Sefa. There may come a verse that isn't a choice. The door, the ember, the last waymark—if it comes down to singing or losing everything, I'll sing. You can write it down afterward. But you can't stop me, and I won't insult you by promising otherwise."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm coming with you all the way to the threshold. Someone has to remember you, if the song won't."

Something moved across his face—too fast to name, too heavy to ignore. He nodded once, and turned back toward the waymark, toward the field where a door stood alone in the grass, and she fell into step beside him, the notebook hard against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Behind them, at the edge of the village, Hesta stood in her west-facing doorway, watching them go. And beside her, half-hidden in the shadow of the jamb, stood a rider in traveling grays with a warden's horn slung at her hip—someone Sefa had last seen across a tribunal table, someone who was supposed to be a hundred miles south.

Someone who raised one hand, not in threat, and not quite in greeting either, and turned her horse toward the road ahead of them.
