## 12. The Vigil-Tower

The Vigil-Tower of Corvane had no door that faced the city. That was the first thing they taught you as a warden: a tower that guards fire should not look like it wants visitors. You entered through the ash-yard, past the kennels where the last hounds of the vigil slept in shifts, and up a stair cut so narrow that two people could not pass without one turning their back. Sefa had climbed it four thousand times. She knew every worn hollow in the stone, every place where her boot found its old groove without asking her permission.

She had never once climbed it with a prisoner.

"Keep your wrists loose," she murmured, not looking at Noor. "If you fight the manacles, they'll read it as resistance and I'll have to answer for it."

"I'm not fighting anything." His voice was low, roughened by the road. Three days since Maro's messenger, three days since he'd learned his whole life was an entry in someone else's ledger, and he had gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. A man with nothing left to lose walks differently. He walks like the ground owes him nothing.

"Good," she said. "Because everything about this is wrong, and if we're caught, wrongness is the only thing they'll see."

The ash-yard was empty at this hour, the sky still the color of wet slate. The hounds lifted their heads as she passed—old Vask, gray-muzzled now, who had been a pup when she took her oath—and she gave them the hand-sign for *duty*, not *stranger*, and watched them settle. Even the dogs would remember this morning. Even the dogs might be asked.

At the tower's foot, a warden of the second rank stood post: young Pellin, who still polished his badge. "Warden Sefa." His eyes went to Noor, to the manacles, to the scorched map-case hanging from her belt like a wound she'd decided to carry openly. "You're... bringing him in?"

"I have first-rights of presentation," she said. "Any warden may present a prisoner of oath-relevance to the ember's chamber. It's in the Codicils."

"It's in the *old* Codicils," Pellin said, and there it was—the hesitation she'd been counting on, and the doubt beneath it. Young wardens were taught the new rules. They were not taught why the old ones existed, only that they'd been struck through in red ink by order of High Warden Ilset herself, eleven years ago, the year the ember's decline began to be measured in seasons instead of years.

"Then you'll want to check," Sefa said pleasantly. "Go on. Wake Ilset. Tell her Warden Sefa has arrived unannounced with the swordspeaker of Vhalcrest bound and presented under first-rights, and ask her whether I'm to be admitted or turned away." She let that sit. "Take your time. I'll wait here. Loudly."

Pellin's throat moved. Every warden knew what happened to the junior who woke the High Warden before dawn over a question of codicils. Every warden also knew what happened to the junior who turned away a first-rights presentation that later turned out to matter.

He stepped aside. "Third landing," he said. "I'll log you through."

"Don't bother logging me," Sefa said. "Log the prisoner. I'm just the chain he's attached to."

* * *

The stair was worse than she remembered, or perhaps she was worse than she remembered. Each turn brought back some old morning—her oath-day, seventeen years old, her palm pressed to the vigil-stone while the priests sang the binding; the first time she'd seen the ember, small as a coal in a brazier the size of a chapel, and felt something in her chest lean toward it like a plant toward light. She had thought then that the leaning was duty. She had spent years learning it was grief, wearing a uniform.

Noor climbed behind her, chains whispering. At the second landing he said, quietly: "Your hands are shaking."

"My hands are fine."

"You're holding the rail like it owes you money."

Despite everything—a fugitive's dawn, a dead country's map against her hip—something in her mouth almost bent toward a smile. "Vhalcrest idiom?"

"Guild idiom. We say it about ledgers." A pause. "Sefa. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong. It'll go *badly*, which is different. Wrong means we made a mistake. Badly just means we're honest people in a dishonest building."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." She stopped at the third landing's door, iron-banded, marked with the vigil's sign: a flame inside a closed fist. She pressed her badge to it and felt the old recognition crawl across her skin, the tower reading her, counting her years of service, weighing her against whatever it weighed now. For one long moment she thought it would refuse her—that the building itself had already been told about the traitor Sefa, the warden who read sealed files, who walked rivers backward with oath-breakers.

The lock opened.

Inside, the ember-chamber.

She had prepared herself for it to be smaller. Everything shrinks when you return to it. But the chamber was vast as ever, a hollowed throat of black stone rising beyond sight, and there at its heart burned the ember of Vhalcrest—the last ember, the true fire, the one the histories called *the hearth that walked out of the burning*.

It was dying.

Not guttering the way a candle gutters, not honestly, not the way fire dies when fire is finished. It was being *starved*. She could see it now with eyes that had spent weeks among exiles' descendants, eating bread baked with ash, learning what Vhalcrest fire was supposed to look like. The ember sat in its great brazier like a heart in a chest too large for it, pulsing slow, each pulse fainter than the last, and around it the air did something air should not do—it *hesitated*, as if the room itself kept forgetting whether to breathe.

"Noor," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Look at the feed-troughs."

There were six of them, channels cut into the floor radiating from the brazier, built—I saw it now, understood it all at once—to carry fuel to the fire. Wood. Oil. In older days, the wardens' own sworn breath, sung down the channels in the ember-feeding rites her grandmother's generation had performed.

Five of the channels were plugged with stone and sealed with Crown wax.

Only one remained open, and down that single channel, in the grey pre-dawn light, came the procession.

They were ash-priests, six of them, robed in white gone grey at the hems, and they carried a litter between them. On the litter, chained down with lead—lead, the metal that hates magic, the metal the Crown uses to make sure gifts stay gifts—lay a bottled thunderstorm.

Sefa heard Noor stop breathing beside her.

She knew that storm. Every keeper in the Guild of Impossible Things knew that storm: catalogued under *Weather, Imprisoned, Medium-Violent*, acquired forty years ago off a drowned ship, traded three times, coveted always. It hummed even through the lead, a low restless note like a hive deciding whether to swarm. And the priests carried it toward the open channel with the tenderness of men feeding a sacred beast, and set it at the channel's head, and broke the bottle.

The storm went down the trough like water finding its old bed. Lightning crawled along the cut stone, blue-white, screaming silently, and poured into the ember, and the ember flared—

—and steadied. For a moment. One bright, furious moment, the whole chamber lit gold, shadows thrown huge up the black walls, and Sefa saw Noor's face in that light and understood that he was seeing it too.

The flare lasted nine heartbeats. Then it sank, faster than it had risen, because a storm is not a hearth-fire, lightning is not dragon-flame, and the ember drank the stolen miracle the way a starving man drinks spirits—a blaze, a shudder, a deeper emptiness after.

"They're feeding it," Noor whispered. "Why would they—if they want it dead—"

"They don't want it dead." Sefa's anger arrived all at once, fully formed, the way storms arrive over mountains. "They want it *dependent*. Look at the seals on those channels. Look at the wax—Crown green, renewed every year. Eleven years ago they closed the true feeds and left one, and now the ember lives exactly as long as the Crown keeps feeding it confiscated impossible things. Every storm, every shadow, every door-piece your guild ever surrendered at the border—" she was shaking now, and let herself shake— "it all comes here. It's not a relic. It's a hostage. And it's not dying, Noor. It's being *murdered slowly*, on a schedule, so that the Crown can decide the hour."

From the shadow of the far arch, a voice said: "Warden Sefa has always understood arithmetic quickly."

Ilset.

Her mentor stepped into the ember-light alone, hands folded, silver-haired, wearing the plain grey of high office rather than the white of the priests. Behind her, at the arch, the six feeding-priests had gone very still, and Sefa counted them without meaning to—six, plus Pellin somewhere below, plus whoever else the tower held—and knew the numbers were bad.

"You logged yourself in," Ilset said. "First-rights. Clever. Old law, and the tower honors old law even when I strike it from the books—the building remembers more than the Order does." Her gaze moved to Noor, to the manacles, and something flickered there that was not quite contempt. "And you brought him in chains. Even cleverer. Or perhaps not cleverness at all. Perhaps you wanted to see it for yourself before you chose."

"I've chosen," Sefa said.

"You chose three weeks ago, on a riverbank, when you didn't put a knife in him." Ilset's voice was gentle, which was how Sefa knew the blow was coming. "I've read your file too, child. All of it. Including the page you weren't meant to find—the exile rolls. Your grandmother's name is written in them. Merel of the Hollow. Do you imagine I don't know? I signed the seal on that file myself, twenty years ago, to keep it from girls like you until you were old enough to survive knowing."

Sefa's breath went out of her. "You *knew*—"

"I know everything in this tower. That is my curse and my function." Ilset walked closer, unhurried, and the ember pulsed behind her, dimming, dimming, the stolen storm already spent. "So understand me clearly. The Crown requires the ember dependent. The priests require it fearful. But I—" for the first time, her composure cracked, a hairline fracture across an old mask— "I require only that someone survives what's coming. If the ember fails wild, unreplenished, it doesn't fade. It *detonates*. Everything within a hundred miles. Every ledger, every door, every impossible thing goes up with it—and takes the city."

Noor spoke from beside her, quiet as a blade leaving cloth. "Or someone carries it out. Through the last door. Before it fails either way."

Ilset looked at him for a long moment. "Yes," she said simply. "That is the other arithmetic. And that is why I am going to do the thing I swore I never would." She reached into her robe and drew out a key—not iron, not lead, but a bar of some dark glassy substance that seemed to drink the ember-light rather than reflect it. "The ember's key. Warden's birthright, passed hand to hand since the founding. Take it. Take him. Go to your door, swordspeaker, and carry the fire out of my tower before the priests decide the schedule themselves."

Sefa stared at her. "Ilset—you'll be—"

"Branded. Executed. Remembered badly." The old woman smiled, thin and dry as paper. "I was born in the hollow village too, child. Doors facing west. Ash in the bread. I stayed behind the wall all these years so that someone inside it would be standing here, on this morning, holding this key." She pressed it into Sefa's palm and closed Sefa's fingers over it, and her hands were steady, and her eyes were not. "Now go. Because I hear boots on the stair, and they are not yours."

Down through the tower, muffled by stone, came the sound of many feet climbing fast—and above them, thin and terrible, the first note of a priest's chant: the rite of seizure, the words that would seal the chamber, seal the tower, seal them in with a dying fire.

Ilset turned to face the arch, alone, straightening her grey robes like a woman dressing for her own funeral.

"Run," she said, without looking back. "And when the ember crosses the threshold—when it finally breathes free air—tell it the tower kept faith. Someone should tell it that."

The chant rose. The locks began to fall, one by one, like a countdown.

Sefa grabbed Noor's chained wrist and ran.
