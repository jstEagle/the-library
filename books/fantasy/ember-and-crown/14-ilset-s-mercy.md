## 14. Ilset's Mercy

The tower's edge was a place Sefa had stood a hundred times before—on watch, on ceremony, on the long gray dawns when the ember's light was measured and recorded and filed away like weather. She had never once thought of it as the edge of anything. It had simply been the top of her world.

Now it felt like the rim of a cliff she'd been walking toward all her life.

Wind came off the vigil-tower's crown in long, cold sheets, carrying the smell of ash and hot iron from the sanctum below, where the last ember of Vhalcrest guttered in its cage of glass and gold. Behind them, the stairwell door hung open and empty—for now. Noor leaned against the parapet, gray-faced, one hand pressed flat to his chest as if holding something in that wanted out. His eyes kept drifting shut and snapping open again, each blink costing him something she couldn't see.

"How much did you lose back there?" she asked.

He considered lying. She watched him decide not to. "The name of the street I grew up on," he said. "And my teacher's face. And—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Something else. I can feel the hole where it was. It's shaped like a song."

Sefa looked away, out over the city of Cindral, where the Crown's lamps were coming up one by one like a rash of orange stars. Somewhere down there, the ash-priests were already moving. She had heard the bells change their pattern two floors below—the alarm cadence, three-and-one, three-and-one, the rhythm every warden learned before they learned to hold a horn.

"They'll seal the tower," she said. "Ilset won't wait for them."

"Ilset." Noor pushed himself upright. "Your mentor. The one who offered to unbrand you."

"She keeps her word," Sefa said. "That's what makes her dangerous. A liar you can predict. Ilset will do exactly what she said: come for us herself, because she doesn't trust anyone else to do it cleanly." Sefa's hand found the warden's horn at her hip—an old habit, a dead language now. Her oath-mark had been burned off her wrist two nights ago; the scar there still pulled tight when she flexed her fingers, a ring of shiny tissue where the Crown's sigil used to sit. "She taught me everything. How to read wind over fire. How to hold a line. How to—" Her voice caught on something small and sharp. "How to know which orders to swallow and which to spit back. I just never thought she'd be on the swallowing side of it."

Noor said nothing. He was good at that, she'd learned. He listened the way other people fought—with his whole body.

The stairwell door groaned.

* * *

Ilset came up the stairs alone, and that alone told Sefa everything.

High Warden Ilset Terrowin did not go anywhere alone. She moved through the vigil like a keel through water, always with priests flanking her, always with the weight of the order arranged around her shoulders like a mantle. But the footsteps on the stair were singular, unhurried, and when she stepped through the doorway into the wind and the ember-light, her hands were empty of everything except a lantern and a ring of keys.

She wore no ceremonial gray. No ash-sigil at her throat. Just a warden's field coat, the kind they wore on the long patrols along the Burnline, patched at both elbows.

"You didn't run," Ilset said. Her voice was even, almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened Sefa more than shouting would have.

"The stairs are sealed below," Sefa said. "I checked."

"I know. I sealed them." Ilset set the lantern down on the parapet stones. Its light made a small warm island in all that cold. "I bought us an hour. The priests think I'm interrogating a traitor. Which, depending on how the next few minutes go, is either true or the worst lie I've ever told."

"Then let me make it easy for you." Sefa stepped forward, putting herself between Ilset and Noor—not because Noor needed shielding, but because some part of her needed to stand in this exact spot, in front of this exact woman, and say the thing she'd been carrying since the hollow village. "I burned my mark. I called the dragons. I'm taking him to the door. Every word of the charge sheet is true, and I'd sign it if they gave me a pen."

"I know." Ilset's gaze didn't waver. "I read your file. All of it. Including the parts the archivists thought were too sensitive for a High Warden's eyes." Something moved behind her expression then—something old, and tired, and buried so deep it had grown roots. "Including your grandmother's name. Vasquez-adjacent line, third register, exiled under the Ash Edict. You found it too, didn't you. That's why you cracked."

Sefa's breath went out of her. "How—"

"Because I found mine thirty years ago." Ilset said it quietly, the way you say a word you've only ever said to yourself. "Page four hundred and eleven of the sealed registry. Terrowin. My family name wasn't Terrowin before the burning, Sefa. It was Tarrowen. Vhalcrest spelling. My grandmother came out of the east hollows with nothing but a bread recipe and a hatred of closed doors." She laughed—a single dry note with no humor in it at all. "I joined the vigil to protect the realm from dragon-fire. I spent forty years guarding the cage of the thing that burned my own country down. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Noor shifted against the parapet. "You're telling us you knew."

"I suspected. There's a difference, and the difference is cowardice, and I have paid for it in full." Ilset turned to look out over the city, and in the lantern light Sefa saw her properly for the first time in years—not the High Warden, not the iron-backed pillar of the order, but an old woman with wind-chapped hands and a coat patched at the elbows, standing at the edge of the only life she had ever allowed herself. "Every year the ember dims, we tell the Crown it's stable. Every year they take more impossible things from your guild and feed them into the fire to prop it up, and we write reports saying the fire is grateful. I signed those reports. I signed them because the alternative was asking questions, and questions lead to registries, and registries lead to page four hundred and eleven."

"And now?" Sefa asked.

"Now the arithmetic has changed." Ilset reached into her coat and brought out a key.

It was small—smaller than Sefa expected, almost insultingly small for what it opened. Iron, unadorned, worn smooth at the bow by decades of handling. It hung from a cord, and the cord was stained dark at one end, and Sefa did not let herself think about what kind of stain survived thirty years on a warden's neck.

"The ember's cage has two locks," Ilset said. "The great seal, which takes three priests and a litany and half an hour of ceremony—that's the lock the Crown watches. And the warden's key, which takes one person and no words at all. It was built that way by the first wardens, back when the vigil remembered what it was for." She held it out across the space between them. "Not containment. Custody. We were supposed to keep the fire alive until someone came to carry it home."

Noor's voice was rough. "And no one came. For a hundred years."

"No one came," Ilset agreed. "Because everyone who might have was dead, or scattered, or had names that got changed at the border. Until a courier burned himself hollow crossing half the world to put a map in the hands of the one man whose oath could read it." Her eyes went to Noor, and there was something in them Sefa had never seen directed at anyone by Ilset Terrowin: reverence, plain and unarmored. "You should know what this costs, swordspeaker. What it costs *her*, I mean. Because she'll never say it, and someone should say it in front of witnesses."

"Ilset—" Sefa started.

"Hold your tongue, Warden. You've earned the rank back for five minutes." Ilset didn't look away from Noor. "If I give you this key, I stay behind. Not heroically—I'm too old and too slow to run, and someone has to stand in the breach and be the traitor they catch, or they'll tear the whole vigil apart looking for two instead of one. They will brand me. They will put me in the deep cells under the sanctum, next to the fire I kept, and they will ask me questions until I am too broken to remember my grandmother's name—which, given the company I'll be keeping, might be a mercy." She smiled then, and it was the worst thing Sefa had ever seen, because it was genuine. "And I will give you this key anyway. Because forty years ago I chose the Crown over page four hundred and eleven, and I am not going to die having made that choice only once."

The wind gusted. The lantern flame bent and recovered.

Sefa found she couldn't speak. Everything in her chest had gone tight and hot, and the words that wanted out were too large for any door she owned.

It was Noor who moved. He crossed the space between them slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal or a holy thing, and he took the key from Ilset's palm with both hands, the way you receive something that cannot be repaid.

"I don't forget things anymore," he said. "Not on purpose. The song takes what it takes, and I don't get to choose. But I'll tell you what I'll do instead." He closed his fingers around the iron. "When the ember crosses, when it's loose in the sky where no Crown can touch it—I'll make sure the story includes you. Not the version they'll write. The true one. The High Warden who sealed the stairs and bought an hour and paid for it in a cell. That's not memory. That's a promise, and promises are the one thing I keep even after everything else burns."

Ilset studied him for a long moment. Then she did something Sefa had witnessed perhaps three times in fifteen years of service: she bowed. Not the shallow court bow of rank. A full bend, warden to swordspeaker, the old form, the one from before the Ash Edict, from a country that officially did not exist.

"Warden Galloway," she said, straightening. "You have your mother's stubbornness. Don't argue—you don't know that yet, but you do. I trained beside her for six years before the reassignments. She spat back every bad order she was ever given, and half the good ones." A pause, and her voice roughened by a degree so slight anyone else would have missed it. "She'd be proud of the brand scar. She'd hate everything else about this plan. Do it anyway."

Sefa's vision blurred. She blinked it clear. "The priests. When they find the stairs sealed—"

"They'll come up the outside. Climbers, within the hour. Which means you have less than an hour, which means this conversation is over." Ilset picked up the lantern. At the stairwell door she stopped, one hand on the frame, her back to them both. "There's a maintenance descent on the north face. Old, unmapped since the second reign. It comes out in the tanners' quarter, and from there the river gate is a ten-minute walk if you don't mind getting wet." She glanced back over her shoulder, and for one unguarded instant she was not a High Warden at all, just a woman from the hollows looking at the road home she would never walk. "The bread in the village. The ash-bread. My grandmother's recipe had rosemary in it. If you get there—if any of you get there—tell them the Tarrowens kept the recipe. Tell them we never forgot the taste."

Then she was gone, down the stairs, into the dark, toward the sound of boots that had begun to echo somewhere far below.

* * *

They crossed the tower's crown in silence, hugging the parapet, the city spinning its slow lights beneath them. The north-face hatch was where Ilset said it would be, rusted shut until Noor put his shoulder to it and the hinges gave a shriek that Sefa was sure would bring the whole vigil down on them. It didn't. The wind ate the sound.

The descent was narrow, black, and smelled of pigeon droppings and old rain. Sefa went first, counting rungs, feeling Noor's weight shift on the ladder above her with every step. Halfway down, in the total dark, he spoke.

"She knew my mother."

"I heard."

"A warden knew my mother, and I—" His voice frayed. "I had a whole life, Sefa. Ledgers and ledgers of it. And I never knew any of the pages that mattered."

Sefa paused on the ladder, the cold stone pressing her cheek, and thought about page four hundred and eleven, and about a woman who had chosen cowardice once and refused to choose it twice, and about the taste of bread she'd never eaten baked from a recipe she'd never known.

"Then we write them down," she said. "All of it. Hers, yours, Ilset's. When this is done, we start a ledger that holds everything the Crown tried to burn." She resumed climbing down into the dark. "That's the guild way, isn't it? Nothing impossible gets lost if somebody writes it down."

Above her, in the black, she heard him laugh—a small, cracked, real sound—and it was the best thing she'd heard in days.

They came out through the tanners' yard at moonrise, soaked to the knees, smelling gloriously terrible. The river gate glimmered ahead, and beyond it the ash-roads ran west toward the hollows, toward the villagers who would be waiting, toward the door.

Behind them, atop the vigil-tower, a light flared—brief, white, furious—and Sefa knew the climbers had reached the crown and found it empty, and knew what would happen next, and made herself keep walking.

Somewhere up there, Ilset Terrowin was turning to face her own priests with her hands empty and her spine straight, buying them their hour.

Sefa did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people with time. She took Noor's arm, and they walked into the west, carrying a key, a promise, and a debt that could only be paid by finishing what they'd started.
