## 15. The Road of Ash

The ash-roads began where the map said they would: at a crossroads with no roads.

Four ruts ran out from the crossing into the gray country, worn deep by wheels that had not rolled in a hundred years, and each one ended in nothing—no village, no ford, no gate. Just grass grown up through old cinders, and the wind moving through it like a hand smoothing a bed no one slept in anymore.

Noor stood at the center and let the map breathe against his chest. It had stopped behaving like paper somewhere outside the hollow village. Now it warmed when he was walking true and cooled when he strayed, the way a sleeping animal warms to the hand it trusts. The third waymark pulsed under his palm, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"That one," Sefa said. She pointed down the westernmost rut, where the cinders lay thickest. "The road remembers being walked."

"You can read that?"

"I'm a warden." She adjusted the strap of her pack and did not look at him. "I read fire. Ash is just fire that finished its sentence."

They had been two days out from Ilset's mercy, and neither of them had spoken of it—the tower's edge, the key pressed into Sefa's glove, the High Warden turning back toward her own priests with her spine straight and her face already becoming a stranger's. Some things were too heavy to carry in conversation. They carried them instead in silence, which held more.

Noor touched the waymark and sang.

He had learned by now how little it took. Three notes, no words—or words so old they had worn smooth of meaning—and the swordsong came up through the oath-mark on his palm like water finding a crack in stone. The air over the dead crossroads shivered. For half a breath he saw it whole: carts, lanterns, a woman laughing at something a child had said, the smell of bread and hot iron. A hundred years ago, compressed into a heartbeat, and then gone.

And with it went something of his.

He didn't know what until he reached for it. He tried to remember his mother's face and found—he found the shape of loving her, wide and warm as a hearth-room, but the face above it was smoke. He knew she had laughed with her whole body. He could not remember what her laugh sounded like. He knew she had a scar on her left thumb from a baking knife. He could not remember her eyes.

"Noor." Sefa's hand closed on his arm. "You went somewhere."

"Payment," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. He had been practicing level. "The song takes its fee."

"How much?"

"One memory per waymark. Roughly. The map doesn't itemize." He smiled, because if he didn't, the thing underneath would get out. "It's honest trade, at least. Everything in Vhalcrest was."

She stared at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do himself. Eleven waymarks between here and the door. He had sung four since the tower. Four memories gone, and seven still owed, and after the last one—

"Don't," he said quietly. "Whatever you're about to say. Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything." She turned west and started walking. "I was going to count."

* * *

The exiles caught up with them at dusk on the second day.

Noor heard them before he saw them—not footsteps but a sound like a ledger being opened, pages turning in the wind, and then the map against his chest grew so warm he had to shift it away from his skin. He turned. On the rise behind them stood perhaps forty figures, backlit by a low red sun, and at their head walked Maren of the hollow village with her ash-bread loaves stacked in a sling across her back, and beside her, leaning on a staff cut fresh from guildhall oak, old Tam Vess, who had sold bottled thunderstorms for thirty years and never once opened one.

"We followed the warmth," Maren said simply. "The map calls to us. Has been calling since the night you sang at the river. Some of us thought we were Vhalcrest-born the way you're born left-handed—a quirk, a story grandmothers tell." Her chin lifted. "We're not quirks. We're a country."

Behind her, Noor counted faces he knew from the guild's ledgers. The widow Osla, whose second shadow had been catalogued under *miscellaneous phenomena, sentimental*. The twins Ferro and Bram, who dealt in keys. A dozen more whose names he had written in his own careful hand without ever once asking what they carried or why.

"You understand what I'm asking you to walk toward," he said. "Ash-priests behind us. A dying country ahead of us. And me—I'm spending myself to open the road. By the end there may be very little of me left to thank you."

Tam Vess laughed, a dry rustle like a ledger closing. "Boy, I've spent sixty years selling impossible things to people who wanted to feel the world was bigger than their kitchens. You think I don't know the price of wonder?" He tapped his staff on the cinders. "Every relic in my pack is a piece of home I helped smuggle out before the burning. I've been keeping it safe. I'd like to see where it goes."

Maren said nothing. She just took a loaf from her sling, broke it, and handed half to Noor. It was baked with ash, the old way, the way that made the hollow village's bread taste like remembering. He ate it standing in the road, and forty people watched him do it, and none of them spoke, because some communions are quiet.

Sefa stood apart, arms folded, watching the exiles the way she watched everything now—with the hunger of someone reading a file she'd once been ordered to burn. When Noor caught her eye she shrugged.

"They'll slow us down," she said. "Old ones, children. Priests ride."

"Priests also burn," Noor said. "And burning takes time. We'll manage."

It was the first lie he told her on the road. He did not know yet that it would not be the last.

* * *

The fifth waymark was a well.

It stood alone in a field of cinders, roofless, its bucket long rotted, and when Noor laid his palm on the cold stone lip the map pulled at him like a tide. This one cost more than the others. He felt it go—felt the specific shape of the loss, though not the loss itself, which was the cruelest part. He knew he had forgotten something large. He could feel the hole where it had been, the way your tongue finds a missing tooth.

He sang. The well filled with light from the bottom up, and for a moment the whole company saw it brimming with clear water, and a girl on the far rim dipping a tin cup, and the smell of cold stone and evening. Then dark again, and dry, and a hundred years of silence settling back over the field like snow.

"What did it take?" Sefa asked. She had taken to asking every time now. She kept a list—she had shown him, once, a page in her warden's field-book with four entries in her blunt handwriting, and she added this one as a fifth. *Waymark five. Unknown.*

"The guildhall," he realized, as he said it. The knowledge arrived whole, the way the losses always arrived after they'd already been paid. "The smell of it. Beeswax and lamp-oil and old paper and Maro's terrible pipe tobacco. Forty years of my life I spent in that smell, and it's gone. I know I loved it. I can't remember why."

Sefa wrote it down. Her jaw was tight.

"You should stop writing them," he said. "It changes nothing."

"It changes everything." She snapped the book shut. "You're paying in a currency only you can spend, and nobody's keeping the books. Somebody has to. That's what a warden *is*, Noor—a keeper of accounts that other people are too frightened or too busy to keep." She looked at him, and in the last light her eyes were the color of banked coals. "When this is over, if there's an over, I'm giving you this book back. Every line. And you're going to hate me for some of them, and I'm going to make you read them anyway."

He wanted to kiss her. He noted the wanting carefully, the way he'd once noted inventory, and filed it under things to survive until the door.

* * *

The priests found them on the seventh day.

Not all of them—Ilset's stand at the tower had cost the order too, and the Crown's reach thinned the farther the road ran from paved country. But six riders came out of the eastern haze at midmorning, white-and-gray vestments stiff with ash, censers swinging, and the exiles scattered like startled birds before Noor could even shout an order.

What happened next happened fast and badly. Ferro and Bram got the children behind the ruined wall of a wayshrine. Tam Vess stood his ground in front of his pack with a walking stick and no sense whatsoever. Maren put herself bodily between a priest and Sefa, and the priest raised his censer, and the smoke that poured out of it was not smoke—it was the anti-song, the ash-choir's counter-melody, designed to smother a swordspeaker's voice in his own throat.

Noor felt it close around him like a hand. The oath-mark went cold. He opened his mouth and produced nothing—not a note, not a whisper, just the horrible ordinary silence of a man with no power at all.

A priest walked toward him unhurried, drawing a blade of gray glass. "Oath-breaker," the man said, almost gently. "The Crown forgives much. It does not forgive singing."

And then Sefa put the horn to her lips.

She had carried it since the vigil-tower—one of the warden's horns, dragon-bone, meant to call what dragons remained. She had sworn she'd never blow it; the last time a horn had sounded in her family, her grandmother had been marched out of her own kitchen at spear-point. But she lifted it now, and the note that came out was not a call. It was a *name*. It went through the counter-smoke like a blade through canvas, and the ash-choir's melody shredded, and every priest within earshot staggered as if the ground had tilted.

In the ringing silence after, Noor found his voice again. He sang the waymark under his feet awake, and the old road blazed into being around them—lantern-light, cart-wheels, the ghost of a market day—and the horses of the Crown bolted, because horses have better sense than men about ghosts.

The priests regrouped. Of course they did. But they regrouped further back than they'd advanced, and by nightfall they had lost the company entirely in the hill country, and the exiles crept out of their hiding places one by one, blinking, alive.

Sefa lowered the horn. Her hands were shaking.

"My grandmother's name," she said, to no one, to the dark. "That note. It was hers. It's in the sealed files—wardens record the songs of everyone they exile, in case—" She stopped. Started again. "I just called dragons using a song the Crown stole from a woman it burned out of her home."

"Yes," Noor said.

"And it worked."

"Yes."

She looked at the horn for a long time. Then she slung it across her back, next to the sword she no longer pretended was ceremonial, and said, "Good," in a voice that promised the Crown a great deal of trouble.

* * *

The eighth waymark took Sefa's first words to him.

He didn't know that until afterward, when he tried to summon the memory the way he'd begun doing each night—lying awake, walking backward through what remained, checking the locks on his doors the way he'd once checked the guild's. *The first time I met Sefa.* He had it: the guildhall, rain, a courier dying on the floor. He had her standing in the doorway. He had her face, wet with storm-light, furious and afraid.

He did not have what she said.

There was a clean absence where the words had been, shaped exactly like a sentence, and he sat up in the dark of the camp and pressed his palms against his eyes and understood, finally and completely, what he was doing. Not trading. Not paying. *Unmaking.* Each waymark unpicked one thread of the person named Noor Vasquez, and the person remaining was going to arrive at the last door lighter than any man should be—held together by duty and by whatever the people around him refused to let fall.

"Sefa." His voice cracked on her name, which frightened him more than the priests had. "Your first words to me. At the guildhall. What were they?"

She was quiet a long moment. The fire had burned down to coals, and their light moved on her face like slow water.

"'You're in my way,'" she said. "'Move, or I move you.'"

He laughed. It hurt, and it healed, both at once. "Of course they were."

"Why?" Sharp now, wary. "Why do you want to know? What did you forget?"

"Nothing," he lied, gently, and turned his face to the fire so she couldn't read it. "Nothing important."

She didn't believe him. He could feel her not believing him across the whole width of the camp. But she was tired, and the road was long, and some mercies are just postponements, and she let it go—for now.

* * *

On the eleventh day they came down off the hills and saw the place where Vhalcrest had been.

There was nothing to see. That was the horror of it. No ruins, no blackened foundations, no mass grave of chimneys. The Crown had not merely burned the country; it had *edited* it. The land where a nation lived had been plowed, planted, and renamed, and now there were wheat fields to the horizon and a fat market town with a name from a Crown charter, and farmers who had never been told what soil they were rich in.

But the map burned against Noor's chest like a coal, and the twelfth waymark glowed beneath the wheat, and when he knelt and pressed his palm to the earth the whole company felt it—a hum, deep as a root, rising through boot-soles and knee-bones and the fillings of old teeth.

"They're still here," Maren whispered. Tears ran freely down her face and she made no move to wipe them. "Under the wheat. Under the *names*. They never left."

"The ember's almost out," Sefa said. She had the warden's instruments out, the old brass instruments Ilset had given her along with the key, and her voice was flat with the effort of keeping it flat. "One moon's turn. Maybe less. Whatever door we find, we find it soon, or we find ashes."

Noor stood. The hum was still in his bones, and the map was warm as a living thing, and somewhere under the wheat a country waited for its people with the patience of the nearly dead.

"Then we don't stop," he said. "Twelve waymarks behind us. How many to go?"

Sefa checked the map, and her face changed.

"Noor," she said. "There aren't any more waymarks."

He took the map from her. She was right. The road of small lights—the wells, the shrines, the crossroads, the doors—simply stopped, a day's walk short of the wheat's edge, and beyond that point the parchment showed nothing at all. Not blank. *Nothing.* The ink itself declined to exist there.

Except, at the very center of the nothing, one mark. Fresh. Wet. Shining as if drawn seconds ago, in ink the color of ember-light:

A door, standing open.
