## 16. Sefa's Choice

The news came by crow.

Not a living crow—a paper one, folded from vigil-stationery and charmed to fly, its wings creased with official seals. It dropped out of the grey morning onto the flat stone where Sefa was washing her hands in a stream, unfolded itself midair, and read itself aloud in the dry, disapproving voice of the Registrar of Wards.

*By order of the Crown's Vigil of Ash, Warden Sefa Idris-Vane is hereby declared traitor in absentia. Her oath-mark is revoked. Her name is struck from the rolls of the dragon-vigil. Any warden, priest, or honest subject who delivers her—or the swordspeaker Noor Vasquez—to vigil authority will be rewarded according to law. Warden—former Warden—Iseth Vane, mentor of record, is commended for her diligence.*

The paper crow caught fire on its last syllable, as such things did when their message was finished, and the ash of it drifted down into the stream and swirled away toward the sea.

Sefa stood very still with her wet hands dripping.

Struck from the rolls. She had imagined this moment, in the way a soldier imagines the wound that might someday find him: clinically, at arm's length, with a certain grim curiosity about how much it would hurt. Now she knew. It did not hurt like a blade. It hurt like a door closing in a house you had lived in your whole life—the small domestic finality of it, the click of the latch.

"Traitor," she said aloud, testing the word against the trees. The trees said nothing. Somewhere upslope, Noor was breaking camp, and the smell of woodsmoke reached her thin and ordinary, and she found that she could not make the word *traitor* mean anything at all when she held it up against that smell, that smoke, that man's careful hands feeding sticks to a fire because she liked tea in the morning and he remembered it even though he was forgetting everything else.

She dried her hands on her coat and went up the slope to tell him.

He took it better than she expected, which was worse somehow. He just nodded, the way he nodded at entries in a ledger, and said, "Then they'll stop sending patrols in twos. They'll send them in fives." And he began repacking the map's case with those same careful hands, as if the Crown's whole machinery were simply another column of numbers to be balanced.

"They'll send more than patrols," she said. "They sent me a pardon."

Noor looked up.

"It wasn't in the crow," she said. "It was underneath it. Folded into the ash before it burned—you learn to read what priests burn, growing up around them." She pulled the scrap from her pocket, though she hardly needed to; she had read it a dozen times since dawn. *Deliver the swordspeaker to any vigil station within one moon's turn and all charges are remitted. Full restoration of rank, mark, and roll.*

"And if you don't?"

"Then at the end of the moon's turn my face goes up on every waystone between here and the capital, and every warden horn in the country learns my name as prey."

Noor considered this with the maddening calm of a man who had already spent his fear somewhere else. "You could do it," he said quietly. "I'd understand. You've paid enough for me already. Ilset offered you the same bargain in the tower, and you chose then—but choices get harder the longer they're open. That's the arithmetic no one teaches wardens."

"I'm not a warden anymore," Sefa said. "Remember? Struck from the rolls." And then, because his kindness deserved honesty and honesty deserved more than she usually gave it: "I did think about it. For about the length of time it takes to burn a letter. Then I thought about the ember, guttering in that tower while they feed it confiscated thunderstorms and bottled shadows to keep it small. I thought about my grandmother's name in the exile file, written in some registrar's neat hand like a debt to be collected. And I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought, whatever I am now, I am not the thing that carries you to that tower in chains."

Something moved behind Noor's eyes—not surprise exactly; recognition. He touched two fingers to his brow, an old gesture, half salute and half apology, and said nothing, which from Noor Vasquez was practically a speech.

* * *

They walked north along the spine of the hills all that day, following the map's third waymark, which pulsed faintly under Noor's palm like a heart heard through a wall. Sefa watched him check it at every rest stop, watched him press his oath-marked hand flat to the vellum and wait, jaw tight, for the shimmer of the road ahead—and each time she saw the small cost of it, the flicker behind his eyes where something used to be stored.

He was paying again. She knew it now. Every speaking of the song spent a year of memory, and he had been spending since the courier died, silently, without telling anyone, the way he did everything—entered neatly in a ledger no one else was allowed to read.

At dusk they came over a shoulder of moorland and saw, far below in a fold of the hills, a ring of standing stones with a fire in the middle of it, and figures moving around the fire. Sefa dropped low out of habit. Noor did not. He just looked at the map, and the map looked back at him, and he said, "That's not a patrol. Patrols don't bake bread."

He was right. As they came closer—Sefa still half-crouched, hand near her hornless hip where the warden's horn used to hang—the smell resolved into woodsmoke and grain and something else, something mineral and old, and she realized she knew that something else. Ash. Bread baked with ash, the way the hollow village baked it, the way—

"You're staring," Noor murmured.

"The way my grandmother baked," Sefa said. "When I was small. Before the vigil took her records and told us she'd never existed. She put ash in the flour. Everyone said it was a famine thing, a poor thing. Nobody ever asked why."

The figures at the fire had seen them. There were perhaps thirty of them—guild exiles, villagers from the hollow, a few faces Sefa recognized from the road behind them, people who had chosen to follow a map home to a country that was dying. An old woman with forearms like tree roots rose from the fire and looked at Sefa for a long moment, and then at the place on Sefa's wrist where the warden's oath-mark sat beneath her sleeve like a brand in reverse, a brand of belonging instead of shame.

"You're Vane's girl," the old woman said. Not unkindly. "Ilset Vane. Born west-facing."

The words went through Sefa like cold water through cloth. "How do you know that?"

"We know everyone born west-facing," the old woman said, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world, as if doors hung facing west and children marked at birth and grandmothers erased from records were all just facts of weather. "Your grandmother kept the western door of our street. When Vhalcrest burned, someone had to remember which way home faced." She turned back to the fire. "There's stew. Sit down or don't."

Sefa sat down.

She ate stew with people who knew her name before she gave it, and it felt like falling—like stepping off a ledge she hadn't known she was standing on, into air that turned out, impossibly, to hold her.

* * *

It was near midnight, the fire burned to embers, when the second crow came.

This one was not paper. It was flesh and feather, a real vigil crow with real seals on its leg, and it landed on a standing stone with the insolence of something that has never been shot at, and screamed once, and waited.

The camp woke. Knives came out. But Sefa raised a hand, because she knew the scream, and she knew the seal—it was the personal cipher of High Warden Ilset, her mentor, the woman who had cornered her at the tower's edge and given her the ember's key at a cost Sefa was only beginning to guess at.

She unbound the message. It was short, and it was not kind, because Ilset had never learned to be kind when frightened, and Sefa knew better than anyone alive that Ilset was frightened now.

*Sefa. The pardon is withdrawn. The Council of Ash voted it down—they want your head as an example, not your service as a tool. Priests move on the last waymark within days. I have bought what time I can by misfiling things; it will not be much. Your mark is revoked but revocation can be survived. I have seen it survived. Burn what binds you while you still choose the manner of burning, because if they take you, they will choose it for you, and their fires are not merciful. —I.*

P.S.: *The horn. You know which one. Your grandmother's cohort sealed three dragons in the high passes rather than let the vigil have them. Two may yet live. A revoked warden can blow a warden's horn; there is no law against it because no one ever imagined a warden would want to.*

Sefa read it twice. Then she stood, and the camp went quiet, because something in how she stood made them quiet.

"My mark," she said. "Does anyone here have a knife they don't love?"

Noor was on his feet immediately. "Sefa—"

"Ilset's right." She rolled up her sleeve, baring her forearm to the firelight. The warden's oath-mark lay along the inside of her wrist: a coiled dragon worked in silver-grey ink that had gone into her skin at sixteen and never faded, never blurred, fed for eleven years by oaths renewed each season—oaths of vigilance, of custody, of the Crown's fire kept small and safe and owned. It was beautiful. It had been beautiful every day of her life, and she had polished it like jewelry, and she had believed in it the way she had once believed the world was only as wide as the vigil's maps.

Now she looked at it and saw what it had always been: a leash drawn by the leashed.

"If I keep it, every sympathetic link the vigil maintains to its marks leads to me," she said. "They can find me across any distance. They can burn me through it, if they're desperate—and Ilset says they are. And worse—" her voice caught, steadied— "worse, it means part of me still answers to them. Part of me signed their ledger. I'm done being an entry in other people's books. I know a man who taught me that."

Noor flinched, very slightly, at that. She didn't apologize. Some truths you leave lying where they fall.

"Someone should hold me down," she said. "It's going to be like cutting a rope that's holding weight."

The old woman with the treelike arms took her right side. Noor took her left. Someone brought a knife heated in the embers, because the mark was oath-work and oath-work did not come off clean with steel alone; it had to be persuaded, and persuasion of that kind meant fire.

"Wait," Noor said, just before the blade touched skin. His voice was rough. "Once it's done, it's done forever. There's no guild trick, no map, no song that grows back an oath. Are you certain?"

Sefa looked at him—at this careful, emptied-out man who had traded pieces of himself away one year at a time and never once asked for the price back—and she felt the strangest thing, which was not courage exactly, but its cousin: the certainty that some things, once seen, cannot be unseen, and she had seen the ember starving in its tower, and she had seen her grandmother's name in a dead file, and she had seen this man give and give and give.

"No," she said honestly. "But I'm not going to be more certain later. Certainty doesn't work that way. You just jump and find out the ground was further down than you thought."

She set her arm on the flat stone. She did not close her eyes.

The hot blade went in along the first curve of the coiled dragon, and the mark screamed—not her, the mark, the oath-work itself shrieking in a register only oath-bearers could hear, eleven years of renewals unraveling thread by thread. Sefa bore down on the stone. Sweat ran into her eyes. The old woman's grip on her shoulder was iron. Noor held her left arm and recited, low and steady, the guild's inventory cadence—one item, two items, three—as if cataloging her pain so it couldn't scatter her, and absurdly, terribly, it helped.

The silver-grey ink blackened and lifted from her skin like a scab of light. Where it came away, her flesh was raw and pale and utterly blank, and she felt the severing go through her like a bell rung inside her ribs: every sympathetic thread to the vigil snapping at once, every seasonal oath burning off, eleven years of belonging to the Crown collapsing into a single white-hot instant of belonging to nothing at all.

She cried out. She would not pretend otherwise. And then it was done, and the night air touched the bare place on her wrist, and it was the coldest and freest air she had ever felt.

Freedom, she discovered, felt exactly like falling. Everyone told you it was flight. It was not flight. It was the long drop before you learned whether you had wings.

* * *

They bound the burn with ash-poultice and comfrey, and Sefa slept two hours and dreamed of nothing, which she decided was also freedom.

Before dawn she climbed alone to the highest standing stone, carrying the bundle Ilset's crow had brought with the letter: wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sleeping animal, the warden's horn.

It was older than her, older than Ilset, older than the vigil's current dynasty. Dragon-warden horns were cut from the shed spirals of the great drakes themselves, and each one remembered the throat that had shaped it. This one had hung in the vigil-house of her childhood district, above the door, gathering dust, because no one had blown it in ninety years—since the last dragons went silent, since the vigil decided that calling dragons was less useful than caging fire. Her grandmother's cohort had carried it into the high passes. Ilset must have gone and taken it back. Sefa wondered what that had cost her, and understood that she would probably never be told, and that this too was a form of inheritance.

She unwrapped it. The horn was dark as stormwood, banded in silver gone green with age, and it was warm. That was the thing no one warned you about. It was warm, faintly, like a hand that had recently been holding yours.

Below her, the camp stirred. Noor came out of his blanket and stood at the base of the stone, looking up at her, and did not tell her to be careful, because he was learning, slowly, what she had known for days: that care was not the same as chains.

"Sefa," he called softly. "What happens if you call and nothing answers?"

"Then I'll have shouted into the mountains and lost nothing but my breath," she said. "And if something answers—" She looked east, where the sky was beginning, grudgingly, to consider the idea of dawn. Far off, past the hills, past the vigil roads where priests were even now converging on the last waymark, the ember of Vhalcrest was failing. One moon's turn, maybe less. Everything rode on whether a dying country's last door could be opened, and whether a swordspeaker had enough self left to sing it open, and whether anything with scales and wings still remembered the sound of a warden's voice.

"Then we won't walk to the waymark," Sefa said. "We'll arrive."

She lifted the horn to her lips. It fit her mouth as if it had been measured for her—of course it had; her grandmother's mouth, her grandmother's hands, the same bones passed down like a debt that turned out to be a gift. She breathed in the cold mountain dark, tasting ash and pine and the iron ghost of her own burned-away oath, and she blew.

The note that came out was not a sound so much as a shape—a long, rising spiral of it, the call that wardens had once used to wake the high-flown, the far-sleeping, the nearly-gone. It rolled down the valleys and up the passes and out over the sleeping world, and Sefa put everything into it that she had left after the burning of her mark: her grandmother's street, her grandmother's door facing west, the file with the names, the starved ember, the man on the rock below her who forgot a little more every day and kept giving anyway.

The note ended. Echoes staggered away into the ranges and died.

Silence.

Ten heartbeats. Twenty. Sefa stood on the stone with the horn still raised, feeling the fall of it, the terrible openness, the sky with nothing in it—

And then, far to the northwest, past the passes where her grandmother's cohort had sealed what could not be surrendered, something answered.

Not a cry. A resonance—a deep, slow thrum she felt in the standing stones and in her own rebuilt wrist and in the hollowed places of her chest, like the world clearing its throat after a century of silence. Once. Twice. A wingbeat, vast and unhurried, somewhere beyond the first ridge.

Below her, the whole camp was on its feet. Noor had his hand pressed flat to the map against his chest, and even in the pre-dawn gloom she could see it glowing through his fingers, the waymarks flaring one after another like lamps lit down a road.

"Sefa," he said, and his voice shook with wonder and dread in equal measure. "The map just changed. There's a new line on it. The waymark isn't a place anymore."

"What is it?" she called down, as the second thrum rolled nearer, nearer, shaking loose stones on the heights.

"It's moving," Noor said. "Whatever comes next—it's coming to meet us."
