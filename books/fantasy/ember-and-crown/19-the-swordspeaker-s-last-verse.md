## 19. The Swordspeaker's Last Verse

The door held for exactly as long as it took the ash-priests to stop being afraid of it.

Noor felt them coming before he heard them—a wrongness in the ember's light, a stutter in the way Vhalcrest's fading country breathed around him. He stood on the threshold with his sister's hand still warm in his, and behind them the hearth where the last ember had burned for a hundred years was empty now, its cradle of black stone cold as a closed eye. The ember sat against his chest inside Sefa's warden-horn case, heavy as a sleeping child, warm as one too.

"They're through the ash-roads," Lira said. She was older than he remembered—of course she was; she had been walking this dying country while he forgot her face twice over—and her voice was calm in the way that only people who have already grieved can manage. "All of them. Ilset slowed them at the river, but she couldn't hold the crossing."

"Ilset stayed?"

"Ilset chose." Lira smiled, and it was their mother's smile, and Noor's chest ached because he could no longer remember their mother's face to hang it on. Only the smile remained, transplanted into his sister like a cutting from a tree that had been cut down. "Go, Noor."

"The door opens inward," he said. "You told me that yourself, when I was small enough to sit on your knee and you were small enough to believe you'd come back out. It opens inward. Nothing walks out of Vhalcrest except what the fire carries."

"Then carry the fire," she said simply.

Behind him, down the long hall of the hearth-house—every surface of which was memory made faintly solid, wallpaper patterned with harvest festivals, floorboards worn smooth by feet that had been gone a century—the sound began. Not footsteps yet. The priests were singing their counter-song, the ash-chant that unmade doors, and Noor could feel it working at the threshold like water working at ice.

Sefa appeared at the far end of the hall, silhouetted in the doorway, the westering light of the real world behind her turning her into a figure cut from gold and shadow. She had run. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, the blood on her knuckles—not hers—and the horn slung across her back with its case empty now, its burden against his heart instead of hers.

"Noor." Just his name. She said it the way she said everything: like she was deciding whether to spend it.

"He's staying," Lira said, not unkindly. "He's decided. Can't you tell? He has the look of a man who has finished counting."

"I haven't finished counting," Noor said. "I've just counted honestly for once."

* * *

Here is what the swordspeaker's oath actually was, stripped of the guild's careful language and Maro's ledgers and a century of euphemism:

A sword is a promise made metal. Every edge is an agreement between the smith and the world: *this will hold*. The swordsong was the first promise ever sung aloud, and every verse of it since has been paid for out of the singer's own keeping. A verse bought a waymark open. A verse bought a door. And there was a last verse—the one the old songs named but never wrote down, the one every swordspeaker's teacher swore did not exist, the way adults swear the dark under the bed does not exist—

The last verse gave everything. Not a year. Not a face or a smell or a first word. Everything. The whole ledger of a self, surrendered to the song so that the song could do what songs alone cannot: carry something living across a border that death itself had drawn.

Noor had known since the courier died in his arms. He had known, and he had paid verse after verse anyway, letting the map drink him down, telling himself each time: *not yet, not all, there will be another way.* He had watched Sefa learn the truth in the hollow village and seen fury turn to something fiercer, and he had let her believe—had wanted to believe—that they would find a cheaper road.

There was no cheaper road. There was only this one, and it ran through him.

"You don't have to be noble about it," Sefa said now, crossing the hall to him, her boots ringing on boards that remembered ten thousand homecomings. "That's what I keep trying to tell you. Nobody's asking you to be noble. Give me the ember and go stand in the corner and be noble later, when we're safe, at length, over drinks."

"The ember won't cross for you."

"It crossed *out* of the vigil-tower for me."

"That was theft," Noor said. "This is return. The fire knows the difference. It's a ledger too—it keeps accounts. And the account says a living oath carries it, and my oath is the only ink left that reads this country." He touched his palm, where the oath-mark had faded to almost nothing, spent down to a ghost of itself. "It's arithmetic, Sefa. You taught me that yourself, in the tower. Ember's arithmetic."

"Don't you dare quote my own sums at me."

"Lira." He turned to his sister. "Tell her."

Lira looked at Sefa for a long moment—warden and exile, Crown and country, two women who between them held every version of the truth—and then she did the cruelest kind thing Noor had ever seen anyone do. She nodded.

"He stays," Lira said. "And you take him out. Both halves of the bargain, warden. That's how our family always sealed things. Ask him what our mother—" She stopped. Her hand went to her mouth. "Ask him," she said again, softer. "Oh, Noor. You've forgotten her already, haven't you. Even now, even here."

"There's a verse left," he said quietly. "The last one. Maybe it gives it back. The old songs say the fire returns what it takes, if you give it everything at once instead of in installments."

"And if they're wrong?"

"Then I'll have been wrong about a great many things," Noor said, "and none of them will have mattered, because the people who needed remembering will be standing in the sun."

* * *

The ash-priests breached the outer wall at moonrise—or what passed for moonrise in a country that was mostly memory now, a pale disc of remembered light rising over remembered hills. They came through the breach singing, twelve of them in their gray vestments, their faces masked in pressed ash, and their chant hit the hearth-house like a physical thing. The wallpaper of festivals curled and grayed. The floorboards' remembered footfalls went silent street by street.

At their head walked a priest whose mask was different—white, unmarked, and Noor understood without knowing how he knew that this was the one who had burned the courier from the inside, the one who had been hunting the map since before Noor ever held it. The white mask turned toward the ember against Noor's chest, and beneath the ash-smell came something worse: hunger. Careful, institutional, patient hunger. The Crown had kept its monopoly on fire for a hundred years by feeding the last ember confiscated miracles, and a free dawn-fire in the sky would end all of it—end the vigil, end the ash-priesthood, end the quiet arithmetic by which wonder was taxed and hoarded.

"Swordspeaker." The white mask's voice was like paper burning slowly. "Give us the ember and the Crown will remember you kindly. We are very good at remembering. It is most of what we do."

"You remember wrong," Noor said. "That's the trouble. You remember *that* things burned. Not why. Not who."

"We remember exactly who." The priest raised one gray hand, and the twelve voices rose, and the doorway behind Noor—the door out, the last door, the door his sister had walked through as a child and he had sworn an oath to follow her through—began to narrow.

Sefa moved. Later, Noor would piece together what happened in those few seconds from fragments: Sefa putting herself between the priests and the door, the warden's horn coming off her back, the note she blew on it—long, low, furious, a call with no dragons left alive to answer it except, perhaps, in whatever way a dead country could answer. The priests faltered. The chant stuttered. And in the stutter, Noor stepped forward past his sister, past the woman he loved and had never found the right tense for, and stood in the center of the hearth-house with the ember against his heart.

He opened the case.

The ember rose. It had been failing for a hundred years, guttering in its tower while priests fed it stolen wonders to keep it small, and now, loose, in the air of the country it had warmed, it flared—not large, but *true*, a coin of light the color of the first morning, and every remaining thing in Vhalcrest turned toward it. The hills. The river that flowed backward. The village of ash-bread and west-facing doors, wherever its echo persisted. All of it, looking home.

"Noor." Sefa's voice cracked down the middle. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"What I should have done at the first waymark," he said. "Saved us both the walking."

He began the last verse.

* * *

It was not a song the way songs are usually meant. It was a *giving*. He felt the first line leave him and knew it forever even as it went: his mother's hands, flour-dusted, shaping ash-bread in a kitchen he had rebuilt from nothing a dozen times in memory and now surrendered entirely. The second line took the guildhall—the smell of dust and lamp-oil and bottled thunderstorms, Maro's laugh, the particular creak of the third stair. The third line took his sister at four years old, laughing, running ahead of him toward a door in a field, and he gave that most gladly of all because she was standing right there, grown, whole, watching him pay it away with tears running freely down her face.

"You gave me a childhood," Lira said. "Now I get to give you back the rest of it."

The fourth line took Sefa's first words to him—*"You're either very brave or very stupid, and I've decided it doesn't matter"*—and he sang them as they went, so that she heard them one last time, and saw him understand, in the same instant, that he would never again know where they had come from.

The fifth line took his name.

He kept singing. That was the terrible mathematics of it: the verse required a singer, and the singer was being spent to sing, and so the last lines had to be sung by whatever remained when the self was gone—by oath alone, by promise made metal, by the shape a man leaves in the world when everything written on him is erased. Noor Vasquez diminished like a candle in a draft, and the song grew, filling the hearth-house, pouring out the narrowing door, and the ember rose higher and brighter, drinking the verse, becoming what it had always been meant to be: not a relic, not a monopoly, not a prisoner in a tower. A dawn.

The priests' chant broke apart. Their masks cracked. The white-masked priest fell to his knees in the ruins of his counter-song, and whether he was grieving or praying or simply, for the first time in a hundred years, seeing clearly, Noor would never know, because Noor was nearly gone—no past, no name, no oath he could name the reason for, only the song, only the giving, only love with nowhere left to live except in the act itself.

The door was closing. The ember blazed up off his open palms like a bird leaving a branch, streaming toward the threshold, toward the world, toward the sky that had waited a century for a fire no crown could own.

And hands seized him.

Strong hands, ash-stained, blood-crusted at the knuckles, hands he did not remember learning the name of and would spend the rest of his life relearning everything else about. Sefa dragged him backward through the closing gap with the last of her strength and both of them screaming—one with pain, one with a grief that had no memories left to be grief *about*—and the door sealed behind them with a sound like a book closing gently, like a ledger balanced at last, like a long exhale.

They fell together onto grass in the real world, in the field where a lone door had once stood in nothing, and above them the night sky caught fire.

Not with destruction. With morning. The ember climbed and spread and steadied, a new dawn-fire burning in the east at midnight, and everywhere across the dark land its light reached, exiles lifted their heads from sleep and knew, without being told, without any ledger to tell them, that they were free to come home.

Vhalcrest, behind the sealed door, dissolved into that same light—gently, the way fog dissolves, the way grief does when it finally becomes love. Noor lay on the grass looking up at the impossible dawn with empty eyes and full hands, holding nothing, remembering nothing, and the woman beside him was crying and laughing and pressing her forehead to his shoulder as though checking that he was still solid.

"Noor?" she said. Carefully. Testing the weight of the name in his own ears. "Do you know me?"

He looked at her for a long time—at the stranger with ash on her face and dawn-fire reflected in her eyes—and felt the one thing the song had not taken, the thing it had been built on, the load-bearing beam of him: that this person mattered. That he would rather start over beside her than be whole anywhere else.

"No," he said honestly. "But I think I'd like to. Will you tell me the story?"

She laughed through her tears, and somewhere overhead the new fire settled into its place in the sky, and far away, in a guildhall with a third stair that creaked, a ledger waited to be begun again.

"Yes," Sefa said. "But it's a long one. We'd better start with breakfast."
