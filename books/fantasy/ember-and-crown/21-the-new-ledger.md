## 21. The New Ledger

The guildhall smelled of rain and old paper and, faintly, of ash that would never quite scrub out of the floorboards. I stood in the doorway with my hands empty for the first time in longer than I could remember—literally could not remember—and watched the light come through the broken shutters in long dusty blades.

I had no memory of this room. Sefa had told me that much: I had kept ledgers here for years, cataloged a bottled thunderstorm, a widow's second shadow, a hundred small impossible things. She had described the shelves, the counting desk with its worn brass corners, the way Maro used to sit behind it like a spider who had grown fond of his web. None of it was mine. All of it had been given to me secondhand, the way you give someone directions to a house they've never seen.

And yet when I stepped inside, my boots found the loose third board without looking for it.

"Careful," Sefa said behind me, carrying a crate of salvaged relics against her hip. "That one bites."

"I know," I said, and then stopped, because *I know* was not something I should have been able to say. My body remembered what my mind had paid away. It was like that more and more lately—a hand knowing a knot before I did, a tongue tasting tea and expecting honey I'd supposedly always hated. I was a stranger walking around inside a life that fit me perfectly.

Sefa set the crate down on the counting desk—the same desk where Maro had made his bargains, though we'd turned it to face the door now, so whoever sat there sat with the guild rather than above it. She caught me looking at nothing and didn't pretend she hadn't.

"Bad morning?" she asked.

"I keep expecting him to be here." I said it plainly. There was no point being delicate about it; delicacy required details, and most of my details were ash. "Maro. Sitting at that desk with his little smile, explaining why everything that happened was arithmetic."

"He's in Ilset's custody," Sefa said. "Being very cooperative, which is somehow worse. He's already offered the priests three different versions of how the guild was founded, each one flattering to himself."

"That sounds like him."

"You remember him, then?"

"No." I ran my hand along the desk's edge. "But everyone who talks about him uses the same tone of voice. That's a kind of memory too, I suppose. Borrowed."

She came around the desk and stood beside me, shoulder almost touching mine, and together we looked out at the hall. It was fuller than I'd ever seen it—or so I was told. The villagers from the hollow had come down in ones and twos over the past week, then in families, bringing their ash-bread ovens' worth of stubbornness with them. The widow whose second shadow I had once shelved—I knew this only because the shadow itself had told me, standing shyly by the door while its owner argued about shelf heights—had claimed the eastern alcove. Two of Ilset's former wardens had arrived yesterday, still wearing their ash-grey cloaks but with the collars cut open where the vigil marks used to sit. Even a few of the exiled dead's descendants, the ones who had crossed through the last door before it sealed, drifted through the aisles touching things as if checking whether the world would let them hold it now.

The world would. That was rather the point of everything we'd done.

"The first page," Sefa said. She set a fresh ledger on the desk between us. It was bound in undyed leather, unmarked, its pages blank as snow. "You said you wanted to write it yourself. I brought it up from the cellar vaults—Maro never opened these. Blank ledgers were the one thing he hoarded harder than secrets."

"Why would he hoard blank books?"

"Because a ledger is a promise about the future," she said. "He liked owning promises."

* * *

We opened the guild at noon, which was traditional, apparently, though no one could tell me *why* it was traditional, and I found I liked that—that some things could simply be customs again, carried by people instead of carved into oaths.

Old habits are strange architecture. The doors that had faced west since the burning—the hollow village's grief-made habit, doors turned toward a country that no longer existed—had been rehung over the winter to face every direction. Here in the guildhall we had done something similar with the rules. There weren't any. Or rather: there was one rule, and we'd painted it on a board above the counting desk because people kept asking.

**THE LEDGER RECORDS NAMES. THE LEDGER DOES NOT SET PRICES.**

It had been Sefa's phrase originally. I'd have liked to remember saying it back to her the night we drafted it, sitting on the tower steps with the new dawn-fire still visible as a faint warm smudge in the sky, but that memory belonged to the song. So I trusted her when she told me I'd laughed, and that the laugh had surprised us both.

People came all afternoon. Not traders, exactly—not at first. They came the way people come to look at a fire, except our fire was a book.

An old woman brought a jar of riverwater that remembered being rain over Vhalcrest, and instead of haggling she gave her name—Maret Ostrevik, born in the hollow, granddaughter of an exile who had died singing—and wrote it herself on the first page, under the jar's description, because the rule board said names and she took us literally in the best possible way. A boy of maybe ten traded us a stone that hummed when held near anyone telling the truth. His name was Pell. He wrote it in enormous careful letters and then stood there radiating pride until Sefa solemnly assigned him the title of Junior Keeper of Acoustics, which meant nothing and delighted everyone.

A man tried, gently, to pay coin for a mended coat. We pointed at the board. He looked confused, then frightened, then—slowly, like ice going off a pond in spring—relieved. He wrote his name: Dossan Ferre. He stayed to help stack shelves.

By evening the ledger's first page held eleven names and not one number. I read them twice, running my finger down the column. Eleven strangers who had walked into a building full of impossible things and left a piece of themselves in ink, freely given, expecting nothing back.

"There," Sefa said quietly, beside me. "That's what he was afraid of."

"What do you mean?"

"A ledger with no prices can't be sold." She tapped the page. "Every bargain Maro ever struck depended on value—on things costing something, so they could be traded, leveraged, betrayed. You've written a book that's worthless in every way he understood and priceless in every way he didn't. If he saw this page, it would hurt him worse than the deposition did."

I thought about that. About Maro, who had sold our route to the ash-priests to buy the guild's survival, and who had confessed—Sefa told me this too—in a voice like a man reading someone else's confession, that he had never once believed the guild could survive by being generous. Generosity, to Maro, was just a price you hadn't collected yet.

"Maybe that's why I don't mind forgetting him," I said. "Everyone keeps offering me memories of how clever he was. But the page in front of me is better evidence."

* * *

Three days later, the trouble arrived, as trouble does, politely.

Her name was Yeva Solenne, and she came in at dusk wearing traveling clothes gone grey with road dust and an expression I recognized even if I couldn't place where from: the look of someone carrying news they've rehearsed the whole way here and now dread delivering.

"We're closed—" Pell started, from his post by the door, where he had appointed himself gatekeeper on the grounds that acoustics included announcing people.

"It's all right," I said. "We're never really closed. That's sort of the idea." I turned to her. "You've come a long way. Sit. Have bread. Ash-bread—it's better than it sounds."

She didn't sit. She reached into her coat and drew out a ledger—old, swollen with damp, its spine stitched twice with different colored thread—and laid it on the counting desk between us with the care of someone setting down a sleeping animal.

"My grandmother was a keeper," she said. "Before the burning. She went south when Vhalcrest fell, and she kept accounts her whole life, and she made me swear that if I ever heard the name *Guild of Impossible Things* spoken without whispering, I was to bring this to whoever kept it now."

The hall had gone quiet. Sefa had come out of the back room and stood at my shoulder. Villagers drifted close without pretending not to. Maret Ostrevik put down her jar of remembering-rain.

I touched the old ledger's cover. Under my palm, faint as breath on glass, ink stirred—just barely, just enough. An oath-marked hand, reading. Some habits outlive countries.

"What's in it?" Sefa asked.

Yeva Solenne looked at me, then at Sefa, then at the small crowd of people who had, over one winter, become a guild.

"Names," she said. "Four hundred years of names. Keepers, couriers, exiles, debtors, thieves, saints—every person who ever carried something impossible out of Vhalcrest and kept it alive. And on the last written page—" her voice caught, steadied— "a list of debts the guild owes. Not money. Debts of return. Things promised to people who were promised a homecoming." She swallowed. "The ember's gone free. The door's sealed. But the promises are still open. Someone has to decide whether this guild keeps them."

I looked down at the swollen old book, and then at the new one lying open beside it, its single page bright with eleven names and no prices.

One ledger recorded what we had. The other recorded what we owed.

I picked up the pen.

"Then we'd better start reading," I said.
