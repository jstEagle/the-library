## 22. Homecoming

The village had a name again.

It had come back the way names do—slowly, then all at once, the way a word you've been searching for surfaces in the middle of an ordinary sentence. Someone said it over bread one morning, and no one argued, and by evening it was painted on the new signpost at the crossroads: **Ashhollow**, in letters that were not elegant but were legible, which the villagers agreed was more important. Beneath it someone had added, in smaller script, *doors open every direction.*

That last part had been Sefa's idea, though she'd deny it if asked. The old custom of hanging doors facing west—facing Vhalcrest, facing the country that burned—had been a way of keeping grief pointed somewhere useful. But grief needed a new job now. So the doors of Ashhollow faced north and south and east and up, where the ember rode the sky as a second dawn-fire, small and stubborn and owned by no crown, no guild, no god. And some doors, the newest ones, simply faced each other across the lanes, so that when two neighbors opened their homes at the same hour they opened toward one another.

It was, Sefa thought as she walked the morning lane with a basket on her hip, a strange kind of architecture. It was also the only kind that made sense anymore.

* * *

Noor Vasquez woke before the sun did—or before the ember rose, which was the phrase everyone used now—and lay still in the narrow bed above the old warden's station, doing what he did every morning: taking inventory.

This was the ritual. He'd learned it from himself, or from what remained of himself, in the first raw weeks after the door sealed and the song finished with him. Each dawn he lay still and asked his mind what it held, the way a man pats his pockets after a long journey. His name: present. The feel of a sword hilt: present, though the sword itself hung on the wall untouched, because some things a body remembers even when the reasons are gone. Sefa's face: present, thank whatever listened, though when he tried to reach behind it—to the first time he'd seen her, to her first words to him, to whatever she'd been before she became the person who read him back to himself every night—he found only smooth blankness, like a page scraped clean for reuse.

He got up. He dressed. He went downstairs and wrote yesterday down.

The ledger lived on the kitchen table, fat and plain-bound, nothing like the guild's old ledgers with their gilt corners and locked clasps. This one had no lock. That had been the whole point, argued about for three nights in the reopened guildhall until Maro's successors—the villagers Bren and old Tamsin, who between them had the temperament of a flood and a stone wall respectively—outvoted everyone. *A ledger anyone can read is a ledger no one can sell,* Tamsin had said, and Noor had written that down too, because it felt like something worth keeping.

Yesterday's entry, in his careful bookkeeper's hand:

*Day 361. Rain in the afternoon; the west field flooded at the low corner and Ilset's people turned it into a skating pond within the hour, because exiles make joy out of anything with edges. A courier came through from the coast—first one since the roads opened—carrying letters and a crate of oranges, which we ate standing up in the rain like thieves. Sefa laughed at something I said and I wrote down what I said so I can say it again someday. The little Ferro girl asked me if I remembered being brave. I told her bravery isn't a memory, it's a habit. She looked skeptical. She's right to.*

He read it twice. Then he closed the ledger, set it by the kettle, and went to feed the fire—which was an ordinary fire, wood and coal, but he lit it anyway with a certain ceremony, because he had once carried a country's last flame in his hands and he had never quite stopped being reverent about warmth.

Sefa came down while the kettle sang. She moved differently than she had a year ago—he knew this the way you know weather, without knowing how you know it. The warden's stiffness was gone from her shoulders. The horn she'd taken up after burning her oath-mark hung by the door, dented and unlovely, and sometimes at dusk she blew it out past the fields, and sometimes—rarely, far off, more feeling than sound—something vast answered.

"Day three hundred sixty-one," she said, reading upside down over his shoulder before he could stop her, which was also ritual. "You skated?"

"I watched skating. There's a difference. One of us has dignity."

"You fell."

"I have no memory of falling," Noor said, "therefore it did not happen. This is the entire principle my life runs on now."

She laughed—that laugh, the one he'd written down yesterday so he could earn it again—and poured the tea, and for a while neither of them spoke, because the best mornings didn't need it.

Then she pulled out the chair across from him, turned the ledger around, and said the words that were as fixed in their household as any prayer: "Yesterday's page. Ready?"

He nodded. And she began to read him his own life.

* * *

Ensemble, the chroniclers called it later, when Ashhollow started keeping records of its records. But that morning it was just a house full of arrivals, because that was the other thing about the village: nobody knocked anymore. Doors faced each other; doors got used.

Tamsin arrived first, ostensibly to deliver eggs, actually to inspect the ledger, which she considered a civic institution and therefore subject to audit. She was seventy if she was a day, had been born in the hollow when it was nameless, and had spent her whole life baking bread with ash in it—"for the taste of home," she'd said for sixty years, not knowing what home meant, and then one day a map and a swordspeaker had told her, and she'd cried into the dough and kept baking.

"Noor," she said, setting down eggs, "the Ferro girl's been asking after you. Wants to apprentice."

"I'm a bookkeeper, not a—"

"She wants to learn the writing-down," Tamsin said, with the gravity of a woman announcing a coronation. "The keeping. You think I don't know what you do? Half this village sleeps better because they know if today goes wrong, tomorrow you'll write it right." She tapped the ledger. "Teach her. Before I'm dead and someone tries to charge for miracles again."

After Tamsin came Bren, mud to the knees, reporting that the east road was passable and the surveyors from the Crown—*the new* Crown, chastened, its ash-priest order dissolved into a historical society that met quarterly and apologized a great deal—wanted to put Ashhollow on official maps. "I told them we're already on the only maps that matter," Bren said, jerking a thumb at the living ledger on Noor's table, and Noor felt something turn over quietly in his chest, because there had been a time when a map had cost him everything, and here was a map being made of him instead, gently, by people who stayed for tea.

Ilset came at midmorning. She walked with a stick now—the price of the tower's edge, of turning to face her own priests with a key in her fist—but her spine hadn't gotten the message. She had brought, of all things, flowers, which she thrust at Sefa with the expression of a woman committing a crime.

"For the table," she said gruffly. "Don't read into it."

"The High Warden brings me flowers."

"The High Warden is dead. I checked the paperwork myself." Ilset lowered herself into a chair, accepted tea, and was quiet for a moment, looking at the window, at the ember-light that even in daytime gave the world a faint gold undertone, like everything had been varnished with remembering. "There's a family coming up from the south," she said at last. "Exile stock, third generation, don't know what they are. Found their grandmother's name in the released files. They want to see the hollow. I told them come."

"They'll be welcome," Sefa said.

"They'll be lost," Ilset said. "That's how it starts. That's how it started for all of us." She drank her tea. "You'll write them down, swordspeaker? So they can find themselves later?"

"Every name," Noor said. "No prices. Only names."

Ilset nodded once, sharply, the way soldiers accept terms, and something in her face—for just a moment, before she buried it—looked like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

* * *

In the afternoon, Noor walked.

He always walked in the afternoons, alone, along a route that had assembled itself out of instinct rather than plan: past the pond, past the signpost, down to the field where a door had once stood alone in the grass opening onto nothing. The door was gone now—dissolved with all the rest when Vhalcrest let go of the world—but the grass grew different there, thicker, greener, and the villagers had left it unmowed, a green rectangle in the gold like a signature on a letter.

He stood on it, as he did every day, and took inventory again.

Here was what he had lost, as far as he could ever reconstruct: his mother's face (he knew he had loved her; the love remained, pointing at nothing, like a door facing west). The smell of the guildhall, forty years of it, candlewax and dust and bottled thunderstorms. His sister—not her face, worse, the *fact* of growing up beside her, the accumulated sediment of a shared childhood. The years themselves, peeled off one verse at a time down the ash-roads, each waymark bought with a season of himself. And the last verse, the one no swordspeaker survived, had taken nearly everything, so that what woke in Sefa's arms under a dissolving sky was a man made almost entirely of the previous ten minutes.

What he had kept: his hands' knowledge of ledgers. His hands' knowledge of the sword, though he never drew it. The words *found family over oath*, which he did not remember learning but recognized as true the way you recognize your own handwriting. And Sefa—Sefa entire, Sefa as a complete continent, though its history was closed to him, only its coastline visible, and he had decided early on that he would rather spend a life mapping the coast than mourn the interior.

A shadow fell beside him. He didn't startle. Only one person walked this route with him, and only when she thought he needed company more than solitude.

"Do you regret it?" Sefa asked. She asked it maybe once a month. Never pitying. Always genuinely wanting the answer, because the answer changed.

Noor considered the green rectangle. Somewhere on the far side of it, in a country that no longer existed even as an echo, a sister older than his memory had pushed an ember into his hands and told him to live loud enough for both of them. He couldn't picture her. He could feel her, faintly, the way you feel sun through a wall.

"I regret that she's not here to see the signpost," he said. "I regret that I can't tell her the doors face every direction now. Everything else—" He turned his palm up, the old oath-mark long faded to a ghost of scar. "Every day I get to choose what I keep. Do you understand what that's worth? Most people carry everything whether they want it or not. Griefs they never chose. Grudges inherited like furniture. I wake up empty, and then I fill myself—with this morning, with Tamsin's eggs, with you laughing at my joke, with the Ferro girl asking about bravery. It's not a wound, Sefa. It's a practice."

"And if you forget the practice?"

"Then you'll read it to me." He smiled. "You've done it three hundred sixty-one times. You're very good. You do voices."

"I do not do voices."

"Tamsin's voice is devastating. You add a wheeze."

Sefa shoved him, and he laughed, and the laugh went into tomorrow's ledger before it had even finished echoing, because that was the whole trick of it: a memory written down by someone who loves you is a memory held in two places, and two places is the beginning of a home.

* * *

Evening came down gold. The ember rose in the east now—it migrated, slow and seasonal, and the whole village tracked it the way coastal towns track whales—and its light touched the signpost, the pond, the unmowed green rectangle, the doors facing every direction.

Inside the old warden's station, supper was underway and overcrowded, because it always was. Tamsin arguing with Bren about road taxes. Ilset teaching the Ferro girl dice, with suspicious patience. The southern family had arrived at dusk—three generations, travel-worn, standing in the lane staring at the signpost with the expressions of people reading their own names in a language they'd forgotten—and Sefa had gone out with a lamp and said the words that were becoming the village's true motto, the ones Noor had already inked into the ledger's front page: *You're expected. Come eat.*

And at the head of the table, Noor opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote the date, and beneath it, slowly, the day's harvest:

*Day 362. New family arrived; seven names, recorded below. The girl beat Ilset at dice, which Ilset will deny. Sefa says I do an impression of her and I demand witnesses. The ember rose red tonight, like it was showing off. Tomorrow I teach the Ferro girl the writing-down. First lesson: a record is a promise you make to someone you haven't met yet.*

He paused, pen lifted. Around him the room roared and clattered and passed the bread—ash in it, for the taste of home, except home wasn't a taste anymore, it was a table, and the table kept getting longer.

One year ago he had been a careful man guarding a quiet life, and a dying stranger had ruined it, and everything since had been paid for in the only coin he had, which was himself. He had walked into a dying country to give his whole remaining memory to a song, expecting erasure, and had been handed back instead—not his past, never his past, but something the song had never promised: a future with witnesses. Belonging, he understood now, was not a place recovered. Places burned. Belonging was this—chosen daily, written nightly, read aloud over breakfast by someone who loved you enough to keep your story when you couldn't.

He added one last line, and underlined it twice:

*Note to whoever reads this page—me, most likely, tomorrow morning: you are a man who is loved in detail. Ask Sefa. She keeps the proof.*

"What are you writing?" Sefa called from the stove.

"Nothing," said Noor Vasquez, last of the swordspeakers, keeper of the new ledger, and smiled at the page like a man greeting an old friend he was meeting for the very first time.
