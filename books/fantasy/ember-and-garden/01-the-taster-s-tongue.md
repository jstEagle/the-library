## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison of the day was almond and bitter orange, folded into a honey-cake so delicate it might have been baked for a bride.

Yara Whitlock knew this before she lifted the small silver fork, before the cake even touched her tongue — knew it by the way the pastry cook's hands had trembled when he set down the tray, by the faint sweetish reek beneath the honey that only a certain kind of nose could catch, and by the way the Queen's under-steward would not meet her eyes. She had been swallowing death for six years now, and death had developed habits. It announced itself. It was, in its way, polite.

She ate the cake anyway.

The court of Vell Maros watched her do it. They always watched; that was half the spectacle, the other half being whether the royal taster lived or died before the second bell. Yara stood at the long side table in the Amber Hall with forty pairs of eyes on her — ladies with fans half-raised, ambassadors pretending to study the tapestries, the chamberlain Ovid standing at his post near the Queen's shoulder with his face arranged into its usual mild blankness, like a wall someone had painted to look like a man.

She let the cake sit on her tongue. Counted. One. Two. Three.

There it was: not the almond, not the orange, but underneath them a cold mineral thread, the taste of well-water from a deep well, the taste of stone that had been made to give up something it did not want to give. *Arsenic of the southern mines*, she thought, *cut with something to slow it. Clever. Someone wanted the Queen dead by evening, quietly, in her chambers, where a taster's death hours earlier would seem an accident of a weak stomach.*

She swallowed. Then she set down the fork, turned to the under-steward, and said, in the clear carrying voice she had learned for exactly this purpose:

"This one is poisoned."

* * *

The hall went quiet the way a pond goes quiet when a heron lands — everything still, everything listening.

"Poisoned," repeated the Master of Ceremonies, whose name Yara had never bothered to learn because he changed every year and always had the same face.

"Bitter almond over arsenic," she said. "Slow-acting. The cook is innocent; the poison came into the honey after baking. Someone with access to the pantry stores." She did not look at the under-steward. Looking was not her work. Naming was her work, and then she could go home and let other people do the ugly arithmetic of who had keys to what.

The Master of Ceremonies sputtered about sending for the Watch, and the hall dissolved into the particular rustling chaos of aristocrats discovering they might be adjacent to danger, which was the closest most of them ever came to a feeling. Through it all, Chamberlain Ovid caught Yara's eye and gave her a single nod — grave, unhurried, the nod of a man acknowledging a job done as thoroughly as it needed doing and no more. He was the only person in the palace who nodded at her like that. Everyone else either flinched or stared.

"You have the constitution of an ox and the tongue of a saint," muttered Sergeant Brakka of the food-tasters' office later, while Yara sat in the little stone room behind the kitchens drinking the chalky restorative they gave tasters after a confirmed poisoning. It tasted like wet slate and cost the crown four coppers a dose, which told you everything about how much the crown valued the people who kept it alive.

"The tongue of a saint," Yara repeated. "Is that canon doctrine or kitchen gossip?"

"Both. Saints are just people who swallow terrible things on behalf of others and don't complain." Brakka was sixty, missing three fingers to an old knife accident, and had been a taster himself before his tongue went numb from years of small doses. He was the closest thing Yara had to a friend inside the walls, which was a statement less about affection than about scarcity. "You didn't complain. Saint Whitlock."

"I complain constantly."

"To me. Which doesn't count. Complaining to me is like complaining to a chair." He tapped the table with his stump. "Third attempt this season. You notice the pattern?"

"I notice I'm tired."

"Pattern's this: nobody poisons the King anymore. Everybody poisons around the King. The Queen, the heir, the chamberlain, now. Like the King's too hard a target, so they're cutting the branches hoping the tree falls over." He shrugged. "Or the tree's already rotten and everyone knows it but him."

Yara said nothing. In Vell Maros there were perhaps ten sentences a citizen was safe to say aloud, and Brakka had just spent two of them. She drank her slate-water and thought about the night ahead, and felt the familiar double weight settle onto her shoulders: the weight of the day's fear, already cooling, and the weight of the night's work, warming up.

Weary competence. That was her whole self, some days. A tongue that knew thirty-one poisons by taste and a mind that knew forty more by reputation, wrapped around a heart that mostly just wanted to be left alone in a room where no one was watching her eat.

* * *

The Edict hung in the Great Square the way a storm cloud hangs over a field — visible from everywhere, touching nothing directly, ruining everything underneath it.

It had been nailed to the Justice Arch for eleven years now, a sheet of black iron taller than three men, lettered in gold that never tarnished because the letters were renewed every month at public expense: **BY DECREE OF THE CROWN, THE PRACTICE OF MAGIC IS FORBIDDEN IN ALL DOMINIONS OF THE REALM. LET THE NAMES OF ITS PRACTITIONERS BE FORGOTTEN. LET THEIR WORKS BE UNMADE. SO IT IS WRITTEN, SO IT IS ENFORCED.**

Below the decree, smaller, in letters the size of a hand: *the country of Vessany never existed.*

That last line had always struck Yara as the strangest part. A law against magic she could understand — fear wears laws the way bodies wear skin. But a law against a *country*, against the mere fact of a place having existed? Eleven years ago, apparently, there had been a nation across the western marches with its own songs and orchards and graveyards, and one day the crown decided otherwise, and now schoolbooks began their histories with a blank page where Vessany should be, and old men who remembered the border roads had been visited by polite officials from the Ministry of Records and had come away politely unable to say why they'd once owned a map of somewhere that wasn't anywhere.

Forgetting, Yara had learned, was not the absence of memory. Forgetting was a craft, like anything else. And crafts could be counterfeited.

Which was why, six nights out of seven, after the palace released her and the sun went down and the Watch changed shift at the river gates, Yara Whitlock walked down into the Drowned Quarter with a basket on her arm and sold charms to people who needed to remember.

* * *

The night market lived in the bones of an old tide-mill at the edge of the Drowned Quarter, where the canal water slapped against pilings and the smell of brine and frying onions covered a multitude of sins. There was no sign. There was never a sign. You found it because someone you trusted told you the way, and if no one trusted you, you wandered the mill district until the Watch found you instead, and that was your own fault.

Inside, between the fishmonger's stall and the woman who sold smuggled southern pepper, Yara had a folding table, two candles, and a tray of small objects laid out on grey cloth like a surgeon's instruments.

Charms, she called them, though that was a lie of convenience. What she actually sold was memory — bottled, braided, stitched into ordinary things. A knotted cord that, held while you slept, would return to you the sound of your mother's voice saying your name. A dried apple slice that tasted, for one bite, of an orchard the eater had loved and lost. A plain iron nail that, driven into a doorframe, kept the Ministry of Records' polite visitors from quite finishing their work. None of it was grand magic. None of it would raise the dead or topple the crown. It was small craft, hearth-craft, the kind of thing that had survived eleven years of the Edict precisely because it was too humble to be worth hunting — except that the Edict hunted everything, and so Yara hunted first, moving stalls every few months, using names that weren't hers, trusting no one further than the length of a transaction.

Her customers came hooded and hurried. The ferryman who wanted to remember his drowned son's laugh without weeping through the whole remembering. The old widow who had been born on the wrong side of a border that officially had never been there, and who paid three coppers a month for a locket of pressed flowers that let her see, for a minute at a time, a village green that the world insisted was empty moorland. The young clerk from the Ministry itself — she'd nearly refused him until he showed her the burn scars on his wrists, self-made, punishment for dreams he couldn't stop having — who bought a charm to keep his grandmother's lullaby from dissolving any further.

"Does it hurt them?" the clerk had asked once, gesturing at the charms. "The memories. Being kept in things."

"They don't mind," Yara had said. "Memories are like cats. They go where it's warm."

Tonight the market was busy in the nervous way it got when the Watch had been seen twice in the district that week. Yara sold a knot-cord to a bricklayer, a remembering-loaf — actual bread, baked over embers from a hearth that had burned before the Edict, one bite and you'd taste a childhood kitchen — to a woman who clutched it to her chest like an infant, and a small vial of canal-water charmed to show reflections of things that used to be reflected in it, to a boy of maybe fifteen who didn't say what he wanted it for and she didn't ask.

Business done, she sat back on her stool and let the market noise wash over her, and here it came again, the feeling she knew best and liked least: the loneliness that arrived precisely when the work was going well. All these people, coming to her with the softest parts of themselves cupped in their hands like water, and not one of them knowing her name — her real one — or her face without the scarf, or the fact that she spent her days swallowing poison for the same crown that would hang her from the Justice Arch if they caught her here. She was two women and neither of them had a friend. The taster belonged to the court. The charm-seller belonged to the dark. And Yara Whitlock, whoever that was underneath, belonged to a basket, a stool, and a room she rented under a false name, where she slept alone and woke alone and counted herself lucky, because alone was safe, and safe was the only luxury the Edict had left anyone.

"You look like a woman doing sums she doesn't like," said Maren, the pepper-seller, packing up her trays beside Yara. Maren was seventy if she was a day, sharp-eyed, and the nearest thing the market had to a grandmother, which meant she noticed everything and approved of nothing.

"Just counting candles," Yara said. "I'm low on wax."

"Mm. And I'm low on teeth, but you don't see me making faces about it." Maren glanced up the aisle of stalls, where the crowd was thinning toward the canal doors. "Watch came by the fishmonger's an hour ago. Asking questions. Not the usual questions."

"What questions, then?"

"Asking after a man. Stranger, come off the western road three days back. Big fellow, they said, travel-worn, asking after—" Maren lowered her voice to almost nothing— "*charms of the old country.*" Her eyes came back to Yara's, and there was something in them Yara had never seen there before: fear, yes, but underneath the fear a strange reluctant awe, like a woman who has seen a comet and isn't sure yet whether it means harvest or ruin. "Nobody sells those. Nobody *has* those. Except—" she stopped herself, which was answer enough.

Except the pressed-flower lockets. Except the ember-blossoms that grew nowhere in the realm, that Yara sourced from a supplier she had never met and paid through a dead-drop she had never watched, and that made the widow weep with joy every month when she opened her locket and saw, for one shining minute, a village green in a country that had never existed.

"I don't know anything about it," Yara said.

"No," Maren agreed mildly. "You don't. That's why I'm telling you to pack up early and go home the long way." She hefted her pepper baskets. "And Yara — if a big travel-worn fellow comes looking, wanting charms of the old country — you sell him nothing, you hear me? Men who ask loud questions get answers from the Watch, and the Watch's answers are all rope."

Maren shuffled off toward the canal doors. Yara sat a moment longer among her guttering candles, her tray of small warm memories, her whole illegal, invisible, carefully balanced life.

Then she looked up, and the big travel-worn fellow was standing at the end of her stall.

He had come in with the last of the crowd, or maybe he had been there all along, folded into shadows the way big men sometimes could when they'd learned to move quietly. Up close she could see the road on him — dust in the seams of his coat, a week of beard, boots gone soft at the soles. And she could see, with the same part of her that tasted arsenic under honey, that something was badly wrong with him. He stood like a man holding himself upright by argument rather than strength. His left sleeve was dark and stuck to his arm. His eyes found hers and they were bright with fever and with something else, something urgent and almost glad, the way a drowning man looks at a rope.

"You're the one," he said. His voice was gravel and blood. "The taster. The one who keeps the flowers."

Every instinct Yara owned screamed at her to deny it, to fold the table, to run. She stayed on her stool. "You've mistaken me for someone else."

"No." He took one step closer, and now she could smell the wound under the road-dust, hot and wrong. "No time. Listen — they're behind me, and I'm past minding." He fumbled at his coat, at the inner pocket, and pulled out a flat brass case the size of a prayer book, and pressed it into her hands with both of his, wrapping her fingers over it like a man pressing a wound closed. The metal was warm. Warmer than his hands. Warm as a living thing.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A map." He smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it was full of relief. "To the Ember Garden. It wouldn't open for me — it chooses, do you see, it *chooses* — but it'll open for you, it's been waiting—" He swayed. Caught himself on the table. Her candles jumped. Somewhere down the aisle, Maren's voice rose sharply, and then cut off.

"Wait — wait, sit down, you're—"

"The garden keeps score," the stranger said, leaning close, and his breath was fever-sweet, and his eyes were fixed on hers with the terrible calm of a man who has finished all his errands but one. "Everything taken, it remembers. Everything forgotten, it keeps. Tell my brother—" and here his voice cracked, the calm splitting down the middle, "—tell Dain it wasn't stolen. Tell him I *gave* it. Tell him—"

The words stopped. Not because he stopped speaking. Because the strength stopped, all at once, like a snuffed candle, and he came down across her folding table with his full weight, scattering charms and candlesticks and six years of careful invisibility, and lay still among the grey cloth and the spilled knots and the little dried apple slices, one hand still resting, lightly, on top of hers.

At the far end of the tide-mill, near the canal doors, torchlight bloomed where no torchlight should be, and Maren's voice rang out high and clear, pitched to carry: "*Run, girl!*"

Yara Whitlock, royal food-taster, keeper of small charms, expert in the exact weight of a fatal dose, stood up from her stool with a dead man's map burning against her palms, and ran.
