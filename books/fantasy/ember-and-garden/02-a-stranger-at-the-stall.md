## 2. A Stranger at the Stall

The night market smelled of tallow and wet stone, of fried onions from the dumpling cart three stalls down, and — beneath it all, if you knew how to breathe it in — of secrets. Yara Whitlock had spent four years learning to read that last scent the way other people read weather. Tonight it hung thick as river fog.

She arranged her wares with the same care she gave the Queen's breakfast tray: everything in its place, nothing that would draw a second glance. To anyone strolling past her corner of Tanner's Row, she was a peddler of trinkets — bone buttons, ribbon, little carved birds for children's windowsills. The trinkets were real. She sold them honestly enough to keep the Watch bored. But tucked inside the false bottom of her button box, wrapped in waxed linen against damp and prying fingers, were the charms: small brass discs no wider than a coin, each stamped with a sigil she'd learned from a woman named Maren who had learned it from someone who had not survived long enough to teach anyone else.

Memory-charms. A pinch of forgetting, or a spoonful of remembering, depending on which face you showed the metal when you pressed your thumb to it. Illegal under the Edict in ways that made selling poison look like selling bread. Yara had swallowed worse things than the law by now; her tongue barely flinched.

"Buttons," she said to a boy who lingered too long, all elbows and curiosity. "Two coppers. Your mother wants the blue ones."

He went away with the blue ones. She watched him go and felt the old familiar weight settle on her chest — not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been simpler. It was more like standing in a doorway between two rooms, belonging fully to neither. By day she knelt before the Queen's table and drank death from a silver spoon so that Her Majesty need never taste it. By night she sold people back pieces of themselves the crown had taken. Both trades required the same skill: knowing precisely what was safe to swallow.

*One more season,* she told herself, as she told herself every season. *Save enough, then disappear somewhere the Edict is a rumor instead of a rope.* She had been telling herself this for four years, and every year the disappearing place grew smaller on the map of things she could actually imagine.

The crowd moved through Tanner's Row like water through a channel — dockworkers off late shift, a pair of dyers' wives with coin to spend and husbands who didn't count it, an old man who came every week to buy one charm he never used, just to hold it, just to remember his wife's voice for the length of a candle. Yara took his two coppers and did not meet his eyes, because meeting eyes made it harder to pretend she wasn't doing something holy and criminal at once.

It was nearly midnight when the screaming started.

Not near — not yet. Somewhere down by the fishmongers' end, where the lanterns thinned out and the canal breathed its cold breath over everything. A woman's voice first, high and cut short. Then men shouting, and the particular sound of a crowd deciding, all at once, to become not-a-crowd.

Yara's hands went still over her button box. Four years of court training lived in her body now, deeper than thought: *when others move, be stone; when others are stone, move.* Around her, the market rippled and broke apart, stall-keepers snuffing lamps, folding awnings, melting into alleys with the practiced ease of people who had done this drill before. The Watch swept Tanner's Row maybe twice a year, usually on rumor, usually finding nothing but honest trinkets and honest lies.

But the shouting wasn't coming closer. It was staying put, down at the dark end, and there was something wrong about the quality of it — not the barking of Watchmen clearing a street but the ragged, urgent noise of men hunting something specific.

She should have packed up. She should have done what everyone else was doing, which was vanish, and counted herself lucky.

Instead she stood on her stool to see over the thinning crowd, and that was when she saw him.

He came out of the alley mouth between the chandler's and the abandoned smokehouse like something the canal had coughed up. Tall, or he would have been if he hadn't been folded nearly double, one arm clamped across his ribs. His coat had been fine once — good wool, city cut — and was now dark with blood from shoulder to hem, glistening where the nearest lantern caught it. He walked the way a man walks when he has decided not to fall until he reaches a particular spot, and Yara understood with a lurch that the particular spot was her stall.

"No," she said, under her breath, absurdly, as if she could refuse him the way she refused a haggler. "No, no —"

He reached the counter and his legs simply stopped being arguments. He went down against the wood hard enough to rock it, and his hand shot out and closed around her wrist with a grip that had terror in it rather than strength.

"You sell memory." Not a question. His voice was wrecked, gravel dragged over gravel, and his accent was none she knew — vowels rounded strangely, consonants soft, like a language heard through a wall. "I looked. I asked. They said the button woman."

"Friend, I sell buttons."

His laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought blood to his lips, and he wiped it with the back of his hand without looking at it, the way a man does when he has already made his peace with the arithmetic of his own body. Up close she could see his face properly now: mid-thirties, hollowed, a week or more of beard, and eyes that were doing something she had only ever seen in mirrors at dawn after bad nights — calculating what could still be carried, and what must be left behind.

"They're coming," he said. "Listen."

She listened. Beneath the scattering market-noise, beneath the canal's whisper: boots. Many boots. And the low, carrying tone of a man giving orders who expected to be obeyed — not Watch-sergeant tones. Something colder. Something that made the hair on her arms rise before her mind caught up.

"Inquisitors," she breathed.

"The General herself sent them." He fumbled at his coat with his free hand, and she saw the wound beneath his arm then, a ragged thing that had soaked through cloth and padding both. "For this. Only this. They burned my brother's house for it. They'll burn yours."

"I don't know what you —"

"Take it." He pulled something from inside his coat, and even half-dead, even hunted, he handled it the way a priest handles relics. A map. Folded, bound in a brass casing the size of a thick book, engraved with lines so fine they seemed to move when she blinked. It was warm. That was the detail her hands reported before anything else, the detail that would come back to her later in nightmares: the brass was warm, like a living thing held close for a very long time. "They can't open it. Only a keeper can. I'm out of keeping."

"I'm not — whatever you think I am, I'm not that. Give it to someone else. There are others —"

"There aren't." For a moment his eyes cleared, and what she saw in them frightened her more than the boots did: certainty. The terrible calm of a man who has run out of alternatives and found, at the bottom of himself, a decision. "Maren's dead eight years. You're what's left of her line. The metal knows its own." He pressed the map into her hands and folded her fingers over it, both of his bloody ones around hers, and the warmth of it sank through her skin into the bones of her wrists. "Hide it. Don't open it. Whatever shows you — don't believe the burning. That's just the door being shy."

"You're dying," she said stupidly. "You need a physicker, I know someone, the canal district —"

"The garden keeps score," he whispered.

And then, softer, with the last of his breath shaped almost into a smile:

"It remembers us all."

He slumped against her stall like a puppet with its strings cut, and the grip on her wrists went slack, and Yara Whitlock — who had drunk poison eleven times without spilling a drop, whose hands did not shake, whose heart rate was a matter of professional discipline — stood in the ruins of the night market holding a warm brass map and a stranger's blood, and shook.

* * *

Later — minutes, hours, she couldn't have said — she made herself move.

The boots were closer now. She could hear the Inquisitor's voice distinctly, unhurried, the way a person speaks when the night belongs to them: *"House by house. He's bleeding; blood leaves a trail; follow the trail."*

Yara looked down at the dead man. In the lantern light his face had gone peaceful in the way faces do, all the terrible certainty drained out of it, leaving just a tired man with a soft accent who had walked a long way to reach a button stall. She noticed things with the part of her brain that never stopped working: the calluses on his right hand, a traveler's calluses; a scar through one eyebrow, old; a wedding band missing but the pale stripe of it still on his finger. Someone had loved him. Someone might be looking for him.

*The garden keeps score.*

She crouched, and with hands that wanted very much to tremble and were not permitted to, she searched him quickly, gently — not for valuables but for names. Nothing. No papers, no tokens, nothing but the blood-soaked coat and the absence where a ring had been. Whoever he was, he had already stripped himself of everything except the map. Everything except the errand.

The boots turned onto Tanner's Row.

There was no time to hide the body, no time to be decent, no time for any of the things a better person would have managed. Yara did the arithmetic of survival the way she did it every morning at the Queen's table: fast, cold, complete. The map went into the false bottom of the button box, wrapped in its waxed linen, beneath forty honest buttons. Her stock went into the satchel. Her awning came down in three practiced motions. And then she was moving, head down, one of a dozen fleeing vendors, a gray woman in a gray cloak in a gray night, while behind her the lanterns of the Inquisition bloomed at the mouth of Tanner's Row like a second, crueler moon.

She made it thirty paces before the voice stopped her.

"You there. Button-seller."

She turned. Every instinct screamed at her to run, and running was death, and so she turned and composed her face into the mild, slightly stupid helpfulness of a woman who sells trinkets and knows nothing, and she looked at the Inquisitor.

He was younger than she expected. That was the horror of them, always — they were never monsters, never the horned devils of chapel paintings. He was perhaps thirty, neat, tired-looking, with ink stains on his fingers like a clerk, and he held a lantern up with the courteous air of a man asking directions. Behind him, four Watchmen were dragging the dead man's body by the arms, leaving a dark seam on the cobblestones that the next rain would take and the next rain would not.

"This man," the Inquisitor said. "Did he speak to you?"

"He fell," Yara said. Her voice came out steady. Eleven poisons had taught it steadiness. "Drunk, I thought. Or stabbed — there was blood. I was packing up, sir, I don't want trouble, I've got a daughter at home."

The lie about the daughter cost her something. It always did. She kept it ready because it humanized her, and humanized women were invisible women, and every time she deployed it she felt the small ghost of a child she did not have turn over in some unlived room.

The Inquisitor studied her. His eyes were pale, almost colorless in the lantern light, and they did not move the way eyes were supposed to move — no flicker to the satchel, no flicker to the button box, just a flat, patient regard, like a man reading a page at his leisure.

"Fell," he repeated.

"Right at my counter, sir. Spooked by all the shouting, I expect."

"And you saw nothing in his hands? He carried nothing?"

"His own guts, near enough." She let a shudder pass through her shoulders, well-timed, mostly genuine. "I stepped back and called for help and nobody came, and then you lot arrived, thank the saints."

Something moved behind the Inquisitor's colorless eyes. Not belief. Not suspicion either, exactly. Cataloguing. She understood suddenly that she was being filed — that somewhere in the machinery of the Inquisition there was now a card with her description on it, and that cards accumulated, and that accumulation was how their kind worked. Not lightning strikes. Water dripping on stone.

"Name?" he said.

"Widow Marsh." The name was older than her career, worn smooth with use. "Of Cooper's Lane."

"Widow Marsh." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen all night, worse than the blood, because it was kind. "If he spoke to you — if he gave you anything, said anything, pressed anything into your hands — you'll bring it to the citadel. Won't you. Good citizens bring things to the citadel."

"Course I would, sir."

"Of course you would." He stepped aside, gracious as a host, and the lantern light swung away from her face, releasing her. "Go home to your daughter, Widow Marsh."

She walked. She did not hurry — hurrying was a confession written on the body — but she walked, past the Watchmen and the dark seam on the stones, past the chandler's shuttered window, past the mouth of the alley the dead man had come out of, where the shadows still seemed to hold the shape of his stumbling. She walked until Tanner's Row was behind her and the canal district swallowed her, and only then did she let herself feel the map against her hip, warm even through the satchel, warm even through the linen, warm as a coal that had no intention of going out.

* * *

Her rooms above the dye-shop were one candle, one bed, one window overlooking the canal, and a floorboard she trusted. She lit the candle because darkness had stopped feeling safe somewhere back on Tanner's Row, and she sat on the bed with the button box on her knees for a long time without opening it.

The sensible thing — the thing the Widow Marshes of the world would do — was to drop it in the canal. Brass sank. Wax-sealed brass sank faster. By morning it would be a story the river kept, and she would be a button-seller again, and the Inquisitor's card would gather dust in some drawer because there would be nothing to find.

She got as far as the window. Opened it. Held the box out over the black water, where the canal breathed its cold breath and ate everything given to it, memories included, if you believed the old stories.

*Don't believe the burning,* the dead man had said. *That's just the door being shy.*

*The garden keeps score. It remembers us all.*

She thought of the old man who bought a charm every week just to hold his wife's voice for the length of a candle. She thought of Maren, who had died with her hands burned, and of the word *keeper*, and of the pale stripe on a dead stranger's ring finger, and of the Inquisitor's kind, colorless smile, and of the Queen eating breakfast every morning off plates Yara had made safe, in a palace built — if half the forbidden songs were true — on the grave of a country no one was allowed to name.

The Edict said magic was outlawed. The Edict did not say why, anymore. That was the trick of it, the deep rotten cleverness: they had erased the crime and the reason together, so that obedience needed no justification, only habit.

Yara closed the window. She set the box on the bed, knelt, and lifted the false bottom with the nail of her thumb.

The map lay in its nest of linen, and the candlelight did something to it that candlelight should not be able to do — slid along the engraved lines and made them seem to deepen, to breathe, to wait. Warmth rose off it like the warmth of a hand.

She did not touch it. Not yet. She wrapped it back up, restored the false bottom, and sat back on her heels with her heart going like a hammer, understanding that she had just made a decision the way you fall ill — gradually, and then all at once.

Because here was the thing she had realized at the window, the thing that would not leave her alone: the Inquisitor had asked for her name, and she had given a false one, and he had smiled as though he knew. And the dead man had known her stall, her trade, her line — *the metal knows its own* — which meant others knew it too, which meant the net around the night market had been drawn tight long before tonight, and she was not a bystander who had stumbled into this story.

She was in it. Had perhaps always been in it. Inheritance, Maren used to say, isn't what you're given. It's what won't let go of you.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to toll the hour — and then kept tolling, past the hour, an irregular, urgent clangor she had never heard before, and below her window the canal district began, door by door, to light up with the Watch's lanterns moving through it, street by street, like fire finding dry grass.

They weren't searching Tanner's Row anymore.

They were searching everywhere — and somewhere in the spreading pattern of those lights, Yara understood, was a list, and her false name was on it, and the warm brass thing beneath her floorboard was already too heavy to throw away.
