## 3. What the Map Refuses

The map had not left her hands all night.

Yara sat in the dark of her rooms above the tallow-chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, the shutters drawn, one candle lit and then snuffed, then lit again because she could not bear the full dark with the thing lying on her table. It was a disc of brass no larger than a saucer, cold as a coin kept in a well, and it refused her.

She had tried everything a charm-seller knew. She had warmed it against her skin. She had whispered to it the way she whispered to memory-charms when she set them — *open, open, be what you are* — the small coaxing words that made wax and bone-dust and dried ember-ash remember their purpose. She had held it over steam until condensation wept down its face. Nothing. The brass stayed shut, seamless as an egg, except for the single groove that spiraled inward from its rim toward a center that would not yield.

And yet it was not blank. That was the worst of it.

When she turned it in the light at certain angles — only certain angles, only when the flame stood just so — the surface showed her something. Not an engraving. Something deeper than engraving, something that moved beneath the metal like a fish under ice: fields. Fields of grain going gold in a wind she could not feel, hedgerows, a road stitched white between them, a village with a bell-tower. And then fire. Always the fire came. The fields burned from the horizon inward, the village went up like a struck match, the bell-tower folded into smoke, and then the brass was brass again, featureless, smug, closed.

She had watched it burn eleven times before she stopped counting.

"You are mocking me," she told it. Her voice sounded thin in the little room. "Or you're grieving. I haven't decided which."

The map said nothing, of course. Maps did not. But this one had been pressed into her hands by a dying man who had looked at her — not through her, not past her, but *at* her, the way people rarely bothered to look at a food-taster, a woman whose whole profession was to be interchangeable — and he had chosen her. Out of every door on that street, out of every passing cloak, he had stumbled into her stall in the night market and put his blood on her hands and his weight on her shoulder and said *the garden keeps score* in a voice like a hinge rusting shut.

She had gone over those last minutes so many times they had worn smooth, like a stair tread. The man — tall, gray at the temples, dressed well enough to be gentry but with the frayed cuffs of someone who had been running for days — had come out of the alley behind the fishmonger's row moving wrong, favoring his left side where a dark bloom spread across his ribs. He had seen her stall's little lantern, the one she hung with a green glass chimney so her customers could find her among the fortune-tellers and the rat-catchers, and he had changed course toward it with the terrible directness of a drowning man swimming for a light.

"Hide it," he'd said. Or she thought he'd said it; his lips had barely moved. Then the map, shoved into her palms still warm from his body. Then, as she knelt and pressed her hands to the wound and felt the heat of him pouring out between her fingers, he had caught her wrist with a strength that startled her and said, close to her ear, each word dropped like a stone into a well:

"The garden keeps score."

Then nothing. His eyes had stayed open. She had closed them, because no one else would, and because she had learned long ago that the dead are owed small courtesies by whoever happens to be standing near.

She had run. Of course she had run. The Watch patrolled the night market's edges on feast nights, and a crowd was already turning, and there is no innocence like a woman kneeling over a corpse with blood to her elbows. She had taken the map because leaving it would have meant explaining it, and she had taken his purse too — two coppers and a button, which told her he had been robbed of everything worth taking long before the knife found him — and she had slipped into the drain-cut behind the tannery and gone home through the dark with her heart slamming like a fist on a door.

Now it was nearly dawn, and she was tired in the marrow-deep way that poison-work makes familiar, and the map lay on her table showing her burning fields for the twelfth time, and she understood, with a dread that settled into her stomach colder than any draught she had ever swallowed at court, that she could not give it back.

There was no back to give it to.

* * *

Court did not wait for grief. Court never waited for anything.

Yara arrived at the palace by the servants' gate as the bells rang six, washed, combed, her hands scrubbed pink, wearing the plain gray of her office with the silver tongue-badge pinned at her collar. She had been the Queen's third taster for four years now, and in four years she had learned that the work was mostly waiting punctuated by moments of exquisite attention. She waited now in the antechamber off the buttery while the morning's dishes were laid out, and she let the rhythm of the place fold around her — the clatter of copper, the bawling of the under-cooks, the smell of bread and roasting meat and, underneath it all, the faint mineral tang of the silverware polish they were forbidden to use too liberally because she had once complained it numbed the tongue.

Her tongue was her livelihood twice over. By day it detected nightshade in the frangipane and bitter almond where almond had no business being. By night, in the market, it was the instrument of a different trade: she tasted every charm before she sold it, a drop on the tongue, reading its making the way a jeweler reads a stone. The Edict had driven magic underground, but it had not driven away the need for it. People forget that forgetting is a service. A widow wants to stop hearing her husband's last words. A soldier wants to unsee a battle. A girl wants to unlove a boy who beat her. Yara sold them small dissolutions, sealed in wax, priced by weight of sorrow, and in four years she had never once been caught, because a woman who tastes poison for a living knows exactly how much danger a body can carry.

"Taster." The steward's clerk snapped his fingers at her. "Queen's table first. Then the nursery tray. Move."

She moved. She sipped the morning chocolate — nothing. Touched her tongue to the queen's honey-bread — nothing but honey and the ghost of rosewater. She worked through the courses with the automatic precision of long habit while her mind sat elsewhere, in a chandler's room, staring at a closed brass egg.

It was during the second course that she heard the name.

"—found him by the fishmonger's row, poor devil," the pastry cook was saying to the larder keeper, both of them pretending to work while they talked, as kitchen staff always did. "Watch found him at dawn. Stabbed, right there in the open, bold as you please."

"Robbery?"

"What would anyone rob off him? He had nothing. But here's the thing—" the cook lowered her voice, and Yara, tasting a spoonful of quince conserve she did not taste at all, lowered her own breathing to listen, "—they're saying the Inquisitor General's men came for the body. Not the Watch. *Hers.* Carried it off themselves, wouldn't let the parish bury him. And now they're going door to door along the market streets asking after him. Who saw him. Who spoke to him."

"Lady Sarel doesn't trouble herself over cutpurses' leavings," the larder keeper said.

"No," agreed the cook. "Which means he wasn't a cutpurse's leavings, doesn't it?"

Yara set down the spoon with a steady hand and asked for the next dish, and her voice did not shake, and inside she was already running.

* * *

They came to Candlewick Row on the third day.

By then Yara had learned three things, none of them comforting. First: the dead man had a name, or half of one — Vey, some said, a merchant's agent, though no merchant house claimed him. Second: the Inquisitor General's men were not asking after him the way Watchmen ask, idly, hopefully, angling for bribes. They asked the way surgeons ask, precisely, cuttingly, and they wrote everything down in little black books. Third: they had visited the night market twice, and the second time they had come with a list of stall-holders, and her green-lantern stall had been on it.

So on the third day she did not go to the market. She sat in her room above the chandler's shop with the door barred and the map before her, and she tried, for the hundredth time, to make it speak.

She talked to it. She felt foolish and did it anyway. "I don't want your secret," she said. "I want to give you back. Tell me who to give you to, and I'll walk him there myself, and we'll call the whole thing done." The brass showed her a burning orchard and went dark. "Fine," she said. "Keep your fires."

She tried the trick she used on stubborn charms: sympathy. Every charm remembers being made, and if you can find the shape of its making, you can find the seam. She held the map against her sternum, closed her eyes, and let herself sink into the slow tasting-attention, the state where flavors become legible, where a drop of wine can tell you the year and the valley and whether the vintner wept at the pressing. She reached for the map's flavor.

What she got was ash. Not metaphorical ash — an actual taste flooded her mouth, dry and bitter and vast, the taste of a country's worth of burned grain, burned orchards, burned houses, a taste so large it had no business fitting in a human mouth, and beneath the ash something sweeter and more terrible: roses. Ember-flowers, she thought, though she had never smelled one and could not have said how she knew the name. She wrenched herself out of the tasting-state gasping, her eyes streaming, her tongue scoured as if she'd gargled with cinders.

The map lay on the table, smug and shut.

"All right," she rasped. "You've made your point."

The knocking started at dusk.

Three doors down first — she heard the muffled voices, the polite menace of them, a woman sobbing about her sick mother and charms, no, never, officer, nothing like that. Then next door. Then, with a calm that terrified her more than shouting would have, a knock at her own door, soft as a lover's.

Yara put the map inside the hollow bedpost where she kept her stock, slid the panel shut, and opened the door on a man in the slate-gray of the Inquisitor General's service. He was young, courteous, and carried a little black book.

"Mistress Whitlock? Food-taster to Her Majesty?" He consulted the book even though he had clearly memorized it. "We're making inquiries regarding a deceased gentleman, name possibly Vey, last seen in the night market off Tanner's Cut. You hold a stall there, I believe. Green lantern."

"I do," Yara said. Her voice was perfectly level. Four years of swallowing poison will teach a person to keep their voice level while their throat closes.

"Were you present in the market three nights past?"

"I was. I left early. Business was poor."

He looked up at her then, and his eyes were mild and bottomless, wells with no water in them. "No one approached your stall? No one spoke to you? No one... gave you anything?"

The pause she allowed was exactly the length of honest recollection. This, too, was craft: she had rehearsed it since dawn. "A drunk gave me a kiss on the cheek and I charged him for the privilege," she said. "If your gentleman died of a stolen kiss, officer, I'm your culprit."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — appreciation, perhaps, or appetite. He wrote in the book anyway. "The Inquisitor General thanks you for your cooperation. She may wish to speak with you further. Do keep yourself available, Mistress Whitlock."

"Her Majesty's service keeps me very available," Yara said. "I go where the Queen eats."

He smiled at that, thin as paper, tipped his head, and went down the stairs. She listened to him knock at the chandler's door below, listened to old Marta deny everything in the flat voice of a woman who has survived three purges by knowing nothing, and only when his footsteps faded entirely did Yara allow herself to slide down against her own door and sit on the floor, shaking.

Because she had lied to an Inquisitor's man, and he had written the lie down, and lies written in little black books do not stay buried. Because he had said *she may wish to speak with you further*, and everyone in the city knew what it meant when the Inquisitor General wished to speak with you: you went into the gray tower on the river, and sometimes you came out, and you were never quite the same person who went in. And because — this was the thing she kept circling, the splinter working deeper — the map had shown her burning fields, and burning fields were not a riddle. Burning fields were a *country*. Somewhere, sometime, a whole country had burned, and this bit of brass remembered it, and the Inquisitor General wanted it badly enough to walk the streets herself asking questions, and Yara Whitlock, who had survived four years at court and four years in the market by touching nothing larger than a widow's grief, was holding it in a hollow bedpost three feet from where an Inquisitor's man had stood.

She could not give it back. There was no one to give it to. She could not sell it — to whom? To whom would one sell a country's ghost? She could not destroy it; she suspected, with the certainty of the tongue that read makings, that the thing could not be destroyed, only passed on, and it had passed to *her*, and the dying man's voice was still in her ear, patient as water dripping in a cave:

*The garden keeps score.*

She got up off the floor. She took the map from the bedpost and set it on the table and lit the candle, and the brass showed her the gold fields, and the road, and the bell-tower, and then the fire, rolling in from the horizon like a tide, eating everything, and for the first time she noticed what she had missed twelve times before: at the very edge of the burning, small as a beetle, a figure was running. Running toward the viewer. Running out of the fire, out of the vanished country, carrying something bright against its chest.

The image guttered and died. The brass went dark.

Yara sat very still, her heart ticking like a cooling stove.

Someone had gotten out. Someone had carried something out of the burning — and that someone, she understood with a dread that felt almost like recognition, had been the man who died with his blood on her hands. And whatever he had carried, it had not finished its journey. It was sitting on her table, waiting for her to finish it.

Below, in the street, boots. Many boots, unhurried, coming up Candlewick Row.

And with them, threaded through the knock that began at the far end of the street and advanced door by door, a voice she had not heard before but knew instantly, from the way the whole street seemed to flinch at it — a woman's voice, level and pleasant and absolutely without mercy, asking her gentle questions of each trembling household:

"The garden keeps score," the voice repeated, musing, to someone who must have confessed it. "How interesting. And who else has heard those words?"

Yara pinched out the candle.
