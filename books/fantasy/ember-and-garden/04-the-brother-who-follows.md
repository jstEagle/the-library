## 4. The Brother Who Follows

Dain Vess had learned to follow a man the way water follows a crack in stone: patiently, without argument, filling every gap the man left behind him.

The woman was harder. She moved like smoke poured through a keyhole — quick, unremarkable, always half-turned so that her face never quite offered itself to the street. Three days he had tracked her through the lower city and she had not once done the ordinary things people did. She did not linger at bakeries. She did not gossip with fishwives. She bought a twist of bread from the same cart each morning, ate it walking, and vanished into the warren of lanes around Tanner's Row as though the city had swallowed her whole.

But everyone surfaced eventually. Everyone had to eat, sleep, sell something. And this one sold something — he'd seen it now, twice, in the ragged hours after midnight when the night market kindled its paper lanterns along the canal. A stall no wider than a coffin, hung with knotted cords and painted beads and little clay whistles that looked like nothing at all.

Charms. The word sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

*Magic*, sold openly to anyone with three coppers and a wish, in a city where the Edict had hung on every gate for thirty years: *NO CHARM, NO CANTRIP, NO CUNNING. THE CROWN FORGIVES NOTHING THAT WHISPERS.*

His brother had died for a charm-seller's trinket. Dain intended to know why.

He crouched in the shadow of a dye-works wall, watching the market wake below him — lanterns blooming one by one like fever-blisters, the smell of fried onions and tallow and river mud rising with the evening damp. Somewhere a fiddler tuned against the noise. And there, third stall past the oyster barrow, the woman set out her wares with the brisk economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

Yara Whitlock. He'd pried the name out of a lantern-keeper with two silver bits and a look he'd learned from their father, who had been able to make men confess with nothing but his eyebrows. The name meant nothing. The face meant everything, because it was the last face Corvin had stood in front of, according to the dockhand who'd found him bleeding into the cobbles off Rope Walk.

*She has it,* the dockhand swore. *Saw her running with something clutched to her chest like a babe. Brass. Glinted under the lamps.*

Brass. Dain turned the shape of it over in his mind again, the way he had every night since they'd buried what was left of his brother: a flat case, hinged, etched at the edges. Corvin's map. Their mother's map. The thing their father had died carrying across a border that officially did not exist, the thing Corvin had spent eleven years and most of his blood earning the right to carry next.

The Ember Garden. The last green lie of Vessany.

And some charm-peddling stranger had run away with it while Corvin cooled on the stones.

Dain's hands curled until his nails bit his palms. He let the pain steady him. Anger was a tool; his father had taught him that too, though he'd taught it with fists, which was why Dain had left home at fourteen and Corvin had stayed and grown soft and clever and dead. He breathed. He counted the exits from the market square — four, counting the culvert under the fishmonger's awning — and began to descend into the light.

* * *

She saw him coming. He gave her credit for that much. Most people didn't notice a big man in a grey coat until he was already inside arm's reach, but when he was six stalls away her hands went still over her wares, and when he was three stalls away she had already begun, casually, to close the lid of her case.

He didn't rush. Rushing was for amateurs and Inquisitors. He stopped at the front of her stall and looked at the goods laid out on the stained velvet: knotted cords dyed seven colors, a dish of iron nails bent into rings, small glass vials of river water that supposedly remembered being rain.

"Which one of these," he said, "tells you where a man will die?"

Her eyes came up. Grey-green, level, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. Up close she was younger than he'd guessed — mid-twenties maybe — with ink stains on her fingers and a burn scar on the back of one hand, the kind a cook or a tinker gets. Nothing about her said *thief*. Everything about her said *careful*.

"None," she said. "I sell forgettings, mostly. Small mercies. You look like a man who could use one."

"I'm looking for my brother."

"Condolences." Her voice didn't change, but her hand drifted, very slightly, toward the edge of the stall. Toward the dark lane behind it. "I don't know your brother."

"Corvin Vess." Dain laid both palms flat on the counter, deliberately, so she could see how large they were. "Tall. Dark hair. A scar through his left eyebrow. Died Tuesday night off Rope Walk with his skull opened by someone who wanted what he carried."

Around them the market went on — haggling, frying, fiddling — but a pocket of quiet had formed at their stall, the way quiet does around a wound. The woman didn't flinch. That was the first thing that made him uneasy. Guilty people flinched.

"If he was robbed," she said, "take it up with the Watch."

"The Watch doesn't work nights in the low city, and we both know it." He leaned closer. Under the smell of onions and tallow he caught hers — beeswax and something bitter, herbaceous, like the inside of an apothecary's drawer. "You were there. A dockhand saw you running with brass in your hands. So here is what happens now, mistress charm-seller. You give me back my brother's property, and I walk away, and nobody learns what you keep under this table."

For a heartbeat — one full heartbeat — something moved behind her eyes that wasn't fear. It looked almost like grief, quickly folded away, like linen snapped flat before it could show the stain.

Then she moved, and she was fast.

Not toward the lane — toward *him*. Her hand came off the counter holding a clay whistle, and she blew it straight into his face, a thin shriek of a note that shouldn't have been loud enough to hurt but went through his skull like an icepick, and the world tilted sideways, the lanterns smearing into long orange ribbons. His knees hit the cobbles. Someone screamed — or laughed; in the night market it was hard to tell. He grabbed for her ankle as she vaulted the counter and caught boot-leather, and then her other boot caught him under the jaw and stars burst across his vision, white and gold, and he lost her.

He lost her for exactly nine steps.

He knew because he counted them as he crawled upright, blood singing in his ears, and then he stopped counting and simply ran, shoulder down, through the crowd, scattering an oyster seller and a pair of drunk weavers, following the grey flicker of her cloak down the lane behind the stall, past the culvert, into the tangle of streets where Tanner's Row rotted into the tanneries' reek.

She was fast. He was bigger, and angrier, and he knew these streets better than she ever would, because he'd grown up two districts over and had spent his youth learning every route a boy could take when the Watch came calling. He cut left where she cut left and came out ahead of her at the mouth of Gibbet Lane, where the walls narrowed to a shoulders'-width and the only light was a single oil lamp burning green above a shuttered shrine.

She skidded to a stop. Turned. Found him filling the lane behind her like a door closing.

"There," he said, breathing hard, "is nowhere charming enough to save you."

"You're making a mistake." Her voice was still level. Still that maddening calm. But she was backing up now, one slow step at a time, deeper into the lane, and her hand had gone inside her cloak. "Whatever you think I took—"

"My brother is dead." The words came out of him rougher than he'd planned, torn rather than spoken, and he hated himself for it. "Eleven years he worked for what he carried. Eleven years of ledgers and licenses and kissing the ring of every guild-master in this city so he could move one item across one border without the Edict tearing it out of his hands. And he dies in the mud on Rope Walk, and a *charm-seller* runs away with it. So either you killed him, or you robbed a dying man, and I find I don't much care which."

"I didn't—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone quieter, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "He gave it to me. Do you understand? He pressed it into my hands. He was dying, and he held onto my wrist hard enough to bruise, and he said—" Her breath caught, just once. "*The garden keeps score.* Then the Watch horns sounded and I ran, because I'm a food-taster with a stall of illegal charms and a dead man's blood on my skirt, and staying would have gotten me hanged before dawn."

Dain stared at her.

*The garden keeps score.* Their father's phrase. A private thing, a family thing, a thing Corvin had only ever whispered once, years ago, drunk on plum brandy and homesickness: *the garden keeps score, Dain, and someday it'll call in ours.* No stranger should know it. No stranger could know it.

"You're lying," he said, because the alternative was unbearable.

"Search me, then." She opened her arms, bitter, breathless. "Go on. If I have it, take it."

He crossed the lane in three strides and seized her by the collar of her cloak, slamming her back against the wall beneath the green lamp, and she let him — let him tear open the cloak, pat down the hidden pockets of her coat, feel along the seams of her sleeves. Nothing. No brass case, no map, no weight of legacy anywhere on her body. Only a knife at her hip, which she pointedly did not reach for, and a small cloth bundle at her breast that crinkled like dried flowers.

"Where is it," he said, close enough now to see the pulse jumping in her throat.

"I don't know," she said, and the terrible thing — the thing that turned his anger cold in his chest — was that she sounded like she meant it. "It wouldn't open for me. It showed me fire and then it went silent. I've tried everything. I thought if I could get it working, I could—" She stopped herself. Swallowed. "I could give it back. To whoever it belonged to. I never wanted it, do you hear me? I inherited a corpse's errand and I can't put it down."

Something in the lane changed. Dain couldn't have said what — a quality to the silence, a wrongness in the way the green lamp flame stood perfectly still — but Yara felt it too. He saw it hit her face a half-second before he understood it himself.

"Lamp," she breathed. "Look at the lamp."

The flame above the shrine had gone still as a painted flame. No flicker. No sway. And beneath it, faintly, the air itself had begun to hum, a note too low to hear properly and impossible to ignore, like a struck bell heard through a wall.

Dain had heard that hum once before, fourteen years ago, from a hillside across the border, watching riders in grey come down toward a village whose name was already being scraped from the maps. He had been a child. He had hidden in a bramble patch and watched, and the humming had come before the screaming, and he had never forgotten either.

"Inquisitors," Yara whispered.

Boots rang on stone at both ends of the lane. Grey coats, six of them, moving with the unhurried precision of men who had already decided how this ended. At their center walked a woman in a black cassock with a chain of office at her throat, and even in the bad light Dain recognized the set of her shoulders, the tilt of her chin — the Inquisitor General herself, Sarel, come personally, which meant someone upstairs considered this matter worth more than a routine burning.

"Charm-work detected," one of the greys called out, flat as a ledger entry. "Two subjects. Cornered. No egress."

Yara's hand found Dain's wrist in the dark. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"They're not here for me," she said, very fast, very low. "They've been asking about a dead man for two days. They're here for the map, and they think one of us has it, and if they take us we'll tell them everything we know whether we want to or not. So." Her grey-green eyes came up to his, and there was no calm in them now, only a fierce, calculating urgency that he recognized, suddenly and horribly, as his brother's own expression. "Are you going to fight beside me, Vess? Or are you going to fight me first?"

Behind them, boots. Ahead, boots. The green lamp flame shivered once, as if something enormous had exhaled.

Dain Vess thought of his brother dying in the mud with a stranger's wrist in his grip, trusting her with the family's whole inheritance, and understood — with the clarity of a man stepping off a ledge — that he had no choice at all.

"Together, then," he said.

He tore the iron nail-ring from her dish-memory of a stall — no; from his own belt, the old ward-ring their father had worn, iron bent cold and true — and slammed it against the shrine wall just as the first grey coat rounded the corner, and the lane filled with light that had no business existing in a city where magic was outlawed.

The last thing he saw before the world went white was the charm-seller's hand locked in his, holding on.

* * *

They came out of the white into an alley that smelled of wet ash and old fish, three streets and a lifetime away from Gibbet Lane, both of them on their knees in the muck, gasping, singed, alive.

"What," Dain managed, "was that."

"That," Yara Whitlock said, spitting out canal water she hadn't drunk, "was the last charm I owned. And it cost me everything I had." She pushed her hair out of her face and looked at him — really looked, appraising, the way she might eye a dish she'd been asked to taste. "Your brother trusted me with something that gets people hunted. Now I suppose you're stuck finding out why."

Somewhere behind them, faint but relentless, the Inquisitors' boots kept ringing on stone.

They ran.
