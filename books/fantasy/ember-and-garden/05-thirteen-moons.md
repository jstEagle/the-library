## 5. Thirteen Moons

The safehouse was a cooper's cellar off Tanner's Row, and it smelled of oak shavings and old brine and the particular sourness of fear that had been living in a room too long. Yara had chosen it herself, three years ago, back when the worst thing she had to hide was a tray of memory-charms sewn into the lining of a fishmonger's cart. She had not imagined she would ever need it for something like this.

She sat on an upturned barrel with the brass map across her knees, and for the hundredth time since the night market burned, she tried to make it open.

It would not. It lay in her hands like a dead man's tongue — heavy, cold, closed. The brass casing was worked all over with vines and leaves so fine they might have been real, pressed into metal by some craftsman who had loved growing things, and at its center was a roundel the size of her palm where the map itself should have unfurled. There was no seam. No clasp. No hinge. Only the roundel, polished smooth by handling, and around its rim a line of script in an alphabet she did not know, looping and thorned like blackberry runners.

She had tried warmth. She had tried blood — a pricked thumb, smeared along the rim, which had accomplished nothing except to make her feel foolish and slightly lightheaded. She had tried speaking to it, first politely, then with the low urgent coaxing she used on frightened customers in the night market, the voice that said *I know what you've lost, I can help you carry it.* Nothing. The map kept its counsel the way the dead keep theirs: absolutely, and with a kind of dignity that made her furious.

Above her, through the floorboards, Dain Corvane paced.

He had been pacing for two days. She could set a water clock by him: forty steps toward the coal chute, forty steps back, a pause at the window slit where he would stand motionless for exactly as long as it took to convince himself no one was watching the house, and then begin again. He ate nothing she offered. He slept, when he slept at all, sitting upright against the wall with his brother's knife across his thighs, and he woke at every sound like a man surfacing from drowning.

*The garden keeps score,* the dying stranger had whispered, pressing this map into her hands with fingers already going cold. *The garden keeps score.*

Yara had spent four nights telling herself she should get rid of it. Drop it in the Lethe Canal, where the water ate memories; let it dissolve into the same gray forgetting that swallowed everything else people couldn't bear. But every time she lifted it toward the canal's edge, her arms simply — refused. It was not sentiment. It was something older and more stubborn than sentiment, a feeling like the map had put down a root through her palm and into the meat of her, and pulling free would take more of her than she was willing to lose.

So instead she sat in a cooper's cellar with a murderer's brother pacing overhead, and she studied an alphabet that did not exist, and she waited for the thirteenth moon without knowing what the thirteenth moon was.

* * *

It was Dain who broke first. He always broke first; she was learning this about him the way you learn the habits of a dangerous animal — reluctantly, and with your weight on your back foot.

On the third evening he came down the ladder without announcing himself, which was his way, and stood at the bottom rung watching her with his arms crossed. In the lamplight his face had the geography of a man who had not forgiven the world for anything in a long time: the scar through his left eyebrow, the bruise-yellow remains of their fight still shadowing his jaw, the eyes that were his brother's eyes and therefore unbearable to look at directly.

"You're reading it wrong," he said.

"I'm not reading it at all." She didn't look up. "That's rather the problem."

"The letters." He came closer, boots loud on the plank floor, and crouched beside the barrel with a roughness that was almost contempt. "You keep turning it to follow the lines left to right. You're reading it like a capital scribe. It doesn't work that way."

"And how does it work, then? Since you're such an expert on scripts that don't exist."

"They exist." Something moved behind his eyes — not softness, never softness, but a door opening briefly onto a room where softness had once lived. "They existed. That's Vessany script. Garden-script. Our mother wrote shopping lists in it."

Yara went very still. The lamplight ticked. Somewhere above them a rat moved along a joist with the confidence of a creature that paid no rent.

"Your mother," she said carefully, "wrote shopping lists in a language that has been illegal for longer than either of us has been alive."

"My mother," Dain said, "was born in a country that has been illegal for longer than either of us has been alive. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." He held out his hand, palm up. Not asking. Demanding. "Give it here."

Every instinct she owned told her not to. The map had come to *her*. It had crossed a dying man's fingers into hers while the Watch's whistles shrilled three streets away, and whatever root it had put down through her palm, she did not trust Dain Corvane not to tear it out and walk away with it and leave her holding nothing but a death sentence and a dead man's debt. He believed she had stolen it. He had told her so, at knifepoint, in an alley that smelled of rotting cabbage, before the Inquisitors interrupted them and made allies of them out of arithmetic — two fugitives running better together than apart.

But he had also, two nights ago, pulled her out of the path of a crossbow bolt in the burning market without hesitating, and had taken a slash across his forearm doing it, and had said afterward only, *Don't make me regret it.*

She gave him the map.

He took it the way a priest takes a relic — both hands, head bowed slightly, as though the brass weighed more than brass. And then he turned it in the lamplight until the roundel faced him, and he began to read the rim aloud, but not left to right. He read it the way water goes down a drain: inward, spiraling, each loop of letters tightening toward the center, and as he read, Yara felt the hair rise on her forearms, because the sound of it was not the sound of language at all. It was the sound of a key turning. Somewhere very far away and also very close, inside the brass, inside her own chest, something *clicked*.

The roundel warmed under his thumbs.

"It's a counting rhyme," Dain said, and his voice had gone strange, flattened, the voice of a man reciting something learned in childhood and never once questioned since. "A children's rhyme. *One moon for the sowing, two moons for the green, three for the blossom the winter never seen—*" He stopped. Swallowed. "Our mother sang it. I thought it was nonsense. I thought—" His jaw worked. "I thought it was just a song about gardening."

"How many verses?"

"Thirteen." He looked up at her, and for the first time since the alley, since the knife, since the whole bloody catastrophe of their acquaintance, Dain Corvane looked afraid. "Thirteen verses. Thirteen moons. Yara — the door opens once. At the thirteenth moon turn. And then—" he turned the map over, and there, on the base where no seam had been, a seam now showed, fine as a hair, "—and then it closes for another cycle. Whatever cycle is. A lifetime. Longer."

"When?" Her mouth had gone dry. "When is the thirteenth moon turn?"

He didn't answer immediately. He laid the map flat on the barrel between them, and the seam caught the light, and around the roundel the vine-work seemed — she would swear this later, and swear she wasn't mad — seemed to have shifted, the leaves turned slightly, as if the whole brass surface had exhaled.

"Our mother died in autumn," he said. "Tam — my brother — he counted from her grave. He told me once, drunk, that he'd found the date. The turn falls at the next dark-of-the-moon after the harvest fair. Six weeks. Maybe seven."

Six weeks. Yara looked at the map, at the fine hairline seam that hadn't existed three days ago, and felt the full weight of what she was carrying settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. Six weeks to find a door she couldn't see, in a country that officially had never existed, while the Inquisitor General's net drew tighter around the city with every passing day.

"And if we miss it," she said. "If we're late, or caught, or dead."

"Then Tam died for nothing," Dain said, "and a country stays dead, and the people who erased it win forever." He stood, and his shadow fell across the map, and for a moment he looked old — old as grief, old as law. "So we don't miss it."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the lamp had burned down to a thumbnail and neither of them had slept — Dain sat against the wall with his knees drawn up and told her about Vessany.

Not the official version. Everyone knew the official version, or rather everyone knew the hole where the official version should have been: a blank space on the maps east of the Thornwater, a name that schoolmasters skipped, a shape in the histories like a tooth knocked out of a jaw. *Unsettled lands*, the atlases said. *Marginal quality soil. Sparsely inhabited.* As if a whole country could be summarized in a tax assessor's disappointment.

"There was a plague," Dain said, to the darkness more than to her. "Twenty-two years ago. Before I was born — Tam was six. They called it the Withering, because it started in plants. Orchards first. Then field crops, then hedgerows, then — the histories say — people. Everything it touched, it touched *green* first. Things budded out of season, flowered in frost, grew wrong, grew *fast*, and then came the gray. The withering. Whole valleys went silver and silent in a single summer."

"And magic caused it." Yara had heard this part. Every child had heard this part, in the same breath as the Edict: *magic brought the Withering, magic killed ten thousand people, and so magic is outlawed, and so we are safe.* It was the first lesson and the last lesson and the whole curriculum between.

"That's what the decree says." Dain's voice was flat as slate. "The Edict of Unmaking. Signed by the old king's own hand, sealed with the sun-and-sword. It didn't just outlaw magic. It outlawed the *country*. You can't have a plague-country on your border; it frightens the neighbors and depresses the grain futures. So Vessany was struck from the rolls. Its towns renamed or razed. Its people dispersed — those who survived the plague and the soldiers that came after, because the decree didn't distinguish between the sick and the practitioners, and in Vessany there wasn't any difference. Everyone gardened. Everyone worked small craft. Green-craft, hearth-craft, memory-craft." He glanced at her, one corner of his mouth twisting. "Like your charms, taster. Like the little sins you sell in the night market. Half the hedge-magic in this city is Vessany craft that survived by going underground and forgetting its own name."

Yara said nothing. Her charms — the memory-knots, the grief-eases, the little braided tokens that helped a widow sleep or a debtor forget a face — had always felt to her like inherited things, skills in her hands older than her hands. She had never asked herself where they came from. Asking felt suddenly like standing at the edge of a well.

"The plague broke," Dain went on, "the year after the decree. Or the decree broke the plague — that's what the priests say, that cutting the country out of the world cut the sickness out with it. But Tam didn't believe it. Tam said the gardeners of Vessany saw the Withering coming years early, and they didn't cause it, they *quarantined* it. They grew a garden around it. A garden so deep and so rooted in the old craft that it could hold the plague the way a jar holds smoke — hold it past the decree, past the erasure, past the lifetimes of everyone who remembered why. And the price of the holding was that the garden had to be forgotten too. Sealed. Locked behind thirteen moons and a door that opens once." His hands had curled into fists on his knees. "And our mother was one of the gardeners. And when the soldiers came, she hid us in a root cellar and walked out to meet them singing that counting rhyme, and she never came back, and Tam never forgot, and Tam spent his whole life finding the way back in."

The lamp guttered. Yara watched the flame right itself and thought about the stranger bleeding out against her stall, whispering *the garden keeps score*, and understood at last that he had not been delirious. He had been giving her the accounting.

"If the garden holds the plague," she said slowly, "then opening the door—"

"—lets the smoke out of the jar. Yes. That's what Sarel's lot believe, anyway. That's the sermon they preach on every corner: the Ember Garden is the plague's heart, and the woman carrying the map is carrying pestilence, and burning her is mercy." Dain's laugh had no humor in it at all. "But Tam believed the opposite. Tam said the Withering is long dead — starved, spent, twenty-two years in the dark with nothing to eat. He said what the garden holds now isn't sickness. It's *everything else*. The orchards. The streets. The names. Twenty-two years of a country that the decree pretended out of existence, kept alive in the dark like a lamp under a blanket." He looked at her across the dead lamp. "Two stories, taster. One door. We open it and find out which one is true."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only one I've got."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell tolled the third hour, and beneath its note Yara heard — or imagined she heard — the faintest answering hum from the brass on the barrel, like a tuning fork struck somewhere underground. She picked the map up. The seam was still there. The vines had settled again into their frozen posture, innocent as furniture.

"Six weeks," she said. "Then we need three things. A door we can't find, in a country that doesn't exist, guarded by an Inquisitor General who wants us dead." She turned the map over in her hands, and the roundel gleamed, patient as a seed. "Tell me the rhyme again. All thirteen verses. Slowly."

Dain watched her for a long moment. Then, in a voice gone quiet and unwilling and absolutely steady, he began to sing — and Yara, royal food-taster, illegal charm-seller, keeper of a dead man's map, listened to a lullaby about moons and gardens and understood that somewhere in its counting was written the shape of everything they had to do.

By the fourth verse, she realized the rhyme wasn't counting moons at all.

It was counting keys.
