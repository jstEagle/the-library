## 6. The First Key Is a Face

The chamberlain's hands smelled of beeswax and old paper.

Yara had noticed this on her first day in the palace, three years ago, when Ovid Marren had taken her chin between two fingers and tilted her face to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone. *You'll do,* he'd said. *A taster with a steady tongue is worth ten guards with swords.* He had not smiled when he said it. He rarely smiled at anything. But his hands had been gentle, and they had smelled of beeswax — the same wax he used to seal the Queen's correspondence, the same wax that scented the long gallery outside her apartments where he spent most of his waking hours.

Now those hands were folded on the table before him, and Yara stood across from him with a silver spoon of plum conserve cooling against her palm, and she was thinking about how easy it would be to hate a man who smelled so gently of beeswax.

*The first key is a face,* the map had said. Or rather, the map had refused to say it outright — the map did nothing so helpful as speak — but after four nights of holding it over candleflame, of turning it under moonlight, of pressing her thumbnail into the brass until her fingers ached, the inscription had finally yielded its second line the way a wound yields pus: slowly, and with a smell of something rotten underneath.

*Three keys keep the garden's door,* the brass read now, letters surfacing like drowned bodies rising. *One in a song. One in a grave. And one in a face that forgot itself.*

Dain had gone pale when she'd shown him. Paler than the night they'd fought in the alley behind the tannery, paler even than the night the Watch burned the market and Marta had died with her sleeves on fire, shoving Yara through the drainage gate with the last strength in her ruined body.

"That's Ovid," Dain had said flatly. "That's the Queen's own chamberlain."

"You know him?"

"Everyone from the river districts knows the name Marren. It was a Vessani name before it was anything else." He'd turned the map toward the light, jaw tight. "My brother used to say the erasure didn't just burn books. It went into people. The Inquisitors had a rite for it — the Unremembering. They took the ones who knew too much to kill and scooped the country out of them like pulp out of a fruit. Left the rest standing." His voice had gone strange. "Left them useful."

Yara had thought of Ovid then — of thirty years of perfect service, of the chamberlain who never misspoke, never misplaced a document, never once in three years had asked her a single question about where she came from or what she'd been before the palace took her in. A man with no past was a man with no loose threads. She had admired that, once. She had thought it discipline.

Now she stood in his private tasting room with poison on her tongue's horizon and betrayal already dissolving in her mouth like sugar, and she understood that what she had admired was a scar.

"Whitlock." Ovid's voice pulled her back. "The conserve. Her Majesty is impatient this morning, and I am expected in the council chamber within the hour."

"Yes, my lord."

She raised the spoon. The conserve was dark red, almost black at the center, made from plums grown in the royal orchards south of the city — orchards that, according to every map in the palace archive, had always been there. Orchards that Dain swore, in a low furious voice, had been Vessani cherry fields before the decree, replanted root by root with something more obedient.

She let the conserve sit on her tongue. Ten seconds. Twenty. The taster's discipline: wait past comfort, wait past the flutter of fear, wait until the tongue either spoke or stayed silent. Around her the room waited too — the footman by the door, the under-steward pretending to inventory the sideboard, Ovid himself watching her with those mild gray eyes that held nothing, nothing, nothing.

Her tongue stayed silent. The conserve tasted of plums, of honey, of the faint bitter almond edge that was only the stones' ghost, harmless, familiar.

She swallowed. "It's clean, my lord."

"Good." He nodded to the footman, who bore the dish away toward the Queen's apartments. Then, as Yara turned to go: "Whitlock."

Her spine went cold. Three years of service had taught her that Ovid Marren saying a servant's name in that quiet tone was either a promotion or an ending.

"My lord?"

"You've been distracted lately." He said it without accusation, which was worse. "Since the fire in the night quarter. You flinched yesterday when the kitchen boy dropped a pan. Your hands were unsteady during Tuesday's wine service — unsteady enough that Master Corvel noticed, and Corvel notices nothing that doesn't concern his own wages." He rose from the table, smoothing his coat, and came around it slowly. "I don't ask my people about their private griefs. The palace is full of people running from something; that's why the palace works. Everyone here has somewhere else they'd rather be, and so everyone stays exactly in place." He stopped an arm's length from her. Close enough that she could smell the beeswax, and beneath it something older, fainter — woodsmoke, maybe, or the memory of woodsmoke. "But if your distraction endangers the Queen's body, I will have to care about it. Do you understand?"

"Perfectly, my lord."

He studied her a moment longer. And there — for half a heartbeat, no more — something moved behind the gray. Not recognition. Recognition would have meant he knew her secret, knew the charms, knew the map sewn into her bodice lining like a second heart. This was smaller and stranger: a flicker of almost-remembering, the way one almost remembers a dream upon waking, reaching for it and finding only the shape of the reaching.

Then it closed over, smooth as water, and he said, "Take the morning. Corvel can manage without you until the noon service," and left the room, and Yara stood alone among the silver and the porcelain with her heart hammering out a single thought, over and over, like a fist on a locked door:

*He almost remembered me. He looked at me and something in him almost remembered.*

Which meant the key was still in there. Buried, but breathing.

* * *

Getting close to Ovid Marren was, ironically, the easiest part.

The chamberlain kept habits the way other men kept grudges: absolutely, and for decades. He took his evening meal alone in the small study off the west gallery, at seven o'clock precisely, a tray brought up by whichever junior servant was lowest in the week's rotation. He drank one glass of elderflower cordial with it — never wine, though he tasted all the Queen's wines through Yara first. He read council papers until nine. At nine he walked the gallery once, end to end, twenty-two paces each way, and Yara had watched him do it often enough from the shadows of the service stair to know that his lips moved as he walked, though no sound came out.

Counting, she had thought, the first hundred times. Men who lived by precision counted things.

Praying, she thought now. Or remembering. Or trying to.

The problem was not access. The problem was that everything she intended to do with that access was a knife slid between the ribs of the only person in the palace who had ever treated her as more than a pair of careful hands.

She told herself the story of necessity while she waited for her chance. She told it again while she bribed the laundry maid with a charm against chilblains — harmless magic, the kind the Edict barely bothered to notice — to learn which nights Ovid sent his coats down early. She told it a third time, less convincingly, while she ground the herbs for a sleeping draught in the taster's workroom, where she was entitled to be, where every powder and measure was part of her office, where no one would think twice about the taster preparing a mild soporific because soporifics were among the few preparations a food-taster legitimately handled — doses for the Queen's sleepless nights, measured to the grain, logged in the book.

The log was the danger. Every draught she prepared was entered in Corvel's ledger, initialed, witnessed. So she would prepare nothing at all. She would take nothing from the stores. She would use only what grew wild along the canal path where she walked home — valerian, yes, but also the pale-flowered thing the night-market women called *lull's-bell*, which was not strictly an herb at all, which carried a whisper of the old craft in its roots, the kind of charm-adjacent botany that sat in the exact gray country the Edict pretended did not exist.

One cup. Mild as mother's milk. Enough to deepen his sleep, not enough to fog his waking mind — because that was the other thing, the thing she had learned from the map's third line, the line that had cost her two burned fingers to raise:

*The key wakes when the sleeper dreams true. Wake him gently, ask him kindly, and the door in his face will open.*

Ask him kindly. As if there were a kind way to do what she planned. As if the map cared about kindness, being brass, being old, being complicit in whatever had been done to Ovid Marren forty years ago when men in Inquisitor gray had scooped a country out of him and left him useful.

*You're no better than they were,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Marta, dead Marta, Marta with her burning sleeves. *They took his memories with rites and fire. You'll take them with lull's-bell and a soft voice. Same theft. Gentler hands.*

"And if I don't," Yara answered aloud, to the empty workroom, to the mortar and pestle, to nobody, "then Sarel finds him first. And Sarel doesn't ask kindly. Sarel cracks skulls open like walnuts and picks through what spills out."

The Inquisitor General had been asking questions. That much had reached even the shattered remnant of the night market — whispers passed between survivors, between the charm-sellers who had gone deeper underground, deeper into the cellars and cisterns beneath the city. *Sarel's pulled the death records from the river district. Sarel's got men asking after a dead man's family. Sarel knows the dead man had a brother.* Dain was a liability she couldn't shed and wouldn't, not anymore, not since the canal, not since he'd spoken his brother's name into her ear like an anchor-rope while the Lethe water tried to drink her whole. And if Sarel traced the dead man to the map, and the map to its keys, then Ovid Marren — born Ovid Marren of somewhere that officially had never existed — was already a dead man walking. The Inquisitors did not leave witnesses to their own rites.

So. One cup. One night. One question asked kindly.

She repeated it like a charm of her own, and hated herself a little more thoroughly each time.

* * *

The chance came on a Thursday, eleven days before the thirteenth moon.

Ovid dined alone as always — cold capon, bread, the elderflower cordial — and dismissed the tray-servant at half past seven, and read his papers, and at nine began his walk, twenty-two paces, turn, twenty-two paces back. Yara watched from the service stair through the gap where the door hinge had been quietly unscrewed by a kitchen boy who owed her for a fever-charm, and she waited for the moment she had timed across six nights of observation: the moment at the far end of the gallery, when the chamberlain paused at the window with its view of the dark gardens, and stood still for exactly as long as it took a man to look at something that wasn't there.

Tonight he stood longer than usual. Tonight his hand came up and pressed flat against the glass, fingers spread, the way a child presses a hand to a window against the cold. And his lips moved, and this time — this time, faintly, brokenly, like a song heard through a wall — sound came out.

Yara could not hear the words. But she knew the shape of them. She had spent years selling memory-charms to people who had lost things: mothers, homelands, faces of the dead, names of streets that had been renamed. She knew what the mouth looked like when it was trying to say a word that had been taken out of it. The lips formed it, and the tongue reached for it, and the word fell through a hole in the middle of the man and was gone.

Ovid Marren pressed his hand to the window of a palace built on a buried country, and tried to say a word that had been stolen out of his mouth, and failed, and turned away, and went to bed.

Yara gave him ten minutes. Then she came up the servants' stair with a covered cup in both hands and her heart in her throat, and knocked at the study door, and heard his tired voice say, "Enter."

"My lord. Forgive the hour." She kept her eyes lowered, the good servant's posture, spine curved into apology. "Corvel sent up a sleeping draught — you've had the shadow under your eyes this fortnight, and the Queen remarked on it at breakfast." A lie braided into a lie; the Queen had remarked on nothing; Corvel had sent nothing. But the lie was built of plausible timber, and Ovid was tired, and tired men accept cups.

He looked at her for a long moment. The lamplight caught the gray of his eyes, and again that flicker moved behind them — that almost — and Yara's skin prickled with the awful intimacy of it, the sense of standing in front of a locked door and feeling, through the wood, someone on the other side leaning their forehead against it.

"Whitlock," he said. "You have honest hands. That's rare here." He took the cup. "Most hands in this palace are holding something back."

"Yes, my lord," she said, and thought: *you have no idea*, and wanted to die.

He drank. Half the cup, then set it down, frowning faintly at it as if the taste had reminded him of something — lull's-bell did taste of something, a green sweetness like the inside of a fresh-cut stem, like sap, like spring in a place that had no spring anymore — and then he blinked, and yawned, and said, with the first genuine warmth she had ever heard from him, "Thank you, child. That will be all."

Child. He had called her child. And some traitorous part of her leaned into the word like a cat into a warm hand, and she hated herself for that too, and she said, "Goodnight, my lord," and withdrew, and did not leave.

She waited in the wardrobe passage off his bedchamber — a servant's trick, the passages ran behind every great house in the capital, built for eavesdropping stewards and discreet removal of scandal — and she listened to him undress, and lie down, and breathe. The lull's-bell worked quickly when it worked at all; within the half hour his breathing had gone deep and slow, deep and slow, and then changed rhythm entirely, catching and turning, the tide-shift of true dreaming.

Yara counted to a hundred. Twice. Then she slipped into the bedchamber.

Moonlight through the shutters lay across the bed in pale bars. Ovid Marren slept on his back, hands folded on his chest like an effigy, an old man carved in wax and laid out under a country's absence. Up close, in sleep, the smoothness of his face cracked at last: lines around the mouth, creases between the brows, the small topography of everything he had endured without knowing what it was. He looked, asleep, like a man who had been carrying furniture up stairs for forty years and had never once been allowed to put it down.

She knelt beside the bed. Her mouth was dry as ash.

"My lord," she whispered. "Ovid Marren. Can you hear me?"

His breath caught. Dreamers hear, sometimes. The map had promised as much: *the door opens inward, in the dark.*

"I'm going to ask you something," she said, barely above breath, "and I need you to answer in your sleep, and I am sorry — I am so sorry — for what I'm about to wake in you."

She leaned close. She asked the question the map had taught her, the words of the second inscription, the ones that had surfaced like bones:

*Where does Vessany begin?*

For a long moment, nothing. The bars of moonlight lay still. Somewhere below, a watchman's boots rang on flagstones, counting his rounds.

Then Ovid Marren's face changed.

It was not violence. It was worse than violence — it was *thawing*. Forty years of smooth gray ice going soft all at once, the whole frozen surface of him shifting and groaning, and his lips parted, and from somewhere beneath the erasure, from some sealed cellar of the man, his voice came out — younger than his waking voice, roughened with an accent she had never heard from him, a rounder vowelsound, a river-district lilt that belonged to no province on any current map —

"The border," the dreaming chamberlain said, "runs where the cherries stop growing. You can taste it. Mother said you could taste it in the jam — Vessani cherries go bitter at the pit, sweet at the skin, like a thing that knows it's ending—"

His hand shot up and seized Yara's wrist. Grip like iron, grip like drowning, and his eyes opened — open and utterly unfocused, seeing through her, seeing through the ceiling, seeing forty years backward at a country of orchards and hedges and a hedge with a door in it —

"—and the garden keeps score," he whispered, and Yara's blood went to ice, the dead man's words in a living man's mouth, "*the garden keeps score, and I signed the ledger, God forgive me, I wrote the decree's wording myself, I chose the words that unmade it, I chose* 'hereby dissolved' *because it sounded gentle—"*

"Ovid." Her voice shook. "Ovid, listen — the key. The key of memory. Where do you keep it? What did they leave in you?"

His eyes found hers. Focused, suddenly, terribly, on her face in the dark — and in them was not confusion but a lifetime of suppressed grief breaking its banks, and beneath the grief, unbelievably, a flicker of relief, the relief of a man who has waited forty years for someone to come and ask.

"They left my mother's face," he said. "They took the country and left her face. I've seen it every day for forty years and never known whose it was." His grip tightened. "You want the key, taster? The key is that I remember her face and cannot say her name. The key is that I helped erase a nation to save two daughters, and the Inquisitors erased my daughters anyway, three winters later, plague-carts, and I signed the condolence register in my own hand—"

He broke off. His chest heaved. And then, quieter, with the terrible calm of a man stepping off a ledge:

"You drugged me. I can taste the bell-flower. Clever girl. Careful girl." A ghost of something that was almost a smile. "Do you know how long I've waited for someone clever enough to steal back what I was too cowardly to reclaim?"

Yara could not speak.

"Come at dawn," Ovid Marren said, and his eyes were closing, the thaw refreezing, the dream pulling him back down. "The servants' door. Bring nothing written." His voice sank to a thread. "And Whitlock — when you find the garden — tell it the chamberlain is sorry. Tell it the wording was mine."

His hand slid from her wrist. His breathing deepened into ordinary sleep, and the smoothness closed over his face again like water over a drowned thing.

Yara knelt in the moonlight with her heart slamming and the taste of lull's-bell still sour in her own mouth, understanding that she had come to rob a man and had instead been given an appointment.

And understanding, too, with a cold creeping certainty that started at the base of her skull and spread downward, that the bedchamber door — which she had latched behind her — was now standing open.

Someone was in the doorway. Someone who had been listening.

The figure stepped into the moonlight, and the light picked out the gray-and-silver collar of the Inquisitorial service, and above it, calm and patient as a spider, the face of Inquisitor General Sarel.
