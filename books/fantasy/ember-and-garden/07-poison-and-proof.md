## 7. Poison and Proof

The morning of the tasting began like any other, which was to say it began with a lie.

Yara Whitlock knelt in the servants' corridor beneath the east wing with her sleeves rolled and her hands cupped around a chipped bowl of barley porridge, letting the steam climb into her face while the kitchen below her feet thundered with the ordinary violence of feeding a palace. Somewhere under that noise — under the chopping and the shouting and the hiss of fat on iron — was the small, patient sound she had trained herself to hear: the sound of something in the food that should not be there.

She could not have said when her tongue had become an instrument. It had happened the way a river cuts a canyon, so slowly and so inevitably that no single moment could be blamed. Ten years of swallowing death in small measured doses for the crown had remade her mouth into a lock and every meal into a key trying its teeth. Bitter almond sang at the back of the throat. Foxglove numbed the gums before it stopped the heart. Hemlock tasted of cold cellars and mouse droppings, and wolfsbane — wolfsbane was almost pretty, a green sweetness like a lie told by someone who loved you.

Today the porridge was only porridge. She ate three spoonfuls, waited out the count of two hundred heartbeats, and rose to dress for court.

The tasting itself would be held at noon in the Amber Room, before the Queen's midday meal, and it was not the Queen Yara feared for. The Queen ate behind walls of protocol thick enough to stop an army; no poisoner worth their salt bothered anymore. It was the chamberlain, Ovid Marren, who worried her — because Ovid took his meals standing, informally, from whatever dish was nearest, because he forgot to eat at all until hunger ambushed him, and because a man who moved through a palace like a ghost through a house made a very easy target.

And because, for six days now, Yara had been circling him like a moth around a lamp she both wanted and dreaded.

*The first key is a face,* the map had whispered to her in its stubborn, burning way — not in words, exactly, but in the way the brass grew warm against her palm whenever she thought of certain people, and coldest and most insistent of all when she stood near Chamberlain Ovid. *The border of Vessany lives in a mind that was made to forget it.* She had not needed the map to tell her whose mind. She had watched Ovid's face for years across the length of state dinners, had seen the strange blankness that came over it whenever anyone spoke of the western provinces, the way his eyes went smooth as a pond in winter. Everyone assumed it was boredom. Yara had sold enough memory-charms in the night market to know what forgetting looked like from the outside.

It looked like a man standing in a room with one door bricked over, who had been made to believe there had never been a door at all.

To reach his memories she would have to betray him. That was the arithmetic she could not make come out clean. He trusted her — not warmly, perhaps, but with the particular trust of a man who had watched her put poison in her own body a thousand times for the sake of his household. And she intended to pick the lock of his skull.

So when the summons came for the noon tasting, Yara dressed in her grey-and-silver livery and told herself that today she would only watch. Only taste. Only keep him alive one more day, and find another way tomorrow.

She was wrong by half past twelve.

* * *

The Amber Room smelled of beeswax and cut pears, and the light came through windows the color of strong tea, laying long amber bars across the table where the dishes were set out in their ceremonial order: cold meats, a pie of pigeon and prunes, a salad of bitter greens dressed with honey, cheese, small cakes dusted with cinnamon, wine in a decanter of green glass. Four tasters attended. Yara knew them all — old Marta with her shaking hands and iron stomach, the twins Pell and Perrin who treated the work as a game of nerve, and herself.

Chamberlain Ovid stood at the head of the table with his ledger open, making notes in his dry, precise hand, supervising the arrangement of a meal he would never sit down to enjoy. He was a tall man gone stooped at the shoulders, silver-haired, with a scholar's ink-stained fingers and a face that seemed built for kindness and then sanded down by forty years of service until only the bones of it remained. He did not look at Yara as she took her place. He rarely did. But he had once, years ago, quietly doubled her wage after her third year of service without being asked, and had said only, "You swallow more than your share," which was either a kindness or a warning, and she had never decided which.

"Begin," said the Master of Ceremonies, and the room went quiet except for the small sounds of forks.

Yara worked the table in order, as ritual demanded. Cold meat — nothing. Pie — nothing but too much pepper, someone's carelessness. Greens — she paused over the honey dressing, because honey was a classic vehicle, sweet enough to drown a hundred bitter things, but it was only honey, dark and floral, from the palace hives. Cheese. Cakes. Wine.

She lifted the green glass decanter and poured a measure into her tasting cup, and the smell reached her before the wine touched her lips.

Most people would have called it nothing. A faintness. A ghost of something vegetal, as if the decanter had once held herbs. But Yara's tongue had been cutting canyons for ten years, and she knew the smell of *gallowleaf* the way a shepherd knows the smell of coming snow — faint, green, wrong. Gallowleaf had no taste at all once dissolved; that was its genius and its horror. It announced itself only in scent, and only faintly, and only if you were looking. It killed quietly over hours, shutting down the breath a lung at a time, and it left a corpse that looked like a man who had simply, peacefully, forgotten how to live.

Her heart did not speed up. Ten years had taught it better. She set the cup down, poured again from the same decanter, and smelled it a second time, slower, cataloguing. Yes. Gallowleaf, steeped — steeped in the wine itself, which meant hours of access, which meant someone inside the household or very close to it.

The dose mattered. She swirled, sniffed, and let a single drop touch her tongue, holding it there, reading it the way a blind woman reads a page. Dilute. Very dilute. Not enough to fell a healthy man in one glass — but Ovid drank wine all day, glass after glass, forgetful, unguarded, a slow accumulation in a slow man's blood. By evening his breathing would begin to shallow. By midnight they would call it his heart. No one would ever look twice at a chamberlain of sixty-one years dying in his sleep.

Someone had designed this murder around Ovid's habits. Someone who watched him the way Yara did.

She set down the cup. Across the table, Marta was working the cakes, Pell the meats, and none of them had caught it — why would they? Their tongues were good; hers was a cathedral built on the ruins of theirs. The Master of Ceremonies was already gesturing the footmen forward to carry the wine through to the Queen's table, where Ovid would preside, where the glasses would be filled and refilled—

"The wine stays," Yara heard herself say.

The room turned. It was not done. Tasters did not speak during the ceremony; tasters were furniture that swallowed. Her voice sounded strange in her own ears, level and quiet, the voice she used in the night market when a customer tried to haggle down a charm that might save his life.

The Master of Ceremonies frowned. "Taster Whitlock?"

"There is gallowleaf in the decanter." She said it plainly, the way one reports a draught under a door. "Steeped. Dilute. Enough to kill a man who drinks steadily through the day, and the chamberlain drinks steadily through the day."

Silence, of the particular quality that follows an accusation inside a palace — everyone suddenly aware of everyone else, everyone's face carefully empty. Ovid had gone very still over his ledger. When he looked up at her, his eyes were the winter pond again, smooth and unreadable, and Yara thought: *he does not believe me, or he believes me and is afraid, and I cannot tell which, and I have just made myself the most interesting person in this room.*

"You are certain," the Master of Ceremonies said. It was not quite a question.

"I am certain enough to drink the whole decanter if I'm wrong."

That got a reaction — a ripple, a flicker of grim appreciation from old Marta. The mathematics of the offer were understood by everyone present. If she was wrong, she died of nothing, and embarrassed herself. If she was right, she died of gallowleaf, slowly, over an afternoon. Either way she staked her life on her tongue, which was, when it came down to it, the only currency a royal taster had ever truly possessed.

Ovid closed his ledger. "No one will drink the wine," he said. His voice was dry as his handwriting. "Seal the decanter. Send for the apothecary and the Master of Keys — I want the cellar passage examined, and the names of everyone with access to this room since dawn." Then, to Yara, in a tone stripped of everything: "You will remain. We will discuss your certainty."

* * *

They discussed it for two hours in a small room off the pantry, over the sealed decanter between them like a prisoner brought to interrogation.

The apothecary confirmed it by sundown — gallowleaf, steeped, expertly masked, the work of someone who knew both the herb and the chamberlain's habits. The Master of Keys found that the cellar passage door had been oiled recently, its lock picked without force, by someone with skill or a copied key. Three servants had vanished from their posts by nightfall, which was as good as a confession written in absence, though it proved nothing about who had paid them.

And Ovid Marren sat across the table from Yara and asked her, again and again, in his dry unhurried way, how she had known.

"Scent," she said. "Barely there. Most wouldn't catch it."

"Most," he agreed. "But you are not most. Ten years I have watched you eat death at my Queen's table." He turned the decanter slowly, watching the lamplight move through the poisoned wine. "Do you know what I thought, the first time I saw you taste? That here was a young woman who had made a profession of trusting no one — not the cooks, not the court, not even her own senses — and surviving anyway." He looked up. "I have wondered for years what a person must be, to live like that."

"Afraid," Yara said. "A person must be afraid, and tired, and unwilling to die stupidly."

Something moved behind the winter-smooth eyes — a flicker, quickly buried, like a fish under ice. "Yes," he said softly. "I imagine it is."

He dismissed her then, with thanks formal enough to be a wall, and Yara walked back through the evening corridors feeling the day settle onto her shoulders like wet wool. She had saved him. Justice had been done — quietly, without tribunal or spectacle, the only kind of justice the palace permitted: a poisoner foiled, three runaways hunted, a decanter sealed as evidence. Outside any court, as all her truest work had always been done.

And still the map burned faintly against her ribs where she had hidden it in her bodice, warm as a held coin, insistent as a second heartbeat. *Him. You know it's him.*

She had saved his life. Surely that purchased something. Surely a debt that large could be spent.

* * *

She found him at dusk in the chamberlain's office, a narrow room stacked with ledgers and smelling of ink and cold tea, and she did not knock so much as arrive, because courage was a thing that spoiled if left waiting.

"My lord chamberlain. I need to ask you something improper."

Ovid set down his pen. For a long moment he simply regarded her, and she watched him decide — she could see the decision happening, the scales moving behind his face — whether to be the official or the man. "You saved my life today," he said at last. "I suppose improper questions are the traditional payment." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Ask."

Yara sat. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady. Ten years of swallowing poison had at least taught them that.

"There is a country," she said, "that no one remembers. West of the Lethe, past the salt marshes. It had a name. There are people alive in this palace who were born there, who helped erase it, who walk past its name every day and feel nothing, because the feeling was taken from them along with everything else." She watched his face with a taster's attention, reading it drop by drop. "I think you are one of them."

The silence stretched so long that somewhere in the palace a clock struck the hour and finished striking, and neither of them moved.

When Ovid finally spoke, his voice was not dry at all. It was cracked down the middle, like a plate that has held together for years and chooses, without warning, this moment to fail.

"Get out of my office."

"My lord—"

"*Get out.*" He was on his feet, both palms flat on the desk, and he was trembling — not with rage, Yara realized, but with something far worse. With recognition. With the terrible vertigo of a man who has just been told there is a door in a wall he has lived beside all his life. "How do you— who told you— *what did you hear?*" And then, quieter, in a voice like ash: "If the Inquisitor General learns that I—"

"She already suspects me," Yara said. "Not you. Me. Which means I am the safer confessor of the two of us." She leaned forward. "My lord. I held a dying man in my arms six nights ago. He gave me something that leads to that country — to the garden that kept it safe, kept *everything* safe, every street and song and grave. I can't open it alone. The map wants a key, and the key is a memory, and the memory is in your head, behind a wall someone built to save your family." She let that land — watched him flinch at *your family*, confirmation arriving like a knife between the ribs. "I am not asking you to remember for yourself. Though God knows you've earned the right. I'm asking you to remember for everyone who can't."

Ovid sank back into his chair. He looked, suddenly, sixty years old and older, a man holding up a roof with his spine. His hand went to his breast pocket — an unconscious gesture, protective, the way a man touches a locket or a letter — and Yara saw it, and filed it away, and said nothing.

"If I tell you what I know," he said finally, "you will understand why it was erased. Why decent people — *I* — helped bury a whole country alive." He laughed, a small broken sound. "You will look at me the way Sarel looks at charm-sellers."

"I look at charm-sellers in a mirror every night," Yara said. "Try me."

For a long time the chamberlain stared at the ledger open on his desk — columns of numbers, supplies, wages, the small honest arithmetic of a hidden life. Then he reached into his breast pocket and drew out a key. Not brass, not iron: bone, yellowed, carved with a script Yara almost recognized, and warm from living so long against his heart.

"Lock the door," Ovid Marren said, and his voice was the voice of a boy from a country that no longer existed. "What I have to tell you begins with a plague ship, and ends with me writing my own home out of the world."
