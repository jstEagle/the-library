## 8. The Chamberlain's Country

The dream always began the same way: with the smell of bread.

Not palace bread — not the pale, obedient loaves that came up from the royal ovens each dawn, weighed and tasted and signed for — but the dark, seed-crusted rounds of home, split open while they still steamed and dragged through honey so thick it caught the light like amber. In the dream Ovid Marren was a boy again, barefoot on warm stone, and his mother's voice came through the window of their house above the dyer's lane, calling him in from the terraces where the ember-flowers burned orange against the dusk.

*Vessany,* said the voice in the dream, in the voice of the dream, which was also his own voice, which was also the voice he had been forbidden to use for thirty-one years. *You are from Vessany.*

He woke, as he always woke, with the taste of ash in his mouth.

Chamberlain Ovid sat up in his narrow bed in the east wing of the palace, in the room assigned to a man whose duties required him to be reachable at all hours, and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the colors behind them stopped being flowers and became only pain. Outside his window the capital lay quiet under a waning moon, its lamps trimmed low by Edict-curfew, its streets swept clean of everything the crown had decided did not exist.

Thirty-one years. He counted them the way other men counted rosaries. Thirty-one years since the Commission on Public Health had convened in this very palace, since he — a junior clerk with a Vessani accent he'd already begun to file down like a rough edge on a doorframe — had been given the task of drafting the language that would unmake a country. Not destroy it. The plague had done the destroying, or so they told themselves, though even then Ovid had suspected the truth was less tidy. No: *erase*. Strike the name from every ledger, every map, every gravestone. Redistribute the survivors' papers. Forbid the tongue. Let the roads grow over and rename the crossings after generals. A country does not die when its people die, the old Commissioner had said, tapping the table with one soft finger; a country dies when no one can say it lived.

Ovid had written the decree. He had been twenty-three years old, brilliant, ambitious, and terrified, because his mother and his sisters were still inside the quarantine lines when the first drafts were drawn, and the Commissioner had made it plain — never in words, never in anything that could be repeated — that the families of cooperative clerks would be moved to safety, and the families of others would not.

So he had written. He had written beautifully. That was the worst of it, the thing he carried under his ribs like a swallowed stone: not that he had betrayed Vessany, but that he had betrayed it *well*, in prose so clean and reasonable that the Council adopted it without amendment. *In the interest of public health and the stability of the realm, let it be known that no territory designated Vessany has ever existed within these borders.* He could still recite it. It lived in him the way a splinter lives in flesh, worked deep past the point where any instrument could reach it.

His mother had survived. She had come to the capital with her two daughters and her dyed cloths and her lullabies sung only in whispers behind locked shutters, and she had died eight years ago in a rented room, and at her burial — an unmarked grave, because marked graves invited questions — Ovid had stood in the rain and found that he could not weep. Grief, he understood now, requires a name to fasten to. He had spent thirty-one years teaching himself not to name things.

He rose, dressed, and went down to work.

* * *

The girl was waiting for him in the antechamber, as she had been waiting every morning for six years.

Yara Whitlock, taster to the Queen's household. Small, quick-eyed, with the kind of stillness that most people mistook for servility and which Ovid, who had spent a lifetime studying stillness in mirrors, recognized as something else entirely. She stood beside the breakfast tray the way a soldier stands beside a wall — aware of it, using it, ready for it to become cover.

"Chamberlain." She inclined her head.

"Yara." He settled into his chair and drew the tray toward himself out of long habit before remembering that the tasting was her office, not his. "What is it this morning? More of those gray eggs the kitchen insists are fashionable?"

"Pheasant, my lord. And a pear compote the cook swears by." Her voice was level, pleasant, nothing at all. But as she lifted the little silver spoon, something flickered across her face — hesitation, or calculation — and Ovid felt the old clerk's instinct stir in him, the instinct that had kept him alive through three reigns: *notice what people almost do.*

She tasted the pheasant. Swallowed. Waited the prescribed interval, lips slightly parted, reading her own body the way sailors read water. Then the compote. Then a sip of the spiced wine.

"All sound," she said.

"You're certain about the wine? It smells sharper than usual."

"A new shipment of cloves. I checked with the cellar master myself." Again that flicker. Again the almost-something.

Ovid ate his breakfast slowly and watched her gather the tray, and thought: *she is carrying something heavier than spoons.* But he said nothing. He had built thirty-one years on saying nothing, and the habit held even now, even with whatever small crisis was standing quietly in front of him wearing an apron.

"Thank you, Yara. You may go."

She paused at the door. Turned halfway back. For one absurd moment he thought she might actually speak — might say whatever it was pressing against her teeth — and something in him leaned forward hungrily to receive it.

"My lord," she said instead, "you dropped your pen."

It was true; his pen lay on the carpet where it had rolled. He thanked her, she retrieved it, set it precisely parallel to his inkwell, and left. Ovid sat alone in the morning light, holding the pen without writing, and wondered why his hands had begun, lately, to tremble.

They had begun trembling the night the stranger died. He knew that much. Word traveled strangely in a palace — sideways, through kitchens and stables and the mouths of laundresses — and by the second evening everyone below stairs knew that a man had bled out at a charm-stall in the night market, whispering nonsense about gardens. Everyone below stairs also knew that the Inquisitor General's men had begun asking questions, and that the questions had a particular flavor: not *who killed him* but *what did he leave behind.*

And Ovid, hearing all of it through the walls he had spent decades becoming, had felt the splinter shift in his flesh, and his hands had trembled ever since.

* * *

That afternoon, the Queen requested him.

Her Majesty received him in the small library, alone but for her lady-in-waiting, which meant the conversation was already private enough to be dangerous. She was older than the Edict — old enough, Ovid sometimes thought, to remember what the world had looked like before the erasure, if anyone had dared let her remember it.

"Ovid." She turned a page she had clearly not been reading. "There are rumors of unrest among the charm-sellers. The Inquisitor General tells me the trade is growing again, despite our efforts."

"The trade never stopped, Your Majesty," Ovid said carefully. "One may suppress a market. One cannot suppress wanting."

"No." The Queen regarded him with eyes the color of wet slate. "One cannot. Tell me, Chamberlain — you were a young man when the sickness came. Do you recall the territories affected?"

Here it was. Here, at last, after thirty-one years of safe silence, the question arriving not from some Inquisitor's cold mouth but from his sovereign's weary one, and Ovid felt the stone under his ribs turn over.

"I recall," he said, "that there was sickness. That many suffered. That the Crown acted decisively."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is the only answer I have, Majesty."

Something moved behind the slate eyes — disappointment, perhaps, or pity. "Very well. Thank you, Ovid. Send word when the accounts are reconciled."

He bowed and withdrew, walking back through the corridors with his spine straight and his heart hammering like a fist on a locked door. *Do you recall the territories affected.* She had asked him directly. She had given him the opening a braver man would have walked through, and he had stood aside from it as he had stood aside from everything, all his life, keeping the door shut, keeping the country dead, keeping himself safe.

In his own chambers that evening he poured a measure of plum brandy he was technically responsible for inventorying and drank it standing at the window, watching the curfew lamps, and the stone under his ribs grew so heavy that he had to grip the sill.

That was when the memories came.

* * *

They did not arrive gently. Suppression, he learned in that terrible hour, is not deletion; it is only weight, and weight will hold until it won't.

It began with the smell of bread again, rising in his own room, in a palace where no such bread had baked in three decades. Then the smell of dye-vats and river water. Then his mother's voice, no longer muffled by thirty years of careful forgetting but suddenly, horribly *present*, singing the lullaby — the one with the strange descending phrases that had never sounded like other lullabies, that had sounded, he realized now with a lurch like falling, like a *sequence*, like something meant to be remembered in order —

He gripped the windowsill. The city outside blurred. And the wall he had built came down all at once, the way a dam comes down: not gradually but catastrophically, drowning him in everything he had held back.

Vessany. Terraced hillsides planted with ember-flowers whose petals glowed faintly at dusk, so that whole valleys seemed banked coals. The market at Lowbridge where you could buy memory-charms openly, legally, blessed by the hedgerow-priests who tended the great garden at the country's heart — the garden the old songs called the Ember Garden, the living archive, the place where Vessany kept itself, every birth and harvest and grief recorded in living wood. His father had taken him once, a boy of seven, and he had pressed his palm to the gate-post and felt — he had *felt* — generations looking back at him. Warmth. Attention. A country noticing its own child.

Then the sickness. The fever that burned through the lowland towns in a single season. And the truth he had buried deepest of all, deeper than the garden, deeper than his mother's grave: the sickness had not been natural. It had begun in the garden's border-wards, in the hedge-magic that the crown's own surveyors had tampered with two years prior, mapping and measuring what should never have been measured, and when the fever came the King's physicians had needed someone to blame, and the garden was right there, ancient and strange and convenient.

*Erase the magic, erase the blame. Erase the country, erase the debt.*

And the Commissioner, tapping his soft finger on the table: *We'll need good clerks. Discreet ones. Men with... flexible attachments.*

Flexible attachments. His mother. His sisters. Himself, aged twenty-three, writing the death warrant of everything warm in his childhood in exchange for three lives smuggled out ahead of the Watch. He had told himself it was arithmetic. Three saved for one country lost — and hadn't the country been dying anyway? Hadn't the plague done the killing? He had told himself that for thirty-one years, and tonight, drowning in returned memory, he finally heard how thin the telling had always sounded.

Because it wasn't just the decree. It was the *after*. It was the lists he had compiled of Vessani names for redistribution. It was the testimony he had coached survivors to give — *I was born in the Eastern Reaches; there has never been another name for it* — sitting across from neighbors and kinsmen, meeting their eyes, teaching them to lie themselves out of existence. It was Mrs. Halloran from the dyer's lane, who had held him as a baby, looking at him across the Commission table with dawning horror as he explained, kindly, reasonably, in beautiful prose, why her childhood had never happened.

He did not remember crossing the room. He found himself on his knees on the carpet, an old man in a fine coat, sobbing into his own shaking hands, saying her name — *Halloran, Bessa Halloran* — aloud, in Vessani, the first time the language had passed his lips in thirty-one years, and it came out unbroken, whole, fluent, the way a drowned man comes up breathing.

* * *

Someone knocked at his door.

He did not answer. The knock came again, soft, patient. And then a voice, pitched low:

"Chamberlain. It's Yara. Please. I know you're awake — your lamp's lit, and I've seen you take your brandy from the third shelf for six years. I need to speak with you. About Vessany."

The name struck him like cold water. He hauled himself upright, wiped his face with the sleeve of a chamberlain's immaculate coat, and opened the door.

She stood in the corridor with a candle, and she was not alone — a broad-shouldered man lingered at the stair-shadow's edge, watchful, hard-mouthed, with the look of someone who had recently been hit and expected to be again. But it was the girl Ovid looked at, the girl whose face wore the expression he knew intimately from thirty years of mirrors: the look of someone carrying more than they can set down.

"You said the name," he managed.

"Yes, my lord."

"In the palace. Where the walls are paper and the Inquisitor General pays for whispers by the word."

"I know." She met his eyes steadily. "I've been trying for a week to find a way to ask you without saying it. There isn't one anymore. May we come in?"

He stood aside. It occurred to him, distantly, that this was the first time in thirty-one years he had stepped away from a door rather than holding it shut.

Inside, the man introduced himself as Dain — Dain Ferro, brother to the man who had died at the charm-stall — and produced from his coat a brass case that made Ovid's breath stop in his throat. He knew that casing. He had seen it last in the Commission archives, catalogued among the effects seized from the garden's keepers: a surveyor's map-case, Vessani-made, keyed to open only for what the makers called *keys of memory* — fragments of the country's record, scattered into human minds when the hedge was sealed, so that the garden's heart could never be wholly extinguished. The Commission had known about them. Had hunted them, in the early years, with a thoroughness Ovid himself had helped organize.

"You know what this is," the Ferro man said, watching his face.

"I helped write the order that confiscated them," Ovid said. His voice came out flat and strange. "Or I wrote the words that became the order. There is very little difference, in the end, between a clerk and a knife. Someone else holds you."

Yara sat forward. "Then you know what it needs. Three keys. We believe the first—"

"—lives in me." He laughed, and it was not a pleasant sound. It came out cracked down the middle. "Yes. It surfaced tonight. All of it. The hills. My mother's lullaby. The garden gate." He looked at his hands. They had stopped trembling, now that it was too late for trembling to help anything. "I was born in Vessany, Yara Whitlock. In a house above the dyer's lane, in a town called Sennet Ford. And when my country needed mourners, I volunteered to be its gravedigger, because the gravedigger's family got to live."

Silence. The candle flame bent and straightened.

"The key," Dain said roughly. "Whatever's in your head. Can you give it?"

Ovid rose. From behind the false panel of his desk — a panel installed by a predecessor, useful to a man who kept certain accounts private — he took a small object wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it with fingers that had finally gone steady. Inside lay a sliver of dark wood, veined with faintly glowing grain, warm to the touch: a cutting from the garden itself, preserved these thirty-one years in a chamberlain's drawer, hidden in the one place no Inquisitor would think to search because no servant of the crown would dare keep such a thing.

"I took it the day the hedge was sealed," he said. "I told myself it was evidence. I told myself a great many things." He held it out to Yara. "This is mine to carry. The key in my head is yours. Both of them — take both. I have been the keeper of a dead country's shame for thirty-one years, and I find tonight that I would rather be keeper of its memory."

Yara took the cutting. As her fingers closed around it, the wood brightened — softly, like a coal remembering fire — and Ovid felt something loosen in his chest, something that had been clenched since he was twenty-three years old, and to his astonishment his eyes filled with tears that were, for the first time in three decades, grief with a name attached.

"Bessa Halloran," he whispered. "Sennet Ford. Ember Garden. Say them back to me. Please. I need to hear someone say them back."

Yara looked at him — at his ruined, respectable face — and said, gently, in flawless Vessani: *"Ember Garden. Sennet Ford. We remember."*

Outside, far off across the sleeping capital, a bell began to ring — not the curfew bell, but the sharp triple-stroke of the Watch alarm — and Dain was on his feet at the window in an instant, swearing softly.

"Fire," he said. "The night market's burning."
