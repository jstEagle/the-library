## 9. Inquisitor's Net

The candles in the Inquisitor General's office were tallow, not wax. Sarel had ordered it so herself, years ago, the day she took the post. Wax burned too sweetly; sweetness invited sleep, and sleep invited dreams, and dreams — in this building above all buildings in the capital — were not to be trusted.

She sat now with her hands folded on a desk of bare black oak, and she listened to the man across from her fail.

"Again," she said.

Corporal Vess swallowed. He was young, pink-cheeked, promoted too fast because the Watch could no longer afford to be choosy about competence. "We — we searched the night market, General. The whole of it. Every stall with a license and half the ones without."

"And?"

"And there's no charm-seller by the name anyone gave us. No Whitlock. There was a woman selling luck-knots two rows down who might have—"

"Might." Sarel let the word sit between them like a stone dropped into still water. She watched the ripples move through the corporal's face: the flush of embarrassment, the flicker of fear, the small animal wish to be anywhere else. She had spent twenty years learning to read faces the way scholars read dead languages, and this one said *I saw her, I think, once, and I did nothing.*

"You might," she said gently, "have arrested her."

"She wasn't doing anything illegal, General. Luck-knots are just — cord. Everyone knows they're just cord."

"Everyone knows," Sarel agreed. "That is rather the point of everything we do, Corporal. That everyone should know." She unfolded her hands and reached for the ledger at her elbow — a slim volume bound in grey leather, unmarked, its pages soft as cloth from handling. "Tell me about the fire."

"The market? Started near the tallow-chandler's row. Accidental, most likely, though we're still—"

"It was not accidental." She turned a page. The handwriting inside was her own, small and exact, each entry dated to the hour. "It was set by three men wearing Watch surcoats over plainclothes, on my order, to flush a single person out of a warren of stalls. And your men found ash, Corporal, and one body pulled from the smoke — an old woman, a lamp-seller, whom witnesses say turned back into the burning row when everyone else was running out of it." She looked up. "Why would a lamp-seller run *into* a fire?"

Vess's mouth opened. Closed.

"Because someone was still inside," Sarel said. "And that someone is the reason you are standing in my office instead of sleeping off your wine somewhere. Sit down."

He sat.

* * *

After he had gone — dismissed with a list of names to find and questions never to ask aloud — Sarel remained at her desk and permitted herself, for exactly the length of time it took a candle to gutter and relight, to close her eyes.

This was the discipline. Not the fasting, not the cold baths, not the recitation of the Edict at dawn and dusk. The discipline was this: to open the door in her own mind for a measured moment each day, look at what stood behind it, and close it again before it could look back.

She had been nine years old when the soldiers came to her grandmother's house in the river country. She remembered — no. She did not remember. There was a shape where the memory should have been, the way there is a shape where a tooth has been pulled: the tongue returns to it obsessively, mapping the absence. A kitchen. A smell of something baking. Her grandmother's voice saying words that had been scooped out of the air like seeds from a fruit. Sarel had grown up in a state orphanage under a new name, taught the Edict as holy writ, and she had believed it so completely, so gratefully, that by sixteen she had volunteered for the Inquisition herself — the girl most eager to burn the very thing that had been burned out of her.

Mercy, she called it. Mercy was the word the first Inquisitors had chosen, and it was a good word, and she had made it truer than they ever managed to. Because pain, she had learned, lived in memory, and if you cut the memory cleanly enough the pain went with it. She had watched condemned witches beg for the Rite of Forgetting rather than the rope. She had watched plague-touched villages kneel in gratitude as the grey-cloaks walked the lanes, lifting the sickness *and the years that carried it* out of people's heads like splinters from a wound. The country that had to be erased — that festering, magic-rotted land whose name she was paid never to speak — had been full of suffering, and suffering needed a past to root in. Take the past, take the root. What remained was peace. She had seen it. She had built her life on having seen it.

So why, then, did the shape behind the door in her mind smell of baking bread?

Sarel opened her eyes and lit a fresh candle from the stub of the old one, and the moment passed, as it always passed, and she was again the most dangerous woman in the capital.

* * *

The reports came in through the afternoon, and she read them the way a surgeon reads a body: unsentimentally, looking for the wound.

A food-taster named Yara Whitlock. Sworn to the royal kitchens six years, clean record, praised twice by the Master of Tasters for a palate "fine enough to taste intent." That phrase had snagged in Sarel's mind when the file first crossed her desk, weeks ago, over some trivial matter of a poisoned hound in a nobleman's kennels. *Fine enough to taste intent.* She had marked the name then without knowing why. She marked things the way spiders mark vibrations — not deciding, merely noting.

Now the web trembled everywhere at once.

Item: a man had died at the night market three nights past, bled out from a knife-wound, and died at — or beside, the witnesses disagreed — a charm-stall that no longer existed. Item: the dead man's effects included no map, no papers, nothing of value, which was itself valuable information, because a man does not cross half the country bleeding slowly to death carrying nothing. Item: the chamberlain Ovid, that grey and punctual servant of the crown, had requested access to his own sealed records — a request so unprecedented that the Archivists had sent it up the chain as a curiosity, and it had landed on Sarel's desk like a fish dropped from the sky. Item: the night market had burned, and in the ash of it, a lamp-seller lay dead with her arms around a trapdoor that opened onto empty cellar tunnels running west toward the river country.

West. Toward the river country. Toward the border that was not a border, toward the fields that were not fields, toward the place where the maps of the realm simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the tongue has been cut out.

Sarel rose and went to the window. Below, the palace gardens lay in their formal order — clipped yew, raked gravel, fountains that sang on the hour. And beyond them, past the wall, the wilder ground where the old hedge ran, the hedge the gardeners were forever complaining about, the hedge that grew too fast and in wrong directions and could not be killed no matter how often it was cut back to the root.

She had ordered it cut back eleven times in her tenure. It had never once seemed smaller.

*You know more than any servant of the crown should,* she thought, and did not know whether the sentence was addressed to the taster or to herself.

* * *

In the lower vaults of the Inquisitory, past the interrogation rooms with their instruments of honest conversation, there was a room with no instruments at all. It held a table, two chairs, and a lantern, and it was the room Sarel used when she wanted the truth rather than the confession. Fear produced confessions. Truth required something subtler: comfort, surprise, and time.

She waited in it now, alone, reviewing the last piece of the net.

The taster had saved the chamberlain's life. That much was certain — the poison in the honeyed pears had been real, sophisticated, aimed with a professional's precision at one man's known weakness for them, and only a palate of extraordinary refinement had caught it in time. Sarel had read the kitchen transcripts four times. What interested her was not the saving but the aftermath: within a day, the chamberlain had petitioned for his sealed records; within two, the taster Whitlock had vanished from her post, her lodging, her entire legible existence, as thoroughly as if she had stepped off the edge of a map.

People did not vanish in the capital. People vanished in the river country, in the erased places, where the roads forgot themselves. For a palace servant to vanish here meant either flight — or passage. And passage required a door.

There was only one door.

Sarel had inherited, with her office, a single document older than the Edict itself: a page of interrogations conducted in the final year of the plague, when the last king of the dying land had bargained for his people's survival. The terms were recorded in cipher, but Sarel had broken ciphers since she was nineteen, and the substance was simple. The land could not be cured, so it would be *kept* — every street, every orchard, every living soul's memory pressed into a single living vessel, a garden behind a door, tended by a keeper, sealed until such time as the world could bear to remember. And the price of the sealing was written in a line the old king himself had dictated, weeping, to the scribes:

*What is forgotten cannot be grieved; what cannot be grieved cannot return.*

Erasure as mercy. Erasure as quarantine. Erasure as the only surgery deep enough to save the patient's neighbors. Sarel had built her faith on that line the way other women built faith on scripture. For twenty years it had held her up. For twenty years, when the shape behind the door in her mind stirred, she had pressed the line against it like a hand against a wound, and it had worked, it had always worked —

Until a food-taster with a merchant's clever face had begun pulling threads, and the whole garment of the world had started, very quietly, to unravel.

Because here was what the archives also said, in a marginal note in a dead archivist's hand, a note Sarel had never shown another living soul: the garden kept score. The garden remembered *everything*, including the forgetting. And a keeper who opened the door would not release a cure, or a plague, or a paradise. A keeper would release the grief. All of it, all at once, into every mind that had ever been spared it — including, the note added, in ink gone brown with age, *the minds of those who wielded the knife.*

Sarel had told herself for years that she feared nothing. Standing alone in the lantern-lit room, she admitted, in the strict privacy of her own iron discipline, that this was the thing she feared: not the garden, not the magic, not the taster. The grief. Her own, coming home to roost after twenty fat and peaceful years, with interest.

Then she straightened her spine, because feeling it and permitting it were different countries entirely, and she had spent her whole life patrolling the border between them.

* * *

They brought the taster in just past midnight.

Not arrested — Sarel had been precise about that. Escorted. Two of her own grey-cloaks, plainclothed, flanking a woman in a servant's cloak with ash still ghosting the seams of it, walking on her own feet with her chin level and her eyes moving constantly, cataloguing exits, counting guards, weighing knives. Good, Sarel thought. A fighter. Fighters were easier than believers; fighters could be given something to fight *for*.

Yara Whitlock was smaller than her file photograph suggested and steadier than any frightened woman had a right to be. She sat when offered the chair, folded her hands almost exactly as Sarel folded hers, and said, "General. To what do I owe the honor?"

"No honor," Sarel said. "Tea?"

"I'd rather taste it first."

Despite herself — despite twenty years of discipline — Sarel felt the corner of her mouth move. She poured from the pot on the table, watched the woman watch her pour, and set the cup down untouched between them. "Clever. But unnecessary. If I wanted you dead or emptied, you would already be both, and we would not be wasting good tea."

"Forgive me if I don't find that comforting."

"Comfort isn't my trade." Sarel sat, laced her fingers, and studied her across the lantern light. Up close the taster's eyes were remarkable — quick, warm even now, the eyes of someone who had spent a lifetime noticing what others missed. Dangerous eyes. Honest-seeming ones. The worst kind. "Your turn to be clever, Mistress Whitlock. Tell me why a woman of your talents — a *royal* appointment, a roof, a wage, safety — walks away from all of it in a single week, following a dead man's trail west."

"I don't know what you mean. I took leave. My mother's ill."

"Your mother died when you were seven. Workhouse record." Sarel let that land, watched it land, watched the woman absorb the blow without swaying. "Try again."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, the palace clock began striking twelve, and Sarel counted the strokes with the patience of a fisherman counting the pull of a line.

"You were at the night market," Sarel said, when the last stroke faded. "The night it burned. You lost your stall. You lost — I'm told — a friend. An old woman who sold lamps and knew more about doors than any lamp-seller should." She leaned forward slightly. "I am not your enemy, Mistress Whitlock. I want you to believe that, because it happens to be true, and because you will need to believe it before this is over. Whatever you think you've found — whatever a dying stranger put into your hands — you do not understand what it is. You see a treasure. I have read the original documents. I have read the terms of the sealing, in the hand of the last king of a country whose name you have never been permitted to learn. And I am telling you, as one woman of ordinary birth to another: what sleeps behind that door must go on sleeping. Not for the crown's sake. For yours. For everyone's."

Something moved behind the taster's eyes — not fear. Recognition, maybe. Pity, possibly, which was worse.

"You've read the documents," Yara said quietly. "All of them?"

"All that matter."

"Then you've read what the garden keeps." The woman's voice did not rise at all; it lowered, gentle as a hand on a fevered brow, and somehow that was more frightening than shouting. "Everything. Every street. Every orchard. Every name. Including—" she paused, and her gaze, unbearably, softened, "—including the things that were taken out of people. They don't stop existing, General. They're *stored*. Kept safe. Waiting."

The lantern flame bent, though there was no draft.

And Sarel felt it then, the shape behind the door in her mind pressing its palms flat against the wood from the other side, and beneath the terror, treacherous and warm as a hearth, a single unbearable image: a kitchen. Bread. A voice, singing, in a language she had been forbidden to know she knew.

She closed the door. She stood. Her hands did not shake; she would have executed them if they had.

"Thank you for the tea," said Yara Whitlock, rising also, unhurried. "You didn't arrest me. That was kind. Or confident."

"It was neither. It was an invitation." Sarel walked her to the vault door and signaled the guards beyond it. "You have until the next new moon to bring me the map, Mistress Whitlock. After that, I stop inviting, and the net closes — and understand me, it is already woven. Your chamberlain. your brother-in-grief, your seed-vault friends, your burned market, your western road. Every thread ends in this room." She held the woman's gaze a moment longer, and made her voice, deliberately, almost gentle. "Bring it to me, and I will protect you from what's coming better than it can protect itself. Believe that erasure was mercy. It is the only mercy left that works."

Yara Whitlock looked at her for a long moment with those quick, warm, grieving eyes, and said, softly, "Who did you lose, General? Who was it that baked the bread?"

Sarel did not answer. Sarel did not move until the footsteps had faded up the stair and the door had booted shut and she was alone with the lantern and the cooling tea.

Then she sat back down at the table, and took out a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote the orders that would put grey-cloaks on every road west, watchers on every gate, and a detail of twelve men in the palace gardens — with axes — at the old hedge.

Her hand, she noted distantly, was perfectly steady.

Only the word *hedge* came out slightly crooked, as if the pen had struck a root.
