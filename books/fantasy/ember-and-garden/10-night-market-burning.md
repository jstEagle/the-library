## 10. Night Market Burning

The night market smelled of tallow and wet stone, of fried dough and river mud, of the particular sweetness that rose from the charm-sellers' row when the moon came up — a smell like crushed mint and hot copper, the scent of small magics waking. Yara had known it since she was nine years old, when her mother first set her down on an overturned crate beside a stall of luck-knots and told her, *watch the hands, not the faces*. She knew it now the way she knew the taste of arsenic: instantly, intimately, as something that lived in her body.

She was laying out her stock when the first torch went up at the far end of Tanner's Row.

Not a vendor's lantern. Not the fat yellow globes of oil-glass that hung over the fishmongers' stretch. This was a soldier's torch, pitch-soaked and thrown, and it landed in the awning of Old Sefa's candle stall with a sound like a struck drum, and the awning went up all at once, greedy and bright.

For three full heartbeats the market did nothing. That was the terrible thing about crowds, Yara thought later; they always waited those three heartbeats, as if the fire might apologize, as if someone in authority would laugh and call it a mistake. Then the screaming started, and the waiting ended.

"Yara." Maren's hand closed hard around her wrist. The old woman had come out of her stall two doors down with her shawl half-fastened and her gray hair still pinned for sleep, which meant she'd been dozing behind her counter again, which meant she'd heard nothing, seen nothing, until the world was burning. "Yara, look at me. Back way. Now."

"The stock—"

"Forget the stock."

But Yara couldn't forget it, because the stock wasn't charms tonight. It was the map. It rode against her sternum in its oilcloth wrapping, sewn into the lining of her coat where she'd hidden it three nights ago after Dain's ambush and the Inquisitors' interruption, and she felt it there now like a second heartbeat, heavier than her own. The map was safe. The map was *always* safe, because she never let it out of her skin's reach anymore.

The stock — the little clay memory-charms, the knotwork blessings, the vials of dream-salt she'd spent eleven years perfecting — that was what burned. She watched a shelf of them go up in a bloom of green-white flame, each charm popping like a tiny egg as the magic inside it died, and she thought: *eleven years*, and then she thought nothing coherent at all.

"They're not fighting the fire," Maren said. Her voice had gone very flat, very quiet, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish. "Look. They're *feeding* it."

She was right. Along the canal-side edge of the market, where the Watch should have been forming a bucket line, soldiers stood shoulder to shoulder with torches of their own, hurling them one by one onto the covered stalls. Not chasing the map-holder out of the market. Burning the market *down around* the map-holder. Someone had decided that if you could not find one woman in a crowd of three hundred, you could simply remove the crowd, and the stalls, and the night, and see what crawled out of the ashes.

An Edict-burning, then. Yara had heard of them, everyone had — the great purges of the early years, before she was born, when they'd burned whole districts in Vessany's name — but she had believed, in the stubborn way of people who survive by keeping their heads down, that the capital had grown too large, too useful, too *taxed* to burn. She had believed wrong.

"Sefa," Maren said suddenly, and her grip on Yara's wrist turned to iron. "Where's Sefa?"

Old Sefa sold candles and charms against nightmares, and he was seventy-one years old, and his stall was the one that was burning. He would be trying to save it. Of course he would be. He'd kept that stall through two Watch raids and a flood, arguing with soldiers twice his size about the price of melted wax, because the stall was his late wife's dowry and he'd sooner lose a hand.

"I'll get him." Yara was already moving, coat pulled tight over the map, ducking under the guy-rope of the next stall as embers came down like orange snow.

"You will *not*—" Maren began, and then a beam went somewhere behind them with a crack like a felled tree, and whatever else she said was swallowed.

* * *

The smoke lay along the market lane in layers, black at knee height and boiling orange above, and Yara moved through it low, the way she'd once watched a poacher move through reeds, one hand on the ground, breathing through her sleeve. Her eyes streamed. Somewhere close a horse was screaming, and she hated the Watch a little more for every second of it.

Sefa's stall was a skeleton of black timber and falling sparks. He wasn't in it.

She found him ten yards past it, at the mouth of the alley that ran down to the canal steps, and for one merciful moment she thought he'd made it, because he was upright, because he was moving, because he had his arms around a young man she didn't know — a stranger, a boy really, wide-eyed and soot-streaked, one of the new vendors who'd set up only last month selling bottled fog to children.

Then she saw the soldiers.

Three of them, coming up the alley from the canal side, cutting off the water route, driving everyone back toward the fire. That was the shape of it, she understood with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm: not chaos, but a net drawn tight. Burn the center, guard the exits, wait for the rat.

One of the soldiers saw Sefa and shouted something — *halt, halt, in the name of the Edict* — and Sefa did not halt. He shoved the boy ahead of him toward the alley's dark throat and turned to face the soldiers with his empty hands raised, and even now, even here, he was talking to them. She could not hear the words over the fire, but she knew their cadence. Bargaining cadence. *Gentlemen, gentlemen, there's been a mistake.* Seventy-one years old and still certain the world could be argued with.

The soldier hit him with the flat of a short sword, backhanded, casual as swatting a fly, and Sefa went down.

Something in Yara's chest tore loose and never quite seated itself again.

She should have run. Every instinct trained by eleven years of double life said *run*: the boy was free, the alley was open, the soldiers were occupied, and the map against her heart was worth more than any life in this burning lane, including hers — that was arithmetic Dain would do, that was arithmetic the chamberlain would do, that was perhaps even arithmetic the dead stranger whose map it had been would have done. Run.

Instead she picked up a shard of broken awning-pole, burning at one end like a spear, and she screamed — wordless, furious, a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice — and she swung it at the nearest soldier's head.

He caught it. Of course he caught it; he was armored and twenty-five and she was a food-taster with soot on her face. But the swing bought two seconds, and two seconds was enough for the boy to vanish down the alley, and it was enough for Maren — Maren, who had followed her, because of course Maren had followed her, the old fool, the dear old fool — to come out of the smoke swinging a kettle of cooling wax that caught the second soldier across the eyes.

And it was enough for Sefa, on the ground, to do the thing Yara would spend the rest of her life turning over and over like a stone she could not put down.

He grabbed the third soldier's ankle.

It was nothing. A dying man's gesture, a child's trick, a hand on a boot. But the soldier stumbled, and the stumble took him backward into the burning stall's collapse, and the noise he made, and the way the others turned — away from Yara, away from Maren, toward their fallen brother and the old man on the cobbles — bought everything. Bought the alley. Bought the dark. Bought Yara's life, spent by a candle-seller in the space between one breath and the next.

"*Run,* girl," Sefa said, from the ground, looking at her over the soldier's boot, and he was smiling, that gap-toothed merchant's smile he used when haggling, as if this were just another negotiation and he'd gotten the better end of it. "Run and don't you dare look—"

The sword came down. Yara looked. She would always have looked. That was the wound she gave herself that night, deeper than any the fire dealt: she stayed long enough to see it, long enough to fix it in herself forever — the flash, the fall, the old man's hand opening on the cobblestones as though releasing something he no longer needed to hold.

Then Maren's arm was around her waist, hauling, and the alley took them both.

* * *

They came out by the tannery sluice, where the water ran red-brown with dye waste and the smell covered theirs, and Maren walked her — half carried her — through the reek and the dark until the glow of the market was a stain on the clouds behind them rather than a thing they could see. Only then did Maren stop, and lean against the sluice wall, and begin, silently, to shake.

"He argued with them," Yara said. Her own voice sounded far away, borrowed. "He was seventy-one years old and he stood there *arguing*."

"That was Sefa." Maren's voice was raw. "That was always Sefa."

"They killed him for a hand on a boot. For nothing. For *nothing*, Maren, they burned the whole market for nothing, for *me*—" And there it was, the other thing, the thing underneath the grief, rising like bile: they had burned it for her. Three hundred lives, forty years of Maren's luck-knots, Sefa's wife's dowry, the boy's bottled fog, all of it ash, because a dead man had pressed a brass map into her hands and she had not had the courage to throw it in the canal. "*I* did this."

Maren took her by the shoulders. In the light of the distant fire the old woman's face was streaked black and wet, and her eyes were fierce and clear.

"You listen to me, Yara Whitlock. I've sold charms in that market for forty years, and every one of those years we knew this could come. We called it the tax. Fire, raids, the Watch — the tax you paid for doing what the Edict says no one may do. Sefa paid it tonight. I'll pay it someday, or you will. But don't you dare take the *blame* too. Blame belongs to the ones holding the torches." Her grip tightened. "Now. The map. Tell me true — is it on you?"

Yara's hand went to her chest without her permission.

Maren nodded slowly. "Then hear the rest. There were Watchmen at the north gate asking names before the first torch flew. Asking after a *woman*, a taster, a charm-seller. They didn't come hunting generally, girl. They came hunting *you*. Which means someone talked, or someone guessed, and either way your stall is known, your face is known, and by morning your name will be on a writ." She paused, and Yara watched her decide to say the next part, watched the cost of it move across her face. "You can't come to my place. Don't ask. I have grandchildren."

"I know." And she did know, and it didn't hurt less for knowing. "I know, Maren."

"Then take the tanner's road south and don't stop till dawn. And Yara—" Maren pulled something from inside her shawl and pressed it into her palm: a luck-knot, old and soft with handling, frayed at the edges, one of the first she'd ever tied. "Forty years I charged for these. This one's free. Go."

Yara held it a moment — a scrap of knotted cord worth nothing and everything — and then, because there were no words large enough, she kissed the old woman's sooty cheek and turned south, and did not look back, because she had promised herself in the burning lane that she was done watching things end.

* * *

She walked until the city walls fell away and the road became orchard-dark, and then she climbed a stile into a fallow field and sat with her back against a hay-rick that smelled of summer gone to seed, and she let herself shake.

The fire was still visible, a dull red bruise on the underside of the clouds. Somewhere behind it, Sefa lay on the cobbles where they'd left him, because the Watch did not return bodies to families when the death was an Edict-death; they burned them separately, namelessly, in the yard behind the Inquisitory. No grave. No knot of mourning-cord. Nothing to hold.

*The garden keeps score,* the dead stranger had whispered. She hadn't understood it then. Sitting in the dark with a dead man's map against her heart and an old man's death on her conscience, she began to. Score was being kept, all right — but not by any garden. By the Inquisitor General, in her neat and patient hand: a market, a candle-seller, a name on a writ. And by Yara herself, whether she willed it or no, in the ledger behind her ribs where Sefa's smile was entered and could never be struck out.

Rage came first, and she welcomed it, fed it, wrapped it around the guilt like a bandage because the guilt alone would have stopped her heart. They had burned forty years of small kindnesses — knots against bad luck, salt for good dreams, candles against nightmare — over *paperwork*. Over an erasure so old no one living could even say what had been erased. What kind of law needed fire to defend it? What kind of mercy needed a graveyard?

And beneath the rage, quieter, colder, taking root: *this is what erasure costs. This is what it has always cost. They erased a country, and the burning has never actually stopped.*

The thirteenth moon was coming. Ovid's key sat in her memory like a swallowed coal. Two keys remained, and a brother who hated her, and an Inquisitor General who had just shown her exactly how far she would be pursued.

Yara closed her hand around Maren's luck-knot until the cord bit into her palm, and stood, and faced south, where the road ran dark toward the Lethe Canal and everything she still had to lose.

Behind her, the market glowed like a wound that would not close. Ahead, somewhere in the dark, she knew — with the certainty of a taster who knows poison on her tongue before the first swallow — that Dain was already walking the same road.

She did not know yet that he was carrying news that would break her heart, or that the song their mother sang was a key. She knew only that she could not carry this alone anymore, and that the man who wanted her dead was the only person alive who might understand why she couldn't put the map down.

She started walking.
