## 11. The Second Key Is a Song

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above the reek of curing hides, and Dain Vess could not sleep in it.

He lay on a straw pallet that smelled of oak bark and lime, arms folded behind his head, watching moonlight crawl across the rafters like something alive and slow. Below him, through the floorboards, came the rhythmic scrape of a stretching knife — the tanner worked nights, paid well to ask nothing — and beneath that, fainter, the sound of Yara Whitlock turning over on her own pallet, restless as he was.

Three days since the Inquisitors had nearly taken them both in the alley behind the chandler's shop. Three days since they had run together, not because either trusted the other, but because running apart meant dying apart. He could hear her breathing now, quick and shallow even in sleep, the breathing of someone who had learned that sleep was just another room where danger could find you.

Dain knew that breathing. He'd heard it in his own chest for two years, ever since Corwin left.

*Corwin.*

He said his brother's name inside his head the way you press a bruise — not to hurt yourself, exactly, but to prove the hurting was still there, that it hadn't gone numb and taken the memory with it.

He had followed Yara Whitlock for six weeks with murder folded up small in his coat pocket, the way other men carried knives. He had watched her swallow poison at court by day and sell little brass-and-bone charms by night, watched her move through both worlds with the weary grace of a woman carrying water across a long bridge, and he had hated her for it. Hated her competence. Hated her calm. Hated most of all that Corwin had spent his last coins and last days on her stall, on her city, on whatever she was — while Dain had spent those same weeks in the hill country, digging ditches for farmers who'd never heard of Vessany, waiting for a brother who never wrote.

He had cornered her. He had put his hands on her. And then the Inquisitors had come out of the dark like ink poured into water, and everything he thought he knew had come apart at the seams.

Because she hadn't stolen anything.

He turned the knowledge over again, testing it, the way he'd test a coin against his teeth. She had told him on the road, in the flat voice of someone reporting weather: *Your brother was bleeding out when he reached my stall. Someone cut him down three streets away. He pressed the map into my hands because I was the nearest pair of hands, and because — * here she had paused, and looked at him with those grey eyes that gave away nothing and somehow gave away everything — *because I think he chose me. Tasters have trained memories. We can hold a thing in our heads without understanding it. Maybe that's what he needed.*

Dain had wanted to call it a lie. He had searched her face for the flicker, the tell, the small dishonesty that would let him go back to hating her, because hating her was easier than the alternative.

There had been no flicker.

And tonight, lying in the tanner's reek with the moon climbing, the truth finally finished its work on him. It hadn't hit all at once. It had come the way rot comes into fruit — from some small place he hadn't checked, spreading quietly until the whole thing softened in his grip.

Corwin hadn't been robbed. Corwin had been *running*. Running with the map, running toward the night market, running toward the one person in Ashfall who might keep it safe — and someone had caught him. Cut him down three streets from safety. Left him to spend his final breath pressing a legacy into a stranger's hands.

Which meant the question that had kept Dain upright for two years — *why didn't he send for me? why didn't he trust me?* — had an answer, and the answer was: he did trust Dain. That was the whole point of the map. That was why Corwin had carried it alone, why he'd told no one, why he'd died in an alley with its secret still whole. Not because Dain wasn't worthy of it.

Because Dain was.

The grief curdled. He felt it turn in his chest, sweet sorrow going sour, and he sat up too fast, straw scattering, because if he stayed lying down he was going to make a sound and Yara Whitlock slept with a knife under her pillow and woke like a cat dropped in water.

He climbed the ladder to the loft's little window instead, shouldered it open, and breathed the night air — even tainted with hide-smell, better than the air inside his own skull.

That was when the song started.

Not from outside. From below. From Yara's pallet, half-dreaming, half-waking: a thread of melody rising through the floorboards, thin as wire.

Dain went cold from the scalp down.

He knew the song. He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — not as notes but as fact, as gravity, as the smell of bread and woodsmoke in a kitchen that no longer existed. Their mother had sung it every night of their childhood, kneeling between their beds in the house above the orchard rows, her voice low and roughened from the day's work, her hand moving on the blanket in time:

*Low the lantern, low the light,*
*low the ember under ash,*
*sleep, my garden, sleep till spring,*
*the thirteenth moon will bring you back—*

He was down the ladder before he knew he'd moved.

Yara sat bolt upright on her pallet, knife already in her fist, eyes wide and unfocused — caught between dream and waking, still singing, the words coming out of her like water from a cracked jar. Her lips were grey. A fine sweat stood on her brow.

"Yara." He kept his voice low, the way you spoke to a spooked animal or a sleeping fire. "Yara. Wake up."

She surfaced all at once, the way he'd seen drowning things surface — a gasp, a scramble backward, the knife coming up between them before her eyes finished focusing on his face.

"It's me," he said. "It's only me."

"What—" She swallowed. Her free hand came up and touched her own throat, as though checking whether the song had really come from there. "Was I singing?"

"You were singing a lullaby," Dain said. His mouth had gone dry. "Where did you learn it?"

"I didn't." She dragged a hand through her dark hair, which had come loose from its braid and made her look younger and more exhausted than any spy ought to look. "I don't know it. I've never — it's not a song I know, Dain, it's a *charm*. One of mine. A memory-charm, the kind I sell in the market — I must have handled it too long today, some of them leak, especially the old ones, the ones made from—"

"From what?" He crouched down slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way you approached anyone holding a blade. "Made from what?"

She hesitated. Then, because three days of shared flight had bought at least that much honesty: "From people. From memory. You take a lock of hair, or blood, or sometimes just a name spoken over the right materials, and you bind a piece of remembering into the charm. Most of what I sell is small. First kisses. Dead mothers' voices. The taste of a meal from before hard times came." Her jaw tightened. "I never sell anything big. Big memories break people."

"And the song?"

"The song isn't mine." She said it carefully, tasting each word. "But it's *in* me. It's been in me since—" She stopped. Since the map. Since the night a bleeding man had pressed brass into her hands and whispered about gardens and scores. "Since Corwin."

Dain sat down on the floor across from her, hard, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

"Sing it again," he said.

"Dain—"

"*Sing it.*"

She looked at him for a long moment — this woman he had hunted, fought, fled beside — and then she closed her eyes, and sang.

*Low the lantern, low the light,*
*low the ember under ash,*
*sleep, my garden, sleep till spring,*
*the thirteenth moon will bring you back.*

Her voice was nothing like their mother's. It was thinner, cooler, a court-trained instrument where Maren Vess had been a field voice, a harvest voice. But the melody was exact. The words were exact. And when she reached the fourth line, Dain heard himself join her — helplessly, the way you sneeze, the way you flinch — his own voice cracking on *garden*, because he hadn't sung it aloud in twenty years and his throat remembered everything his mind had tried to bury.

They stopped together. Silence rushed back into the loft like water into a footprint.

"That's a key," Yara said quietly. It wasn't a question. She was looking at him now with the expression he'd begun to dread and rely on in equal measure: the look of a woman doing arithmetic with other people's souls. "Isn't it? The inscription on the map — *three keys of memory, held in minds made to forget* — I thought it meant objects, or places, or secrets. But it means *this*. Songs. Words. Things carried in the body so deep the Edict couldn't burn them out."

"Our mother wasn't from here," Dain said. The words came slowly, dredged up from silt. "I always knew that much. She had an accent she never lost, and she cooked things nobody in our village ate, and once — I was maybe seven — a tax assessor came through asking odd questions, and she went white as flour and made us stay in the cellar all day. She told us we were from the hill country. She told everyone we were from the hill country." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the hollow ring of a man hearing a door open onto a room he'd boarded shut long ago. "She wasn't from the hill country. She was from Vessany. Wasn't she. And she taught us the song so that even if the Edict took everything else —"

"—it couldn't take the key," Yara finished. "Clever woman. Desperate woman." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Do you know what it means, the words? *Sleep, my garden, sleep till spring. The thirteenth moon will bring you back.*"

"It's a lullaby," Dain said. But even as he said it, he heard it differently — heard it the way you hear a familiar word repeated until it turns strange. A garden sung to sleep. An ember banked under ash, kept low, kept alive, waiting. Waiting for a specific moon. "Or it's instructions," he amended. "A recipe wearing a cradle-song's clothes. Sing the garden to sleep so no one finds it. Keep the ember under ash so it doesn't die. And on the thirteenth moon—"

"—the door opens," Yara said. "Once. For one night." She was pale again, but it was a different pallor now — the pallor of vertigo, of ground shifting. "Your mother hid a key to the last living heart of Vessany inside a bedtime song and fed it to her children for years, and neither of you ever knew. That's what erasure does, Dain. It doesn't just burn the country. It makes the survivors into unwitting vaults. She carried her whole homeland in her throat and probably died thinking she'd only ever passed on a pretty tune."

Dain thought of his mother's grave — unmarked, hidden, a secret he and Corwin had kept even from themselves until the map forced it into the light. He thought of her hands on the blankets. He thought of the fourth line, *the thirteenth moon will bring you back*, sung over two boys who slept through the loss of an entire nation every single night, guarded by a song they mistook for love.

Love *was* the disguise. Love was how it survived.

His eyes were burning. He let them.

"I spent two years hating you," he said. It came out rough, unsanded. "Six weeks hunting you, and two years before that hating the idea of you. Some stranger with my brother's money in her pocket and my brother's death on her doorstep. I had it built, brick by brick — you seduced him, or tricked him, or used him, and when he was spent you let him bleed in an alley." He shook his head. "And the whole time, he was *protecting* it. Protecting the map. Protecting Mother's work. He died three streets from your stall because he wouldn't lead them anywhere else, wouldn't risk it, and his last act was to put it in the safest hands in Ashfall." He looked at her — really looked, past the exhaustion and the knife-calluses and the careful grey eyes, at the woman underneath, who sold dead mothers' voices to strangers because she understood what it was to lose a country. "You were never the thief, Yara. I was. I stole two good years from his memory and spent them on hate."

Yara was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice was gentle in a way that almost hurt worse than anger would have.

"He chose you too, you know. Whatever he wrote on that map, whoever he meant to carry it forward — he grew up on the same song you did. The key is in you, Dain. It's been in you since you were small enough to believe lullabies were only lullabies." She unfolded herself and reached across the space between the pallets — not touching him, just laying her open hand on the floorboards between them, palm up, an offer rather than a demand. "Two keys of memory. Your mother's song is one. We need a third before the thirteenth moon, and the moon is coming fast."

He looked at her hand. Rough palms, taster's scars, a burn mark along the thumb he'd noticed the first day and hated himself for noticing.

Then he put his hand in hers, and let her pull him up, and the guilt in his chest shifted — not gone, nothing was ever gone, but *transmuted*, base metal beaten toward something that could hold an edge.

"We'll need the map," he said. "And we'll need to know what the third key is before Sarel's people do. Because if the Inquisitor General figures out that keys live in *people* — in songs, in graves, in heads —" He stopped. He didn't want to finish the sentence, and Yara's face said she'd already finished it for herself.

Every person who might remember Vessany had just become a target.

From below the floorboards came the tanner's knife, scraping, scraping, patient as the moon. And somewhere across the sleeping city, Dain knew, the Inquisitor General was awake too, doing her own arithmetic.

"Get your coat," Yara said, already moving, already braiding back her hair with quick soldier's fingers. "We're going to see a man about a chamberlain."
