## 12. River of Forgetting

The Lethe Canal began where the map said all rivers in Vessany used to end: nowhere. It was not on any chart Yara had ever seen, though she had memorized the waterways of the capital for her work — the taster's trade required knowing which wells fed which houses, which cisterns could be poisoned, which pipes ran beneath which kitchens. This canal appeared on none of them. It simply *was*, cutting a silver-green seam through the industrial quarter's edge, running between warehouses that leaned toward it like mourners over a grave.

"It eats memories," Dain said, standing at the towpath's edge with his arms crossed, as if the water might hear him and take offense. "That's what the inscription means. *The river keeps what the country lost.* The Edict didn't just burn books, Yara. They poured the past into the water."

Yara looked at the canal properly. It was beautiful, which frightened her more than ugliness would have. The surface moved without wind, slow and glassy, and beneath it she could see shapes — not fish, not weeds, but flickers of color and light, like lanterns sunk to the bottom and still burning. Once, she would have called it pretty.

Now she understood she was looking at a graveyard that hadn't finished dying.

"We don't have to go by water," she said, though they both knew they did. The Inquisitor General's net had tightened around the land roads after the night market burned; every gate out of the city had Sarel's gray-cloaked watchers at it, asking questions with polite voices and unpolite eyes. The canal ran under the walls through an old drainage arch, and the map showed no other way.

Dain didn't answer her. He had barely spoken since the market. Since Marla. Yara carried the old woman's death like a stone behind her ribs — Marla, who had sold luck-knots beside her stall for eleven years, who had grabbed Yara's arm and shoved her down the coal chute while the Watch's torches came down the row, and who had still been standing there, arms spread, when the flames went up. Dain had pulled her out of the culvert half-drowned and furious at her for being alive, and neither of them had found words for any of it since.

So they rented a flat-bottomed skiff from a lockkeeper who took coin and asked nothing, because the people who lived along the Lethe had learned long ago that questions were dangerous in both directions.

The boat smelled of tar and old fish. Yara sat in the stern with the brass map open across her knees, and Dain took the pole, and they slid out onto water that did not ripple around their hull so much as *receive* it.

* * *

The first hour was ordinary enough. The canal ran straight between high stone banks furred with moss, and the warehouses gave way to orchards gone wild, and then to orchards that were not wild at all but *wrong* — trees bearing fruit Yara had no names for, purple-skinned and heavy, hanging over the water as if offering themselves. She reached for one and Dain caught her wrist.

"Don't," he said. "Anything that grows here grew from what they drowned. You eat it, you don't know whose memory you're eating."

She withdrew her hand slowly. The fruit swung back and forth, back and forth, patient as a pendulum.

It was in the second hour that the water began to speak.

Not aloud. Not in words. But Yara became aware, gradually, the way one becomes aware of a headache, that she was remembering things that had never happened to her. A kitchen with blue tiles. A woman singing in a language that made her chest ache with homesickness for a home she'd never had. Snow falling on a harvest festival, warm snow, snow that smelled of cinnamon—

"Yara." Dain's voice came from very far away. "Yara, look at me."

She looked. His face was pale and beaded with sweat, and his knuckles were white on the pole, and she realized with distant surprise that he was fighting too — that whatever the river offered, it offered to both of them, tailored and sweet.

"It shows you what you want," he said through his teeth. "My brother told me about this place. He said the trick is to hold on to something the river can't replace. Something true and ugly. Something you'd never trade."

"What did you hold on to?"

His jaw worked. "Grief," he said. "I held on to grief. It's the one thing I've got plenty of."

The blue-tiled kitchen dissolved. Yara gasped as if surfacing from deep water, and found tears on her face without knowing when they'd started. The canal slid on beneath them, serene, its sunken lantern-lights drifting past below the hull like souls going somewhere slowly.

They poled on. Neither of them suggested stopping.

* * *

By the fourth hour, the banks had narrowed and the moss had thickened until it hung in curtains, and the light turned green and submarine, and Yara stopped being able to say how long they'd been on the water or why.

That was how it started — not with visions, but with subtraction. Small things first. She reached for the memory of her mother's face and found a smooth blankness where it should have been, and felt no alarm, only a mild curiosity, the way one notices a missing button. Then she reached for the taste of the queen's breakfast that morning — almond cake, laced with nothing, safe — and couldn't find it. Then she tried to remember why she was in a boat, and the question itself seemed rude, intrusive, unnecessary.

The water was very calm. The water was very kind. The water wanted nothing from her except everything, gently, the way sleep wants you.

Somewhere above her, someone was shouting her name.

"Yara!" Hands on her shoulders, shaking her. A face swam into focus — young, sharp-boned, eyes the brown of river mud, wet with sweat. She knew him. Didn't she? There was a name that went with that face. She reached for it and the water smoothed the reaching away like a hand smoothing bedclothes.

"You're losing it," the young man said, and his voice cracked with something worse than fear. "Listen to me. Listen. My name is Dain. Dain Vess. And my brother—" He stopped, swallowed, started again, louder, as if shouting across a great distance rather than eighteen inches of quiet air. "My brother was Corren. Corren Vess. He was born in Vessany before the Edict, in a house with a red door on Tanner's Lane, and he had a scar on his left thumb from a carving knife when he was nine, and he laughed like a dog barking, terribly, everyone hated it, and he died three weeks ago in your stall with a hole in his side and my name on his lips, and he gave you the map because he trusted you, you stupid, stubborn, *brave* woman—"

Each detail landed in her like a stone dropped into still water, and each one broke the surface the river had laid over her. The red door. The scar. The terrible laugh. She felt them sink into her and take root, and around their roots her own memories began to regrow — her mother's face, sudden and unbearable; the almond cake; the burned market; Marla's arms spread wide against the torchlight.

"—and he called the map *the debt*, and he said the garden keeps score, and he made me promise, he made me promise I'd finish it even if—" Dain's voice finally failed. He was gripping her shoulders hard enough to bruise, and crying without seeming to know it, and looking at her as though she were the last solid thing in a dissolving world.

"Dain," Yara said. Her own voice sounded borrowed. "Dain Vess. Your brother's name was Corren."

"Yes."

"He trusted me."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry I forgot him for four seconds." She put her hands over his. "I'm sorry anyone ever forgot him at all."

He let out a breath that was half sob, half laugh — Corren's terrible bark of a laugh, briefly, horribly, inherited — and pulled her upright, and pushed the pole down into water that suddenly felt cold again, and hostile again, and real.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The green light thickened toward evening. When Yara finally trusted her own mind, she said, quietly:

"You saved me with him. With his name."

"That's what names are for," Dain said roughly. "Why do you think they erased a whole country instead of just burning it? Burning leaves ash. Ash remembers it was something. But a name—" He shook his head. "Take the name away and there's nothing left to grieve. That's what the Edict really was. Not a law against magic. A law against mourning."

Yara looked at him — really looked, for the first time since the alley where they'd fought like animals over a dead man's legacy. He was twenty-three at most, and he had walked out of a burned market carrying a stranger because his brother had told him to, and he had just spent the last of his grief to buy her back from the water.

"The second key," she said. "The song. Your mother's lullaby. Is it — is it still yours? After the river?"

Dain went still. Slowly, warily, he closed his eyes. His lips moved. No sound came out at first, and Yara's heart dropped — and then, faintly, cracked and off-key and *there*, he began to hum.

The melody was small and looping and old. It sounded, Yara thought, like a door opening somewhere very far underground.

"It's still mine," he whispered. "Corren taught it to me. He said the river can't take what two people carry together." He opened his eyes and looked at her with something new in them — not trust exactly, not yet, but the raw material of it, the ore before smelting. "You held on too. Down there. Whatever you grabbed, you didn't let go."

"I grabbed the taste of poison," Yara admitted. "Almond and bitterleaf. The first assassination I ever caught. It's the ugliest thing I know." She almost smiled. "Turns out ugly is good ballast."

* * *

They made camp that night on a shelf of stone where the canal bent around the roots of a hill, and Dain built a small fire from driftwood that burned with a faint amber smell, like the flowers pressed in a locket neither of them had dug up yet. Yara sat with the map open on her knees. Under the moonrise, the brass had begun to change — lines surfacing that hadn't been there before, the engraving rearranging itself like a sleeper turning over.

"Look at this," she said.

Dain crouched beside her. New script ringed the canal's course on the map, fine and slanted, and beneath it a fresh line had etched itself across the metal, pointing onward, past the hills, toward a symbol shaped like a seed split open.

"It knows we crossed," Dain said uneasily. "The map knows."

"The garden keeps score," Yara murmured. "Corren's words. Maybe keeping score isn't a threat. Maybe it's bookkeeping. Maybe the garden is counting what's left to save."

But Dain wasn't listening anymore. He had gone rigid, staring past her shoulder, down the dark curve of the canal — where, far off, moving against the current with no visible boat and no visible pole, rode a line of gray-cloaked figures walking on the water as if it were road.

"They're not getting wet," he breathed.

Yara's hand found the map and closed over it. The figures were small yet, and slow, but they were coming, and the water that ate memory did not seem to be eating theirs.

"Inquisitors," she said. "Sarel found the river too."
