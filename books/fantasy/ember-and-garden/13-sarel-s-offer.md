## 13. Sarel's Offer

The trap was not sprung in the night market, because the night market was ash. It was not sprung at the canal gates or the seed vault or any of the dozen places Sarel had salted with watchers over eleven days of patient, grinding work. It was sprung in a millhouse on the Lethe Canal's upper reach, where the water ran slow and forgetful and the wheel turned with a sound like a great stone tongue lolling in its mouth.

Sarel had chosen it herself. She always chose her own ground.

She stood now in the loft above the grinding floor, behind a screen of hanging sacks, and watched through the gap between two of them as her men brought the Whitlock woman and the Vessani brother up the outside stair. Four Inquisitors, two to each prisoner, hands bound with cord that had been soaked in tallow and ash — the old recipe, the one that deadened small magics, though Sarel doubted either of them could work so much as a warming charm with their wrists tied. The woman walked like someone counting exits. The brother walked like someone who had already decided which of the four men he would kill first if it came to killing.

Good, Sarel thought. She wanted them sharp. A dull prisoner told you what they thought you wanted to hear. A frightened one told you nothing at all. But a proud one — a proud one you could talk to.

"Sit them," she said, and came out from behind the sacks.

* * *

Yara Whitlock knew her the moment she stepped into the light, of course. Everyone at court knew the Inquisitor General by sight: the grey coat cut plain as a widow's, the iron-grey braid coiled at the nape of her neck tight enough to crack walnuts, the face that never did anything so wasteful as express an emotion. Yara had seen her perhaps six times across the width of throne rooms and tasting halls, always at a distance, always feeling the way you feel when a hawk crosses the sun.

Now the hawk was three feet away, pouring tea.

That was the thing Yara had not been prepared for. There was a table in the millhouse loft — an actual table, with a cloth, and a teapot, and two cups, and a plate of honey-cakes that smelled of real butter and lemon peel. Sarel set the pot down with the unhurried grace of a woman hosting a garden party and gestured to the chairs.

"You'll take tea," she said. It was not quite a question. "You've had a long walk from the vaults and the canal water does things to the appetite."

Dain made a sound low in his throat, half laugh, half snarl. "You burned the market," he said. "You burned it with people in it."

"I burned a stall row," Sarel said, pouring. "The deaths were regrettable and fewer than they might have been, because I gave the Watch orders to clear the residential lanes first. You may verify this with survivors, if you can find any willing to speak to you." She set a cup before Yara. Steam rose off it, carrying bergamot and something underneath, something green and strange that Yara's trained tongue caught at once and could not name — and then could, and felt the floor tilt under her.

Ember-flowers. The tea was scented with ember-flowers.

"You're staring at the cup," Sarel observed. She sat down opposite, folded her hands, and looked at Yara with an expression that was almost gentle. "Yes. I know what it is. I have known for thirty years, Whitlock. Sit down. Drink it or don't. It's only scent; the flowers themselves are long extinct outside one walled place, and I am not in the habit of poisoning witnesses I've spent a fortnight catching."

Yara sat. Dain stayed standing until one of the Inquisitors put a hand on his shoulder, and then he sat too, because sitting let him keep his eyes level with hers, and his eyes were doing all the violence his hands were denied.

"The map," Sarel said. "Where is it?"

"Gone," said Dain.

"Burned," said Yara, a half-breath later, and hated how the two answers braided together into one obvious lie.

Sarel did not sigh, did not frown. She simply picked up her own cup and drank, and over its rim her gaze moved between them with the patience of a woman reading a ledger she had already audited twice. When she set the cup down she said, "I'm going to tell you a story. You may interrupt it if you like; most people do, early on, and then stop."

* * *

Here is what Sarel told them, in a voice as level and unornamented as a surveyor's report:

Thirty-one years ago, the country of Vessany had stood where the eastern provinces now stood — orchards down to the river, a capital called Ilvane with white bridges, a population of some four hundred thousand souls. And in its last decade, its gardeners had done something no gardener had ever done. They had grown a place that remembered. Not a record, not an archive of paper — a living thing, rooted, that took into itself every memory its people fed it and kept those memories alive the way soil keeps a seed alive. They called it the Ember Garden, and they were proud of it, and they taught their children to feed it.

"And then the blight came," Sarel said. "Not a plague of flesh first. A plague of memory. It started in the Garden itself — a rot in the roots, we think, though no one living knows, because everyone who studied it is dead. The Garden began giving back what it was fed. Wrongly. People drank from wells near the hedge rows and woke up remembering lives they had never lived. Remembered being their own grandmothers. Remembered being strangers. Remembered dying. Whole villages woke up other people, Whitlock. Husbands didn't know wives. Mothers ran from children they'd birthed that morning. And the sickness spread outward from the Garden like rings in a pond, and the physicians of Ilvane — gifted ones, charmers, healers, the best magic could produce — could do nothing, because the disease was made of magic and fed on magic, and every charm they worked against it fed it faster."

Yara's hands had gone still around her cup. She was aware of Dain beside her, rigid, listening the way a man listens to a description of his own house on fire.

"The crown here watched," Sarel went on. "And the crown here panicked, because the rings in the pond kept widening, and there was no reason on earth they would stop at Vessany's borders. So a decision was made. Not lightly. Not gladly. By tired people in a cold room, most of whom are dead now." Her eyes flicked, briefly, toward Dain. "Some of whom are not."

"The Edict," Yara said quietly.

"The Edict," Sarel agreed. "Magic outlawed, root and branch — not because magic was wicked, but because the blight traveled along it like fire along a fuse. And Vessany—" she paused, and for the first time something moved behind her face, something old and carefully buried, "—Vessany was erased. Its name struck from maps and records. Its people dispersed under new names. Its history rewritten so thoroughly that within a generation, scholars argued about whether it had ever existed. Because a country that is remembered can be grieved, and a country that is grieved will be sought, and the Garden at its heart was the source of the sickness. Forgetting was the quarantine. We amputated a limb to save the body."

"You murdered a country," Dain said. His voice cracked on it.

"We quarantined a country," Sarel said, "and yes, it died, and yes, I have carried the weight of that since I was younger than you are now. I helped write the erasure edicts. I personally signed the order that renamed your mother's village." She said it without flinching, and somehow that was worse than flinching. "So believe me when I say I am not your enemy out of ignorance, boy. I know exactly what was lost. I have spent thirty years making sure no one else ever has to find out why."

She leaned forward then, and the lamplight caught her face, and Yara saw — really saw — that the Inquisitor General was not a hawk at all. Hawks were simple. This was something that had looked into the fire and walked away carrying it.

"Now," Sarel said. "Here is my offer, and I will make it once."

* * *

She laid it out plainly, the way she laid out everything.

The map, surrendered. The location of the door, disclosed. In exchange: full immunity for Yara Whitlock — her tasting post restored, her night trade pardoned, every charm she'd ever sold struck from the record. Immunity for Dain Vessani as well, though Sarel's voice cooled slightly on his name, because his brother had died running and that was a choice, whatever grief said otherwise. Money. Passage. New names if they wanted them, good ones, deep ones.

"And in return," Sarel finished, "the Garden stays closed. The thirteenth moon comes and goes, and the door stays shut, and the world goes on not dying. That's all I ask. Not your silence. Not your loyalty. Just — leave the door shut."

"Why?" Yara asked. And it wasn't defiance; it was genuine, exhausted curiosity. "If it's as dangerous as you say, why offer immunity? Why not just take the map and kill us?"

For the first time, Sarel smiled. It was a small, dry, terrible thing.

"Because I need you to understand what you'd be opening," she said. "Martyrs make excellent钥匙— " she caught herself, the foreign word gone as fast as it came, "—martyrs make excellent legends. Dead, you become a story told in night markets for a hundred years, and stories make people curious, and curiosity makes people dig. Alive, pardoned, comfortable, and *knowing* — you become a witness. The one person in the realm who saw the Garden's heart and chose, freely, with full knowledge, to leave it shut. That testimony is worth more than any cell or gallows. I am not buying your silence, Whitlock. I am asking you to *agree with me.*"

The lamplight hissed. Below them the millwheel turned its slow stone tongue. And Yara sat very still, because here was the terrible thing, the thing she would not admit even to herself for years after:

It was tempting.

Not the money, not the pardon. The *rest*. She was so tired. Eleven days of running, of the map burning a hole against her ribs, of friends dead in burning markets, of a stranger's blood drying on her palms in memory. Sarel was offering her back her life — the whole of it, the day-life and the night-life both, legal, safe, *finished*. All she had to do was agree that forgetting was mercy. All she had to do was look at four hundred thousand erased people and call the erasure medicine.

And she thought of the chamberlain, Ovid, and the shame that had come off him like heat off a stove when his memories surfaced — shame, but also *relief*, the relief of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was a weight. She thought of the seed vault, the impossible green things growing in the dark, alive, *waiting*. She thought of the old friend dead in the market flames, whose name she refused to let herself forget even for a moment, because forgetting him would be letting the fire win.

"No," Yara said.

Sarel waited.

"It wasn't mercy," Yara said. "I've sold memory-charms for nine years, General. Do you know who buys them? Not criminals. Widows. Old men. People who lost someone and can't carry the shape of the hole. And do you know what every single one of them says when I ask if they want the memory *removed* instead of eased?" She leaned forward, matching Sarel, and her voice did not shake. "'No. I want to keep it. It hurts, and I want to keep it.' That's what people are, General. We keep what hurts because it's *ours*. You didn't quarantine a sickness. You amputated a country and called the wound clean."

"The blight was real," Sarel said, and now there was steel under the calm. "I saw the records. I interviewed the survivors who crossed the border raving in other people's voices."

"Then the Garden was sick," Yara said. "Sick things can be healed. Dead things can't. You didn't cure it — you buried it alive and posted guards at the grave, and now the guards are me and him, and we're telling you the thing in the grave is *breathing*."

Silence. The wheel turned. One of the Inquisitors shifted his weight, and Sarel lifted one finger without looking, and he went still again.

"You sound like your brother," she said at last, to Dain, and something in her voice had changed — softened, or hardened, Yara couldn't tell which. "He said nearly the same thing, the night I found him. Nearly." She rose, smoothed her grey coat, and looked down at the two of them with an expression that had finally, fully, stopped pretending to be hospitality. "I offered once. That was the bargain. You've refused. I find I respect it more than I expected to, and it changes nothing whatsoever."

She nodded to her men. "Take them to the cells at Thornmark. Separately. No visitors." Then, at the door, she paused — and did not turn around, and spoke to the room rather than to either of them.

"For what it's worth, Whitlock. The tea really is only scent. I don't poison guests." A beat. "I burn their markets instead. Get some sleep. Both of you will want your strength — the thirteenth moon is four nights away, and I intend for us all to be present at the door when it opens. Someone has to make sure it stays shut."

The stair creaked under four sets of boots going down, and then two prisoners being marched after them, and then the millhouse was quiet except for the wheel and the rain starting against the windows, and Yara sat with her untouched tea going cold, turning over the one thing Sarel had said that mattered more than all the rest — the thing she suspected the Inquisitor General had said deliberately, the way you leave a door unlocked on purpose:

*Someone has to make sure it stays shut.*

Which meant Sarel needed the door found too.

Which meant, somewhere between this millhouse and the thirteenth moon, the Inquisitor General intended to use them — or their map, or their bones — to reach the Ember Garden herself.

And Yara, bound and marching into the dark toward Thornmark's cells, began — quietly, wearily, with the part of her mind that had survived nine years of double life — to plan.
