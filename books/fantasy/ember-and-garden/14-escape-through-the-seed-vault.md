## 14. Escape Through the Seed Vault

The knock came at moonset — three slow, two fast, one long — and Yara's heart lurched before her mind caught up to what it meant. She hadn't heard that rhythm in three years, not since she'd stopped supplying charms to the underground sellers and gone quiet, keeping her head down beneath the Inquisitor General's widening shadow. But her hands remembered. Her hands were already moving to answer.

She cracked the door of the safehouse — a tanner's abandoned drying loft that smelled of salt and old lime — and found a face she knew only by its absence. Merrin Vael had been the night market's best charm-grinder before the Edict's teeth closed around the trade, and Yara had assumed, along with everyone else, that he'd fled south or drowned in the Lethe or simply stopped existing, the way people did when the Watch took an interest in them.

He looked older than his thirty years ought to allow. His coat was scorched at the hem, and there was ash worked so deeply into the creases of his knuckles that it seemed part of him now, like freckles or scars.

"You're alive," Yara said, which was stupid, because he was obviously alive, standing there breathing smoke-smell into her doorway.

"Barely, and badly." His eyes went past her shoulder to where Dain sat sharpening a knife against a whetstone with the grim patience of a man who had nothing else to do but wait for the world to end or improve. "And you've collected company."

"He's with me."

"That's what worries me." Merrin slipped inside, and Yara barred the door behind him. "The whole network is talking about you, Whitlock. The map-holder. Sarel has sketches of your face nailed up in every guardhouse from here to the coast. There are rewards. There are informants who'd sell their mothers for less than they're offering."

"I know what I'm worth."

"You don't." He said it gently, which made it worse. "Nobody knows what they're worth until the price gets high enough. Come here often enough and you'll meet someone who decides a purse of gold is worth more than your friendship. It's why we don't stay anywhere twice anymore."

Dain looked up from his knife. "You have somewhere to take us."

It wasn't quite a question, and Merrin didn't treat it as one. He crossed to the room's single window, a slit of glass fogged with grime, and peered out at the grey beginning of morning. "I have somewhere the Watch can't follow," he said. "Whether it leads anywhere worth going is another matter. You told the network you need passage out of the capital without touching a road, a gate, or a checkpoint. There's one way left. The old way."

"Which is?"

Merrin turned, and in the half-light his face did something complicated — hope and grief fighting for the same ground. "The seed vault."

Yara frowned. "There hasn't been a royal seed vault since—"

"Since Vessany," Merrin finished. "Yes. That's rather the point."

* * *

They went down instead of out, which Yara would learn was the whole philosophy of the old network: when the world above belongs to the law, the world below belongs to whoever kept their keys.

Merrin led them through the tannery's cellar, past racks of stiffened hides that hung like flayed ghosts, to a wall of stacked stone that looked no different from any other wall. He pressed his palm flat against a particular block — third from the floor, second from the corner — and whispered something too low for Yara to catch. The block sank inward with a grinding sigh, exhaling air that hadn't moved in years: cold, mineral, and underneath it, faintly green. The smell of growing things.

"The vault runs under the old palace district," Merrin explained as they descended a spiral stair cut into living rock, the walls sweating with condensation. "Built two hundred years ago to preserve the crown's seed stock through famine and war. When Vessany was erased, the decree ordered everything connected to that country destroyed — records, portraits, boundaries on maps, names from gravestones. But the vault was built *underneath* the erasure. Nobody thought to look down. Or perhaps somebody made sure nobody thought to look."

"Who built the tunnel?" Dain asked, his voice echoing strangely off the stone.

"A chain of people, over generations. Charm-sellers, mostly. People who dealt in memory even when memory was contraband. Each generation passed the route to the next, and each swore never to speak of it above ground. I'm breaking that oath right now by bringing you." He glanced back at Yara. "But the network voted. After the market burned. After Odessa."

Odessa. The old woman who had died covering Yara's escape from the burning night market, who had shoved Yara toward the drainage culvert with hands like tree bark and shouted *run, girl, run, you're the one carrying it*. Yara's throat tightened. She still caught herself reaching for the pouch of charms Odessa used to keep at her belt, forgetting, every time, that both the woman and the pouch were ash now.

"They voted," Yara managed. "To help me."

"To help the map," Merrin corrected, but not unkindly. "Though between us, most of us can't tell the difference anymore."

The stair ended at a corridor lined with iron doors, each stamped with a faded royal seal — a sunburst split by a sword. Merrin walked past door after door until he reached one whose seal had been deliberately defaced, hammered smooth, and replaced with nothing. He produced a key of black iron, worn bright at the bow from decades of handling.

"Here's the thing about erased countries," he said, fitting the key. "You can burn the books. You can chisel the stones. You can make it treason to say the name aloud. But you cannot unmake seeds. A seed doesn't remember being illegal. A seed just remembers how to grow."

The lock turned. The door swung open onto darkness — and then Merrin struck a shuttered lantern, and the darkness bloomed.

* * *

Yara had seen gardens all her life. The palace gardens, where she walked each morning to clear her head before a day of swallowing poison; the night market's potted herbs; the desperate window-boxes of the poor. She thought she understood what green things did. They grew toward light, they drank water, they lived their span and died.

What grew in the vault obeyed none of it.

The chamber stretched farther than lantern light could reach, a cavernous underground hall ribbed with stone arches, and from its floor rose plants — *impossibly* — in rows that followed no gardener's logic. There was no sun here. There was barely air. And yet trees stood forty feet tall with their crowns lost in shadow, their roots splitting the ancient flagstones. Vines climbed the arches and flowered in colors Yara had no names for: a blue so deep it seemed to hold night inside it, a red like coals under ash, a gold that moved, slowly, as though the flowers were remembering sunlight they'd never seen.

Beside her, Dain made a sound she'd never heard from him — small, involuntary, the sound of a man kicked in the chest.

"What is this place?" she whispered.

"Ember-flowers," Merrin said quietly, lifting his lantern toward a bank of blooms whose petals glowed with a dull inner heat, warm orange shot through with threads of black like cooling lava. "That's what the growers called them. Before. They were Vessany's signature crop — grown nowhere else in the world. The decree named them specifically. Every ember-flower in the kingdom was to be pulled and burned. Every seed confiscated." He smiled, and it was a broken sort of smile. "Every seed they *found*, anyway."

Yara stepped closer to the glowing bank. Heat radiated from the petals — real heat, gentle as a hand held near a hearth — and when she leaned in, she caught their scent, and something in her chest cracked open.

She knew that scent.

Not from life. From work. From the memory-charms she ground and sold, the ones that fetched the highest price, the ones customers described as *the smell of a country I've never been to, a homesickness with no home attached*. She had always assumed it was some blend of her own devising — dried rowan and smoked honey and something floral she'd never been able to identify. For years she'd chased that missing note, blending and reblending, and never once matched it.

Here it was. Growing out of the dark. The note she'd spent three years failing to reproduce was the smell of a country that officially had never existed.

"It's in my charms," she breathed. "I've been selling pieces of this place for years and I didn't know."

"Half the old recipes came out of Vessany," Merrin said. "The charm-trade is older than the Edict, Whitlock. Where do you think the craft came from? The Edict didn't outlaw magic because magic was foreign. It outlawed magic to bury where it came from."

Dain had wandered ahead, and now he stood motionless before one of the great trees, his lantern raised. Its fruit hung heavy and strange — pale globes veined with silver, too large for any orchard Yara knew. At the tree's base, half-buried in the upheaved stone, was a wooden marker, its letters carved deep and then gouged shallow by some official chisel — defaced, but not destroyed. Dain crouched and brushed away the dirt with his fingers, tracing the ruined grooves the way a man reads braille.

"Someone tried to read this before us," he said. "Recently. The dust is disturbed."

"The network comes sometimes," Merrin said. "To harvest. Carefully. We've been seeding the surface with these for years — smuggled into kitchen gardens, hedgerows, ditch banks. Little acts of resurrection. If the country can't come back, let its tomatoes grow in poor men's yards. Let its flowers bloom where the law can't see them."

Yara turned slowly, taking in the impossible hall again — the size of it, the age of it, the sheer stubborn patience of plants that had been growing in the dark for thirteen years without permission. Hope arrived in her like a physical event, painful as blood returning to a numb limb. All this time she'd thought she carried the last evidence of Vessany in a brass tube against her ribs. But the evidence had been here, rooted and flowering, the entire time. The country hadn't waited for her. It had simply refused, in its slow green way, to accept the decree.

"It's still alive," she said. "Sarel says the garden's magic caused the plague. She says erasure was mercy. But this—" she gestured at the flourishing dark, "—this doesn't look like a sickness. This looks like health. Thirteen years sealed underground and it's *thriving*."

"Magic isn't the disease," Merrin agreed. "Fear is. Fear is the plague, and the Edict is its fever."

Dain straightened from the marker, and in the lantern light his face had changed — harder and softer at once, grief transfigured into direction. "My brother died running messages for the garden," he said. "For whatever's at the end of that map. I used to think someone stole him from our family twice — first the erasure took our mother's history, then the map took Corvin. Now I think maybe nothing stole him. Maybe he went home the only way anyone can go home now."

He looked at Yara, and for the first time since the alley fight, since the interrupted blades and the shared flight, there was no suspicion in his eyes. Only a fierce, exhausted alliance.

"Show me the map," he said. "I want to see if the inscription mentions this. If the vault connects to the route—"

The map chose that moment to act on its own.

Yara felt it before she understood it — a warmth against her sternum, sudden as a fever spike. She pulled the brass tube free, and the metal was hot, uncomfortably hot, humming faintly like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. Around them, the vault responded. The ember-flowers flared brighter, their glow pulsing in rhythm — and Yara realized with a lurch that the rhythm matched her own heartbeat.

"It's answering them," she whispered. "Or they're answering it."

"Thirteen years," Merrin murmured, staring. "It's never done that. Not once."

The warmth spread from the tube into Yara's palms, and for a heartbeat — one suspended, singing heartbeat — she *saw*. Not with her eyes. With something older. She saw rows of these same trees under open sky, under two moons instead of one, tended by hands that sang to the roots in a language that tasted like rain. She saw the flowers harvested at dusk and carried singing to a door woven of living hedge. She saw—

The vision snapped shut. The tube cooled. The flowers dimmed to their patient ember-glow, and Yara stood gasping in the dark, Dain's hand suddenly gripping her arm.

"What did you see?"

"A door," she said. "A hedge. Two moons." She swallowed. "And I think — I think the vault isn't just a hiding place. I think it's a root system. I think everything growing here is connected to the Ember Garden, the way a cutting remembers the mother plant. And I think the map just woke up because it finally found soil it recognized."

Merrin had gone pale. "Then we need to move. Now. If the map is waking up, it's making noise in ways I can't predict — and there are people who listen for exactly this kind of noise. Sarel has listeners. Devices. Things I don't ask about." He was already moving toward the far side of the vault, where the great trees thinned and a secondary door stood half-hidden behind a curtain of silver-veined vines. "The route continues east from here, under the river, coming up beyond the customs houses. It's a day's walk in the dark. You'll want food, water, and a reason to keep putting one foot in front of the other."

"And you?" Yara asked.

"I close the doors behind you and go back to being nobody." Merrin paused at the vine-curtain, and his voice dropped. "Whitlock. Whatever's at the end of this road — the garden, the keys, all of it — when you get there, remember it wasn't only you. There were hundreds of us. Generations. Keeping seeds in the dark and hoping. Don't let them erase *that* too. Don't let the story be about one girl and a magic door. Make it about everyone who refused to forget."

Yara held out her hand. He took it, his grip ash-rough and strong.

"I'll make it about Odessa," she said. "And about you."

"Then that's payment enough." Merrin pushed aside the vines, revealing the eastern door and the long dark beyond it. "Go. And Whitlock — walk quickly. The thirteenth moon won't wait for tired feet."

They stepped through, and the vines fell closed behind them like a held breath released.

The dark ahead was total, and the map hummed against Yara's heart like a second pulse, guiding her eastward — toward the river, toward the last key, toward a grave dug in the capital itself. Somewhere above, she knew, the thirteenth moon was swelling toward fullness. And somewhere closer than comfort, she could almost believe she heard footsteps that were not theirs, following the same buried road.
