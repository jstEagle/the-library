## 15. The Third Key Is a Grave

The capital had a way of pretending it had always been itself.

Dain felt it every time he came back — the way the streets lay over older streets like skin grown over scar tissue, smooth and unbothered, as if nothing underneath had ever bled. He had lived in this city for eleven years and only in the last two moons learned that he had been walking over his mother's grave the whole time.

Not in the Necropolis, where the honored dead went with their names cut deep into basalt so the stone would remember them when their families could not. Not in the paupers' field beyond the tanneries, where the unclaimed were folded into lime and anonymity. Somewhere inside the walls themselves. Inside the city that had swallowed her country whole and then swallowed her too, quietly, without ceremony, without even the dignity of a hole in official ground.

*The third key is a grave,* Yara had said, reading the map's last inscription by candlelight while rain needled the shutters of the safehouse above the old chandlery. *The grave of the one who sang.* And Dain had known before she finished the sentence. He had known the way you know a wound is yours before you look down at it.

Their mother was buried in the capital. Their mother, who had died in Vessany — or what had been Vessany — in the last season before the Edict fell like an axe, buried by their father and their brother Corren in a place no decree could reach, because their father had understood something that took Dain twenty years to understand: that the crown did not merely erase countries. It erased graves. It erased the ground where grief could stand.

So their father had hidden her inside the enemy's own body. Buried her in the capital, under its stones, among its living, where the Inquisitors would never think to dig because no one was supposed to be there at all.

Now Dain stood in the Ropewalk district at dusk, hands in his coat pockets, feeling the weight of the spade wrapped in sacking across his back like a yoke.

"You're staring at a wall," Yara said beside him. She had her hood up and her hair tucked away, and she looked, in the failing light, like any other woman coming home from work — which was, he had learned, exactly how she preferred to be seen, and exactly the opposite of what she was.

"I'm staring at what's behind the wall," Dain said.

Behind the wall was a garden. A long, narrow strip of green wedged between a dyer's warehouse and the back of a counting-house, the kind of forgotten plot that cities grow the way old men grow moss — not planted, simply permitted. There was a fig tree gone wild against the south wall, a stand of nettles, a collapsed cold frame furred with lichen. And beneath the fig tree, if Corren's letter told true — the letter Dain had carried for two years without understanding it, folded into the lining of his pack through every mile of this cursed journey — there was a grave with no marker but a ring of river stones, and under the river stones, a locket of pressed ember-flowers, and inside the locket, the last key of memory, sung into metal by a woman who had known she was dying and had chosen to leave her voice behind instead of her bones.

"Corren chose well," Yara said softly. "A dyer's yard. No one looks at a dyer's yard."

"He didn't choose it for the hiding." Dain's throat was tight. "He chose it because of the wall. Look at the mortar."

She looked. She was quick — quicker than him at this kind of seeing, though he'd never say so. "The lower courses are older," she said. "Vessani bond-pattern. Herringbone."

"The wall was already here when they built the warehouse around it. This was a piece of home. Someone salvaged it — a border-stone wall from a farmstead, maybe, or a churchyard. Our father heard about it through the network and bought the lease on the garden so he could bury her against Vessani stone." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He put her back together with a piece of the country. Then he died, and Corren kept the lease paid through the dyer's guild for twenty years, and neither of them ever told me because I was the one who left."

Yara said nothing. That was one of the things he had come, grudgingly, to trust about her: she knew when silence was the kinder gift.

* * *

They went over the wall at moonrise, because going through the gate meant signing the dyer's ledger, and the ledger meant names, and names were the currency Sarel's people traded in now. Dain went first, boots finding the old handholds — and stopped, halfway up, struck dumb by the realization that the handholds were worn smooth. Worn by use. His father's hands. Corren's hands. Twenty years of visits, up and over, kneeling in the nettles, pulling weeds from an unmarked grave while the city slept on top of them, dreaming its stolen dreams.

He dropped down into the garden and stood very still until Yara landed beside him.

The moon was three nights short of full — three nights short of the thirteenth turn, the map said, the single hinge on which the door swung open and then closed for another age. They had crossed half the country to be here, had lost the night market to fire, had lost Tammas — Dain still could not say that name without tasting ash — had lost each other briefly to Sarel's cells and found each other again through Ovid's treachery, and now there were only these three nights, and this garden, and this spade.

"It has to be tonight?" Yara asked. She knew the answer. She asked anyway, he thought, because asking gave him the chance to hear himself commit.

"It has to be tonight. If we're seen digging tomorrow, with the moon full and the door hours from opening—" He shook his head. "Tonight. While it's still just treason and not prophecy."

"Sacrilege too," Yara said quietly. "Don't forget sacrilege. Digging up the dead is the oldest crime there is."

"Then we'll be criminals in the oldest way." He unwrapped the spade. "Stand watch. And Yara—" He made himself look at her. "If I can't do it. If I get down there and I can't—"

"You'll do it," she said. "Because your brother trusted you with it, and because your mother left her voice in that locket on purpose, and because she'd have dug up anyone who stood between her song and the world." She turned toward the wall, bow strung, and added, not unkindly: "Dig, Dain."

* * *

The earth came up soft.

That was the thing he hadn't prepared for — that after twenty years it would still be soft, worked once and never again, held loose by roots and rain and the fig tree's sheltering canopy. As if the ground itself had agreed to keep her gently. He cut the first spade-depth and smelled it: loam and leaf-rot and, faintly, unmistakably, ember-flowers. The scent of Vessany in high summer, the scent his memory had been starving for since he was nine years old, rising out of the dark like breath.

He knelt in the pit and dug with his hands.

Stone by stone he went down — past the frost line, past the clay seam, past the layer where the soil turned darker and sweeter — and all the while the city murmured around him, oblivious. A cart rattling on the Ringway. A drunk singing three streets over. The great clock of the Assize Hall tolling the second hour, the sound rolling over the rooftops the way it rolled over everything, indifferent, imperial, as if time itself belonged to the crown. Dain dug against the clock. Every bell-toll was a measure of how much longer they had, and every shovelful was a small mutiny, and he understood suddenly why his father had done it this way — why hiding her here, under the empire's own floorboards, had been worth the risk. Because every day the city rang its bells over her head and did not know. Because she got to lie beneath the conquerors' sleep and wait. Waiting was a kind of victory, when you were patient enough. His family had been patient for twenty years.

His fingers hit cloth.

He stopped. The whole world narrowed to the touch of his own hands — wet-rotted linen, the weave of a burial shroud, and beneath it, when he brushed the soil aside with a tenderness that surprised him, the shape of bone. Small bones. Her hands had been small; he remembered them kneading bread, braiding Corren's hair, pressing a cool palm to his fevered forehead during the sickness that had finally taken her. He remembered, and the remembering hurt worse than any blow Sarel's interrogators had landed, because these memories were his own, unsuppressed, unpaid-for — the Edict had taken countries but it had never managed to take this, and he had spent years wishing it would, and now, kneeling in her grave, he would have burned the whole Edict to ash for one more hour of it.

"Dain." Yara's voice, low, from the wall. "Lamp at the end of the lane. Moving slow."

"Keep watching. I'm nearly—" His voice broke. He cleared his throat. "I'm nearly there."

He lifted the shroud's edge away from her sternum, hating himself, blessing himself, both at once, the two feelings fused like solder. And there, where her folded hands had held it for twenty years, was the locket.

Brass, like the map. Tarnished green-black with age, but intact, the clasp sealed with wax that had hardened to amber. Ember-flowers pressed under glass on its face — five-petaled, impossibly red, flowers that should have crumbled to dust decades ago and instead looked as if they'd been picked that morning. Living color in dead metal. His mother's craft. She had been a hedge-charmer before the Edict, before any of it — small magic, kitchen magic, the kind the crown called superstition right up until it started hanging people for it — and she had pressed a spell into these petals that was still working, still burning, after twenty years underground.

*Dain.*

He flinched so hard he cracked his elbow against the pit wall. The voice had come from everywhere and nowhere — from the locket, from the soil, from inside his own skull — a woman's voice, warm and worn, singing more than speaking.

"Dain," Yara whispered above him. "Tell me you heard that."

"I heard it." His hands were shaking around the locket. "It's her. Gods, Yara, it's *her*."

"Get up here. Now. Two Watchmen at the corner of the warehouse — they've stopped. They're talking."

He climbed out of his mother's grave with the locket clenched in his fist, and the moment his boots left the pit the voice faded, as if it needed the nearness of her bones to carry. He filled the grave in fast, blind, savage, packing the earth down with the flat of the spade, scattering nettle-cuttings and fig-leaves over the disturbed ground until the garden wore its old face again. Below him, through six feet of soil, his mother settled back into her patience. He had stolen her voice and given her back her silence, and he did not know which of those things she would forgive.

* * *

They crouched in the shadow of the cold frame while the Watchmen argued their way along the lane and past the gate, lantern-light sliding across the wall like water across glass, and then moved on, bored, cold, human. Dain let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the second hour bell.

Yara took the locket from him — carefully, the way she handled everything, as if the world were made of things that could remember being mishandled — and turned it in the moonlight. "Wax seal. We shouldn't break it here."

"No." He was looking at the fig tree, at the wall, at the ring of river stones he'd uncovered and covered again. "At the hedge. When the map opens. All three keys together, at the door." He paused. "You should know what I heard. Before we go any further. You should know what she said."

"What?"

"My name." He laughed again, that same broken sound. "Twenty years in the ground and the first thing she does is say my name. Not Corren's. Mine. The one who left."

Yara was quiet a moment. Then: "Maybe because you're the one who came back."

He looked at her. In the moonlight her face was half shadow, and he thought — not for the first time, but with new weight — that she had lost a friend to fire for this, a career, a name, a whole life built on careful double-dealing, and she had never once asked whether it was worth it, because she already knew it wasn't, and was doing it anyway. That was the difference between them, he was beginning to understand. He had come back for his brother. She had never had anyone to come back for, and had decided, somewhere along the way, to become someone who would.

"The third key," he said. "That's the last of them. Chamberlain's border-memory. Your song. My mother's grave. Three keys, one door, three nights." He shouldered the spade, wrapped again in its sacking, heavier now than it had been on the way in, though it weighed the same. "And Sarel knows we're in the capital. Ovid said her riders reached the city yesterday."

"Then we move tonight," Yara said. "There's a place under the palace gardens — the old nursery tunnels. If the map is honest, the hedge grows somewhere above them."

"And if the map isn't honest?"

"Then we're traitors standing in a tunnel under the palace with a dead woman's locket," she said, "and honestly, Dain, at this point, what's one more crime?"

He almost smiled. Almost. Behind them the garden settled into its long patience, the fig tree keeping its vigil over six feet of undisturbed earth, and ahead of them the palace rose black against the stars, its gardens sleeping, its hedges dreaming — and somewhere in that green labyrinth, grown through the heart of the empire that had murdered a country, a door was waiting for the thirteenth moon.

Dain touched the locket once, through his coat, over his heart.

"Hold on, Mama," he whispered. "We're almost there."

From far below the ground — or perhaps only from inside his own blood — he thought he heard her begin to sing.
