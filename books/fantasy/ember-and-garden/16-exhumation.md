## 16. Exhumation

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as it *arrived* — a pale disc swollen past its proper size, hanging low over the capital like an eye that had been closed for twenty years and had finally decided to look.

Yara felt it in her teeth before she saw it. She had been tasting the air all evening without meaning to — an old habit from the palace kitchens, where she'd learned to read danger in the faintest bitterness — and the night tasted of iron and turned earth and something sweeter underneath, like flowers pressed too long between the pages of a book.

"It's close," Dain said beside her. He stood at the mouth of the alley with his hood up, watching the moon the way other men watched fires. "Two nights. Maybe three."

"Then we dig tomorrow."

"We dig *tonight*." He turned, and in the thin light his face was all angles and exhaustion. He hadn't slept properly since the seed vault; none of them had. The vault's impossible green still clung to their clothes — a smell of growing things that no one in this city was supposed to remember existed. "Sarel knows about the grave by now. She has to. The locket is the last key, and Sarel doesn't leave last keys lying in the ground."

Yara leaned against the cold brick and let herself feel, for one unguarded moment, how far they had come and how little of them remained. Her stall was ash in the night market. Her stock of charms — months of careful work, each one stitched with a name someone wasn't allowed to say — had burned with it. Old Maren was dead, her body carried out of the smoke by strangers who never learned what she'd died protecting. And now the only thing left of all that loss was a map that had finally begun to trust her, and a grave in the middle of the capital that two people were about to commit treason upon.

"The Vessany quarter," she said quietly. "It's not even a quarter anymore. It's the Fountain District. There are gardens there. Children play there."

"There's a garden there," Dain corrected. "One garden, behind the old customs house, where nobody goes because the roses grow wrong." His voice caught on the word *wrong* and he steadied it. "Our mother chose the spot herself. She said if Vessany was going to be erased, then at least one piece of it should sleep where the erasers would have to walk over it every day and never know."

The moonlight made the alley silver and sharp. Somewhere across the city, a Watch bell rang the third hour, and Yara counted the hours left to them the way she once counted drops of poison on her tongue: precisely, and with a strange calm that was really just fear wearing its best clothes.

"All right," she said. "Tonight."

* * *

They went into the Fountain District as beggars, because beggars were invisible. Yara had dressed the part well — she'd spent years learning how the poor moved through the city's edges, back when she was buying charm-components from the rag-pickers — and Dain had simply stopped washing, which required no artistry at all.

The customs house was a ruin of pale stone, its windows bricked over, its lintel carved with the crowned sun of the Edict. Behind it lay the garden Dain had described: a rectangle of dark hedges and untended beds, hemmed in by high walls, forgotten by everyone except the city's gardeners, who had long ago given up trying to make anything grow there and simply locked the gate.

But things grew there. That was the trouble. The roses climbed the walls in thickets that no pruning could tame, and their blooms were the color of coals — deep red shading into orange at the heart, as if each flower held a spark it refused to surrender. Ember-roses. Yara knew the name now; the map had taught her, whispering it into her mind during the long weeks of travel like a patient tutor. They grew nowhere else in the world, because the world had been ordered to forget them, and flowers, unlike people, obeyed no decrees.

"They kept growing," Dain murmured. He stood inside the gate — Yara had picked the lock in four seconds flat, a skill she declined to explain — and stared at the coal-colored blooms with an expression she couldn't read. "Twenty years. Nobody watered them. Nobody tended them. And they just—" He touched one petal and flinched as though it had burned him, though it hadn't. "She's here," he said roughly. "Under all this. She's actually here."

Yara gave him a moment. Then she took the spade from under her cloak and drove it into the earth beneath the largest rosebush, and the sound of it — the wet, honest sound of steel parting soil — seemed impossibly loud in the sleeping district.

They dug in shifts. The ground was soft, unnaturally soft, as though it had been waiting to be opened, and Yara tried not to think about what that meant. The moon climbed. The ember-roses shed petals around them like slow embers falling. Twice, Watch patrols passed along the street beyond the wall, lantern-light sliding across the hedge tops, and twice they froze with dirt on their hands and held their breath until the light moved on.

Three feet down, the spade struck wood.

Dain dropped into the hole beside her and cleared the soil away with his bare hands, fast and desperate, and then the coffin lid was exposed — plain pine, unmarked, exactly as anonymous as an erased woman deserved. For a moment neither of them moved.

"You should do it," Yara said.

Dain shook his head. "You're the key-bearer. The map chose you." His jaw worked. "And I don't— I can't be the one who opens it. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

So Yara knelt on the coffin lid of a woman she had never met, in a country that officially did not exist, under a moon that came once in a generation, and pried up the boards.

There was very little inside. A shroud gone grey with time. Bones arranged with care, the hands folded. And resting in the hollow of the folded hands, exactly where a mother would keep it, a small brass locket, tarnished green, its chain wound twice around the finger bones as if the dead woman had refused, even in death, to let it go.

Yara lifted it free. The chain slid from the finger bones with a whisper, and the whole garden seemed to exhale — every rose on every wall releasing its scent at once, coal-sweet and overwhelming, and the locket in her palm grew warm as a living thing.

"Open it," Dain whispered.

She worked the clasp. Inside, pressed flat and perfect after twenty years underground, were three ember-flowers — small five-petaled stars, still faintly luminous, still burning their slow private fire. And beneath them, etched into the brass in a hand Yara recognized from the map's inscription, a single line:

*What was buried living will rise remembering.*

The third key settled into place behind her eyes like a coin dropping into a lock. She felt the map stir against her ribs, felt it open — truly open, for the first time — unfurling streets and orchards and rivers across the inside of her mind, and at the center of it all, a door.

A door hidden in a hedge. In the palace gardens themselves.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it was *there* all along—"

Lanterns bloomed at the gate.

* * *

Later — much later, in the cells of memory she would carry forever — Yara would try to reconstruct those next minutes and find that they came back to her in fragments, sharp as glass.

The lanterns: six of them, maybe eight, arranged in a crescent at the garden gate with a precision that was itself a signature. No Watch patrol ever stood like that. These figures wore grey, not black, and they did not shout or draw weapons. They simply waited, the way a net waits, already closed.

Sarel stepped through the gap in the hedge as if she owned it, and perhaps she did. The Inquisitor General looked smaller by lantern-light than she did in rumor — a narrow woman in plain grey wool, silver-streaked hair pinned severely back, a face built for patience. Only her eyes betrayed her: they were fixed on the open grave, on the locket in Yara's hand, with an intensity that was almost hunger and almost grief.

"The Vessany lullaby-key, the chamberlain's border-memory, and now the flowers," Sarel said. Her voice was quiet, conversational, terrible. "Do you know how long I have watched for someone to gather all three? Twenty years, Whitlock. I have read every file, interviewed every survivor of the erasure who was permitted to remain alive. I have waited for the thirteenth moon knowing that someday, someone would try to open that door." She tilted her head. "I confess I expected a scholar. Or a fanatic. Not a food-taster."

"You know my name."

"I know everything about you. It's my profession." Sarel took one step closer, and the lanterns advanced with her. "Which is why I'm going to offer you something better than you deserve. Put down the locket. Walk away. You'll be given a new name, a new city, money enough to open ten stalls. The brother dies, of course — he's seen too much — but you, you get to live a long comfortable life having never found out what's behind that door."

"And why would you want me to walk away?" Yara asked, though her heart was hammering. "If the garden's just plague-magic, if erasure was mercy — why not let us open it and prove yourself right?"

Something flickered across Sarel's face, quick as a fish under ice. "Because I am not certain," she said, and the honesty of it was more frightening than any threat. "And I cannot permit myself uncertainty. Not in my position. Not with what I've done." She extended one gloved hand. "The locket. Now."

It was Dain who moved.

He didn't announce it, didn't cry out — he simply stepped sideways between Yara and the Inquisitors, and swept his arm through the nearest lantern, and suddenly the garden was full of fire and shouting and the crash of bodies against hedge. Yara saw him go down under three grey cloaks, saw him twist and drive an elbow into someone's throat, saw blood — whose, she couldn't tell — black in the lantern-light.

"*Run!*" he roared at her. "The door's in the *palace*, Yara — run, you stupid, brave—"

An Inquisitor's baton came down. Dain folded. But even folding, even falling, he twisted toward the grave, toward the roses, and Yara understood: he was making sure they took him looking at his mother's resting place, so they would know, whoever they were, whatever they believed, that this ground mattered. That she mattered.

Yara ran.

She went over the wall with the locket clenched in her fist and its chain cutting a line across her palm, and she dropped into the customs house yard, and she ran through streets that blurred past — fountain square, the tanners' lane, the long stair down to the canal — while behind her the garden blazed with lanterns and the city's bells began, at last, to wake.

She did not let herself think about Dain's face when the baton fell. She filed it away with Maren's body and the ashes of her stall, in the room inside her where she kept the cost of every step forward, and she ran.

* * *

She stopped running at the edge of the Palace Ward, where the walls turned from brick to honeyed stone and the guards' lanterns made pools of gold along the pavement. Here she became someone else — not a beggar now but a laundress, sleeves rolled, basket borrowed from a doorway, moving with the tired authority of a woman who had somewhere to be. The ward gates admitted servants at all hours; the palace ate constantly and digested poorly.

Inside, the palace gardens spread out below the terraces in their formal perfection: clipped yew, raked gravel, fountains that sang to no one. And somewhere in all that order, grown through it like a splinter through skin, was a hedge that led out of the world.

Yara knelt by the ornamental pool and opened her hand.

The locket lay in her palm, warm, the pressed flowers glowing faintly through the tarnished brass. Three keys now lived in her — the face, the song, the flowers — and the map was fully awake, unfolding itself through her mind with a clarity that almost hurt. She could see the garden's location as if it were drawn in light on the gravel before her: past the orangery, past the Queen's rosary walk, in the oldest corner of the grounds where the hedges had been allowed to grow wild since before the Edict.

Where the hedge had been allowed to grow wild. Where no gardener dared prune.

Two nights until the thirteenth moon. Dain in Sarel's cells, or worse. Sarel certain to guess, within hours, where the map would lead — because Sarel had waited twenty years for exactly this, and people who wait that long do not lose track of the door.

Yara closed her fist around the locket and felt its small heat pulse against her fingers, steady as a heartbeat, patient as roots.

"Hold on," she whispered — to Dain, to the dead woman in the broken grave, to the whole buried country waiting on the other side of a hedge. "Hold on. I'm coming."

Above the palace, the thirteenth moon swelled against the dark, one night closer to full, and everywhere in the gardens the ember-roses — planted decades ago by hands that hoped, in corners no decree could reach — began, very softly, to glow.
