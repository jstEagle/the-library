## 17. The Door in the Hedge

The palace gardens at night were a country of their own.

Yara had walked them before — once, years ago, as part of the tasters' procession on the Queen's Naming Day, her hands folded and her mouth shut, one gray thread in a rope of gray threads. She had seen them then only as a servant sees: gravel to be kept off hems, roses to be admired quickly and without lingering. Now she moved through them like a thief through a cathedral, and everything she had dismissed was suddenly, unbearably alive with meaning.

The moon overhead was wrong. She had been counting its faces since Dain first spoke the inscription aloud in the flooded dark of the Lethe Canal — *once, at the thirteenth moon turn* — and she knew, the way a taster knows poison by the shape of it on her tongue, that this moon did not belong to any calendar the court kept. It hung too low, too large, its light the color of struck brass, and where it fell the shadows lay at angles the garden's lamps could not explain. The thirteenth moon. The moon that had no name because no one had been permitted to remember why it needed one.

Three nights remained. Perhaps fewer. Sarel had Dain, and Sarel had the locket, and Sarel had men enough to ring the palace grounds twice over — but Sarel did not have what Yara carried folded against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping bird.

The map had not opened for her in six weeks of trying. It had shown her burning fields, always burning fields, wheat and orchards and hedgerows going up in orange ruin while somewhere beyond the frame of the vision something vast exhaled smoke. *The garden keeps score*, the dying man had whispered, and Yara had begun to fear the score included her: a woman who sold memory for coppers, who had spent ten years unpicking the Edict one charm at a time and still could not make a dead country's map trust her with its door.

She stopped at the garden's heart, where the paths met in a star around an old fountain, dry these twenty years, its basin furred with moss. And there she saw it.

A hedge.

It should not have startled her. The royal gardens were full of hedges — clipped yew corridors, boxwood mazes, topiary peacocks frozen mid-strut. But this hedge stood apart from all of them, at the edge of the fountain-star where no gardener would plant anything, and it was *wrong* in a way her body understood before her mind did. The other hedges were obedient. This one grew like weather. Its branches rose fifteen feet and folded inward at the top, curving toward each other like two hands about to close in prayer, and its leaves — she stepped closer, breath held — its leaves were not green at all but a deep ember-red, the red of coals banked for morning, and they gave off a heat she could feel on her face from three paces away.

No frost touched it. Everywhere else the autumn cold silvered the grass; here the ground steamed faintly, softly, as if the earth beneath were breathing.

"Oh," Yara said aloud, to no one, her voice very small in the enormous quiet. "Oh, you've been here all along."

Hidden in plain sight. Grown through the palace gardens themselves — through the seat of the crown that had decreed Vessany out of existence, so close to the throne that the King's own windows must have looked down on it. She thought of every gardener who had pruned around it and never seen it, every lady-in-waiting who had strolled past it and felt only a vague warmth, a vague unease, and turned away. That was what erasure looked like when it was done well. Not absence. A thing so present it became invisible, the way the tongue forgets itself until it aches.

Her hands were shaking. She made herself kneel at the hedge's base, made herself unwrap the map with the same steady motions she used at the tasting table, where trembling meant death. The brass was cold despite the hedge's warmth. She laid it flat on the moss between her knees.

"Please," she said. It was not a word she used often. "I'm not asking you to open for me. I'm asking you to open for *them*. For everyone you kept. I'm the only one left carrying it, and I know I'm not much — I'm a food-taster, I sell charms out of a blanket, I've lied to everyone I love — but Ovid gave me his key, and Dain bled for the second, and there's a locket full of pressed flowers sitting in an Inquisitor's strongbox that I mean to get back if it kills me. So." Her voice cracked and she let it. "So please."

The brass warmed under her palms.

Not gradually — all at once, like a hand closing around hers, and Yara gasped as the etched lines began to move. Not the burning fields this time. Rivers uncoiled across the metal like veins filling with blood; borders drew themselves and then, deliberately, like a hand wiping a slate, erased themselves again — and in the erasing she saw them, saw the shape of the vanished country, Vessany whole and entire, its coastline stitched along the inside of the map's border, its capital marked with a small stamped flower. An ember-flower. The same bloom pressed into the locket in Sarel's keeping.

Then the map opened.

Not unfolded — *opened*, the way a chest opens, the way a mouth opens, the flat brass splitting along a seam she had never found in six weeks of searching, and from within it rose a smell that undid her completely: woodsmoke and ripe apples and rain on warm stone, bread baking, cut hay, the particular green smell of a river in summer. A country's whole air, pressed flat for thirty years, exhaling into the night. Yara knelt there and wept without deciding to, tears dropping onto the brass, and where they fell the metal drank them in and brightened.

Inside the opened map lay a single seed. Black, glossy, no larger than a peppercorn.

She understood without being told. Some knowledge arrived in the bones before the head, and this came to her the way the taste of bitter almond came: instantly, totally. The seed was the last key. The three keys of memory had opened the map; the seed would open the door. And the door —

Yara lifted her head and looked at the hedge, and the hedge looked back.

There was no other way to describe it. The branches had shifted in the moments since she knelt, folding back from a space at their center, and in that space — framed in ember-red leaves, lit from within by a glow like sunset through honey — stood a door. Not a gate in the hedge. A *door*: paneled, iron-hinged, ancient, standing free among the living branches as though the hedge had grown around it for centuries the way a tree grows around a fence-post. Its wood was black oak gone silver at the edges. In its center, set where a handle might be, was a small round socket shaped exactly like nothing Yara had ever seen, except that when she looked down at the seed in the map, the seed fit it perfectly.

The thirteenth moon poured its brass light down through the gap in the branches and fell across the door's face, and in that light Yara could see, faint as breath on glass, marks scored into the wood. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Tiny scratches, tally-marks, names.

She crossed the steaming grass on her knees, because her legs would not hold her, and put her fingers to the door.

The marks were names. She realized it slowly, tracing one line after another, each scratch a different hand, some careful, some clawed, some the wobbly effort of a child. Names cut into the wood over years — over decades — by people who had come to this door and could not open it, and who had wanted, more than anything, to leave proof that they had existed. *Maren.* *Olek.* *Vessany endures.* *We are here. We were here.* One line, deeper than the rest, cut with something sharper than a fingernail: *If you can read this, remember us out loud.*

Yara pressed her forehead against the cool silvered oak and sobbed like a child, alone in the middle of the enemy's own garden, with the whole sleeping palace at her back and the whole buried country under her hand.

* * *

She did not know how long she knelt there. Long enough that her knees went numb against the warm earth. Long enough for the moon to move the width of two fingers across the sky, and when it did, the light through the branch-gap slid along the door and caught the tally-marks differently — and Yara saw, with a lurch, that the newest scratches were fresher than thirty years old.

Some were days old. One was hours old, the wood-pale wound of it still sharp-edged, and beside it, in a cramped hand she recognized from a hundred confiscated charm-notes and a hundred Watch reports she'd read over the years in the tasters' quarters — because a taster learned to read everything — someone had written: *S. was here. She does not remember why she weeps.*

Yara sat back on her heels, cold walking up her spine despite the hedge's heat.

Sarel. The Inquisitor General had stood at this door. Had touched it. Had cut her name into it — or someone had cut it for her — and had wept without knowing why, and had walked away and built thirty years of law on top of the weeping. Yara thought of Sarel's face across the interrogation table, that terrible serene conviction, *erasure was mercy* — and understood suddenly, horribly, that mercy was the story Sarel had to believe, because the alternative was the door, and the door remembered.

The garden keeps score. It had been keeping score of Sarel too.

Yara got up. Her body protested; she ignored it. There was no time for kneeling and no time for weeping, though she suspected she would do both again before this ended. Three nights. Two keys' worth of work already spent, one seed in her pocket, one friend in Sarel's cells, one locket of pressed flowers locked in a strongbox somewhere in the Inquisitors' wing — and the door would open once, at the thirteenth moon turn, and never again.

Once. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Whatever she chose at that door, she would choose it a single time, in front of witnesses or utterly alone, and there would be no second turning of any moon to undo it.

She wrapped the map carefully, tucked it away, and made herself walk the perimeter of the hedge, learning it the way she'd learned the layout of the palace kitchens: by the feet, practically, without romance. The hedge stood in a shallow fold of ground behind the old orangery, screened on three sides by walls and on the fourth by a yew walk gone leggy and untrimmed — the one corner of the royal gardens where neglect had grown like a conspiracy. Someone had tended this hiding for a long time. Or perhaps the hiding tended itself. Magic outlawed for thirty years, and yet here was a hedge that no edict could see.

From the orangery's roofline she heard, far off, the changing of the watch — boots on gravel, lantern-light swinging pale along the garden wall. She flattened herself into the shadow of the yew walk and held still, a taster's stillness, the stillness of a woman whose survival had always depended on being overlooked, and felt the old loneliness rise in her like water in a well.

Alone. That was the truth of this chapter of it, whatever came after. Ovid was safe and shattered and could help her no further. Dain was in a cell below the Inquisitors' wing, and every hour she spent planning his rescue was an hour the thirteenth moon climbed closer, and every hour she spent reaching the door was an hour Dain climbed closer to whatever Sarel did to people who knew about gardens. The network that had burned with the night market was scattered ash. Old Maretta was dead covering her escape, and Yara carried that the way she carried the poison-residue in her blood: invisibly, permanently, part of the body now.

And still the door waited, glowing softly in its cage of ember leaves, patient as only a thing that has waited thirty years can be patient.

"I'll come back," she whispered to it. "With the flowers. With him, if I can. Don't open for anyone else."

The leaves stirred, though there was no wind. Warmth washed over her, brief as a hand on the shoulder, and Yara chose — deliberately, the way she chose to swallow poison — to believe it was answer enough.

* * *

Getting out of the palace grounds was easier than getting in, which frightened her more than the reverse would have. Too easy. The watch rotations had gaps in them tonight, gaps shaped like a net being quietly drawn closed, and twice she caught the glint of lanterns positioned not to search but to *herd*. Sarel was not looking for her in the city anymore. Sarel had guessed where the door was — of course she had, she had stood at it and wept — and had decided, with that terrible patience of hers, to let the mouse run to the cheese.

Yara slipped out through the drain-cut in the south wall that she'd scouted two nights ago, dropped into the reek of the canal-road, and pulled her hood low. Behind her the palace rose against the sky, its windows gold, its gardens dark, and somewhere in that darkness a door with a thousand names cut into it glowed like a coal banked for morning.

Three nights. She counted them as she walked, the way she counted drops when testing wine. One night to reach the Inquisitors' wing. One night for Dain, for the locket, for the flowers pressed thirty years ago by a mother singing a song that was a key. And the third night —

The third night the thirteenth moon would stand directly over the palace gardens, and a door would open once, and Yara Whitlock, royal food-taster, seller of small charms, keeper of other people's memories, would stand before it and decide what a country was worth.

In the reeking dark of the canal-road, she put her hand flat against her ribs, over the map, and felt it beat.
