## 18. Inside the Ember Garden

The door closed behind her without a sound.

Yara had expected a slam, or at least the rustle of hedge-leaves knitting shut — some announcement that she had crossed from one world into another. Instead there was only the sudden absence of wind, and the smell: warm stone, ripe fruit, woodsmoke that had never quite gone out. She stood on a cobbled lane under a sky the color of late honey, and the lane ran downhill between two rows of houses with blue-shuttered windows, and every one of those windows held a light.

She knew this street. She had never seen it in her life, and she knew it the way you know a lullaby sung to you before memory began — the crooked third house with the iron boot-scraper shaped like a heron, the well at the bend with its rope worn pale, the smell of bread from an oven that had no baker. Vessany. The name rose in her like water finding its level. The country that did not exist. The country the Edict had eaten down to the bone and then eaten the bone.

"Hello?" Her voice came back to her off the shuttered walls, thinner than she'd meant it.

No answer. But the lights in the windows brightened, very slightly, all together — as if the street itself had turned toward her, attentive.

Yara pressed the brass map flat against her ribs, where it had grown warm as a sleeping bird, and walked downhill.

* * *

The lane opened onto a market square, and Yara stopped so abruptly that her heel clicked against the cobblestones.

It was a night market. Of course it was; the garden had taken the shape of what she carried into it, and what she carried was stalls and lanterns and the low murmur of trade done after lawful hours. But this market was Vessany's own, preserved the way amber preserves the fly: awnings striped in colors she had no names for, a fountain at the center carved with leaping fish whose mouths still ran with clear water, and along the edges — along every edge — the people.

They were not ghosts. That was the first thing she understood, standing very still among them. Ghosts flicker. These figures were steady as painted portraits, caught mid-gesture: a woman haggling over a basket of quinces, a boy chasing a hoop with a stick, two old men playing tiles outside a tavern whose sign read THE DROWNED BELL. They did not breathe. Their eyes, when Yara dared look into them, held the fixed shine of someone remembering rather than seeing. But they were not faded, not worn smooth by centuries. They were *kept.*

"The garden keeps score," the dying man had whispered, pressing the map into her bleeding hands. She had thought it a riddle, or a curse. Now, watching a mother tuck a blanket around a child who would never wake, Yara understood it was neither. It was a promise. Everything erased above ground had been written down here, stroke by stroke, life by life.

"You're new."

She spun. An old woman sat on the fountain's rim, shelling peas into a bowl that never filled. Unlike the others, her eyes moved. They found Yara's face and stayed there, sharp and brown and amused.

"You can talk," Yara said. "You're — not like them."

"I'm the part of the garden that does the keeping." The old woman tipped a pea into the bowl. It vanished before it landed. "The rest are the kept. You'd be the map-bearer, then. Thirteen moon-turns, and here you come, right on time." She looked Yara up and down with frank appraisal. "Tired. Frightened. Smells of canal water and burnt stall-wood. They always send us someone half-drowned."

"They?"

"Keepers. There's been one in every age since the erasure. The garden calls, and someone answers, and they walk through that door and don't walk out again." The woman's tone was mild, almost kind, which made it worse. "I was the last. Before me, a stonemason. Before him, a nun who'd forgotten her own order's name. We keep the score. We tend the streets. And when the door opens, we ask the newcomer if they'd like to stay."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," the old woman agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. Walk first. The garden likes to be walked."

* * *

So Yara walked.

She walked out of the square past the fountain-fish and up a hill where orchards climbed in silvered terraces — apple trees heavy with fruit that glowed faintly at the core, like coals wrapped in cloth. Ember-trees. She recognized them now from the locket Dain had dug out of his mother's grave: pressed flowers the color of banked fire, petals that had held their color for twenty years because they had been picked here, where nothing decayed and nothing left.

She reached into the locket — she still wore it, though the map had been the true key — and shook three brittle blossoms into her palm. When she held them toward the nearest tree, the tree shivered. Every ember-flower on its branches opened at once, a soft sound like a hundred small sighs, and the whole terrace lit gold.

"Oh," Yara said, to no one.

The orchard path led her onward, and the garden unspooled around her like a story told by someone who loved every word. A schoolhouse with slates still chalked in a child's uneven hand: *Vessany has four rivers and one sea.* A bathhouse steaming gently under a glass roof. A shrine where hundreds of small clay lamps sat in niches, each one labeled with a name, each one burning — the recorded dead, or perhaps the recorded living; here there seemed to be no difference. She passed a print-shop, a granary, a wedding-clothes maker's window with a gown of gray silk waiting for a bride who had been unwritten from the world.

And everywhere, the kept ones. Frozen mid-life, luminous, patient. A fiddler with his bow raised. A girl asleep over her embroidery. A man on a balcony looking at the honey-colored sky with his mouth open, mid-laugh, forever.

Yara had spent her whole adult life swallowing poisons for other people. She knew something about what it cost to hold harm inside yourself so it couldn't reach anyone else. Looking at the frozen fiddler, she felt a kinship so fierce it frightened her. This entire country was doing what she did. It had swallowed the erasure. It had held the poison of a royal decree inside its walls for twenty years so that somewhere, somehow, the harm might not be final.

Her eyes were wet. She let them be. There was no one here to see weakness except the garden, and the garden already knew everything about her — she could feel it now, a pressure like sunlight on the back of her neck, curious and gentle and vast.

* * *

At the top of the highest terrace stood a house unlike the others: small, whitewashed, with a garden gate of green iron and smoke rising from its chimney. Smoke. Real, moving, living smoke. Yara's heart knocked strangely against her ribs.

Inside, the rooms were simple and clean and utterly familiar. The kettle on the stove was the kettle from her mother's kitchen in the river quarter, lost to debt collectors when Yara was nine. The chair by the window was the chair she'd sat in at her charm-stall, the one with the wobbly leg she'd never fixed. On the table lay a spread of her own charms — memory-charms, little knotted cords and wax-sealed buttons — arranged in neat rows, priced in her own handwriting.

"It shows you your own life," said the old keeper, appearing in the doorway with her bowl of never-filling peas. "First thing it does. So you understand what's being offered."

"Offered?" Yara's voice came out flat. "By whom? What is this place, truly?"

The old woman set down her bowl. For a moment her amusement fell away, and underneath it Yara saw something ancient and exhausted and terribly sad.

"This is Vessany," she said. "All of it. Every street, every orchard, every mind. When the Edict was pronounced, the last mages of the country did the only spell left to them. They could not stop the decree, so they made the country too large to erase. They folded it inward. Every person, every memory, every field and bell-tower — written into a seed, and the seed planted behind a door, and the door hidden in a hedge in the conquerors' own gardens, where no one would think to look." She smiled thinly. "Pride of the Vessani. Hiding the treasure in the thief's pocket."

"And the people outside? The ones who forgot?"

"Forgetting is the price of the folding. To hide a country, you must take its memory out of every head that holds it. The chamberlain who signed the decree. The soldiers who burned the fields. The mothers who sang the songs without knowing what they sang." The old woman's eyes went distant. "They walk around with holes in them. Most never notice. Some do. Some spend their lives reaching for the shape of the absence and calling it grief they can't explain."

Yara thought of Chamberlain Ovid, weeping over a border he could not remember drawing. Thought of Dain, humming a lullaby whose notes were a key. Thought of Sarel, cold and certain, so certain, and wondered what hole sat at the center of *her.*

"And the offer," Yara said. "Say it plainly."

The keeper folded her hands. "When the thirteenth moon rises, the door opens outward once. The map-bearer may leave, and the garden sleeps another cycle, and waits. Or—" she paused, and the pause was the whole weight of twenty years, "—the map-bearer may stay. Become the keeper. Take my place, and I'll finally be allowed to sleep, to join them—" a nod toward the frozen streets below, "—and the garden will give you everything. Not just Vessany. *Everything it holds.* Your mother's kitchen, real again, kettle singing. Your stall rebuilt, your friend Marta alive in it, laughing, because the garden recorded her too, the day she bought a charm from you and told you her daughter's name." The old woman's voice softened cruelly, gently. "Every person you've lost. Every kindness the world took from you. Restored. Kept safe behind these walls forever. No Edict reaches here. No Inquisitor. No poison, ever again, in your mouth or your life."

The room was very quiet. The kettle — the impossible, remembered kettle — began, softly, to sing.

Yara stood in her mother's kitchen and felt the seduction move through her like warm water. That was the horror of it, she thought distantly. It wasn't a trick. Nothing here was false. Marta *had* died covering her escape from the burning market, and here she could be alive. Her mother *had* been taken by debt and sickness, and here the kitchen waited, complete, smelling of cardamom. The garden was not lying. The garden was offering her the one thing no power in the world could give: retreat. A country-sized refuge where nothing more could ever be taken from her.

Stay. Keep the score. Tend the frozen streets and the sleeping dead and the beautiful, unbearable stillness, forever.

She looked at the chair by the window. Her chair. It looked so comfortable. That was the most frightening thing of all.

* * *

She walked out of the house without answering, and the keeper let her go, and the evening light of the false honey-sky lay long across the terraces.

Yara climbed to the highest orchard wall and sat on it, the way she used to sit on the roof of her stall when the night market emptied, and looked down at the whole preserved country glowing beneath her. Somewhere down there, the boy with the hoop would chase it forever and never catch it. Somewhere down there, the mother tucked the blanket a second time, and a third, and a ten-thousandth.

"We could have been happy here," she said aloud, testing the words. They tasted like the Lethe Canal water — sweet going down, hollow after.

Because that was the flaw in the offer, the crack straight through the amber. The garden kept Vessany safe by keeping it *stopped*. And above the hedge, beyond the door, the world went on hurting: Ovid carrying his shame like a stone, Dain grieving a brother whose sacrifice he'd misread, Sarel hunting them with the serene conviction of the truly deceived, thousands upon thousands walking around with holes where their history should be, singing lullabies they didn't understand. The garden could restore everything *lost*. It could not heal anything *wounded*. It could only close the door and keep the wound's twin, safe and shining, in here.

Marta would not want to be a lamp in a niche. Marta had died buying her thirty seconds, and the least Yara owed her was to make those thirty seconds mean the world instead of a walled garden.

And her mother — her mother had taught her the knot-charms, the memory-knots, back when such things were merely grandmother's craft and not yet crime. Her mother had never once taught her to hoard.

Yara realized she was crying properly now, great silent gulps of it, and that the garden was holding her while she cried — a warmth at her back like a hand, patient, hopeful, wanting her to say yes. It even grieved beautifully. Even its begging was lovely.

"I'm sorry," she told it. "You're the most beautiful prison I've ever seen. But I didn't come here to escape the world. I came to give it back."

The warmth at her back went very still.

Far below, across the preserved rooftops of the country that did not exist, Yara heard a sound that did not belong to any century the garden had kept: the ring of steel, the shout of men, and — thin and furious and unmistakable — a voice she knew, bellowing her name.

Dain. Out of Sarel's cells, across the city, through the hedge — and behind him, spilling through the breach like ink through water, torchlight. Sarel's forces had found the door.

The thirteenth moon was rising, and it was rising red.
