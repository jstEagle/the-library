## 19. Trade of One Life

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Dain had learned to measure time by the drip.

One drop for each breath. He had counted past ten thousand before he stopped counting, because counting was a thing a man did when he believed there would be an end to reach. There was no end here. There was only the drip, and the dark, and the memory of Sarel's voice as she had locked him in — *You are worth more to me unbroken, Master Vessany. For now.*

He sat with his back against the wall because standing took strength he might need later, if later ever came. His wrists were raw where the manacles had been. They had taken his boots, his knife, the locket's empty chain — everything but the name he kept turning over in his mouth like a stone warmed by a fire: *Yara.* She was running. She had the locket. She had the third key. Every hour he sat in this dark was an hour she ran without him, and every hour Sarel's inquisitors fanned out through the capital after her, and the thirteenth moon was coming whether any of them willed it or not.

*The garden keeps score,* Corren had whispered as he died. Dain had spent months hating that phrase, hating the map, hating the woman who carried it. Now he understood it at last. The garden remembered everything — every field burned, every name struck from the rolls, every brother who died bleeding in a night market so that a stranger could carry a brass map one street further. And it would remember, too, whether Dain Vessany had died on his knees in a cell or standing up against the dark.

The drip fell. He breathed.

Then, somewhere above him, a door opened that should not have opened.

Footsteps came down the stair — not the heavy tread of the turnkey, not the whispering glide of the inquisitors in their grey. These steps were slow, deliberate, the steps of an old man descending carefully, one hand presumably on the rail. Dain knew those footsteps. He had heard them crossing marble floors in the Queen's palace, back when he had still been allowed inside its walls, back when he had still been a man with a name and a trade.

"Chamberlain Ovid," Dain said to the dark. "They'll hang you for this."

"They will do considerably worse than hang me," Ovid said, and a key turned in the lock, and lamplight spilled into the cell like water into a cracked bowl. "Which is why we should not waste time discussing it."

The old man stood in the doorway holding a lamp in one trembling hand and a ring of stolen keys in the other. He wore his chamberlain's grey, the livery of the crown he had served and betrayed in the same breath, thirty years ago, when he had helped write a country out of the world. His face was hollowed. His eyes were the eyes of a man who had finally set down something enormous and found his arms still shaped around its absence.

"You," Dain said slowly, getting to his feet, testing his legs. "Why? You gave Yara your key. Your part was done. You could have stayed behind your walls and let the rest of us drown."

"Yes," Ovid agreed mildly. "I considered it. I have considerable practice at considering things and then doing nothing." He held out the lamp so its light fell across Dain's face, studying him the way he might study a ledger. "You have her look, you know. Your mother's look. I knew her, when I was young. Before."

Dain went very still. "You knew our mother."

"I knew everyone worth knowing in Vessany, once upon a time." Ovid said it flatly, without self-pity, the way a man names his own wound. "She sang at the harvest fires. Everyone said her voice could make the ember-trees bloom out of season. I was a clerk then. A nobody with a good memory, which is why they used me — why they needed me — when the Edict was written." He turned away, lifting the lamp toward the corridor. "Come. We have perhaps two hundred steps between here and the cistern gate, and every one of them is a chance for me to be caught and for everything to be lost again. I have lost enough. Walk."

Dain walked.

* * *

The cells beneath the Inquisitor General's tower were built in a spiral, and Ovid navigated the spiral like a man walking a road he had traveled in dreams. Twice they pressed themselves into alcoves as grey-robed figures passed above them on the landings, their lamps swinging, their voices low and unhurried. Once a turnkey rounded a corner directly ahead of them, and Dain had a heartbeat to decide — and then Ovid stepped forward, straightening his spine into all his old authority, and said, "The General requires this prisoner moved to the upper interrogation room. Stand aside," and the turnkey stood aside, because thirty years of habit does not ask questions of a chamberlain's voice.

"You lie well," Dain murmured as they descended again.

"I governed well," Ovid corrected. "It is nearly the same skill, and it has cost me far more." At the bottom of the spiral, behind a door whose lock was older and stranger than the others — a lock with no keyhole, only a shallow depression shaped like an open hand — Ovid paused. "This is where I must tell you the rest of it, quickly, because beyond this door I may not be able to speak at all."

Dain looked at the door. The depression in it was worn smooth, as if many hands had rested there long ago. "What is it?"

"The cistern gate opens to blood of the founding families. It was meant as a mercy — a way for Vessany's last officials to flee if the erasure ever turned on its own architects. It has waited thirty years for a hand." Ovid drew a small knife from his sleeve. "Mine will open it. It will also mark me, and marked men cannot pass back through, and the Watch will find me within the hour regardless. So understand what I am buying with this." He met Dain's eyes, and his voice did not tremble at all now. "I am buying the chance that someone reaches the garden before Sarel burns it. I am buying my country one more dawn. I am not buying absolution. Do not offer it to me. I could not carry it and run at the same time."

He cut his palm and pressed it into the depression.

The door did not open so much as *remember itself*. Stone ground against stone somewhere deep in the wall; a seam of darkness appeared and widened; cold air breathed out, smelling of water and green things and — faintly, impossibly, underneath everything — woodsmoke and blossoms, the smell of a country that no longer existed. Dain's throat closed. He had been four years old when the borders dissolved. He had thought he remembered nothing. But his body remembered this smell, the way a body remembers its mother's shoulder, and he stood in the doorway shaking like a man come home to a house burned to its foundations.

"Go," Ovid said. "Down the cistern stairs, along the channel, until you see light that isn't lamplight. Then follow the light. And Dain—" The old man smiled, small and tired and terribly young suddenly, a clerk of nineteen standing in a harvest fire's glow. "Tell the garden the chamberlain is sorry. Tell it Ovid kept the ledgers. Tell it he remembers every line."

Dain gripped the old man's cut hand once — hard, wordless, everything he could not say compressed into pressure — and went down into the dark.

Behind him, he heard the door begin to close, and Ovid's voice, steady to the last: "Two hundred steps, boy. Spend them well."

* * *

The cistern channel ran black and slow, and Dain ran beside it with bare feet slapping wet stone, counting steps because counting was a thing a man did when there was finally an end to reach. A hundred. A hundred and fifty. The air grew warmer. Green smells thickened — moss, then grass, then something flowering. At a hundred and ninety he saw it: light ahead, gold-green and moving, dappled like sunlight through leaves though they were forty feet underground.

At two hundred exactly, the channel ended at a wall of living roots, woven loose as basketry, and through the gaps he could see a garden.

Not a garden. A *country*.

Terraces of orchard climbing hillsides that could not fit inside the space the palace occupied. Lanes of pale stone. Rooftops of blue tile. Ember-trees lining every road, their leaves the color of coals, shedding soft light instead of shadow. And everywhere, drifting on the warm air, voices — hundreds of voices, murmuring, singing, arguing, laughing, the ambient sound of a city going about its evening, preserved like a fly in amber. Vessany. All of Vessany, folded into a seed the size of a heart, waiting under the palace of the people who had erased it.

Dain pressed his hands to the roots and wept without shame, briefly, fiercely, the way a man weeps at a grave that turns out to hold a sleeping child. Then he wiped his face and began pulling the roots apart, because Corren had died for this door and Yara was somewhere ahead of him running out of moonlight, and grief could wait. Grief had learned to wait. It was the only thing in his life that had never left him.

The roots parted. He climbed through.

* * *

Inside, the garden received him the way deep water receives a stone.

The moment his feet touched Vessany's pale stone lanes, the murmuring voices changed. They turned toward him — not the people, who were memories given form, translucent figures moving through their eternal evening — but the voices themselves, the garden's attention, vast and patient and old. He felt it settle over him like a hand on his head. *Vessany,* it seemed to say, tasting his blood. *Founding blood. Come home, little ledger-keeper's countryman. Come home and stay.*

"No," Dain said aloud, and his voice cracked on the cobblestones. "I'm not staying. I'm looking for a woman. Dark hair, quick hands, sells charms, carries a brass map and my mother's locket. Where is she?"

The garden hummed. Somewhere to his left, a translucent woman hung washing that was already dry. Somewhere to his right, children chased a hoop down a lane, their laughter thin as paper. The whole country watched him with the mild curiosity of the dead.

"The ember-trees know her," Dain tried. "She tasted poison for a queen. She broke bread with a chamberlain. She—"

A tree answered.

At the end of the lane, one ember-tree flared — its coal-colored leaves brightening, brightening, until they threw off actual sparks — and leaned, unmistakably, deliberately, pointing its crown down a side path toward terraces of vineyard climbing into golden haze. Dain stared. Then he laughed, short and disbelieving, and ran.

The tree guided him. As he passed, other trees kindled in sequence, a relay of burning crowns lighting his way up through the orchards, and the translucent figures of Vessany turned to watch him go, and some of them — he was almost sure — smiled.

He found her at the top.

She stood in a clearing among the oldest ember-trees, ones so vast their trunks were wider than houses, and before her — he saw it even from a distance, even with his lungs burning and his legs failing — stood a door. A frame of white wood standing free in the grass, empty, humming, its threshold full of a darkness deeper than night. The thirteenth-moon door. The way out. Beyond it, through the frame, he could see the palace gardens of the capital, grey and ordinary and real.

And Yara Whitlock stood before it with her back to it, facing down the slope, because coming up the terraces below her, torches blazing, boots hammering the orchard rows, came the army of the Inquisitor General.

There were hundreds of them. Grey robes, iron masks, the Watch's red banners, and at their center a figure in white-gold armor that Dain recognized with a jolt of pure animal dread: Sarel herself, sword drawn, her voice carrying up the hillside like a blade laid flat against the world:

"Burn the hedge! Burn the trees! Nothing living crosses that frame!"

Torches arced into the lowest orchard rows. Fire bloomed among the ember-trees — and the trees *screamed*, a sound like a hundred choirs catching in a hundred throats, and the translucent figures of Vessany scattered like startled birds, and the whole preserved country shuddered around Dain like a sleeper in pain.

"YARA!" he bellowed, and threw himself down the slope.

She turned. Even at that distance, even in the firelight, he saw her face change — shock, then fury, then something fiercer than both. "You're supposed to be in a cell!"

"I brought a chamberlain!" He reached her, gasping, grabbing her arm. "Ovid — he opened the cistern gate — he's dead or taken by now, and it doesn't matter, because *they're here*, Yara. The thirteenth moon rises in hours. This is it. This is the only it there is."

Below them, the fire was climbing the terraces. Sarel's voice rang again, closer now: "Take the woman alive! Kill the brother — he's surplus!"

Dain put himself between Yara and the slope, knifeless, bootless, armed with nothing but a dead man's name and a founding family's blood, and laughed at the absurd arithmetic of it. "So," he said. "The garden keeps score. Let's give it something to write down."

Yara's hand closed around his. Her grip was steady. Her eyes, when they met his, held no fear at all — only the terrible calm of a woman who had already decided what she was going to do, and was only waiting for the moon.

"Dain," she said quietly, over the roar of the burning orchards and the screaming trees and the army climbing, "when I open that door — whatever happens — don't let go of my hand."

Above the eastern hills, through the smoke, the thirteenth moon began to rise.
