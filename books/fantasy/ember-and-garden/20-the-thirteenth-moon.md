## 20. The Thirteenth Moon

The thirteenth moon rose the color of a wound.

Yara stood at the heart of the Ember Garden and watched it climb through branches that had never known winter. The trees here did not shed; they smoldered. Every leaf held a coal of light beneath its skin, and when the wind moved through them — and there was wind, though they were underground, or under everything, or perhaps simply elsewhere — the whole canopy breathed out a soft red shimmer, like embers stirred in a hearth that had been banked for thirty years and refused, stubbornly, to die.

She had been inside for what felt like hours and might have been minutes. Time behaved strangely here, pooling and draining like water in cupped hands. Behind her, down the long avenue of ember-trees, the door stood open: a frame of white hedge-wood grown through into the shape of an archway, and beyond it, impossibly, the night sky of the capital, the palace gardens, the world she had come from. Through that arch she could see the thirteenth moon hanging over the hedges of home, and it was the same moon, and it was not the same moon at all.

*Once,* the map had said, in letters that had finally opened to her like a hand unclenching. *At the thirteenth moon turn, the door stands open from either side. What is kept may leave. What was lost may return. Choose.*

The garden had made its offer already. It had shown her Vessany entire — the terraced orchards climbing hills that no longer appeared on any map, the river town of Sel with its blue-tiled roofs, the school where children had learned to fold light between their fingers the way her mother's mother had once folded dough. It had shown her the recorded dead, thousands upon thousands of them, standing patient in rows of memory like trees in a nursery, waiting only for someone to carry them back across the threshold. All of it hers, if she stayed. Keeper of the last living country. Bride of a beautiful tomb.

*Stay,* the garden had said, in a voice like warm bread, like her grandmother's kitchen, like every safe place she had ever invented for herself on the worst nights. *And nothing else need ever be lost again.*

And she had wanted to. That was the shame of it, the human weight of it — she had stood among the ember-trees and wanted, with her whole tired heart, to close the door behind her and let the world outside go on forgetting.

Then she had heard the horns.

* * *

They came through the hedge-line on the eastern side where Sarel's sappers had spent two days cutting, and they came with fire, because Inquisitors always came with fire; it was the one tool of magic they permitted themselves, having ruled it not magic but purification. Torches bloomed along the avenue like ugly orange flowers. Boots on the pearl-dust paths. The sound of a hundred men trying to be quiet and failing, because the garden amplified everything — every footfall rang like a bell struck in a chapel, every order hissed carried to Yara's ears as clearly as if it had been spoken against her neck.

She should have run. The door was open. The map's inscription had been explicit, and Dain — gods, Dain, who had escaped Sarel's cells with Ovid's help and reached her three days ago half-dead and grinning — Dain had begged her to run. *Take the memories and go somewhere they can't follow. Let them have the shell.* He was somewhere in the garden now, she thought, doing something reckless with the locket of pressed ember-flowers, their mother's grave-soil still under his fingernails. She had lost sight of him when the first torches came over the hedge.

But running meant closing the door, and closing the door meant choosing the tomb after all — just a larger one, one that traveled. Running meant the Edict stood. Running meant every person in the capital who had ever hummed a lullaby they couldn't name, who had dreamed in a language they'd never been taught, who had wept at nothing and called it weakness — all of them would go on bleeding quietly forever, cut off from a part of themselves they didn't even know had been amputated.

Yara Whitlock had swallowed poison for the crown for eleven years. She knew exactly what it was to hold death in your mouth and smile.

She turned to face the torches.

* * *

Sarel came down the avenue alone.

Her soldiers stopped at the tree line — whether by order or by some older instinct, Yara never learned. Perhaps even Inquisitors could feel it, standing at the edge of all that remembered light: the sense of walking into a room where the dead were sleeping. But Sarel walked in. Her gray coat was immaculate despite the march, her silver chain of office catching the ember-light and throwing it back cold. Thirty years she had hunted the scattered remnants of Vessany's people, and here at last was the heart itself, beating under her boots.

"You," Sarel said, when she was ten paces away. Not surprised. Almost pleased, in the way of a woman whose arithmetic has finally balanced. "The taster. Of course it was the taster. We watch the king's food and never think to watch what feeds the city's dreams."

"General." Yara kept her voice level. Eleven years of practice. "You're standing in a country you helped erase."

"I'm standing in a hospital," Sarel said.

The words landed like a slap, precisely because they were sincere. Yara saw it in her face — the terrible calm, the conviction worn smooth as a river stone from decades of handling. Sarel believed it. Had always believed it. The plague had come out of Vessany's working-magic, wild and hungry, eating memory before it ate flesh; the crown's answer had been to cut the country out of the world like a tumor. Erasure as surgery. Forgetting as mercy. Sarel had been young then, a junior clerk in the Office of Records, and she had written the decree's fair copy herself, and she had spent thirty years making sure no one ever doubted it, because the alternative was to doubt it herself.

"The plague killed forty thousand people," Sarel said, circling slowly, and the ember-trees seemed to lean away from her. "Magic without law is a fire in a grain store. We didn't destroy a country, girl. We amputated a gangrene limb to save the body. And now you want to tear off the bandage."

"I want to stop pretending the arm was never there."

"The arm is *gone.*" For the first time, something cracked in the General's voice — not doubt, but the effort of holding doubt down, the way you press a lid on a boiling pot. "There is no Vessany. There is a garden full of ghosts wearing the shape of a country, and a door that opens once, and a choice. I am offering you the merciful one. Walk out. Forget this place exists — I'll even make it easy; my clerks are very good. Live. Sell your little charms openly, if the new order permits such things. Or stay here and rot beautifully among the dead. Either way, the door closes tonight, and the world stays sane."

"And everyone under the Edict?" Yara asked. "Everyone carrying a hole where a homeland used to be? The chamberlain, who signed his own birthplace out of existence to save his family? Your own soldiers, who sing songs in a dead language and don't know why they cry?"

"They are *safe,*" Sarel said, and her hand went to her chain of office, an old gesture, a talisman. "Safety is worth any price."

"No," Yara said. "It isn't. I've tasted enough poisons to know. Safety is just the slowest one."

* * *

It happened the way the garden must have planned it, or hoped, or simply allowed — Yara would never know which. Sarel stepped forward to seize her, one gauntleted hand reaching for the map-case at Yara's belt, and her boot came down on a seam of pearl-dust path that ran, Yara realized too late, directly along the oldest root of the oldest tree.

The garden woke under her.

Not violently. That was the horror of it. The light simply rose — up through the soles of Sarel's boots, up her legs, her spine, flooding into her the way dawn floods a valley — and Yara watched the Inquisitor General of the realm stop mid-stride and go utterly still, like a woman hearing her own name called from a great distance.

"No," Sarel whispered. "No — keep it out — I *forbade*—"

But you cannot forbid memory. That was the whole point. That had always been the whole point. The Edict could burn books and salt fields and unmake borders, could reach into minds with sanctioned rites and scoop out a country like the flesh of a fruit — but it could not reach here. Inside the Ember Garden, everything that had been taken was kept. Everything Sarel's own clerks had extracted from a thousand condemned souls over thirty years, every confiscated recollection filed and sealed in the vaults beneath the Office of Records — the garden had grown roots through all of it, patiently, the way roots grow through anything, and some of those roots had found *her.*

Yara saw it happen. Saw the fair copy of the decree surface in Sarel's eyes — and behind it, the ink still wet, the younger woman's hands shaking as she wrote, and behind *that*, the thing the decree had been built to bury:

A village. A small one, on the wrong side of the old border. A woman kneeling in a garden row, pulling weeds, looking up and smiling — Sarel's mother, before the plague, before the cull, before the Office of Records took a nine-year-old girl's whole world and told her it had never existed. Told her the ache in her chest was illness. Told her the song she hummed was a symptom.

Told her, year after year, decade after decade, until she became the telling.

"Oh," Sarel said. Very small. The chain of office slipped from her fingers and lay coiled on the pearl-dust like a dead snake. "Oh, *no.*"

Yara had imagined this moment a hundred ways on the road here — Sarel raging, Sarel scheming, Sarel dying by her own doctrine. She had never imagined the General of Inquisition standing among the ember-trees with tears cutting clean lines through road dust, whispering her mother's name in a language the Edict had declared never to have existed.

"It was real," Yara said gently. "All of it. It was real, and it is *here,* and it can be everywhere, if you let me finish."

"Don't—" Sarel's hand shot out, caught Yara's wrist — grip of iron, grip of drowning. "Don't you dare pity me. I burned people. I burned *children* for humming. If what you're about to do gives me back what I—" Her face contorted. "I will spend the rest of my life remembering what I did while I forgot. Do you understand what you're condemning me to?"

"Yes," Yara said. "I understand exactly. It's called justice, General. The kind no law can revoke."

* * *

Somewhere behind them, shouting — Dain's voice, hoarse, calling her name, and the crash of soldiers being thrown back from something, the hedge itself perhaps rising to defend its keeper. There was no time. The moon stood at its height; the door's frame of white hedge-wood was beginning, even now, to knit itself closed at the edges, petals sealing like a wound healing, and once it shut it would not open again for another cycle of thirteen moons, and there would not *be* another cycle, because Sarel's army would raze this place to mineral by morning.

Yara pulled free of Sarel's grip and ran for the door.

The plan — Dain's plan, argued out in whispers among the roots three nights ago — had been to carry the keys out. Three keys of memory: a face, a song, a grave. Open the door wide and walk the world planting them, one mind at a time, a lifetime of quiet work. Safe. Gradual. Deniable.

Standing before the archway with the thirteenth moon burning through it, Yara understood that gradual was just another word for letting them catch you. One mind at a time meant one arrest at a time. One lifetime meant Sarel's successors, and theirs. The Edict had lasted thirty years on fear alone; give it a martyr's garden to guard and it would last three hundred.

The door wasn't meant to be walked through. She saw it now, finally, the way you see the last line of a cipher once someone speaks the key aloud. The garden had offered her keeperhood because it was dying of loneliness — thirty years sealed, feeding on its own stored light, waiting for a hand willing to break the jar. It hadn't needed a keeper.

It needed a gardener willing to scatter the seed.

Yara pressed both palms flat against the white frame of the door, against the wood that was alive and knew her, and spoke the only charm she had ever sold in the night market that was truly hers — the one that cost a piece of the seller, the one her grandmother had taught her before the Edict reached even their poor quarter: *what is held too tightly strangles; what is given freely grows.*

"Come home," she said — to the garden, to Vessany, to every nameless thing. "All of you. Come home."

And she broke the frame.

It did not shatter like wood. It shattered like morning. The white hedge-wood burst apart in a silent bloom of light, and the boundary between the garden and the world dissolved — not opening the door but erasing the wall, and the Ember Garden poured outward like water finding its level, like dawn pouring over a ridge, into the palace gardens, into the streets, into the sleeping city and the waking camps and the cells and the courts and every sealed skull of every soul the Edict had hollowed.

Yara felt it pass through her too — a tide of other lives, other kitchens, other songs — and she let it, laughing and crying at once, rooted to the spot where the door had been, watching memory reclaim the world at the speed of moonlight.

Below, in the capital, forty thousand stolen histories came home to forty thousand startled hearts. Somewhere a baker dropped his bread and began to weep in a language he suddenly remembered. Somewhere the chamberlain Ovid lifted his head from his ledgers and said his birthplace's name aloud for the first time in thirty years, and the candles guttered with the force of it. Somewhere a soldier of the Inquisition sat down in the mud of the palace road and sang his grandmother's lullaby to the end, all the verses, badly, gloriously.

And beside her, on her knees among the falling petals of the broken door, the Inquisitor General of the realm bowed her head over her mother's name and broke — cleanly, completely, the way a dam breaks, the way a fever breaks — and what flooded through the ruin of her was not madness but grief, vast and specific and *hers*, and grief, Yara knew better than anyone alive, is only love with nowhere left to go.

Except now, she thought, watching the ember-light stream out into the world like a river released from thirty years of ice —

Now it had somewhere to go.

* * *

When the light settled, the garden was still there — changed, no longer sealed, its trees throwing ordinary shadows under the ordinary moon, its paths open on every side like any park, like any commons, belonging to everyone and no one. The thirteenth moon hung above it, beginning its long slide down toward morning.

Dain limped out of the tree line, soot-streaked, bloodied, alive, with the locket of pressed ember-flowers clutched in his fist. He looked at the broken doorway, at the open horizon of the garden, at Yara standing in the ruins of the threshold.

"You were supposed to run," he said.

"I know," Yara said.

He looked around at the ember-trees, at the soldiers beyond them standing dazed and disarmed by their own returning pasts, at Sarel still kneeling in the pearl-dust, weeping without shame. Then he laughed — a rough, disbelieving sound — and held out the locket.

"There's a hillside in here," he said. "My mother's hillside. The seeds are still good. I can feel it."

Yara took the locket. Below them, in the city, bells were ringing — not alarm bells, she realized, but the old pattern, the forgotten pattern, the ones that had rung for festivals in a country that officially had never existed.

Morning was coming, and it was bringing everything with it.
