## 21. After the Edict

The law died quietly, the way most laws do — not with a battle but with a signature.

Yara heard it secondhand, in the palace kitchens, where the news arrived faster than any decree could travel. A scullery girl came running with flour still on her hands, breathless, half-laughing: *the Edict's been struck from the books. Struck. They read it out in the Hall of Records and then they burned the page.* The kitchen went silent for a heartbeat, and then it did not go silent at all — it erupted, because for three generations the cooks and carvers and tasters of the royal household had been passing small magics among themselves like contraband sugar: a charm to keep milk from souring, a whispered word to soften tough meat, a grandmother's rhyme that made bread rise even on cold mornings. All of it had been crime. Now it was only habit.

Yara stood by the tasting table with a silver spoon in her hand and let the noise wash over her. She had imagined this moment so many times — in the night market, hunched over her little charms, she had imagined trumpets, or riots, or at least weeping in the streets. Instead there was flour on a girl's hands and a kitchen shouting itself hoarse over risen bread.

It was better than trumpets. It was truer.

She set down the spoon. Her tongue, trained to catch hemlock under honey and arsenic beneath wine, tasted something new in the air of the palace: the absence of fear. It had a flavor, she realized. Faintly sweet. Like the first bite of an apple you had been told was poisoned your whole life.

* * *

Chamberlain Ovid dressed for confession the way other men dressed for execution.

He chose his own clothes — no livery, no chain of office — a plain grey coat he had not worn since Vessany burned, since before it burned, since the morning he had sat at a table with eleven other frightened men and helped write a country out of existence. He brushed the coat himself. His hands shook, and he let them shake. There was no more use pretending to steadiness; he had spent forty years being steady, and it had purchased him nothing but a longer fall.

In the Hall of Records, before the assembled court — the Regent's council, the Inquisitors stripped of their black robes and looking strangely naked in ordinary grey, and crowds spilling through the doors because the guards had stopped turning people away — Ovid stood at the lectern and did the thing he had rehearsed a hundred times and failed to say aloud even once.

"My name," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, "is Ovid Maran. I was born in Vessany, in a town called Liss, on the river Sennet, which does not appear on any map you have ever seen."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere near the back, a child asked her mother what Vessany was, and the mother said, *hush, listen,* and that was the whole of the interruption.

Ovid told them. He told them about the plague that had come up the river valley in the year of his twelfth birthday — how it killed one person in five and drove the rest mad with grief-magic, widows raising ghosts, fathers binding their children's memories into amulets so they would not have to bury them twice. How the crown's answer had been simpler than medicine: if magic had caused the suffering, erase the magic, and erase the place that held it, and erase the memory of the place, and call it mercy. He told them he had been twenty-two years old, a junior clerk with a wife and a newborn daughter inside Vessany's borders, and that when the crown offered amnesty to any Vessani who would help draft the Edict, he had signed his own country's death warrant to buy his family's lives.

"I wrote the clause," he said. "Clause Nine. *No record, monument, song, or grave shall bear the name.* I wrote it in my own hand. I buried my mother's letters in my garden and told my daughter she had no grandmother." His voice failed, recovered. "I have been chamberlain to four monarchs. I have been honest in every accounting but one. Here is the last entry."

He unrolled a scroll — the original Edict, smuggled out of the archives during the chaos after the thirteenth moon, when half the Inquisitorate had been on their knees in the palace gardens, weeping over memories they had never known they carried. He read Clause Nine aloud, and then he read the names. Not all of them; there were too many, two hundred thousand souls erased in a single bureaucratic season. But he read for an hour, name after name after name, Liss and Sennet and the Ember Garden and the families who had kept its gate, until the scribes recording the session ran out of ink and had to send for more.

When he finished, the Regent — a careful woman, not yet crowned, chosen precisely because she owed nothing to the old order — rose and said the words that would be carved above the archive door within the year:

"Let it be entered into the record that Vessany existed. Let it be entered that it was destroyed by decree, unjustly. And let it be entered that this kingdom remembers."

Ovid walked out of the hall through a corridor of people who did not cheer. They did something worse, or better: they touched him as he passed. An old woman took his sleeve. A young clerk pressed a sprig of some flower Yara would later identify as an ember-bloom into his hand. Nobody spoke. It was not forgiveness — he had not asked for it and did not expect it — but it was witness, and after forty years of enforced silence, witness felt almost like grace.

That night he wrote in his journal, in a hand that finally did not shake: *My mother grew roses the color of coals. I remember. I remember. I remember.*

* * *

Sarel did not attend the reading of the names.

She stood instead in what had been her office in the Inquisitorate — now being catalogued, shelf by shelf, by archivists who handled her files like evidence, which is what they were — and watched through the window as workers dismantled the black iron sign above the door. THE EDICT IS ETERNAL, it had read. A man with a chisel took the words apart letter by letter, and each falling fragment rang against the cobbles like a small bell.

Her memories had come back to her in the garden, all at once, the night Yara Whitlock shattered the door's frame and the past poured outward like water from a broken dam. Sarel remembered now. That was the cruelty of it — the justice of it, depending on who you asked. She remembered her grandmother singing in a language the Edict had outlawed. She remembered a village called Brast, on hills that were now officially nowhere, and a festival of lanterns every autumn, and her grandmother's hands folding paper boats to carry wishes down the stream. She remembered the day the Inquisitors came to Brast, and she remembered — this was the part that broke her open every morning, fresh, like a wound re-dressed — that she had been *proud*. A girl of nine, watching the black robes burn the lanterns, thinking: *now we are safe. Now we are clean.*

They had taken the orphan of Brast and raised her in the Inquisitorate's own houses. They had built her out of the rubble of her own country and aimed her at anyone who tried to dig the rubble up. She had spent thirty years calling that mercy.

She could have fled. Some of her colleagues had — north, over the mountains, to kingdoms that still kept their own erasures. The new Regent's men had come to her cell — she had surrendered herself, the morning after, walking into the Hall of Records and laying her seal on the table like a surgeon setting down a blade — and offered her testimony in exchange for leniency. She had refused leniency. She had asked instead for a task.

So now Sarel Vane, formerly Inquisitor General, keeper of the Edict, walked the roads of the former Vessany with a cart full of blank ledgers and a mandate from the Regent herself: *go and ask what was lost. Write it down. Do not correct it. Do not rank it. Only write.* In every village she found someone who had woken on the morning after the thirteenth moon remembering a grandmother, a song, a field that had never officially existed. She filled ledger after ledger. Her handwriting, once famous for its terrible precision, grew looser with every page.

On the road to Brast — the hills were exactly as her grandmother's songs had promised, green and folded like a sleeping animal — she met a boy selling charms at a crossroads, openly, in daylight, with a little painted sign: CHARMS, BLESSINGS, REMEMBRANCES, FAIR PRICES. The boy couldn't have been more than twelve. Under the Edict she would have taken his stock and his fingers.

Sarel bought a remembrance-charm. It cost her a copper. It was badly made, crooked knotwork, probably useless.

She wore it anyway, for the rest of her life.

* * *

Dain planted his mother in spring.

Not all of her — what remained of her was ash and a locket and a handful of pressed ember-flowers the color of banked fire — but the seeds inside the locket were another matter. Ember-flowers did not keep, everyone knew that; they crumbled to dust within a season of picking. These had waited thirty years in a grave beneath the capital, sealed behind silver, and when Dain pried the locket open on the night of the exhumation they had glowed faintly, like coals breathing, and Yara had said, very quietly, *they weren't dead. They were waiting for permission.*

He chose the plot himself: a hillside outside the city, facing east, on land that the new surveys had just relabeled — reluctantly, at the chamberlain's insistence — *Vessani Marches, historical.* The borders would not return; everyone understood that. Countries were lines drawn by treaties and armies, and no amount of memory could redraw them. But the land remembered differently than maps did. The land had never signed anything.

He dug the hole with his brother's spade. That mattered to him. Corvin had carried that spade across half the kingdom, hidden in a beggar's bundle, and had died with the map pressed into a stranger's hands rather than let the Watch take it. Dain had spent a winter hating that stranger, and a spring understanding her, and he supposed he would spend the rest of his life doing both at once, the way you can love and grieve the same person without contradiction.

He knelt in the turned earth and opened the locket. The ember-flowers had gone to seed heads, brittle and dark, and when he tipped them into his palm the seeds spilled out — dozens of them, tiny, warm. Actually warm. He laughed, and the laugh came out wet.

"You'd have liked her," he told his mother, because the dead should be introduced to the living, that was the whole point of planting anything. "She tastes poison for a living and sells charms against it. Terrible business sense. Stubborn as a mule about other people's memories." He worked the seeds into the soil, one pinch at a time. "Corvin trusted her with everything we had. So."

He watered the plot from the river Sennet — or what had been the Sennet, before the canal engineers renamed it the Lethe and taught it to eat memories; the waters ran clean now, remembering nothing but water. Then he sat back on his heels and waited, because Yara had said ember-flowers came up fast once they decided to, and she was rarely wrong about growing things.

They came up in three days. By the end of the week the hillside was a low blaze of coal-red blooms, and people began to walk out from the city just to see them, and to leave things at the edge of the patch: a wooden horse, a wedding ring, a name written on paper and weighted with a stone. Dain tended the flowers every morning. He did not own the hillside. He simply could not imagine leaving it untended, and neither, it turned out, could anyone else who came. Within a month there were six of them, then twelve, kneeling in the dirt with watering cans, most of them strangers to each other and none of them strangers to loss.

His mother's grave marker was a flat stone he cut himself, and on it he carved only what the stone could honestly hold:

*HERE GREW A COUNTRY. HERE GROWS ITS GARDEN.*

* * *

Yara reopened her stall on the first market-day of summer, in the rebuilt night market — though it was not a night market anymore, because there was no need for darkness now, no need for shadows and shuttered lanterns and passwords whispered through knots in wood. The stall stood in full sun, between a cheesemonger and a bookbinder, with a sign she had painted herself in letters a child could read:

CHARMS & REMEMBRANCES — YARA WHITLOCK — FAIR PRICES, HONEST KNOTWORK

Business was strange at first. For years her customers had come to her trembling, hooded, buying memory-charms the way starving people buy bread — fast, furtive, ashamed. Now they lingered. They browsed. An old soldier spent an hour deciding between a courage-charm and a sleep-charm and bought both, chatting the whole time about his granddaughter. Children came on errands for their mothers and stayed to watch her tie the knotwork, mouths open, asking questions no one had dared ask in three generations: *how does it work? Where do you learn? Can I?*

"Can you what?" she asked a small girl with flour on her sleeves — the same scullery girl, or one very like her.

"Make things," the girl said. "Remember things. My gran knew a song about a river with a name nobody uses anymore, and Ma says maybe now somebody will want to hear it."

"Somebody wants to hear it right now," Yara said, and put a copper in the girl's hand, and listened to the whole song, and wrote it down afterward in a notebook that was getting fat with such things — songs, recipes, blessings, the names of plants and saints and streets, all of it flowing back into the world like water finding its old channels. She sent copies to Sarel's ledgers, and to the archive, and to the school that had just opened in the old Inquisitorate building, where children learned knotwork beside their letters.

Behind her stall, visible over the rooftops, the hedge had grown enormous. What had been a door in the palace gardens was now a wall of living green visible from half the city, ember-flowers climbing it in rivers of red, and at its base the new public gate stood open dawn to dusk. People went in weeping and came out weeping, and queued to do it again. Inside was Vessany entire — the streets of Liss, the orchards, the recorded voices of two hundred thousand people preserved in leaves and light — and the garden had made Yara a promise before she shattered its frame: *what is remembered cannot die.* She had not become its keeper forever, as it had first demanded. She had become its keeper in the human way: imperfect, mortal, paid in coppers, going home at night.

Some evenings Dain came by the stall after tending the hillside, smelling of earth and coal-red petals, and they ate supper together on crates behind the awning while the market roared around them. Neither of them spoke much about Corvin, or the thirteenth moon, or the taste of the Lethe water. They didn't need to. Grief, she had learned, braids itself into renewal the way a charm braids into a cord — you don't remove the mourning thread to finish the work. You weave it in. It makes the knot hold.

One evening, as the lamps were lit — ordinary lamps, lit for ordinary light, and wasn't that still a miracle — the scullery girl came running again, flour on her hands, breathless.

"Mistress Whitlock! At the gate! There's a woman asking for you — says she's come from over the mountains, from a place she thought she'd dreamed, and—" the girl caught her breath, eyes huge, "—and she's carrying a map. Says it won't open for her. Says someone told her you'd know why."

Yara set down her supper. Across the market square, framed in the glow of the garden gate, a travel-worn figure stood holding a brass cylinder the way you hold something that has already cost you everything.

Yara wiped her hands, straightened her sign, and went to meet her.
