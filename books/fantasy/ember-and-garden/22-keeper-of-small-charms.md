## 22. Keeper of Small Charms

The stall was smaller than the old one, and that was the first thing Yara loved about it.

She had built it herself in the weeks after the thaw — after the Edict came down like a rotted tooth pulled from the crown's jaw, after the Inquisitors' offices were emptied and their ledgers burned or, worse, opened to the public so that everyone could read what had been done in the name of mercy. She had carried the planks down from a carpenter on Tanner's Row with splinters working into her palms, and she had sanded them smooth by lamplight while Dain sat across from her sharpening a knife he no longer needed for anything but bread.

The new stall stood at the mouth of the Ember Garden's public gate, where the palace gardens had once been the sole property of queens and their secrets. Now there was a wrought-iron archway with its gates flung wide, and beyond it the hedge that had hidden a door out of a vanished country — the hedge still grew there, green and patient, though the door within it had shattered into a thousand living branches the night Yara broke the frame and let Vessany pour back into the world.

People came through that gate every day now. Some came weeping. Some came laughing so hard they had to sit down on the low wall beside her stall until the laughter spent itself. Some came angry, fists clenched, demanding of the garden why it had waited so long, why it had let a decree written in ink unmake a country of two million souls — and the garden never answered, because gardens do not argue; they only grow, and hold, and keep score.

Yara set out her wares as the morning light came slantwise over the rooftops: bundles of dried ember-flowers pressed flat between sheets of glass, their petals still faintly warm to the touch even a year after picking. Little clay beads fired in the garden's soil, which hummed against the skin of anyone who had lost someone and could not remember their face. Ribbons dyed with marigold and rue. And in a shallow wooden tray at the front, the charms themselves — small things, stitched and knotted and whispered over, each one labeled in her own careful hand.

*For remembering a name you were made to forget.*

*For sleeping without dreaming of fire.*

*For speaking to the dead without fear.*

*For children born after the Edict, who deserve to know what their grandparents' voices sounded like.*

She had sold charms in secret for eleven years under penalty of the rope. Now she sold them in daylight, haggled openly, kept an honest ledger that any tax clerk could read, and still sometimes caught herself glancing over her shoulder when a customer lingered too long at the tray. The habit would take years to die, if it died at all. Perhaps it shouldn't. A person who has lived hunted should keep some of the hunter's eyes; it seemed disrespectful otherwise, to forget what forgetting cost.

"Keeper!"

The voice came shrill and certain from the gate, and Yara looked up to see them coming — the children, six of them this morning, running ahead of the nurse who had long since given up trying to make them walk. They arrived at the stall in a clatter of elbows and breath, and the smallest, a girl named Pip with a gap where her front teeth ought to be, planted both hands on the counter and demanded, "Tell us the one about the river."

"You told me last week," said the oldest, a boy called Merrick who fancied himself above such things and had walked all the way here anyway.

"Then tell us the one about the *moon*," Pip said, undeterred. "The thirteenth one."

Yara wiped her hands on her apron and leaned her elbows on the counter, and around her the morning market went on — fishwives crying their prices, a man arguing cheerfully about the quality of his onions, somewhere a fiddler tuning up for the noon crowd. All of it ordinary. All of it impossible, once.

"The thirteenth moon," she began, in the voice she saved for stories, "rose over a city that had forgotten it had ever been afraid."

* * *

They listened the way children listen — wholly, hungrily, interrupting only to ask the questions adults had learned not to ask. Why did the Queen sign the decree? Was Sarel really drowned in her own memories? Did it hurt, breaking the door?

To the last question Yara always gave the same answer: "It hurt like birth hurts. Something was coming into the world through me, and I was too small a passage for it." The children nodded solemnly, the way children do, accepting pain as a fair price for wonders.

When the story was done — she always stopped at the shattering of the frame, before the grief parts, before Dain's capture and Ovid's confession and the long gray winter of hearings — Pip tugged her sleeve.

"What happened to the lady? The Inquisitor General?"

Yara looked past the child, toward the gate, where the hedge threw its long shadow across the flagstones.

"She lives in a house by the sea now," Yara said. "With people whose job is remembering alongside her. They say she spends her days writing down everything that comes back to her, page after page, and sends it all to the archive. Every memory the flood gave back to her, she gives away again." She paused. "Some say it's penance. I think it's simpler than that. I think when you spend your whole life believing forgetting is kindness, and then you drown in everything you helped erase — the only mercy left is to put it all down where no one can lose it again."

Merrick frowned. "Do you forgive her?"

Ah. There it was, the question underneath all the questions, the one the whole city was still asking itself in a hundred registers — in courtrooms and kitchens and the letters column of the broadsheets. *Do we forgive? Can we? What does forgiveness cost, and who has the right to pay it?*

"I forgive her the way the garden forgives," Yara said at last. "Not by pretending nothing happened. By growing anyway."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then apparently found it sufficient, because she slapped a copper coin on the counter and pointed at the tray. "That one. The blue knot. For my gran."

Yara took down the charm — a simple slipknot of blue thread over a bead of garden clay — and did not take the coin. "Your grandmother's name is Maren Ostrow," she said. "She sang you a song about a boat, and you've been humming it wrong, and this will fix that." She pressed the charm into the girl's palm and folded the small fingers over it. "And keep the coin. Buy yourself an apple. Your grandmother would want you fed."

Pip stared at her. "How do you know Gran's—"

"Because the garden keeps score," Yara said, smiling, "and it shares its notes with me."

The children ran off toward the apple cart, and Yara watched them go, and felt the ache that lived permanently now beneath her ribs — not grief exactly, and not joy either, but the braid the two made when they were wound tight enough. Somewhere in the city, Maren Ostrow was alive, humming a broken song, unaware that her granddaughter carried her name home in a knot of blue thread. Somewhere in the ground, Dain's mother lay in a grave that was no longer unmarked — the city council had voted in spring to raise a stone, and Dain had insisted on carving the epitaph himself, and had wept over it for three days, and shown it to no one until it was done:

*HERE LIES A WOMAN OF VESSANY, WHO KEPT HER COUNTRY ALIVE IN TWO SONS.*

*One son carried the map. One son carried the song. Both carried her home.*

* * *

Midday brought the pilgrims.

They came up from the provinces now that the roads were safe and the checkpoints dismantled — farmers and millers and midwives, whole families in their best clothes, walking the last mile in silence because someone's grandmother had told them that the garden deserved silence. They passed Yara's stall in a slow river, and many of them stopped, because word had traveled: the keeper sells memories, the keeper knows names, bring her what you have lost and she will tell you if it can be found.

An old man with a miller's shoulders stood before her tray for a long time before speaking. "They say you can find a smell," he said. "My wife's kitchen. Bread and something else — I've chased it forty years and it slips me every time. Since the flood came through our valley I remember her face, her voice, her hands. But the smell runs off like water."

Yara closed her eyes and reached — not far, not with effort anymore; the garden's roots ran under the whole city now, and touching them was like dipping a hand into a warm stream. She found it in seconds: a low-beamed kitchen, a woman with flour to her elbows, yeast and woodsmoke and underneath it all a sprig of something burning sweetly, because the woman of Vessany had burned rosemary in her ovens the way her mother had, the way her mother's mother had, all the way back to a country that officially had never existed.

"Rosemary," Yara said, opening her eyes. "In the oven floor, under the loaves. It was a Vessany custom — they believed the smoke carried prayers up through the crust."

The old man's face did something complicated and slow, like ice going out of a lake. He thanked her. He tried to pay. She waved him off and pointed him instead to the cutting she wanted him to take from the herb bed just inside the gate — *take it home, plant it by your oven, and in three years you'll bake her bread* — and he went through the archway with his hat in his hands like a man entering a church.

This was the work now. Not the furtive trade of the night market, charms sold in whispers with a lookout posted — but this: standing in sunlight at the door of the returned dead, handing people back the small things. A smell. A song sung right. A grandmother's name in a child's fist.

Dain appeared in the early afternoon, as he always did, carrying a basket of the locket-seeds' second harvest. The ember-flowers from his mother's grave had proven hardier than anyone guessed; they had naturalized along the garden's outer wall, and their seeds germinated in strange places — gutters, chimney pots, the crack between the courthouse steps — as if the flowers had opinions about where justice was needed most.

"Trade you," he said, setting the basket down. "Seeds for lunch."

"You drive a hard bargain." She handed him the bread and cheese she'd wrapped that morning, and they ate side by side behind the stall, shoulders almost touching, watching the crowd move through the gate.

He was thinner than he'd been in the canal days, and quieter, and the rage had gone out of him the way a fever breaks — leaving him pale and new and unsure of his own weather. He taught at the garden school now, three mornings a week, teaching children the old Vessany script off tablets Ovid had smuggled out of the erasure forty years ago and hidden inside a false bottom of his writing desk. Ovid himself had died in the autumn, quietly, in his sleep, having lived just long enough to see his confession read aloud in the restored Hall of Records and to hear a historian call him, to his face, *the man who saved the paperwork of a murdered country.* Yara had gone to the funeral. So had half the city. The other half stayed home, and that too was honest, and honesty was the whole point.

"Dain." She turned the cheese rind over in her fingers. "The council sent another letter. About the keeper's post."

"They want you to take the title formally. Warden of the Ember Garden, Keeper of the National Memory, whatever grand thing they're calling it this week." He chewed, swallowed. "Are you going to?"

She looked through the archway. Inside, the garden went on being itself: orchards heavy with fruit that ripened in every season at once, streets of preserved houses where recorded memories drifted like pollen, the great central tree — the first ember-tree, the heartwood — lifting its crown of faintly glowing leaves against the sky. The garden had offered her eternity once. Had offered to restore everything, hold everything, keep her forever as its solitary warden behind a sealed door. She had chosen differently. She had broken the frame and let it all out, and in doing so had learned the lesson the garden itself had perhaps been waiting eleven generations to teach: that memory hoarded is a tomb, and memory shared is a country.

"No," she said. "I'll keep the stall."

Dain laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him. "You're turning down a title."

"I'm keeping a counter." She nudged the tray of charms with her elbow. "Anyone can be a warden. Wardens guard things. I'd rather be the person at the gate who hands you back your grandmother's kitchen." She paused, and heard the words land in her own chest like seeds in turned earth. "My inheritance isn't the garden, Dain. It never was. It's the *stall*. Eleven years of selling small mercies in the dark — that was the apprenticeship. This—" she gestured at the sunlight, the crowd, the archway hung with its new bronze plaque reading THE EMBER GARDEN: OPEN TO ALL WHO REMEMBER — "this is the shop finally coming out of hiding."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he reached into his coat and produced something wrapped in linen, and set it on the counter between them.

She unwrapped it and went very still.

A brass map case. Scratched, dented, stained with a dead man's blood that no amount of polishing had ever fully lifted. Inside it, when she thumbed the clasp, the map itself — the one that had refused her, shown her only burning fields, and then finally, under the thirteenth moon, opened like a flower. Except the map was not a map anymore. The vellum had changed in the year since the shattering; the inked lines of Vessany's borders had faded to ghosts, and in their place the surface showed a living image, shifting slowly, like pond ice: the garden seen from above, and beyond its walls the whole sprawling city, and beyond the city roads going out and out, and tiny marks of green appearing along those roads like a rash of spring — the seedlings, the cuttings, the rosemary by a miller's oven, the ember-flowers in the courthouse cracks. The map was mapping the return.

"It does that now," Dain said. "Every few weeks it grows a little more green. I thought—" He stopped, started again. "I thought you should have it back. It was yours before it was mine. You held a dying stranger's hand and took his burden when you could have run. That makes it yours."

Yara touched the vellum gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "I don't want to keep it locked in a case."

"So don't."

"Then what—"

"Hang it in the stall," Dain said simply. "Let people see it. Let them watch the green spread. Let some kid point at it and ask what the little dots are, and let some old woman find her village on it and cry." He shrugged, and there was his brother in the gesture suddenly, so sharply that Yara's throat tightened — Corvin alive in the set of his brother's shoulders, the way the dead stay alive in the small mechanics of the living. "The garden keeps score. But somebody's got to read the score aloud."

* * *

Evening came soft and gold. The market emptied by degrees, the fiddler packed up, the fishwives folded their awnings, and Yara banked her stall for the night — charms covered, ledger squared, the brass map hung at last on the little nail she'd driven into the stall's back post, where the last of the sunset caught it and made the new green marks shine like scattered embers.

She was blowing out her lamp when she saw the figure waiting at the gate.

Small. Hooded. Standing where the hedge's shadow pooled darkest, hands clasped like a supplicant's. For one irrational moment the old fear woke in her — *Watch, Inquisitors, rope* — and then the figure pushed back the hood, and it was a girl of maybe sixteen, thin as a wren, with the hollow-eyed look Yara knew from mirrors eleven years deep.

"Please," the girl said. Her voice shook. "They said — the market people said — you're the one who helps people find what was taken." She swallowed. "I was born in Vessany. Before the decree. I was four when they took it. Everyone says I'm too young to remember anything, but I *do*, I remember a door, a green door, and a woman singing behind it, and nobody believes me, they say Vessany never existed, they say I dreamed it—" Her breath hitched. "Did I dream it? Please. Am I mad?"

Yara came around the counter slowly. Behind the girl, through the archway, the garden breathed its warm green breath into the dusk, and the first ember-flowers along the wall had begun their nightly glow, small constellations at knee height.

"You're not mad," Yara said. "You're the only one telling the truth. Come and sit down. Tell me about the door."

And as the girl began to speak — haltingly, then faster, words tumbling like water through a broken dam — Yara lit the lamp again, and settled onto the stool behind her counter, and listened.

Outside the gate, the city went on with its evening. Inside the stall, a keeper kept her count. And on the brass-hung map, in the dark, another fleck of green quietly bloomed.
