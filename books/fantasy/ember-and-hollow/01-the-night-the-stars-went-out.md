## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with impossible things, Mara Villalobos had learned, was that they kept books.

Everything else about them was smoke and rumor—a miracle traded in a back alley, a memory sold for a season of good luck, a promise pawned and never redeemed. But the Guild of Impossible Things ran on ledgers. Ink, paper, arithmetic. Someone had to know what had been bought, what had been owed, and who would come screaming for it when the interest came due.

Tonight that someone was Mara, third desk from the coal stove in the guild's counting house, auditing a column of trades so small they barely qualified as miracles: a widow's grief exchanged for one perfect summer afternoon; a boy's fear of water sold to a fishmonger at half price; someone's name—just the name, nothing else—bought by an anonymous buyer with deep pockets and no return address.

Mara copied figures. Checked sums. Kept their head down the way they had for six years, ever since they'd walked into the guild hall with a sword wrapped in oilcloth and asked Sef, the memory-broker, to take something heavy off them. *Make me nobody,* they'd said. And Sef, being good at his work, had made them a clerk with steady hands and no history worth mentioning.

It had worked beautifully. That was the trap of it. Invisibility always does.

Mara was halfway down the page when the ink began to move.

Not smear—*move*. The letters lifted off the ledger like startled birds, circled once above the paper, and settled again crooked, as if the words themselves had heard something terrible and couldn't sit still afterward. Mara set down the pen. Around the counting house, the contraband miracles on their shelves—the bottled second chances, the jarred laughter, the luck that came in threes—all flickered at once, like candles in a draft.

"Anyone else seeing that?" called Pell, the night auditor, from her desk by the window.

Mara didn't answer. They were looking past her, through the frost-ferned glass, at the sky over the city.

The stars were going out.

Not fading. Not clouding over. Going out—one, then ten, then all of them at once, snuffed like a hand passed over a row of flames, until the sky was a single seamless black, deeper than any night Mara had ever seen. No moon either. The moon had simply stopped being there, the way a word stops being there when you forget it mid-sentence.

Below the dead sky, the border wards along the old wall lit up—not their usual patient silver but a strangled red—and then shattered. Mara felt it in their teeth, in the scar tissue over their knuckles, in some buried place behind their sternum that hadn't made a sound in six years. A soundless breaking, like a bone snapping inside a glove.

Every lamp in the counting house guttered. Every miracle on every shelf went dark and stayed dark, except one: a small vial of borrowed courage on Mara's own desk, which now glowed like a dying ember, pulsing in time with Mara's heartbeat.

Which was wrong. Miracles didn't recognize their owners. Unless—

"No," Mara said quietly, to no one, to themselves.

Pell was on her feet. "The wards are down. The whole border line, down. That's not possible, that's—" She stopped, because everyone in the room was already doing the same arithmetic she was. Impossible things were suddenly, catastrophically, possible.

Then someone started hammering on the guild's front door.

* * *

They found the courier in the entryway, half-frozen and half-dead, snow melting pink around him. He'd ridden hard enough to kill three horses; you could see it in the way he held himself, the way his breath came in wet rags. One of the younger clerks knelt beside him uselessly while Mara pushed through the crowd, and when the courier's eyes found Mara's face, he smiled with cracked lips.

"You," he rasped. "Ledger-keeper. I was told—find the quiet one. Third desk."

"Who told you?"

"He did." The courier fumbled at his collar with fingers that wouldn't obey him. "The swordspeaker. Before the end. He said—you'd know what it means. He said you'd pretend you don't, but you will."

Mara's hands had gone very still. Stillness was a skill they'd practiced for years; it had never betrayed them before tonight.

The courier pulled free a scrap of parchment wrapped in oilskin, and even before it opened, Mara knew. Knew the weight of it. Knew the smell of oath-parchment, which is the smell of a promise burning slowly forever.

On it, in a hand Mara recognized because it had once been their own, was a fragment of the old covenant—the pact between the mortal realm and the Hollow, the one that bound the thirteen moons, the one that died with the last of its speakers. Three lines survived, and beneath them, pressed into the parchment like a brand, an oath-mark: a blade crossed with a crescent, drawn in ink that still moved faintly, still breathed.

Mara's mark. Drawn in Mara's blood, twenty years ago, by a person Mara had paid good money to stop being.

"I don't know what this is," they said.

The courier's smile widened, terrible and kind. "He said you'd say that too." His hand found Mara's wrist, gripped with the last of his strength. "Listen. The stars aren't gone. They're being *eaten*. Someone's harvesting the oaths—buying them up, hollowing out the speakers, feeding on everything they promised. He's building himself a crown out of other people's vows. He means to be the thirteenth moon, and if he finishes—" The courier coughed, and the cough did not stop, and when it finally did, he finished anyway, in a whisper: "If he finishes, there won't be anything left to swear by. Anywhere. Either side of the border."

His grip loosened. The light in the entryway steadied around a body that no longer needed it.

For a long moment, nobody moved. Then the clerks began to murmur, and Pell said something about waking the guildmaster, and the machinery of the Guild of Impossible Things ground into motion around a corpse, efficient and unbothered, because death was just another tradeable commodity and everyone here knew the exchange rate.

Mara stood holding the fragment and did not hear any of it.

*He.* There was only one *he* it could be. Corvane. Rival broker, rising star of the guild's inner circle, the man whose trades had been getting stranger and more expensive these past two years—whose buyers never complained, whose sellers never spoke again. Mara had audited his columns a dozen times and signed off on them, because the numbers balanced, because balancing was the job, because looking closely at where the inventory came from was not.

Six years of invisibility, and it had been an illusion the whole time. You couldn't bury an oath. It sat under the floorboards of you, breathing, waiting for the night the stars went out to remind you it was still there.

Mara looked down at the oath-mark, and it looked back—or felt like it did—patient and accusing, a signature waiting twenty years for its counterparty.

Behind them, a voice said, dry as old paper:

"Well. Isn't that interesting."

Sef stood at the top of the stairs, memory-broker's rings on every finger, watching the fragment in Mara's hands with an expression Mara had never seen on him before. Not surprise. Something older. Something like recognition.

"I think," Sef said, coming down slowly, "you and I need to talk about what you actually sold me, six years ago. Because I've been checking my books, Mara. And the memory you gave me isn't in them."

He smiled, thin and unreadable.

"It was never in anyone's books. Which means someone took it out. And I'd very much like to know who's been editing my ledger."

Outside, under a sky with no stars, the first of the Hollow's bells began to ring.
