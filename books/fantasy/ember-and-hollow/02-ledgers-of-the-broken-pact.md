## 2. Ledgers of the Broken Pact

The covenant fragment weighed nothing and everything.

Mara had carried it home from the courier's body wrapped in a ledger page, the way the guild handled anything that might still be listening. Now, in the grey hour before the guild woke, they laid it flat on their desk beneath a single candle and let themselves look at it properly for the first time.

A strip of vellum, blackened at one edge. Ink that moved when you weren't watching—slow tectonic drifts of script rearranging themselves like ants rebuilding a nest. And there, in the lower corner, burned into the fiber rather than written: an oath-mark. A blade crossed with a spoken word, rendered as a spiral of thirteen turns.

Mara's mark. The one they had cut from their own palm twenty years ago and buried in salt.

*Invisibility was always an illusion,* they thought, and the thought tasted like copper.

Around them, the guild's contraband miracles flickered in their jars and reliquaries. A bottled sunrise guttered. A love that had outlived its lovers dimmed to a sullen ember. Without stars, without wards, everything impossible in the building was running on borrowed light—and Mara knew, with the sick certainty of an auditor reading a falsified column, exactly where the borrowing was coming from.

They pulled the night's ledgers toward them and began, properly this time, to audit.

* * *

Sef kept the memory-brokerage on the third floor, in a room that smelled of cold iron and old rain. She was small, silver-haired, precise as a scalpel, and she had been buying and selling other people's yesterdays since before Mara was born—or so she claimed, though her own memories were conspicuously not for sale.

"You look terrible," she said, not looking up from her scales. On one pan sat a phial of someone's first snowfall; on the other, counterweights made of pressed grief. "Everyone looks terrible lately. Starless nights are hard on the complexion."

"Corvane's ledgers," Mara said. "I need the ones he keeps off-book."

Sef's hands went still over the scales. That was answer enough, but she gave words anyway, because Sef never left a transaction unpriced. "Off-book entries cost. You know my rates."

"I know your rates. I'm asking what he's paying."

That got her attention. She set down the phial and studied Mara with eyes like polished slate. "He's paying in oaths," she said. "Wholesale. Do you know how much a sworn oath weighs, child? A swordspeaker's oath could tip a kingdom. Corvane has been buying them by the crate."

"Hollowing them out," Mara said. "Like the courier."

"Worse than hollowed." Sef's voice dropped, and for the first time in the years Mara had known her, it sounded old. "An oath needs a speaker. Take the oath, keep the speaker breathing, and you have a well that refills itself. He's got forty of them in the undercroft below the counting-house. Forty people who sold him their vows and can't remember why their mouths taste of ash." She paused. "Forty-one, if you count the one who came in last night. Young woman. Sold a marriage-vow. Cried the whole time. Corvane called it *good stock*."

Mara's hands were steady on the desk. They had learned steadiness the way other people learned prayer. "Where is the first witness?"

Sef went very quiet. The scales ticked, ticked, ticked, weighing nothing at all.

"That question costs more than coin," she said finally. "That question costs me everything I've built here, if anyone learns I answered it."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

Sef looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached beneath her workbench and drew out a ledger older than any in the guild's archive—older, Mara realized with a lurch, than the guild itself. Its cover was scarred leather, and stamped into it was a spiral of thirteen turns.

"The first witness is a woman named Ilsa Vey," Sef said. "She was the covenant's record-keeper before it broke. She keeps a lighthouse on the Saltreach coast, two days' ride north. She's been waiting thirty years for someone to come ask her the right questions." Sef slid the ledger across the desk. "Corvane knows about her. He's been saving her for last. Witnesses are strongest when they're the only ones left."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I broker memories, child, and I know a debt when I see one." Sef's slate eyes held something that might, in a softer person, have been shame. "Thirty years ago, I sold Corvane his first oath. I told myself it was just commerce. One vow, one buyer, no harm done." She turned back to her scales. "You want to know where complicity lives? It lives in the interest. Every year, the debt grows. Go north. Find Ilsa Vey. And when you reforge whatever's broken—" her hands resumed their work, steady now, deliberate, "—remember that some of us would rather be forgiven than forgotten."

* * *

The counting-house stood at the heart of the guild district, all black stone and honest arithmetic, and Mara walked past it twice before letting themselves be seen.

They had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being overlooked. A clerk's stoop. A ledger-clutching hurry. Eyes that apologized for existing. It had worked on customs officers, on wardens, on the guild's own enforcers. But as Mara paused beneath the counting-house eaves, pretending to check a receipt, they felt the building notice them.

Not the guards. Not the clerks. The *building*—or whatever Corvane had grown into its foundations. The stones hummed at a frequency just below hearing, and Mara recognized the frequency because they had felt it once before, long ago, in the presence of a swordspeaker mid-oath. Power, drawn up through the earth like water through roots.

Through the high windows, lamplight fell across rows of desks. And between the desks moved figures that did not quite move like people. Smooth. Economical. Faces pleasant and vacant as unsold goods. Oath-speakers, walking, working, breathing—their vows extracted and stored somewhere below, their selves worn thin as secondhand parchment.

One of them paused at a window. A young man, ink-stained fingers, a face Mara almost recognized from the guild's hiring rolls. He stared out at the starless sky with an expression that wasn't longing, exactly. Longing required knowing what you'd lost.

Mara's hand found the covenant fragment inside their coat. The moving ink had stilled against their skin, as if listening. As if *waiting*.

*I signed those hiring rolls,* they thought. *I audited those ledgers. I saw the columns that didn't balance and I looked away, because looking away was cheaper than looking.*

Twenty years of invisibility, and every year of it had been purchased with someone else's hollowing. The dread from the courier's death curdled now into something harder. Not guilt—guilt was a luxury for people with time. This was closer to resolve, the kind that comes from realizing the door you've been hiding behind was load-bearing, and you helped build it.

Below, the young man turned away from the window. Mara memorized his face. There would be a ledger entry for him somewhere—a purchase price, a storage location, a line item in Corvane's ascension. When this was over, if there was an *after*, someone would have to find every one of those lines and speak them back into the people they'd been taken from.

But first: Saltreach. First: Ilsa Vey, and the right questions.

Mara turned from the counting-house and walked north, and behind them the building hummed its hungry note, and above them the sky stayed dark, and in their coat the covenant fragment began, very softly, to warm—like a hand remembering how to hold another hand, like a word remembering it had once been spoken aloud.

Two days' ride. Thirty years of waiting. And somewhere ahead, a lighthouse burning against a sky with nothing left to guide anyone home to—except, perhaps, a promise that had never actually died.

Mara walked faster.
