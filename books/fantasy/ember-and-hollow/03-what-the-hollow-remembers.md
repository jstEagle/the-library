## 3. What the Hollow Remembers

The Hollow did not open. It *remembered*.

Mara stood at the edge of the salt flats where the border wards had died, and the ground simply recalled that it had once been a door. The air split along a seam older than language, and through it came the smell of cold iron and cut grass — the two smells of every promise ever kept.

"You're certain?" Sef asked behind them. The memory-broker's hands were shaking, which for Sef was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"No," Mara said. "But certainty is just a trade with bad terms."

They stepped through with their two witnesses: Ivetta Crane, who had signed the original pact as a girl of nine and now carried her signature in a scar across her palm; and Old Brammel, last of the ward-masons, who had built the borders and never forgiven himself for how well they'd held.

The Hollow received them like a creditor receiving debtors.

It was not the grey nothing the guild's forbidden texts described. It was a country of standing stones and unlit hearths, of rivers that ran backward toward their sources, of doors set into hillsides with no houses behind them. Everything here was a memory of usefulness. Everything waited to be needed again.

And above it all hung the unborn moon — a pale disc like an unfired plate, thirteen faint rings etched into its face, twelve of them dark. Corvane had filled eleven already. Somewhere in that sky, a twelfth ring was drinking itself full.

"First clause is at the Hearthstone," Ivetta said, walking without hesitation, as if forty years hadn't passed since she'd last stood here. "We spoke it around fire. So we speak it around fire again."

Mara drew the covenant fragment from their coat. Their own oath-mark glowed on it, thin and silver: *I will hold the line between what is owed and what is taken.* They had written those words at nineteen, kneeling on stone, certain they would die before they broke them.

They had been wrong about so many things.

At the Hearthstone — a black boulder split down the middle like a mouth — Mara knelt and laid the fragment flat. "How does this work? Exactly?"

"Exactly?" Brammel laughed, a dry sound like a shutter closing. "You speak the clause true, and the pact takes payment. It doesn't take blood or years, girl. It takes *you*. Every line you forge costs a memory of who you were before the first oath. That was the price the original signers paid, and why they scattered afterward. Why do you think none of them could say what they'd lost?"

Mara looked at Ivetta. "What did you lose?"

"I don't know," she said simply. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Corvane felt them enter the way a man feels lice.

He sat in the crown of the Unfinished Tower — his tower now, raised from the bones of the old moon-temple — and watched the twelfth ring fill. Below him, the harvested oaths churned in their great glass vessels, thousands of small bright promises stripped from their speakers, who wandered the mortal realm now with pleasant smiles and nothing behind their eyes. He had told himself, at the beginning, that he was saving them. An oath spoken into a vessel could never be broken; a broken oath damned the speaker. He had *saved* thousands of souls from damnation.

That story had stopped working somewhere around the ninth ring. He no longer required stories. He required only completion.

"They've crossed," said his steward, a hollow-eyed man whose name Corvane had purchased along with everything else the man owned.

"I know." Corvane rose. His reflection in the obsidian wall startled him, as it always did now — half of it still the guildmaster's lean, grey-bearded face, half of it something luminous and cratered, a face becoming geography. "Villalobos. The little ledger-keeper. Hiding in my own guild for six years, and I never once checked the oldest accounts." He smiled, and the smile cracked the luminous side of his face like drying mud. "Sentimental of me to leave them alive."

"Shall I bring guards?"

"No. Bring the *offer*. And bring my writing desk. If I'm going to negotiate, I'll do it properly."

* * *

The first clause took Mara's name.

Not the name people called them — that stayed, worn and practical, *Mara* like a tool handle. It took the name their mother had whispered over their cradle, the one they'd kept folded away all these years like a letter never sent. They spoke the words of the clause — *Let the line be held between what is owed and what is taken, and let the holders be many, not one* — and the Hearthstone drank, and the memory went out of them like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

They knew they had lost something. They could feel the shape of the absence, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. But they could not grieve it, because grief requires knowing what you mourn.

"That's the price," Ivetta said quietly. She had gone grey-faced watching. "Every time. Are you—"

"Second clause," Mara said. Their voice sounded strange to them. Lighter. Emptier shelves creak differently. "*Let the witnesses be bound by choice, renewed each turning, never inherited.*"

The stone drank again. This time it took the taste of bread baked in a specific kitchen, on a specific morning, by hands that were not theirs but belonged to someone who had loved them. Mara knew this because they could remember *that* there had been such a morning, and remember loving it, and remember nothing else. A memory with the center scooped out.

Brammel caught their elbow as they swayed. "There are nine clauses left, girl. You'll walk out of here a stranger wearing your own face."

"Then I'll introduce myself," Mara said, "and mean it."

* * *

Corvane arrived at the Hearthstone alone, carrying a desk.

It was, Mara had to admit, a very fine desk — black lacquer, brass fittings, inkwell carved from meteoric iron. He set it down on the scorched earth as though the apocalypse were a dinner party, seated himself, and began to write while his half-luminous face watched them over the top of it.

"Mara Villalobos," he said pleasantly. "Or whatever's left. I can see the holes from here. You're forging clauses the honest way — paying retail. Admirable. Stupid, but admirable." He turned the page. "Here is my counteroffer. One document, one signature. I restore every memory the covenant has taken from you — all of them, back to your mother's voice, back to your cradle-name — and in exchange you give me the third witness."

"There are three of us," Mara said. "Me, Ivetta, Brammel. Which third?"

"The mason. The wards were his design. His craft-memory is the keystone of the thirteenth turn. Give him to my vessels and I'll make you whole." He looked up, and for one moment the guildmaster's face was entirely human, entirely tired. "Mara. We are the same. Two people who buried themselves in ledgers because the world outside asked too much. I'm offering you the only mercy either of us will ever get: to be who you were again."

And the terrible thing — the thing Mara would carry into the next hour like a splinter — was that they understood. Completely. They wanted their mother's whisper back more than they wanted breath.

"No," Mara said.

"No?" Corvane's pen stopped.

"You want me to buy myself back with Brammel's self. That's not restoration, that's a *trade route*, and I've spent six years auditing trades like it. Every impossible thing in our ledgers was bought from someone who couldn't afford it." Mara stood, and the emptiness inside them rang like a struck bell. "You didn't save those oath-speakers, Corvane. You foreclosed on them. And I helped file the paperwork."

Something moved behind his cratered face — not anger. Fear. "Then you'll die empty," he said, "and the moon will rise anyway."

"Probably," Mara agreed. "But the stars come back either way. That's the difference between us. I'm willing to pay for it."

They drew their blade.

* * *

Swordspeech was not fencing. Fencing was argument; swordspeech was *law*.

When Mara's blade met Corvane's beneath the unborn moon, the strikes were sentences. His opening cut was a claim of ownership — *this sky is mine, purchased and paid* — and it hit like a falling tower, and Mara answered with the second clause, *bound by choice, never inherited*, and the air between them tore white.

They fought across the Hollow's remembering country, through fields of doors and rivers running home, and every exchange cost them both. Corvane's blows stole — he was harvesting even now, even mid-duel, pulling fragments of Mara's past with each touch, and Mara felt childhood rooms go dark, felt faces blur, felt languages they'd once spoken drain out of their mouth. But Mara's blade gave back. Each parry was a clause, and each clause forced the land itself to remember its obligations, and the standing stones began to hum, and the backward rivers hesitated, confused by hope.

Corvane fought brilliantly and fought frightened, and frightened brilliance makes mistakes. On the fourteenth exchange he reached for the twelfth ring to fuel a killing stroke — pulled power forward before it was fully gathered — and the ring *stuttered*.

Mara saw the opening and did not take his life. They took his *claim*.

"*This sky is not owned,*" they said, blade at his throat, voice ringing with borrowed law. "*It is held. In trust. By all who breathe under it.*"

The words sank into him like water into ash. The luminous half of his face dimmed — not healed, not human again, but *unclaimed*, returned to the sky it had been stolen from. He fell to his knees among the standing stones, a man suddenly holding nothing, and began, horribly, to laugh.

"You think that wins?" he gasped. "The twelfth ring is still filling, girl. My vessels are still pouring. Even dead, the ascension completes by dawn — and then there's no covenant left to forge, because there'll be no *mortal realm* left to bind. You've won a duel. You haven't won the night."

Above them, the unborn moon shuddered. Eleven rings burned. The twelfth, stuttering, resumed its slow, greedy fill.

Mara turned to Ivetta and Brammel, and found they could not remember why the woman's scarred palm mattered, or why the old man's tears made their chest ache — only that both things were true.

"How many clauses left?" they asked.

"Seven," Ivetta said.

"Then we'd better start spending," Mara said, and knelt at the Hearthstone as the twelfth ring drank the last of Corvane's harvest, and somewhere far above, the thirteenth ring — the one no signer had ever survived to speak — began, very softly, to glow.
