## 4. A Covenant of Thirteen Turns

The duel was over. Corvane lay in the ash of his own ambition, half-moonlight and half-man, his borrowed oaths bleeding out of him like light from a cracked lantern. The Hollow was quiet around us, the kind of quiet that comes after a scream.

I knelt beside him because someone should.

"You could still take it," he rasped. His face kept shifting—guildmaster, stranger, a boy I almost remembered being afraid of. "Restoration. Every memory back. Everything you were before you buried yourself in ledgers. Just say the word, Mara. Just betray them one more time."

"I already did," I said. "For years. That's how we got here."

He laughed, and it turned to dust in his throat. The last of the stolen oath-light left him in a long exhale, rising toward the empty sky where the thirteenth moon hung unborn—a pale ring, a promise with nothing inside it.

The two witnesses stood behind me. Old Vess, who had signed the first covenant with a thumbprint of her own blood, and the ferryman Halloway, who had carried the original signatories across the border when the worlds were new to each other. They had come this far on my word alone. Now they waited for the rest.

"The final clause," Vess said gently. "You know what it costs."

I did. That was the whole cruelty of it. The first clauses had taken pieces—my mother's kitchen, the smell of rain on the road to Halvern, the name of the person I'd loved before any of this. Each line spoken had hollowed me further, until I'd dueled Corvane running on fumes and fury and the stubborn arithmetic of a guild accountant: *what remains must be spent well.*

What remained was everything else. Who I was before the oath. Before the Guild of Impossible Things. Before I learned to trade in other people's miracles because I couldn't bear my own.

"Then let's be honest," I said, standing. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "That's the whole point of the new accord, isn't it? No more hiding."

Halloway bowed his gray head. Vess took my hand.

I turned to face the unborn moon and drew the blade—not to fight, but to speak. Swordspeech is not words. It is the body remembering truth and putting it into steel. I raised the blade the way you raise a lantern.

*Here is the last clause,* I said, in the old tongue that lives in the wrist and the shoulder and the breath. *I surrender every memory of who I was before the oath. Not to the Hollow. Not to the sky. To the accord itself—so that both realms may hold what I held, and know me as I was, and judge whether I am worth trusting now.*

The blade sang.

It didn't hurt, exactly. It was more like setting down a weight so heavy you'd forgotten you were carrying it, and discovering the shape it left behind. Memories rose out of me like heat off summer stone. A harbor town. A sister's laugh—I hadn't thought of her in twenty years, and now I never would again. A door I'd once been too much of a coward to knock on. My own name, before Mara. The face I wore before I learned to wear none at all.

I gave them all away. And the Hollow caught them, tenderly, the way you catch something you intend to keep.

Above me, the moon began to turn.

Not the slow patient turn of moons—it spun like a wheel finding its axle, and as it turned, the stars came back. One by one. First a single point of white over the eastern dark, then three, then a scatter, then a flood, as if the night sky were a ledger being repopulated line by line by a clerk who finally understood the accounts. The thirteenth moon completed its turn and settled into its place—not ruling the others, not crowned, just *present*. One more light among many, bound to all of them.

And through every star, faint as a pulse, I felt the memories I'd given. Somewhere up there was my sister's laugh. Somewhere, my mother's kitchen. Anyone under any star might dream them tonight. That was the bargain. That was the honesty of it: nothing hoarded, nothing hidden, everything shared or surrendered.

Vess's grip tightened on my hand. "It's done," she whispered. "Both realms are bound. An honest accord—imperfect, but honest."

"Imperfect," I agreed. My voice sounded strange to me. Thinner. Newer. "Good. Perfection is just another kind of lie."

* * *

We walked out of the Hollow at dawn, which existed again, which meant something.

The border wards reknit themselves around us as we crossed—not walls this time, but seams. Visible ones. Honest ones. You could see where one world ended and the other began, and instead of pretending the seam wasn't there, the new accord made it a doorway. Travelers would need papers. Witnesses would need signatures. Miracles would need receipts. The Guild of Impossible Things would have to become, impossibly, legitimate.

Corvane's tower stood gutted on the hill above the guild quarter, and the people were already climbing it—not to loot, but to look. To see the sky they'd gotten back. A child pointed at the thirteenth moon, still visible in the daylight, faint as a scar, and asked her mother what it was.

"That," said her mother, who had no idea she was talking to the woman who'd put it there, "is new."

Sef found me at the guild steps. The memory-broker looked worse than I felt, which I hadn't thought possible. Their coat was scorched, their ledgers gone, their hands shaking.

"They'll want a reckoning," Sef said. "The guild. For what Corvane did. For what we all let happen."

"I know."

"And you're the only oath-bound swordspeaker left. They'll want you to lead. Or they'll want you gone." Sef managed a crooked smile. "Possibly both before lunch."

"Let them want." I looked out at the city, at the seam-doorways opening along the border, at the first traders cautiously crossing with honest goods and honest prices. "But first—the ledgers are all ash. Every record of what was traded, what was owed, what was stolen. We start clean."

Sef blinked. "Start clean?"

"A fresh ledger. New entries only. What we owe going forward, not what we stole getting here." I pulled a blank book from the ruins of the guildmaster's desk—Corvane wouldn't be needing it—and set it on the step between us. "First entry: the realms are bound. Second entry: we remember what we did, even if I can't remember who I was. Third entry—"

I paused. Sef waited, quill ready.

"Third entry," I said slowly, "is blank. For whoever comes next. For whatever gets traded across that border that turns out to matter. For the child who asked about the moon." I tapped the page. "An honest ledger has room in it for things we can't predict."

Sef wrote the first two lines in a careful hand. Then they looked up at me, and their expression shifted—curiosity sharpening into something more urgent.

"Mara," they said. "There's a fourth thing. I found it in Corvane's private records, right before the tower fell. Something he bought early—before the oaths, before the ascension. A trade so old it predates the guild itself." They hesitated. "It's a debt. Owed *to* the Hollow. And according to the record, it was never paid."

The thirteenth moon hung overhead, faint and patient, and I felt it watching.

"What kind of debt?" I asked.

Sef turned the charred page toward me. At the bottom, in ink older than the guild, older than the border, was a single line—and beneath it, an oath-mark I recognized, because I'd seen it that first terrible night on a dying courier's fragment.

My own mark. But dated two hundred years before I was born.

"The original signatory," Sef said quietly, "never finished paying. And the new accord says all debts must be honest." They met my eyes. "Mara—the covenant remembers you. All the way back."

I picked up the quill. Some entries, it seemed, wrote themselves.

"Fourth entry," I said. "*Investigate.*"
