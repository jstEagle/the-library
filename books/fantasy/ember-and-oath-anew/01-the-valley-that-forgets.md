## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ash came down soft as snow.

Lior Hartwell had been told all her life that the Hollow Valley forgot things—that it was a place with holes in its memory, where the wind carried away names the way it carried away leaves. She used to think this was only an old woman's way of explaining why no mapmaker ever bothered to draw them properly, why travelers who wandered in on accident always seemed relieved to wander back out, why her own earliest years sat behind her like a wall of fog with only a few shapes showing through: a door, a fire, a voice saying *stay close to me*.

But standing in the Ember House at dusk, with the last light failing through the high windows and forty-one jars of ash arranged before her in their cedar niches, she understood that forgetting was not a metaphor here. It was a discipline. It was the whole architecture of their lives, laid brick by brick by people long dead, and Lior was one of the bricks now too—she just hadn't been one for very long.

She lifted the first jar from its shelf and held it up to the ember-light.

Inside, the ash was fine and grey, sifted twice through muslin so that nothing coarse remained, nothing that might once have been something else—a splinter of bone, a fleck of cloth, a tooth. The guild did not speak about what made the ash. They spoke only of what it *was*: the residue of souls that could not stay, ground down into something safe, something the valley's silence could hold without trembling. Each jar bore a number burned into its lid in Elder Maren's careful hand, and beneath the number, a word or two in the old script that nobody under thirty could read anymore. Lior had asked Maren once what the words said. Maren had looked at her for a long moment—the kind of look that measures you against something you cannot see—and then said only, *They say remember, which is a strange thing to write on a jar of forgetting.*

Lior set the jar back in its niche and took down the next.

This was the work of the youngest keeper, and everyone in the guild made sure she knew it was an honor. Tending the jars meant turning each one at the turn of the season so the ash settled evenly. It meant trimming the wicks in the ember-lamps that kept the cedar shelves warm and dry, because damp ash clumped, and clumped ash *sang*—a faint, high whine at night that old Tamlin swore he'd heard once in his boyhood and never wanted to hear again. It meant walking the rows after dark with a lamp and counting, always counting, because a missing jar was not a small thing. A missing jar was a wound in the valley's skin.

Forty-one jars. Forty-one souls, if souls could be counted the way grain was counted, and the guild had decided long ago that they could, because the alternative was to count them the way grief was counted, and grief did not keep ledgers well.

Lior worked down the third row, her fingers moving with the automatic tenderness of ritual. The Ember House smelled of cedar smoke and cold stone and, underneath everything, a sweetness like apples going over in a cellar barrel—the smell of ash itself, which she had stopped noticing years ago except when she left the valley and came back, and then it hit her like a wave, and she understood that other places must smell of other things entirely. What, she sometimes wondered. What did the world outside smell like, when it wasn't this?

She would find out soon enough. Though she didn't know that yet.

* * *

Outside, the valley was putting on its festival face.

The Founding Festival fell tomorrow—the one night of the year when the whole guild gathered in the lower meadow and lit the great bonfire, and the children ran the bounds of the wards with lanterns, and everyone ate honey-cakes until they were sick, and Elder Maren stood on the flat stone and read the Oath of Ash aloud while the fire cracked and spat and the valley listened. Everyone said the valley listened. Lior had grown up believing it the way she believed in rain: not as a doctrine but as a fact of weather. When four hundred voices swore themselves to silence together, the air above the meadow went still in a way that stillness elsewhere never managed—not empty stillness but *held* stillness, like a breath taken and kept.

She stepped out of the Ember House into the blue hour and felt the familiar press of the mountains on either side of her, the two great shoulders of the Karroch range leaning together overhead, leaving only that narrow slit of sky the valley got, the one the old folk called the Hearth-Gap because the evening star came through it every night like a spark rising off a banked fire. Tonight the star was already there, early and bright, and below it the terraces were strung with unlit lanterns waiting for tomorrow, hundreds of them, paper and horn and one or two of actual glass that the guild traded salt for, decades back, from people whose names had worn off the ledger pages.

The valley floor was green this time of year, thickly green, the barley high enough to hide a kneeling child, the orchards heavy with fruit nobody sold because there was nobody to sell it to. The Hollow Valley fed itself. It clothed itself in wool from its own sheep. It buried its dead on the north slope where the soil turned to stone, and carved no names on the stones, because names were the first thing the valley forgot and the last thing it could afford to keep. Instead each grave marker bore a symbol: a wheel for a farmer, a flame for a keeper, a bell for the crier's office that hadn't existed in Lior's lifetime, a cup for the brewer, a needle for the seamstress. You lived your trade and then your trade stood guard over your bones, and your name went into the wind, and the wind took it down to the river, and the river carried it out past the ward-stones into the world that had forgotten you first.

Lior walked the path toward the lower meadow, past the drying sheds and the smithy that only ever shoed horses and mended plows, because iron shaped for war was forbidden—had been forbidden since the founding, since whatever founding actually was. That was another thing the valley kept vague on purpose. There was a story the children were told, and Lior had been a child once, and had been told it: that long ago the gifted ones of the world had grown terrible, that magic had eaten kingdoms from the inside like rot in a beam, and that a handful of ordinary people had fled up into the mountains and found this hollow place where magic could not follow, and had sworn an oath upon cold ash to keep it that way forever. The oath was simple. The oath was the whole religion, if you could call it that:

*We carry no flame but the hearth-flame. We keep no power but the power of our hands. We will be small, and we will be safe, and we will forget what we were so that we need never become it again.*

Something like that. Every year Maren read the true words and every year Lior heard them fresh, and every year by midwinter she had let them blur again, the way the valley blurred everything, gently, like water wearing at a footprint.

She had never questioned it. That was the thing she would understand later, much later, standing in places where questioning was the only currency worth anything: that she had been raised inside an answer so complete that she had never once thought to ask what question it replied to. Why *here*? Why *ash*? Why did the guild keep jars of the stuff in a locked house with ember-lamps burning, if fire was the one thing they'd sworn off? Why did the wards hum at night in winter, a sound you could feel through your boots if you stood on the north road too long? Why did Maren flinch, just slightly, just once a year, when the festival fire leapt highest?

These were questions Lior did not ask yet. But they were there, the way a stone is there under a field, waiting for the plow.

* * *

The meadow was busy with preparation. Tables dragged out of the meeting hall and scrubbed. Boys stacking brushwood for the bonfire in the old pattern—hollow at the center, always hollow, though none of them could say why, only that the pattern was older than the pattern's reason. Girls braiding rush-lantern handles. Old Tamlin sitting on his usual stump, whittling a whistle he would give away tomorrow and regret giving away by the following morning, and complaining, as always, that the young people stacked wood like people who had never seen a proper fire, which was true, because proper fires were another thing the valley had voted to forget.

"Lior!" called Wenna, the brewer's daughter, sixteen and loud, waving a honey-dripping spoon. "Taste this. If it's gone wrong I have to start over and I'll die."

Lior tasted it. It had not gone wrong; it had gone magnificent. "You'll be the death of us all," she said, and Wenna laughed, and for a moment Lior felt the thing she felt most evenings in the valley, the thing that was both the best and most dangerous feeling she knew: that she belonged here so completely that leaving would be like leaving her own hands behind.

That feeling again. She kept catching herself thinking *leaving*. It had started in the spring, a restlessness like an itch under the skin, and she had blamed the weather, and then the barley, and then a bad dream, and now it was nearly autumn and the itch had not gone. It had grown teeth. At night she lay awake listening to the wards hum and imagining the sound as a voice, and the voice saying one word over and over, a word she couldn't quite make out, a word that might have been *come* or might have been *out* or might have been her own name spoken by someone who knew it better than she did.

"You're staring at the Gap," Wenna said, following her gaze up to the slit of sky. "You do that lately. Staring at the sky like it owes you money."

"I'm staring at the star."

"The star's always there."

"That's what I mean," Lior said, and didn't know how to explain further, and Wenna didn't ask, because in the valley you learned early not to pull threads. Pulling threads was how sweaters unraveled, and sweaters were wool, and wool was survival, and survival was the whole point of everything.

* * *

Back in the Ember House, the last row waited.

Row seven, the oldest row, the jars with the deepest burn-marks on their lids, the script beneath the numbers faded almost to shadow. Lior saved it for last every season, though nobody had told her to. There was something about these jars—the weight of them, slightly heavier than the newer ones, as if age had packed the ash denser—and something about the quiet of that end of the house, where the ember-lamps burned lowest and the cedar shelves had gone dark as old blood.

She took down jar thirty-nine and turned it, as she always did, a quarter-turn clockwise, and as she always did, she paused with it in her hands a moment longer than the work required. Not because anything happened. Nothing ever happened. But her grandmother—no, not her grandmother, she corrected herself, *the woman who raised her*, because Lior's own line was one of the fogged-over parts, one of the shapes behind the wall—had tended this same row before her, and had taught her the quarter-turn, and had done it with a reverence that Lior had absorbed without understanding, the way you absorb a language before you know it has grammar.

*Someone is in here,* the reverence said. *Someone was a person.*

And then, quieter, the counter-voice, the guild's voice, the valley's voice: *and it is mercy that we do not ask who.*

Lior set jar thirty-nine home and moved to forty, and then forty-one, the last, the newest, added the spring before she was born—which meant, if she did the arithmetic she tried not to do, that no soul had come to the jars in twenty years. Twenty years of peace, Maren called it. Twenty years since the last time the valley had needed its terrible mercy. Twenty years of nothing arriving at the ward-stones asking to be let in, nothing burning, nothing hunted, nothing fleeing down from the passes with its edges smoking.

Twenty years of quiet. And yet the itch in Lior had started this spring, exactly this spring, as if some part of her had counted the years along with the jars and found the number significant, and had begun, quietly, without her permission, to wait.

She trimmed the lamps. She banked the hearth. She checked the lock on the cedar doors twice, because the youngest keeper always checked twice, and stepped out into the full dark.

The Hearth-Gap held its single star. The lanterns along the terraces waited, dark, for tomorrow's flame. Somewhere up-slope, the wards hummed their winter note, though it was only autumn, and Lior noticed that, and noticed herself noticing, and filed it away in the growing drawer of things she noticed and did not mention.

At the edge of the meadow, alone by the unlit bonfire, stood Elder Maren, leaning on her staff, looking not at the fire-pit but at the mountain pass beyond it—the notch in the western ridge where the world outside came nearest, where the road climbed down and away and became, eventually, other valleys, other towns, a river, a kingdom, a king. Maren was very old. Her spine had curved like a drawn bow over her seventy-odd years, and her eyes had gone pale as frost, but they were fixed on that notch with an intensity that made Lior stop walking.

"Elder?" Lior said.

Maren didn't turn. For a moment she said nothing at all, and the valley's silence pressed in around them both, that held-breath silence, that listening.

"Tomorrow," Maren finally said, "when I read the Oath, listen to the fourth line. Truly listen. Not the way you've listened every year of your life." Her voice was even, but underneath it Lior heard something she had never heard from Maren before, and it frightened her more than any sound the valley had ever made: hesitation. An old woman who had spoken the same words for fifty years, hesitating over them.

"What's in the fourth line?"

Maren turned then, slowly, and looked at her—looked at her the way she had looked at her over the jar-script, measuring her against something unseen—and whatever the measurement returned, it made her mouth go dry.

"Come to the Ember House after the festival," Maren said. "There is a page in the ledger I should have shown you years ago. There is a price written in it that the guild paid for this valley's safety, and I have kept it buried past all decency, and I find—" she stopped, steadied herself on her staff, and the frost-pale eyes closed briefly, "—I find I am too old to bury it another winter."

Then she walked away, up the terrace path, small and bent against the dark, and left Lior standing alone beside the hollow heart of the unlit fire, with the star overhead and the wards humming their out-of-season note, and the itch beneath her skin suddenly gone very still, the way a rabbit goes still—not calm, but listening.

Tomorrow the valley would swear its oath. Tomorrow the fire would leap and four hundred voices would promise to forget.

But tonight, for the first time in her life, Lior Hartwell understood that forgetting was not only what the valley did.

It was also what the valley was *for*—and that somewhere, buried in a ledger page, was the receipt.
