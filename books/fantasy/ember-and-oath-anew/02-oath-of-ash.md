## 2. Oath of Ash

Lior woke before the bell, the way keepers do.

It was not discipline that pulled her from sleep—after six years she had stopped pretending it was anything so clean. It was the jars. She could feel them the way a mother feels a sleeping child in the next room: not hearing them, exactly, but knowing their weight against the silence, and knowing instantly when one of them shifted. This morning none had shifted. But the valley itself felt strange, taut as a drumhead, and she lay for a long while under her wool blanket listening to the wards hum their wrong note through the stone floor of the keeper's dormitory, thin and high, like a wineglass sung by a wet finger.

Then the bell rang anyway—Hallow, the deaf old sexton, pulling the rope with his usual disregard for the hour—and Lior rose into the blue-black cold of Founding morning.

* * *

The ash-house stood at the center of the guild compound, older than every other building, older than the compound itself if you believed Elder Maren, who claimed it had been raised before the valley had a name. It was a low round structure of black river-stone with no windows and a single door, and inside it the guild's true treasure waited in the dark: three hundred and eleven jars of ash, each one sealed with wax and wire, each one holding what the guild existed to hold.

Lior lit the lamps one by one as she entered, and the light came up soft and amber across the shelves.

The jars were not beautiful. She had thought, when she was small and first apprenticed to the keeping, that they would be—that souls, even stored ones, even surrendered ones, would leave some glamour on their vessels, some shimmer visible only to the faithful. They did not. They were clay jars, squat and plain, glazed the color of old honey, filled to the shoulder with fine grey ash. The ash was the point. When a gifted person chose—or was chosen—to give up their gift, the gift did not vanish. Magic could not be destroyed; Maren said that the way other people said water is wet. It could only be moved. And in the Hollow Valley, where magic could not follow, the safest place to move it was into something inert and ordinary and utterly without power: ash, burned at the right hour, spoken over with the right words, packed down by hands that had sworn to forget what they were holding.

Three hundred and eleven jars. Three hundred and eleven people, most of them dead now, who had walked into this valley carrying fire in their blood and walked out of it hollow, safe, and diminished—and who had left the burning part behind on these shelves so that no hunter, no king, no hungry thing beyond the ridgeline would ever smell it on them again.

Lior walked the rows slowly, as she did every morning, touching each jar once with two fingers. It was not ritual, exactly. Or it was, but a private one, unwritten. *Good morning. Still here. Still held.* Some jars were warm to the touch and some were cool, and no one had ever been able to tell her why, and she had long ago stopped asking, because there were questions the elders answered and questions they looked at you strangely for asking, and this had become one of the second kind.

Jar ninety-one was warm today. It was always warm. It had been warm the day she was apprenticed, eight years ago, when she was nine and the jar was already older than her mother, and in all that time the warmth had never faded, never spread to its neighbors, never cooled. Lior rested her fingers on it a moment longer than the others.

"You'll make it proud," said a voice behind her, dry as shelf-dust.

She didn't jump. You learned early, in the ash-house, to know Maren's tread—the elder walked softly everywhere else in the valley but here she let her boots sound, deliberately, because startling a keeper among the jars was a sin with a name. Lior turned. Maren stood in the doorway with the dawn behind her, a grey shape in grey wool, her white braid coiled at the nape of her neck like a sleeping animal.

"It's always warm," Lior said. "Ninety-one."

"Everything is always something." Maren came forward into the lamplight. Her face was a map of creases, and like all maps it told you mostly where not to go. "Have you eaten?"

"I'm not hungry."

"That's not what I asked."

Lior almost smiled. "No, Elder."

"Then come eat. Today of all days, the youngest keeper swears on an empty stomach, and faints into the fire pit, and I have to explain to four hundred people why my best student has a burnt backside." Maren's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile and never quite arrived. "Out. The jars will keep. They're very good at keeping. It's the living who are bad at it."

At the door Lior paused, hand on the iron ring, and asked the question she had promised herself on the terrace last night that she would not ask.

"Elder. Last night. The wards. Have they ever sounded like that before?"

Maren was quiet long enough that Lior turned fully around. The old woman stood among the shelves with the lamp-light pooling in the hollows of her face, and for one unguarded moment she looked—not frightened, nothing so useful as frightened. She looked like someone doing arithmetic she hated.

"Once," Maren said. "Before your time. Before nearly everyone's time." Then she lifted her chin, and the moment closed over like water. "Eat your breakfast, Keeper Hartwell. Tonight you'll swear an oath, and oaths sit better on a full belly."

* * *

The Founding Festival began, as it always began, with hunger.

By midmorning the whole valley had come down from its farms and terraces and fold-houses into the bowl of meadow below the guild compound, and the air filled with woodsmoke and frying batter and the wet-wool smell of four hundred people standing close together in autumn cold. Children ran between the adults' legs with whistles carved from reed. Old Ferren's fiddle struck up the same tune it struck up every year, the one about the miller's daughter and the river, and nobody danced yet because dancing came after the oath, but everybody tapped a foot, and the tapping sounded like rain on a roof.

Lior ate standing at the edge of it all, a bowl of porridge sweetened with the year's last honey, and watched the valley be happy, and found that happiness, today, looked like something she was watching through glass.

She knew everyone. That was the thing about the Hollow Valley—you couldn't help knowing everyone. There were the Calloways with their apple orchard and their seven loud daughters. There was Widow Ashe, who mended nets though the nearest river was a day's walk, because her hands needed work the way lungs need air. There was Tam's—there was the Hartwell plot, empty now, the farmhouse sold years ago to a family named Brook who kept the orchard well and whose children had never heard the name that used to belong to it. Lior's own family, reduced to herself and a father who had gone quiet as a shut book after the sickness took her mother, and who stood this morning at the far side of the meadow talking to the smith as if the day were any day, as if his daughter were not about to swear away the last loose thread of her childhood.

Four hundred souls. Every one of them descended from the gifted, or gifted themselves once, or kin to the gifted—every one of them a person the world outside had wanted to burn, cage, or use, depending on the decade and the king. And every one of them safe, because the valley forgot, and made them forgettable.

"Lior!" Pell Brook, ten years old, gap-toothed, skidding to a stop in front of her with his brothers behind him. "Is it true you get to hold the ember this year? Da says the youngest keeper carries it. He says he saw it once when he was little and it's the size of a fist."

"It's smaller than a fist," Lior said. "And it's not a thing you hold. It's a thing you carry. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

Pell's middle brother, Hesta, age twelve and therefore omniscient, rolled her eyes. "The difference is you don't drop it, stupid."

"The difference," Lior said, crouching to their level, "is that holding is wanting to keep something. Carrying is promising to bring it somewhere. You carry the ember to the fire. You don't keep it. Nobody keeps it."

"Where does it go? After?"

That was a question with an answer the children were given every year and never remembered, because the answer was the kind that slides off young minds like rain off slate. "Into the fire," Lior said. "And out of the fire into all of us. That's the whole point of tonight. Eat your festival bread before Hesta eats it for you."

They scattered. Lior stayed crouched a moment longer than she needed to, looking at the trampled grass, thinking: *nobody keeps it.*

Then why, she wondered, did it feel lately like something was being kept? Kept from her. Kept in her. The itch beneath her skin had not returned since last night—it had gone still, rabbit-still—but stillness was not absence. She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, feeling the ordinary thud of her ordinary heart, and told it to be ordinary, and it pretended to agree.

* * *

At dusk, the guild assembled.

Not the valley—the guild. Four hundred became sixty: the keepers, the wardens, the sextons, the hearth-tenders, those who had taken the low vows of service and would tonight take or renew the high one. They filed into the great circle of standing stones above the compound, the Oath Ring, which predated the guild and possibly the valley, twelve stones tall as three men arranged in a ring wider than Maren's ash-house, worn smooth and lichen-silvered by centuries of weather and swearing. In the center of the ring, a fire pit waited, stacked with oak and ash-wood, and on top of the stack, in an iron cradle, sat the ember.

Lior carried it.

It was smaller than a fist—a lump of slagged glass and metal no bigger than a hen's egg, dark until you looked too long, and then not dark at all. It sat in her cupped palms like a sleeping bird, heavy past its size, warm past reason, and through her skin she felt it doing the thing it always did on oath-night: pulling. Not hard. Gently. The way the tide pulls at sand. As if it wanted to be reminded of what it was part of.

The sixty took their places by rank and seniority, and Lior, youngest, stood at the ring's northern point with the ember in her hands and the cold coming up through her boot-soles, and watched the sun die behind the western ridge in a spill of copper and bruise-colored cloud.

Elder Maren entered the ring last, as she always did, leaning on her staff of blackthorn. She stopped at the fire pit. She did not look at the sixty. She looked, for a long moment, at the sky overhead—the same sky Lior had searched last night from the terrace, hunting the star that should have been there and wasn't.

"Sixty years I have led this swearing," Maren said, and her voice, old and cracked as it was, reached every corner of the ring the way it always did, as if the stones themselves carried it. "And every year I say the same words, because the words are older than me and wiser than me, and because some things must be said the same way or they stop being true. But this year I am going to say one thing differently. Bear with an old woman. The words can spare a minute."

Silence in the ring. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"You know the story we tell," Maren said. "You've known it since you were small enough to believe stories are simple. A hundred years ago, the world outside went mad for magic. Kings hunted the gifted, churches burned them, cities built collars for them, and the gifted of this country did what the hunted always do—they ran. And a handful of them, our founders, found this valley: a wound in the world where magic cannot follow. A place of silence. They built walls of forgetting, they poured their gifts into jars of ash, and they made a bargain—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the word, "—a bargain, whose terms we honor tonight. And the hunters came to the ridgeline and looked down and saw nothing worth descending for. Empty hills. Forgettable people. And they rode on, and they have ridden on for a hundred years, and here we all stand."

She paused. Somewhere beyond the ring, a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

"That is the story we tell," Maren said. "It is true. But it is not the whole truth, and I am too old, this year, to pretend the difference doesn't matter. So hear the rest of it, once, and then forget it again if you must—that is your right, and the valley's mercy, and I will not think less of you."

Lior's heart was suddenly very loud in her ears.

"Bargains have two sides," Maren said. "We paid. We did not pay in coin. We paid in the only currency such powers accept." Her eyes moved across the sixty faces, and Lior felt them pass over her like a searchlight, felt them *stop*, for half a heartbeat, on hers—and move on. "What we gave to buy this valley's silence, I will not say tonight. Perhaps I will never say it. But understand this much: the peace you were born into was not free, and it was not cheap, and somewhere under this valley lies the receipt. If ever the wards flicker—if ever the silence sounds less like safety and more like a held breath—remember that debts do not die of old age. They wait. They are patient the way stone is patient. And the price of protection, children, is always collected in full."

Nobody breathed. The ember pulsed once in Lior's hands, slow, like a second heart answering the first.

Then Maren straightened, and the strangeness passed off her like a coat removed, and she was only an old woman with a staff and a ceremony to run. "Kindle the fire," she said.

* * *

The lighting of the pyre was Lior's alone to do.

She stepped to the pit's edge. Below her, the stacked wood waited, and above the wood, in its iron cradle, the space where the ember would sit through the burning. The rite was simple and terrible: the keeper carried the ember three times around the unlit pyre—once for the founders, once for the forgotten, once for those to come—and on the third turn she set it in the cradle and spoke the first line of the oath, and the ember leapt to the wood without spark or flint, because that was what embers did, that was the one small miracle the valley permitted itself, the seed remembering how to be a fire.

Lior walked the circle. Once for the founders. The stones slid past her, silvered faces in the torchlight. Twice for the forgotten. Her feet knew the path; her mind was elsewhere, in a ledger page, in a held breath, in the arithmetic on Maren's face. Three times for those to come—

—and on the third turn, setting the ember into the cradle, she heard it.

Not with her ears. With the place beneath her skin where the itch lived, the rabbit-still place that had gone quiet last night and had been lying about staying quiet ever since. The ember touched the cradle and the whole ring *sounded*, one deep note through the ground and up through her bones, and for a single instant Lior Hartwell stood inside the founding miracle and understood it from the inside: the ember was not a relic. It was a plug. A keystone. The smallest visible piece of something vast that was turned *off*, and every year the guild renewed the turning-off, and every year the valley forgot that anything had ever been on.

And beneath the note, so faint she would spend years deciding whether she'd truly heard it, something answered. From below. From under the valley. One pulse, patient as stone.

*Debts,* Maren had said, *do not die of old age.*

The pyre caught. Flame climbed the stack with a sound like a long exhale, and the ember blazed at its heart, and the sixty voices rose together in the old words:

*"From fire we came, to ash we return; what we were, we yield; what we are, we keep; let the valley hold us, and let us hold the valley, and let no flame follow us home."*

Lior's mouth moved with theirs. Her voice joined the sixty. And all the while, in the place beneath her skin, the stillness listened, and did not speak, and did not leave.

Afterward, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the guild had dispersed into the dark singing the miller's daughter song, Maren touched Lior's elbow at the edge of the ring.

"You heard it," the old woman said quietly. It was not a question.

"Heard what?" said Lior.

Maren studied her for a long time, torchlight moving in her eyes. "Nothing," she said at last. "Go to bed, Keeper Hartwell. Tomorrow is a festival day, and festivals are for forgetting."

She walked away, staff clicking on stone, and left Lior standing in the ring with the dying fire and the missing star and the certainty, settling into her bones like cold, that tomorrow—festival or no—she was going to find that ledger page if she had to read every book in the guild archive down to its binding-glue.

Because the valley had paid a price. And Lior had heard it breathing underneath her feet.

And it had sounded, she thought, horribly, unmistakably, *familiar*.
