## 3. The Crier's Bell

The bell woke the valley before the sun did.

Lior heard it first as a tremor in the ash-jars, a thin vibration running through the shelf where forty souls slept their long sleep of gray powder and glass. She had her hands around the third jar of the morning—old Wenna's, whose ashes always seemed warmer after rain—and she felt it hum against her palms like a struck tuning fork.

She stood very still.

In nineteen years of keeping, the jars had never sung.

* * *

The Hollow Valley did not get visitors. This was not a boast but a condition, as natural to the valley as the way fog pooled in its low fields or the way the river ran backward on certain mornings, seeping uphill toward the mountains as though remembering something. Magic could not follow you here. That was the whole of the covenant, spoken at every Founding Festival until the words wore smooth as river stones: *what is gifted stays outside; what is human is sheltered within.* The wards that ringed the valley were not walls so much as an absence—a hole cut in the world's weave, through which nothing enchanted could pass. Spells slid off it. Curses forgot themselves at the ridgeline. Even prophecy, Elder Maren said, went blind crossing the fells, which was why the guild had chosen this place two hundred years ago and why no one with power had ever managed to find it since.

So bells did not ring. Roads into the valley carried carters and pilgrims-of-nothing, peddlers who sold buttons and salt, the occasional lost shepherd. No one rode down the switchback trail with brass on their saddle and purpose in their spine, because no one who mattered knew the valley existed.

But someone was ringing the chapel bell—the alarm peal, three-and-one, three-and-one—and the sound came down through the morning mist like a hand closing around the valley's throat.

Lior set Wenna's jar back on its shelf with exaggerated care, the way her mother had taught her: *two hands, always two hands; they trusted us with everything they had left.* Then she pulled her cloak from its peg and ran.

* * *

The village green was already filling when she reached it. She came around the tithe-barn and stopped, breath smoking in the cold, taking in the scene the way Maren had taught her to read a room—slowly, completely, before speaking or acting.

The whole valley had turned out. Farmers in their good coats, millers still dusted white, the tanner's children barefoot and wide-eyed because there had been no time for shoes. They arranged themselves without being asked into the loose crescent they formed every festival, faces toward the chapel steps. It was habit older than any of them.

On the steps stood a stranger.

He was not what Lior expected, though she could not have said what she'd expected—a soldier, maybe, or a sorcerer with frost on his shoulders. Instead he looked like a clerk. A narrow man in traveling grays gone muddy to the knee, his horse steaming at the rail behind him, a scroll case slung across his back and a bell of polished bronze hanging from his belt. He had the patient, faintly bored expression of a man who had delivered bad news so many times it had become merely weather to him.

At the foot of the steps stood Elder Maren, and Lior's stomach dropped, because Maren was not looking at the stranger at all. She was looking up at the sky above the chapel, at the old wardstone set into its gable—a fist-sized lump of gray granite that had done nothing whatsoever for two centuries except sit there being ordinary.

The stone was flickering.

Not glowing—that would have been remarkable enough. Flickering, the way a candle gutters in a draft, its presence stuttering between *is* and *isn't*, between stone and something older than stone. When it flickered *in*, Lior saw veins of ember-light crawl across its surface, orange as forge-coal, the same light she tended every evening in the guildhouse lamp. When it flickered *out*, it was just a rock again, and somehow that was worse, because she understood suddenly that the rock-look was the disguise and the fire was the truth, and the truth was failing.

A hundred years, Maren had told her once. The wards hadn't wavered in a hundred years.

"Lior." Maren didn't turn her head. Her voice was low, pitched for Lior alone beneath the murmur of the crowd. "Stand beside me. Say nothing."

"What's happening to it?"

"I don't know." And Maren's hands, folded at her waist, were trembling—not much, barely a shiver, but Lior had watched those hands calm panicking animals and hold the guild oath-stone steady through lightning storms. "That frightens me more than the man does."

The crier waited with professional courtesy until the crowd stilled. Then he unslung the scroll case, drew out a roll of vellum heavy with ribbon and wax, and rang his own small bell once—a bright, clean note, absurdly pretty against the guttering wardstone.

"Hear ye," he said, in the flat carrying voice of a man reciting for the hundredth time, "the proclamation of Vessic, First of His Name, rightful King of the Nine Reaches and the Free Cities beyond them."

Murmurs. Vessic's name had arrived ahead of him the way storms arrive—rumors on the autumn caravans, a merchant swearing the old king's line was ended, another swearing it was lies. The valley had filed it all away under *outside*, under *not ours*. Now the name stood on their chapel steps wearing boots.

"By decree of the Crown," the crier continued, "all persons possessing the gift of sorcery, hedge-craft, spirit-speaking, soul-sight, or any art wrought by means beyond the natural strength of mortal flesh—" he paused for breath, unhurried, "-are hereby commanded to present themselves for registration at any crown office within thirty days of this reading."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; Lior realized with a cold detachment that this was craft, that fear was a thing men learned to pour like water, filling whatever vessel they held it over.

"The registered shall be marked, licensed, and bound to such service as the Crown determines, in gratitude for their preservation." Another pause, another sip of silence. "Those who do not register within thirty days"—and here his voice changed, dropped its clerical boredom, took on something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet—-"shall be accounted outlaws of the gift, and burned."

No one moved. Somewhere behind Lior a child began to cry and was hushed too quickly.

"Burned," someone repeated near the front—it was Bram Ostler, the smith, and his voice cracked on the word like a boy's. "Burned. You mean—"

"I mean what the parchment says, master smith." The crier rolled the vellup closed with a snap. "I have read it in eleven towns and four cities this season. In Corren's Landing they built the pyres in the market square before I'd finished the second paragraph. I'm a reader, not an arguer. My work is the words; the fire has its own arrangements." He glanced at the crowd—at the farmers, the children, the ordinary frightened human faces—and something flickered across his expression that might, in a better world, have been pity. "Though I'll say this much, since no one pays me to: your valley smells wrong to my horse, your chapel stone has been watching me since I crested the ridge, and I've been doing this job long enough to know when a place doesn't want finding." He shouldered the scroll case. "Thirty days. There are crown offices in Harrow Ford and Southreach. That's all."

He rang his little bell once more, mounted his mud-gray horse, and rode back up the switchback trail without looking behind him, and the valley watched him go the way people watch a wave draw back from a shore they suddenly distrust.

* * *

They gathered that evening in the guildhouse, all nine keepers, while the valley below shuttered its windows early and spoke in whispers.

The guildhouse was the oldest building in the Hollow Valley, older than the chapel, older than any family now living—dry-stone walls furred with moss, a roof of turf where wildflowers came up in summer. Inside it smelled of beeswax and cold hearth-smoke and, underneath everything, of ash: the particular sweet-mineral smell of the jars, forty souls' worth, which had soaked into the timber so thoroughly that new apprentices said you could taste it in the air you breathed.

The jars sat along the eastern wall in their cedar niches, and tonight Lior found herself unable to stop looking at them. Forty souls. Forty people, once—gifted people, witches and wonderworkers and hedge-healers, hunted in the old persecutions, who had come to the guild carrying the last free piece of themselves in their cupped hands and asked the keepers to hide it. The guild's founding work. Not sheltering the gifted—the valley couldn't do that, magic couldn't cross the wards—but sheltering what the gifted loved most: their souls, smuggled in over the fells in sealed jars, kept safe while their owners lived out plain, unremarkable, *unhunted* lives as ordinary farmers and weavers, stripped of power but alive.

It had always seemed to Lior the saddest and most beautiful thing in the world. Tonight it looked different. Tonight, with the crier's words still hanging in the rafters—*registered, marked, bound; burned*—it looked less like sanctuary and more like a larder.

"The wards flickered twice more at noon," said Keeper Aldous, the guild's oldest man after Maren, his knuckles white around his tea mug. "Dellan saw it from the high pasture. Said the whole chapel gable lit up like coals blown on."

"And went dark again?" Maren asked.

"Went dark again."

"That's not the point," said Yeva sharply. Yeva kept the southern jars, quick-tongued, sharp-eyed. "The point is it flickered *at all*. Grandmother used to say the wards were cut from the world's own bone. What shakes bone?"

"The crier shook it," said Aldous. "Or what the crier carries. That scroll had royal wax on it. Royal working."

"There's no working left on it," Lior said, and then everyone looked at her, because she hadn't meant to speak. Heat crawled up her neck. "I—I stood close to him. On the steps. There was nothing. No spell on the scroll, no charm on him, not even a blessing-charm, and every crown messenger I've ever heard tell wears one. If he'd been carrying active magic, the wards would have—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the gable, the ridge, the whole trembling architecture of their safety. "They'd have known he was coming. They'd have turned him around at the fells. He walked through because he was carrying nothing. Because to the valley he was just a man."

Silence. The hearth ticked and settled.

"Then what flickered the stone?" Yeva demanded.

"Perhaps the stone wasn't answering the crier at all," Maren said quietly.

Every head turned to her. She sat at the head of the table where she had sat for forty years, small and straight-backed, her gray braid coiled at her neck like a sleeping adder, and for a long moment she only looked into the fire. Lior had known her all her life—had been given to the guild as an infant, fostered among the keepers, raised on jar-tending and oath-learning and Maren's dry, sparing affection—and she knew every weather of the elder's face. She had never seen this one. It was grief rehearsing itself.

"Maren?" Aldous prompted gently.

"When I was young," Maren said, "newer than you, Lior, younger than Yeva, I asked the elder who kept me—old Mother Cass, rest her—why the valley needed hiding so badly. Why the persecution had followed the guild for two hundred years when other orders bought peace with tribute, with oaths, with service. Cass told me the valley's safety had been purchased like everything else." Her eyes stayed on the fire. "I asked what it cost. She said: *a price paid once, in full, to parties below.* And she told me never to ask again, and I was young, and I wanted to be elder someday, so I didn't."

The fire popped. Down in the village a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Parties below," Yeva repeated. "You don't mean—"

"I mean what I was told and no more, because no more is what I was given." Maren finally looked up, and her gaze crossed each of them and landed, and stayed, on Lior. Something passed over her face then—guilt was the nearest word for it, guilt arriving late and unwelcome, like a debt collector at midnight. "Tomorrow I will open the guild ledger to its deepest pages. There may be answers there. There may be nothing. But the wards are flickering, and Vessic's criers are riding every road in the Nine Reaches, and I find I am too old to keep other people's secrets when the bill for them comes due."

"What bill?" Lior asked. Her voice sounded strange to her, thin in the smoky air. "What bill, Maren? Whose?"

The elder held her eyes for a long moment. Outside, the wind came down off the fells and pressed against the turf roof, and somewhere above them all—Lior felt it rather than heard it, the way you feel a change in pressure before rain—the wardstone flickered again, a single pulse of ember-light through the dark, like a heartbeat skipping.

Like something waking.

"Sleep if you can," Maren said, rising, gathering her shawl. "All of you. Tomorrow we open the ledger."

And later, lying in her narrow bed in the keepers' dormitory with the smell of ash in the rafters and forty souls breathing their slow glass-bound sleep beside her, Lior stared at the ceiling beams and found her thoughts circling one image she could not put down: not the crier, not the fire-word *burned*, but Maren's face in the hearth-light when the old woman had said *the bill comes due*—that look of a person who has known for forty years exactly what was spent to buy the valley's peace, and has just remembered, with horror, that she was the one holding the receipt.

The compass of Lior's unease swung, and swung, and pointed nowhere she could name.

Yet.
