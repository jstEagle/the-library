## 4. What Maren Buried

The ledger smelled of tallow and old smoke, the way all the guild's records did, because every page of it had been written by candlelight in a valley where no magic could follow. Maren had tended that smell for forty years. She knew it the way a shepherd knows the breath of her oldest ewe—without thinking, without wanting to, without any escape from it.

She sat alone in the archive beneath the guildhall, three hours past midnight on the eve of the Founding Festival, with a lamp turned low and a key of black iron warming in her fist. Above her, she could hear the muffled sounds of preparation: hammering as the festival poles were set, laughter from the young keepers who had sneaked wine from the autumn casks, and once—faintly, unmistakably—the sound of Lior Hartwell's voice, rising and falling as she sang to the ash-jars the way she always did when she thought no one was listening.

Maren closed her eyes.

*Sing to them while you can, child,* she thought. *Sing to them while you still believe they are only jars.*

The key was for a drawer that officially did not exist. The guild's charter said the Ember Guild kept one record: the roll of souls in keeping, updated each Founding Festival, signed by the elders in ash-ink. That was true, as far as it went. But there had been a second book once—a thin one, bound in grey hide, kept by whoever among the elders carried the weight of what the valley's safety had cost. Maren had inherited it from Elder Corvane, who had inherited it from Elder Sef, who had inherited it weeping, if the story was true, from the founder herself.

Twenty years ago this winter, Maren had put that book in its drawer and told herself she would never open it again unless she absolutely had to.

She opened it now.

* * *

The drawer came out whole, a slab of cedar fitted so precisely into the archive wall that even Maren sometimes forgot which seam hid it. Inside lay the grey book, a bundle of letters tied with wire, and a single loose page wrapped in oilcloth—the page she had sworn, twenty years ago, to burn, and had not burned, because some part of her had understood even then that burning a thing does not unmake it. It only makes it harder to confess.

She unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that were steadier than she deserved.

The page was guild vellum, heavy and cream-colored, stamped at the head with the ember-and-circle that every keeper wore branded or inked or stitched somewhere on their body. The handwriting was her own. She remembered writing it. She remembered every stroke of it, the way a woman remembers the strokes of a knife.

*Received of the Undercourt, by the hand of its Broker of Names, one covenant of concealment, to hold for one hundred years or until the harvest ends, whichever comes first.*

*Given in payment: one soul, freely surrendered by its kin, unregistered, unbaptized in ember-light, age six years.*

*Name: Tammas Hartwell.*

*Witnessed and entered by: Maren Voss, Keeper of the Roll, under seal of the Ember Guild.*

Below the entry, in ink of a different color—red-brown, the color of dried blood though she had always insisted to herself it was only rusted iron-gall—someone had written a single line. Her own hand again. She could not remember writing it, which meant she had written it in the worst hour of her life, the hour whose edges her mind had worn smooth as river stone from handling:

*He asked for his sister.*

Maren set the page down on the desk very carefully, as if it might wake, and pressed both palms flat against the wood until the trembling stopped.

* * *

The valley had not always been called the Hollow Valley. In the old maps—the ones locked in the same drawer, because maps were confessions too—it was called Vaelmoor, and it had been green in a way that hurt the eyes, threaded with rivers that glowed faintly at night where the gifted had blessed them generations back. Magic had been in the water, in the wheat, in the marriage customs and the funeral songs. Children in Vaelmoor learned their first cantrips the way children everywhere learned to whistle: badly, joyfully, without anyone thinking much of it.

Then had come the first purge. Not Vessic's—Vessic was a late frost, an heir to cruelty rather than its author—but the purge before it, and the one before that. Each time, the gifted of Vaelmoor had died in numbers that made the word *purge* seem gentle. Each time, a few survivors had crept back to the ruined valley and asked the question that founded the Ember Guild: *how do we become invisible?*

The answer had been found by the founder, a hedge-witch named Osla Ember, and it had been terrible in the way that true answers usually are. Magic, Osla had reasoned, is a scent. The hunts of the world tracked it the way hounds track blood. So the valley must be made scentless—and the cheapest way to make a place scentless was to feed its magic to something that ate it.

There was such a thing beneath the eastern mountains. The Undercourt. The Broker of Names. A cathedral of jars, though Osla had never seen it; she had only met the broker's emissaries at the mountain gate, faceless ones in grey, who spoke politely and wrote contracts in ink that never dried.

Osla had sold them the valley's magic. All of it. Every blessing in the water, every ward on every door, every gift in every gifted body that remained. The Undercourt drank Vaelmoor dry, and in exchange the valley became a blind spot—a hole in the shape of a place, unwritten on the hunt-maps, unnoticed by scrying and divination alike. The Hollow Valley. The name was not poetry. It was a description.

That had been a hundred years ago, and it should have been enough. The covenant held. The valley slept scentless and safe, and the Ember Guild grew around its secret like bark around a wound, generation upon generation of keepers who tended the ash-jars—vessels where the last free souls of the purged could be hidden, sealed in ordinary ash, invisible even here—and who swore each year an oath whose older lines most of them no longer understood.

*What we keep, we keep whole. What we hide, we hide together. What was given, stays given.*

Maren had recited those words for forty years before she had gone looking, in the grey book, for what *was given* actually meant.

She had found out the year the plague took Elder Corvane. The covenant, it turned out, was not permanent. It ran a hundred years, and then it lapsed—or rather, it could be renewed, but renewal required payment again, fresh payment, because the Undercourt did not extend credit and did not forgive debts. And the hundred years would be up in two winters' time.

Corvane had died knowing. He had gripped Maren's wrist on his deathbed with a strength that frightened the younger keepers, and he had said, *It comes due, girl. It comes due, and I am too weak to sit at the table. You will have to pay.* Then he had wept, which Maren had never seen him do, and said nothing more intelligible for three days until the end.

So Maren had gone to the mountain gate in the winter of the year Lior Hartwell was four years old and her brother Tammas was six. She had gone with a delegation of three elders and a wagon, and she had spent eleven days in the antechambers of the Undercourt learning exactly how little leverage a hidden valley has against the thing that hides it.

The broker had been courteous. That was the unforgivable part, Maren thought now, all these years later. Not cruel. Courteous. It had offered tea. It had expressed sympathy for the guild's predicament. It had explained, in the tone of a merchant explaining weather to a farmer, that the price of another hundred years of invisibility was calibrated to the covenant's value, and the covenant's value was considerable, and the guild's coffers contained no gold worth having.

*But,* the broker had said, folding its grey hands, *we deal also in souls. One soul, freely surrendered by its kin, would balance the account nicely. We find that freely-surrendered souls keep best. Taken souls sour. Bought souls go flat. But a soul given up by family—ah. Those have a sweetness that lasts a century.*

Maren had refused. Of course she had refused. She had stood up from the table and said the Ember Guild would sooner let the wards fail and take its chances with the hunts than sell a child, and the broker had inclined its featureless head and said, *Of course. Take what time you need. The gate is open for nine more days.*

And then, on the sixth day, the emissaries had brought her a document. Not a demand. An offer, marked *voluntary*, signed at the bottom in a careful child's hand that had clearly been guided by an adult's:

*I give my soul to the Undercourt freely, to save my home. Please take care of my little sister. Her name is Lior.*

Tammas Hartwell, aged six, son of Joss and Wren Hartwell, who had died in the fever-winter two years before. Orphan. Ward of the guild. A quiet boy who sat in the back of lessons and drew maps of places he'd never been, and who had overheard—children always overhear—that the valley would be hunted if the elders couldn't pay, and that the elders had refused to pay with a person, and that therefore everyone he loved would burn.

No one had coerced him. That was the sentence Maren had turned over ten thousand times in twenty years, like a stone in the pocket, wearing it smooth. No one had coerced him. The Undercourt had simply made sure he knew the arithmetic, and a six-year-old who loved his sister had done the sums himself, and the elders—who could have refused, who could have carried the boy out of the mountain over their shoulders, who could have chosen the fire and the honor and the deaths of everyone—they had accepted the signature of a child because accepting it was easier than dying.

Maren had witnessed the transfer. She had held his hand while they did it, because he asked her to, because he was frightened, because she was the only adult in that grey cathedral who would look at him. She had felt the exact moment his soul left him—not a sound, not a light, just a kind of settling, the way a house settles when someone leaves it forever. His eyes had stayed open. They had looked at her. And then the jar had been stoppered, and the broker had said, *Payment received. The covenant is renewed. Safe travels, Keeper,* and had offered her tea again.

She had not taken the tea. She had walked out of the mountain into the snow and vomited beside the road, and then she had walked home and lied to everyone.

* * *

The lie had been elaborate, because lies built to protect children must be. The story the guild was told was that Tammas Hartwell had run away in the night, grief-mad over his parents, and been lost to the winter. Search parties went out. Ash-candles were lit for him at the next three Festivals. Wren's cousin, a woman in the lowlands, was paid through intermediaries to confirm she'd taken him in—a kindness Maren had bought with the last of her private money and hated herself for every day of.

And Lior—Lior had been four. Four-year-olds forget. That was the mercy Maren had clung to, the rope she had tied around her own guilt to keep it from dragging her under: *she is four, she will forget him, and forgetting is a wound that closes.* The guild had gently, deliberately, helped the closing along. No one spoke Tam's name. His drawings were quietly removed from the common room. The keepers answered the child's questions with soft vagueness—*he went away, my love, he went away and he is safe*—until the questions stopped, and the memory thinned, and by the time Lior was seven she spoke of her brother the way you speak of a dream: fondly, uncertainly, already half-convinced she had invented him.

Except.

Maren rose from the desk and went to the shelf where the guild's medical rolls were kept, and pulled the thin volume for the year of the transfer, and found the entry she had read perhaps fifty times and never once been able to explain.

The vessel-work. It was old craft, pre-purge craft, half-lost: the making of a living jar. When the guild hid souls in ash, the ash-jars were a compromise, a poor substitute. The original vessels had been people—gifted ones, born hollow, able to carry a soul inside them the way a lantern carries flame. Such births were rare. Rarer now. But the Undercourt's brokers had mentioned them, in that final negotiation, almost idly: *should your guild ever require more elegant storage, we do occasionally breed for the purpose. Living vessels. They walk, they talk, they pass every inspection. Far superior to glass.*

Maren had asked—one question, one, in eleven days—what became of the vessels' own selves. The broker had said, pleasantly, *They are not emptied, Keeper. They are shared. A vessel is a house with two hearths. Most vessels never know. Why would we tell them? Why would anyone?*

Lior Hartwell had been born eight months after the transfer. Born small and silent, in the guild's sickroom, to a mother who had lost a husband to the fever and a son to the road within two years. Born—and this was in the midwife's record, in the midwife's own puzzled hand—*with a warmth in her chest that will not cool. No fever. No illness. The child is simply warm, here, as if someone keeps a candle lit inside her.*

Maren had arranged the adoption herself. Joss Hartwell's elderly aunt, childless, glad of the girl. A family name preserved. A story with no gaps in it. And the child had grown warm-hearted and stubborn and gifted in ways the valley's dead magic shouldn't have allowed—small magics, ember-magics, the kind that needed no outside power because they burned on internal fuel—and she had become the youngest oath-keeper in guild history, and she sang to the ash-jars as if she recognized something in them, and twice a year, on no particular schedule, she woke in the night crying for a brother she could not name, and in the morning remembered nothing but the crying.

Two hearths. One house.

Maren had told herself for twenty years that she didn't know. That the timing was coincidence, that the midwife's note was folklore, that the night-terrors were grief echoing down a child's blood. She had told herself this so long and so firmly that the telling had become a kind of truth, load-bearing, holding up the roof of the person she had decided she was.

But she was old now, and old women's roofs come down, and in the quiet of the archive with the page spread before her, Maren Voss finally admitted what she had known since the moment the broker's emissaries had first shown her the vessel-cradles beneath the mountain, rows of them, each one warm:

She had known. She had suspected from the beginning and confirmed it within a year, quietly, with the midwife's help, and she had done nothing, because doing something would have meant saying everything, and saying everything would have broken the valley open, and the valley was all she had been sworn to protect since she was younger than Lior was now.

*You chose the valley over the child,* she said aloud to the empty archive, and her voice cracked on the last word, because the child was thirty-four now, or twenty-four—no, twenty-three, the numbers swam—and she was upstairs singing to jars, and one of the things in this valley that Maren had kept whole was a lie the size of a life.

* * *

She heard footsteps on the archive stair and froze, the page half-covered by her hand, an instinct of concealment so deep it moved before thought.

It was only Brannic, the night-keeper, checking the festival lanterns stored below. He paused at the sight of her. "Elder. Late for you."

"Festival accounts," Maren said. The lie came out smooth as poured cream. Forty years of practice. "Go up, Brannic. I'll bank the lamp."

He nodded and climbed away, and Maren sat in the dark he left behind and listened to her own heart.

Tomorrow was the Founding Festival. Tomorrow the guild would swear the oath, and Lior would sing the keeper's verse in her clear voice, and the ash-jars would be blessed with salt and candle-smoke, and everyone would believe, as Maren had once believed, that the valley's peace was a thing earned and innocent. And in two winters—no. Less. The covenant ran a hundred years from the *renewal*, not the founding. She had checked the dates a hundred times, hoping to be wrong. Nineteen years had passed since the mountain. Five winters remained. Perhaps fewer, if the new king's hunts drove more refugees to the valley and strained the wards, if the Undercourt decided to renegotiate early, if—

A bell rang above. Not the festival bells—it was too early, and the wrong tone, high and urgent, the crier's bell that hung by the valley gate and had not sounded in Maren's lifetime.

Footsteps again, many of them, running. Voices. Somewhere in the tangle of sound, she heard Lior's voice call out—sharp, alarmed, alive—and heard the guildhall doors bang wide against the cold.

Maren folded the page along its twenty-year-old crease and slid it into her sleeve, not the drawer. Some things should be close to the skin when they finally come due.

She climbed the stairs toward the ringing bell and the voices and the girl she had betrayed, carrying the weight of the grey book's worst secret against her heart, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief:

*It begins tonight. Everything buried comes up. God help us all, it begins.*
