## 5. My Brother's Name

The ledger page would not leave her.

Lior had not meant to see it. That was the truth she kept telling herself as she climbed the narrow stair to Maren's loft above the oath-hall, the morning after the crier's bell had cracked the valley open like a struck bell of its own. She had gone up to fetch more candlewax for the jar-room—Maren kept the good wax there, beeswax from the southern slope, because ash-tallow smoked and smoked smoke was an insult to the dead. The wax sat in its usual crock beside the elder's writing desk, and the desk had been left open, and the page had been lying there in the lamplight like a wound that hadn't been bandaged.

She had only read the first line before she heard Maren's boots on the stair.

*One soul, sold whole, to the Undercourt beneath Mount Vael. In exchange: perpetual warding of the Hollow Valley and all within it.*

And beneath that line, in ink so old it had gone brown as dried blood, a name.

*Hartwell.*

Now Lior stood at the top of the stair with the crock of wax forgotten in her hands, listening to the slow, deliberate climb of a woman who had walked these steps for sixty years and never once hurried. Maren came into the lamplight with her grey braid coiled at her neck and her staff in hand, and she stopped when she saw Lior's face.

She did not ask what Lior had seen. Lior watched her not ask it—the way the elder's eyes went first to the desk, then to the girl standing between the desk and the door, and something in them settled, heavy as snow sliding from a roof.

"You were meant to find it," Lior said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Thin. "You left it out."

"I left nothing out," Maren said. "I am an old woman, and old women forget to close their desks." She set her staff against the wall, unhurried, and Lior understood suddenly that every motion was deliberate—that Maren was giving herself time the way a swimmer gives herself air. "But I have been leaving it out for three nights now, child. So perhaps you're right."

"What is this?" Lior put the crock down on the floor without looking at it. She could not look away from the elder's face. "What is *Hartwell*? We don't—we don't have Hartwells anymore. It's just me. It's just my name."

"Sit down, Lior."

"No." The word came out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard. Three days ago she would never have spoken so to Elder Maren. But three days ago a royal crier had stood in the valley mouth and read out the Gift Hunt, and the wards had flickered for the first time in a hundred years, and everything solid in Lior's world had shown itself to be a skin stretched over water. "I've been sitting since yesterday. I've been sitting my whole life in this valley, tending jars, saying oaths, being *good*. You hinted last night. At the fire. You said the guild paid a price. You looked right at me when you said it, Maren, don't tell me you didn't." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "Tell me whose soul we sold."

Maren crossed the room slowly. She did not go to the desk. She went to the window instead—the small deep-set window that faced south, toward the mountain they never named, the one that stood on the horizon like a closed fist—and she stood looking at it for a long moment while the morning light made her shadow long and thin across the floorboards.

"You were four years old," Maren said. "When it happened. Perhaps five. Too young to keep anything, and the valley helped you along. The valley is kind that way. It takes what would hurt us."

"Maren."

"His name was Tammas." The word fell into the room like a stone into a well, and Lior heard it fall all the way down. "Tammas Hartwell. Your brother. He was nine years older than you, and he had your mother's laugh, and he could make light sit in his palm like a caught moth. He was gifted, Lior. Gifted past anything the guild had sworn in a generation. And King Aldric's court had just fallen to Vessic's father, and the hunts had begun in the lowlands, and the guild came to me and said: the wards are failing. The valley will be found. Unless."

"Unless," Lior repeated. Her mouth had gone dry. Somewhere below her ribs, in the place where fear lived, something had begun to move—a slow turning, like ice shifting on a river.

"The Undercourt buys souls," Maren said. "It always has. The brokers beneath the mountain pay in protections, in wards, in invisibility. They bought ours with your brother's."

The room tilted. Lior reached for the back of a chair and gripped it until the wood bit her palms.

"I don't—" she started, and stopped, because her mind was doing something terrible and smooth, sliding off the words the way rain slides off oiled cloth. *Tammas. Tam.* The name should have opened something. A face, a voice, a memory of small feet running ahead of hers on the valley path. There was nothing. There was a shape where a memory should be, the way a glove holds the shape of a hand that has been withdrawn, and the emptiness of it was worse than any grief she had ever felt, because grief needs a body, and this had none. "I don't remember him at all."

"No," Maren said quietly. "You wouldn't. When he was taken, I asked the valley to take him from you too. I asked it to take him gently. I have regretted that gentleness every day for twenty years, and I will regret it until I die."

"You took him twice." Lior's voice cracked down the middle. "They took him once and you took him again. Out of *me*."

"Yes."

"How could you—" She was shouting now; she could hear herself shouting and could not stop. "How could you decide that? How could you decide what I got to keep?"

"Because I watched your mother die of it!" For the first time in Lior's life, Maren raised her voice, and the sound of it was shocking as a slap—old Maren, patient Maren, granite Maren, and here was grief coming through her like fire through a shuttered lamp. The elder pressed her lips together, drew one breath, another, and when she spoke again her voice was low and wrecked. "Your mother carried that loss like a coal in her chest, Lior. It burned her down to nothing in four years. I held her hand while it happened. And then I looked at you—at four-year-old you, asleep in the jar-room among the ashes because you wouldn't sleep anywhere else—and I thought: this one is next. This one burns next. Unless I take the coal out while it's still small."

Lior's legs gave. She sat down hard in the chair she'd been gripping, and the room swam, and the lamplight swam, and outside the window the mountain stood on the horizon with its fist still closed around whatever it had taken.

"They didn't kill him," she said. It wasn't a question. She wasn't sure why she was sure, but she was—sure the way you were sure of the weight of your own hands. "Selling isn't killing. What do they do with them? With souls?"

"No one knows," Maren said. "That is the mercy of the trade, if it has any. The brokers keep them. The jars beneath the mountain hold them, the way our jars hold ash. Some say the court drinks them, slowly, to keep its magics alive. Some say worse. But he is not dead, child. Dead is a thing I could bury, and I could not bury this, and that is why I have been an old woman for twenty years."

Lior looked down at her own wrists. Thin wrists, work-scarred, the wrists of a keeper who had spent ten years carrying jars of other people's leavings. On the inside of the left one, faint as a watermark, sat the mark she'd had since birth—a small whorl of darker skin, shaped like nothing anyone in the guild had ever seen, which Maren had always told her was a birthmark and nothing more.

It had been warm lately. Since the bell. Since the wards flickered. She had told no one.

"This," she said, holding her wrist out, the lamplight catching the whorl. "This isn't a birthmark."

Maren came to her then. Slowly, the way you approach a bird you're afraid of startling. She knelt—not bowed, knelt, elder to child, which she had never done—and took Lior's wrist in both her hands, and turned it to the light, and her thumbs rested on either side of the mark without touching it.

"When the brokers took Tam," she said, "they did not take him empty-handed. A soul cannot simply be scooped out, Lior. It must be poured. And a pour needs a vessel to come from and a vessel to be sealed in. They sealed his soul in glass beneath the mountain—or so the contract says. But a pour leaves residue. A pour leaves... a trace. Something of the giver stays in the vessel he was poured from, the way wine stays in the wood of a barrel forever after."

The ice in Lior's chest shifted again. Turned over. And this time she felt it—felt it distinctly, unmistakably, a warmth behind her sternum that was not her heartbeat, that had never been her heartbeat, that had been keeping time beside her heartbeat her entire life like a second clock in a dark room.

"There's something in me," she whispered.

"I believe so." Maren's voice was very gentle now, gentle the way hands are gentle on broken things. "I believe some ember of Tam stayed behind when they poured him out. I believe it has lived in you since you were small. I believe it is why the jars settle for you when they won't settle for anyone else. Why the ash warms in your hands. Why you can stand in the jar-room at midnight and feel—" she paused, searching, "—*company*."

Company. Yes. All those nights Lior had spent among the jars telling herself it was devotion, calling it duty, the youngest keeper so faithful to her charges—and it had never been duty. It had been a boy's warmth folded up inside her, leaning toward the ash of others because it recognized what it was. She thought of all the evenings she'd sat among the jars feeling less alone than she ever felt among people, and her eyes filled so fast and so completely that the room became a smear of gold.

"He's been with me," she said. "All this time. He's been *in* me, and I didn't even know his name until this morning."

"Would knowing have changed it?" Maren asked softly. "Would it have made the valley safer, or him freer, or you happier? Or would it only have made you burn, the way your mother burned?"

"Don't." Lior pulled her wrist free. "Don't you dare make this a kindness. You decided what I could carry. You decided what I could grieve. You don't get to call that kindness now, not when the whole world is about to burn anyway—did you hear the crier, Maren? Did you hear what's coming? The wards flickered. The price we paid isn't buying us anything anymore. We sold my brother for a roof, and the roof is coming down, and you let me spend my whole life loving a house built on him."

The silence after that was long. Outside, the valley went on being beautiful—she could hear it, the stream, the pines, someone laughing down by the bakehouse, ordinary sounds from a world that had no idea what it was built on—and Lior hated it, suddenly and completely, hated the beauty of it the way you hate a face you've caught in a lie.

When Maren finally spoke, her voice had gone flat and old and true.

"You're going to go after him."

Lior hadn't known it yet. But the moment Maren said it, she felt it settle into place in her chest, next to the second heartbeat, like a key finding its lock. She looked at the window, at the mountain, at the closed fist on the horizon.

"The guild will vote to hide," she said. "They'll vote to dig deeper and keep quieter. They'll vote the way they've voted for a hundred years."

"Yes."

"And I'll be the only one who walks out."

"Yes," Maren said again. "I know. I've known since the bell rang. An old woman knows the shape of a thing by the shadow it throws." She rose, stiffly, one hand on the windowsill, and looked down at Lior with an expression Lior had no name for—grief and guilt and pride all melted together like metals in a crucible. "I won't stop you. I want you to understand that I want to—I want to lock you in the jar-room and let the world break against the wards—but I spent twenty years deciding what you could carry, and look what my decisions grew. So I won't decide for you again. But I'll tell you one thing more, and then you may hate me freely for the rest of your life."

Lior wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "What."

"The road remembers what the valley cannot hold." Maren's eyes were distant, fixed on the mountain. "Everything the valley took from you—every softened memory, every eased grief—it didn't destroy them. Nothing is destroyed. It's all still *out there*, Lior, strung along the roads like lanterns, waiting. If you walk far enough, things will start coming back to you. Things you never knew you'd lost. And some of them will be his." Her voice caught, just barely. "Some of them might even be yours."

* * *

Lior did not go back to the jar-room that afternoon. She couldn't. She walked instead to the eastern rim of the valley, past the terraces and the bee-fences, up the sheep path to the shelf of granite where the wards were thinnest—where, on clear days, you could see all the way to the pass and the grey thread of the king's road beyond it.

She sat there with her knees drawn up and her wrist turned toward the sky, watching the ember-mark catch the afternoon light.

*Tam,* she thought, deliberately, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape. *Tammas Hartwell. My brother. Nine years older. Our mother's laugh.*

Nothing came. Of course nothing came. But the warmth behind her sternum stirred when she thought the name—stirred and leaned, the way a sleeper leans toward a voice calling from another room—and Lior pressed her palm flat over her heart and breathed, and breathed, and let herself feel it fully for the first time: that she had been two people's worth of alive her whole life and had only ever counted one.

Below her, the valley lay green and golden in the late light, smoke rising straight from the chimneys, the Founding Festival banners already going up along the lane for a festival that suddenly seemed like it belonged to another century. Somewhere down there, the guild would gather tonight and vote to hide. She already knew how the vote would go. She already knew, too, what she would say when it did—and that she would say it alone, and walk out alone, and that the walking out would feel like dying, because Maren had told her once, years ago, that leaving the valley was a small death, and Lior had laughed then, and did not laugh now.

The mark on her wrist pulsed. Once. Twice. Warm as a hand closing around hers.

"All right," she said aloud, to the warmth, to the brother-shaped stranger living under her ribs, to the mountain on the horizon with its fist full of stolen light. "Hold on. I'm coming to get you."

Far to the south, past the pass, past the burning cities she could not yet imagine, the mountain waited—and somewhere in the dark beneath it, in a vault she could not yet picture, a jar that had been empty for twenty years waited too.
