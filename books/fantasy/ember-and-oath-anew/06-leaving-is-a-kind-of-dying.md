## 6. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old smoke when the vote was called.

Lior stood at the back of the long room, her hands still gritty with ash from the morning's jar-tending, and watched the elders file in one by one. There were eleven of them now. Once there had been thirty, Maren said, back when the valley's borders were young and the Founding Festival had been a thing of fire rather than candlelight. The empty chairs along the curved table stood like headstones, and no one ever moved them, and no one ever sat in them.

Maren took the elder's seat at the center. She looked smaller tonight, Lior thought—folded into herself like a letter someone had tried to unsent. Her hands, spotted and knuckled as winter apples, lay flat on the ledger before her. Lior's stomach turned at the sight of it. That book. That page. *Hartwell.* Two days since she'd seen the name written in faded iron-gall ink, and she could still feel it sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"The crier's bell has rung," Maren said, and her voice carried the way old voices do—not loud, but heavy, the way a full sack is heavy. "You've all heard the proclamation by now. King Vessic calls for the registration of every gifted soul in the nine provinces. Registration, or the pyre." She paused. "There are those who say the wards have flickered."

A murmur went through the gathered guild—forty-odd keepers, jar-tenders, wardwrights, the quiet men and women who swept ash and sang the sealing songs. Lior saw Brannic the well-warden wring his cap in his fists. She saw Yeva, who had taught Lior the lullabies that kept the jars calm, close her eyes as if praying to something that had stopped listening.

"The wards have held for ninety-seven years," Maren continued. "They held through the Purges. They held through the Long Winter. They will hold through this, if we are careful. If we are *quiet*." Her gaze swept the room, and for half a heartbeat it caught on Lior and stayed there, and Lior felt the weight of everything unspoken between them press down like snow on a roof. "I call the vote. Do we open the valley to those fleeing the Hunt?"

"No," said the room.

It came as a chorus, ragged but unanimous. Brannic said it loudest. Yeva didn't say it at all; she just shook her head slowly, tears cutting clean lines down her flour-dusted cheeks. Old Tammas—the other Tammas, the cooper, not her brother, never her brother, because her brother was a name on a ledger page and a hollow where a memory should be—old Tammas spoke of his grandchildren, of the festival fires, of how one opened gate was a wound you couldn't close twice.

"We stay hidden," Maren said, when the last voice had fallen. "We keep the oath. We keep the ash. So moved and so sealed."

"So sealed," the room answered.

Lior did not answer. She stood at the back with her arms crossed over her ember-marked wrist, hidden beneath her sleeve, and she felt the word *sealed* settle over the hall like a lid on a coffin.

Her home. Her people. Voting to become a grave.

She slipped out before the closing song.

* * *

Outside, the valley was doing what the valley always did: being beautiful in a way that felt like forgetting.

The Founding Festival was three days past, and the lanterns still hung from the eaves of the keepers' row, guttering their last. Snow had begun again—fine, dry snow, the kind that fell without sound, without weight, erasing footprints almost as fast as they were made. Beyond the rooftops the valley walls rose black against the starless sky, sheer cliffs of granite veined with quartz that never caught the light quite right. Nothing grew strange here. Nothing grew *at all*, really, beyond what hands planted. The Hollow Valley was a place where magic could not follow, and so it was a place where nothing wild happened, and so it was, Lior had always believed, the safest place in the world.

She walked the keeper's path toward the jar-house without deciding to. Her feet knew the way the way her mother's hands had known loom-work: without thought, without question.

Inside, the ash-jars breathed their slow light.

There were sixty-one of them now, arranged on stone shelves in careful rows, each one a squat vessel of grey clay packed with hearth-ash that glimmered faintly from within, like coals remembered rather than burning. Sixty-one souls. Sixty-one people who had come to the valley over the decades carrying gifts they could not bear—or gifts the world would burn them for—and who had chosen, or been persuaded, to set those gifts aside. To pour them out. To sleep in jars of ash until some gentler age came to wake them.

Lior had tended these jars since she was seven years old. She knew their sounds. Jar twelve hummed when the wind came off the western cliffs. Jar forty whispered names in its sleep—old names, dead names, names no ledger held anymore. Jar nineteen, the newest, had gone quiet only last spring, and Lior still spoke to it every evening out of habit, telling it about the weather, about the bread, about small things, because Elder Maren had taught her that souls in jars were not gone, only waiting, and waiting things liked to be talked to.

She stopped in front of jar nineteen and pressed her palm flat against the cool clay.

"I'm going," she told it quietly. "Someone has to go. They voted to hide, and hiding is just dying slowly with better manners, and I can't—I can't do that. Not when he's under a mountain."

The jar said nothing. Of course it said nothing. But the ash inside it shimmered once, softly, the way embers breathe when stirred, and Lior chose—with the particular stubbornness of the very young—to believe it was an answer.

She thought about Tam. Not the ledger entry, not the fact of him, but the shape of him, which was all she had: a boy of eight or nine standing at the valley gate waving, a boy whose face she could not picture, whose laugh she could not summon, whose absence was itself absent—she didn't even know what she was missing. Grief for a memory she never had. It was the strangest mourning in the world, mourning someone you'd never met, and yet it filled her up like water fills a cup, to the brim, spilling over.

And beneath the grief, stranger still: the thing she carried. The thing Maren had finally named, two nights ago, in a voice like a woman confessing to a crime. *You carry something of his, child. Something we could never take out and never explain.* The ember-mark on her wrist. The warmth that lived behind her sternum like a second heart. The dreams of corridors lined with glass.

She was twenty years old and she had been a locked room her whole life, and tonight she had decided to walk out of the valley and go looking for the key.

"You'll freeze out here."

Lior turned. Maren stood in the doorway of the jar-house, wrapped in her grey traveling cloak, snow gathering on her shoulders like a shawl she hadn't asked for. Behind her, the guildhall's windows still glowed with the closing song, thin voices raised in the old words: *keep the ash, keep the door, keep the silence evermore.*

"You should be in there," Lior said. "Leading them."

"I led them. I called the vote I knew they'd cast." Maren came forward, her boots whispering on the stone floor. Up close, in the jar-light, her face was a map of every hard year the valley had survived. "A leader tells people the truth only when the truth won't kill them. Tonight the truth would have killed them. Hope does that, sometimes, faster than despair."

"Then you lied to them."

"I protected them." Maren's eyes were steady. "As I protected you. As I failed your brother. Those three things are the same story, Lior, and I have been telling it wrong for twenty years."

Lior's throat tightened. "I'm going after him."

"I know."

"Alone. If I ask anyone to come, Vessic's hunters follow them back here. If I tell anyone the road I'm taking, the wards flicker harder. It has to be alone. One girl, no trail, no name worth shouting." She heard her own voice shake and hated it, and kept talking anyway, because if she stopped she would break. "I'm the youngest keeper this guild has ever sworn. I know the sealing songs and the jar-tending rites and exactly four useful things about the outside world. I'm not ready. I'm going anyway."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "You are."

That undid Lior more than any argument would have. She had braced for a fight—for the elder who had buried the truth for two decades to bury this too, to lock the gate, to sing the sealing songs over *her*. Instead Maren reached into her cloak and drew out something small, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a leather cord gone stiff with age.

"I found this in the ledger chest, beneath the false bottom, where I put it twenty years ago because I couldn't bear to look at it." Maren unwrapped it with fingers that trembled only slightly, and held it out on her palm.

A compass.

Brass-cased, dented at the rim, its glass clouded with scratches. The needle inside didn't point north—it swung lazily, drunkenly, settling nowhere, because compasses were useless in the Hollow Valley, where even direction seemed to forget itself. But engraved around the rim, worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had rubbed it thousands of times, was a single word:

*TAM.*

"He carried it everywhere," Maren said. "He was always pointing it at things. At the cliffs, at me, at you in your cradle. He used to say it was broken because it pointed at nothing, and I used to tell him it wasn't broken—it was waiting. That somewhere outside the valley, it would find what it was looking for." Her voice frayed at the edges. "I let myself believe that was just a boy's fancy. It wasn't. Nothing in this valley is what I told you it was, child. Least of all me."

Lior took the compass. It was warm. That was the impossible part—that after twenty years in a locked chest it should be warm as a held hand—and she closed her fingers around it and felt the warmth sink into her palm and up her wrist, into the ember-mark, which prickled like a sleeping limb waking.

"Why give me this?" she whispered. "Why help me leave, if you helped build the cage?"

"Because the cage was built with love and it was still a cage, and I am too old to pretend otherwise." Maren straightened, and something in her face hardened—not coldness, but resolve, the look of a woman picking up a debt she intended to pay in full. "But hear me, Lior Hartwell, and hear me well, because this is the last teaching I will ever give you. Outside those walls, the road remembers what the valley cannot hold."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the valley is a place of forgetting—gentle, merciful, blind. Magic cannot follow you here, and neither can consequence, and neither can the true shape of what was done to you and your brother. But the moment you pass the gate, all of it comes back. Every mile of road between here and that mountain remembers everything: every trade, every betrayal, every soul sold. The road will remember *you*, Lior. What you carry. What you are. And things that remember have a way of waking up." Maren gripped her shoulders, and her hands were strong despite their age, strong as roots. "Your magic will come back the moment you cross the boundary. It will be loud. It will be visible. It will be *hungry*, because it has been starved for twenty years inside a girl who was never allowed to know it existed. You must learn it before it learns you. Do you understand? Before it learns you."

Lior nodded, though understanding sat far away, across mountains she hadn't climbed.

"And one more thing." Maren's grip tightened. "When the loneliness comes—and it will come, on the third night, or the seventh, when the fire is low and the dark is wide—when it comes, do not mistake it for a reason to turn back. Loneliness is just love with nowhere to land. Let it land on him. Walk toward your brother, and let every step hurt, and count the hurting as proof you're still yourself." She released her, stepped back, and became once more the elder, the keeper, the woman who had traded a child's soul and carried it for twenty years. "Now go. Before the song ends. Before I find my courage fails and I call you back."

"Maren—"

"*Go.*"

Lior went.

* * *

The valley gate stood at the northern end of the keeper's path, where the cliffs narrowed to a throat of grey stone. It wasn't much to look at—a wooden door banded with iron, set into the rock, older than the guild, older than the ledger, older than the lie. Generations of keepers had passed through it for supply runs and never spoken of what waited on the other side, the way you don't speak of a scar.

Lior stood before it with her pack on her shoulder and the compass in her fist, and she made herself look back.

The valley at night, from the height of the gate path: forty roofs furred with snow, lantern-light leaking gold from shuttered windows, the jar-house chimney breathing its thin smoke, the guildhall where the closing song had ended and the silence had begun. Everything she had ever loved, arranged below her like a hearth seen from a mountaintop. Safe. Sealed. Small.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, though she wasn't sure to whom—to the guild for leaving, to Tam for taking so long, to the girl she had been yesterday, who had believed the valley was the whole world and the whole world was enough. *I'm sorry, and I'm going, and I'm not coming back the same.*

She pushed the door.

Cold air hit her like a wall—real cold, mountain cold, a cold with teeth and intention, utterly unlike the valley's tame winters. And with it came everything else.

It started in her wrist. The ember-mark flared white-hot, and Lior gasped and staggered, and then the rest of it arrived: a rush, a flood, a *roar* of something rising through her from the soles of her feet to the crown of her skull, filling spaces she hadn't known were empty, lighting corridors in her mind she had never known were dark. Colors sharpened until the snow-blind night blazed. Sounds layered themselves—the wind's voice splitting into a dozen distinct whispers, each one saying her name in a different tone. Beneath her sternum, the second heart, the warmth she had carried all her life without questioning, *woke*, and beat, and beat, and beat.

Magic. Hers. Twenty years starved and now unleashed in a body standing alone on a dark mountainside, and it was the most terrifying thing she had ever felt, and the most alive.

Behind her, the valley's wards flickered—one great pulse of dimming light along the cliff-tops, visible even through the closing door—and she heard, faintly, the guild raising the alarm, bells ringing, voices crying out. She had done that. Just by leaving. Just by being what she was, outside the walls, for the first time.

*The road remembers,* Maren had said. *Things that remember have a way of waking up.*

Lior stumbled three steps down the switchback path, clutching the compass, fighting for breath, fighting the flood to a trickle, a thread, a candleflame she could cup in two hands. Slowly—so slowly—the roar settled to a hum. The whispers faded. The night became merely night again, vast and freezing and utterly indifferent to her.

She opened her fist.

The compass needle, drunk and wandering for twenty years in the valley's forgetting, had stopped. It pointed south and east, unwavering, certain, toward the horizon where the war-torn free cities smoldered and the Undercourt slept beneath its mountain.

Toward Tam.

Lior laughed—a cracked, breathless sound that steamed in the frozen air—and wiped her eyes with the back of her glove, and turned her back on the only home she had ever known.

"One mile," she told the dark, the needle, the brother she couldn't remember. "One mile at a time."

She walked. The snow took her footprints. The road, patient as a creditor, began to remember her name.

And three miles on, hungry and shaking and glowing faintly at the wrists like a lantern learning to burn, Lior Hartwell crested a ridge and saw, in the valley of the next river down, the lights of a town she had never seen—and among them, moving between the market stalls that should have been shuttered hours ago, a figure watching her watch the town, a figure whose smile she would learn to distrust and then, much later, to trust completely.

The figure raised one hand. Not a wave. A greeting between thieves.

Lior's new-born magic flared warning-hot against her palms, and she did not yet know that the thief had already seen her coming—or that the road had only just begun to collect.
