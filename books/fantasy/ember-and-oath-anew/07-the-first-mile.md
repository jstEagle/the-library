## 7. The First Mile

The valley let her go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a crowded market—slowly, with fingers that did not want to uncurl.

Lior felt it happen. She had walked the pilgrim's path out of the Hollow Valley a hundred times in her life, but never past the boundary stones, and she had never once noticed how the air changed there. Now she could not have missed it if she'd been blind and deaf and buried alive. The world on the far side of the stones was *loud*.

Not loud with sound. Loud with everything else.

It began as a pressure behind her eyes, like the moment before a sneeze, or before crying, or before lightning. Then it spread—down her throat, into her chest, along her arms until her fingertips buzzed as though she had slept on them all night. The grass beyond the boundary was greener than any grass in the valley, and she could *hear* it growing. Not metaphorically. She stood at the edge of the tree line with Tam's compass cold in her palm and listened to an entire hillside of grass drinking sunlight, and it sounded like rain on a tin roof, like a thousand tiny mouths whispering *more, more, more*.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one. "Oh, no."

Because Maren had warned her. Maren had sat her down the night before she left, in the guildhouse that smelled of ash and tallow, and taken both her hands in those papery ones and said: *The valley doesn't keep magic out, child. It keeps you quiet. Outside, you will be a bell in a world full of ears.*

She had thought she understood. She had nodded solemnly, the way the young nod at the old, storing the words without weighing them.

She understood now.

The magic came back into her the way blood comes back into a limb that has slept too long—a flood, a burning, an almost unbearable rush of sensation. Twenty years of silence pouring into a vessel built to hold it. She sank to her knees in the wet grass at the edge of the pilgrim's road and pressed her palms flat against the earth because it seemed suddenly, desperately important to touch something real.

The earth answered.

Warmth bloomed up through the soil into her hands, up her wrists, into the ember-mark beneath her sleeve—which she felt for the first time not as a birthmark but as a *door*, thin and hot, like a coin left too long in the sun. Somewhere below the ground, roots shifted. Somewhere above, a crow on a dead branch cocked its head at her and made a sound that was, unmistakably, a word in a language she didn't speak but somehow understood: *small one, small one, what are you?*

"I don't know," Lior whispered back.

And the terrible thing—the thing that made her laugh even as tears ran down her face—was that it felt *good*. It felt like the first breath after nearly drowning. It felt like coming home to a house she had never lived in. All her life she had been a cup kept deliberately empty, and now someone had turned the tap, and the water was scalding and sweet and endless, and she wanted more of it even as it hurt.

She thought of the jars in the guildhouse cellar. Rows upon rows of gray glass, each holding a soul folded small as a moth. She had tended them since she was six years old, dusting their shoulders, turning them so the light wouldn't crack them, singing to them on festival nights because Elder Maren said they liked songs. She had always assumed the souls inside were sleeping.

They weren't sleeping. They were *muffled*. And now, kneeling in the loud green world outside the valley, Lior understood what each of those jars must feel like from the inside, every hour of every year, and she pressed her hand over her mouth and grieved for them properly for the first time.

The compass trembled in her other palm. Its needle spun lazily, uselessly, then steadied—not pointing north, but pointing south and slightly east, toward the mountains, toward the Undercourt, toward a brother whose name she had learned only three days ago and whose face she had never once seen in a memory of her own.

"Tam," she said, trying the name out loud in open air for the first time. It tasted strange. New words always did. "I'm coming."

She got up. Her legs shook. She shouldered her pack—the good pack, the one Maren had packed with dried apples and hard cheese and a change of clothes and, tucked secretly into the bottom seam where she thought Lior wouldn't find it, a small knife and a strip of dried meat wrapped around a note that read only *come home or don't, but live either way*. Lior had found it at dawn and cried into her collar about it, quietly, so the valley wouldn't hear.

Then she walked south, and the first mile began.

* * *

The first mile was the longest mile of her life, and it lasted three days.

Not because of distance. Because of *this*—because the world outside the valley was a cathedral of noise and she had been born deaf to it and now she wasn't, and every step taught her a new instrument in the orchestra.

Fire was the worst and the best. On the first evening she built a small cookfire in the lee of a fallen oak, and when the flames caught she heard them—not the crackle and pop her ears reported, but underneath that, a low continuous song, hungry and joyful at once, the sound of a thing doing exactly what it was made for. When she held her hands over it, the fire leaned toward her. Actually leaned, the way a cat leans into a scratching hand. She snatched her fingers back and the flames drooped, sulking, and she sat there in the dark laughing shakily at a fire that behaved like a dog.

Water was gentler. At a stream crossing on the second morning she cupped her hands to drink and felt the current pause beneath her fingers, attentive, listening, the way a horse listens to a familiar voice. She apologized to it—for what, she couldn't say—and it went on its way, carrying a little of her warmth downstream like a message.

Living things were loudest of all. Every bird, every squirrel, every worm in every rotten log broadcast itself in a language of heat and hum, and by the second day Lior had learned, out of sheer self-defense, to close whatever door it was inside her that received all this—to pull the noise down to a murmur, the way you'd draw a blanket over your head. It took effort. It took concentration. It was like learning to blink, or breathe, or walk, all over again, all at once, at nineteen years old, alone, on a road full of people who wanted her dead.

Because that was the other thing. The Gift Hunt.

She saw the first poster nailed to a mile-stone on the morning of the second day, the paper still stiff and white, the ink barely weathered:

*BY ORDER OF KING VESSIC, TRUE AND SOLE SOVEREIGN: ALL PERSONS POSSESSING THE UNSANCTIONED GIFT SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION WITHIN FORTY DAYS. THOSE WHO CONCEAL THEIR NATURE SHALL BE BURNED. REWARD OFFERED FOR DENUNCIATION. THE KING SEES ALL.*

Beneath the proclamation, in different ink, someone had written in a hurried hand: *the king sees nothing. run.* Someone else had scratched that out. Beneath the scratch-out, a third hand had written simply: *we are burned already. come to Vell.* And beneath that, four words that made Lior's chest go tight and hot: *the jars remember us.*

She stood before the stone a long time, memorizing it, and then she pulled the poster down, folded it into her pack, and walked on. She told herself she took it as evidence. She knew, in some honest corner of herself, that she took it because she could not bear to leave it hanging there like a sentence passed on everyone she was about to meet.

That night, practicing in the firelight, she discovered she could make the flame stand up straight as a candle when she asked nicely. She discovered she could feel the boundaries of things—the exact shape of a stone in the dark, the hollow inside a fallen log, the buried architecture of a mole's tunnel under her bedroll. She discovered that when she was frightened, the fire flared, and when she was calm, it settled, and that her fear and her power were braided together so tightly she could not yet tell which was pulling which.

And on the third day, on a long gray stretch of road between two hills with the wind combing the wheat on either side, she heard the horses.

* * *

There was no mistaking them. She had grown up on stories of the hunt patrols—every gifted child in hiding grows up on them, the way children in flood country grow up on stories of water. Four riders, black cloaks, gray hoods, and the dogs. Always the dogs. Regular soldiers you might outrun or outwit, but the hounds of the hunt were bred in kennels beneath the royal menagerie from stock that had, the stories said, a drop of something older in them—something that could smell the gift the way other dogs smelled blood.

What the stories hadn't told her was that she would hear them coming in her *teeth*.

It started as a wrongness in the wheat. The field to the west of the road had been singing all afternoon—the long slow song of ripening grain, thousands of stems swaying in unison—and then, all at once, the western half of the song stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a room goes quiet when a certain person walks into it. The birds went up off the hedgerow in a single startled sheet. And through the gap where the wheat held its breath came the drumming of hooves, and under the hooves a lower sound, a panting many-throated sound, and threaded through it all, faint but unmistakable, a smell that reached her not through her nose but through the ember-mark on her wrist: iron, oil, and something scorched.

They had burned someone recently. The mark knew.

Lior did the arithmetic of terror in about two seconds. Road behind: open wheat, no cover, a quarter mile to the tree line she'd passed an hour ago. Road ahead: the same. Ditch: shallow, dry, useless. Riders: four, closing fast, and her legs were strong but no legs were stronger than horses.

Her body decided before her mind did. She dove off the road into the wheat on the eastern side, crashing through the stalks, and dropped flat into a drainage furrow some farmer had cut years ago, half-choked with last autumn's leaves. She pulled her cloak over herself and lay still and did the only other thing her body knew how to do, the thing the valley had trained into her across twenty years of silent cellars and held breath and festival nights spent not-singing:

She went quiet.

She reached for that door inside her, the one she'd learned to close against the noise of the living world, and she slammed it shut on herself. She gathered everything that was burning and humming and leaking out of her—everything the hounds could smell—and she pushed it *down*, down past the ember-mark, down into some deep sealed room at the bottom of her, and she locked it, and she lay in the dead leaves with her heart slamming and made herself into the next best thing to a stone.

The hoofbeats slowed. She heard them come abreast of her hiding place—she could count them now, four sets, and the creak of leather, and a man's voice saying, flat and bored, "Nothing on this stretch. Third empty mile today."

"The dogs say otherwise." A woman's voice, harder. "Hold."

The panting of the hounds drew closer. Lior lay in the leaves and felt them arrive at the edge of the furrow—felt them the way you feel a hand hovering an inch from your face. Two of them. Big, lean, gray-muzzled things with nostrils like wet caves, and behind their animal smell that other smell, the old drop, the thing that made them more than dogs. They cast back and forth along the furrow's rim, whining low in their throats, and Lior lay beneath their scrutiny with her whole self clenched around her own secret like a fist around a coal, and the coal *burned*, and holding it closed was like holding her hand in a fire and smiling.

One of the hounds put its head over the edge of the furrow. Six inches from her face. Its breath washed over her—meat and ash and kennel straw—and its eyes, pale amber, found hers through the gap in her hood.

She did not move. She did not breathe. Inside her sealed room, the coal screamed.

The hound's nostrils flared. Its head tilted. And then—it puzzled at her the way a man puzzles at a lock that won't turn, nosing the air, nosing the leaves, nosing *her*, finding nothing, finding less than nothing, finding a girl-shaped absence where its nose promised it a blaze. It whined again, a frustrated, questioning sound, and its pale eyes stayed on hers a moment longer, and Lior had the sudden mad certainty that it was not confused at all—that it saw exactly what she was and was deciding, in whatever way such creatures decide, whether to say so.

Then the woman's voice cracked like a whip: "On! We've wasted the morning!"—and the hound withdrew, shaking its ruff, and the hoofbeats resumed, and the patrol went west up the road at a canter, and the wheat slowly remembered its song.

Lior lay in the furrow until the light went long and gold. Then longer. An hour, maybe two, while the shaking worked its way out of her arms and legs in waves. Only when the shadows of the wheat lay fully across her did she unclench—carefully, carefully, like opening a fist around a live coal—and let herself burn again.

The relief was obscene. Magic rushed back up through her like a spring tide, and the nearest wheat stalks bowed toward her, and somewhere overhead the crow from her first day called *small one, small one,* and she laughed—high, ragged, half hysteria—as she lay in the dirt grinning at the sky.

Because here was the thing she had realized somewhere in the middle of the longest hour of her life, lying still as death while a hound breathed on her face:

She hadn't known how to do that. Hiding, muffling, sealing herself away—yes, the valley had taught her that. But what she had done in the furrow was something else. She hadn't just gone quiet. She had gone quiet *in a shape*—had wrapped her silence around her like a garment, tailored it, worn it. She had made herself unreadable, and she had done it instinctively, smoothly, the way you catch a ball thrown at your face, without thinking, without ever having practiced.

Somebody had known she could do that. Somebody had built her knowing it.

The thought arrived and sat down in her chest and refused to leave. She thought of Maren's face when Lior had demanded the truth about Tam—the guilt, yes, but underneath the guilt something else, something that looked horribly, in retrospect, like *recognition*. Like an elder looking at a finished work and grieving it.

*I'm not just his sister,* she thought, lying in the cooling ditch with the stars coming out. *Whatever I am, I was made on purpose.*

She got up. She brushed off the leaves. She ate half a piece of hard cheese standing up because sitting still felt impossible, and she walked south in the dark by the compass's stubborn needle and the fire she carried in a lantern-shell Maren had given her, which burned without fuel as long as she fed it attention.

Near midnight she came down out of the hills into the first outskirts of a town—Corren's Landing, according to a leaning signpost, though the sign had been defaced and re-nailed so many times it looked like a quilt. Even at this hour the place was awake; she could feel it before she saw it, a sprawl of warm human noise, cookfires and watch-lanterns and hundreds of sleeping hearts, and threaded through it, sour and sharp, the iron-oil-ash smell of the hunt. They were everywhere out here. Or everywhere was becoming them.

She was hungry. Three days of dried apples and hard cheese and nerves had hollowed her out, and the town smelled of bread—actual bread, fresh bread, a bakery somewhere on the near street already firing its ovens for the morning. She had coin, a little, sewn into her hem. But coin drawn in public meant questions, and questions meant eyes, and eyes—

Eyes were the problem. Because she had looked at herself in a stream that morning and seen, for just a moment before she'd mastered it, the faint light of the ember-mark glowing through her sleeve like a coal through cloth. Out here, in the loud world, she leaked. She glowed. She was a bell in a world full of ears, and she had no idea yet how to stop ringing.

So she did the thing hungry desperate girls have done since the invention of bakeries. She waited for the baker's rounds-song to carry him to the far end of his yard, and she slipped through the side gate, and she took a loaf off the cooling rack by the kitchen door—only one, only what she needed—and she was three steps back toward the gate when a voice behind her said, pleasantly,

"You're doing it wrong, ember-girl. You want the second rack. First one's counted."

Lior spun, fire leaping unbidden to her palm, and found herself looking at a slight figure perched on top of the woodshed like a cat that owned the town—a young woman, sharp-faced and sharp-eyed, with silver-pierced ears and a grin that showed too many teeth, turning something small and glittering over in her fingers.

Something that looked, Lior realized with a lurch, exactly like the little brass clasp that had held her coin-purse shut since dawn.

"Now," the thief said, hopping down without a sound, "let's talk about the road south, and who's hunting you, and why your pocket just tried to pick *me*."
