## 8. The Thief Who Sells Yesterday

The city of Corren's Landing smelled of fish and tar and the particular sourness of people who had learned to be afraid in public. Sable Vane knew that smell the way other people knew their mothers' voices. She had been breathing it since she was six years old, when a man with soft hands and a ledger had bought her childhood out from under her one afternoon at a time.

She sat on the roof of the tannery on Weft Street because roofs were where she did her best thinking, and her best thinking today was about hunger. Not hers—hers was an old companion, easily ignored—but the girl's. The valley girl with the ash-colored cloak and the walk of someone who had never once had to look over her shoulder until three days ago.

Sable had been watching her since the bridge at Marrow Ford.

Not for any noble reason. Noble reasons were expensive, and Sable had learned early that sentiment was just another currency, and like all currencies it could be counterfeited. She watched the girl because the girl was interesting, and interesting things in Corren's Landing had a way of getting people killed, and Sable had a professional interest in staying un-killed.

But there was more to it than that, though she'd have bitten anyone who said so aloud.

Three nights ago, at the ford, she had watched the girl sleep by a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, and she had felt something brush against her own mind like a moth against glass. A whisper of magic, untrained, leaking out of the girl the way heat leaks from a badly built stove. Sable had spent twenty years around the gifted—the buying of them, the selling of them, the quiet removal of what made them valuable—and she knew what raw, unshaped power looked like when it didn't know it was being seen.

It looked like a lantern wrapped in wet cloth. It looked like trouble.

And trouble, these days, was the only commodity still rising in price.

* * *

The market on Weft Street opened at dawn and lied about its wares until dusk. Sable moved through it in her gray coat with its seventeen pockets, nine of which held things that were not things at all—a stolen sunset, a dead man's name, the smell of rain on the day someone's daughter was born. Inventory. Stock. The merchandise of a memory-thief, which was a polite word for what she was, and she preferred impolite words anyway because they were harder to prosecute.

She clocked the girl before the girl clocked the baker's cart.

That was the thing about valley folk—they walked like the world owed them nothing and they'd never tested the claim. Lior Hartwell—Sable had picked the name off her surface thoughts two days ago, careless as an open purse—Lior Hartwell stood at the edge of the fishmonger's row with her hood up and her hands hidden, watching a baker's boy unload morning loaves with the fixed, desperate attention of someone doing arithmetic with her stomach.

Sable leaned against a post and waited. Watching a bad theft happen was like watching a play you'd already seen: you knew where the bodies would fall.

The girl drifted closer to the cart. Waited for the baker to turn. Reached—

And here was where it got strange.

Because the reach was wrong. Not clumsy-wrong, not the fumbling of a sheltered farm girl with honest hands. The girl's fingers stopped a hair's breadth above the crust, hesitated as if touching something hot, and then—Sable saw it clearly, saw it with the part of her that saw everything, the part that had made her valuable and ruined and free all at once—the girl's hand *twitched sideways*, a small involuntary jerk, and knocked a copper coin off the cart's ledge into the mud.

The baker turned. Shouted. Grabbed.

And Lior Hartwell, oath-keeper of some hidden guild in a valley that didn't exist on maps, stood there holding a loaf she hadn't meant to take, looking as surprised by it as the baker was.

"No," Sable said under her breath. "No, you absolute *idiot*."

She was moving before she finished the sentence. Seventeen pockets, and the eighteenth—the one over her heart—held the only thing she'd ever kept rather than sold: a sliver of a memory of running, of small feet on cobbles, of a door closing behind her. She didn't know whose feet they'd been anymore. That was the joke of her trade. She sold yesterday so thoroughly that some mornings she woke up as a stranger in her own skull.

She caught the baker's wrist as his hand closed on the girl's collar.

"Master Odo." She smiled the smile that cost her nothing and bought her everything. "Your wife sent me. Says the miller's shorting your flour again, and she wants you home before noon to see it."

Odo's face went through the small war that faces go through—suspicion, greed, the fear of wives—and lost. He released the girl's collar, glared at her, spat into the gutter near her boot, and stomped off toward his domestic crisis, which did not exist and would not exist until Sable decided whether to make it exist.

Then Sable turned to the thief who couldn't steal.

"Walk," she said quietly. "Now. Before he remembers he has questions."

* * *

They ended up in the shadow of the aqueduct, where the city's noise went to die. The girl had the sense to keep walking without argument, which raised her stock slightly, but she also had the look of someone assembling questions behind her teeth, which lowered it again.

"You took the bread," Sable said. "You didn't mean to take the bread."

"I—" The girl stopped. Started again. Her voice had an accent Sable couldn't place, smooth as river stone, worn down by somewhere very quiet. "I was going to pay for it. I was going to leave the coin. My hand—it moved on its own."

"Mm." Sable studied her. Up close, the valley girl was younger than she'd seemed at the ford—nineteen, maybe twenty, with a burn-scarred wrist she kept pulling her sleeve over and eyes the color of banked coals. Literally the color of banked coals. Sable had seen a lot of eyes in her line of work. She had never seen eyes that looked like they were deciding whether to catch. "Has that happened before? Things moving when you don't tell them to?"

"No."

"Liar," Sable said pleasantly. "Or worse, truth-teller who doesn't know she's lying. Which is it?"

The girl's jaw tightened. "Who are you?"

"Sable Vane. Memory-thief, road-finder, occasional rescuer of fools who steal bread with their elbows." She let the silence stretch. "You're going south. To the mountain."

Something flickered across the girl's face—fast, but Sable made her living catching fast things. Fear. And under the fear, a stubbornness like iron rebar in wet concrete.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"And I don't care." Sable pushed off the wall. "Here's what I care about. You've got power coming off you like smoke off a doused fire, you've got no idea how to hold it in, and this city has three hunt patrols walking rounds between here and the south gate. Yesterday they burned a family in Tanner's Square. The child first. They do the child first so the parents scream louder—it draws the rest of us out to watch, and watching is how they find the next ones." She said it flatly, without performance, because the truth needed no decoration. "You want to reach the Undercourt alive, you need me. And I need—"

She stopped.

This was the part she always stopped at. Twenty years of selling pieces of herself had taught her exactly how much of her story a stranger got to buy, and the answer was: none of it, unless the price was right.

But the girl was looking at her with those coal-ember eyes, and there was something in them—not pity, thank every merciful absence, but recognition. The look of someone who had also lost something to a ledger.

"What do you need?" the girl asked.

Sable laughed, short and ugly. "Straight to it. No haggling. Valley folk really are terrible merchants." She looked away, up at the aqueduct, where pigeons nested in the joints of old stone. "There's a soul in the Undercourt with my name on it. Has been since I was six years old. A man came to our house with soft hands and a book, and my mother signed, and I remember—I *remember*—because remembering is the one thing they can't take from me, taking is what I do—" Her voice snagged. She hated that. She smoothed it flat. "My soul sits in a jar under that mountain, and every year it gets a little further from me, and every year I get a little more hollow, and one day there'll be nothing left to sell and nothing left to sell *with*. So. That's my price. You want the roads, the passwords, the ways past the gate-wards that even the hunt doesn't know? Fine. But we go for my jar too. Both of ours or neither."

* * *

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Sable counted the sounds of the city around them: a hammer somewhere, gulls arguing over the fish docks, the far-off bell of the hunt-patrol changing shifts.

"My brother's soul is there too," the girl said finally. "Tam. He was sold before I could walk. I'm going to get him back."

"Alone?" Sable let the word do its work. "Through the free cities, with Vessic's proclamations nailed to every post from here to the salt marshes? With magic you can't control leaking out of you at every checkpoint?" She shook her head. "You'll last four days. I give you four days, and that's generous, because you've got luck and I can see it hanging around you like flies."

"That's flattering."

"It's inventory assessment." Sable pulled a strip of dried apple from her pocket, bit it, chewed. Let the girl wait. Negotiation was mostly waiting; everyone else blinked first. "Terms. One: I navigate, you follow. When I say run, you run. When I say hide, you become furniture. Two: you learn to cage that fire in you, or I put you out myself—I know tricks for that, ugly ones, and you won't enjoy them. Three: nobody asks anybody what they've sold. Not memories, not years, not names. What's gone stays gone, and we don't dig in each other's graves."

The girl flinched at that last one, just barely. Interesting. Something already buried, then. Something fresh.

"And in return," Sable continued, "you get a guide who knows every smuggler's cut and every wight-toll from here to the mountain's root, and who has exactly one reason to get there besides you, which means I'll actually try to keep you breathing. Partners. Fifty-fifty. Not friends." She stuck out her hand. "Friends are a liability. Friends are what the hunt uses."

The girl looked at the hand. Then up at Sable's face, with that disconcerting directness, that total lack of the flinch most people wore when they looked at a memory-thief—as if Sable's trade were written on her somewhere, in ink only the guilty could read.

"Lior," she said. "Lior Hartwell." And she took the hand.

Her grip was warm. Warmer than it should have been, warm as a hearthstone, and through the skin-to-skin contact Sable felt it again—that moth-wing brush of untrained power, curious, reaching, *sniffing* at her like a dog at a stranger's coat. For half a heartbeat it pressed against the walls she'd built around her own mind, and Sable felt something rattle in those walls, something old and locked, and she snatched her hand back faster than she meant to.

"Don't do that," she said sharply.

"Do what? I didn't—"

"I know you didn't. That's what worries me." Sable flexed her fingers. Behind her ribs, in the hollow place where her soul used to sit, something had stirred. Like a jar knocking against a shelf in a house with no one home. She hadn't felt it move in eleven years. "Keep your fire leashed, valley girl. Some doors in me are better shut."

* * *

They walked south through the city as the light went long and amber, and Sable talked, because talking was cheaper than trusting and filled the silences where doubt grew. She showed Lior which gutters ran toward the patrol houses and which ran away. She taught her the beggar's cant for *soldiers ahead* (two coughs) and *clear road* (a hummed tuneless note). She pointed out the proclamation nailed to the customs house, Vessic's seal fat and black as a tick at the bottom:

BY ORDER OF THE KING: ALL GIFTED PERSONS SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION WITHIN FORTY DAYS. THE UNREGISTERED SHALL BE BURNED. REWARD OFFERED FOR REPORTS. THE CROWN PROTECTS ITS OWN.

"The crown protects its own," Lior read aloud, and her voice had a cold edge Sable hadn't heard from her yet. "What does that mean, coming from him?"

"It means the gifted who serve him get collars instead of pyres." Sable spat. "Collars are just pyres you can ride to work."

At the south gate, the queue was long and the guards were bored, and bored guards were the dangerous kind. Sable pressed a coin into the gate-sergeant's palm with her left hand while her right hand, hidden in her sleeve, brushed two fingers against his temple—light as adjusting a collar—and lifted the memory of having checked her papers. It was a small theft, a crumb, the kind she did forty times a day. The sergeant waved them through with the vague contentment of a man who had done his duty recently and well.

On the far side, where the road unspooled brown and rutted toward the hill country, Lior stopped.

"You took something from him," she said. Not accusing. Worse—understanding. "Just now. At the gate."

"A crumb. A nothing. He won't miss it." Sable shrugged. "That's the trade, valley girl. I take yesterdays and sell them to people who want to be somebody else for an hour. Rich men buy first kisses. Grieving widows buy last arguments, replayed kinder than they happened. Soldiers buy whole afternoons of peace. Everyone's hungry for a life that isn't theirs." She started walking. "Everyone except me. I sell mine and stay empty. That's the joke. Go ahead and laugh."

Lior didn't laugh. She fell into step beside her, and for a while the only sound was their boots and the wind coming down off the hills, carrying the smell of woodsmoke from farms that might already be burning.

"Why the Undercourt?" Lior asked at last. "Why now? If your soul's been there fifteen years—"

"Fourteen. And because the mountain's going to close." Sable kept her eyes on the road. "You're not the only one who hears things. There's talk in the low taverns, talk that comes down from the labor gangs—Vessic means to seal the Undercourt at midwinter. Seal it and keep what's inside. Every jar. Every soul. His, forever." She glanced sideways. "So whatever you're planning to do when you get there, sister, plan it fast. Because after midwinter, there is no getting in. And there is *certainly* no getting out."

Ahead of them the hills rose dark against the dying light, and somewhere beyond them, beneath miles of stone, Sable's soul sat in its jar, patient as a debt.

She walked faster.
