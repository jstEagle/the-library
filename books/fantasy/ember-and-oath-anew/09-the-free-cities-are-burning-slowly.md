## 9. The Free Cities Are Burning Slowly

The road to Corren's Landing smelled of salt and smoke long before we saw its walls.

I had thought at first it was the sea—Sable had told me the city sat on the gray water like a gull on a spar, all weathered timber and hanging lanterns—but by midday of our second day walking, I knew better. Smoke has a different voice than brine. Brine is clean; it stings and passes. Smoke settles into the back of the throat and stays there, patient as a debt.

"Woodsmoke," Sable said, when I asked. She said it too quickly, the way people say things they've already decided not to discuss. "Kilns. Tanneries. A city that size burns something day and night."

But her eyes went to the horizon, to the smudge standing above the rooftops where no single chimney should have been able to reach, and her jaw did a small, hard thing that I was learning to read as fear she refused to name.

We had been traveling together for six days. Long enough for me to learn that Sable walked with her weight on the balls of her feet, like someone perpetually about to change her mind about staying. Long enough for me to learn that outside the valley my blood carried something loud in it—a heat behind my sternum that woke when I was frightened and whispered suggestions I didn't understand. Long enough to know that Sable had noticed it too, and watched me the way a sailor watches weather.

"Corren's Landing is a free city," she said now, as if reminding herself. "Vessic's writ doesn't run here. Not officially."

"And unofficially?"

She smiled without any humor in it. "Unofficially, everything runs everywhere, if the price is right. Come on. Keep your hood up and your hands in your pockets. If you feel the heat start—" she tapped her own sternum, mirroring mine— "you pinch the inside of your elbow. Hard. Trust me."

"Why would that help?"

"Because pain is louder than magic," she said, "and you're not practiced enough yet to be both at once."

I wanted to ask how she knew so much about what burned inside me. I had learned already that asking Sable direct questions was like throwing stones down a well—you heard them fall a long way before anything answered. So I kept my hood up and my questions in my pockets alongside my hands, and we walked toward the smoke.

* * *

Corren's Landing announced itself first by sound.

The walls were old, older than Vessic's grandfather's kingdom, green with sea-salt lichen, and crowded against their outer face were tents and lean-tos and cookfires—the sprawl of refugees who had come seeking the word *free* and found only a wall instead. We moved through them in a river of strangers: a woman carrying a loom's worth of yarn on her back, children driving geese, an old man selling cups of broth from a cart whose wheels had been mended with rope. Everyone looked over their shoulder. Everyone counted their children twice.

At the gate there was no line, which should have been a kindness but felt like a held breath. Two guards in the colors of the city—not the crown's black-and-ember, I noted, and some knot in my chest loosened—waved us through with the bored indifference of men paid too little to care.

Inside, the city climbed. Streets stacked on streets, stairs cut into the hillside, laundry strung between leaning houses like signal flags. Lanterns hung everywhere, even in daylight—hundreds of them, thousands, glass and paper and tin, unlit, waiting. They swung gently in the harbor wind and made a sound like slow rain.

"Why so many?" I asked.

"Festival of First Light," said a fishwife beside us, overhearing, her basket silver with scales. "Week after next. We light every lantern in the city for the drowned and the lost." She said it easily, kindly, and then her eyes flicked to my hood, to the shape of my face beneath it, and the kindness cooled into caution. She moved on.

"The drowned and the lost," Sable repeated softly, when she was gone. "That's one way to say it."

"One way to say what?"

Sable bought two skewers of grilled eel from a stall and handed me one, and it was only when the hot, oily, wonderful taste hit my tongue that I realized I hadn't eaten since dawn. "Every city has its dead," she said around her food. "Corren's just honest enough to hang lights for them." She nodded up a narrow street, toward a stone tower rising above the roofs, banded with bronze. "There's a man in the Lanternkeepers' Hall. Odo. He moves people south, out of Vessic's reach, for a price. If anyone knows a road under the mountain, it's him."

"You said you knew the roads."

"I know the roads *to* the Undercourt," Sable said. "I don't know how to get *in*. There's a difference, and the difference is why you need me, and why I need whatever Odo's selling." She finished her eel and licked her thumb, casual as a cat. "Also I'm hungry, and theft is more fun with a witness."

"You stole these?"

"I *bought* these," she said, wounded. "With money I acquired elsewhere."

The heat behind my sternum laughed. I pinched my elbow.

* * *

We heard the drums at midafternoon.

They came from the direction of the harbor square—a deep, ceremonial pounding, nothing like festival music. Festival music climbs. This rhythm descended, beat after beat, like something being nailed shut. The crowd in the streets began to move toward it, drawn the way crowds always are, and Sable grabbed my sleeve.

"No," she said. "Whatever it is, we don't need to see it."

But the crowd was a current and we were in it, and currents do not care what you need. We were pushed along the narrowing street, past shuttered windows, past a tavern keeper pulling his sign inside, past a mother pressing her palm flat over her son's eyes without slowing—and then the street opened onto the harbor square, and I understood what the smoke had been saying since morning.

There was a pyre in the center of the square.

Not one pyre. Three. Stacked pallets and broken furniture and tarred rope, built high as a house, and tied to the central stake were four figures: a man, a woman, a girl of perhaps twelve, and a baby wrapped against the woman's chest.

The girl was the reason for it. I understood that within three heartbeats, because a crier stood on a platform nearby in the black-and-ember of the crown, reading from a scroll in a voice trained to carry over wind and grief alike: "—found guilty of harboring a registered gift, namely the child Wren Corlos, age eleven years, gift of finding, unregistered and undeclared, contrary to the Gift Edict of King Vessic, first of his name. The sentence is fire. Let all who shelter the gifted look upon them and remember."

The crowd made a sound I had never heard a crowd make. Not a roar. Not a booing. Something lower, wetter, collective—a sound like a city swallowing.

"They're free-city people," I said. My voice came out strange, thin. "You said Vessic's writ doesn't run here."

"It doesn't." Sable's hand was iron on my arm. "It doesn't have to. Half this square took crown coin to stand here today. Look at them. Look at the ones not looking."

She was right. Around the edges of the square, pressed into doorways and alley mouths, were people who had turned their backs entirely—shopkeepers facing their own walls, a lamplighter studying a lamp that needed nothing, an old woman with her forehead resting against a doorpost, her lips moving. Praying, or counting, or simply enduring the arithmetic of being alive while others were not allowed to be.

The drums stopped.

A priest of the Ember Rite—I knew the vestments, we wore their echo in the valley, ash-gray robes with a single coal-colored sash—stepped forward with a torch. And the crowd's sound died completely, and in that silence I could hear the baby crying. Small. Furious. The only voice in the whole square saying exactly what it meant.

The heat behind my sternum opened its eye.

I don't know how else to describe it. All my life in the valley I had been told I was ordinary, ward-kept, safe, dull as ditchwater, and I had believed it because believing was easy. But since the first mile beyond the wards, something had been waking in me, and I had felt it only in fragments—a trick with a patrol's lantern flame, a warmth in my palms on cold nights, dreams of falling upward. Now, watching the priest lift the torch, watching the mother on the stake press her cheek to her daughter's hair, watching the baby's fists churn the air, the thing in my chest stood all the way up and *looked*.

"No," Sable breathed, and I don't know if she said it to me or to the world.

The torch touched the kindling.

Fire is fast when it wants to be. It ran up the tarred rope like a living thing, orange and eager, and the crowd flinched as one body, and the heat in my chest became a roar—

And the fire *stopped*.

Not died. Stopped. Every tongue of flame on that pyre froze mid-lick, hanging off the kindling like painted light, like a painting of fire, and the square went from silence to a silence underneath silence, the kind of quiet that has a hole in the middle of it. Somewhere a gull screamed. The baby kept crying.

I felt it leave me. That's the truth I can't make prettier. I felt the heat pour out through my ribs and my arms and my open mouth, across forty feet of cobblestones, and wrap itself around that pyre and *hold*, the way you'd hold a door shut against a flood, and I felt the cost of it immediately—a dragging, hollowing ache, as though something were drinking me through a straw—and I heard myself say, out loud, in a voice that did not entirely sound like mine:

"Not this one."

The priest turned. He looked at the frozen fire. He looked at the crowd. And then, slowly, terribly, he began to walk the pyre's edge, scanning faces, looking for the source, and his ember-colored sash seemed to burn brighter than the fire he'd failed to light.

Sable hit me like a wave takes a swimmer.

She didn't drag me—dragging leaves a shape, she'd tell me later, dragging tells everyone which way you went. She folded herself around my arm and walked, fast and ordinary, a woman helping a drunk friend home, and she steered us sideways into the crowd's confusion, into the alley mouths full of people pretending not to have seen, into the smell of fish guts and brine, and behind us the square erupted—shouting, the crier's voice cracking, someone screaming *witch, witch, find the witch*—and the frozen fire, released, roared back to life all at once with a sound like the sky tearing.

I heard the mother scream once. Then the drums started again, faster, to cover it, or to celebrate it, or both.

I don't remember most of the next hour. Sable says I walked well. Sable says I only cried twice, quietly, and that she talked the entire time about nothing—about the price of eel, about a pickpocket she once knew who specialized in left shoes—so that I would have something to hold besides the sound of those drums. She got us through the tanners' quarter, down the cliff-stairs, into a net-mender's shed she apparently knew about, and there she sat me on a coil of rope, put a cup of water in my hands, and waited.

My hands wouldn't stop shaking. The water rang against the cup's rim like a tiny bell.

"I killed them," I said.

"You didn't."

"I froze the fire. I let go. I let go and they—"

"You held it as long as a person could hold it," Sable said, "and then your body took the choice away from you, which is the only mercy in this whole rotten day, because if you'd had the choice you'd still be standing there, holding it, until they burned you too, and then the fire would have taken all five of you." She crouched in front of me, and her face, usually so quick and armored, was bare for once, and tired, and young. "Listen to me, valley-girl. What you did today was impossible. I've seen court mages fail to do what you did by accident. And it changed nothing, and it saved no one, and it will get us both killed if you ever do it again in a city square. All three of those things are true at once. That's what the free cities are now. Everything true at once, and none of it bearable."

"The baby," I said. The word came out of me like a splinter working loose. "There was a *baby*."

"I know." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word, and she covered it by standing, turning away, busying herself with the shed's little stove. "I know," she said again, steadier. "So. Here's what happens now. You have two choices, and I'll only offer them once. Choice one: we go north, or west, anywhere, and you spend your life hiding what you are, pinching your elbow, keeping your hood up, surviving. Plenty of people do it. It's not shameful. Choice two—"

She stopped. Fed a twist of paper into the stove. Watched it catch.

"Choice two," she said, "is that we stop running *from* something and start running *at* it. The mountain. The Undercourt. Whatever's down there that makes kings want jars full of souls, whatever taught Vessic that people are fuel. You want to know what's inside you? So do I. And I think the answer's in the same place as the answer to all of it." She turned back to me, and the stove-light made her eyes look like banked coals. "Because I'll tell you what I saw in that square, Lior Hartwell. I didn't see a girl freeze a fire. I saw the fire *obey* her. Like it knew her. Like it was glad to."

Outside the shed, far off, the drums finally stopped. In the ringing quiet, the harbor bells began to ring instead—all of them, hundreds of lanterns being lit early for the drowned and the lost, the whole city glowing gold against the coming dark, mourning four people whose names would be on no ledger except the crown's kill-list.

I looked at my hands. Beneath the skin of my wrists, faint as a brand seen through cloth, something glimmered—an ember-mark I hadn't noticed before, or hadn't let myself notice, shaped like a small closed ring.

"Sable," I said. "What does it mean, if the fire obeys me?"

She looked at my wrists. I watched her decide not to lie, and then watch her decide to anyway—or half of one.

"It means," she said, "that whoever sold your brother's soul to the mountain may have gotten more than they bargained for. Because vessels, valley-girl, are supposed to be empty. And you—" she nodded at my wrists, at the faint ring of light, at all of me— "are the least empty person I have ever met."

Above the harbor, the lanterns burned. Below the mountain, whatever waited, waited. And somewhere between the two, I felt my purpose close around me like a hand—no longer a question of whether I would go to the Undercourt, but only how fast my legs could carry me.

"We find Odo tomorrow," I said. "First light."

Sable almost smiled. "Now *that*," she said, "sounds like fire talking."
