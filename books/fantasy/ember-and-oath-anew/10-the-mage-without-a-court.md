## 10. The Mage Without a Court

The smuggler's den beneath the Tannery Row smelled of wet leather, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of men who had stopped believing in better days. Iven Ashgrave had lived in that smell for eleven months, and he had learned to love it the way a drowning man learns to love brackish water—gratefully, and without illusion.

He sat in his corner with his back to the wall, as always, a cup of tannin-bitter tea going cold at his elbow. Around him the den did its evening work: dice rattling in a bone cup, a woman with river-tattooed arms arguing freight rates over a map of the Silt Canal, two boys—runners, maybe twelve years old—dozing against a barrel of salted eels. Nobody looked at him. That was the arrangement. In Corren's Landing, not-looking was a currency, and Iven paid it gladly.

Once he had been looked at everywhere he went. Once, whole rooms had turned when he entered—not for his face, which was ordinary enough, but for what hung around him: the silver chain of court office, the violet sash, the faint shimmer of working wards that clung to a court mage like perfume. *Iven Ashgrave, First Circle, Keeper of the King's Seals.* He could still feel the weight of those titles sometimes, sitting on his shoulders like a yoke made of glass.

Then Vessic had come. Then the Gift Hunt. Then the night Iven had stood in the throne room and refused—with words, in front of witnesses—to sign the writ that made gifted children crown property. He remembered the sound his chain had made when they cut it from his neck. A small sound. A cheap sound, for all it had cost him.

He had run. That was the part no ballad would ever sing kindly. Not martyred, not defiant to the last—he had *run*, down through the palace drains and out past the eastern wall and south along the canal roads until his boots split, and he had kept running until he reached this den under the tanneries, where a man named Osk paid him three coppers a week to read contracts and forge bills of lading, because even disgraced court mages could write a convincing hand.

Three coppers a week. He told himself it was penance. Most nights he managed to believe it.

* * *

The trouble began, as most troubles did in Corren's Landing, with bread.

Iven noticed her because she was doing everything wrong. She came in off the street with the particular stiffness of someone who had never learned how cities watched you—a girl, seventeen perhaps, travel-worn, with a hood pulled up in a way that drew the eye rather than deflecting it. She moved through the den's edge like a wading bird through shallows, quick and light and utterly conspicuous, and she went straight for the kitchen shelf where Osk's cook left loaves to cool.

She took one. She was fast—Iven would grant her that; her hand barely seemed to pass through the space above the loaf—but the cook's boy was faster, or luckier, and his shout cracked across the den like a whip.

"Thief! She's got the—stop her!"

And here was the thing that made Iven set down his cup: the loaf caught fire.

Not burned. *Caught fire.* Flame bloomed along its crust in a soft gold corona, gentle as candlelight, and the girl snatched her hand back as if the bread had bitten her, and the fire guttered out just as quickly, leaving the loaf whole and steaming and utterly impossible. For half a heartbeat the whole den stared. Then the den did what dens do—it decided it hadn't seen anything, because seeing things brought soldiers, and soldiers were bad for business. The dice resumed. The argument over freight rates climbed back to its previous volume.

But the girl stood frozen in the middle of the floor, staring at her own hand, and Iven saw her face beneath the hood, and he thought: *she didn't know it would do that.*

She didn't know her own fire.

Two of Osk's enforcers were already moving toward her, unhurried, professional. In Corren's Landing you didn't call the watch for a bread thief; you broke the thief's fingers yourself and saved the watch fee. Iven calculated, in the cold arithmetic he'd once used on matters of state, exactly what interfering would cost him. Exposure. Questions. Possibly Osk's displeasure, which was survivable, and possibly the enforcers' curiosity about why a scrivener knew warding gestures, which was not.

He interfered anyway. Later he would tell himself it was strategy—that a mage who could burn bread uncontrolled was a walking beacon for every hunt patrol between here and the mountains, and beacons needed handling. It was even true. But underneath the strategy there was something older and less forgivable: when he had refused to sign that writ, he had promised himself that next time—and there was always a next time—he would not simply refuse. He would act.

"Osk," he said, rising, pitching his voice to carry. "That's my new runner. The one I told you about. From the canal contracts."

Osk, a slab of a man with a ledger-keeper's squint, looked from Iven to the girl and back. "You never said nothing about no runner."

"I'm saying it now." Iven crossed the room, took the girl's elbow—she flinched, then didn't—and pressed three coppers into the cook-boy's palm, plus a fourth for the boy's silence, which was the going rate. "She's green. She'll learn."

Osk grunted, which in the grammar of Osk meant *this costs you*, and returned to his ledgers. The enforcers melted away with the professional indifference of men whose violence was only ever a wage.

Iven steered the girl toward the shadows by the salt barrels, out of the lamplight, and only then did he get a proper look at her—and only then did the world tilt sideways under his feet.

Because her sleeve had ridden up when she'd flinched. And on the inside of her wrist, just below the heel of her hand, there was a mark: a small whorl of scar tissue, fine as lace, shaped like an ember seen through smoke. A ring of darkened skin around a paler center, branching at the edges into delicate filaments, as though something bright had been sealed inside her flesh and had left its shadow behind.

Iven had seen that mark before. He had drawn it, once, in a vault beneath a mountain, in a circle of chalk and blood and obedience, while a man he had called mentor dictated the terms and a child screamed behind a screen.

Vessel-mark. Third order. The kind that was never supposed to leave the Undercourt.

His hands went cold. His tea, forgotten, might as well have been a thousand miles away. The den's noise—the dice, the haggling, the drip of the eel-barrel brine—all of it receded, and Iven Ashgrave heard instead the memory of his own voice, younger and more obedient, reading the binding formula aloud: *that the soul be housed, that the house be hidden, that the house forget the door.*

"Sit down," he said quietly. "Before you fall down."

"I don't—" The girl's voice was rough with fear and something under the fear, some banked heat. "I wasn't stealing. Well. I was. But I'll pay for it, I have coin, I just—"

"Sit." He guided her onto the crate himself. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and road dust and, faintly, of ash—real ash, hearth-ash, the smell of a place where fires were tended rather than escaped. Her eyes were gray-green, wary, exhausted, and set too deep for her face, as though she hadn't slept properly in weeks. Ordinary eyes. Traveler's eyes.

Except that when the lamplight shifted, something in them flickered. Something amber. There and gone, like a coal breathing.

*I know what you are,* Iven thought, and the knowing sat in his chest like swallowed ice. *God help me, I know exactly what you are, and I helped build the thing you're running from.*

"You're not from here," he said aloud. "Corren's Landing, I mean. You walk like the road is still under your feet."

"Is that a crime now too?" Defiance, but thin, stretched over exhaustion. She was cataloguing the exits. He respected that. He'd done the same thing his first week in the den.

"Everything's a crime now. That's rather the king's whole project." He lowered himself onto the opposite crate, keeping his hands visible, open—an old habit from negotiating with frightened courtiers, repurposed for frightened girls. "My name is Ashgrave. Iven Ashgrave. I keep books for Osk and ask no questions, which is the safest trade in this city. What should I call you?"

A long pause. He watched her decide how much lie to spend. "Lior," she said finally. Just that. No family name, and the omission itself was information.

"Lior." He let it sit. Then, softly, watching her face the way he'd once watched ambassadors: "Your wrist is showing."

Her hand snapped down over the mark so fast she nearly knocked the tea cup from its saucer. The color drained out of her face and then flooded back, red and furious. "It's a burn," she said. "From when I was small. A hearth accident."

"Yes," Iven said. "That's what everyone who bears one calls it. A hearth accident. I've read the phrase in four different hands, in three different centuries of Undercourt records. It's remarkable how consistent the lie stays." He leaned forward, and pitched his voice beneath the den's noise, beneath hearing. "It's a vessel-mark, third order. It means that somewhere beneath the mountain, in a vault lit by ember-light, there is a jar with your name on the outside of it—or rather, on the inside of it, which is worse. And it means that whoever bound that jar chose a living house for what it held, and hid the house somewhere the harvesters couldn't easily reach."

The girl had gone very still. Not the stillness of disbelief—Iven knew disbelief, its flutter and denial. This was the stillness of someone hearing a thing they had half-known for years, the way you half-know a word on the tip of your tongue. "You're mad," she whispered.

"Almost certainly. It runs in my profession." He rubbed his eyes. Eleven months of hiding had softened him; he could feel the court-mage's precision going blunt at the edges, and he needed it sharp now, because what he said next would change the shape of both their lives, and probably several others'. "Listen to me, Lior-of-no-family-name, because I will only say this once, and then we are both going to pretend this conversation happened differently. There is a patrol of the Gift Hunt quartered at the Saltgate barracks—six riders and a handler with a scent-hound, rotated out every ninth day. They are here because the Hunt has had reports of unregistered gift-signs along the canal roads. Fire-signs. Small ones. A baker's oven that lit without kindling in Millbrook. A lantern that burned green in a ferry-house upriver. Someone is following the same trail you're leaving, and they are nine days behind you at most, and when they find you, you will not be broken on a wheel or ransomed. You will be taken south, to the mountain, and what happens to vessels in the Undercourt makes burning look like mercy."

Her breath had gone shallow. "How do you know all this?"

"Because until a year ago I was the court's First Circle mage. I wrote half the formulas the Hunt uses. I signed the seals on the writs." He held her gaze and did not look away, because looking away was the coward's portion and he had eaten enough of it. "I refused one writ too many, and they cut my chain off in front of the whole court, and I ran. So yes—I know how they think, because I *was* how they think. And I am telling you that whatever you came to Corren's Landing to do, you cannot do it alone, and you cannot do it while your gift flares every time you're startled."

"It doesn't flare," she said, too fast. "I control it. Mostly. The valley—where I'm from, magic doesn't—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. Too late. Iven felt the pieces click together like tumblers in a lock.

"The Hollow Valley," he breathed. "Of course. Of *course*. That's where they'd hide a vessel—in the one place in the world where the mark can't be read and the gift can't wake. Clever. Cruel and clever. They put you behind wards that smothered your own fire so you'd grow up ordinary, and the moment you stepped past the boundary—" He gestured at her scorched sleeve, at the air around her, which even now carried a faint warmth, a shimmer like heat off summer stone. "Every mile you've walked since has been waking you up. And every waking hour, you shine brighter to anyone with the instruments to see."

For a long moment she said nothing. The den churned on around them—dice, bargains, the small commerce of the overlooked—and the girl from the valley sat with her thumb pressed over her impossible wrist, and Iven watched her decide, again, how much truth she could afford. He recognized the expression. He wore it in the mirror every morning.

"My brother," she said at last, very quietly. "They say his soul was sold. To the court under the mountain. Twenty years ago, before I can remember. I'm going there to get it back."

The ice in Iven's chest cracked clean through.

He had spent eleven months telling himself that the vault's contents were abstractions—entries in ledgers, units of power, the cold bookkeeping of a usurper's treasury. Names, but distant names. And here was a girl with ash-smell in her hair and a hearth-accident on her wrist saying *my brother* as if the ledger were a wound.

"And you think," he said carefully, "that you can walk into the Undercourt and take him out."

"I think I can try."

"They will peel you apart to find out what you know. If they discover what you *are*—" He stopped. Because that was the thought, wasn't it? The thought that had been circling him since the lamplight caught her eyes. A vessel, third order, hidden in the Hollow Valley, walking free with her mark uncovered and her gift waking. Either the binding had failed—which happened, rarely, catastrophically—or the vessel had been *sent out*, deliberately, by someone who wanted the house far from the vault. Maren, he thought. Old Maren of the Ember Guild, if she still lived, with her ledgers and her terrible guardianship. Hiding the jar by making the jar believe it was a girl.

Or—his stomach turned—hiding the jar by making the jar *forget the door*. The formula's own words. *That the house forget the door.*

"Come with me," he heard himself say.

Her eyebrows rose. "Where?"

"Away from here. Tonight, before the Saltgate patrol rotates. There's a woman I've heard of—a thief, a memory-thief, works the market district—who knows the southern roads better than any cartographer alive, including the roads that lead under the mountain. If anyone can get a vessel to the Undercourt without the Hunt closing around her, it's her. She owes me nothing and I owe her less, but I can pay, and I can lie convincingly, and between us we might manage what neither of us could alone." He paused, and gave her the last of his honesty, because she deserved to price it. "Understand me, Lior. I am not helping you out of kindness. Kindness died in me somewhere between the throne room and the tanneries. I am helping you because I built the machine that took your brother, and I have spent eleven months hiding from it, and hiding has bought me nothing but tea and shame. If there is a debt, I intend to start paying it. With interest."

She studied him for a long time. Outside, somewhere above the tannery roofs, a bell rang the ninth hour, and the sound fell through the floorboards like a stone through water. He watched her weigh him—the disgraced mage, the forged bills of lading, the trembling precision of a man who had been somebody and was now nobody—and he watched her arrive, with the terrible speed of the young, at a decision.

"The thief," she said. "What's her name?"

"Sable. They say she sells yesterday. Whether she'll sell to us is another matter."

Something almost like a smile touched the girl's mouth—tired, crooked, carrying more courage than comfort. "Then I suppose," said Lior Hartwell, vessel of the Undercourt, keeper of a brother's soul she did not yet know she carried, "we'd better go find out what yesterday costs."

Iven rose, gathered his meager bundle from beneath the salt barrels—eleven months of hiding, packed in thirty seconds—and followed her toward the stairs, toward the street, toward the mountain.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the dead coals of the den's hearth glowed briefly brighter, and settled, and waited.
