## 11. Vessel

The smuggler's den beneath the tannery smelled of salt, rot, and old smoke—the smell of a city pretending it had never been anything but free. Iven Ashgrave had lived in that smell for three years now, and he had learned to read the den the way he'd once read the court's weather: by what was *not* said. Tonight the silence had a shape. It sat in the corner with its hood down and its wrists wrapped in bandages, eating bread like someone who had learned hunger as a discipline rather than an accident.

The girl. Hartwell.

He had seen the mark when she'd reached for the lamp—two heartbeats of ember-light glowing through torn skin, coiled at the pulse point like a sleeping serpent of fire. He had not moved. He had not breathed. Twenty years since he'd last seen that sigil, and he still felt it in his teeth: the vessel-mark of the Undercourt, the brand they pressed into living jars so the brokers could find their property in any crowd, in any dark.

*Property.* The word tasted like the wine he'd drunk the night he'd signed his students' names into the court's service. He poured himself another cup and did not drink it.

"You're staring," Sable said from her perch on the barrel by the door. She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her fingers were doing something small and precise with a sliver of glass—a thief's habit, Iven guessed, keeping hands busy while the mind counted exits.

"I'm thinking," Iven said.

"Careful. In my experience that's how people get hurt."

"More often than you'd believe." He set down the cup. "Girl. Lior. Show me your wrist."

Lior looked up. In the lamplight her face was all sharp angles and stubbornness, ash-brown hair cut short in the practical way of someone who had never owned a mirror worth trusting. "Why?"

"Because I spent eleven years in Vessic's court learning what every mark on every body means, and because if I'm wrong about yours, I'll apologize and buy you dinner. And if I'm right—" He stopped. Chose the words carefully. "If I'm right, then everything you think you are has been a lie told by people who loved you, which is somehow worse than a lie told by people who didn't."

The den went quiet. Even Dara, who had been cleaning her crossbow in the corner with the mechanical patience of a soldier avoiding conversation, paused with the string half-drawn.

Lior unwound the bandage slowly, as though she were peeling back something more than cloth. The mark caught the light and held it—that deep ember-glow, alive, patient, *waiting*. Iven had seen jars glow like that. Hundreds of them, ranked in the vaults beneath the mountain, each one humming with a soul that had been folded up small enough to fit inside clay.

But never on skin. Never walking around wearing a face and asking questions about the road south.

"That's not a guild mark," Lior said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles weren't. "The Ember Guild marks are different. Mine's just—Maren said it was a birthmark that came in wrong."

"Maren," Iven repeated softly. "Elder Maren, of the Hollow Valley. Yes. I imagine she said a great many things." He rose, crossed the room, and crouched before the girl so their eyes were level. Up close he could see the glow pulsing faintly in time with her heartbeat. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide whether to hit me. Agreed?"

"I make no promises about the hitting."

"Fair." He took a breath. "Twenty years ago, the Hollow Valley should have burned. Every gifted soul in it, every hidden jar, every keeper who ever swore the oath. Vessic's predecessor found the valley—I know because I catalogued the reports. But the valley is still there, and you're sitting in front of me, which means someone bought its safety. And there is only one currency the Undercourt accepts for something that large."

Sable's glass-sliver stopped moving. "A soul."

"A soul," Iven agreed. "Sold to the brokers beneath the mountain. And here is the part that matters, girl: souls don't keep. They leak. A jar holds one the way a cracked bell holds a note—for a while, and then the sound goes out of it. So the brokers developed a better vessel. One that heals itself. One that walks, eats, sleeps, and carries its cargo somewhere no ledger can be robbed from." He touched his own wrist, where no mark glowed. "They press the sigil into a living body. Bind the soul into flesh and blood. And the vessel grows up knowing nothing, because a vessel that knows is a vessel that can choose to open."

Lior had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear—Iven knew fear well, had worn it like a uniform for years—but the stillness of someone holding a door shut against a wind they can already feel on their neck.

"No," she said.

"The mark—"

"The mark is a birthmark. Maren said—"

"Maren lied to you," Iven said, gently, because some truths must be handled like eggs. "Or Maren lied to herself so long the lie wore smooth. Either way. Think, girl. You told us your brother vanished before you could form memories of him. You told us the valley keeps no records older than twenty years. You told us magic cannot follow into the Hollow Valley—and yet you walked out of it carrying enough power to light a patrol's hounds like festival lanterns. Where do you suppose that fire lives, if not in you?"

"It's mine. It's always been mine."

"Is it?" He leaned closer. "Has it ever once felt like yours? Or does it feel like something borrowed—something that hums at night, something that answers before you call it, something that knows tricks you never learned?"

He watched her face and saw the answer land there, saw it strike home the way truth strikes: not like a blade but like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was on her. Because he'd seen her fight on the road out of Corren's Landing. He'd seen the trick she used against the hounds—a binding-flare, old court-craft, the kind taught in the Undercourt's own scriptoriums, certainly not in a valley where magic couldn't follow.

"I don't—" Lior stood abruptly, knocking over the stool. "You're wrong. You're a disgraced court mage who reads marks on people's skin, and you're wrong, and I'm going to go check on the horses."

"There are no horses," Dara said quietly. "We sold them at the gate."

"Then I'll check on the absence of horses!" And she was gone, up the ladder, boots hammering the boards above.

Sable exhaled. "Well. That went well."

"She'll come back," Iven said. He sat heavily, suddenly feeling every one of his forty-odd years. "That's the trouble with vessels. They always come back. They're built to hold things together."

* * *

She came back at moonrise, when the tannery vats below had gone silent and the den smelled only of cold ash. Iven was awake—he was always awake; sleep had left him around the same time his reputation did—and he heard her descend the ladder slowly, each rung taken like a question.

She sat across the dead fire from him. Wrapped her arms around her knees. For a long time neither spoke.

"My brother's name was Tam," she said finally. "Is Tam. Maren gave me his compass before I left. She said he liked the north needle best because it pointed at things instead of away from them." Her laugh came out broken. "I've been carrying it for two weeks and I talk to it sometimes. Like it's him. Like some part of him is still—"

She stopped. Iven let the silence do its work.

"If you're right," she said, "if I'm what you say I am—then he's not gone. He's been *here*." She pressed her fist against her sternum, hard, as though trying to knock on a door from the outside. "All this time. Every night I dreamed of a boy teaching me to whistle through grass, every morning I woke up grieving someone I couldn't name—that wasn't memory leaking. That was *him*. Pressing against the walls. Trying to get out. Trying to get *home*."

"Lior—"

"And nobody told me." Her voice cracked open, and underneath it was fury, white-hot and clean. "Maren raised me. Fed me. Taught me the oaths. And she let me grow up as a *jar*."

"Yes," Iven said, because lying to her now would be the final cruelty. "And I would wager everything I have left—which is not much—that she hated herself for it every single day. Guardianship and betrayal wear the same face from the inside, girl. I know. I betrayed thirty students to save my own skin, and I told myself it was protection too."

Lior wiped her face with the heel of her hand, rough, angry. "How do I know? How do I *know*, Iven? If you're wrong—if this is some court trick, some way to control me—"

"You don't take my word," he said. "You take the mountain's word. The mark answers to its maker. There's a working—an old one, from before Vessic, from when the brokers first learned to fold souls into flesh. It shows a vessel what it holds. It doesn't open anything. It just... lets you see."

"Show me."

"Now? In the dark, alone, with no one to catch you if it goes wrong?"

"Show me," she said again, and her eyes in the ember-light of her own wrist were her brother's eyes—Iven realized it even then, before the mirror, before the proof, and wondered how he had missed it for two full weeks—and there was nothing in them he could refuse.

So he built the working.

It was a small thing, as magics went: a bowl of water, a pinch of salt, three drops of blood from the marked wrist, and a word in the old court-tongue that meant *show the tenant*. He whispered it, and the water stilled, and the ember-light in Lior's wrist rose up her arm like dawn climbing a hill, and the surface of the bowl began to shine.

"Look," Iven said softly. "Only look. Whatever you see—it cannot reach you. It's a window, not a door."

Lior leaned over the bowl.

At first there was nothing but her own reflection—hollow-cheeked, road-worn, a stranger she'd been becoming since she left the valley. Then the water shivered, and the reflection *shifted*, the way heat shifts air above a forge. The jaw squared. The shoulders broadened under the road-worn coat. The hair grew longer, darker, falling into eyes that—

That were not hers.

A boy of perhaps seventeen looked up at her from the bowl of water. Tam. Undoubtedly, unmistakably Tam—not the child from her fractured dreams but grown, aged year by year inside her the way a tree ages around a fence post, and his eyes were wide and wet and *looking back at her*, and his mouth shaped a word without sound, and Iven read the shape of it easily because he had seen dying men mouth it a thousand times:

*Sister.*

The bowl shattered. Not the water—the bowl, split clean down the middle as the ember-light snapped back into Lior's wrist, and she staggered upright with her hand pressed over her heart and a sound coming out of her that was not quite a scream and not quite a word.

"He sees me," she whispered. "Iven. *He sees me.* He's in there and he *sees* me and he's been watching through my eyes—"

"Lior—"

"Every time I looked at the compass. Every time I thought his name. He was *right there*—" She turned on Iven, and the glow was climbing past her elbows now, licking up her throat, and the temperature of the room had changed, and somewhere above them a beam groaned. "Get it out. Can you get it out? Take it out of me, cut it out, burn it out, I don't care—"

"No." He said it flat and hard, the voice he'd used on panicking apprentices. "Listen to me. What you're feeling right now—that's the binding reacting to being *seen*. Fight it and it tightens. Panic and it burns. You have to breathe, girl. Breathe and let it settle, because the only thing worse than being a vessel is being a broken one."

"*Don't tell me to breathe!*"

The lamp flared blue. The barrels by the door rattled. And then—footsteps on the ladder, fast ones, and Sable dropped into the room with Dara behind her, crossbow in hand, taking in the burning girl and the shattered bowl and saying, very calmly:

"What happened."

"She looked," Iven said. "Now we all know."

Dara lowered the crossbow slowly. She looked at Lior—at the light crawling under her skin like veins of sunrise—and something in the soldier's face shifted from wariness to a kind of grim recognition, the look of someone reading a list of names and finding her own conscience already written there.

"Sable," Dara said. "Get the water."

Sable was already moving. She filled a tin cup from the rain-barrel and pressed it into Lior's shaking hands, and then—this was the part Iven would remember later, the part that made him revise his opinion of memory-thieves forever—she put her own palms over Lior's glowing fists and said, in a voice stripped of all its usual coin-and-con:

"Hey. Look at me. Whoever's in there, whoever you are, whatever you're carrying—you're still the girl who shared her bread with me in Corren's Landing when she had half a loaf and no reason to. That's not a jar. Jars don't share bread."

The light flickered. Wavered. And slowly, slowly, like a tide remembering the moon, it sank—down her arms, past her elbows, back to the coil at her wrist, where it dimmed to its old patient pulse.

Lior drank the water. Her hands steadied. When she finally spoke, her voice was small and scraped raw.

"How do I get him out?"

Iven met her eyes. Around them, the others waited—Sable with her thief's hands gone quiet, Dara with her unfinished list and her finished hesitations—and he understood that whatever he said next would become the road they all walked.

"You don't," he said. "Not here. Not with a knife or a spell or any craft I know. The binding was made beneath the mountain, by the brokers themselves, and only the mountain can unmake it. Which means, Hartwell, that you're going to have to walk into the Undercourt—into the one place in the world that wants you most—and ask it, nicely, to open its own jar."

Silence. Then Lior laughed—a terrible, exhausted, utterly fearless laugh, the laugh of someone whose worst fear had already come true and found her still standing.

"Fine," she said. "Then we'd better move fast. Before whatever's left of me decides it likes being a jar."

Above them, far off toward the palace quarter, a bell began to toll—not the hour, not the alarm, but the slow ceremonial peal that meant a royal proclamation. Iven closed his eyes. He knew that peal. He had arranged it often enough, in another life.

Vessic was speaking.

And whatever the usurper had to announce, Iven would have bet his last secret that it concerned the mountain—and that the road ahead had just grown shorter than any of them wanted.
