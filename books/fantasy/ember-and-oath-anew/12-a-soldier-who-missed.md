## 12. A Soldier Who Missed

The rain had been falling on the road to Corren's Landing for three days, and Dara Ferro had stopped believing it would ever stop.

It came down in gray sheets off the hills, filling the wheel ruts until they ran like small rivers, soaking through her oilskin at the shoulders where the wax had cracked. It turned the world the color of old pewter — the road, the hedgerows, the faces of her own riders when she called the halt at dusk. Only the hunt-banners stayed bright: Vessic's black stag on white, stitched with silver thread that never dulled, never rotted, never showed a single day of the four years since the usurper had raised it over the capital.

Dara had helped design that banner. She tried not to remember that.

"Captain." Sergeant Vellum, hunched under the eave of a burned-out tollhouse, holding out a strip of dried mutton as if it were an apology. "Scouts say the gifted girl's ahead of us. Half a day, maybe less. Traveling with a woman and a man — thief and mage by their look."

"You're certain it's her?"

"The ember-mark. A carter saw it when she bought bread in Millbrook. Girl didn't even try to cover it." He shook his head, and water flew from the brim of his helm. "Arrogant, or stupid."

Or desperate, Dara thought, and did not say. She took the mutton because refusing would raise questions, and chewed it without tasting anything, which had been the way of all food for two years now.

*Half a day.* She looked out past the tollhouse eave into the rain, toward the south, where somewhere in that gray wash a nineteen-year-old girl was walking toward a mountain full of soldiers with a mark on her wrist that might as well have been a noose drawn in fire.

The list was in her breast pocket. It was always in her breast pocket. She could feel its edges through the wet wool, soft now from handling, the ink long since blurred to ghosts where her thumb worried it.

Twenty-three names. Twenty-three lines written in her own careful hand, each one a person she had been ordered to find, register, or burn. Fourteen crossed out. The ones who had run far enough, hidden deep enough, died lucky enough. Nine still open.

She had not added a name to that list in fourteen months. She had orders for six more. The orders sat in her dispatch case like stones in a wound.

"Captain?" Vellum was still there. "Orders for morning?"

"We ride at first light," Dara said. "Full kit. We'll want the crossbows dry."

He saluted and went back into the rain, and Dara stood alone under the broken eave and listened to the water drumming the dead shingles, and thought about the first name on the list. *Merrin Osta, weaver, Corren's Landing.* A woman who had once sold her mother thread. A woman whose gift, according to the informer's report, was the ability to make dye hold color through forty washings instead of ten.

Forty washings. That was what the crown of King Vessic called a threat to the realm.

Dara went inside, found the driest corner of the tollhouse, and did not sleep.

* * *

They caught up with the trio two hours past dawn, on the long causeway where the road crossed the marshes north of Corren's Landing.

Dara saw them before her scouts reported — she always did, some hunter's instinct she'd been born with and had never learned to be ashamed of until recently. Three figures small against the flat silver marsh: the tall soldier-stride of the mage, Iven Ashgrave, if the descriptions held; a slighter figure moving like water beside him, the thief; and between them, half a step behind, a girl with a pack too big for her shoulders and hair the rain had darkened to the color of wet slate.

Lior Hartwell. Youngest oath-keeper of the Ember Guild. The report said *dangerous*, the way reports said dangerous about everyone with a gift, but Dara had read deeper than the report. She'd pulled the guild's old registration files from the archive at Vessic's Reach, back when pulling files was still something a hunt-captain could do without explanation. The Ember Guild. The valley that forgot. A whole community that had kept its head down for a hundred years, hurting no one, hiding nothing but itself.

And this girl had left it. Left the one place in the world the hunt couldn't follow, walked out into the open country with a mark on her wrist, heading — if Dara's map-reading meant anything — straight for the Undercourt.

Nobody walked toward the Undercourt. Not unless they had a reason worth dying for.

"Riders!" The thief saw them first. Of course she did. "Company of horse, coming up the causeway — Lior, *run*—"

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

The trio broke for the reed beds off the causeway's edge, where horses couldn't follow. Dara's riders spread across the road in the practiced fan of a hunting line, and the order came up the column — not from her, though every rider would believe afterward that it had; that was the trick of command, the lie baked into rank — the order came, and crossbows came up, and Dara Ferro found herself with a clear shot at the back of a running girl thirty yards away.

Thirty yards. Rain-slicked ground. A moving target. Every excuse in the world.

Her bolt took the thief's shoulder-pack strap instead, tearing it clean and spinning Sable half around, and the second volley — her riders', loosed on her signal — fell short and wide, bolts vanishing into the reeds with a sound like hail on a roof.

"*Down!*" the mage shouted, and threw up his hands, and light stuttered across the marsh — a shield, ragged, the work of a man running on fumes, but enough. Enough to blur the air. Enough to make every shot after that a guess.

By the time the haze cleared, the reeds had swallowed them. Dara rode to the causeway's edge and looked down at the churned mud, the cut strap lying in the shallows like a shed skin, and felt nothing at all, which was the most frightening feeling she knew.

"Lost them, Captain," Vellum said, riding up, breathing hard. "Reed beds go on for a mile. They could double back to the Landing or push south. Your call."

Your call. Always her call. That was the arrangement she'd made with herself, wasn't it — that as long as the decisions were hers, she could keep pretending the list was someone else's fault.

"Back to the road," she said. "They'll go south. Toward the Landing. Set pickets on the south gates."

"And you, Captain?"

"I'm going to have a look at those reed beds."

Vellum gave her the look sergeants give captains — the one that says *I see more than I say* — and wheeled his horse away. Dara dismounted at the causeway's edge, told the rider holding her mare to walk her, and stepped down alone into the marsh.

The reeds closed over her head like green water. Rain fell through them in a fine cold mist. Somewhere ahead, faint as breath, she heard voices — three people arguing in low, urgent tones, the argument of people deciding whether to trust each other, which is the oldest argument in the world and the only one that matters.

She should turn back. She should give the signal, bring the company around, run these three to ground properly. That was her oath. That was her pay and her rank and her name.

Instead Dara Ferro stood hip-deep in freezing marsh water, listening to strangers argue about trust, and took the list out of her breast pocket, and unfolded it under the reed-shadow even though the ink had long since gone the color of the rain.

*Merrin Osta.* Still open. Still waiting.

She put it away. She waded back to the road. And when Vellum asked what she'd found, she said, "Cold water and nothing else," and gave the order to march south at a pace no horse could keep and no quarry could outrun — the pace of a hunt that wants very badly to arrive second.

* * *

Corren's Landing smelled of fish and tar and smoke, and on the third day it smelled mostly of smoke.

Dara stood in the market square in her captain's whites, helmet under her arm, watching the pyre being built, and understood that whatever fragile fiction she'd been living in — the fiction of the slow hunt, the merciful delay, the captain who missed by accident — was about to be burned with everything else.

The child's name was Pella Voss. Eight years old. Her gift, per the informant's deposition, was that flowers bloomed out of season when she held them. Her parents had refused to surrender her for registration. Registration, everyone in the square understood, meant the mountain. The mountain meant the Undercourt. And the Undercourt meant a door that opened inward and never again out.

So: the family would burn together. Vessic's new doctrine, posted on every gate in the free cities that autumn. *The gift runs in blood. So does the punishment.*

"They're just people," said a voice beside her, low. Vellum, off-duty, out of uniform, a cup of mulled wine in his hands he wasn't drinking. He was looking at the pyre too. At the mother, who was not screaming, which was worse than screaming. At the father, who was holding the child's hand and saying something to her, over and over, something none of them would ever hear. "Captain. Tell me this is necessary. Tell me you believe it. Because I've been telling myself for two years that you must believe it, and I can't — I can't carry it anymore if you don't."

Dara looked at her sergeant — good Vellum, steady Vellum, who had followed her through four years of hunts and had never once asked her to explain a miss — and felt the list in her breast pocket like a coal.

"Look at the crowd," she said quietly.

He looked. The square was packed — fishwives and ropemakers, dockhands off the morning tide, old men who remembered the free cities before the war. And not one of them was watching the pyre. They were all looking at the ground, at their hands, at the middle distance. Two hundred people practicing the same skill at once: the skill of seeing nothing.

"That's not consent," Dara said. "That's arithmetic. Everyone here is doing the sum — *if I speak, my children burn too* — and getting the answer the crown wants. Don't mistake silence for belief, Vellum. Silence is just fear wearing belief's clothes."

"And ours? What are we wearing?"

She didn't answer. The trumpets sounded. The magistrate read the proclamation in a voice like a man reading a grain inventory. Torches went to the pitch.

Dara had seen burnings before. She had stood honor-guard at eleven of them, motionless, expressionless, the perfect instrument. But this time — because the child was eight, because the mother wasn't screaming, because the thief and the mage and the marked girl were somewhere in this city and she had spent three days failing to find them with a thoroughness that was starting to feel like prayer — this time, at the moment the flames caught, Dara Ferro looked down at her own hands.

They were shaking. Her hands, which had never shaken, not at Marrow Ford, not in the sack of Tessel, not once in four years of doing the crown's dirty arithmetic.

*This is what you are,* said a voice in her that sounded like her father, the honest butcher, the man who had taught her that a blade is not guilty, only the hand. *You are the hand.*

Across the square, at the edge of the crowd, she saw a flicker of movement — a girl turning away from the pyre, face lit orange, and even at fifty yards Dara saw the moment the grief hit her like a wave, saw the air around her shiver, saw a lantern three stalls down flare bright enough to sting eyes and burst its glass in a spray of sparks. Saw the thief grab the girl's arm and haul her into an alley an instant before twenty heads turned.

Their eyes met across the burning square. The girl's eyes — wide, horrified, lit from below by firelight — met Dara's, and Dara watched her understand exactly what a hunt-captain's uniform meant, watched her mouth form a word that might have been *run* or might have been a name, and then the alley swallowed her.

Dara did not raise the alarm. She stood at attention through the rest of it, through the dispersal order and the magistrate's smug benediction, and when Vellum touched her elbow and asked, hoarse, "Captain — the flare, the lantern, there were witnesses saying a marked girl—" she heard herself say:

"A lantern burst. Cheap glass, cheap oil. Happens in every market." She met his eyes. "There's no marked girl in Corren's Landing. Write it so in the log."

He stared at her. Something passed between them in that stare — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but recognition. The look of two people standing in the same burning house agreeing, silently, on which door to leave by.

"Yes, Captain," Vellum said. "No marked girl."

* * *

She deserted at moonrise.

Not dramatically. Desertion, done right, looks like paperwork. Dara sat in the garrison command room and wrote her final report in a steady hand: *Quarry believed to have quit the city southward via river barge. Company strength insufficient to pursue along the water-maze without reinforcement. Requesting authority to hold at Corren's Landing pending instructions.* She signed it, sealed it, addressed it to the regional commander at Vessic's Reach — nine days' hard ride away — and left it on the desk where the duty clerk would find it at dawn.

Nine days. By then she'd be beyond any pursuit that mattered, and the report would buy the company nine days of standing still, and standing still was the kindest order she would ever give anyone.

She took almost nothing. Sword, knife, oilskin, the purse she'd never spent her prize-money from, and the list. God help her, the list. She stood a long time in her quarters with it in her hands, weighing it, wondering if carrying it made her a coward or a keeper. Twenty-three names. Fourteen crossed out. Nine open — and now, tonight, she took out her pen one last time and did the thing she had refused to do for fourteen months.

She wrote a tenth name. Not a quarry's. Her own.

*Dara Ferro, hunt-captain, deserter.* She looked at it a long moment. Then, beneath it, in smaller letters: *gift: none. crime: memory.*

Then she folded it away, banked the stove, and went out the stable door into the rain.

The city was asleep except for the places that never sleep — the docks, the taverns, the alleys where the smuggled and the hunted moved by night. She went the way she'd watched the trio go, past the burned-out lantern stall, down the alley that smelled of brine and rotting cabbage, following not tracks but logic: fugitives need a fence, a fixer, a den. In Corren's Landing there was one den worth the name, under the sign of the Crooked Eel, and every hunted thing in the city eventually flowed toward it like water finding a drain.

She found the alley mouth blocked by a knife. Of course she did. The thief was good.

"Captain Ferro," Sable said, stepping out of the shadow with the blade low and easy, and behind her, in the deeper dark, the gleam of the mage's prepared hands and the pale oval of the girl's face. "Come to finish your miss?"

"I came to offer you a sword," Dara said. She set her captain's insignia on the wet cobbles between them — the black stag, the silver antlers — and stepped back from it. "I've got nowhere else to put it."

Silence, except the rain. The girl — Lior, Hartwell, vessel, whatever she was — came forward a step, studying Dara's face with an intensity that made her skin prickle, the way you feel weather change.

"You missed," Lior said. "On the causeway. Both volleys. You're a hunt-captain; you don't miss."

"No," Dara agreed. "I don't."

"Why?"

And here was the question, the one she'd been walking toward for two years, down every road, past every pyre. She thought of the list against her heart. She thought of Merrin Osta, weaver, whose colors held through forty washings. She thought of a father who said *the blade isn't guilty, only the hand* — and how he'd never imagined his daughter would spend her life trying to prove him wrong by cutting the hand loose from the arm that fed it.

"Because I've finished twenty-three hunts," Dara said, "and every one of them follows me home at night and sits on the end of my bed. And I am done making new ghosts." She looked at each of them in turn — the thief, the mage, the girl with the ember-mark. "I know the roads south. I know the mountain's patrols, its passwords, its blind spots — I trained half the wardens at the gate myself. I know what Vessic's doing at midwinter, because I've read the requisitions nobody outside the high command is supposed to read. You're walking into the Undercourt, all three of you, and you're going to die without me."

"You don't know where we're walking," the mage said sharply.

"I know the ember-mark," Dara said, "and I know what vessels are for, and I know that whatever you're planning, midwinter is a wall coming down on all of it." She let that land. Let them see she'd done her reading. "Take me or don't. But decide fast, because come dawn, every garrison from here to the salt marches gets my description, and I'd rather be useful to you than meat on a bounty board."

The thief laughed — a short, disbelieving sound — and looked at the others. The mage's jaw worked. And Lior Hartwell looked at Dara Ferro for a long, long moment, rain running off her chin, and Dara had the strange, vertiginous sense of being weighed by something older than the girl's years, something patient and ember-warm, as if the soul in her — whoever's it was — were taking Dara's measure too.

"You'll follow us anyway," Lior said at last. "Even if we say no. Won't you."

"Yes," Dara said simply.

"Then you may as well walk where we can see you." The girl turned to the others. "Sable. Iven. This is Dara. She's with us now."

Just like that. No oath, no trial, no blood. Dara picked up her discarded insignia — not to wear it, but because leaving crown silver in a smuggler's alley was how informants got paid — and fell into step behind them as they moved off through the rain, four shadows going south along the dark streets of a city that had burned a child that morning and would burn more before the snows came.

Behind them, the pyre's embers still glowed in the market square like a red eye that would not close. Ahead, past the rooftops, past the hills, the mountain waited under the clouds, sealing its doors for midwinter.

Dara touched the list through her coat, feeling the weight of ten names now instead of nine, and walked on into the dark with the strangest company she'd ever kept — and for the first time in four years, the road ahead felt less like a sentence and more like a choice.
