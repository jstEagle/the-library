## 13. Four Against the Road

The fire was too small for four people and too large for a secret.

That was the trouble with campfires, Lior thought as she crouched at its edge, feeding it twigs one at a time like a miser paying out coins. Light carried. Warmth gathered people to it whether they wanted gathering or not. Somewhere behind her, in the dark beyond the ring of stones, three strangers were pretending they weren't watching her hands.

She could feel their attention the way she'd learned to feel weather coming—pressure first, then a change in the air, then the thing itself arriving whether you liked it or not.

"Your fire's crooked," Sable said from somewhere above her. The memory-thief had claimed a fallen log as her throne and sat on it with her boots off, rubbing her feet, radiating the particular comfort of someone who had decided the danger could wait until morning. "Leans east. Smoke'll drift toward the road."

"The road's half a mile away."

"And Vessic's hounds can smell smoke at two." Sable wiggled her toes toward the flames. "I've watched men die over less than a crooked fire. Well—I've *taken* men dying over less than a crooked fire. Different thing, but the view's the same."

"You could help," Lior said. "Instead of narrating."

"I'm providing expertise." Sable smiled with all the warmth of a coin purse. "Expertise is what you're paying me with, remember? You steal my bread, I take your memories of the road. Fair trade. Everyone in the free cities does business this way now."

Lior didn't answer. She adjusted the fire anyway, nudging the burning end of a branch westward, because Sable was right and being right entitled her to nothing but Lior's grudging compliance.

By the log's other end sat Iven Ashgrave, the disgraced court mage, wrapped in a cloak that had once been fine and was now mostly road. He held his hands out to the flame the way a man holds them when he's forgotten how else to hold them. His fingers were long and ink-stained and trembled slightly—not from cold, Lior thought. From something older than cold.

And Dara Ferro stood watch at the treeline, twenty paces out, her back to the fire, her bow strung and slung across her shoulders like an apology she hadn't finished making. A hunt-captain who had put an arrow into a tree instead of Lior's chest, then walked out of the King's service and into theirs without asking permission. She hadn't spoken more than ten words since noon.

Four of them. Four against the road.

Lior looked around the little circle of light—at the thief, the traitor-mage, the deserter—and felt something twist in her chest that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite hope. It was the feeling of standing on a bridge made of rope, knowing the far bank existed but not trusting the planks between here and there.

*You are the vessel,* Iven had told her, three nights ago, in a smuggler's den that smelled of tallow and old fish. *You carry your brother's soul inside you like a jar carries ash.*

She still didn't know if she believed it. But she knew the ember-mark on her wrist burned hotter every day they traveled south, and she knew the hounds were getting closer, and she knew that whatever she was—a girl, a keeper, a jar—she would not find out alone.

"We should talk about it," she said.

No one asked what *it* was. That was how she knew they'd all been thinking the same thing.

* * *

Dara came back to the fire first, which surprised Lior. She'd expected the soldier to stand her post until dawn, silent as a fencepost. Instead she crossed the dark ground with that rolling hunter's walk and sat down hard on the dirt opposite Lior, knees apart, forearms on thighs, a woman built for waiting suddenly done with it.

"Talk," Dara said.

"Trust," Lior said. "We don't have any. And we're going to need it before the mountain."

Sable snorted. Iven said nothing, which from him was nearly agreement.

"Speak for yourself," Sable said. "I trust plenty. I trust that everyone in this company will sell everyone else the moment the price is right. It's a very reliable kind of trust. It's never once been wrong."

"That's cynicism, not trust," Lior said.

"It's *experience.*" Sable leaned forward, firelight catching the sharp planes of her face. "Girl, I have lived inside other people's heads. Do you understand what that means? I have seen what people do when they think no one is looking. There is no loyalty down there. There's fear wearing loyalty's coat, and the moment the coat gets heavy, they drop it in the mud and run." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and Lior wondered—not for the first time—what Sable had seen in someone's head that taught her that. What she'd sold to learn it.

"You took my memories of the journey," Lior said quietly. "To get us through the gate when the time comes. You're holding days of my life in your head right now. If anyone has reason not to trust, it's me."

"Yes," Sable said simply. "And yet here you are, arguing for it. Which either makes you brave or stupid, and I haven't decided which."

"Both," Dara said.

Everyone looked at her. Dara stared into the fire like it owed her money.

"You're both," she said. "Brave and stupid. I've commanded forty soldiers. I've written the names of—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I've written a lot of names. Brave and stupid describes every one who survived longer than a week."

"And the ones who weren't?" Iven asked softly.

Dara's eyes came up, gray and flat as river stones. "The ones who were careful are dead too. Just later. Just quieter." She turned back to the fire. "Careful doesn't save you. It just means you die with better notes."

A silence settled, thick as wool. Somewhere in the dark, an owl called, and Lior felt the sound pass through her like a hand through water.

"Iven," she said. "You haven't said anything."

The mage's hands had gone still over the flames. When he lifted his face, he looked older than he had in the smuggler's den, older than he had on the road, older maybe than he was. "What would you have me say? That I'm trustworthy?" He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a dry rattle like paper burning. "I taught eleven students at the court. Eleven children of the gifted families, hidden away, promised protection. When Vessic came for them, I signed the transfer orders. All eleven. My signature is on their souls' receipts." He spread his ink-stained fingers. "This is what trust looks like, Lior Hartwell. It looks exactly like mine."

"You regret it," Lior said.

"Regret is a luxury for people whose sins are small." But his voice shook, and Lior saw the truth move under it like a fish under ice. "I came with you because I cannot un-sign those orders. Because the only pen I have left is this one"—he touched his own chest—"and it writes very slowly, and it may never finish."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out and pulled a branch from the woodpile beside her and set it in the fire between them.

"There," she said. "Now you've fed the fire. That's a start."

Iven blinked at her. Something moved across his face—bewilderment, then a fragile, disbelieving almost-smile. "That's a start," he repeated, as if testing whether the words would hold weight.

"It's how the valley does it," Lior said. "Oaths are just words. Feeding the fire together—that's the actual oath. Anyone can say words. Not everyone shows up with wood."

"Hollow Valley wisdom," Sable said, but her voice had lost its edge. She pulled her boots back on. "Fine. Wood. I'll contribute wood. But I want it noted that I'm doing it ironically."

"Noted," Dara said, and for the first time since Lior had met her, the hunt-captain's mouth twitched toward something that might, in better light, from further away, be mistaken for a smile.

It lasted perhaps four heartbeats.

Then Dara went rigid, her head snapping toward the treeline, and everything warm in the world drained out of the night at once.

* * *

Lior smelled it a heartbeat later: musk and wet iron and something underneath, something wrong, like copper left in milk. The hounds.

"Down," Dara breathed. "Fire out. Now."

They killed the flames with earth and cloaks, and the darkness came down on them complete, and in that darkness Lior heard what Dara had heard: the soft, rhythmic press of many paws on forest loam. Not running. Walking. Patient. Hounds that walked were worse than hounds that ran; running hounds wanted to catch you, but walking hounds already knew where you were.

"How many?" Sable whispered.

"Six," Dara said. "Maybe eight. They'll have handlers behind them." She was already moving, stringing her bow by touch, an arrow appearing in her fingers as if conjured. "Listen to me. They're not hunting to kill. Vessic wants the gifted alive—the girl especially. So they'll drive us. Split us from each other and herd whoever runs toward the handlers." Her voice was flat and fast, the voice of a woman reading from a manual she hated. "Standard pattern. We break formation, we die separately. We stay together—"

"We might die together," Sable finished. "Wonderful. I've died with worse company."

"Have you actually died?" Iven whispered.

"Twice. Both times disappointing."

The sounds were closer now. Lior pressed her palm flat against the cold ground and felt it—vibrations, faint, spreading through the soil like ripples through a pond. And beneath the vibrations, something else. Something that made the ember-mark on her wrist flare hot enough to sting through her sleeve.

The hounds weren't animals.

She'd known it intellectually—everyone knew the King's hounds were bred with a thread of gift, scenting magic the way normal dogs scented blood—but knowing it and *feeling* it were different things. Through the ground, through her marked skin, she could feel them tasting the air for her. Six narrow, hungry attentions, fanning out through the trees, all of them bending, slowly, inevitably, toward the place where she knelt.

"They're finding me," she whispered. "I'm—they can smell the mark. I'm the loudest thing in the forest."

"Then we make you louder," Iven said.

She felt him move in the dark, heard cloth whisper, and then his hand closed around her wrist—gently, questioningly, giving her a moment to pull away. She didn't.

"What are you doing?"

"Borrowing your light." His voice had changed—all the brokenness gone out of it, replaced by something crisp and cold and terribly competent. This, she understood suddenly, was what he had been at court, before the fall. This was the mage the king had discarded. "When I say run, you run east. Don't argue. Don't look back. Dara—left flank, the big oak with the split trunk. Sable—"

"I know my job," Sable murmured. "I've been stealing bread from under noses since before you lost your court. Give me thirty seconds and those handlers will be chasing a memory instead of a girl."

"Twenty," Iven said. "Hold still, Lior."

His grip tightened on her wrist, and heat poured into her—not pain, exactly, but pressure, like a second heartbeat waking up under her skin. The ember-mark blazed. She bit down on a cry. In the blackness she saw, briefly, impossibly, the whole forest lit up in her mind's eye: six hounds as smears of hungry red, three human shapes further back carrying lanterns of colder light, and herself—

Herself as a pillar of white fire, blazing through the dark like a signal pyre.

*Oh,* she thought, with strange calm. *So that's what I look like to them.*

"Now," Iven said.

He did something—she felt it as a wrenching, a redirection, her own light seized and thrown—and the pillar of her flung sideways through the mental forest, north and west, away from them, a decoy blaze crashing through the undergrowth that wasn't her at all. The hounds' attention snapped after it like compass needles.

"Run," Iven gasped, and his voice was ragged now, spent. "East. *Go.*"

They went.

* * *

Lior ran the way she had never run in the Hollow Valley, where running was pointless because there was nowhere to run *to*. Branches clawed at her. The ground heaved and dipped. Behind her she heard the hounds turn—heard the change in their voices, from patient silence to full-throated belling, a sound like church bells dropped down a well—and heard them crash off after Iven's phantom.

Thirty seconds of pure flight. Forty. Then a whistle cut the night—Dara, short and sharp—and Lior swerved toward it, crashing into a small clearing where the soldier crouched behind the split-trunked oak, arrow nocked, breath steaming.

"Down," Dara said, and Lior dropped, and the arrow sang past her ear so close she felt the wind of it.

A handler screamed.

Lior twisted around. At the clearing's edge, a man in the King's gray staggered, Dara's arrow buried in his shoulder, a lantern spilling cold witchlight from his nerveless fingers. Behind him the trees were full of movement—hounds wheeling, confused, the false scent dying as Iven's borrowed strength gave out.

"Second wave's coming," Dara said calmly, drawing another arrow. "Get behind me."

"No." The word came out of Lior before she knew she was going to say it. Heat was rising in her chest, in her wrist, in her teeth—the same terrible rush she'd felt outside the valley on her first day, the magic that had rushed back into her like a flood into a dry riverbed, dangerous and loud and *visible*. "They're mine. The hounds. They're hunting *me*."

"Lior—"

"They'll just keep coming. For all of us. As long as I'm the loudest thing in the forest." She stepped past Dara, out from behind the oak, into the open clearing, into the spilled witchlight. Her heart was slamming. Her hands were shaking. And underneath the shaking, something vast and unfamiliar was waking up, something that felt less like power than like *recognition*—like a door in her opening onto a room she'd always owned and never entered.

The lead hound broke the treeline.

It was huge, gaunt, wrong—wolf-shaped but stretched, its hide smoking faintly where the gift in its blood ate at it from inside, its eyes twin coals of the same ember-light that burned on Lior's wrist. It saw her and stopped. Its pack fanned out behind it, six shapes in the witchlight, hackles high.

For a moment, hound and girl regarded each other across the clearing.

And Lior, in the door of that newly opened room, found that she knew something. She didn't know how she knew it. The knowledge was just *there*, the way Tam's name was there, the way the guild's oath was there—lodged in her deeper than learning.

The hounds were made of harvested fire. And fire knew its kin.

"*Sit,*" Lior said.

She didn't shout it. She said it the way Maren said the evening blessing, low and certain, and she pushed the words with the heat behind her sternum, with the ember-mark, with whatever she was—the keeper, the vessel, the jar, the girl, all of it at once.

The lead hound sat.

The others wavered, whined, and sat—one, two, three, four, five—like candles guttering out. Their coal-eyes dimmed to embers, then to something almost like dog. The nearest one tilted its head at her, and in its ruined face Lior saw, horribly and unmistakably, relief.

Behind it, the wounded handler struggled up, screaming for the others, reaching for a horn at his belt—

An arrow took the horn out of his hand. Dara stood with her bow leveled, walking forward, and her face was terrible and calm.

"Here's how this goes," she said to the handler. "You report that the Hartwell girl is heading east toward the coast. You report six hounds lost to a marsh. Or you report nothing ever again. Choose."

The handler chose. Lior heard him crashing away south through the dark, cradling his shoulder, and then the forest was quiet except for breathing—hers, Dara's, and the low, ragged breathing of six impossible dogs.

Sable materialized at the clearing's edge with Iven leaning heavily on her arm, his face gray as ash. "Well," the thief said, surveying the seated hounds. "That's new. Usually when I see dogs sitting like that, someone's got food."

"They're not sitting for food." Lior knelt slowly, and the lead hound watched her come, and did not growl. Up close she could see the harvest-scars on its hide, the places where its own fire had been drawn off and bottled—jars, she thought, jars like the guild kept, jars like the Undercourt was full of. Everything came back to jars. "They're sitting because I told them to. And they obeyed because—" Her throat tightened. "Because part of what's in me is the same thing that's in them. Stolen fire knows stolen fire."

She laid her hand on the hound's great scarred skull. It shuddered under her palm, and leaned into her, the way a tired animal leans, the way, she thought suddenly, a brother might lean.

Behind her, none of them said anything. But she heard Dara unstring her bow. Heard Sable sit down right there on the cold ground, graceless, spent. Heard Iven let out a long breath that sounded like the end of a prayer he'd stopped believing in years ago.

They stayed that way while the moon crossed a hand's width of sky—four humans and six hounds in a witchlit clearing, enemies a moment ago, something else now, something none of them had a word for.

It was Sable, finally, who broke the silence, and she did it in the only language she trusted.

"I'm hungry," she announced. "Someone stole my bread earlier. Terrible crime. Unsolved."

Lior laughed. It came out wet and startled, half sob, and she didn't bother hiding it. "I'll replace it. Tomorrow. First town."

"First town," Sable agreed. "Put it in writing. Actually don't—I'd just steal the writing."

Dara snorted. Iven chuckled, a rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. And Lior sat among them in the ruins of the night with her hand on a monster's head and thought: *this is what the fire was for. Not the oath. Not the words. This.*

Something like family, beginning in the dark, unspoken, because speaking it might frighten it away.

It was Dara who ended it—Dara, whose head came up with a hunter's suddenness, whose eyes went to the southern sky.

"Light," she said.

Lior followed her gaze. Far to the south, beyond the black shoulders of the hills, the horizon glowed. Not sunrise—it was hours yet, and the glow was the wrong color, orange shot through with a sickly violet flicker. Fire, but fire fed on something stranger than wood.

"Corren's Landing," Iven whispered. "Or what's left of it. That's a purge-burning. I've seen that color before." His voice went hollow. "Vessic burns the gifted's families first. To draw out anyone hiding. He calls it 'airing the nests.'"

Lior rose. The hound beneath her hand whimpered at the loss of contact. She looked south at the distant, obscene glow, and felt the ember-mark pulse against her wrist like a second heart, and thought of a child somewhere in that light, and of eleven students whose names Iven had signed away, and of a brother walking around inside her bones.

"We're going there," she said.

Sable stared at her. "Girl, that is the single worst idea—"

"We're going there," Lior said again, and found that her voice did not shake. "Not to save anyone—we may be too late, I know that. We're going because I'm done running past fires. Whatever I am—keeper, vessel, jar—I am done letting it be a reason to hide." She looked around the circle of faces, fire-lit by nothing now but the distant burning, and held out her empty hand. "Feed the fire. All of you. Or tell me now, and walk east, and I'll never think less of you."

Nobody walked east.

Dara took her hand first. Then Sable, muttering about irony. Then Iven, his ink-stained fingers closing around hers with the careful desperation of a man gripping a rope over a long drop.

Six hounds rose silently around them, coal-eyed, and turned south with the four of them, toward the burning.

Toward Corren's Landing, and whatever was waiting in the light.
