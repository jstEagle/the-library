## 14. The Bridge of Small Mercies

The river found us before we saw it.

We heard it first as a hush in the trees, a sound like a thousand people whispering behind their hands, and then the land simply opened—dropped away into a gorge so deep the far rim looked painted on the sky. The water at the bottom was the color of old pewter, moving with the slow, muscular patience of something that had been erasing mountains since before there were names for them.

"Greymere," Iven said, and his voice did something strange in the middle of the word, like a man stepping on ground he'd thought solid and finding it hollow.

"You know it," Dara said. Not a question. She'd been watching him more closely since the ambush, cataloguing him the way she catalogued everything—threats first, wounds second.

"I know *of* it." Iven adjusted the strap of his pack and would not look down into the gorge. "There's one crossing within forty miles. The Bridge of Small Mercies."

"That's a grim name," Lior said.

"It's an honest name," I said. "You'll see."

She turned to me with that look she'd been wearing more and more lately—the one that said she could tell when I knew things I wasn't saying. It was inconvenient, being known by someone you were actively lying to about how much of yourself you had left to give. "Sable. What does it cost?"

"Everything costs," I said. "The question is always what, and who pays."

* * *

We reached the bridgehead at dusk, and I watched each of them understand it in their own order.

Dara understood it as a soldier: a chokepoint, indefensible, a place where four travelers became four targets lined up on a span barely wide enough for two abreast. Her hand went to her sword and stayed there.

Iven understood it as a scholar: the bridge was not built but *grown*, black stone rising out of the gorge in a single unbroken arch, veined with channels that glowed faintly green, like light trapped under ice. Old work. Pre-Imperial. The kind of magic that had been outlawed twice over by two different crowns.

Lior understood it as a keeper. She knelt at the edge of the bridgehead and touched the stone, and I saw her flinch—not from cold, though the stone drank warmth like a wound drinks blood—but from recognition. "It's like the jars," she murmured. "Not the same. But the same *family*."

And me? I understood it as a thief understands all tolls: this one would be paid in the only coin I had left, and I had already counted that coin too many times.

The wight came up out of the gorge at full dark.

I want to say I saw it coming, because thieves are supposed to see everything coming. But the truth is that one moment the bridge stood empty against the last grey light, and the next there was a figure standing at its center, tall and thin as a heron, draped in something that might have been robes or might have been river-fog given patience. Its face was smooth as river stone, featureless except where eyes should have been—and there, two points of pale light, like lanterns seen through deep water.

It did not walk toward us. Distance simply stopped mattering, and then it was standing at the near end of the bridge, and the temperature dropped enough that our breath bloomed white.

"*Toll,*" it said. The voice came from everywhere—the water, the stone, the space behind my teeth. "*What do you carry?*"

"Four travelers seeking passage south," Dara said, stepping forward, captain's voice, the one that expected obedience. "Name your price in gold or goods."

The lantern-eyes turned to her slowly, without haste, the way a glacier turns. "*Gold is dead weight. Goods are dead weight. The Greymere takes only what lives.*"

"Then we find another crossing," Dara said.

"There is no other crossing." Iven's voice was flat. "I told you. Forty miles either way, and both ways are Vessic's roads now. This is the door, or there is no door."

"*Three have crossed this season,*" the wight said, and its head tilted, birdlike, considering. "*One paid a song she learned at her mother's knee. One paid the name he was born with. One paid nothing, because he carried nothing left worth taking.*" A pause, and those pale lights swung to Iven, and lingered, and I saw the mage go grey around the mouth. "*He wept on the far side. They usually do.*"

"What do you take?" Lior asked. She hadn't moved from where she knelt, and when she rose, she rose slowly, the way you rise in a temple. "Exactly. What is it you take?"

"*Memory,*" the wight said. "*A moment held. Given freely, or it sours the crossing and the bridge drops its passengers. That is the whole of the law.*"

I felt every eye come to me. Of course they did. I was the memory-thief; this was memory-toll; clearly this was my department, my jurisdiction, my problem to solve with some clever trick of my trade.

Here is what none of them knew, what Sable-with-the-smile had spent three weeks on the road making sure none of them suspected: I didn't have a warehouse of stolen moments to spend. I had a vault with almost nothing in it, because I had been paying tolls of one kind or another my whole life, and the vault was nearly empty, and what remained in it I had sworn—sworn on the only thing I had left to swear on—I would never touch again.

"Sable," Lior said quietly. "What's in your trade?"

"Nothing you need to worry about."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give for free."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. Dara wanted to fight it—a wight, a creature made of gorge-fog and older-than-empires law, and she wanted to put steel in it, because Dara Ferro's answer to everything she couldn't protect was to hit it until it stopped hurting someone. Iven wanted to negotiate, which meant lie, which meant the bridge would drop us into the Greymere somewhere past the second pillar, and I told him so, and he went quiet in the way of a man remembering exactly how much he didn't know.

And Lior stood apart, looking at the bridge, looking at the wight, looking at me.

"I'll pay," she said finally. "Take one of mine."

"No," I said, faster than I meant to.

"You don't even know what I was going to offer."

"I don't need to. You're not paying." I heard my own voice come out wrong—too sharp, too bare—and I smoothed it back down and gave them the smile, the market smile, the one that cost nothing. "Professional courtesy. Memory's my trade. If anyone negotiates with a toll-collector, it's me. Besides—" I jerked my chin at the wight. "Look at it. It's not interested in her. It's been looking at me since it surfaced."

That much was true. The lantern-eyes had barely left me. Wights could smell a mind that had been opened before, a vault with worn hinges. Mine probably smelled like a house with all its doors broken.

"*The thief,*" the wight said, agreeable as death. "*Yes. Come. Let us see what you carry.*"

I walked forward. Behind me, Lior said my name once—not sharp, just my name, the way you say a word you're afraid will break—and I didn't turn around, because if I turned around I would see her face, and I had already decided, and seeing her face would make deciding again too tempting.

The wight extended a hand. Under the fog-drapes its fingers were long and grey and ended in no nails at all, just smooth tapering points, like reeds.

"The law is freely given," it said, softer now, almost gentle, the way butchers learn to be gentle. "Show me your moments. I will choose."

So I opened the vault.

I won't describe what it's like, showing a stranger the inside of your remembered life. Imagine laying out everything you own on a table in a dark room while a stranger walks the rows with a lamp. Imagine knowing that whatever they point at, you lose—not dimmed, not faded, *gone*, a hole shaped like itself forever after.

Most of what I had left was road. Three weeks of these four: Lior's terrible singing on the night march, Dara teaching Iven to throw a knife and Iven hitting a tree instead of the target and Dara laughing—actually laughing, I hadn't known she could—Tam's compass glowing in Lior's cupped hands like a caught firefly. Small change. Moments I'd been hoarding without admitting I was hoarding them, because I had never in my life had anything I wasn't willing to sell, and here was a pocketful of things I suddenly, appallingly, was.

The wight drifted through them. Past the road-moments. Past the ugly ones I kept for armor—the first face I'd stolen from, the client who'd paid me in a beating, the years of sold childhood I'd already narrated to them once around a fire and would not relive for a monster's convenience.

It stopped at the far end of the vault. At the oldest thing in it.

My mother.

Understand: I had already sold most of her. That's what I'd done, before any of this, in the years when selling was all I knew how to do—sold her laugh in pieces, sold her hands kneading bread, sold the sound of her calling me home at dusk. Thieves are told never to steal from themselves, and I had robbed my own childhood down to the studs. What remained was one moment. One. I had kept it through famine and flight and three separate occasions when I'd been hungry enough or frightened enough to consider it, and I had kept it the way you keep a coal alive under ash: the morning she braided my hair by the window, and the light came through the glass all gold and dust, and she said, *there. Now you're beautiful,* and kissed the top of my head, and I was six years old and the world was a kitchen and a window and her hands, and nothing had ever hurt yet.

The wight's fingers hovered over it.

"*This one,*" it said.

"No," I said.

"*You may refuse. The bridge remains closed.*"

"I know the law." My voice sounded very far away. "I'm telling you no. Choose another."

"*There is no other that will bear four across. The others are pebbles. This is a stone.*"

"Then the bridge stays closed."

"Sable." Lior's hand on my shoulder. When had she come up beside me? "Whatever it is—you don't have to. We'll find another way. We'll walk the forty miles. We'll walk *four hundred* miles."

"And Vessic's patrols will pick us off the road like flies off meat, and midwinter comes anyway, and the mountain seals anyway, and Tam stays in a jar anyway." I didn't look at her. I looked at the wight. "No other way. There's never another way. There's just the price and who pays it, and I've decided a long time ago that it's always me, because I'm the one who's practiced."

"Sable—"

"It's one memory." The lie came out smooth as river stone. "One stupid morning. I barely remember it anyway."

The wight waited. It was very good at waiting. Things that live in gorges usually are.

I took the moment out of the vault myself, because if it took it, it would take it roughly, and this at least I could do gently. I held it the way you hold a bird—loose enough not to crush, tight enough not to lose—and for one last second I let it play. Gold light. Dust turning in the air. Her hands in my hair, quick and sure. *There. Now you're beautiful.*

I had forgotten, until that second, that she'd hummed while she worked. Some little tuneless thing. I had forgotten the humming.

I gave it to the wight, and the wight took it, and the hole it left was very small and very clean and went all the way through me.

* * *

The crossing took an hour. None of us spoke.

The bridge sang under our feet—or hummed, rather, a low note through the stone, the toll settling into the structure, the memory becoming masonry. I tried not to think about that. Somewhere in this bridge now was a kitchen and a window and a woman humming, and travelers a hundred years from now would walk over her without knowing, and I supposed that was a kind of permanence, and I supposed I hated it.

On the far side, the wight stood aside and inclined its smooth head.

"*Passage is paid. Passage is honored.*" Then, as I passed it—close enough to smell the river on it, cold stone and wet iron—it added, in that everywhere-voice, pitched low, almost private: "*You gave the truest thing. The bridge remembers givers. Walk in peace, thief.*"

I walked. I did not run, though every part of me wanted to run, wanted to put the gorge and the bridge and the whole grey singing country between me and what I'd done.

They caught up to me at the tree line, where the southern road began, and the others hung back—Dara with her jaw set, furious on my behalf and knowing it was useless; Iven with his eyes carefully anywhere else, a man who recognized grief and respected its borders.

Lior fell into step beside me and matched my pace, which was fast, and then faster.

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're about to."

She was quiet for maybe fifty paces. The road unwound ahead of us through dark pines, and somewhere behind us the Greymere whispered its thousand whispers, and I kept my eyes on the road because the road couldn't look at me with pity.

"Liar," she said at last, softly. "You remember it fine. You remember it right now. I watched your face."

"Congratulations. You watched a face. Want a medal?"

"You told me once you'd sold everything of her. Down to the laugh. And you still had one thing left, and you kept it through everything, and tonight you gave it away so we could cross a bridge." Her voice cracked in the middle, just slightly, and she pushed through it. "Don't you dare call it one stupid morning."

"It's done, Lior."

"I know it's done." She stopped walking. So I had to stop too, or leave her standing alone on a dark road, and she knew I wouldn't. She stepped around in front of me, and in the thin moonlight her face was fierce and wet-eyed and absolutely certain. "So hear me. I can't give it back. Iven can't. Nobody can. But I can promise you this, and I don't break promises—ask Maren, ask the whole Ember Guild, they'll tell you what I'm like about promises."

"Lior—"

"I promise I will never ask you to trade. Not for passage, not for safety, not for me, not for Tam, not for anything. Never. If the choice ever comes down to your memories or our lives, I choose our deaths, and I'll say so out loud, and I'll mean it." She held out her hand, palm up, the way the guild swears oaths—she'd shown us the gesture once, ember-palm, an oath offered skin to skin. "Swear it back or don't. But I'm swearing it either way."

I looked at her hand. Small for a girl who'd crossed a war-torn country on foot. Calloused from jar-work and road-work. Steady.

Everything in me that was a thief—the whole architecture of me, the deflection and the smile and the price-always-me—that whole self wanted to laugh it off, wave the oath away, keep the ledger balanced, keep everyone at arm's length where arm's length was safe.

But the vault was empty, and the ash was still settling, and I was so tired.

I put my hand in hers.

"Never ask," I said. My voice came out rough as gravel. "And I swear the same. No more selling. Not of me. Not of any of you. Whatever's left in here—" I tapped my temple with my free hand, "—stays."

"Whatever's left," she agreed, "stays."

We stood there a moment, hands clasped over the ember-mark on her wrist, two liars swearing honesty to each other on a dark road, and it should have meant nothing. Oaths are cheap. I've bought and sold a hundred of them.

It didn't feel cheap. It felt like the first entry in a new ledger, one I hadn't kept before, one where the columns weren't *bought* and *sold*.

Dara cleared her throat behind us. "If you two are finished bonding, we've got patrol schedules to beat and I'd like to be behind walls before dawn."

"She swore an oath," Lior announced, to the whole company, glowing like the ember she was named for.

"They do that," Iven murmured.

We walked south. And I noticed—maybe they all noticed, maybe only I did, having just lost the ability to notice certain other things—that nobody asked me what I'd given. Not Dara, who wanted to know everything. Not Iven, who needed to know everything. Not Lior, who had every right.

They just made room for the hole in me and walked on either side of it.

* * *

We made camp in the shell of a waystation, roof half-burned, walls standing, the kind of ruin this country grew like weeds now. Dara took first watch. Iven fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, which would have been funny any other night.

Lior sat across the fire from me, feeding it sticks one at a time, and I could feel her thinking. She'd been doing that all evening—glancing over, opening her mouth, closing it. Finally she said, "Tomorrow. Around the fire tomorrow night, I want us each to say one true thing. Anything. Something real. Iven first, he owes us a few." A crooked, tired smile. "Will you come?"

"A game of confessions. You realize you're traveling with a professional liar, a deserter, and a disgraced mage. Your odds are terrible."

"I know." She fed the fire another stick. "I'm asking anyway."

And because the vault was empty, and because the wight had taken the truest thing and left the truth behind like change, I heard myself say: "All right. One true thing. Tomorrow."

She smiled and lay back against her pack, and the firelight moved on her face, and I sat up a long time watching the coals, guarding a hole shaped like a kitchen, waiting for morning.
