## 15. Letters We Never Sent

The ruins had been a chapel once, or a granary, or a watchtower—Dara couldn't tell anymore, and the road between Corren's Landing and Vell was littered with buildings that had stopped being anything at all. War did that. It took a thing with a name and a purpose and left a shape that birds nested in.

She chose it anyway because the walls still stood on three sides, and because the roof had fallen inward in such a way that their fire would be visible only from directly above, and Vessic's hounds did not fly. Yet.

"Perimeter's clear," she said, dropping her pack against a wall that smelled of old smoke and older stone. "Two hundred paces in every direction. Nothing moving but crows."

"Crows count as nothing?" Sable was already inside, already claiming the driest corner with the instinct of someone who had slept rough more nights than she'd ever admit to. "Crows are informants with feathers."

"Then we'll bribe them," Iven said. He came in last, as he always did, moving like a man perpetually apologizing for taking up space—a strange habit in someone who had once stood at a king's right hand. Dara had seen court mages before, in the capital, in their silver-trimmed robes with their chins up and their eyes elsewhere. Iven wore a beggar's coat and kept his gaze on the ground, and somehow he was the only one of them who looked like what he was: dangerous, and sorry about it.

Lior came in last of all, which meant Iven came second-to-last, which meant Dara had been walking point with the girl at her shoulder all day and hadn't noticed until now how Lior watched the horizon. Not fearfully. Longingly? No—that wasn't right either. The way you'd watch a door you'd locked yourself behind, wondering if you remembered why.

Dara knew that look. She wore it most mornings.

They built the fire small, the way Dara insisted—dry wood only, no green branches spitting sparks, flame no higher than a cupped hand. Old habits. In the hunt they'd taught recruits that a careless fire was how patrols died in their sleep, and then they'd taught them to make careless fires anyway when they wanted to draw desperate people out of hiding. She had lit fires like bait twice. She could name the villages.

She didn't say this. Not yet. But the thought sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed, and it had been sitting there since the bridge, since Sable had handed over a memory of her own mother to a ferry-wight without blinking, without flinching, and Dara had lain awake after and understood something uncomfortable: that she was traveling with three people who had paid prices she couldn't imagine, and that she herself had never paid anything except attention.

That was a lie, of course. She'd paid desertion. There was a price on her head now too, inked in some clerk's ledger: *Ferro, Dara, captain, third company, absent without leave, property of the crown to be reclaimed.* Reclaimed. Like she was a jar. Like any of them were jars.

But there was another debt, an older one, and it had been whispering to her for days.

* * *

It was Lior who broke the silence first, the way she always did—not with words but with an act. She pulled the last of the dried plums from her pack and divided them into four portions without being asked, and hers was visibly the smallest, and she arranged the others so that no one could tell whose was whose, and Dara pretended not to notice and took one at random and made sure it wasn't the largest.

"You always do that," Sable said, watching her.

"Do what?"

"Feed everyone before yourself. Like you're paying a toll."

Lior turned the fruit over in her fingers. Firelight moved across her face, and for a moment—the way it sometimes did, more often lately—her features seemed to flicker between two faces, one younger, one stranger, though Dara could never catch the change happening. "Habit," she said. "From the valley. You eat last so the jars get tended first." A pause. "No. That's not—I don't know where that came from. I don't remember anyone teaching me that."

Iven looked up sharply. Dara saw him look, saw him decide not to say whatever he'd been about to say, and filed it away. The mage carried a whole library of unsaid things.

The wind moved through the broken roof, sifting ash off the rafters, and it fell into the fire and made the flames gutter green for a heartbeat. Somewhere out in the dark, a crow called—or something wearing a crow's voice.

"We should sleep in shifts," Dara said.

"No," Sable said.

Dara turned. The thief was sitting cross-legged, plum untouched in her palm, looking into the fire with an expression Dara had never seen on her: unguarded. On Sable, even sleeping, there was a guard. "We should talk," Sable said. "Properly. Because tomorrow we hit the salt road, and the day after we're in territory where they check teeth and hands and eyes for the gift, and one of us—" she tipped her chin at Lior, whose magic sat under her skin like a banked coal, visible to anyone trained to look, "—glows when she's frightened. And none of us trust each other yet. Really trust. We've fought together twice and shared bread for a week and I could not tell you a single true thing about anyone at this fire, including myself."

"That's not true," Lior said quietly. "You told us your name."

"Sable is a work name. Like a knife has a handle." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "So. One true thing each. Tonight. Around the fire, like children, like soldiers before a battle, like—like people writing letters they never intend to send. You go first if you want, Hartwell. Or last. Dealer's choice."

"Why?" Dara asked. Not hostile—genuinely asking. She found she genuinely wanted to know.

Sable was quiet long enough that the fire popped. "Because I've spent my whole life taking things from people," she said finally, "and I have learned that the rarest thing in the world isn't gold or grimoires or royal signets. It's a truth given free. Nobody guards those properly. They just... hand them over, and then they're yours, and they can't take them back." Her thumb moved over the plum's wrinkled skin. "And because if we die on the salt road, I'd rather die knowing who I died beside."

Nobody laughed. Nobody said *that's morbid*. They were past that, the four of them. The road had burned the comfort out of them one mile at a time.

"I'll go," Iven said, to Dara's surprise. She'd expected him to deflect, to fill the air with scholarship, to hide in his own eloquence the way he probably had his whole life. Instead he set down his cup and folded his hands, and when he spoke, his voice had changed—it had dropped its apologetic softness and become something level and terrible, a voice used for confessions in stone rooms.

"My name is Iven Ashgrave. I was Vessic's mage for eleven years. And I taught children."

The word *children* landed strangely. Dara shifted.

"There's a school beneath the palace," Iven went on. "Not a school the way you're thinking—no courtyards, no bells. A school the way a mine is a mine. Vessic collected gifted children the way he collects everything: quietly, legally, with paperwork. Their families were told they'd been given scholarships. Honors. And I was their instructor, because I was the best, and because I believed—" he laughed, short and joyless, "—gods, I *believed*, that's the unforgivable part. I believed the court was protecting them. Harnessing their gifts for the realm's good. I taught a girl named Wren Corvane to hold light in her hands without burning herself, and when she was fourteen they took her downstairs to the vessel chambers, and I signed the transfer order, because the paperwork required an instructor's signature, and I told myself it was an honor. I told myself that right up until the night I read the manifest and saw forty names and realized not one of those children had ever gone home."

He looked into the fire. His hands were steady. Dara thought that was the worst part—how steady they were, how much practice it must have taken.

"Wren's mother wrote me letters. Nine of them, over two years. Asking how her daughter was progressing. I answered six. I invented progress reports. I described lessons that never happened, milestones she never reached, because the alternative was telling a woman in a fishing village that her daughter was a jar in a cellar." His jaw worked. "I kept the letters. All nine. I don't know why. Punishment, maybe. Or evidence. When I ran, I took them with me, and I've read them so many times I could recite them, and every time I think I've touched the bottom of what I did, I find there's another floor below."

Silence. The fire cracked. Lior had gone very pale, and her wrist—the one with the ember-mark—had risen unconsciously toward her chest, as if shielding something.

"They're alive," Dara said. It wasn't a question; it was a fact she needed stated aloud, nailed down. "The children. The vessels. Whatever they are—they exist. We've seen the mountain."

"Some of them," Iven said. "Souls keep. Bodies keep, fed and maintained. Whether what wakes up when the jar opens is still the child who went in—" he shook his head slowly. "That's the question I can't answer. That's the question I'm going to the mountain to answer, and if the answer is no, then everything I did was worse than I've let myself believe, and if the answer is yes, then I still have to stand in front of Wren Corvane and tell her what I signed." He picked up his cup again, drank, set it down. "There. One true thing. I find I feel no better. Someone lied to me about that too."

* * *

Sable went next, and she did it the way she did everything—lightly, quickly, like picking a pocket.

"My mother sold me when I was seven."

She said it while looking at the fire, conversational, and Dara felt the words go through her like a blade slipped between ribs.

"Not sold-sold. Rented, at first. That's how they phrase it. A memory-broker in the Tallow District needed young minds—young memories take clean, come out bright, no scar tissue. My mother had debts. I had a good childhood worth harvesting. First they took my sixth birthday. Then the summer at the lake. Then my grandmother's face—she cried when I asked her who she was, and the broker gave my mother extra for that one, fresh grief takes beautifully." Sable's voice stayed level, almost pleasant, a performer's cadence, and Dara realized she'd heard this speech before, in a hundred variations, from witnesses and prisoners and deserters: the recitation of damage by someone who had rehearsed it until it couldn't hurt them anymore. Almost. "By ten I'd learned to watch other people's memories instead of living my own. By twelve I'd learned to take them. By fifteen I'd taken enough that I sold my services back to the brokers—poacher turned gamekeeper—and by nineteen I'd stolen my file out of the Tallow District archive and burned it, and do you know what was left?"

She opened her empty hand.

"Eleven memories. That's what I own. Eleven. Everything else in here—" she tapped her temple, "—is stolen goods or vacant lot. I don't know my mother's real name. I don't know if she's alive. I don't know if she sold me because she was evil or because she was starving, and I will never know, because the memory of deciding is the one thing I'd pay anything to get back and the wights don't stock it." She finally ate the plum, one bite, mechanically. "On the bridge today, the ferry-wight offered me a choice. Passage for passage. It had a memory in its hoard that might be mine—might, mind, wights lie—but the price was the last piece of my mother I carry. Her hands. Just her hands, braiding my hair. I paid it. And I'd pay it again, and that's the truest thing I know about myself, and I hate it."

"Sable." Lior's voice was rough. "I promised you—"

"I know what you promised." For a moment the mask cracked, just a hairline, and Dara saw underneath: not a thief, not a performer. A girl standing in an empty house. "Don't you dare pity me, Hartwell. Pity is just theft with kind eyes. Just—sit here. All of you. Sit here and know me. That's payment enough."

Dara sat. They all sat. The fire burned low and green-edged where the rafter-ash fed it, and outside the ruin the crows had gone quiet, and the dark pressed close against the broken walls like something listening.

* * *

"My turn," Dara said, before the silence could curdle.

She hadn't planned to speak. She had planned, specifically and elaborately, not to speak—to keep her one true thing buried where she'd kept it for three years, under discipline, under duty, under the captain's mask that fit her face so well people had stopped checking whether there was anyone behind it.

But Iven had talked about letters. And Dara had written letters. Hundreds of them. None sent.

"I was hunt-captain for six years," she began. "Third company. You know what the hunt does—everyone in these cities knows, they cross the street when our colors pass. What you don't know is the paperwork." She pulled her pack over, dug past the rations, past the spare bowstrings, and brought out a leather folio, cracked and water-stained, tied with a cord. She held it a moment before untying it. "Every captain keeps a list. Names of the gifted registered in her jurisdiction. It starts as administration. Census work. You tell yourself it's protection—registered means documented means safe, that's what the crown says, that's what I said to the families while I wrote their children down."

She untied the cord. The pages inside were dense with names, columns of them, in her own neat soldier's hand. Some entries were marked with a small circle. Dara didn't explain the circles. She couldn't yet.

"And then the registrations started becoming collections. And the collections started becoming burnings. And the list—" her throat tightened; she pushed through it, the way you push through a river current, one step at a time, "—the list became something else. Every name I wrote after that, I knew where it went. So I started keeping a second list. In my head. Names of people I could warn. People I could misfile. Families I reported as 'moved, address unknown' when they hadn't moved at all, because I'd been the one to move them, at night, in a grain cart, with my own coin paying the carter."

"How many?" Lior asked softly.

"Forty-one saved that I know of. Maybe more; word travels ahead of itself sometimes." Dara turned the folio over in her hands. "And eleven lost. Eleven names I wrote down clean and proper, followed procedure, and eleven people went into the wagons, and one of them was nine years old and she waved at me from the cart because she trusted me, because I'd sat in her mother's kitchen and drunk her tea and told her registration was safety." She looked up, and made herself meet their eyes, all three, one by one. "I missed my shot at you on the Veldt road, Lior. Everyone thinks it was wind. It wasn't wind. I'd already decided. But here's the true thing, the thing I've never said out loud: I didn't desert because of you. You were just the excuse. I deserted because I opened this folio three years ago and started writing letters to every family on the lost list—apologies, explanations, the truth—and I wrote two hundred and six of them and sent none, because sending them would have meant burning my uniform and running, and I wasn't brave enough, so I kept writing letters I'd never send and calling it conscience." She retied the cord. Her hands weren't steady now. Good. Let them see that. "This folio is going to Vell. There are archivists there, Maren's friends, people who keep the true rolls. These names deserve to be somewhere that isn't my guilt."

Lior reached across the fire and put her hand flat on the folio, over Dara's knuckles. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Dara had spent six years reading people for a living, and everything false in the world was easier to spot than that girl's plain, unguarded warmth.

* * *

Which left Lior.

She took her time. She fed the fire a stick, watched it catch, watched the light climb the ruined walls and find the places where old frescoes still clung—a saint, maybe, or a harvest queen, faded to a ghost of blue and gold.

"When Maren told me about Tam," she said at last, "everyone was very careful with me. The guild, and then you three, in your different ways. Careful like I'm made of glass." She smiled a little. "I'm not made of glass. I'm made of something worse. I'm made of gaps."

She drew her knees up, wrapped her arms around them.

"Here's what nobody understands about losing a brother you don't remember. Grief needs a shape. You grieve a face, a voice, a joke, a smell—someone's coat on a hook, someone's boots by the door. I have none of it. Tam is a hole in the middle of my life with my own name written around it, and I can't even mourn him properly because mourning him feels like pretending, like performing a feeling for a person who was never really there." Her voice caught; she steadied it. "Except he *was* there. Iven says the mark says the mirror says—he was there for years, and then he was taken out of my life so cleanly that even the valley forgot him, and I'm left loving someone I have zero evidence ever existed. Do you know what that does to a person? It makes her doubt everything. Every memory I have left, I hold up to the light and ask: are you real? Are you mine? If they could take a brother, what else did they take? Was my childhood harvested like Sable's? Did I feed everyone before myself because someone taught me to, and then took the teacher away?"

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Above them, through the broken roof, the stars were hard and cold and numberless.

"So that's my true thing," Lior finished. "I'm not afraid of dying on this road. I'm afraid of arriving at the mountain and freeing my brother and discovering that I've forgotten him *again*—that getting him back costs me the last of whatever connected us, and I'll stand next to a stranger wearing my brother's face and feel nothing, and he'll look at me and see a girl who doesn't know him, and that will be the second time they take him away." She laughed, wet and quiet. "Letters we never send. I've got one too. It's addressed to Tam. It says: *I'm coming. Wait for me. And if I forget you, wait longer.*"

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then Sable leaned over and bumped her shoulder against Lior's, deliberately, clumsily, the way you'd touch someone you didn't know how to comfort. Iven murmured something in the old formal tongue—a blessing, Dara guessed, or half of one; the rest seemed to have been cut out of him along with everything else.

And Dara, who had spent six years believing that love was a liability the hunt taught you to amputate, heard herself say:

"If you forget him, we won't. Any of us. That's what we're for now. Four sets of memory where one used to be."

Lior looked at her across the embers, and something passed between them that Dara had no military vocabulary for.

* * *

They slept in shifts after all—Sable got her truths, but Dara still got her watch rotation—and it was near dawn, during the coldest hour, with the others breathing slow under their blankets and the east showing a thin gray line above the ruined walls, that Dara heard the sound.

Hooves. Many. Moving fast on the salt road, southbound, with the particular rhythm of a courier relay—fresh horses, urgent riders, no attempt at stealth.

She rose, silent, and put her eye to a gap in the stonework.

Torches. A dozen riders in crown colors, and behind them a herald's banner, and the lead rider carrying a horn with a silver mouthpiece—the kind used for proclamations, the kind that got read aloud in every market square from here to the coast.

The column slowed at the crossroads below the ruin. Dara heard the horn sound once, clear and awful in the frozen air, and then the herald's voice, pitched to carry:

*"By decree of King Vessic, sole and rightful sovereign: the Undercourt shall be sealed at midwinter. All souls within its vaults are declared property of the crown. Any soul-freed, any vessel-born, any who aid them—death. Let every ear hear: the mountain closes, and what is the king's stays the king's, forever."*

Midwinter. Dara counted days without meaning to, the way she'd once counted arrows. Nineteen. Nineteen days to cross the salt flats and be sold through a gate that would soon shut for good.

Behind her, she heard Lior sit up in the dark. She didn't have to turn to know the girl had heard it too—that they'd all heard it, that the countdown had just begun ticking inside all four of them at once.

"Nineteen days," Dara said quietly, to the ruins, to the road, to the gray light coming up. "Then we run."
