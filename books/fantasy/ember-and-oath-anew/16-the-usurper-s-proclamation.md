## 16. The Usurper's Proclamation

The news came the way winter comes—not all at once, but as a cold that seeps under doors and into bones until you cannot remember what warmth felt like.

Iven Ashgrave felt it first in his teeth. He had been awake since the small hours, sitting in the smuggler's den beneath the tannery row of Corren's Landing, watching the girl sleep. Not watching in any way that would shame him—watching the way a man watches a fuse he has lit himself. The ember-mark on her wrist glowed faintly even in sleep, a coal beneath skin, pulsing with a rhythm too slow for a heartbeat and too steady for chance. He had seen that rhythm before, once, twenty years ago, in a ledger written in his own hand.

*Vessel,* the old texts called such marks. *Living jar.* And jars, he knew better than anyone alive, were made to be opened by their owners.

He was still turning that thought over when the bells began.

Not the harbor bells, which rang in threes for tide-change. Not the temple bells, which rang at dawn in a lazy, scattered peal. These were the proclamation bells—the long bronze throats of the Crown's criers, installed in every free city after the war, rung only when the king had words the whole world was required to hear. Iven had heard them twice in his life. Once when Vessic took the throne. Once when the Gift Hunt was declared.

They rang now in a pattern he did not know, which meant the words were new, which meant they would be worse.

* * *

By full light, the streets of Corren's Landing had gone strange. It was not panic—that came later, and slower, the way rot comes to fruit. It was the stillness of people listening. Shopkeepers stood in their doorways with their hands idle on their counters. A fishwife set down her basket in the middle of the street and forgot it. Even the gulls seemed quieter, wheeling without crying, as if the sky itself had bent an ear.

Iven moved through the crowd with his hood up and his court-trained walk folded away into something plainer. Twenty years of hiding taught a man how to be furniture. He found Sable already at the fountain square, leaning against the dry basin's rim with the boneless ease of someone who had decided in advance exactly where to stand, and Dara beside her, arms crossed, wearing the particular stillness of a soldier hearing orders she had not chosen.

"Where's the girl?" Iven asked.

"Asleep," Sable said. "Or pretending. She cried half the night, then stopped all at once, which is worse." Her eyes flicked up to him, quick and gray and unreadable. "You know what this is?"

"I'll know in a moment."

The crier mounted the scaffold at the square's center—a boy, no older than sixteen, in the black-and-gold of the crown, with a scroll he unrolled with hands that shook only slightly. Behind him stood two hunt-officers in their ash-gray cloaks, and behind *them*, something that made the crowd's breath catch: a cage on wheels, tall as a house, and inside it, dim behind iron bars, a shape that might once have been a person. It moved wrong. It moved like something remembering, slowly, how limbs worked.

"That's a hollowed one," Dara said quietly. "They paraded those through the camps sometimes. To show what happens to the gifted who resist registration." Her jaw tightened. "It doesn't happen from resisting. It happens because they take the soul out first. The body just—" She didn't finish.

The crier lifted the scroll and read.

"Hear the word of Vessic, third of that name, rightful King of the Nine Cities and the Mountain Between them, Keeper of the Flame, Lord of Souls." The titles went on, each one a small stone dropped into the crowd's silence. "Let it be known throughout his dominions and beyond them: that the Undercourt, that ancient offense against the crown's unity, shall be sealed at midwinter."

Iven closed his eyes.

"From the hour of midwinter's bell, no soul within the mountain's courts may pass out, and none shall pass in. All souls held there—every jar, every vessel, every bound flame—are declared property of the crown, to be gathered to His Majesty's keeping as the law provides. Any who aid the Undercourt, who trade with it, who shelter its agents, or who seek entry to it, are guilty of treason against the King's own flesh, and shall burn." A pause, the sound of paper. "Registered gifted persons are commanded to present themselves for labor levy at the mountain gate by the feast of Saint Ellowen. Unregistered persons discovered after midwinter will be assumed traitors. So says the King. So let it be done."

The scroll rolled shut with a snap like a small bone breaking.

For three heartbeats, nobody in the square moved. Then the crowd exhaled all at once, a thousand small fears becoming one large murmur, and the ordinary machinery of the city lurched back into motion—but changed, the way a room is changed after someone has died in it. Iven watched a woman pull her child close and cover the child's left hand, where a birthmark sat, with her own palm. He watched a young man in a dyer's apron look at the cage, then at the sky, then turn and walk very quickly and very casually toward the harbor district, and understood that the dyer would not be in Corren's Landing by nightfall. He watched Dara's hand drift toward a sword hilt she no longer carried.

"Well," said Sable lightly, to no one, "the walls just got closer."

"They were always going to close," Iven said. "We just learned the date."

* * *

Back in the den, with the door barred and the tannery stink seeping through the floorboards to mask anything stranger, Iven laid it out for them the way he had once laid out battle-magic theory for students who hung on his every word. That memory arrived unbidden and bitter, and he pushed it down where it lived.

"Midwinter is forty-one days off," he said. He drew a line in the dust on the floorboards. "The mountain gate closes at the bell. After that, everything inside belongs to Vessic—and I mean *everything*. You've seen what the jars hold. Now imagine a king who can drink them."

"Drink them?" Lior sat up straighter. She looked like she hadn't slept, and like sleep had abandoned her rather than the reverse. There was a new quality to her since the burning in the market square—since her magic had flared wild and gold in front of two hundred witnesses and Sable had dragged her clear by the collar. She held herself carefully now, the way people hold themselves when they've learned their own hands are dangerous. "Souls can be *drunk*?"

"The old kings did it. That's what the Undercourt was built for, originally—not a prison. A larder." Iven kept his voice flat, clinical, because the alternative was letting it shake. "A soul is power that remembers being a person. Burned properly, channeled properly, one jar could level a city wall. A hundred jars could stop an army. A thousand—" He met her eyes. "There are more than four thousand souls under that mountain, Hartwell. Vessic isn't sealing it to keep people out. He's sealing it so nothing escapes before he's finished harvesting."

Dara spoke from the doorway, where she'd stationed herself out of habit, back to the wall, sightline to the street. "The levy order matters too. 'Present yourself for labor levy at the mountain gate.' Registered gifted, marching themselves into the mountain voluntarily." Her mouth twisted. "That's how they'll fill the vaults faster. Make the hunted dig their own cellar."

"And us," Sable said. She was turning one of her stolen-memory vials between her fingers, the glass catching the lamplight, and Iven noticed her thumb had worn a smooth patch into the wax seal. "We're walking toward a door that's about to shut forever. With a girl who glows in the dark." She nodded at Lior's wrist, where the sleeve had slipped. "No offense meant, ember-girl, but you're a lantern in a cellar full of mice."

"None taken," Lior said, and pulled the sleeve down, and Iven saw her do it without flinching, which was either courage or numbness, and he genuinely could not tell which frightened him more.

"So we go faster," Lior said. "Forty-one days. We've come this far in—what, three weeks? We make the salt road, we get to Vell, we find Maren's friend, and we get inside before the bell rings midwinter." She said it simply, the way she said most things, as if the plan were a rope and she'd already tied it around her own waist. "What's the actual problem?"

Iven hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Sable caught it—of course she did; reading people was her trade the way reading locks was other thieves'—and her eyes narrowed.

"You know something," Sable said. "About the mountain."

"I know the mountain," Iven said. "I served my apprenticeship there. Before the court, before—" He waved a hand that encompassed his disgrace, his exile, the burned bridge of his whole former life. "The Undercourt isn't a place you break into, girl. It's a place you're *invited* into, or sold into, and both doors are watched by things that can smell intention the way sharks smell blood. Every ward down there reads desire. Walk in wanting your brother, and the wards will want him too—and they always win."

Lior had gone very still. "Then how does anyone get in?"

"Nobody gets in," Iven said. "People get *taken* in. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole problem." He rubbed his eyes. He was fifty-three years old and tired in a way that sleep had never once touched. "But there are ways to be taken in that leave you free to move. Ways that require help from people in Vell who owe me—or owed me, twenty years ago, before I became the sort of name that gets spat in taverns. Whether those debts still stand, I don't know. Debts rot faster than men do."

"Then we collect them anyway," Lior said.

And there it was again, Iven thought. The thing about her that was going to get them all killed, and the thing that made him follow her anyway. She did not argue like a general or scheme like a thief. She simply decided, the way weather decides, the way fire decides, and the rest of them rearranged their lives around her decision and called it strategy.

Dara laughed once, short and humorless, from the doorway. "Forty-one days. Salt road in winter. Four fugitives, one of them glowing, one of them a deserter with a price on her head, one of them the most famous traitor-mage in nine cities." She ticked them off on scarred fingers. "And a pickpocket."

"Memory-thief," Sable corrected. "Pickpockets have *standards*."

"We'll need papers," Dara went on, ignoring her. "Real ones, not forgeries—Vessic's inspectors carry truth-lenses now, they burn through fake seals. And we'll need to move at night. And we'll need—" She stopped. Looked at her own hands. "I know the patrol rotations on the salt road. I wrote half of them." Her voice went quiet. "Every checkpoint between here and Vell, I built. And now I get to teach my friends how to betray them."

Nobody said anything comforting, because there wasn't any, and because they had passed, somewhere on the road behind them, the point where comfort between the four of them had become unnecessary. Iven noticed this the way he noticed all the slow changes in their company—with a scholar's detachment and a father's grief. They had become a unit. Units broke. Units got people killed. And yet here they were, planning around a table in a tannery cellar like a family arguing over supper, and some treacherous part of him had already begun, without permission, to think of them as *his*.

His last students had thought of him the same way. He remembered what the crown's men had done with that trust.

* * *

That night, while the others slept—Iven had taken the watch, another habit of the road he hadn't asked for—he climbed the den's ladder to the trapdoor and let himself up onto the tannery roof, where the city spread out below him like a bed of coals banked for the night.

Corren's Landing had always been loud. Harbor-cries, cart-wheels, the argument of ten thousand lives packed tight along the waterfront. Tonight it was quiet in patches, and the quiet had a shape: it was the shape of families sitting up late, talking low, deciding whether to run, whether to register, whether to burn their own histories before the inspectors came. Somewhere north, a dog barked and was silenced. Somewhere east, a single window lit and stayed lit past midnight, and Iven imagined the person behind it, awake with a list of names, doing arithmetic with lives.

Forty-one days.

He tried to remember midwinters from his youth in the mountain's shadow, and found that memory had done what memory does—softened the edges, kept the festivals, lost the fear. He remembered the midwinter bells of the Undercourt itself, great deep-throated things that rang once a year to mark the longest night, and how the sound had traveled up through the rock and out across the snowfields, and how the mountain folk had said, in the old days, that the bells were singing the souls to sleep for another year. Pretty story. Ugly truth: the bells were a census. Each ring counted the jars. Each year the count grew.

This year, the count would grow one final time, and then the counting would end, because the counting would become eating.

"Can't sleep either?"

Sable's voice came from the roof's far edge, where she sat with her knees drawn up, looking out at the dark water beyond the harbor lights. He hadn't heard her climb up. Of course he hadn't.

"You should be sleeping," he said.

"So should you." She patted the shingles beside her without looking at him. He went, because the night was cold and company was warmer than principle.

For a while neither spoke. Below them, the city breathed. Above them, the stars did their indifferent ancient work, and Iven found himself searching the northern sky for the constellation the mountain folk called the Jar-Bearer, and hating that he still knew its name.

"She asked me today," Sable said finally, "if I could take memories out of someone without them knowing. Without their consent." Her voice was carefully casual, which from Sable meant the opposite. "I told her no. Which was a lie. You can do it. It's just—it breaks something in them. Little cracks. They spend years wondering why certain rooms in their own heads feel haunted." She turned the vial in her fingers again. Iven saw now that it wasn't one of her empties. It was sealed, and heavy, and she held it the way people hold things they've decided never to use. "She wanted to know because of the wards. Because if the mountain can smell intention, maybe the way in is to not intend anything. To walk in empty."

Iven went cold. "She wants you to strip her."

"She wants me to strip her *again*." Sable's laugh had no floor under it. "She's already asked once, back on the road, before I told her what it costs. And now the deadline's moved, and she's doing that thing she does where she decides the price and pays it before anyone can stop her." She finally looked at him, and in the starlight her face was stripped of all its thief's polish, and underneath it she was just young, and tired, and afraid. "Tell me I'm wrong to refuse her. Tell me there's another way in. Tell me anything, mage. You're the one who knows the mountain."

Iven looked out at the water for a long time.

"There's a network in Vell," he said at last. "Maren's friend, if she still lives—Old Nan of the Lantern Rows, they called her, though her name was something else once. If anyone knows a door into the Undercourt that the wards don't watch, it's her. If anyone knows how to make a vessel walk in unnoticed, it's her." He paused. "And if she can't help us, then yes. The girl's plan is the only plan. And you'll have to decide whether to break her a little to save her a lot."

"That's not a decision," Sable whispered. "That's just choosing which of us bleeds."

"No," Iven said. "That's what it's been since the day Vessic took the throne. We've all just been pretending otherwise." He rose, joints cracking, an old man's music. "Sleep, thief. Tomorrow we buy horses and lies, and start the long walk to Vell."

He was halfway to the trapdoor when she spoke again, so quietly he almost missed it.

"Forty-one days," Sable said. "Do you think it's enough?"

Iven stood in the cold and thought of the bells under the mountain, counting their jars. Thought of a ledger page with a boy's name on it, written in his own younger hand. Thought of the ember-mark pulsing on a sleeping girl's wrist, patient as a second heart.

"No," he said honestly, because she deserved honesty and he had given so few people that. "But it's what we have."

Below them, far to the north, past the salt flats and the frozen river country, a mountain waited with its mouth beginning to close. And in Corren's Landing, the proclamation bells' echo still lived faintly in the bronze of every church tower, humming at frequencies only the gifted could hear—a sound like a countdown, like a key turning, like walls drawing shut around the last free souls in the world.

Forty-one days.
