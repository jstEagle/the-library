## 17. City of Salt and Lanterns

Vell announced itself by smell before sight: brine and smoke and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long near a fire. We crested the last hill at dusk, four travelers road-worn to the color of dust, and there it was below us—a city built on a drowned coastline, its streets stitched together with lanterns.

Thousands of them. They hung from every eave and bridgepost and ship's mast, floating on the canals in little glass boats of their own, so that from the ridge Vell looked less like a city than like a spilled constellation trying to climb back into the sky. The lanterns were not festival lights. I understood that as we descended. They were lit in windows where no one wanted to be seen clearly, in doorways where people came and went without names. A city that had learned, over twenty years of Vessic's slow tightening, to glow instead of speak.

"Salt preserves," Sable said beside me, pulling her hood lower. "That's what they say here. Salt keeps things from rotting." She smiled with only half her mouth. "They mean it about secrets."

The road down was lined with fish-racks and drying nets, and with notices nailed up at every crossroads—Vessic's proclamation, printed now, official, the ink still sharp enough to cut. *By order of the Crown, all persons of unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for registration.* Below the words, someone had painted over the seal with a crude lantern symbol. Below *that*, someone else had written, smaller, in charcoal: *the mountain is hungry but the city has teeth.*

Dara read it twice and said nothing. She had been saying nothing more often since Corren's Landing, since the burning, since she had stood in the crowd with her captain's reflexes screaming at her to do something and done nothing, because doing something would have gotten all four of us killed and saved no one. I watched her hands sometimes. They were never still.

Iven coughed—the wet, careful cough he'd been carrying since the smuggler's tunnels—and adjusted his pack. "We're looking for a woman called Odile Ferrane," he said. "Maren's letter said the Lanternhouse, whatever that means, behind the chandlers' row. And Lior—" He caught my arm gently. "Whatever happens in there, let me talk first. Maren trusted this woman once. That cuts both ways. Old friends keep old debts, and old debts get collected."

"I know how debts work," I said, and immediately regretted the sharpness. It wasn't his fault that everything anyone said to me lately sounded like a warning about myself. *You may be the living jar that holds his soul.* Iven's words, three weeks old now, and still lodged in me like a splinter I couldn't reach. My reflection had shown me Tam's eyes. I had stopped looking at reflections since.

* * *

Chandlers' row smelled of tallow and beeswax and hot metal, a street devoted entirely to making light against the dark, which struck me as either the most hopeful trade in the world or the most frightened. The shops were shuttering for the night. At the far end, past a yard stacked with wick-spoolers, stood a narrow building with no sign at all—just a single lantern burning green in the window, the color of deep water.

Sable knocked a pattern: two, pause, three, pause, one. Nothing happened for long enough that Dara's hand drifted toward her knife. Then the door opened six inches and an eye appeared in the gap, old and quick and unimpressed.

"We're closed."

"We're freezing," Sable said. "And we have a letter from a woman who signs her letters with a pressed ash-leaf."

The eye moved across each of our faces, lingering on mine a half-second longer than the rest. The door opened.

Inside, the Lanternhouse was warm in the way of places built against cold rather than decorated for comfort. Racks of candles climbed every wall. A low fire burned in a stove whose chimney ran up through the center of the building, and I realized after a moment that the whole structure was designed around heat and light the way other buildings were designed around walls—everything angled to move warmth through the rooms, to leave no corner dark enough to hide in, or perhaps to make sure no one inside ever had to sit in darkness alone.

The woman who owned the eye was small and iron-haired, with forearms scarred in the mottled pattern of old wax burns. She looked at us the way a chandler looks at wicks: assessing what we'd burn like, how long we'd last.

"You'll be Hartwell's girl," she said to me.

I went very still. "You know Maren?"

"Odile Ferrane." She didn't offer a hand. "I knew her before she was Elder anything, back when we were both young and stupid in ways the guild doesn't allow anymore. Come in properly. Shut the door. And you—" this to Dara, "—hunt-captain. I can smell the garrison wool on your coat even now. You'll sit where I can see your hands."

Dara sat where she could be seen. To her credit, she did it without flinching, and without explaining herself, and Odile watched her do it with something that might have been approval filed away for later.

* * *

She fed us first—fish stew, dense with barley, the best thing any of us had eaten in weeks—and made us eat in silence while she worked at a candle-dipping frame, turning the wicks with mechanical patience. Only when our bowls were empty did she set the frame aside and pull a stool close to the fire.

"Maren sent word ahead. A bird, three days ago. Short letter." Her eyes were pale gray, the color of the sea before weather. "It said you'd come asking after the Undercourt. It also said, and I quote, *tell her everything, Odile, even the parts that will make her hate me.*"

My stomach dropped. "What parts?"

"All of them, eventually. But not tonight." Odile leaned forward. "Tonight you need the shape of the problem, and then you need sleep, because the shape of the problem is going to take your appetite anyway and there's no sense wasting good stew."

She talked, then, and the firelight moved on her face, and outside the lanterns of Vell burned their quiet defiant thousands.

"The Hollow Valley hid itself," she said. "That was always the difference between us. Your guild bought invisibility—one payment, one terrible price, and then a century of pretending nothing happened. Vell couldn't afford invisibility. So we bought tolerance instead. Every gifted soul in this city is registered under a false craft-guild. Candle-makers, actually, most of us—it's a beautiful joke if you think about it. Fire-workers, licensed by the crown to work flame. Vessic's inspectors walk our rows every season and see honest tradesmen making tapers." She smiled without any joy in it. "The lanterns aren't decoration, girl. Every green lantern in that window means a registered gift. There are four hundred and eleven green lanterns in Vell tonight. Last midwinter there were five hundred and six."

Nobody spoke. The number did the work of speech.

"Some fled north. Some were taken. Some—" she spread her burn-scarred hands, "—some are in jars under the mountain, if they were lucky enough to be worth jarred. The rest burned. So when you tell me you mean to go into the Undercourt, understand that I have spent thirty years learning exactly what that place is, and my first instinct is to lock my door and mourn you politely."

"But your second instinct?" Sable asked quietly.

Odile looked at her a long moment. "My second instinct is why Maren wrote to me and not to someone safer." She rose, crossed to a shelf, and took down a candle—not a finished one, but a workpiece, half-dipped, its wax cloudy. She turned it in her fingers.

"There are three roads into the Undercourt," she said. "The gate above, which is sealed to everyone but the crown's own, and will be sealed absolutely come midwinter—that's not rumor anymore, it's proclamation; Vessic means to close the mountain like a fist. The deep ways, the old mining galleries, which the court flooded deliberately forty years ago and which I have spent a foolish amount of money trying to reopen, without success. And the third road." She set the candle down. "The third road is the one the goods take."

"Goods," Dara repeated flatly.

"The Undercourt runs on supply," Odile said. "Souls don't jar themselves. The brokers need bodies brought in—vessels, vessels' kin, the rare gifted whose souls hold a particular... density. And they need labor. The mountain eats. Someone has to carry food down, haul out slag, tend the ember-works. The crown contracts it out through intermediaries, and the intermediaries buy from slavers, debt-brokers, and—" her eyes flicked to Sable, "—memory-thieves with the right connections. People go down that third road in chains or in ledgers, and almost none of them come back up. But they go down."

"And you're telling us," Iven said slowly, "that the only way in is to be sold."

"I'm telling you it's the only way in that exists." Odile's voice was gentle in the way of a surgeon. "There is a broker in the salt-market district named Corvas Hale who buys for the Undercourt. He doesn't ask questions a seller can't answer. He reads minds—I won't lie to you about that; he'll turn each of you over like a stone and look at what's underneath. Whatever you carry that you can't afford to show him, you bury it deep or you lose it before you stand in front of him."

Every head turned toward Sable. She stared into the fire, and I saw her jaw tighten, and I remembered the bridge, the ferry-wight, the memory of her mother traded away like a coin, and my chest hurt with the promise I'd made her: *never again.*

"No," I said.

"Lior—" Iven began.

"I said no. Not her. Not again." I stood, and the firelight caught the ember-mark on my wrist, and Odile's breath audibly changed.

Because she was looking at it. She had been looking at it since the door, I realized—quick glances, professional glances, the way she'd assessed our wick-burning potential. But now she set down everything she was holding and came to me and took my wrist in both her scarred hands, and turned it to the light, and her face did something complicated.

"Where did you get this?"

"It's mine. It's always been mine."

"No," Odile said softly. "Child, this isn't a birthmark and it isn't a guild-mark. This is a broker's mark. A vessel's mark. I've seen it exactly twice in my life, on the wrists of two people sold south, and both times the price paid was—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. "Maren's letter said *tell her everything.* But it also said *not all at once,* and I am beginning to understand why."

"Tell me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Please."

"Not tonight." She released my wrist and stepped back, and for a moment the iron in her face gave way to something older and sadder. "Tonight I'll tell you the part that matters for the road. You want into the mountain. Fine. There is a way, and it's ugly, and it starts with Corvas Hale's ledger. If you go to him as fugitives, you die in his cellar. But if you go to him as *merchandise*—if someone walks you in on a chain with papers and a price—Hale will look inside you, find what he expects to find, and wave you down the third road with the rest of the cargo."

"And what does he expect to find?" Dara asked.

"A vessel," Odile said simply. "A marked vessel with a hollowed mind, delivered by a bounty driver with clean papers. The court has been waiting years for another vessel to surface. They'll pay a fortune. They'll pay fast. And they'll process the delivery quickly, because midwinter is coming and the mountain is hungry." She looked around at the four of us, at whatever she saw in our faces. "Yes. It means exactly what you think it means. One of you plays the goods. One of you plays the driver. The other two find their own way in as contract labor—Hale always needs haulers, and he never looks twice at the ones with strong backs and empty purses."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere above us, a green lantern swung in the wind off the salt flats, and its shadow swept the ceiling like a slow pendulum.

"They'd have to take my memories," I said. Not a question. I had already done the arithmetic; I could feel Sable not looking at me. "If Hale reads minds, then whoever goes in as the vessel has to go in empty. No names. No faces. No—" my voice caught, just slightly, "—no journey. Nothing that leads back to any of you."

"That's the price," Odile said. "Hope with teeth, girl. Everything worth having in this country costs exactly that."

I looked at Sable. Sable looked at the fire. Between us lay the bridge, and the ferry-wight, and the promise, and the knowledge—both of us knew it—that there was no other way, and that knowing didn't make it kinder.

"We'll need papers," Dara said at last, into the silence, her voice rough with everything she wasn't saying. "Bounty-driver papers. Clean ones. And chains that look real but open easy." She was already planning it; I could see the soldier waking up in her, the terrible competence that had once served the hunt now bending itself toward breaking it. "And I'll need to know exactly what kind of monster Hale expects a hunt-captain to be, so I can play him right."

"He expects one who missed her shot and got reassigned to cargo detail," Odile said, watching her. "He expects resentment. Give him that."

* * *

Later, when the others had been shown to bunks in the upper rooms among the candle racks, Odile kept me back by the stove. She poured two cups of something bitter and herbal and handed me one, and for a while we just sat, two people warmed by the same fire, saying nothing.

"She made me swear an oath once, your Maren," Odile said finally. "Fifty years ago, on a beach, with the tide coming in. We swore that whatever the guilds did to us—hiding, hushing, selling—we would never become the kind of people who did it to each other." She turned her cup slowly. "I've kept it badly. She's kept it worse. But oaths don't stop being true just because we fail them. Remember that, when you learn what she buried."

"You keep saying that. *What she buried.* Everyone knows something I don't."

"Yes." Odile met my eyes, and hers were kind and merciless. "And I'll tell you, because I swore to. But not tonight, and not because I'm cruel. Because the moment you hear it, you'll want to run—not away, child, *toward*, straight at the mountain, alone, tonight, and get yourself killed before midwinter. And the world cannot spare you. I've only known you an evening and I can see the world cannot spare you." She touched my wrist, lightly, over the ember-mark. "Sleep. Tomorrow we plan. The day after, you go down into the salt-market and let a man put a price on you, and you smile while he does it, because that is what hope looks like in Vessic's kingdom: merchandise that hasn't given up."

I climbed the stairs past the sleeping racks, past Dara sitting upright with her unfinished list in her lap like a rosary, past Iven murmuring old ward-cantos in his sleep, past Sable curled small with her back to the room, guarding whatever was left inside her head.

At the window, Vell burned its thousand green lights against the dark, four hundred and eleven promises that refused to rot.

Tomorrow I would be sold. I lay down among the candles and found, to my surprise, that I wasn't afraid of the chains.

I was afraid of coming back empty-handed from my own mind—and finding out whether my friends would still know me, or whether I'd have to earn them all over again, stranger by stranger, name by name.

Above me, the lantern swung. Its shadow crossed the ceiling like a slow pendulum, counting down to midwinter.
