## 18. The Price of Entry

The safehouse in Vell smelled of tallow and wet stone, and it was there that Sable laid out the price like a merchant laying out knives.

She did it the way she did everything—sideways, with a joke first. "Good news," she said, dropping onto the crate beside the cold hearth. "You're going to forget me."

Lior looked up from the map she'd been pretending to read. The lantern guttered. Somewhere above them, the city went on with its slow burning—bells, patrols, the criers calling names through the rain.

"I'm listening," she said, which was not the same as agreeing.

* * *

They had come to Vell three days ago, down out of the hills where the road turned to salt-crusted mud and the lanterns hung from every eave like a second sky. Old Fen—the friend of Maren's who ran the hidden network here, a woman shaped like a question mark and twice as sharp—had listened to their plan with her hands folded and her eyes doing arithmetic.

"The Undercourt," she'd said at last. "You want to walk into the mountain that eats souls."

"We want to walk *out* of it," Iven had corrected gently. "With what we came for."

"Mm." Fen had poured tea no one drank. "Then hear how it's done. There is one gate. One. And the warden at that gate is a mind-reader, girl—a hollow-eyed thing the court made, or found, or grew. It looks at you and it reads you like a ledger. Every face you love. Every road you've walked. Every name you've said in the dark." Her gaze had settled on Lior with terrible gentleness. "It would have your whole journey before your second breath inside the wall. Your friends' faces. Your purpose. And then the mountain closes on all four of you like a fist."

Dara had said what they were all thinking: "So we don't go in the gate."

"There is no other way in. I've spent thirty years looking." Fen had leaned forward then, and her voice had dropped to the register people use in sickrooms. "But there is a way to be empty. The thief girl. She could take it out of you. The journey. The faces. The why. Leave you clean as a coin fresh from the mint—and coins go into the mountain every day. Merchandise. The court buys vessels, laborers, bodies. A girl with nothing behind her eyes is just another shipment."

Sable had laughed then, quick and bright, the laugh she used when something hurt. "And there's my résumé finally paying off."

Now, in the safehouse, the laughter was gone, and the price sat between them on the floor like a third person.

"Here's how it works," Sable said, turning her stolen silver ring around and around on her finger. "I reach in and I take the thread. Not the whole cloth—you keep knowing how to talk, how to fight, how to hate the king. But the last two months? Gone. Corren's Landing, gone. The bridge, the ruins, the campfires—all of it. Me, Iven, Dara—" she flicked a glance toward the door, where the sound of Dara sharpening her knife came faint and rhythmic, "—all of it goes into my head instead of yours. You'll wake up knowing only what you knew when you left the valley. Maybe less. Memory doesn't always come out tidy."

"And you'll carry it," Lior said. "All of it. In your head. While you walk into the same mountain."

"I'm good at carrying things I shouldn't." Sable smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Occupational hazard."

"Or I refuse," Lior said quietly. "And we find another way."

"There isn't another way. Fen spent thirty years—"

"Fen didn't grow up in a valley built on secrets." Lior stood. Her legs were stiff; she'd been sitting too long, staring at maps of tunnels that didn't exist yet. "I've watched people I love trade pieces of themselves away and call it protection. My whole life is standing on top of a bargain somebody else made without asking me. I'm not—" Her voice caught. She hated that. "I'm not going to become another page in someone's ledger."

Sable was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the shutters.

"You think I want this?" she said finally, and the brightness fell off her voice entirely, leaving something raw underneath. "You know what I do, Lior. You've seen it. I take memories because taking is the only thing I was ever taught. And every single one I've ever taken, I kept, because throwing them away felt like murder. Do you understand what you're asking me to hold? Two months of you. Of us. Of—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "I'll be walking around inside your head while some warden reads mine. If it digs deep enough, if it finds *you* in me—"

"Then we're both caught."

"Then we're both *dead*, and worse, so is everyone whose names are in those memories. Iven. Dara. Tam." The name landed between them like a dropped stone. "This isn't courage, Lior. This is arithmetic. And the numbers are bad."

Lior crossed to the window and lifted the shutter a finger's width. Below, Vell glittered in the rain, ten thousand lanterns strung along canals the color of pewter. Beautiful, the free cities were, right up until you noticed the ash drifting from the public squares.

"I left home to stop being safe," she said to the glass. "That was the whole point. Maren wanted me to stay hidden. The guild voted to stay hidden. Everyone I've ever loved has a talent for hiding, and look where it got them—my brother sold, my valley blind, my own name a lie I didn't know I was living." She let the shutter fall. "If I go into that mountain carrying everything, I get us all killed. If I go in empty, I might get him out. That's not arithmetic, Sable. That's just the only door."

"It's a door with teeth."

"So was leaving the valley." Lior turned. "I did it anyway."

* * *

They told the others at supper, such as supper was—barley and salt fish and hard bread, eaten by candle stub because light drew patrol eyes to windows.

Iven set down his spoon with the care of a man defusing something. "There may be an alternative. Certain disciplines can shield a mind—"

"You tried shielding your students' minds once," Sable said, not cruelly, just factually, and Iven went still as poured wax. "No. Sorry. That was low. But no shields, Iven. Shields are what the court hunts for. Emptiness reads as innocence. Innocence gets waved through."

Dara said nothing for a long time. She was turning her knife over, watching the firelight run along the edge. When she finally spoke, her voice was the flat, careful voice she used for the things that mattered most.

"When I was in the hunt, we had a saying. *A soldier who won't burn her own map shouldn't carry one.*" She looked up at Lior. "It means: if you can't destroy the thing that could betray you, you shouldn't be holding it. I burned my list rather than finish it. I know what it costs to hand over the only copy of yourself." A pause. "I also know what happens to people who go into that mountain with full pockets. I've driven the carts, Lior. I've seen what comes back out. Sometimes it's not even them anymore."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you decide with your eyes open." Dara sheathed the knife. "For what it's worth—I'd trust her with my worst day. I've seen what she keeps. She keeps it like it's holy."

Sable stared at her plate. "Stop being nice to me, Ferro. It ruins my reputation."

* * *

They did it at midnight, in the room with the cold hearth, because Sable said memory-work went easier in the dark and Lior suspected she just didn't want anyone watching her face while she did it.

Lior sat on the floor with her back against the hearthstone. Sable knelt across from her, close enough that their knees nearly touched. She had taken off the silver ring and set it aside, and without it her hands looked strangely young.

"Rules," Sable said. "One: you can stop me any time before I take hold. Once I've got the thread, pulling back rips. Two: it doesn't hurt exactly. It hurts like waking up does. Like the moment you realize the dream was a dream. Three—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—when it's done, you won't know why you trusted me. You'll probably be frightened of me. That's correct behavior. Don't fight it. Fighting makes it messy."

"What will it be like for you?"

"Like drinking a river." Sable's mouth twisted. "Don't ask questions you don't want answered. Give me your hands."

Lior gave her hands.

They were warm. That was the thing she would remember—or wouldn't remember, she corrected herself, and the correction itself was dizzying, a thought about its own future absence, a hall of mirrors opening under her feet. Sable's thumbs pressed into her palms, and her eyes changed—not glowing, nothing so kind as glowing, but *deepening*, like wells filling.

"Say something true first," Sable murmured. "It gives me a place to stand."

Lior's throat was dry. She thought of all the true things stacked inside her like cordwood: *My brother's name is Tam. I never met him. I am a jar. These three people are the first family I chose.* She picked the one that scared her least.

"I'm glad it was you who caught me stealing bread," she said. "Of everyone on all the roads. I'm glad it was you."

Something moved across Sable's face—fast, quickly buried, a fish under ice.

"Hold still," she whispered. "This is the part where you're brave."

Lior felt it begin at her temples, a coolness like snowmelt, like the first morning of the valley's brief spring. It moved inward, gentle as water finding cracks, and where it touched, things came loose—not painfully, but the way autumn leaves let go, the way you only notice the stem was already broken. The road unspooled. The first mile outside the wards, magic roaring back into her like blood into a sleeping limb. The bread, warm through the cloth. Sable's grin over a stolen apple. Iven's ember-lit lecture in the smuggler's den. The child's pyre in Corren's Landing, and the smell, and her own scream. The bridge. The ferry-wight's gray hands. The campfire where Dara had almost smiled. The night she'd said—

*the night she'd said—*

*family,* something in her insisted, *there was a night I called them—*

"Don't hold on," Sable breathed, tears sliding freely down her face now, though her voice stayed level as a mason's line. "Holding on rips. Let it go, Lior. Let it go, let it go—"

*I called them family. When? Where? Whose faces—*

The coolness closed over it like water over a stone.

* * *

She woke on the floor of a strange room with a stranger kneeling over her.

The stranger had dark hair cut short and crooked, a thief's quick hands, and tear tracks drying on her cheeks. She was holding Lior's hands in both of hers, and her expression was doing several things at once, none of them simple.

Lior pulled back and scrambled upright, heart slamming. "Who are you? Where—" The room assembled itself around her: cold hearth, crates, a lantern, a door with a knife-sharpening rhythm beyond it that stopped abruptly. "Where am I? What did you do to me?"

"Easy." The stranger raised her palms. "You're safe. You're in Vell. Nothing's wrong."

"Nothing's—" Lior's own voice sounded foreign in her ears. She knew Vell. She knew she knew Vell; the name came with lanterns attached, with canals, with a network, with an old woman named Fen. But the knowledge was flat, a map with no footprints on it. She had arrived here somehow, recently, and the arriving was a blank white page. "I don't remember getting here. I don't remember—" She pressed her hands to her temples. "What did you take from me?"

The stranger flinched as if struck.

"Everything I asked you to," she said softly. "That's all. Everything we agreed."

*We agreed.* Lior searched the stranger's face for evidence of the agreement and found only grief wearing patience like a mask. Behind her, the door opened: a tall woman in a deserting soldier's coat, a thin man with court-broken posture and kind ruined eyes. They looked at her the way people look at someone returned from drowning.

"Lior." The tall woman—she knew the name fit, the way a key fits a lock it's been cut for—crouched at a careful distance. "It's Dara. We're your friends. All of this was your choice. I need you to hear that part. It was your choice, and it was a brave one, and we're not going to let you drown in the blank spots."

"I don't know you," Lior whispered.

"I know." Dara's voice cracked, just slightly, and was steadied. "Not yet."

The thief—the stranger with the wet cheeks—rose and stepped back into shadow, giving her space, and Lior understood suddenly that the space was for the stranger's sake too. That this person had carried something heavy out of her and was now carrying it alone, and that the weight must be considerable, because her shoulders were shaking.

"Why are you crying?" Lior asked her. "Did you—was it bad? What did you see?"

The stranger smiled, and it was the saddest thing Lior had ever seen, though she couldn't have said why she thought so.

"You'll forgive me if I don't answer that," Sable said. "Some cargo you don't unpack in front of the owner."

* * *

They left for the mountain gate at dawn, when the fog off the canals was thick enough to drink.

Fen met them at the safehouse door with a bundle: plain gray wool, a laborer's cloak, papers stamped with a salt-merchant's seal. "Merchandise travels ugly," she said, pressing the bundle into Lior's arms. "Remember that. Ugly, quiet, and empty. The warden will look at you and find a girl who knows nothing but her name and her hunger. Let it find exactly that."

"And if it finds more?" Dara asked.

Fen's eyes went to Sable, standing slightly apart with her hood up. "Then it finds a thief who's very good at locked doors. Pray it stops there."

They walked. The fog thinned as the road climbed, and the mountain came up out of the earth ahead of them like a verdict—black rock, snow-veined, its lower slopes studded with the gate-forts of the court, its summit lost in cloud. Wagons lined the approach road: ore carts, grain carts, and carts with barred tops from which came no sound at all, which was worse than sound.

Lior walked among her friends and felt the shape of them without the story. The tall one walked like a drawn blade held carefully pointed down. The thin one talked too much when he was afraid and was afraid now. The thief hadn't spoken since the safehouse and kept glancing at Lior sideways, quick little looks, like someone checking on a wound.

At the last bend before the gate, the thief fell into step beside her.

"Last chance for instructions," Sable said lightly. "When we're inside, follow the ember-mark. It warms when you're going the right way. Don't ask me how I know that. And Lior—" she hesitated, and for a moment something vast moved behind her eyes, a whole drowned country surfacing, "—if you meet yourself in there, somewhere deep, be kind to her. She's been through more than you know."

"I don't understand."

"I know," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

Ahead, the gate loomed out of the fog: iron-banded, tall as a cathedral door, flanked by wardens in black lacquer. And before it, on a chair of black stone, sat the reader—hollow-eyed, ageless, its gaze moving over each traveler in the line like a lamp passing over water.

It turned its head.

It looked at Lior.

And Lior, empty-handed, empty-headed, terrified and trusting, walked forward into its gaze.
