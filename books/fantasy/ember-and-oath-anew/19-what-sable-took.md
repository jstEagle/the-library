## 19. What Sable Took

There is a room inside me where I keep what isn't mine.

It has no windows. It has no door that opens from the outside—only from within, and only when I choose, and even then it opens like a wound, slow and grudging, the way a fist uncurls after a long day of holding something too tight. The thieves' guild in Corren's Landing called this talent *the cellar*. They taught me to think of memories as bottles: corked, labeled, shelved in the dark. They never told me what happens when you put someone else's life on your shelf and have to live beside it.

I had been careful for eleven years. Careful the way a woman carrying water across ice is careful—one measured step at a time, eyes down, breath shallow. I had stolen griefs and sold them back to their owners at a markup. I had taken a soldier's courage before a battle because he could not afford to feel fear and fight at once, and returned it after, thinner, like cloth washed too many times. I had kept my own childhood locked so deep in the cellar that some nights I genuinely forgot I had ever been small.

Then Lior Hartwell sat across from me in a salt-stinking room in Vell and said, *Take them all,* and I learned that careful was a word for people who had never been asked to carry something holy.

* * *

She was asleep in the loft above the lantern-maker's shop, curled on her side with one hand open against the floorboards, as if even in sleep she was reaching for something. Dara had taken first watch. Iven sat below with his books, pretending to read, his candle burning down to a stub he would not replace because replacement meant admitting how late it was. And I knelt beside Lior with my fingers hovering over her temple like a priest over a dying man, and I was afraid.

Not of the taking. Taking is easy. Taking is a door and a draft and a bottle uncorked, and the memory pours itself out glad to be gone. What frightened me was the shape of what she'd asked. Not one memory. All of them. Every mile of the road since the valley, every face, every fire, every name—so that when the Undercourt's interrogators pried her skull open like an oyster looking for its pearl, they would find nothing but a girl who woke up sold and knew nothing worth killing for.

"You don't have to do this," I had told her. "There are other ways in."

"There aren't," she said, "and we both know it. Take them, Sable. Keep them safe." And then, with that terrible plainness she had, that way of saying enormous things in the voice of someone asking for bread: "You're the only person I trust to hold them."

So now I touched two fingers to her brow, and I opened my cellar, and I let her pour.

* * *

The first memory came in like weather.

Rain on the first mile out of the Hollow Valley—the sound of it drumming on leaves she had never seen before, because she had never seen leaves, not really, not outside the valley's gray husbandry. I felt her lungs fill with wet green air and her whole body flinch from it, magic rushing back into her like blood into a limb that had slept for years, pins and needles made of lightning. She fell to her knees in the mud. She laughed. She was terrified and laughing, and somewhere behind her the valley's wards guttered out behind her like a lamp carried past the edge of its oil, and she did not look back, because looking back would have been the end of leaving.

I set that one on the shelf gently. Rain-memory. First-mile-memory. It hummed against my ribs like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading.

The second was bread. Of course it was bread—it was the day we met, though neither of us had known it yet. Her hands around a loaf she hadn't paid for, my hand around her wrist, the market square of Corren's Landing smeared gold with evening light. But the memory wasn't of the theft. It was of the moment after, when I'd expected fear or shame, and instead she had looked at me—really looked, the way people look at weather or scripture—and said, "You move like someone who used to be caught a lot," and I had hated her for about four seconds, and then I hadn't.

I had forgotten that. My own half of that afternoon was still mine, but hers—hers showed me my own face as she'd seen it: sharp, tired, amused in a way I hadn't known I still managed. It is a strange thing, to be given your own reflection by accident, from the inside of someone else's remembering.

Shelf. Label. Breathe.

They came faster after that, the way water comes faster once the dam cracks. The burning in Corren's Landing—I took that one carefully, wrapped it twice, because even secondhand it burned. The smell of pitch and hair. The child's family kneeling in the circle of stones while the crowd sang the king's hymn off-key, and Lior's magic rising in her like a flood against a door, and my hand clamped around her arm dragging her backward through the crowd, both of us running, and her sobbing—not for herself, never for herself—for people whose names she didn't know. *We have to get faster,* she kept saying. *We have to get there faster.*

Then Iven's den, and the ember-mark, and the night her reflection showed her Tam's eyes. That one I held longest before shelving. In it she stood before a cracked mirror in a smuggler's washroom, and the face looking back at her was her face and not her face—a boy's jaw ghosted over her own, a boy's dark eyes swimming up beneath her hazel ones like fish under ice—and I watched, from inside her, the exact moment the truth went through her. It didn't break her. That was the terrible thing. It went through her like a spear goes through water, and the water closed, and she turned from the mirror and said, "Then we go get him," as if identity were a thing you could lose and simply pick back up on the way out the door.

Dara's arrow. I found that one near the top of the pile, recent, raw. The hounds' ambush on the ridge road, the bolt singing past Lior's ear close enough to part her hair, and the long terrible pause where any sane person would have run—and then Dara walking down the hill with her crossbow slung and her captain's honor in pieces at her feet, saying, "I missed. I'm going to keep missing. Take me with you or leave me, but I won't shoot again." And Lior looking at this stranger in hunt-leather, this instrument of everything hunting her, and saying—"not deciding, just knowing"—"Then walk with us."

And the bridge. The ferry-wight with its lantern-teeth, and Sable—that is, me, seen from outside, seen from beside—trading away a memory of my mother's voice for passage, and afterward standing at the rail with my hands empty and not crying, because I had done the arithmetic and the passage was worth more than the voice, I had decided that years ago, I had decided it every time—

And Lior taking my wrist. Gently. The way you hold a bird.

"I'll never ask you to trade again," she said. "Not for me. Not for any of us. Whatever you gave, you gave already. From here, you give nothing."

I had forgotten the exact words. I remember the promise; I had built my days on it. But I had forgotten the warmth of her grip, the mud on her knuckles, the way the river-light moved on her face. Memory keeps meaning and loses texture. Hers kept both. That is the cruelty and the gift of holding another person's remembering: they notice things about the people they love that the people themselves never think to keep.

* * *

And then I came to the campfire.

It was three nights ago, in the ruins outside Vell, the night we each told one true thing. Iven's confession about his students. Dara's unfinished list. My childhood, sold in installments until there was almost nothing left to sell. And last, Lior, staring into the coals with her chin on her knees, saying in a voice barely louder than the fire:

"I'm afraid I'll forget him again. Tam. I barely remember him now—his face is mostly gone, just... an impression, like a hand pressed into wax. And if I forget him completely, then whoever sold him will have won twice. So I keep saying his name to myself. Tam. Tam. Like a prayer with nowhere to land." A pause. A log settling, throwing sparks. "But here's the strange thing. I'm less afraid than I should be. Because of you three. If I lose him again, I'll still have—" and she lifted her head, and the firelight was in her eyes, and she smiled the crooked, unguarded smile she never wore in daylight— "I'll still have my family. However weird and broken and armed-to-the-teeth it is."

Silence around the fire. Dara snorting to cover whatever her face was doing. Iven adjusting glasses that didn't need adjusting. Me, saying something dry and deflecting, because that is what I do, that is the armor I have worn since I was nine years old and learned that wanting things gets them taken.

But the memory didn't keep my deflection. Memories keep what mattered. And what mattered, in Lior's remembering, was the moment after the deflection—when nobody moved, and nobody laughed it off, and the four of us sat in the ruin of somebody else's house with the stars coming out through the broken roof, and it became true. Not spoken again. Just true, the way a knot becomes a bowline once it's pulled tight. Family. Chosen, unasked, irrevocable.

I sat in the dark of my own mind with that memory in my hands, and I understood what I was holding.

She had given me this. She had handed me the best hours of her life—the rain and the bread and the bridge and the fire—and said *keep them safe*, trusting me with the only treasure she had, which was the knowledge of being loved. And I had corked them and shelved them in a windowless room, and now they were mine to keep and not mine to feel. That is the thief's oldest law, the one they teach you before they teach you the finger-work: *what you hold, you cannot hold.* The cellar doesn't warm. The bottles don't open from the inside. I could walk these memories a thousand times and they would stay hers, bright and sealed, and I would remain what I have always been—a person standing outside the lit window, counting what's on the table.

Except.

Except that she had said *family*, and she had meant me.

Eleven years I had told myself the cellar was a mercy. That feeling things was a liability, that a thief who loves is a thief who can be bought or broken. I had sold my mother's voice in three installments and told myself it was arithmetic. I had watched Lior give away everything—her past, her name, her selfhood, soon, maybe, forever, down under that mountain—and I had thought: *she is braver than me.* And she was. But sitting alone in the dark with her bottled joy in my lap, I finally understood that her bravery and mine were not the same shape. She gave because she believed things could be gotten back. I had never believed that. I had hoarded emptiness and called it safety.

My face was wet. I noticed it the way you notice rain starting—gradually, then all at once. I was kneeling on the loft boards beside a sleeping girl who didn't know my name anymore, because her name-knowledge was in my keeping now, and I was crying, silently, the way I did everything, and there was no one to see, and that was the whole problem, wasn't it? That was always the whole problem. Love stored where it can't be felt is just weight. It's cargo. It's a jar of ash on a shelf in a guildhall, waiting for someone brave enough to break the seal.

Lior stirred. Her open hand flexed against the floorboards, reaching for the thing that wasn't there anymore.

"It's safe," I whispered, though she couldn't hear me, couldn't know me, might wake and see a stranger leaning over her and reach for a knife. "All of it. Every mile. I've got them."

Her hand stilled. Whether it was my voice or chance, I couldn't say. She slept on.

* * *

Below, Iven's candle had gone out. Through the gap in the shutters I could see Vell spread along its cliffs, salt-white in the moonlight, lanterns strung through the lower streets like a net of small fires—the city that had agreed, for a price, to sell us south. Tomorrow at dusk, Dara would bind Lior's wrists with bounty-cord, real cord, the kind that burns, because the warden at the mountain gate would check. Tomorrow the others would walk into the Undercourt's belly disguised as laborers, and I would walk in as merchandise too, my own past scrubbed thin enough to pass inspection, and none of us would speak of what came after, because there was no after we could count on.

I looked down at my hands. Thief's hands. Keeper's hands, now.

One bottle in my cellar was not like the others. I had taken it without asking, in the last minute before she slept—her final memory before the taking, the one she'd kept back herself and then surrendered anyway, pressing it into my care with the last of her trust. It was small. It was this evening: the four of us at the table below, going over the plan one last time, and Lior looking around at our faces and saying quietly, "Whatever happens down there—if I come back wrong, or empty, or not at all—don't let this have been for nothing. And don't let me be a stranger to you. Promise me you'll make me remember."

Dara had promised. Iven had promised. I had said nothing, because promises are debts and I had spent my whole life staying out of debt.

I held her memory of that silence now, and saw my own face in it, and knew what my face had been saying even though my mouth said nothing.

Tomorrow we sell her to a mountain full of monsters. Tonight I sat alone in the dark with a stranger's love in jars all around me, and I made the promise properly, in the only place I keep things that matter.

Then I went down the ladder, quiet as my name, to tell the others the warden's schedule had changed—and that we would be going through the gate a day early, whether we were ready or not.
