## 20. Sold South

The chains were the worst part. Not because they were heavy—Dara had carried heavier—but because of what Lior's face did when Dara closed the first shackle around her wrist.

"Say it," Lior whispered, so quietly that only a hunt-captain trained to catch confessions in the dark could have heard it.

"You're not my prisoner," Dara said. "You're cargo I'm delivering. There's a difference."

"There isn't."

There was. Dara knew exactly how thin it was, and she hated that she was the one holding the hammer about to drive it shut. She'd spent eleven years learning every cruelty of the Gift Hunt from the inside—the paperwork of it, the procedure, the way atrocity dressed itself in ledgers and wax seals and the polite language of *processing*. She knew what happened to gifted souls between the gate and the mountain's throat. She had written some of those reports herself, back when she still believed the reports were true.

Now she stood in the gray hour before dawn at the edge of the salt flats below Vell, fastening iron to the wrists of a girl who was going to let her do it, and the difference felt like a hair's breadth over an abyss.

"The warden will read you," Dara said, keeping her voice flat and official, because Sable and Iven were watching from the wagon and the performance started now. "Empty mind. Nothing but road-dust and fear. Can you do that?"

Lior lifted her chin. In the pre-dawn light her eyes did something strange—Dara had noticed it before, on the road, in firelight: a flicker of someone else looking out. Tam, maybe. The brother who lived behind her ribs like a coal banked under ash.

"I've been empty before," Lior said. "I can be empty again."

It wasn't bravado. That was the thing that made Dara's chest ache. It was practice. This girl had been hollowed out once already by people who loved her, and she spoke about emptiness the way soldiers spoke about old wounds—as familiar country.

Dara clicked the second shackle home and hauled her up by the arm, rougher than necessary, because the wagon driver two rows over was watching, and bounty drivers didn't cradle their merchandise.

* * *

The plan had been Iven's, which meant it was elegant and slightly obscene.

"You cannot sneak into the Undercourt," he'd said, drawing lines in spilled flour on the tavern table while the innkeeper's dog watched with professional interest. "The mountain has one mouth and the mouth has teeth. But mountains eat. They need bodies—laborers for the deep galleries, drivers for the soul-wagons, meat for the parts of the work no mage will stoop to do. And every consignment needs a licensed handler."

"A bounty driver," Dara had said.

"You," Iven agreed, "with your very real license, your very real scars, and your very real talent for looking like a man who hates his work." He'd paused, flour on his fingers, and added with terrible gentleness: "Which, I suspect, you soon will."

So: Dara Ferro, captain of the Third Hunt Company, deserter, would deliver one Lior Hartwell, registered gift-bearer, to the Undercourt gate at Grayfall Pass—for the bounty, she would say, and for the pardon that came with a delivery of this size. Sable would ride in the false bottom of the wagon among the salt-barrels, because memory-thieves traveled light or not at all. Iven would go as a laborer, hood down, hands blistered on purpose, his court accent buried under the gravel vowels of the mining camps where he'd spent his disgrace. And Dara would drive them all south through the pass, past the checkpoints, up to the one door in the world that must never open for them—and watch it open anyway.

She checked the wagon's underside one last time. Sable looked back at her through the gap between barrels, pale as the salt packed around her, and mouthed something Dara couldn't read. Probably a joke. Probably at Dara's expense. She chose to believe that, because the alternative was admitting the thief looked frightened, and if Sable was frightened then Dara was entitled to be terrified too.

"Hartwell!" she barked, and yanked the chain. "Wagon. Now."

Lior climbed up with her head bowed and her shoulders slumped, and she was good—gods, she was frighteningly good—at being small. Dara climbed onto the bench, took the reins, and clicked her tongue at the mules.

Behind them, the free city of Vell burned its morning lamps against the lingering dark. Ahead, the road ran south into the pass, and at the end of the road, crouched in the mountain's shoulder like a tumor of black glass, waited the gate.

*Three days,* Dara told the mules, and the wind, and whatever god still bothered with deserters. *Three days, and then we're inside.*

* * *

The first checkpoint was nothing—a bored sergeant, a lantern, a glance at Dara's papers. The second was worse, because the officer knew her.

"Ferro." Captain Ollis Vane stepped out of the guard hut with his cloak buttoned wrong and his eyes already narrow. They'd shared a mess tent for four years. He'd drunk her wine at her promotion. He'd wept at Rellam's funeral beside her, both of them pretending the dead boy had died cleanly. "They said you'd gone. Ran off after the Corren's Landing business."

"They say a lot of things." Dara kept her hands loose on the reins and her heartbeat somewhere below audibility. Beside the wagon, chained to the tailboard, Lior stared at the mud with the perfect vacancy of the broken. Dara had coached her: *don't act numb, just be tired.* Apparently the girl had decided to be genuinely exhausted, which was either method acting or the honest toll of everything she carried.

Vane walked the length of the wagon. His boots squelched. He read the manifest upside down without touching it—an old habit, a good officer's habit, and Dara hated him a little for still being good at this.

"Lior Hartwell," he said. "That name's been up on the boards since autumn. Youngest keeper of the Ember Guild. The Elder's pet project." He glanced up. "You brought her in alone?"

"She came quietly."

"They always do, at the end." Something moved across Vane's face—not suspicion, worse: sympathy. "You look like hell, Ferro."

"Roads'll do that."

He signed the manifest. Then he paused, quill hovering, and said, too casually: "There's talk the Third's been dissolved. Warrants out. You know anything about that?"

*Here it is,* Dara thought. *The door with the wolf behind it.*

"I heard the same talk," she said. "Why do you think I'm delivering this one personal? A Hartwell buys a lot of forgiveness."

Vane laughed—one short, ugly bark—and waved the wagon through. "Give my regards to the mountain," he called after her. "If it talks back, don't answer."

Dara drove. Her hands didn't shake until the checkpoint was a mile behind them, and then they shook so badly she nearly lost the reins, and from beneath the wagon floorboards came Sable's voice, dry as the salt she lay among:

"For what it's worth, I believed every word. And I lie professionally."

"Shut up, Sable."

"Yes, Captain."

* * *

Grayfall Pass took two days to climb and gave them everything it had: sleet that fell sideways, a bridge of rope and prayer over a gorge full of grinding ice, a night camped in the lee of a shrine to some forgotten hearth-god whose stone face had been worn smooth by travelers' touches. On the second night, huddled under the wagon with the mules breathing steam above them, Iven sat beside Dara and passed her a flask of something that tasted like pine tar and courage.

"You're doing well," he said.

"Don't patronize me, Ashgrave."

"It's not patronage. It's inventory." He nodded toward the wagon bed, where Lior slept—or performed sleeping—her chained hands tucked against her chest like a child holding something invisible. "In three days she walks into a place designed to unmake people like her, wearing nothing but an empty mind and our word that we'll follow. You're the one driving her there. I wanted you to know that I know what that costs."

Dara drank. The pine tar burned all the way down and settled in her chest like a coal.

"You want to know what costs?" she said. "I spent eleven years driving wagons like this one. Real ones. Full ones. I told myself the guild's story—that the gifted went to the mountain to be *helped*, that the jars were medicine, that the screaming I heard through certain walls was treatment." She turned the flask in her fingers. "Then I stood on a wall at Corren's Landing with a bow in my hand and orders to put an arrow through a girl who reminded me of my sister, and I missed. First miss of my career. And in the half-second the arrow sailed past her ear, I understood that I hadn't missed at all. Every shot before that, I'd hit exactly what I was aiming at. This was the first time I aimed at what I saw."

Iven said nothing. Below them the gorge groaned with ice, patient as a stomach.

"So no," Dara finished. "This doesn't cost me. This is the first thing in eleven years that pays me back."

* * *

On the third day the trees gave out, and then the grass gave out, and then there was only the mountain.

Dara had seen it before—from a distance, always, the way you see a executioner only from the neck down. The Undercourt was not built so much as excavated; the whole southern face of the peak had been opened like a wound, and out of the wound poured roads, cable-lines, smoke, and the constant subterranean thunder of machinery older than any kingdom. Black glass domes studded the slopes. Wagons crawled up the switchback road in a line so long it looked like the mountain was being fed by an artery.

And above the gate, carved into living rock in letters forty feet tall:

**ALL GIFTS ARE THE CROWN'S. ALL SOULS RETURN TO THE CROWN.**

"Subtle," Sable murmured, emerging from the wagon's belly for the first time in three days, salt dusting her hair like premature frost. She squinted up at the inscription. "I've met burglars with better slogans."

"Get down," Dara said, but without heat. Her mouth had gone dry. The line of wagons ahead of them moved at a funeral pace, and each vehicle that reached the gate paused there—just long enough—and then rolled through into the dark, and did not come out.

"That's us soon," Iven said quietly. He'd rejoined them at the last switchback, falling in among a gang of laborers with a shovel over his shoulder and a limp he hadn't had that morning. "Remember: once inside, we're strangers. We don't know each other. We find the vault, we find the broker's records, and we wait for Lior's mark to tell us where to go."

"And if the mark doesn't speak?" Dara asked.

Iven looked at the gate. "Then we improvise. I'm told I'm good at that. It's how I got disgraced."

The line crept forward. An hour. Two. The sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the black glass domes, and light came off them in sheets, and Dara understood suddenly why the valley had been made blind—the world outside was full of things that caught fire when you looked at them wrong.

Then they were next.

* * *

The warden of Grayfall Gate did not look like much. That was the horror of it. Dara had expected a monster—something scaled, or crowned, or visibly hungry. Instead a small woman in a gray robe sat at a desk of black glass set into the rock beside the gate, with a ledger, a bell, and a teacup, and she looked up at Dara's wagon with the mild impatience of a customs clerk at the end of a long shift.

"Papers."

Dara handed them down. The warden read. Somewhere beyond her, inside the gate's throat, something vast exhaled—a sound like a cathedral clearing its lungs.

"Delivery or surrender?" the warden asked.

"Delivery. Registered bearer, Lior Hartwell, taken under warrant in the Vell district. Bounty claim pending." Dara recited it like scripture, because it was scripture now; it was the only liturgy left to her.

"Mm." The warden rang a small silver bell. From a recess in the rock, a second figure emerged—tall, robed darker, face wrapped in gauze. A reader. Dara's stomach dropped through the wagon bench and into the mountain. She'd forgotten the readers. Or she'd never let herself remember them.

"Standard reading," the warden said. "Bearer first. Driver after, if the bearer's account is unclear."

The gauze-faced figure drifted to the wagon's side. Lior knelt on the tailboard, chained, head bowed. Up close, Dara could see the fine tremor in her shoulders—the body's honesty betraying the mind's discipline.

"Look at me," the reader said. Its voice was genderless and soft, like sand poured on sand.

Lior raised her eyes.

Dara held her breath and gripped the brake lever until the wood creaked, and prayed—to the hearth-god with the worn-smooth face, to Maren, to Tam wherever he was—to anyone listening inside that girl's skull: *be empty, be empty, be empty.*

The reader went still. Its wrapped head tilted. Seconds passed. A crow argued with the wind somewhere above the gate. The warden sipped her tea.

Then the reader stepped back, and its soft voice carried something almost like confusion.

"There is nothing," it said.

"Nothing?" The warden frowned. "Gifted bearers are never nothing. Even the quiet ones have noise. Fear, at minimum. Guilt. The guilds train them to hide, but hiding is a shape, and shapes can be read."

"No, Warden." The reader's gauze face turned back toward Lior, and though its eyes were hidden, Dara felt its attention like a hand laid flat on her own sternum. "There is no shape. No fear. No guilt. No self looking out. It is—" the pause stretched, and the reader's voice dropped, almost reverent, almost troubled— "*hollow.* Whatever she was, it has already been taken. She is an emptied vessel. There is nothing here for the Crown to claim."

Silence. The mountain breathed. Dara's heart slammed against her ribs so hard she was sure everyone on the switchback could hear it.

Because that was the genius of it—Sable's work, three nights of careful theft in a shrine while Lior slept, taking the journey, the names, the campfire, the family, sealing it all away—and because underneath the theft there had never been anything to find. Lior Hartwell had been hollow from the beginning. The guild had built her that way. The reader was telling the truth, and the truth was the perfect disguise.

The warden set down her teacup.

"An empty vessel," she repeated. She looked at Lior for a long moment, and something flickered across her plain gray face—not pity, Dara thought. Recognition. Appetite. "Well. The Court does love its vessels. Broker Vesh has been asking for intact ones." She stamped the manifest. The sound cracked across the pass like a bone breaking. "Driver—unload the bearer at the inner yard. Laborers, east gallery intake. Move along."

The gate loomed. Ten feet. Five. The dark inside was not the dark of a tunnel; it was the dark of a mouth, warm and slow and ancient, and ember-light glimmered far down in it like a swallowed star.

Dara clicked her tongue. The mules leaned into their collars. The wagon rolled forward, and the shadow of the gate slid over them like water closing over a sinking stone, and the last thing she saw of the sky was the inscription, burning white in the sun:

**ALL SOULS RETURN TO THE CROWN.**

*Not this one,* Dara thought, as the mountain swallowed them whole. *Not hers. Not any of them. Not if I have to burn this place down from the inside with my bare hands.*

Ahead, deep in the dark, the ember-light pulsed once—twice—like a heartbeat answering.

And Lior, kneeling chained in the wagon bed, lifted her head, and smiled.
