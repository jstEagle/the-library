## 21. Beneath the Mountain

The gate closed behind us with a sound like a held breath finally let out.

I had expected darkness. What I got was light—so much light that for a moment I thought we had walked into the heart of a sunrise buried under the world.

The corridor opened without warning into a vastness my eyes could not finish measuring. The ceiling was stone, but it had been carved and polished until it shone like the inside of a shell, ribbed with veins of pale gold that pulsed, slowly, the way a sleeping thing breathes. And hanging from that ceiling, tier upon tier upon tier, descending in spirals toward a floor so far below it seemed more memory than place, were jars.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

Each one glowed.

Not with firelight—I knew firelight, had tended it every night of my life in the Hollow Valley—but with ember-light, the deep amber-red that lived in the coals of our hearths, in the guild's oath-braziers, in the small lamp Maren kept burning by the ledger. The same color. The exact same color, down to the way it flickered at its edges as if shy of the dark around it.

My knees nearly went. Dara's hand caught my elbow—she was still dressed as my bounty driver, her captain's insignia hidden under her coat, her face arranged into the bored cruelty the warden had wanted to see—and she steered me forward as if I were merely stumbling under the weight of chains. Chains I did not wear. The performance saved me. I let her save me.

"Keep walking," she murmured, barely moving her lips. "You're merchandise. Merchandise doesn't stare."

But oh, I stared.

* * *

They marched us down a stair cut into the living rock, wide enough for twenty abreast, worn hollow in the center by centuries of feet. Other laborers moved around us—men and women in gray smocks pushing carts of salt, of ore, of things wrapped in oilcloth that I made myself not look at too closely. Guards in Vessic's black-and-gold walked the railings above us, their boots ringing on iron grating. No one looked up at the jars. That was the first terrible thing I understood: everyone here had learned not to look up.

Sable walked three rows behind me, in a laborer's smock, her head down, her hands bound loosely with rope she could slip in half a heartbeat if she chose. She had slipped them twice already just to remind herself she could. When our eyes met across the column of prisoners-turned-workers, she gave me nothing—not a flicker, not a nod. We had agreed on that. To her, and to the warden who had read my emptied mind at the gate, I was a girl sold south with no memories worth taking, a blank page with an ember-marked wrist. Whatever I felt now, I had to feel alone.

That was the price of entry. I had paid it in the free city of Vell, sitting in a cellar that smelled of tallow and wet stone while Sable knelt before me with her fingers at my temples and her eyes full of apology. *I'll keep it safe,* she had said. *Every mile of it. You'll have it back.* And then the road to Vell had gone out of me like a candle pinched between wet fingers—the ambush, the bridge of small mercies, the night we told each other one true thing apiece—all of it folded away into someone else's keeping, leaving me a stranger walking beside strangers I trusted for reasons I could no longer name.

I trusted them anyway. Trust, it turns out, lives deeper than memory.

Now, descending beneath the mountain, I looked up at ten thousand glowing jars and felt something older than trust stir in me, something that predated Sable's theft, predated the valley, maybe. Recognition. My mark burned.

I glanced down at my wrist. Beneath the laborer's sleeve, the ember-mark lay quiet—a curling sigil like a flame seen through smoke, the mark Iven had gone white-faced over in a smuggler's den half a world ago. Only Undercourt vessels bore it, he'd said. Vessels. Living jars.

Around me, the great jars hung in their silent constellations, and every single one of them burned with my guild's light.

* * *

They quartered the new laborers in a gallery carved off the main vault, a long low room lined with straw pallets and lit by lanterns that burned that same amber red. Of course they did. Even the lamps here were fed on souls—or fed on whatever fed the souls; I couldn't yet tell where the harvest ended and the hoard began.

A foreman with a voice like gravel in a bucket assigned us to the ash-works: hauling spent residue from the refining galleries to the slag chutes. Filthy work. Perfect work. Ash-work put us deep in the mountain, close to everything, noticed by no one.

"Vessel-girl." The foreman squinted at my wrist where the sleeve had ridden up. "Marked stock, eh? Broker'll want you catalogued. Report to the Hall of Names at second bell."

Dara shifted beside me, a half-step, the soldier's weight coming forward onto the ball of her foot before she caught herself. "She's mine to deliver," Dara said flatly. "Bounty contract. Paperwork."

"Paperwork burns," the foreman said, and laughed at his own joke, and moved on.

When his back was turned, Dara's hand found my shoulder—brief, hard, a promise shaped like a grip. *I'm here. We're here.* Somewhere in this mountain Iven moved among the laborers with his court-bred posture deliberately broken, stooping, mumbling, invisible; somewhere Sable was already learning which corridors the guards avoided and why. My family, scattered through the dark like embers raked apart, each one still burning.

Second bell came too soon.

* * *

The Hall of Names was smaller than the great vault but worse, because it was intimate. A round chamber paneled in dark wood—actual wood, hauled down from some murdered forest, absurd and precious this far underground—and shelved floor to ceiling with ledgers. Thousands of ledgers. In the center stood a desk, and behind the desk sat a thin man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on a skull shaved smooth, and he was writing when we entered, unhurried, the scratch of his pen the only sound.

He finished his line. Then he looked up.

"Turn in the light," he said.

I turned. The amber glow caught my wrist and the mark woke—not flaring, not hurting, just *answering*, the way a tuning string answers a note sung across a room. I felt it hum against the bone.

The broker rose, came around the desk, took my arm in fingers dry and cool as paper, and studied the sigil the way a jeweler studies a stone he already knows is flawless.

"Hartwell vessel," he said softly. "Twenty years in the making. Do you know how long we waited for you to be brought home?"

"I don't know anything," I said, and it was even true. "I was told I'd be catalogued."

"You will be." He released my arm and returned to his desk, opening a ledger whose spine crackled with age. "Name?"

"Lior."

"Family name?"

I hesitated one heartbeat too long. Dara, behind me, made a small impatient sound, the sound of a bounty driver whose merchandise is being difficult.

"Hartwell," I said.

The pen scratched. "Hartwell, Lior. Vessel-class. Provenance: north, valley-stock." He wrote a number beside my name, and the number was large, and I understood with a cold sliding feeling that he had just written down what I was worth. "You'll be housed in the vessel gallery. Not the laborers' quarters. Your kind doesn't haul ash."

"My kind."

"The marked." He looked up over his spectacles, and his eyes were the pale gray of paper left too long in sun. "Girl, do you know what this place is?"

"A prison," Dara said before I could answer.

The broker smiled, mild as milk. "A library," he said. "Every book a life. You are standing in the greatest collection ever assembled, Captain-of-nothing—I know desertion paperwork when I see it, incidentally; your forgery is good but your boots are wrong—and the crown pays me to keep the collection orderly until midwinter, when His Majesty comes to read." He tapped the ledger. "You, Hartwell Lior, are not a prisoner. You are a first edition."

Something in my chest went very still and very cold.

"Take her to the gallery," he said to the guard at the door. "And send word to the Under-Keeper. Tell her the northern vessel has come home at last."

* * *

They led me up, not down—up along a spiral gallery that hugged the wall of the great vault, close enough to the hanging jars that I could have reached out and touched them, and I am ashamed to say I did touch one, once, when the guard's attention drifted.

It was warm. Warm as a sleeping child's forehead. Through the glass I saw the ember-light coil and press toward my palm like a plant toward sun, and I snatched my hand back as if scalded, though the burn was not on my skin. It was somewhere deeper.

Inside the jar, dimly, was a shape. Not liquid, not gas—something between, a slow-turning luminous haze that had, when I looked with the corner of my eye rather than straight on, edges. Shoulders. The suggestion of a head bowed as if in sleep or sorrow.

Faces. They all had faces, if you didn't look directly. Ten thousand faces, turning slowly in their warm glass prisons, lit by their own stolen light.

I counted my steps after that. It was either count steps or scream.

* * *

The vessel gallery was a smaller vault off the main one, holding perhaps two hundred jars, and here the horror refined itself into something sharper, because these jars were labeled. Brass plates, engraved names. I read them as the guard walked me past, because reading them was the only rebellion available to me and I took it greedily.

*Maren of the Fenmarch.* *Odalys Bright, gifted: weather-sense.* *Kesh, age nine, gifted: mending-touch.*

Age nine.

I stopped walking. The guard prodded me on with the butt of his spear, and I went, but the name stayed with me, Kesh, age nine, mending-touch, and I thought about what mending-touch meant—a child who could knit a broken cup whole, probably, maybe a broken bird's wing, a child whose entire crime was kindness with power in it—and I carried that name up the last flight of stairs like a coal carried in bare hands.

At the gallery's end stood an empty alcove. Fresh-cut brackets. A brass plate already mounted, waiting for its jar.

The plate read: *HARTWELL.*

No first name. Just the family name, and beneath it, in smaller letters: *Vessel, paired. Contents: one (1) soul, male, reclaimed-at-will.*

Reclaimed-at-will.

Tam.

The word rose in me without permission—*Tam, Tam, Tam*—a name I had learned only weeks ago from Maren's guilt-stricken mouth, a brother I had never been allowed to remember, and yet my body knew him the way a riverbed knows water. My brother's soul was in me. Had been in me for twenty years, folded so small and kept so quiet that I had mistaken its warmth for my own heartbeat. And this alcove, these brackets, this engraved plate—they had built his shelf. They had prepared the jar he would be poured back into, the moment the crown decided to pour him out of me.

The guard locked me into a cell adjoining the gallery—"vessels get walls, not bars, company policy, keeps the goods unbruised"—and left me alone with a cot, a bucket, a lantern burning soul-light, and the soft, ceaseless breathing of two hundred labeled lives through the wall.

I sat on the cot. I did not cry. Crying was a luxury with a deadline attached, and midwinter was coming down on us like an avalanche.

Instead I did what the Ember Guild had trained me to do since I was six years old. I tended the light.

There was a brazier in the corner of the cell, ceremonial, decorative, fed with that same amber flame. I crouched before it and did the old motions without thinking—cup, breathe, shape—and the flame leaned toward me like a dog hearing its name.

And in that moment, watching it lean, watching it *recognize* me, the last piece fell into place, and awe curdled in my chest and turned, slowly, surely, into fury.

The Hollow Valley was a place where magic could not follow. Every child of the guild learned it as we learned our own names: the valley eats magic, the valley keeps us safe, the wards are the guild's oldest gift. We tended our little hearth-fires and thanked the founders for a haven where the gifted could sleep unmarked and unafraid.

But there are no wards. There never were any wards.

The valley isn't a shield. It's a *drain*. Everything magical that has ever touched the Hollow Valley—every gift born there, every spark, every whisper of wonder—runs downhill through the rock, through a hundred miles of cold stone, and pools here, beneath the mountain, in ten thousand jars. The valley's silence isn't protection. It's a pipe. Maren's guild hasn't been hiding the gifted for a century. It has been *farming* them—unknowing, unwilling, but farming nonetheless—keeping the herd calm and fertile and ignorant in a pasture designed to bleed them dry, generation after generation, while the Undercourt filled its shelves.

My whole childhood was a field lying fallow. My whole life, a crop ripening.

And the ember-light—the light I had tended every night of my life, the light of every oath the guild ever swore—that light was the same light burning in these jars because it had always been the same light. It had simply, briefly, been on loan. Every hearth in the Hollow Valley was a window into this vault. We had warmed our hands on other people's souls and called it home.

I knelt there on the cold stone with the flame bending toward me and I let the fury come, because fury was cleaner than grief and I would need fuel for what came next. I let it fill me the way the jars were filled, slow and patient and total.

Somewhere above us, King Vessic was planning to drink all of this at midwinter. Somewhere in these corridors, my friends were moving through the dark with my stolen memories between them. Somewhere in my own chest, my brother slept on, twenty years deep, dreaming whatever souls dream inside their sisters.

The plate outside the alcove said *reclaimed-at-will*.

"Well," I said aloud, to the brazier, to the two hundred names behind the wall, to the mountain itself. "We'll see about the *will* part."

The flame stood up straight, all on its own, like a soldier called to attention.

And far off, down the spiral dark, a bell began to ring—second bell, third bell, I couldn't tell—and beneath its ringing I heard, faint and rising, footsteps. Many footsteps. Coming toward the vessel gallery.

The door of my cell had no lock on the inside.
