## 22. The Broker of Names

The Undercourt smelled of tallow and old iron, of candle-smoke layered over candle-smoke until the air itself seemed to have a memory. Iven Ashgrave walked its galleries with his hands folded behind his back the way he had walked them twenty years ago, when he was a student with ink-stained cuffs and a mentor who taught him that power was not taken but *kept*—kept in jars, in ledgers, in names written twice so that no one could ever be certain which copy was true.

He had sworn, on the day they stripped his court robes from him, that he would never walk these halls again. He had also sworn, once, to protect his students. He was learning that oaths were like coins: they wore smooth with handling.

Above him, far above, midwinter was coming down through the mountain's stone like water through a cracked roof. He could feel it in the ember-lamps that lined the galleries—each one fed by a jar somewhere below, each one burning a shade too eagerly now, as if the souls beneath them knew what season it was and were pressing against their glass.

*Three days*, he thought. *Perhaps four.*

He adjusted the collar of his borrowed coat—a smuggler's coat, greasy at the shoulders—and reminded himself that he had been a liar for longer than he had been anything else. It was the one craft Vessic had never managed to take from him.

* * *

The Broker of Names kept an office at the mountain's heart, in a chamber that had been carved to look like a throat. That was not metaphor; Iven had checked the old survey maps during his court years. The Undercourt's architects had built the vaults as organs—vault as stomach, gallery as lung, and here, where every corridor narrowed toward a single door, the throat through which all transactions passed. Everything sold into this place came down through that door. Nothing came back up except by the Broker's leave.

Two wardens flanked it, gray-robed, faceless behind masks of hammered tin. They did not ask his name. In the Undercourt, you were what you carried, and Iven carried a ledger page wrapped in oilcloth against his chest—the one thing in the world the Broker wanted more than coin.

He knocked, three times, then twice more. The old rhythm. The rhythm of a student arriving late to lessons.

"Enter," said a voice like dry paper folding, "before I decide you're a memory."

* * *

Corvane Ash had not aged so much as *condensed*. The man Iven remembered had been tall and loose-limbed, a heron of a man who gestured like a conductor. The man seated behind the desk of black glass was smaller now, denser, as if the years had pressed him down into something that would keep. His hair was white as salt-foam and cropped close to the skull. His eyes were the color of the jars downstairs: amber, lit from within, patient.

"Iven." He said the name the way a man tastes wine he suspects has turned. "They told me a smuggler's runner wanted audience. They did not tell me my own failure had come home."

"Your finest work," Iven corrected. "You used to say failure was just success that hadn't found its buyer."

"And you used to laugh when I said it." Corvane gestured to the chair across the desk. It was the same chair—Iven recognized the burn mark on its arm where a careless apprentice—he—had set down a live coal thirty years ago. "Sit. You have five minutes before the wardens wonder why the throat is open."

Iven sat. The chair remembered him, or he remembered it, and for a moment the two memories lay together like hands.

"You look well," he said.

"Liar."

"Yes. It's good to know some things hold."

Corvane almost smiled. Almost. On that face, the almost was worth more than another man's whole expression. "You have something for me. You would not have gotten past the gate otherwise—you are known to the crown, Ashgrave. Disgraced, attainted, your name struck from the rolls. There are wardens on the upper levels who would sell their mothers for the bounty on you." He laced his fingers. "So either you are the bravest fool ever to walk these halls, or you carry something worth more than your head. Show me."

Iven unwrapped the oilcloth slowly. He had learned, under this very roof, that revelation was a currency best spent in small denominations.

The ledger page was old, soft as cloth at its folds. Three columns of names in faded brown ink. Souls bought, souls sold, souls *held*. And in the third column, circled twice in a hand Iven knew better than his own: *Hartwell, T.* — vessel prepared — *paid in full.*

Corvane read it without touching it. His stillness was total, the stillness of a man who had spent a lifetime teaching his body that movement cost money.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get this?"

"The valley."

"There is no valley."

"There is a valley that does not appear on any map drawn since the Founding," Iven said. "A valley where magic cannot follow, because it was all harvested out of it and poured into this mountain. A valley the crown paid for with one soul, and the guild paid for with its silence. You brokered the sale, Corvane. Your hand circled the name."

The lamps guttered. Somewhere far below, in the vault of jars, something shifted—a sound like a congregation turning in its pews.

"You have been busy," Corvane said at last. "For a disgraced man."

"I've been busy for a dead man. That's what disgrace is—it kills you, and then everything you do afterward is free." Iven leaned forward. "Here is what I am selling, and I will only say it once. I know about the Hartwell sale. I know the vessel was a child. I know the child was raised in the valley, kept ignorant, kept *safe*, because a broken vessel spills and a whole one keeps. And I know—" he let the pause stretch, thin as wire— "that she is inside your mountain right now."

Something moved behind the amber eyes. Not fear. Calculation. But calculation, in a man like Corvane, was the shape fear took when it dressed for company.

"If that is true," Corvane said, "she will be found. The warden at the south gate reads every mind that passes. An empty mind walks through; a full one goes to the pits."

"She *was* empty. She gave up her journey-memories to get in. Which means she is in your halls with nothing but her name and her purpose, and no way to prove either." Iven smiled, and hated himself for how easily the smile came. "You taught me that, too. The most dangerous prisoner is the one with nothing left to confess."

"Why are you telling me this?" Corvane's voice dropped, and the dry-paper quality of it softened into something older, wearier. "Twenty years ago you stood in this room and swore you'd never trade in souls. You called me a graverobber of the living. You wept, Iven. I remember the weeping. So tell me why the weeping man comes back carrying secrets instead of fire."

Because I am tired, Iven thought. Because I betrayed forty students to keep my post and lost the post anyway. Because there is a girl downstairs with her brother's soul folded up inside her like a letter she cannot read, and every hour I spend negotiating is an hour she spends walking corridors full of people who would crack her open to see what shines.

What he said was: "Because fire is cheap, and I want to buy something."

* * *

They negotiated the way old duellists fight—with each pass knowing exactly where the other's scars were.

Iven wanted three things. First, access to the vessel vault, unaccompanied, for the space of one bell. Second, word of what the crown intended for the jars. Third—and this he held back, letting it seem like an afterthought—an assurance that when whatever was coming came, the Broker's wardens would find urgent business elsewhere.

Corvane wanted the ledger page, the name of everyone Iven had spoken to since entering the mountain, and, above all, time. Time was the one commodity the Undercourt was running out of, though only two men in it knew that yet, and both of them were sitting at this desk pretending otherwise.

"A bell in the vault," Corvane said. "Not a minute more. The vault answers to the crown's seal now, not mine. I can open it, but I cannot unopen it, and every opening is logged in wax."

"One bell. And the log entry will say?"

"That a senior broker inspected the seals. My seal, my inspection. If anyone asks, I will swear you were never here." He tapped the desk with one finger, and the sound was like a knuckle on a coffin lid. "In exchange: the page, and the girl's location, and your oath—your *real* oath, Ashgrave, the kind that binds—that you take nothing out of the vault but what you carry in."

"And if what I carry in is a lie?"

"Then the mountain keeps it. The mountain keeps everything." For the first time, Corvane looked tired—not old, tired, the tiredness of a man who has counted the same coins so long he has begun to hate their faces. "Give me the page."

Iven slid it across the black glass. Watching Corvane's hands close around it was like watching a man take back a debt he'd forgotten he was owed; the fingers trembled, just once, and were still.

"Now," Iven said, "the second item. What does the crown intend for the jars? And do not insult me with the official answer. I wrote half the official answers myself."

Corvane rose. He went to the window—or rather, to the slit of smoked crystal that served this deep in the mountain for a window—and stood looking through it at nothing, at the dark that pressed against the glass like a sea.

"You remember the Rite of Communion," he said. "The theory of it. We taught it to you in your second year. A gifted king, a willing court, the pooled strength of a hundred souls lent upward for a single night, to turn a flood, to break a siege. Power held in common, spent for the many."

"I remember. I also remember it was never done. The theology outran the plumbing."

"It was never done *willingly*." Corvane turned. The lamp-light made his face into a mask with holes for eyes. "Vessic means to do it unwillingly. At midwinter, when the mountain's gates are sealed and the courts above are his, he will open every jar in the vault—all nine hundred and forty-one—and pour them into himself. Not lend. *Drink.* He believes the old rites failed because the souls resisted. He intends to remove resistance as a variable."

The words went through Iven like cold water through cloth. Nine hundred and forty-one. He had seen the vault registers during his court years; he had signed off, God forgive him, on the acquisition schedules, telling himself each signature was mercy, that a jar in the Undercourt was kinder than a pyre in the square. Nine hundred and forty-one souls, and one usurper's mouth.

"He'll burn out," Iven said. "No single frame holds that much. The first kings who tried it—"

"—died screaming, yes. Vessic has read the same histories. He believes they failed because they drank from jars, secondhand, the strength already stale. He means to drink from vessels still warm." Corvane came back to the desk and sat, and now Iven saw it plainly, the thing the Broker of Names had been hiding behind his patience: terror, banked and rationed, spent a coin at a time. "He has had the throne room rebuilt. There is a basin under the dais now. Stone channels run from the vault floor to the dais, cut by masons who were themselves jarred when the work was done. Do you understand what I am telling you, Iven? In three days, this mountain becomes a chalice, and everything in it—including, I suspect, your girl—is on the menu."

* * *

Afterward, Iven walked the galleries alone, and the mountain walked with him in the only way it could: through sound. The drip of condensation. The creak of ancient timber bracing. And beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the hum of the jars—nine hundred voices too many to count, singing without words, the way a kettle sings before it boils.

He had come to buy time and had learned that time was already spent. Three days. Midwinter bells. A chalice carved beneath the throne.

He stopped at a gallery rail and looked down into the central shaft, where the ember-light rose from the vault in slow columns, gold and red, beautiful the way plague-fires are beautiful from a distance. Every light down there was a person. He had known that abstractly for thirty years. Standing here, with Corvane's confession still cold in his ears, he knew it the way you know a wound: locally, specifically, with a name attached.

*Lior Hartwell,* he thought. *Girl with a compass she doesn't know how to use and a brother she doesn't know she carries. You walked into a mouth.*

And Sable, who had traded away her childhood in pieces and would trade the rest before she let them touch Lior. And Dara, whose desertion papers would hang her if the hunt didn't. And Tam—Tam, wherever Tam was, walking free on legs that were not entirely his own, a soul wearing a borrowed life like a coat that fit too well.

Old sins negotiating with new ones. That was all Iven had ever been good at. He had sold his students' names to save his post; now he sold his mentor's secrets to save a stranger. The arithmetic never changed. Only the numbers got worse.

But there was one number Corvane did not know. One fact Iven had kept in the deepest pocket of his coat, wrapped tighter than the ledger page had ever been:

The ember-mark on Lior's wrist was not merely a brand. It was a *key*. He had seen the old diagrams—heretical ones, burned ones—in the years before his fall. The vessel that holds a soul is not a lock on the soul. It is a lock *for* the vault. Whoever opens the vessel opens everything.

Vessic did not need to catch Lior to drink the jars.

He needed only to make sure she was standing in the right room when the bells rang.

Iven gripped the rail until the iron bit his palms, and far below him, the ember-light pulsed—once, twice—like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

Three days. One bell in the vault. And somewhere in those winding dark corridors, a girl with an empty mind and a full heart was already moving toward the one room in the mountain where the end of the world was scheduled to begin.

He pushed off from the rail and went to find her before the mountain did.
