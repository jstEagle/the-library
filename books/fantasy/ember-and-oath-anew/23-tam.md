## 23. Tam

The dark beneath the mountain was not the dark of night. Night had edges—a horizon, a ceiling of stars, the soft gray promise of morning at its hem. This dark had no hem. It pressed against Lior's eyes like water against glass, and it breathed.

She walked with one hand on the wall, because the wall was the only honest thing down here. Everything else—the corridors that curved when they should have run straight, the doors without hinges, the whispering air that seemed to know her name before she'd spoken it—everything else lied by existing.

*Left at the third arch,* Iven had told her before they split apart in the antechamber, his voice pitched low enough that only she could hear it over the drone of the court's machinery. *Then follow the ember-light. It will know you.*

*It will know me.* She had almost laughed. Nothing knew her here; she was a blank page walking, a girl whose mind had been scoured smooth as river stone so that no interrogator could read what wasn't there. Sable's theft had taken the road from her, the cities, the burning at Corren's Landing, Dara's steady hands, Iven's bitter scholarship, the campfire where she had said the word *family* out loud for the first time since Tam died—or didn't die, or whatever the truth of him was. The memories were gone, sealed away in a thief's careful hands, and what remained was purpose without texture: *find the jar, free the soul, get out.*

Except now, standing in the third archway with ember-light bleeding up through cracks in the floor like veins of fire in black stone, Lior felt something else stirring under the blankness. Not memory. Memory was gone. This was older than memory—older than the valley, maybe. A pull, low in her chest, like a second heartbeat trying to sync itself with hers.

The ember-light thickened as she walked. It came up through the floor in branching rivers, and where the branches converged, the stone grew warm beneath her bare feet—she had lost her boots somewhere above, during the descent through the labor tunnels, and hadn't noticed until Dara pointed at the blood. Her own blood. She had walked miles on cut feet and felt nothing, because feeling required a self to feel with, and hers was in storage.

*Follow the light. It will know you.*

It did. That was the terrible part. Every fork in the tunnel, the brightest branch bent toward her, leaning like wheat toward wind, and every time she chose wrong the light dimmed behind her with something that felt unmistakably like disappointment.

The corridor opened.

Lior stopped breathing.

It was a cathedral. There was no other word her emptied mind could find for it—a vaulted cavern vast enough to swallow the whole guildhall, the whole valley, the whole sky she couldn't see anymore. Pillars of natural rock rose into darkness overhead, wrapped in spiraling veins of ember-glow, and between the pillars, shelf upon shelf upon shelf, rising hundreds of feet into the black—

Jars.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Clay vessels the size of a fist, the size of a skull, the size of a child curled small, each one stoppered with wax and iron, each one lit from within by a soft gold pulse. They sat in rows like eggs in an impossible nest, like fruit on an orchard's shelves, like books in a library where every book was a person.

And the light inside them—Lior's hand went to her wrist without deciding to, pressing against the ember-mark there, the brand she'd carried all her life and never questioned until Iven went white looking at it. The jars pulsed in the same rhythm. The same color. The same warmth she had tended every Founding Festival in the Hollow Valley, kneeling before the guild's own modest row of ash-jars, believing—with reverence, with love—that she was keeping magic safe from the world.

They hadn't been hiding it. They had been storing it. And the storehouse was here, and it was this, and it was *full*.

Her fury arrived before her fear did. It came up through her like the floor-light, branching, filling channels she hadn't known were carved in her. Somewhere above, Vessic's garrison marched; somewhere below, Iven was trading secrets with a man he called mentor; somewhere in the middle of it all, twenty years of stolen souls sat in clay and waited for a king to drink them. And the valley—the valley that had raised her, fed her, sworn its gentle oath over her head every single year—had drawn its invisible borders with power siphoned from *this*. From people. From children, some of these jars were the size of children.

"Careful," said a voice behind her. "Fury is loud, down here. The jars hear it."

Lior spun. Her hand found the knife at her belt—Dara's spare, pressed into her palm at the gate with a look that had meant more than any of the words Sable had taken from her.

The broker stood at the mouth of a side corridor, and he was not what she expected, which she supposed was the point of brokers. He was small. Old in the way of things that had never been young—smooth-faced, white-haired, with eyes the pale gray of ash after the fire goes out. He wore court silks gone shabby at the cuffs, and he held a ledger under one arm the way Maren used to hold hers, and that resemblance made Lior's stomach turn over.

"You're the broker," she said. "Iven's—"

"Mentor. Yes. He'll be along shortly with his pockets full of secrets, buying us time we may or may not spend wisely." The old man drifted closer, and the jar-light leaned toward him too, but differently than it leaned toward her—warily, the way animals lean from a whip. "You're the merchandise. The empty-headed girl the warden waved through. Though I confess," and here he smiled, and the smile had too many teeth in it for so mild a face, "the warden reads surface-thoughts, dear child. Surface. He saw nothing because there was nothing on top. He did not think to ask what might be underneath."

Lior's grip tightened on the knife. "Where is he?"

"Who?"

"You know who." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, hated the way grief found the gaps in her even with the memories scraped out. "Tam. Tam Hartwell. His soul was sold here twenty years ago. Sold by my own—" She couldn't finish. The word *guild* stuck in her throat like a fishbone.

"Ah." The broker set his ledger down on a shelf, gently, the way one sets down a sleeping infant. "The Hartwell transaction. Yes. One of my favorites, if a broker may be permitted favorites. Such clean paperwork. Such a cooperative seller. Elder Maren, wasn't it? She wept beautifully."

"Don't." The knife was up now. She didn't remember raising it. "Don't you say her name."

"I say everyone's name. It's my trade." He gestured, unhurried, at the endless shelves. "Every jar has a name written on the wax. Names are how we keep them. A soul without its name forgets it was ever anything but light." He began to walk, and though every instinct screamed at her not to follow, Lior followed—because he was walking deeper into the cathedral, and deeper was the only direction Tam could be. "Row nine hundred and forty-one. The Hartwell jar. You'll want to see it, I imagine. Siblings always do. We get very few visitors, you know. The families prefer to pretend their sold ones are dead. Dead is cleaner. Dead doesn't sit in a jar two hundred feet underground, glowing."

The rows scrolled past. Lior read names as she went, because she couldn't help it—names inked on wax seals in a spidery court hand. *Wren. Ollivan. Maren-who-is-not-that-Maren. Sweetmilk. A name that was just a string of numbers. A name that was just the word please, written over and over until it filled the seal.* Her eyes burned. The blankness where her journey-memories should have been ached like a pulled tooth; she could feel the shape of what was missing, the way a tongue finds a gap. Sable had those shapes safe. Sable would give them back. Lior clung to that the way a drowning woman clings to a rope she cannot see.

"Here," said the broker.

Row nine hundred and forty-one was shorter than the others, tucked into an alcove where two pillars met, and the jar on its shelf was smaller than most. Fist-sized. The clay was old, fired in a kiln style Lior half-recognized from the guild's oldest pieces, and the wax seal was yellowed with age, and the name on it was written in a hand that shook:

*Tammas Hartwell.*

Lior reached for it with both hands.

And stopped.

Because the jar was cold.

Every other vessel in the cathedral had radiated its small warm sun, had pulsed its slow heartbeat into the stone, had leaned its light toward her like a flower toward noon. This jar gave off nothing. She lifted it—it weighed nothing, less than the bread she'd once stolen in a city whose name lived in Sable's head now—and held it to the ember-light, and the light passed straight through the clay as though the clay were glass, as though the jar were glass, as though the jar were *empty*.

"No," Lior said.

The wax seal was intact. Unbroken. Twenty years old and unbroken, and inside it, nothing. No glow. No pulse. No soul.

"No, no, no—" She turned the jar over, pressed it against her ear as if a soul might make a sound, shook it gently, desperately, the way you shake a lantern whose flame has guttered. Empty. Empty empty empty. Cold clay and old wax and a shaking hand's inscription and nothing, nothing inside.

"He's not here." Her voice came out very small in the vast dark. "He's supposed to be here. They sold him *here*. Maren said—I read the page, I read the ledger, it says Undercourt, it says received, it says—"

"It does say received." The broker had not moved. He stood at the mouth of the alcove with his ash-gray eyes bright and his many-toothed smile widening, and Lior understood, in the last moment before he spoke, that she had made a mistake coming down here alone, that Iven's bargain had bought time but not safety, that the warden's wave and the empty mind and the whole long descent had been watched, catalogued, permitted. "Received, sealed, shelved. All true. I am a scrupulous record-keeper, child. I have never lost a soul in my life."

He spread his small, elegant hands.

"Souls do leave, occasionally. On their own legs."

The floor dropped away.

Not literally—though later, when the memories came back to her, Lior would swear she felt the whole cathedral tilt, the ten thousand jars swaying in their shelves like lanterns in a storm. The broker's words went through her and kept going, out the far side, and what they left behind was a silence so complete she could hear her own heart stuttering, could hear the faint wet sound of her breath, could hear—faintly, terribly—the ember-mark on her wrist, humming.

"What," she whispered, "did you say?"

"The Hartwell soul was never content in clay." The broker spoke the way Maren spoke when reciting the annual oath—reverent, practiced, enjoying every syllable. "Some souls burn too hot for their vessels. We had intended, of course, to transfer him eventually. A larger jar. A finer one. But souls are impatient, and ours are especially so, and there is an art—an *old* art—to binding a soul into living flesh. Flesh keeps them warmer. Fresher. Far more portable." He tilted his head, studying her with the frank appraisal of a merchant examining goods. "A vessel with legs can walk wherever the court requires. A vessel with a face can pass among the gifted unnoticed. A vessel with a *family*—"

"Stop."

"—can be placed exactly where the court wants it, and no one will ever—"

"*Stop!*" The knife-hand came up and she didn't even know what she meant to do with it, slash him or drop it or drive it into her own chest, because the thing he was describing was impossible, it was monstrous, it was—

It was her.

The mark on her wrist. The mark only Undercourt vessels bore—Iven's words, spoken in a smuggler's den in a city whose name she couldn't recall, his face gone gray as he took her arm and turned it to the light. The trick she hadn't known she knew, the first mile outside the valley, magic rushing into her dangerous and loud and *practiced*, ducking a patrol with movements that belonged to someone else's muscle. The compass in her pack that had never once pointed north. The way the ember-light down here leaned toward her, knew her, *recognized* her. The hollow where her memories should have been—not just the ones Sable took. Older hollows. Whole missing years. A brother whose face she had never been able to picture no matter how hard she tried, a brother she had grieved for like a stranger, a brother whose name she'd had to learn from a ledger page—

Twenty years ago, a soul was sold beneath the mountain.

Nineteen years ago, a baby was born in the Hollow Valley, and Elder Maren held her at the Founding Festival and wept, and no one ever told Lior why.

"No," she said again, but it was a different *no* now, thinner, cracking down the middle. "No. I'm Lior. I'm Lior Hartwell, I'm the youngest keeper of the Ember Guild, I have a mother, I had a mother, I have—" She groped for the facts of herself and found them slippery, found them thin as the wax on a seal, found that every fact she owned had come from someone else's mouth. *You were born in the valley. You've never left. Your brother died of fever when you were small.* Said by Maren. Said by the guild. Written in ledgers she had never been allowed to touch.

"Are you?" the broker asked softly. "How odd. Neither do our records say so."

He picked up his ledger from the shelf beside him and opened it, unhurried, and turned it toward her, and there on the page in that same spidery court hand was an entry dated nineteen years past, and Lior read it because her eyes betrayed her, because reading was the one skill no one could take:

*Hartwell, T.—transferred from jar 941 to living vessel per contract addendum. Vessel designated: H., L. Disposition: north, to the valley. Status: dormant. Recall condition: midwinter, or upon the drinking of the jars.*

Below it, in fresher ink, a single line had been added within the year:

*Recall imminent.*

The jar slipped from Lior's fingers.

It struck the stone floor and did not break—of course it didn't break, it was already broken, it had been empty for nineteen years—and it rolled, wobbling, in a lazy circle, and came to rest against her bare foot, and the name on its wax seal stared up at her from the ground.

*Tammas Hartwell.*

She looked down at it. She looked at her wrist, where the ember-mark pulsed in time with ten thousand jars. She looked at her hands, which were shaking, which had always been good with fire, which had never once been burned.

"There she is," the broker murmured, with something horribly like tenderness. "There's the Hartwell soul's sister. Or perhaps—" and the smile again, the teeth again, "—there's the Hartwell soul's *keeper*. Nineteen years in a valley that magic cannot enter, kept safe from knowing, kept sweet and sealed and dormant, waiting for the king to come collect. Do you know, child, you are the single most valuable object in this entire court? More than the crown. More than the throne. Vessic would burn every city in the free lands to possess you, and here you walked in through the front gate with an empty head and a knife you don't know how to use."

Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring.

The broker's head lifted. His smile faded—for the first time, truly faded—and Lior saw underneath it something she recognized from her own mirror: fear.

"Midwinter," he said. "Early. The bells aren't due for three days." He snapped the ledger shut. "Your mage's bargain just expired, little vessel. And your friends—" he glanced toward the corridor Iven had taken, then back at her, and his ash-gray eyes were almost pitying, "—your friends are about to learn what the court does with trespassers. Run if you like. But I'd see the vault first, if I were you. Down the ember-vein, past the ninth pillar. There's a corridor of mirrors there, and one of them has been waiting nineteen years to show you your brother's face."

He bowed—actually bowed, silk and ceremony, a merchant seeing off his finest customer—and withdrew into the dark, and left her alone in the cathedral of stolen souls with an empty jar at her feet and a bell tolling midwinter above her head and a single vein of ember-light glowing at the alcove's far end, brighter than all the rest, bending toward her like a hand reaching out.

Like a hand saying *come*.

Lior looked at the jar one last time. Then she stepped over it, and followed the light down.

* * *

The corridor of mirrors was colder than the cathedral, and quieter, and wrong in a way her skin understood before her mind did. The ember-vein ran along the floor and climbed the walls, and set into the walls, framed in black iron, hung mirrors—dozens of them, tall as doorways, their surfaces fogged with age except one.

That one was clear. That one was waiting.

Lior approached it slowly, knife forgotten, breath clouding in the freezing air, and the figure in the glass approached her step for step—a thin girl with cut bare feet and ash-brown hair and her mother's jaw, wearing the face she had worn every day of her life without once wondering whose it really was.

She stopped. The reflection stopped.

And then, in the space of a single heartbeat, the reflection smiled—and it was not her smile. It was wider, crooked, a boy's grin breaking across a girl's borrowed face, and the eyes looking back at her out of her own features were not her eyes at all. They were amber. They were laughing. They were the exact color of the ember-light in ten thousand jars, and they crinkled at the corners with a joy so familiar it tore her open, because she had never seen them before and she had missed them her whole life.

"Hi, Lior," said her brother, in a voice like her own voice played backward and made new. "Took you long enough."

Behind her, far back down the corridor, boots rang on stone—many boots, running, and a voice she knew was shouting her name, a voice that lived in a memory she couldn't reach, and the mirrors on either side began, one by one, to glow.
