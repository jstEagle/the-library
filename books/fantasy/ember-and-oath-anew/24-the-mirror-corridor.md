## 24. The Mirror Corridor

The ember-mark woke before she did.

Lior had been sleeping in a niche between two jar-shelves, curled like a child in the crook of the Undercourt's endless architecture, when the heat bloomed at her wrist and pulled her upright out of a dream she could not remember. The mark had been quiet since they'd entered the mountain—quiet the way a held breath is quiet—and now it burned with a patience that frightened her more than pain would have. It was not calling for help. It was calling *her*.

She sat in the dark and listened to the cathedral breathe.

That was the only word for it. The vaulted spaces above and below took air in through vents she would never find and let it out again slow and warm, carrying the smell of hot stone and tallow and something sweeter beneath—something like the ash-jars back home, like the guild's hearth on Founding morning. Every corridor here smelled faintly of that sweetness. She had told herself it was coincidence. She was running out of coincidences.

*The valley's magic wasn't banished,* she thought, and the thought still made her sick. *It was harvested.* Twenty years of the Hollow Valley's silence, twenty years of the guild's gratitude, and all of it bought with a soul that sat somewhere in this mountain, glowing in a jar exactly like the ten thousand jars around her, each one lit by ember-light identical to the light she had tended every morning of her life.

She had come to find Tam's jar. That was the whole of her purpose, worn smooth by the road like a river stone: find the jar, break it, bring him home. Everything else—the burning cities, Sable's traded memories, Dara's unfinished list, Iven's careful lies—had been weather around that stone.

She stood. The mark pulled gently, insistently, westward and down.

* * *

She left no note. There was no one left to note. Iven was still deep in his negotiations with the broker, trading secrets like a man spending coins he didn't own; Dara was mapping guard rotations with a stolen charcoal stub and the flat concentration of a woman building an escape route out of arithmetic; Sable was—Sable was wherever Sable went, moving through the Undercourt's corridors as if memory itself had taught her their grammar. Lior had meant to tell them. She had opened her mouth twice and closed it twice, because what was there to say? *My wrist is pulling me somewhere and I intend to follow it alone into the dark.*

They would have come anyway. That was the problem. And some animal part of her, some part older than the road and older than the guild, wanted to face whatever waited at the end of the pull without witnesses.

The corridors changed as she descended.

Above, the Undercourt had been grand in the way of things built to be seen: pillars carved with the names of brokers dead five hundred years, floors of black marble polished to hold the ember-light like water holds stars. Here, three levels down past the great vaults, the grandeur thinned. The stone went rougher. The jars grew sparser, then stopped altogether, and the absence of them was its own kind of loudness—after hours among ten thousand small lights, a dark corridor felt like deafness.

But the mark kept burning, and the sweetness in the air thickened until she could taste it on her tongue, honey and smoke, the exact smell of the guild's oath-fire.

Then she saw the mirrors.

The first one hung where a torch should have been—a tall oval of glass in a frame of tarnished silver, mounted on the wall and lit from within by no source she could name. Her reflection looked back at her, pale and road-worn, hair escaping its braid in gray-brown wisps, the ember-mark visible even through her sleeve because the sleeve had burned away days ago in Corren's Landing and she had never replaced it. She looked thin. She looked tired. She looked, she thought, exactly like herself, which after everything was almost a comfort.

Ten paces on, another mirror. Then another. Then a dozen, then dozens, lining both walls of the descending corridor so that walking between them was like walking through a crowd of herself, every pane catching her at a slightly different angle, a slightly different moment. The corridor curved, and the mirrors curved with it, and the ember-light within them shifted from white to gold to the deep orange of banked coals.

Her reflections began to move wrong.

Not much. Not at first. A half-step of lag, as if the glass were thinking before it answered. When she raised her right hand, the woman in the third mirror raised hers a heartbeat late. When she stopped, the woman in the seventh mirror kept swaying, just slightly, like someone standing on a deck at sea.

Lior pressed her back against the cold stone between two frames and made herself breathe.

*Iven said,* she thought. *Iven said vessels bear the mark. Iven said the mark is not given, it is grown.* And she had refused it, had refused it with her whole body, because the alternative was unthinkable—not painful, not frightening, but *unthinkable*, a thought the mind slid off of like rain off oiled leather. She was Lior Hartwell. She was Maren's student and the youngest keeper of the Ember Guild. She had a mother's laugh in her memory and a brother's absence in it and a valley that had raised her like its own crop. Whatever she was, she was *herself*. The world had confirmed it daily for twenty years.

But the world had also confirmed, daily, that magic could not enter the Hollow Valley. And the world had lied about that too.

"Keep walking," she said aloud, and her voice came back to her from forty mirrors at once, forty small ghosts saying *keep walking, keep walking,* and none of them sounded afraid, which was its own kind of lie.

She walked.

* * *

Deeper, the mirrors changed.

They stopped showing her the corridor. One by one—or so it seemed; she could not have sworn to the order—they began showing her other things. A kitchen with steam on the windows. A festival square strung with lanterns. A boy of maybe nine sitting on a fence rail, turning a brass compass over in his hands, squinting at the needle with the ferocious concentration of a child who has decided the needle owes him an answer.

Lior stopped so hard her boots skidded on the stone.

She knew that boy. Not the way you know a person. The way you know a scar you've never been able to explain—a shape in her that predated her memories, a warmth with no story attached. Maren had shown her a sketch once, in the terrible week after the ledger page, a drawing copied from some old guild record: *Tam Hartwell, aged six.* The boy in the mirror was older than six. But it was him. It was him the way her own reflection was her.

"Tam," she whispered.

In the glass, the boy looked up from the compass. Looked—at her? Past her? His mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the boundary, she knew the shape of the word. Everyone knew the shape of that word. It was the first word every child learned and the last word spoken over graves.

*Mama.*

The mirror dimmed. The next one brightened.

In it: a room she did not know, stone-walled, candle-lit, and a table, and on the table a contract written in ink so red it might have been fresh, and hands signing it—Maren's hands, older than Lior had ever known them, steady with the terrible steadiness of a person doing the worst thing they will ever do carefully. Across the table, a figure in broker's gray, its face lost in the shadow of its hood. Between them, held in a bowl of black glass, a light. Small. Gold. Flickering like a trapped moth.

The light in the bowl turned, somehow, toward her.

Lior's legs gave. She caught herself against the frame, and the silver was warm under her palms, warm like a living thing, and the mirror showed her now not the contract-room but her own face again—except it wasn't only her own face. The features were hers, and over them, through them, like sunlight through water, lay another face. Broader jaw. Darker eyes. A boy becoming a man becoming something ageless, superimposed on her skin as if her face were a window and he stood behind the glass, pressing close, looking out.

Looking out with her eyes.

"No," she said.

The word fell into the corridor and the corridor gave it back forty times, and every reflection said it with his mouth and hers at once.

"No. No, that's not—" She was shaking her head and all the reflections shook theirs, the lag gone now, perfectly synchronized, which was worse, infinitely worse, because synchronization meant they weren't lagging at all, meant the delay had never been in the glass, meant—

Meant the delay was in her.

She thought of the trick on the first mile outside the valley, the ward-bending she had never been taught, the knowledge that had simply been *in her hands* like water in a well she'd never dug. She thought of how the valley's wards had flickered when the crier came, as if recognizing something. She thought of Maren's face when Lior had demanded the truth—grief, yes, guilt, yes—but underneath both, a strange and awful *relief*, the look of a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

She thought of the way she had never, not once in twenty years, dreamed of her brother. Grieving children dream. The guild had told her grief simply didn't work that way in the valley, where forgetting was a feature of the stones themselves.

But she hadn't forgotten him.

She had *held* him.

The truth did not arrive like a blow. Blows end. It arrived like water rising in a locked room—slow, level, patient, climbing her body inch by inch while she stood too stunned to climb—and when it reached her heart she understood that it had always been rising, all her life, and that every strange thing she had ever been was only the surface of it breaking into ripples.

She was the vessel.

There was no jar in this mountain holding Tam Hartwell's soul. There was no jar because there was no need for one. The Undercourt had built its finest container out of flesh and named it a girl and carried it, weeping at her brother's grave-mound that wasn't a grave, into the one place in the world where nothing could ever call the soul home. She had not lost her memories of Tam. Her memories of Tam had been *made room for*, pruned back like an orchard, so that he could grow inside her undisturbed. She had tended the guild's ash-jars every morning of her life, reverent, gentle, learning the care of souls without knowing she was practicing on herself.

She had been both siblings all along. The sister who mourned and the brother who was mourned. The keeper and the kept. The girl who left the valley to rescue him and the boy who had never left her side, because there was no side of her he was not touching.

Lior Hartwell slid down the warm silver frame to the floor of the mirror corridor, and the forty reflections came down with her, and not one of them looked surprised.

* * *

How long she sat there, she could not say. Time in the deep places of the mountain moved like syrup. The ember-mark had stopped burning; it pulsed now, slow and steady, in time with her heart—or with his, she thought, and a sound came out of her that was half laugh and wholly broken.

When footsteps finally came, she knew them before she heard them. Sable walked like someone who had spent her life not being followed: silent on stone, weight always forward, a rhythm like a sentence that refuses to end. Lior watched her appear at the top of the corridor's curve, small and sharp-eyed, a knife already loose in her hand, and watched the knife lower as Sable read the scene—the mirrors, the girl on the floor, the forty faces looking up.

"You went off alone." Not a question. An accusation delivered softly, which was Sable's way. "Dara's going to be unbearable about this."

"Sable." Lior's voice came out sanded smooth, emptied. "Look at them. Tell me what you see."

Sable's eyes went to the nearest mirror, and Lior watched a memory-thief look at a truth she could not steal or sell. Watched her go very still, the stillness of a person standing at the edge of something with no bottom.

"That's not possible," Sable said. "Vessels are—" She stopped. Started again. "The old texts say a vessel burns out. Years, at most. They say the soul eats the life around it. They don't say—" Her hand rose, unbidden, toward the glass, toward the doubled face. "Twenty years. You've carried him twenty years and you're *alive*. You're more than alive. You're—" She looked at Lior with something close to awe, and awe on Sable's face was a frightening thing, because Sable had seen everything and priced most of it. "You're the strongest vessel anyone has ever made. Maybe the only one that works."

"Made." Lior laughed again, that broken half-sound. "Yes. Made. Like the jars. Like everything here." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, against the pulse that was hers and not hers. "Do you know what the worst part is? I'm not even surprised. Some part of me has known since the first mile outside the valley. Since before. Some part of me has been waiting my whole life for the rest of me to catch up."

Sable crouched beside her. Close enough that Lior could see the fine scars on her knuckles, the map of every trade she'd ever made written on her skin. "Listen to me," Sable said. "Whatever you're deciding right now—and you are deciding, I can see you deciding, you have your mother's stubborn chin and your brother's fatal eyes—whatever it is, wait. Wait for Iven. Wait for Dara. We didn't cross half the free cities so you could make the biggest choice of your life alone in a hallway full of haunted furniture."

"My whole life was the choice," Lior said. "It was made before I could walk. The only thing left to decide is whether I finish it."

"And what does finishing mean?"

Lior looked down the corridor, to where the mirrors thinned and the darkness gathered, to where the pull in her wrist pointed like a compass needle—like Tam's compass, like the needle a nine-year-old boy had once squinted at, certain it owed him an answer.

"It means opening," she said quietly. "And I don't think anything closes again after."

Far above them, muffled by a hundred feet of living rock, a bell began to ring. Once. Twice. A rhythm Lior did not know—but Tam knew it, some buried part of him knew it, and the knowledge surfaced through her like a fish rising: *midwinter bells. The sealing bells.*

The mountain was closing its gates. And somewhere above, an army was helping it.

Sable stood, and pulled Lior up, and for once did not let go.
