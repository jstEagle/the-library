## 25. Maren's Last Lie

The ledger burned the way all true things burn: slowly at first, then all at once.

Maren had carried it down from the archive at dusk, when the valley was already folding itself into lamplight and the smell of festival bread still lingered in the lanes from the morning's baking. She had carried it under her arm like a sleeping child, and she had thought, absurdly, of how many times she had carried it that way over the years—into council meetings, into oaths, into the small stone room where the guild recorded what it owed and what it had paid. Forty years of entries in her own hand. Forty years of ink.

She built the fire in the guild-yard herself, kneeling on cold flagstones with her bad hip complaining, stacking kindling the way her own elder had taught her when she was a girl no older than Lior Hartwell had been last spring. The wood caught. The light climbed the walls of the meetinghouse and turned the carved lintel—the old words, *We Keep What the World Cannot Hold*—into something molten and trembling.

The guild gathered without being summoned. That was the way of the Hollow Valley; word moved through it like water through loam, silent and inevitable, and by the time Maren stood with the ledger open on her knees, every keeper in the valley had come out into the cold. Old Wenna with her shawl pinned crooked. The twins, Brann and Colm, who had never once in their lives been seen apart until tonight, standing a careful arm's length from each other as if they already sensed this would be a night for standing alone. Young Pell, who kept the southern jars, his face still soft with the roundness of boyhood. And behind them, filling the lane beyond the yard, the ordinary folk of the valley—the farmers, the weavers, the children too curious to be sent to bed—all of them drawn by the same instinct that draws moths to a flame they cannot survive.

They knew something was wrong. The wards had flickered three nights past, and the crier's bell was still ringing in everyone's blood. They knew their elder had gone into the archive and not come out for two days. They knew, the way animals know weather, that the world outside their valley had turned its face toward them at last.

Maren opened the ledger to its first page and read aloud, because some things must be said in a voice before they can be believed.

"*In the year of the Long Frost, the Ember Guild of the Hollow Valley entered into covenant with the Court Beneath the Mountain. We gave them one soul, freely surrendered by its kin, and in exchange the Undercourt wove our wards: a country where magic cannot follow, where the gifted may live unregistered, unburned, unknown.*"

Her voice held. She was surprised by that. She had expected it to break on the first line, the way it broke every time she rehearsed this alone in her rooms, but there is a difference between speaking to walls and speaking to faces, and faces demand a steadiness that walls do not.

"*The name given was Tammas Hartwell. Age eleven years. The signature of consent is that of his mother, Elira Hartwell, who died of the grief within the season, as the covenant required her silence and grief does not keep silent.*"

Someone in the crowd made a sound—not a gasp, not a cry, something smaller and worse, like a stitch tearing.

Maren turned the page. The paper was old enough now to have gone the color of weak tea, and the ink had faded to brown, but she did not need to read it. She had written it. She had stood in this same yard forty years ago—no, twenty-two years ago, though some nights it felt like yesterday and other nights like another life entirely—and she had watched Elira Hartwell press her thumbprint into wax while her son slept in the next room, drugged with valerian tea so he would not have to be awake for what was done to him. Maren had held the lantern. She remembered the way the flame bent toward the door, toward the sleeping child, as if even fire grieved.

"The rest you know," she said. "Or you know the shape of it. A child was taken south beneath the mountain. His soul was poured out of him into a jar, and the jar became the seed of our protection, and we have lived safe inside that bargain ever since. Every ward that has kept the Gift Hunt from our door. Every winter we have passed unburned while the free cities burned. It has all been paid for." She lifted the ledger slightly, so the firelight caught the pages. "Paid for in advance. Paid for by a boy who never swore our oath."

Wenna stepped forward, her knuckles white around her walking stick. "Maren. Where is the jar? If the covenant holds, the jar is still—"

"The jar is empty, Wenna."

The words dropped into the yard like stones into a well, and for a long moment the only sound was the fire eating.

"Empty," Pell repeated. He was the youngest keeper after Lior, and he said it the way young men say things they hope will turn out to be a mistake if spoken plainly enough.

"Empty," Maren said again. "And I will tell you where it went, because you deserve the whole of it, and because I have spent twenty-two years telling pieces of it, and pieces are just lies arranged politely."

She closed the ledger. She looked at each of them in turn—the way an elder should, the way she had failed to do for two decades—and then she told them everything.

* * *

She told them about the vessel.

How the Undercourt's brokers had come back to the guild three years after taking Tam, when the war in the east was going badly and Vessic's predecessor was hungry for leverage. How they had offered a renewal of the covenant—a century of protection instead of twenty years—if the guild would agree to one further term. Not another soul. Something stranger. The brokers had explained it in their dry, courteous way, the way merchants explain interest rates: a soul removed from its body grows unstable in a jar. It thins. It frays at the edges like cloth rubbed against stone. But a soul bound into living flesh—a vessel grown around it, woven from the same blood, the same root—holds it whole. Holds it warm. Holds it *waiting*.

"They wanted a sister," Maren said. "A sibling of Tam's blood, conceived and carried for the purpose, bound before birth. And Elira—" Her voice did break here, finally, and she let it. "Elira was already dying of what she'd done. But she was still a mother, and a mother's consent was required, and she gave it. She said—I wrote her words down, I was so afraid of forgetting them, and I have never once forgotten them—she said, *'Then part of me gets to keep him. Then he won't be alone in the dark.'*"

She heard the crowd understand it before she said it. She saw it move through them like wind through wheat, heads turning, breath catching, eyes finding other eyes. Because everyone in the valley knew Lior Hartwell. Everyone had watched her grow up in the keeper's house with Maren herself, the orphaned daughter of a dead mother and a brother lost to fever—that was the story, the kind story, the lie worn smooth by twenty years of telling. Everyone had seen her tend the ash-jars with those careful hands. Everyone had noticed, and thought nothing of it, that the girl could stand closer to the jars than any keeper without flinching, that the ember-light seemed to lean toward her like a plant toward sun.

"She was born here," Maren said. "In the birthing room behind the meetinghouse, with two Undercourt midwives standing at the foot of the bed wearing borrowed faces, and I held Elira's hand and lied to her at the end, told her the binding hadn't taken, told her the brokers were furious, told her her daughter would be free. She died believing it. I have told myself for twenty-two years that the lie was mercy." She shook her head slowly. "It was not mercy. It was cowardice wearing mercy's clothes. The binding took. Of course it took. The brokers do not leave such things to chance. Lior Hartwell has carried her brother's soul inside her since before she drew her first breath, and she does not know it, and none of you knew it, because I made sure no one could know it."

"Why?" The voice came from the back of the crowd—one of the farmers, a broad quiet man named Aldous whose family had been in the valley six generations. "Why hide it from her? From us?"

"Because knowing is a wound," Maren said, "and because the covenant required it. Do you understand? That was the final term. The vessel must never know what she carries. A vessel that knows begins to strain against the binding; a vessel that knows can be found; a vessel that knows might, in grief or rage or love, open herself and pour him out—and then the wards fail, and the valley burns, and every soul in these ash-jars goes to the crown along with every living gifted person behind our borders. The brokers were very clear. So I raised her in ignorance. I taught her the oath. I put the compass in her hand—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again. "I gave her a life, and I built the life out of lies the way you build a wall out of stone, and I told myself every single day that it was protection. Hers. Yours. Everyone's."

"And now?" Wenna's voice was thin. "The crier came, Maren. The wards flickered. What happens now?"

"Now the covenant is breaking," Maren said. "Vessic has torn up every agreement his predecessors honored. The hunt is at our borders. And the girl—the vessel—is gone. She left four days ago, alone, on the south road, carrying a compass that points not north but toward the thing it was made to find." She looked down at the ledger in her hands. "I gave her the compass myself. I told myself it was a keepsake of her brother's. It was a leash. I thought—if she must go, better she go where the binding pulls, better she be found by the Undercourt than by the hunt, better the brokers hold her than Vessic. Twenty-two years of choosing which lie keeps people safest, and I chose one more."

The fire cracked. A knot of resin burst and sent sparks climbing into the dark, and for a moment the whole yard was lit gold, every face visible, every face stricken.

Brann spoke—or Colm; in the firelight even Maren could not tell them apart, and perhaps that was fitting. "You're burning the ledger," he said. "Why burn it now? Why not twenty years ago, or never?"

"Because a record is a confession that outlives the confessor," Maren said, "and because there are hunters in the world now who read. If Vessic's men take this valley, I will not leave them the map to what Lior is. And because—" She hesitated. This was the hardest part, harder than the vessel, harder than Elira's death, and she made herself say it plainly. "Because I am old, and I am tired, and I have discovered that a lie you maintain for twenty-two years becomes load-bearing. Take it out and you must see whether the house stands. I am burning the ledger so that I cannot rebuild the lie tomorrow. So that none of us can. Whatever comes next, we will build it on truth or not at all."

She held the book over the fire.

For a moment her arms would not obey. Forty years of entries. The founding hands of the guild, generations back, the first oath in a dead keeper's careful script. Names of the jarred. Dates of surrender. All the terrible arithmetic of survival, balanced year after year in her own steady hand. It was the only honest thing the guild owned, in its way—it had never pretended to be anything but what it was—and she was about to feed it to the flames, and some part of her screamed that this was desecration, that the dead deserved their record, that—

That Lior deserved it more. Lior, walking south alone with a hole in her memory shaped exactly like a brother, carrying the truth of him in her blood without one word of it in her mind.

Maren let the ledger fall.

It landed open across the kindling, pages fanning, and the fire took it greedily—the early pages first, the beautiful old script curling black, then her own entries, her own hand, her own long complicity rising in smoke. Sparks swarmed upward. The heat pushed against her face, and she stood in it and did not step back, because penance should hurt at least a little, and this was the least of what it should do.

The guild watched in silence. Behind them, the ordinary folk of the valley watched too, and among them Maren saw mothers pull children closer, and saw others—Aldous among them—stand a little straighter, as if something heavy had been set down that they had all been carrying without knowing its name. The valley had always felt safe. Now they knew the price of the safety, and strange to say, some of the faces showed not horror but a grim relief, the relief of a debt finally named.

When the last page curled to ash, Maren spoke again, and her voice was quieter now, almost gentle, the voice she had used over cradles and deathbeds for forty years.

"I do not ask your forgiveness. Forgiveness is Lior's to give, if she ever learns enough truth to know what was taken from her, and I doubt I will live to ask it. I ask something harder. I ask you to finish what I started badly." She turned to face them fully, this old woman with her burned book and her bad hip and her twenty-two years of careful lies. "The guild was founded to hide the gifted. Hiding has a price, and we have learned tonight that the price is always paid by someone who wasn't consulted—a boy in a jar, a girl born to be a box. I say the hiding ends. Not recklessly—we are not fools, and Vessic's armies are real—but ends. When the time comes, and it is coming, this valley will not buy its safety with anyone's soul again. Not a stranger's. Not a child's. Not mine. Swear it with me or tell me now, and I will step down and one of you will lead who can swear it clean."

No one spoke. The fire settled. Somewhere beyond the wardline, far off, thunder rolled across the mountains—or something that sounded like thunder, though the sky overhead was clear and salted with stars.

Then Wenna, oldest of them all, leaned her stick against the well and lowered herself—slowly, joints cracking—to her knees on the cold flagstones. Not to Maren. To the fire. To the ash of the ledger.

"To the Hartwells," she said. "Both of them."

And one by one, keepers and farmers and weavers and children too young to understand but old enough to feel the weight of a room—they knelt, or bowed their heads, or pressed their fists to their hearts in the old gesture of witness, and the yard filled with murmured names: *Tammas. Elira. Lior.*

Maren stood among them and did not kneel, because someone had to remain standing, someone had to keep watch, and because her knees had not yet finished their penance. She looked south, past the wardline, past the dark shoulders of the mountains, toward the road where a girl with a borrowed compass was walking toward the thing she had been made to be.

"Hold on, child," she whispered, too low for any of them to hear. "Hold on, both of you. We are coming to be honest with you at last—even if honesty kills us."

Behind her, the last scrap of the ledger's cover flared and died, and in the sudden darkness Maren saw it clearly: the ember-light of the nearest ash-jar, through the meetinghouse window, flickering. Once. Twice.

Like something answering.

Like something, far away beneath a mountain, beginning to wake.
