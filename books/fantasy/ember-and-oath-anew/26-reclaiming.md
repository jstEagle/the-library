## 26. Reclaiming

The mirror corridor let me out into a chamber shaped like the inside of a heart.

I don't mean that it was heart-shaped. I mean that everything about it—the vaulted ceiling ribbed with pale stone, the slow pulse of ember-light moving through channels cut into the walls, the warmth that pressed against my skin like something breathing—made me feel I had walked inside a living thing, and that the living thing knew me.

The vessel vault. Iven had described it in whispers back in Corren's Landing, before any of us knew what the word *vessel* would come to mean. A cathedral of jars, he'd said, each one holding a soul, tended by brokers who counted them like coins. But this room was different from the great hall where the jars hung in their hundreds like lanterns at a funeral. This room held nothing at all except a shallow basin of black glass at its center, ringed by seven pillars carved with names so old the letters had been worn smooth as river stones.

And the ember-mark on my wrist had begun to burn.

Not painfully. Not yet. It glowed through my skin the way a coal glows under ash, and when I lifted my arm, the light answered the pulse in the walls—*my* light, *the guild's* light, the same slow rhythm I had watched over every night of my life in the Hollow Valley while I turned the ash-jars and whispered the keeping-words. Twenty years of tending other people's souls in jars of ash, and all the while I had been the jar. The valley hadn't been hiding me. It had been *keeping* me, the way Maren kept the ledger, the way a cellar keeps wine—cool, dark, patient, until the day someone comes to drink.

I knelt at the basin because my legs would no longer hold me, and the mark flared brighter against the black glass, and in the glass I saw not my own face but Tam's—young, sharp-eyed, seventeen forever—and beneath his face, faint as breath on a window, mine.

"Hello," I said to him, because there was no one else to say it to. My voice cracked on the second syllable. "Hello, Tam. I'm your sister. I think—I think I've always known. Somewhere under everything."

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat learning my rhythm. Like an answer.

I had come down here with Sable's taken memories sitting hollow in me, with Dara's list of names folded against my heart, with Iven somewhere above bargaining his old sins for our lives, with the midwinter bells—hours away now, maybe less—waiting to seal the mountain and make every soul in it crown property forever. I had come down here to find a jar. Instead I had found a mirror, and in the mirror I had found myself, and the finding had broken something open that was never going to close again.

There was no jar to break. There never had been. To free Tam, I would have to break *me*.

---

I didn't decide right away. I want to be honest about that, whoever reads this, whoever I am afterward. I sat at the edge of the basin for a long time with my knees drawn up and the ember-light washing over me, and I tried to find a third door.

That was the trick of it, you see. All my life I had believed in doors. The valley taught it: there is always another way around the wall, another path through the trees, another oath you can swear that costs a little less. Maren's whole guild was built on the idea that safety could be negotiated, that price and protection could be weighed on scales and balanced. And maybe that was true once. Maybe it had even been true the night they sold my brother.

But there was no third door here. I felt it in the mark on my wrist the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. The binding between Tam's soul and my body was woven through every year of me—not just my memories of him, which were thin enough already, but everything. The taste of the valley's bread on festival mornings. The weight of the compass in my palm. Sable's laugh, low and crooked, in the dark of a stolen barn. Iven saying *you are more than what was done to you*, and meaning it. Dara standing between us and the hounds with her bow lowered and her jaw set like a woman deciding her whole life in one breath.

If I opened the vessel, all of it poured out with him. He would take back his own—the years of him that lived in me, the shape of his soul that my body had been grown around—and my memories would go with them, because you cannot untangle a thread from a weaving without unraveling the cloth. The broker had said it plainly, gloating: *the vessel empties or the vessel breaks, little keeper. Those are the only two endings ever sold.*

Emptied. That was the word that kept circling back to me. Not dead. Emptied. A girl who woke up knowing how to speak and walk and fear, but not knowing why the strangers weeping over her called her Lior Hartwell. Not knowing why the compass in her pocket made her chest ache. Not knowing she had ever been loved.

And still.

Still, my brother had spent twenty years as a prisoner in his own sister's body, and he had never once, in all those years, pushed. Never fought me for the wheel. Never crowded my dreams or soured my food or whispered in the spaces of my thinking. He had just—waited. Quietly. The way the guild kept the ash-jars. The way the valley kept me. Everyone in my life, it seemed, had learned the same terrible skill: how to be kept.

"I'm sorry," I told the light. "I'm sorry it took me so long. I'm sorry they did it at all."

The pulse stuttered—grief or gratitude or both—and I stopped waiting for a third door.

---

The ritual was not written anywhere. That surprised me, at first, and then it didn't. Of course there were no instructions. No one had ever meant for a vessel to open herself. The binding was designed by people who thought of souls as stock, who built vaults and ledgers and locks, who never imagined that the jar might have opinions. So I did what the guild had taught me without teaching me—the thing underneath all the oaths and the keeping-words, the thing Elder Maren had pressed into my hands on the night she gave me the compass and told me the road remembers what the valley cannot hold.

I listened to the ember.

I pressed both palms flat to the black glass of the basin and let the mark burn, and instead of flinching I leaned into it, the way you lean into a wind to keep from being knocked down. The light climbed my arms like ivy finding a trellis. Warmth spread through my ribs, my throat, the soft architecture behind my eyes, and in that warmth I felt him—not as a voice, not as words, but as a direction. South-and-down, the way a compass needle feels true. Toward himself. Out of me.

"You have to pull," I whispered. "I can't do it alone. I'll hold the door. You walk through."

The heat gathered. It gathered the way grief gathers, the way a wave gathers over a drowning sailor—all at once and slowly, impossibly both—and I felt the first thread begin to move.

It was small. A smell, really: woodsmoke and snow, the particular cold-clean smell of the Hollow Valley in early winter, the smell of the night Maren had put the compass in my hand. I felt it loosen from the rest of me like a leaf letting go of its branch, drift toward the light pooling in the basin, and dissolve. And where it had been, there was—a smoothness. A place where a memory had been, worn now to polished blankness, like the worn names on the pillars.

Oh, I thought. Oh, it doesn't hurt. That's worse somehow. It doesn't hurt at all.

The next thread was bigger. Maren's face, the first time I could remember seeing it clearly—wrinkled, fierce, kind, lit by hearthlight as she taught me the keeping-words. It went, and the hearth went with it, and then the guildhall went, and the long table where the elders sat, and the sound of the annual oath rising from thirty throats in the dark—

I gripped the edge of the basin hard enough to hurt. *Hold the door,* I told myself. *Just hold the door.*

Thread after thread. Festival lanterns. The taste of honey-bread. My mother's hands—I hadn't known I remembered my mother's hands until they left me, and then their absence was a wound shaped exactly like hands. The first time I saw the mountains beyond the valley's rim and felt the restlessness that had lived in me ever since, the restlessness that had never been mine at all but his, leaking through the walls of the vessel for twenty years, looking for a road.

And then—newer things. Brighter things. Things that had happened only weeks ago and burned going out like green wood.

Sable crouched over a stolen loaf in a market alley, grinning at me with crumbs on her chin, saying, *You steal like you were born rich—everything at once and apologizing to nobody.* Gone. The alley went gray. I felt the name *Sable* start to slide, and I grabbed at it, and the grabbing itself was like catching smoke.

No. *No.* Keep that one. Keep her.

But the ritual did not negotiate. The light took what the light took, and I understood, kneeling there with my fingers white on the basin's rim, that this was the real price—not death, not pain, but this: the slow subtraction of everyone who had ever made me *me*. I had thought I was surrendering my memories of Tam. I had not understood that Tam and I were braided into everything, that pulling him out pulled the color out of the whole cloth.

The bells, I thought wildly. The midwinter bells will ring and I won't know what they're ringing for.

Another thread: Dara's arrow splitting the air a hand's width from my shoulder, deliberate, perfect, a miss that saved my life. Gone. The gray spread.

Another: Iven's voice cracking in the smuggler's den as he read the mark on my wrist. *Only vessels bear it. Only the Undercourt makes them.* Gone.

Another—

Boots. Running boots, many of them, hammering down the mirror corridor behind me, and voices—voices I knew, though the knowing was already fraying at its edges, and I clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to a rope.

"Lior!" Sable's voice, ragged, echoing off the ribbed stone. "Lior, stop—*stop!*"

I didn't stop. I couldn't. Half-open, I was dying either way now; the binding had torn loose from its moorings and the light was pouring through me whether I willed it or not, and all I could do was hold the door steady so he could walk out clean.

They burst into the chamber the way weather bursts through a broken window—Dara first, sword drawn, scanning the pillars for threats; Iven behind her, robes scorched, a line of blood at his hairline, his hands already weaving some warding-shape that died half-formed when he saw me; Sable last, sliding on her knees across the last few feet, reaching for me.

I was glowing. I understood that distantly, from the look on their faces. The ember-mark had spread past my wrist, up the veins of my forearm, across my collarbones, threads of gold-white fire visible through my skin, and every pulse of it shook loose another memory and sent it streaming into the basin, where it burned like offerings.

"What did you do?" Sable's hands closed on my shoulders—warm, real, shaking. Her face swam in front of mine, and I watched it happen: the memory of her name loosened, drifted, dissolved. I held onto her *face*. Faces lasted longest. "Lior. Look at me. What is this? What are you doing?"

"Tam," I said. It came out as almost nothing, a thread of sound. "He's in me. He was always in me. I'm the jar, Sable. I'm the jar and I'm opening it."

"No." Flat. Immediate. The word of a woman who stole things for a living and knew a theft when she saw one. "No, absolutely not, there's another way, there's always—we'll find—"

"There isn't." I smiled at her. It was strange, smiling. My face was doing it mostly on its own. "I looked. There's no third door."

"Lior." Iven dropped to his knees on my other side, and his hands hovered over the light in my veins without touching it, the way you hover near a fire. His eyes were wet and furious. "Listen to me. I served that court for fifteen years. I studied vessel-craft under the broker himself. If you empty yourself here, you will wake up a stranger in your own skin. You will not know us. You will not know *yourself*. Is that—do you understand what you're giving—"

"I understand." The words cost me something; I felt the understanding itself tremble, ready to be taken. "Iven. Iven, listen. He waited twenty years. He never once pushed. Do you know what that—" The word *cost* vanished from me. I stumbled, rephrased. "Do you know what that is worth? I can't leave him in here. I won't be the vault. I won't be the ledger. I'm done being the thing that holds people so other people can sleep at night."

"That's not fair," Dara said hoarsely. She had not knelt. She stood at the basin's far side with her fists clenched, and her eyes were red-rimmed, and her voice was the voice of a woman reading a list. "You're talking about the guild. You're talking about Maren. Fine. Be angry. But we're not the guild. We're not a price. We came down here *for you*—"

"You came down here for a girl who's about to stop existing," I said gently. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorrier than I know how to say, and I'd say it better if I could remember how we—" Another thread. Another gray patch. I laughed, a small cracked sound. "You see? It's already starting. If you're going to argue, argue faster."

"We're not arguing," Sable said, "we're *begging*." And then she did beg—she, the proudest thief in three cities, the woman who had traded her own childhood piece by piece and sworn never to be owned again—she bent her forehead to my glowing hands and begged. "Don't go where I can't follow. Don't make me the one who remembers everything. I can't carry it all, Lior. I've carried too much already. Don't add you to it."

For one moment—one whole heartbeat—I nearly broke. The door in me shuddered. Tam's soul paused in its pouring-out, and I felt him feel it too, felt his hesitation like a hand hovering over a latch: *if you want to stop, sister, say it now and I will go back and wait another twenty years and hate myself quietly, because you are the only family I have ever had and I will not take you unwillingly.*

That undid me more than anything. That he would go back. That he would choose the jar rather than take what wasn't freely given.

"He offered to stay," I told them. My voice came out steadier than anything I'd felt all day. "Did you hear me? He offered to *stay*. In the dark. For me. And you're asking me to let him."

"We're asking you to live," Dara said.

"This is living." I turned my palms upward in Sable's grip, let her see the light running through me, unafraid now, or afraid and past it—there wasn't much difference anymore. "This is the only version of living I've got. You want to know the truth? Everything good in me—every stubborn thing, every restless thing, every time I chose the harder kinder road—it was him, bleeding through. And everything good in *him* has been kept alive in *me*. We're not two people, we never got to be two people, and the cruelest thing anyone ever did to either of us was make us one. Let me unmake it. Let us both be somebody. Even if the somebody I turn out to be doesn't remember your faces."

"She won't remember our faces," Iven said to the others, quiet and terrible, confirming it like a physician confirming a death. "Anything she builds after tonight will be new. Every bond, rebuilt from nothing. If it can be rebuilt."

"Then it will be rebuilt." Sable lifted her head. Tears cut clean lines down her dusty face, but her eyes had gone hard and bright, the eyes she wore over locked doors and impossible odds. "You hear me, Lior Hartwell? Whatever you pour out tonight, I'm keeping copies. I'm a thief. It's what I *do*. You emptied yourself into the wrong room if you thought nobody in it would rob you back." She laughed, wet and wild. "Every fragment. Every scrap. I'll steal you home piece by piece if it takes the rest of my life, and I won't ask permission, and you can't stop me, so you may as well get on with it."

And that—*that*—was the moment the choice crystallized, the way frost crystallizes on a window, all at once, beautiful and irreversible. Because I looked at her tear-streaked, furious, magnificent face, and I understood that whatever I lost tonight, I had already won something that could survive losing. They would not let me vanish. They would stand at the door of my emptiness and refuse it entry. The girl who woke tomorrow might not know them—but they would know *her*, and they would tell her, and tell her again, and again, until the story took root.

"Then hold the door with me," I whispered.

They didn't understand at first. Then they did. Dara sheathed her sword and knelt at last, taking my left hand. Iven set aside his half-woven ward and took my right, murmuring the old court-forms of witness, the words that made a thing *true* in the sight of law and magic alike. Sable pressed her brow back to my knuckles and held on.

Above us—far above, through a mile of stone—the first bell began to toll.

Midwinter. Vessic's army sealing the gates. The deadline arriving like a blade coming down.

The light surged. Tam rose through me like dawn through water, and the last thing I remember choosing to remember was all four of us, kneeling in the heart of the enemy's house, holding the door together.

Then the memories went out of me like birds going out of a burning tree, thousands upon thousands, winging home—and the bells rang, and rang, and rang.
