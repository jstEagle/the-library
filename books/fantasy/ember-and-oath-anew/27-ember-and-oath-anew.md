## 27. Ember and Oath Anew

The bells began at dusk.

Lior felt them before she heard them—a tremor in the stone beneath her knees, a shiver running up through the vault floor like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the sound arrived, rolling down through a hundred fathoms of rock, deep and bronze and final: the midwinter bells of the Undercourt, rung not for worship but for sealing.

*The gates are closing,* she thought. *Above us, right now, Vessic's soldiers are swinging the great doors shut.*

Somewhere overhead—she could not see it, could only imagine it—the last grey light of the shortest day was dying on the snowfields of the pass. Somewhere up there Dara's deserters were still fighting their retreat toward the lower galleries, ballistae screaming, the king's guard pouring down the stairways like black water. Somewhere Iven was holding a door with words instead of hands, his voice raw from bargaining and then from battle. Somewhere Sable was running toward her through corridors that ate light, carrying everything Lior had given her to keep.

But here, in the vessel vault, there was only stillness.

The room was round as a held breath. Its walls were mirror-silver from floor to unseen ceiling, and every surface showed her—not one reflection but dozens, hundreds, a congregation of Lior Hartwells kneeling in a circle of ember-light, each one wearing Tam's eyes.

She had stopped flinching at that. It had taken the whole walk down the Mirror Corridor, one reflection after another peeling back her face to show the boy beneath it, but she had stopped flinching. That was its own kind of grief—to grow accustomed to your own erasure—and she carried it now like a stone sewn into her coat.

In the center of the vault stood the plinth. On the plinth lay the ember-mark's source: a shallow bowl of black glass, older than the guild, older than the valley, its rim carved with an oath in a language no one living spoke. The broker's chains had been struck from it—Iven's doing, paid for with secrets Lior didn't want to know—and now it waited, patient as a grave, for what she had come to give.

Her own self.

*You don't have to,* Sable had said, gripping her shoulders in the corridor, hard enough to bruise. *There's another way. There's always another way, we'll find it, we'll—*

*There isn't,* Lior had said, and kissed her friend's forehead, and walked on.

Now she knelt alone, and the bells rang again, and she made herself remember.

That was the cruelty of it, she thought. To open the vessel she had to be full. Every memory she meant to lose, she had to hold up to the light first, one by one, like a keeper counting jars. So she counted.

The Hollow Valley, green and dim under its ward-haze, where magic pooled away from everything like water from oil. The ash-jars lined along the guild cellar walls, each one warm as a sleeping bird against her palms. Elder Maren's hands, spotted and steady, teaching her the turning-rite. The smell of woodsmoke and turned earth and bread. The Founding Festival lanterns rising over the terraces while the whole valley pretended, together, that forgetting was the same thing as peace.

Maren's face when the ledger page lay open between them. *His name is Tam. His name was always Tam.* Twenty years of a lie shaped like love.

The road. Gods, the road—the first mile outside the wards when magic had come rushing back into her like a river through a broken dam, loud and bright and terrifying, and she had thrown lightning at a hunt patrol with a trick she never remembered learning because some buried piece of her brother had known it all along.

Sable, quick-eyed and sharp-tongued, offering partnership over stolen bread. Sable trading her mother's memory to a ferry-wight without blinking, and weeping after, where she thought no one saw. Sable holding Lior's journey inside her skull like a lantern she refused to light.

Iven, disgraced and magnificent, recognizing the ember-mark on her wrist and going white as bone. Iven confessing his students' names into a campfire dark. Iven dueling his own past in the halls above even now, buying her minutes with his blood.

Dara, who missed on purpose. Dara, who carried a list of names she couldn't finish and wouldn't abandon. Dara's deserters turning their ballistae around this very hour, choosing conscience over orders, one bolt at a time.

And Tam. Not memories—she had none, that was the whole wound of it—but the shape where memories should have been. A boy who liked the high terraces and hated boiled turnips, who whittled, who laughed, who was sold so that a valley full of strangers could sleep safely. She knew him the way you know a room you've never entered but have dreamed of every night of your life.

*I'm coming to give you back,* she told that shape. *I'm sorry it took me twenty years to learn your name.*

The bells rang a third time, closer now—or perhaps just deeper, the mountain itself ringing—and the ember-light in the bowl stirred like something waking.

Lior rose to her feet.

Her legs shook. She let them. Fear was allowed; fear was honest. What mattered was what you did with your hands while you were afraid, and her hands were steady as they unclasped her coat, as they rolled back her sleeve, as they bared the ember-mark burning on her wrist like a small red sun seen through skin.

She laid her palm over the black glass bowl.

It was cold. Then it wasn't. Heat climbed her arm—not pain, not yet, but recognition, the bowl knowing her the way the mirrors knew her, the way the whole mountain had known her since before she was born. *Vessel,* it seemed to say. *Little jar. Little keeper of a stolen boy.*

"No," Lior said aloud, and her voice rang off the silvered walls, off the hundred reflections, off the dark. "Not a jar."

She had been raised on oaths. She had sworn the guild's oath every year of her life, kneeling among the ash-jars with Maren's hand on her head: *to hide, to keep, to forget, to endure.* An oath built out of fear, mortared with silence, roofed over with forgetting. It had kept people alive. It had also kept them small.

This oath would be different. This one she wrote herself, here, now, in the space between one bell and the next, and she spoke it loudly enough that the mountain would carry it up through the stone to everyone fighting in its throat:

"I am Lior Hartwell, youngest keeper of the Ember Guild, and I swear a new oath—not to hide, but to stand. Not to keep souls in jars, but to set them free. Not to forget, but to be remembered, even if I cannot do the remembering myself."

Her voice cracked. She let it crack.

"I give back what was put in me. I give back the boy who was sold. And if the price of his freedom is every memory of my name—" her palm pressed harder against the glass, and the heat bit now, truly bit, climbing past her elbow, past her shoulder, blooming behind her sternum like a second heart catching fire— "then let the price be paid. Let my friends keep me. Let their love be my ledger. Let what I lose be found again in them."

The bowl blazed.

Light poured up through the black glass and into her arm and she did not scream, though every nerve she owned sang like a snapped wire. The mirror-walls caught the blaze and threw it back a thousandfold until the vault was a sphere of white fire and somewhere inside it, impossibly, she could see them all—every reflection of herself opening like a door.

And out of the doors came the light of her life.

It left her gently. That was the miracle no one had warned her about. Not torn—*poured*. Each memory rose out of her like a spark off a bonfire, glowing, distinct, and hung in the blazing air around her before streaming toward the center of the vault, toward the place where the vessel would break open.

She watched them go, and she named each one as it left, because naming was a keeper's work and she would be a keeper to the last:

*The valley. Gone—up in light.*

*Maren's hands. Gone.*

*The festival lanterns. Gone.*

*Sable's crooked grin over stolen bread. Gone—oh, gone, and it hurt worse than the fire, and she blessed it anyway.*

*Iven's confession by the campfire. Dara missing on purpose. The bridge of small mercies. The ruins where they told one true thing each. The moment—she clung to this one, clawed at it, lost it anyway—the moment around a small fire when she had looked at three hunted strangers and said, *you're my family*, and meant it with her whole chest.*

Gone. All of it, rising like embers off a burned letter, beautiful and irreversible.

What remained was strangely quiet. A girl kneeling in white fire who knew how to speak and how to kneel and almost nothing else. Who knew that the people shouting her name from the corridor were hers, though she could not have said why. Who knew only this: she had come here to give something back, and she was giving it, and it was good.

The last thing to leave was not a memory at all. It was smaller than memory—a warmth behind her ribs, a presence that had ridden in her blood for twenty years without her knowing. Her brother. Her other half. The boy whose eyes looked out of every mirror.

It paused at her heart, the way a traveler pauses at a doorway.

*Thank you,* it said, without words, in a voice like the high terraces and the smell of woodsmoke, in a voice she had never heard and somehow knew better than her own name—which was fortunate, because she no longer knew her name.

Then it went home.

* * *

The vault broke like an egg.

The black glass bowl shattered into powder. The mirror-walls cracked from crown to floor, and through the cracks came a sound Lior's emptied mind had no framework for but her body understood completely: a roar, a chorus, a hundred voices breathing in at once after twenty years underwater.

He stood up out of the light.

He was taller than she was. Of course he was—he'd been sold at nine and had lived twenty years more somewhere she couldn't follow—but some part of her had expected a boy, and meeting the man was like meeting a stranger wearing a beloved coat. Tam's face. Tam's eyes, wet and blazing. Tam looking down at her with an expression too large for any single word—grief and fury and wonder and a tenderness so fierce it frightened her.

"You," he said. His voice was rust and honey. "You're the one who carried me."

She opened her mouth. Nothing came. She didn't know what a sister was anymore, or a vessel, or a Hartwell. She knew only that this person was the reason the world felt newly made, and that her empty hands wanted, more than anything, to be held.

Behind him, boots on stone—running, many sets—and then the doors burst wide and they were there, all of them, battered and smoke-streaked and alive.

Dara first, sword drawn, putting herself between Lior and the corridor out of pure habit. Then Iven, robes scorched, one eye swelling shut, still clutching the broker's ledger like a weapon. Then Sable—

Sable, who took one look at Lior's face and made a sound like something tearing.

"She did it." Sable dropped to her knees beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Gods below, she actually—Lior. *Lior.* Look at me. Do you know me? Say you know me."

Lior looked at her. The woman's face was a stranger's face, tear-tracked, soot-grimed, lit by the dying fire of the broken bowl. And yet. And yet something in Lior's hollow chest leaned toward her like a plant toward a window.

"I don't know you," Lior said slowly. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "But I think—I think I was keeping something for you. I think I promised not to ask you to trade again."

Sable broke. Folded forward and pressed her forehead to Lior's shoulder and wept like the ferry-wight had taken her all over again.

Tam knelt on her other side. Up close, the resemblance was unbearable and wonderful—the same jaw, the same stubborn line of the mouth, wearing his years like she'd worn hers. He reached out and touched the ember-mark on her wrist, which had faded now to a pale ghost, a scar where a sun used to be.

"I spent twenty years in the dark," he said, "and the whole time, the brightest thing in the mountain was walking around on two legs, carrying me. I felt every mile of your road, little sister. I felt you choose me." His voice fractured. "I will spend the rest of my life paying that back."

"I don't know what a sister is," Lior whispered.

"Then we'll teach each other," Tam said. "We'll start from ash. We're guild folk. Ash is what we know."

Above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the midwinter bells changed their note—from the slow tolling of the sealing to something faster, ragged, urgent. And beneath the bells came another sound, rising through the cracked floors and the broken mirrors and the shattered dark of the Undercourt: shouting. Hundreds of voices. The jars were breaking everywhere at once, and the re-gifted were waking in their cells and their chains, and the mountain that had swallowed souls for a century had begun, at last, to choke.

Dara pulled them all to their feet. "The garrison will come for this room," she said, "and they'll come angry. Can you fight?"

Tam flexed his hands, and light gathered in them like heat off a forge—the harvested power of the vault answering its freed master. "I can do more than fight," he said. "I've been saving up for twenty years."

Iven laughed—a wild, disbelieving sound—and slung the ledger under his arm. "Then let's go introduce the king to his inventory."

They moved for the doors, four soldiers of a rebellion that hadn't existed an hour ago. But at the threshold Lior stopped, and the others stopped with her, because she was staring back at the ruined vault—at the powder of the bowl, at the spiderwebbed mirrors, at the ember-light guttering on the floor like a hearth waiting for new hands.

Someone had to keep the flame now. Someone had to swear the oath again, properly, in front of witnesses, and mean it.

"Lior?" Sable's hand found hers. Warm. Familiar in a way that bypassed memory entirely. "What is it?"

Lior looked down at their joined hands, at this stranger who was evidently the keeper of everything she'd lost, and felt the first small spark of something kindle in her emptied chest—not memory, not yet, but the place where memory would live when it came home.

"I don't know who I am," she said. "But I know what I swore. And I think—" the bells crashed overhead, and the mountain roared, and she smiled for the first time since waking to herself— "I think the rest of you are going to have to remind me. Starting now. Starting with your names."

Sable laughed through her tears and squeezed her hand.

"Well," she said, drawing her toward the roaring dark, "there's a war going on, so we'll do it on the way. My name is Sable. Remember it if you can."

And Lior Hartwell—who was not yet anyone at all—walked out of the vault of vessels into the breaking of the mountain, holding on tight.
