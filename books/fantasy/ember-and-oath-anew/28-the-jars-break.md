## 28. The Jars Break

I woke in a body I did not know, kneeling on stone that hummed like a struck bell, and the first thing I felt was not gratitude.

It was fury.

It came up through me the way water comes up through cracked earth—slow at first, then all at once, everywhere. Twenty years of it. Twenty years pressed flat into a jar no wider than my two hands, twenty years of listening to the dark and learning its every texture: the drip of condensation down glass, the muffled thunder of boots on the vault floor above, the ember-light that never went out and never warmed. Twenty years of knowing, distantly, dimly, that somewhere a girl with my mother's stubborn jaw was growing up without me, because someone had sold me like a sack of grain to buy a valley's silence.

The last thing I remembered before the jar was my sister's hand in mine. Small. Sticky with honey. She was laughing at something I could no longer name, because her name had been taken from me along with everything else, and all I had kept, all those years in the dark, was the shape of her laugh and the warmth of her hand.

Then light had poured into me like a river breaking a dam, and a voice—not mine, mine now, but hers, older, worn smooth by roads I'd never walked—had said words I didn't understand, an oath in a tongue the guild must have taught her, and the glass around me had sung one long note and burst.

So I knelt there among the shards, in a stranger's knees, with a stranger's heart hammering blood through me, and I understood three things at once.

I was free. I was furious. And I was not alone.

* * *

The vault stretched away from me in every direction, a cathedral built for grief. Shelves rose out of the ember-dark like cliffs, tier upon tier, and every shelf was crowded with jars—thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one glowing faintly from within, each one holding what remained of a person. The light they gave off was the same light I had grown up beside in the Hollow Valley, the same hearth-glow the keepers tended with such reverence, and seeing it here, hoarded in this cold place, made something in my borrowed chest twist until I thought a rib would crack.

The valley's magic hadn't fled. It hadn't died. It had been *harvested*. Carried south in jars by men who called themselves keepers of order, and burned slowly, year after year, to hold a ward over a handful of hiding souls while the rest of the gifted world rotted in the dark beneath a mountain.

They had used my soul to buy that silence. My soul, and the light of everyone else in this room.

I got to my feet. The body was wrong—taller than I remembered being, thinner, hands scarred in ways I didn't recognize—but it obeyed me, and obedience was enough. The ember-mark on my wrist, twin to the one I had glimpsed on the girl's wrist as she collapsed, pulsed once, twice, like a second heartbeat. Hers. The tether between us still sang, thin as spider silk. Through it I could feel her: emptied out, drifting, a house with all the furniture carried away but the walls still standing.

She had done this. She had opened herself like a door and let me walk out of her, and she didn't even know my name anymore.

*I'm sorry,* I thought down the tether, though I don't know if a soul can speak such things. *I'm sorry, little sister. Whoever you were.*

Then I turned to the shelves, and the fury found its work.

* * *

The first jar I broke with my bare hands.

I wrapped my fingers around the glass—it was warm, warmer than it should have been, warm as a sleeping animal—and I pulled. The seal fought me. Old craft, court craft, knots tied by people who had learned their binding from the same books as the broker whose voice I could still hear laughing somewhere above us: *the vessel walked out on its own legs.* The knot bit into my palms. The glass sang a high complaining note.

I thought about my mother's kitchen. I thought about honey on small fingers. I thought about twenty years of dark.

The jar broke.

The soul came out of it like a bird from a closed fist—not flying so much as *unfolding*, unfolding and unfolding, wings of light spreading wider than the shelf, wider than the aisle, filling the whole cathedral aisle with a brightness that had been folded up too long. It circled me once. It touched my face, and in that touch I felt a name: *Wren. My name is Wren. I was a lampwright. I had daughters.* And then it was gone, streaming upward through the stone as if stone were mist, gone home to whatever waited for it—or gone hunting for the body it had been peeled from, if that body still breathed somewhere in this mountain's upper rooms.

I stood there shaking, glass dust glittering off my knuckles, and I heard, far above, a sound I would come to know well in the hours that followed: a scream. A human voice, waking in its own flesh after years of absence, screaming and then laughing and then screaming again, all three tangled together.

The garrison had bodies stored here too. Of course they did. A soul is worth nothing to a broker without a body to rent it back to.

I picked up the next jar.

* * *

I do not know how many I broke. A hundred. Three hundred. My hands bled; the blood hissed where it touched the seals, and after a while I learned to let the ember-light do the work—to cup a jar in both palms and *push*, the way I had once pushed a door open against winter wind, and feel the old knot unravel. The guild's craft. The valley's craft. The craft they had stolen from the very people they caged, turned back upon the cages at last.

Around me the vault transformed. One by one, then ten by ten, then in cascades, the jars burst, and the light they released stopped behaving like light. It moved. It *chose*. Souls poured upward through the tiers in rivers of gold and white, some slow and dazed, some fast and shrieking with joy, and where they passed, the ember-shelves guttered and the great lanterns that lit the Undercourt flared and died and flared again, drunk on freedom.

And everywhere, everywhere, the mountain began to wake.

I felt it through the floor before I heard it—a tremor, then a rolling, then a sound like a city-sized gasp. Somewhere above us, in the barracks and the workrooms and the warden's tiers, bodies were sitting up. Bodies that had shuffled and served and stared for years with nothing behind the eyes were suddenly, violently *inhabited* again, and the first thing most of them saw was a guard's face, and the first thing most of them did was exactly what you would do.

The screams became a chorus. The chorus became a roar.

A woman came stumbling down the vault stairs while I worked—middle-aged, in a laborer's smock, her eyes blazing with a light that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with rage. She stopped at the bottom step and stared at the ruined shelves, at the storm of freed light, at me standing bloody-handed in the middle of it.

"You," she said. Her voice cracked. "You're the one. They said—the whisper went through the workrooms like fire—you're the Hartwell soul."

"I don't—" The name hit me strangely, a key turning in a lock I hadn't known I owned. "That was my name. Before."

"It's your name now." She crossed the floor, took my bleeding hands in hers, and held them up like something precious. "Lampwright's daughter, listen to me. There are four hundred of us awake on the third tier and more coming. The garrison is sealing the stairwells. If we stay down here, we die in the dark again, only this time in our own skins." Her grip tightened. "Can you do what you did to the jars to the doors?"

I looked at my hands. At the ember-mark burning on my wrist like a brand and a promise. At the hundreds of jars still waiting on the shelves, still dreaming their long dreams, and beyond them the sealed gates of a kingdom built on theft.

Twenty years ago, men had decided what my soul was worth and spent it. Every door in this mountain had been locked by that decision. Every chain, every seal, every knot of court-craft—all of it purchased with the currency of people like me, like Wren, like the laughing girl whose memories I wore now like a coat that didn't fit.

"No," I said. "I can't open the doors."

Her face fell.

"I can *unmake* them." I let the fury rise all the way, let it fill the borrowed lungs, let it pour out through my palms as light. "Every lock in this place was paid for with us. Which means every lock answers to us. Stand back."

* * *

What happened next, they will tell different ways in different cities, and every telling will be true.

I walked the vault from end to end with my hands raised, and the seals broke ahead of me like ice breaking on a spring river. Not just the jars—I broke the wards on the stairwells, the bindings on the iron doors, the quiet little knots of compulsion woven into the collars the garrison made its laborers wear. Court-craft is a language, and I had spent twenty years inside it, pressed against its walls, learning its grammar the way the drowning learn water. Every spell in the Undercourt had been built to hold souls, and I was a soul, and I was *furious*, and fury turns out to be a master key.

Behind me, the freed came pouring up the stairs.

I saw it only in flashes, because I stayed at the rear, breaking what needed breaking, but the flashes were enough. The lampwright woman—her name was Odile, and she had been a warden herself once, before they took her gift and left her body to serve—organizing the awakened on the third tier into lines, into groups, into something that was not quite an army but was no longer a crowd. A boy of maybe fourteen walking untouched through a volley of crossbow bolts because he had been a shield-mage before the hunt took him, and the bolts bent around him like rain around a roof. Two sisters, re-gifted that same hour, setting the garrison's ballistae alight with a touch and laughing, actually laughing, wild and cracked and alive.

The garrison fought. I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not pretend the fighting was clean. Soldiers died. Some of them were conscripts, boys from the salt provinces who had never seen a jar and didn't know what they were guarding. Some of the freed killed in ways that made even me look away, twenty years of darkness coming out of them as violence, and there was no stopping that, no more than you can stop a flood from carrying off what's in its path. Odile tried. I tried. But you cannot ask people to be gentle in the first hour of waking from a nightmare that lasted half a lifetime.

What I remember most clearly, though—what I carry now, the way I once carried only my sister's laughter—is quieter than the battle.

I remember a man on the fifth tier finding his own body in the warden's registry room, a body kept breathing in a chair for eleven years because a living vessel is worth more than a dead one. He knelt in front of it for a long moment, this ghost in the air, this light shaped like a man. Then he lay down inside himself the way you'd lie down in your own bed after a long road, and he wept, and when he stood up he was whole, and the first thing he said was his daughter's name.

I remember the jars that were empty. Rows of them, near the vault's far wall, their seals intact but their light long since drawn out—spent, poured into the wards, into Vessic's engines, into whatever hunger the crown fed upstairs. I stood before those rows longer than I stood anywhere else. I put my palm against the cold glass of one and felt nothing at all, and that nothing was worse than any scream.

"Some of them are just gone," Odile said quietly, beside me.

"Not gone." My voice came out low and strange. "Spent. There's a difference. Spent means someone chose to spend them, and someone can be made to answer for it."

"Vessic comes at midwinter," she said. "To drink the rest. That's the word from the upper tiers—that's why the sealing, why the deadline. He means to pour every jar left in this mountain down his own throat and become something the world hasn't got a name for."

"Then midwinter is tonight," I said, "and he's going to find his cellar empty."

* * *

By the time we reached the great gate at the mountain's root, the Undercourt was no longer the Undercourt. It was a bonfire wearing a mountain's shape.

Freed souls and freed bodies filled the galleries. The ember-lanterns, cut off from their harvested fuel, had gone dark one by one, and in their place the re-gifted had lit the halls with their own hands—small flames cupped in palms, light woven into the air, a hundred small magics doing openly what a hundred lifetimes had been forced to do in hiding or not at all. Children who had been registered and branded ran through the corridors with sparks trailing off their fingers like ribbons, and no one told them to stop, and no one ever would again.

At the gate itself, the garrison made its last stand, and might have held it—for they had ballistae, and walls, and the terrible discipline of men defending the thing they had built their lives on believing in.

But the deserters turned their own machines around.

I saw it happen from the gallery above. A line of soldiers in hunt-grey, led by a tall woman with a scarred jaw and a list clutched in one fist like a talisman, walking to the ballistae their own officers had aimed at the crowd—and turning them. The first bolt took the gatehouse banner down. The second took the gatehouse door off its hinges. The third was a message, and the message was received: the garrison broke, streamed out the shattered gate, and scattered into the snow like startled birds.

The tall woman looked up, once, directly at me. She had eyes like someone who had counted every name she'd failed to save. She touched two fingers to her brow—not a salute, I think, or not only one—and turned back to her people.

Later I would learn her name was Dara Ferro. Later I would learn she had missed her shot at my sister on purpose, and that my sister's friends were even then fighting their way down through the collapsing tiers toward the vessel vault, toward the girl lying empty on the stone floor with my oath still warm on her lips. But in that hour I knew none of it. In that hour I stood in the gateway of my prison with snow falling past my shoulders and my hands still bleeding, and I watched the mountain give up its dead and its living alike into the night.

Odile came and stood beside me. Behind us, the vault burned with clean light, every jar broken that could be broken, every soul that could go home already streaming toward home.

"What now?" she asked. "The king's army is sealing the passes. By dawn there'll be nothing moving on this mountain but his soldiers and us."

I reached down the tether—the thin silver thread that still connected me to the girl who had given everything so that I could stand here. Through it I felt her friends around her, fierce and grieving, refusing to let her be nobody. I felt Sable's stubbornness and Iven's guilt and Dara's crossed-out names, though I couldn't have told you then how I knew those things. The tether doesn't carry thoughts. It carries weight. And what I felt was this: that the girl who had freed me was not finished, and neither was I.

"Now," I said, "we finish it. Vessic thinks he's coming to a harvest. Let him come to a reckoning."

I stepped out through the broken gate into the falling snow, and behind me, tier by tier, window by window, the mountain blazed like a coal caught in wool—and the whole dark sky above the free cities began, very faintly, to glow.

Somewhere down that road, an army was marching toward us with a king at its head.

Let him march. His cellar was empty. His secret was loose. And his prisoners had learned, in a single night, the one lesson no throne survives:

Light, once freed, does not ask permission to burn.
