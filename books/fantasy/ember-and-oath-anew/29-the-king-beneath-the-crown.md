## 29. The King Beneath the Crown

Iven Ashgrave had spent twenty years learning how kings lie, and he had learned it first from Vessic himself, back when the man was only a prince with soft hands and a hunger like a well with no bottom. So when the mountain began to shake—not the deep groan of settling stone but a rhythmic, arrhythmic shudder, like something vast being struck from within—Iven did not need a scrying glass to know what had happened.

The jars were breaking.

He stood in the antechamber of the vault, alone among the wreckage of his negotiation with the Broker of Names. The old man's body lay where Iven had left it, folded into the corner behind a shattered display case, and Iven found he could not look at him directly. Master Corvane. The man who had taught him the grammar of magic and then taught him, in the same breath, that grammar could be bought and sold like anything else in the Undercourt. Iven had come down here intending to bargain with him, to trade secrets for time, and instead he had put a knife between the ribs of the only teacher he'd ever loved.

*Debts,* he thought. *Everyone in this mountain is collecting debts tonight.*

The shaking came again, harder now, and somewhere far above them dust sifted down through the cathedral dark like grey snow. The ember-light that filled the vault—the same light Lior's guild kept in their jars, the same light that burned in the mark on her wrist—was changing. For twenty years it had pulsed with the slow, patient rhythm of contained things. Now it flickered. Stuttered. And then, all at once along the length of the great corridor, the jars went dark.

Not all at once. In waves. Like a tide going out.

Iven pressed his palm against the nearest jar—a squat thing of black glass holding what looked like a curl of smoke—and felt nothing. Emptiness. Whatever had lived inside it was gone, released or fled or *risen*, and the silence where its presence had been was louder than any scream.

"Oh," he said softly, to no one. "Oh, you magnificent fool. You actually did it."

He meant Lior. He meant the girl who had walked into this mountain as merchandise, empty-headed by her own choice, and who was—somewhere below him, beyond the mirror corridor—choosing again. Choosing to open herself like a door.

Iven had betrayed students before. That was the sin he'd confessed around a dying fire in the ruins outside Corren's Landing, the true thing he'd offered up when Dara asked each of them for one honest wound: he had named names, given up the children under his protection to save his own position at court, and the court had burned three of them. He had told himself for years that he'd had no choice. Standing here now, feeling the vault exhale a century of stolen souls, he understood at last that there had always been a choice, and that he had simply been too much of a coward to pay its price until a girl younger than his dead students showed him what paying it looked like.

The doors at the far end of the antechamber boomed.

Once. Twice. A third time, and then they were not knocked upon but simply *opened*, torn from their hinges by hands that did not ask permission, and through them came the King.

Vessic had dressed for a feast. That was the detail Iven would remember afterward, the detail that made everything else make sense: the man wore white and gold, a coronation robe though he'd been crowned six years ago, and his crown sat low on his brow like something he'd grown into rather than earned. Behind him came six sorcerers in the grey-and-silver livery of the Crown Circle, and behind *them* came throne guard in ranks, halberds gleaming, filling the antechamber wall to wall.

Vessic stopped. Looked at the darkened jars. Looked at Iven, standing among them with blood on his sleeve that was not his own.

"Ashgrave." The king's voice was mild. It was always mild; that was the horror of him. "I was told you died in the purge. One of my few administrative disappointments."

"Your Majesty flatters me. Most people forget me entirely."

"I don't forget assets." Vessic walked forward, unhurried, his boots clicking on stone. He trailed one gloved finger along a dead jar as he passed, the way a man might trail a hand along a fence of livestock. "Six hundred and forty jars in this vault. Each one a reservoir. Do you know what I intended, Ashgrave? Tonight, at midwinter, when the seals are thinnest? I intended to drink."

He said it plainly, without theater, and somehow that was worse than any villain's speech. He turned to face Iven fully, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without shame.

"A king is a vessel too," Vessic said. "A vessel for the realm. And a realm this broken needs... refilling." He gestured at the darkened jars with something almost like grief. "And someone has emptied my cellar."

"The cellar emptied itself," Iven said. "That's rather the nature of souls. You can cage them, but they keep trying to go home."

Something moved behind the king's mildness—something quick and cold. "Where is the vessel?"

"Gone. Walking out on her own legs, I'd imagine. Or perhaps not walking. Perhaps running. Perhaps flying, if the stories about her brother are true." Iven smiled, and it cost him more than the knife had. "You sold a boy's soul once, Majesty. Did you really think it wouldn't come back for interest?"

Vessic regarded him for a long moment. Then, mildly: "Kill him."

* * *

The sorcerers of the Crown Circle were not duelists. They were technicians—men and women who had spent their careers maintaining wards, calibrating harvest-engines, keeping six hundred jars at a simmer. Iven knew this because he had trained half of them. He had stood in this very chamber a decade ago, in the silver sash of a rising court mage, teaching them how to draw soul-light without scorching the vessel's memories.

So when the first of them raised her hands and spoke the opening cadence of a binding, Iven did not dodge. He *sang back.*

His voice caught the cadence mid-phrase and inverted it, and the binding that should have wrapped him instead bloomed outward and wrapped the sorceress who'd cast it. She went down gasping, pinned in her own working, and Iven was already moving, already pulling power from the only source left in this room—which was, he realized with a wild laugh, himself. Twenty years of hoarded discipline. Twenty years of guilt banked like coal. He burned it all.

The second sorcerer threw fire. Iven took it on a shield of ash—he had always favored ash; it was the guild's element, humble and hungry—and stepped through the flames close enough to strike. His fist connected with the man's jaw with a crack that echoed. Magic was elegant; sometimes fists were faster.

But there were six of them, and they recovered quickly, and they spread out in the pattern he himself had taught them—the crescent formation, converging pressure, no gaps. Iven felt the net closing. Felt his shields thinning. Somewhere in the back of his mind, a cold arithmetic: *three of them die before they take me, maybe four.*

Then the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section of the antechamber's high vault, sheared away as if by a giant's chisel, and through the gap poured light—daylight, impossible daylight, torchlight, ember-light—and with it came stones and splinters and one enormous iron ballista bolt that buried itself in the floor between Iven and the sorcerers, quivering like a harpoon.

Through the dust strode figures in deserters' grey. At their head, a woman with a scarred jaw and a captain's bearing she hadn't yet burned off, carrying in one hand a signal horn and in the other an expression Iven had last seen on the bridge of small mercies: grim, exhausted, absolutely done negotiating.

"Dara Ferro," Vessic said, and for the first time his mildness cracked, just slightly. "Your list, Captain. You never finished your list."

"No," Dara agreed. She raised the horn to her lips and blew.

From above—from the gate-tunnels, from the switchback roads carved into the mountain's throat—came answering horns. Not one. A dozen. Two dozen. And beneath them, a sound Iven would carry to his grave: the groan of ballistae turning. The great siege engines Vessic had mounted to seal the mountain's gates, engines meant to hold the free cities' armies at bay while the king drank his kingdom's soul—they were turning *inward*. Turning toward the throne guard in the antechamber, toward the ranks of halberds, toward the crown itself.

"You gave the order to seal the gates at midwinter," Dara said. "You gave me the order. And every soldier on those walls heard you say that everyone inside becomes crown property." She let that hang. "Turns out, Majesty, we're all inside."

The first ballista bolt came down through the broken vault like the wrath of heaven and took the throne guard's front rank apart. The second scattered them. And then the deserters were among them, and it was not a battle so much as a collapse—an army discovering, all at once, in the same heartbeat, that it had already decided.

The Crown Circle sorcerers wavered. Iven saw it happen: the crescent formation loosening, eyes darting toward the raining light, toward the king in his feast-day white who had promised them power and delivered servitude. One of them—young Pellam, who had wept at his mother's burning two winters ago and sworn loyalty anyway because loyalty was safer than grief—lowered his hands.

"Pellam," Vessic said, still mild, still terrible. "Finish the mage."

Pellam looked at the king. Looked at Iven. And Iven, who had betrayed students, who had named names, who had spent twenty years collecting the interest on that debt—did the only thing left in his account. He opened his hands. Lowered his shields. Showed the young man everything: the exhaustion, the guilt, the ember-mark's afterglow still faintly visible on his own wrist where Lior had gripped him.

"I'm sorry," Iven said. "For all of it. For what I taught you. For what I didn't."

Pellam turned his back on the king.

Vessic moved then. Faster than a man in a coronation robe had any right to move, faster than mildness should allow—one hand coming up wreathed in pale fire, the harvested light of a hundred jars still banked in his body from earlier feedings, and he hurled it not at Pellam but at Iven, at the traitor standing closest, at the debt he could still collect.

Dara hit the king from the side like a battering ram.

They went down together in a tangle of white silk and deserters' grey, and the pale fire scorched a line across the flagstones and guttered out, and Iven—scrambling forward, dragging Dara clear as Vessic thrashed and screamed words of power that cracked the remaining jars' casings like ice—felt the mountain itself lean over them. The whole Undercourt was awake now. Above, the re-gifted were pouring through the corridors, hundreds of returned souls with a century of theft to answer for, and the garrison was breaking everywhere at once, and the bells of midwinter were ringing overhead in the sealed city, ringing and ringing, ringing in the end of something.

Vessic rose. His crown had gone crooked. Blood ran from his mouth, and he wiped it with the back of a white-gloved hand and looked at the smear almost with curiosity, as if he had never seen his own interior before.

"You cannot hold this mountain," he said. His voice had lost its mildness at last, and what was underneath was small and cold and very old. "I am the crown. I am the—"

"You're a thief," Dara said, hauling herself upright beside Iven. "And thieves get caught."

She did not kill him. Later, Iven would understand why—would learn that Tam Hartwell and his sister's friends had taken the king alive below, in the mirror corridor, where the last of the jars lay broken around a girl who had given away everything. But in that moment, in the antechamber, all Iven saw was the throne guard's remnant closing ranks around their fallen king, retreating, dragging him back toward the upper gates where loyalist companies still held the switchbacks—and Dara blowing her horn again, twice, calling the deserters home, calling the ballistae to walk the king's own roads ahead of him.

"They'll rally at the gate-fortress," she said, spitting blood. "We've got hours, not days."

"Then we'd better spend them well." Iven flexed his burned hands, wincing. Around them, the antechamber lay in ruins—dead jars, dead broker, dead certainties—and through the torn ceiling the midwinter snow was falling gently into the vault, landing on black glass, melting, running in bright threads across the floor like the mountain itself was weeping and finding it a relief.

Dara glanced at him sideways. "Ashgrave. When this is done—when he's deposed and the hunt's dissolved, if it ever is—what will you do?"

Iven thought of students. Of three names he carried like stones. Of a valley he'd never seen, where a guild kept jars of ash and called it safety, and where a girl with her brother's eyes had chosen to forget herself so that someone else could exist.

"I'm going to go somewhere they teach children instead of harvesting them," he said. "And I'm going to spend the rest of my life being sorry in useful ways."

Above them, muffled by stone and distance, the midwinter bells rang on—and beneath their peal, rising through the mountain's bones, came a new sound. Voices. Hundreds of them, hoarse and disused, singing or screaming or simply saying their own names aloud, over and over, like people waking from a long sleep and refusing to fall back into it.

Dara listened. Her scarred jaw tightened.

"That sound," she said quietly, "is going to be at the gates before we are. Come on, mage. Let's go collect the rest of our debts."

They ran together into the smoking heart of the mountain, toward the upper gates, toward the falling snow, toward whatever waited in the white morning above—and behind them, in the dark of the broken vault, the last ember-light guttered, steadied, and began, slowly, to rise.
