## 30. Who Is Lior Hartwell?

The first thing was warmth.

Not fire-warmth—she would learn later that she knew the difference in her bones even when she knew nothing else—but the low, patient warmth of a body that had been tended by other bodies. Blankets layered over her. A hand pressed flat between her shoulder blades, steady as a heartbeat that wasn't her own. Somewhere near her ear, someone was breathing in a slow, deliberate rhythm, the kind of breathing people do on purpose to keep from sobbing.

She opened her eyes.

Light came in sideways, amber and thick, the color of honey held up to a lamp. It fell across a ceiling of dark stone veined with something that glowed faintly, like embers banked under ash. She lay on a pallet heaped with wool, and the stone beneath it hummed—not sound, exactly, but a vibration she felt in her teeth, in the soft hollows behind her eyes.

She did not know where she was.

That should have frightened her. She understood, distantly, that it should have—the way a person understands that a wound ought to hurt before the pain arrives. But there was no fear. There was nothing at all where a self should have been. She reached inward, the way one reaches for a familiar stair in the dark, and found only smooth emptiness, vast and quiet, like a hall after everyone has gone home.

*Who—*

The thought snagged. She tried again.

*Who am I?*

Nothing answered. Not silence, precisely. Silence implies the expectation of sound. This was different—a room swept clean, a page washed white, a jar with nothing in it.

She became aware, then, of the faces.

There were four of them, crowded close, lit by the ember-veins in the walls. A woman with cropped black hair and quick, clever hands, currently gripping the edge of the pallet so hard her knuckles had gone the color of bone. A tall man in a mage's coat gone gray at the seams, his left sleeve scorched, his eyes red-rimmed behind an expression he was clearly trying to make into calm and failing. A broad-shouldered soldier woman with a fresh scar along her jaw, standing slightly apart, arms crossed, as if she could hold herself together by force. And a young man—her age, or near it—with her own dark hair and a face that made something ache in her chest, though she could not have said why. He looked at her the way people look at a door they'd given up believing would open.

They were all crying. Even the soldier. Especially, perhaps, the soldier, who cried the way stones weather: slowly, invisibly, all at once.

"There you are," the black-haired woman whispered. "Oh, there you are."

Lior opened her mouth. Her throat was raw, as if she had screamed for a very long time, or sung, or burned. When she spoke, her voice came out rusted.

"Who are you?"

The words dropped into the room like stones into still water. The ripples went through all four faces at once—the flinch, the grief, the terrible tenderness—and she watched them take the blow and absorb it, each in their own way. The mage closed his eyes. The soldier turned her head. The black-haired woman did not flinch at all; she leaned closer instead, as if the question had been an invitation.

"You don't know us," she said. It wasn't a question either. "Of course you don't. Of course you don't." She laughed, wet and broken. "I'm Sable. I'm—I steal things. Memories, mostly. I stole yours, some of them, to get you into this mountain, and I swore I'd give them back, and I will, I swear it on every road I've ever walked, but—" Her voice cracked clean through. "But what you gave up, what you *poured out*, that isn't mine to carry back. That has to find its own way home."

"I don't understand any of that," Lior said.

"I know," Sable said. "I know. That's all right. You don't have to understand yet. You just have to stay."

* * *

They told her things, in the hours that followed, the way people feed a fire one stick at a time.

The tall man—Iven, Iven Ashgrave, disgraced court mage, though he said *disgraced* like a man spitting out a tooth—told her about the mountain. The Undercourt beneath it. The cathedral of jars, each one holding a stolen soul, lit by ember-light identical to something called the guild's. He told her that the king who built it had come to drink its power at midwinter, and had found it already broken, already rising against him, already burning.

He told her that she had done that.

"You opened yourself," he said, kneeling beside the pallet so their eyes were level, and his voice was careful the way hands are careful around glass. "You were made to hold a soul—your brother's, taken before either of you could consent to anything. And when the moment came, you chose to give him back. Every memory you had of being yourself went with him. That was the price. You knew it. You paid it anyway."

"Was it worth it?" she asked.

Iven's composure finally broke. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and laughed, or wept, or both. "You're asking *me*? Girl, you freed three hundred souls and toppled a king, and you're lying there with nothing—not your name, not your mother's face, not one single day of your own life—and your first real question is whether it was *worth it*?"

"Is that strange?" she asked.

"It's the most you thing you've ever done," said the soldier, from her corner. "And I've known you six weeks."

Her name was Dara Ferro. She had been captain of the hunt—the Gift Hunt, they called it, as if hunting children were a season like any other—and she had missed her shot on purpose, and deserted, and carried a list of names she refused to finish. She said all of this flatly, like a confession delivered to a wall, and then she said, quieter: "I shot past you once. Best miss of my life."

"And him?" Lior asked, because the young man had not spoken. He sat at the foot of the pallet, knees drawn up, watching her with an intensity that should have been frightening and was instead the opposite—as if he were memorizing her, hoarding the sight of her awake against some future famine.

"That's Tam," Sable said softly.

"Tam," Lior repeated. The name fit in her mouth like a key fits a lock it was not cut for—close, almost, shape without turning.

"He's your brother," Sable said. "His soul lived inside you for twenty years. You grew around each other. And now he's out, and he's free, and he's sitting right there, and neither of you remembers a single thing about each other, and it's the cruelest thing I've ever seen, and I've seen—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again. "It's cruel. That's all."

Tam lifted his head. His eyes were brown; hers, she saw when she caught her reflection later in a polished shield, were also brown, but there was something else in them, some ember-colored fleck that the mirror held and she could not see directly. When he spoke, his voice was rough with disuse.

"Do you want me to go?" he asked. "I'll go. If my face hurts you. If I'm just—proof of what it cost. I can wait outside. I can wait anywhere. I've gotten good at waiting."

Lior considered the question honestly, because she had nothing else to be but honest. She searched herself for revulsion, for grief, for the ache a stranger's face might provoke—and found, beneath the emptiness, a single warm coal. Not a memory. Nothing so structured. Just a pull, faint and stubborn, like a compass needle that had lost its north but remembered that north existed.

"No," she said. "Stay. I don't know why. But stay."

Something in Tam's face came apart and reassembled itself, gentler. "Okay," he said. "Okay. I can do that."

* * *

Sleep took her in pieces after that, and in the gaps between sleeping and waking, the strangers kept their vigil.

She learned them the way you learn a language by immersion—without grammar, without rules, only repetition and need. Sable never left for more than an hour, and when she returned she always brought something small: a strip of dried apple, a comb, a scrap of cloth that smelled of woodsmoke and rain. She talked constantly, filling the silence the way a dam fills a crack, telling stories that began in the middle because their beginnings belonged to Lior now-lost self.

"—and then Dara says, dead serious, 'the horse is not haunted,' and Iven says, 'then why does it only scream at mages,' and honestly, you laughed so hard you fell off the bridge—"

"I fell off a bridge?"

"The Bridge of Small Mercies. You were fine. Mostly fine. There was a wight involved. It's a long story, and half of it is mine to tell and half of it is yours to remember, and I'm trying very hard not to mix them up, because—" Sable's hands twisted in her lap. "Because taking what isn't freely given is how I got started down a bad road, and I promised myself I wouldn't do it to you. Not even to comfort you. So you'll have to be patient with me. With all of it."

Lior reached out—slowly, the way one reaches toward a strange animal—and touched Sable's wrist. "Tell me the wight part," she said. "I don't care whose it is. Tell me everything. I'm empty. I'd rather be full of borrowed things than nothing at all."

Sable stared at her. Then she laughed through her tears and pulled her stool closer, and talked until the ember-light shifted and the mountain's deep hours came on.

Dara kept watch at the door. Lior learned this without being told: the soldier slept in snatches, always facing outward, always between Lior and the corridor. Once, waking in the grey time, Lior found her sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, and asked, "Are we still in danger?"

Dara considered the question with the seriousness she seemed to bring to everything. "Vessic's army is routing. His sorcerers are dead or scattered. The garrison surrendered this morning." A pause. "So no. Probably not."

"Then why do you guard the door?"

Another pause, longer. The knife whispered against the whetstone. "Habit," Dara said at last. "And because guarding things is the only prayer I know." She glanced over, and for one unguarded moment her face was nakedly, unbearably kind. "Get some sleep, stranger. Nobody gets past me. Nobody ever did. That's rather the problem, historically."

Iven came and went with books. He read aloud sometimes—not stories, but records, histories, anything that might anchor her to a world she couldn't recall. He read about the Hollow Valley, where magic could not follow. He read about the Ember Guild, keepers of jars of ash, and stopped reading abruptly when he reached the part about oaths, and closed the book, and said, "Some of this you should hear from someone who loves you. Or from yourself, when it comes back. Whichever arrives first."

"Do you love me?" Lior asked. It seemed a reasonable question. Everyone here wept; the weeping implied attachment; the attachment implied love. But Iven went very still, the way men do when a child asks something true.

"I failed students of mine," he said finally. "Sold secrets to keep my place at court, and the secrets got people burned. I've spent every mile of this road trying to be the sort of man who doesn't do that anymore." He set the book down. "You gave me a second chance before you ever knew my name—you stood in front of a hunt patrol and lied for me with a straight face and shaking hands. So yes. I think I love you the way one loves a debt that turned into a gift. Which is to say: completely, and with great embarrassment."

* * *

On the third day—or what she believed was the third day; time moved strangely in the mountain's amber light—they brought her a mirror.

It was Iven's idea, and Sable argued against it, and Tam said nothing at all, which was its own argument. In the end they brought it anyway, because, as Dara put it, "she's going to catch her reflection in a sword blade eventually, and better a mirror than a blade."

Lior looked at herself and felt nothing.

A young woman. Dark hair, lank and unwashed, falling past her shoulders. A face thin from whatever ordeal had emptied her, cheekbones sharp as drawn blades. Brown eyes with that fleck of ember-color, like sparks caught mid-flight and frozen there. On the inside of her left wrist, a mark: a small spiral of scar tissue, old, deliberate, shaped like a flame folded around a hollow.

"That's the vessel-mark," Iven said quietly. "Only the Undercourt's containers bear it. It's how I first knew what you were."

"What am I?" she asked the mirror. The woman in it asked it too, mouth moving in perfect, useless synchrony. "Who is Lior Hartwell?"

The name startled her. She hadn't known it five seconds ago—had they said it since she woke? Perhaps. Perhaps she'd heard it and let it slide off her like rain off oilcloth. But now it landed. *Lior Hartwell.* It tasted of woodsmoke and cold rivers and a word she almost grasped—*home*, maybe, or *hollow*—and then it was only a name, two syllables and change, belonging to a stranger in a glass.

"She's the girl who left," Sable said from behind her. Lior met her eyes in the mirror. "That's what she called herself once, on a bridge, at night, when she thought none of us were listening. She said leaving home was a kind of dying, and she did it anyway, because her brother was worth a death." Sable's voice wavered. "She was right. It was. And she's worth every hour we sit here waiting for her to come back to us."

"But I'm not her," Lior said. It was not self-pity. It was cartography—an honest marking of the border. "I'm what's left after her. I'm the room she lived in."

"You're the room she *saved*," Tam said.

He had been silent so long that his voice startled them all. He rose from his corner and came to stand beside her, and in the mirror their faces hung side by side—same hair, same jaw, same ember-flecked eyes, siblings written in features if nowhere else.

"I remember things now," he said. "Coming back to myself, piece by piece. Twenty years of being poured into you, and some of it soaked in. I remember warmth. I remember a valley that smelled of ash and apples. I remember—" his breath caught— "someone singing. Badly. All the words wrong, all the heart right. Was that you?"

"I don't know," Lior whispered.

"It was you," Tam said. "It had to be. I was inside you. There was no one else." He laid his hand over hers on the pallet's edge, and his fingers were warm, and the warm coal in her chest—the compass needle with no north—swung hard toward him and held. "So listen to me, whoever you are now. I don't care if you never remember a single day of your life. I'll remember enough for both of us. I'll tell you everything, every night, until it sticks or until it doesn't. You gave me twenty years of your own mind. The least I can do is lend you mine."

Lior looked at the stranger in the mirror—at the hollow-eyed girl with the folded-flame scar—and felt, for the first time since waking, something move in the vast swept-clean hall inside her. Not a memory. Not yet. Just a question, small and alive, like a seed under snow:

*What will I grow into, if I choose to grow?*

"I'd like that," she said to Tam. "The telling. Every night."

"Every night," he agreed, and his voice broke on the second word, and nobody pretended otherwise.

* * *

That evening—it might have been evening; the mountain kept its own hours—the four of them gathered around her pallet, and Sable took a deep breath like a diver at the edge of the water.

"I have something for you," she said. "Not much. A fragment. One of the ones I took before the gate, so the warden would read your mind and find it empty. I've been carrying it for days, and I—" her hands shook, and she steadied them with visible effort— "I want you to know that giving memories back is harder than taking them. Taking, you can pretend you're doing the person a favor. Giving, you have to watch them realize what was lost." She looked around at the others. Iven nodded. Dara nodded. Tam took Sable's free hand without being asked. "So. Here's the first one. It's small. It's a bridge, at dusk, and you're laughing, and someone says your name for the first time as a friend says it. Ready?"

Lior wasn't ready. She said so. Sable smiled, sad and bright as a lamp in fog.

"Nobody's ever ready," she said. "Close your eyes anyway."

Lior closed her eyes. She felt Sable's fingertips rest featherlight against her temple, felt something loosen—like a knot untied after years, like a window opened in a sealed house—and then,

*dusk, and the bridge singing under her boots, and wind off the river carrying woodsmoke, and Dara saying her name—* "Lior" *—not as a target, not as a charge, but the way you say a thing you're glad exists, and the sound of it going through her like a struck bell, and her own laugh answering, high and astonished, the laugh of a girl who had never in her life been glad out loud—*

It lasted three seconds. Then it was gone, folding back into the amber dark, leaving only the shape of its absence and one blazing certainty:

*I had a name, and it meant something, and I will have it again.*

Lior opened her eyes. She was crying. She didn't know when that had started, and she found she didn't care.

"Again," she said.

Sable wiped her cheeks with the back of her hand and grinned through it, fierce as sunrise. "Again," she agreed. "And again, and again, however many it takes. We've got time now—all of us. That's the whole point of what you bought."
