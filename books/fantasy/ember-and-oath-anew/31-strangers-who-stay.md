## 31. Strangers Who Stay

The mountain was still breathing.

Sable felt it in the stone beneath her knees—in the slow, shuddering exhale that came up through the ruined vault and out through the shattered gates above, carrying the smell of burnt iron and freed souls. Somewhere far overhead, snow was falling into a wound in the world, and the world did not seem to mind.

She knelt beside Lior Hartwell and held a memory in her cupped hands like water she was afraid to spill.

It had been three days since the vessel opened. Three days since Tam rose out of his sister's chest like a flame given legs, since Lior's eyes went wide and then—wider, and then empty, the way a house goes empty when everyone you love has walked out of it at once. Three days of Lior waking in the smugglers' quarters Iven had found them, deep in the mountain's abandoned service ways, looking at four strangers with the polite, frightened courtesy of someone meeting new neighbors.

Three days of Sable holding every hour of Lior's journey inside her own skull, feeling them press against the backs of her eyes like birds against glass.

"You don't have to," Dara had said that morning, arms crossed, voice low. "She's alive. She's safe. Let her build something new."

"She built something already," Sable said. "I'm just giving it back."

And it was true, and it wasn't, and both truths sat in her chest like two stones in the same pocket, knocking together. Because here was the thing about being a memory-thief that no one ever understood until they watched one work: taking was easy. Keeping was easy. Keeping was the whole trade, the whole temptation, the whole quiet rot of it. Every thief Sable had ever known kept something. A first kiss. A mother's lullaby. A face from a market twenty years gone. You told yourself you were preserving it, safeguarding it, doing the owner a kindness—and then one night you took it out and turned it over in the dark, and it was yours, and you could not remember anymore whether it had ever been theirs.

Sable had sold her childhood before she was old enough to know what a childhood was for. She had spent her whole life buying back other people's yesterdays because she could not bear to have none of her own. And now she sat with a fortune of borrowed light—the entire road from the Hollow Valley to the heart of the mountain, every mile of it worn by a girl who had called her *family* without knowing what the word would cost—and she was going to give all of it away.

Every fragment. Even the ones that fit so perfectly in her hands.

Especially those.

* * *

Lior was sitting up when Sable came to her, propped against the wall with a blanket around her shoulders, watching the ember-light of the broken vault seep through the doorway in long amber fingers. The light was different now. Sable had noticed it the first night: the jars were gone, shattered, their souls flown home or scattered free, but the ember-glow remained in the stone itself, soaked in over decades, the way wine stains a cup. The mountain glowed faintly everywhere now, like a banked fire that no one would ever put out.

"Hello again," Lior said. Her voice was careful. Polite. It made Sable want to break something. "You're the one who keeps coming."

"I'm the one who keeps coming," Sable agreed. She sat down across from her, cross-legged, close enough to touch and not touching. "I have something for you. Well. Several somethings. About four hundred miles' worth."

Lior blinked. "I don't understand."

"No," Sable said gently. "I know. That's rather the point." She flexed her fingers. They ached. Memory-work always ached on the return trip; taking was a theft, but giving was a surgery. "Here's how this goes. I hold a piece of your road. I press it back where it belongs. It'll feel like remembering a dream—fast, and bright, and then just *true*. Some of it will hurt. One piece of it will hurt more than the rest, and I'm telling you now so you can hate me in advance instead of after."

"Hate you," Lior repeated, tasting the word like an unfamiliar spice. "I don't think I could. I've been watching you come in here for three days. You look like someone carrying something heavy up a very long stair." She tilted her head. "What's your name? Nobody's told me. Or they have, and I've lost it, which seems to be the shape of my whole life now."

The question landed harder than Sable expected. She had spent years making herself unmemorable—a professional necessity, a survival art. Names were handles; handles got gripped. But hearing Lior ask, open-faced and honest, made her want suddenly, violently, to be memorable.

"Sable," she said. "Sable Vane. Memory-thief, retired as of—" she glanced at the glowing stone, "—as of recently, if I have anything to say about it. Which I do. I'm very good at saying things about things."

"That sounds like something a friend would say."

"It is exactly what a friend would say. Your taste in friends is impeccable." Sable swallowed. "Ready?"

"No," Lior said. Then, with a ghost of something that might once have been stubbornness: "Yes. Do it."

Sable reached out and took her hand.

The first fragment was small on purpose. She'd sorted them in the nights while Lior slept, arranging them like a jeweler sorts stones—not by size but by weight, by what they'd cost the girl who lived them. This one was nearly weightless: bread. A bakery in Corren's Landing, dawn-grey, the smell of woodsmoke and yeast, Lior's fingers closing around a loaf warm enough to burn, and Sable's own voice hissing *amateur* in her ear, and the alley, and the running, and the laughing afterward—

Lior gasped. Her hand clenched around Sable's. For a moment her eyes weren't empty at all; they were full of grey morning light and flour dust.

"Bread," she whispered. "I stole—I was stealing bread and you caught me. No—you *saved* me. There were guards—" She pressed her free hand flat against her sternum. "I can smell it. How can I still smell it?"

"Because it's yours," Sable said. "It always was. I was only keeping it warm." She managed a crooked smile. "One down. Four hundred miles to go."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the morning became afternoon, and the mountain hummed its low ember-hum around them like a mother singing to a house that had lost its children.

Sable gave her the roads. That was how she thought of them, in the sorting: *the roads.* The salt city of Vell with its lantern-fishermen and its hidden network of the gifted. The bridge of small mercies, black water sliding beneath, and the ferry-wight's voice like wet gravel. The ruins where they'd camped and told each other one true thing apiece—she gave Lior that one early, because it was load-bearing, because everything else they'd built stood on top of it.

Each time, the same ritual: Sable's palm against Lior's, the fragment passing between them like a coal passed hand to hand, Lior's breath catching, her body remembering before her mind did. Her shoulders loosening. Her chin lifting. Little by little, the stranger on the blanket began to sit like Lior Hartwell—spine straight, jaw set, that particular tilt of the head that meant she was about to argue with someone older than her.

"The ruins," Lior said after that fragment settled. "We told true things. I told—" Her brow furrowed. "There's a hole there. In the middle of it. Like a tooth knocked out."

"That's mine," Sable said quietly. "You'll get it last. It's the heaviest."

"What is it?"

Sable looked at the ember-light on the wall. "You'll see."

Dara came by in the late afternoon with food—real food, hoarded from the garrison stores the deserters had thrown open, hard cheese and dark bread and dried apples that tasted of nothing much and everything at once. The soldier moved differently now. Sable had watched it happen over three days: the constant coiled readiness draining out of her by degrees, replaced by something rawer and less armored. She set the plate down and crouched, eye-level with Lior, studying her with the frank assessment of a woman used to reading faces for a living.

"Do you know me?" Dara asked.

Lior frowned, reaching. "Tall. Scary. Kind, underneath, in a way you'd deny under torture." She paused. "That's not a memory. That's just—you. Isn't it? I can tell it just by looking at you."

Something moved through Dara's face like weather. "Yeah," she said gruffly. "That's me." She stood, cleared her throat, and left with the abruptness of a woman fleeing a battlefield she couldn't fight in.

"She's crying," Lior observed when the footsteps faded.

"She'd gut you for saying that."

"Noted." Lior ate her apple slice and looked at Sable with those clear, unburdened eyes. "Give me another."

* * *

By evening, Sable's hands shook. Giving was worse than taking. Taking was a sleight, quick and clean; giving was opening a vein and letting it run backward. Each fragment left her lighter and emptier, and the terrible thing—the thing she would never say aloud—was how much of the lightness felt like loss.

She gave her Iven. The smuggler's den, the mage's ink-stained fingers hovering over the ember-mark on Lior's wrist, the moment recognition cracked his careful face open like a dropped plate. Lior laughed at that one, actually laughed, a sound that made Iven himself look up from his books across the room with an expression of stunned, cautious hope.

"Iven Ashgrave," Lior said, testing it. "Court mage. Disgraced. Betrayed his students and has been paying for it ever since, and he'd pay forever if we let him, so we don't let him." She nodded slowly. "He reads when he's frightened. He's frightened now, isn't he? Of me."

"He's frightened you won't come back to yourself," Sable said. "We all are."

"And if I don't?"

Sable held her gaze. "Then we learn who you are now. And we keep her too."

Lior was quiet for a long moment. "You talk like someone who's done this before. Losing someone and finding them again in a different shape."

"I talk like someone who's stolen enough memories to know that people are not their pasts," Sable said. "They're what they do with them." She flexed her aching fingers. "Which is why I'm not keeping a single one of these. Not one. Before you ask—yes, I could. Yes, some of them are—" *beautiful*, her traitor mind supplied, *the campfire, the word family, the way she looked at all of us like we were worth something*—"some of them are valuable. Doesn't matter. They're not mine. Nothing in my head is going to be borrowed again. Not ever."

Lior studied her. And though the memories of their journey were mostly still locked away in Sable's keeping, something in Lior's face said she understood anyway—understood in the bone-deep way of people who had also been containers, also been emptied, also been someone else's storage.

"Thank you," Lior said simply.

"Don't thank me yet. The heavy one's coming."

* * *

Night came down through the mountain's wound, and the others gathered without being asked. Iven closed his book. Dara leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, pretending she happened to be there. Tam stood apart, as he always did—three days old and twenty years displaced, wearing his sister's cheekbones and his own grief, unable to come closer and unable to leave.

Sable knelt in front of Lior one last time.

"This is the last one," she said. "The heaviest. I saved it because everything else needs it underneath it, the way a wall needs its foundation stone. Are you ready?"

"Tell me what it is first."

Sable took a breath. "It's a campfire. On the road south, after the ambush, after the bridge. Everyone was asleep except you. And I was awake, because I'm always awake, because sleeping means dreaming and my dreams are all other people's leftovers." Her voice wavered and she hated it and kept going. "And you looked at the four of us—all of us, even him"—a nod toward Tam, who flinched—"and you said it. You said we were your family. Not the guild. Not blood. Us. The thief and the deserter and the disgraced mage and the brother you'd never met. You said it like it was the most obvious thing in the world."

Lior's eyes had filled without her seeming to notice.

"And then," Sable said, "you asked me to take it."

"*What?*"

"For the plan. So the interrogators couldn't read it. You handed me the best thing that had ever happened to you and trusted me with it." Sable's vision blurred. She let it. "And I carried it for six hundred miles, and I told myself I was just keeping it safe, and I lied, because part of me wanted it. Part of me wanted to be somebody's family so badly I would have kept that memory in a jar and fed on it for the rest of my life." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, rough, impatient. "So this is me not doing that. This is me giving it back. All of it. Everything. I kept nothing, Lior. Do you hear me? I kept *nothing*, because you don't get to be loved secondhand."

The room had gone very still. Somewhere above, snow hissed into the burning mountain.

"Now," Sable whispered, and pressed her palm to Lior's, and let go.

* * *

It came back like sunrise.

Sable felt it leave her—the campfire warmth, the crackle of the flames, Dara's snores, Iven's murmured warding-cant, Tam's restless turning—and pass through the space between their hands like light through water, and land where it belonged. She felt the exact moment it arrived, because Lior made a sound Sable had never heard a person make: half sob, half laugh, the noise of a soul coming home to a house it had forgotten it owned.

"Oh," Lior breathed. "*Oh.*"

And then she was crying, and reaching, and Sable caught her—an actual embrace, which neither of them was built for, which they did badly, clinging like shipwreck survivors who had somehow become each other's wreckage and shore at once.

"I remember," Lior was saying into her shoulder. "I remember the fire, I remember the bridge, I remember the wight, I remember—Sable. *Sable.* Your name. I remember learning your name in an alley in Corren's Landing while I was holding stolen bread like an idiot—"

"A gifted idiot."

"A gifted idiot," Lior agreed, wetly, laughing. She pulled back and looked around the room, and Sable watched her do it—watched her turn to each of them in turn, gathering their names like a woman gathering spilled coins, saying them aloud one by one, deliberate as prayer.

"Dara Ferro. Hunt-captain who missed on purpose." Dara's jaw tightened; she didn't deny it. "Iven Ashgrave, who taught children magic and never forgave himself for what the court made him teach after." Iven's book slipped in his grip. "Tam." Her voice broke on it, gentled itself, tried again. "Tam Hartwell. My brother. Who I carried for twenty years without knowing, and who I would carry again, and who is *not allowed* to walk out of this room feeling guilty, I can see you doing it, stop it."

Tam made a sound that was almost a word. He didn't stop looking guilty. But he came closer—one step, two—and that was more than he'd managed in three days.

"And Sable Vane," Lior finished, turning back, gripping her hands. "Who sells yesterday and gives away today. Who kept nothing." She squeezed hard. "I remember everything. And the first thing I remember clearly, the thing that came back brightest—it wasn't the road, or the fear, or even the mountain. It was you. All of you. Around a fire. Being mine."

"Being yours," Sable echoed. Her voice came out wrecked. She decided not to care.

Outside, far above, the wind changed. Sable heard it before the others did—a shift in the note of the snow-hiss, a thinning, a settling. She had grown up listening to cities breathe, and she knew the sound a place made when its crisis passed and its ordinary life began to seep back into the gaps.

But beneath the wind, fainter, there was something else. Footsteps on the service stairs. Many of them, unhurried, and among them a voice calling down through the dark of the mountain—a voice rough with ash and freedom, a voice that belonged to the boy who had been a jar and was now a man:

"They're coming up from the valley," Tam called. "Dozens of them. Gifted, walking out of the west—and at their head, an old woman leaning on a staff, asking for Lior Hartwell by name."

Lior stood. The ember-light caught her face, and she was not the girl who had left the Hollow Valley, and she was not the stranger of three days ago, and she was not, quite, either of the sisters the world had broken between them. She was something new, and she was smiling.

"Maren," she said. "She came. After everything—she *came*."

Sable rose beside her, empty-handed and lighter than she had ever been, and followed her toward the stairs.
