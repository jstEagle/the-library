## 32. The Long Walk Home

The mountain began to die the way old soldiers do: slowly, then all at once, and mostly in the dark.

Dara Ferro felt it through her boots before she heard it—a long shudder rolling up from the deep places, like a giant turning over in its sleep. Dust sifted down through the ember-light of the great vault, catching gold in the air where hundreds of freed souls had been burning only hours ago. The jars lay broken around them in drifts of glass and ash, and the light they had released still hung in the corridors above like weather that refused to move on.

"Everyone up," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. "We walk now. We grieve later."

They were a strange procession. She counted them out of habit—she had spent eleven years counting people for the hunt, tallying names into ledgers, so counting was what her hands knew how to do even when her heart wanted nothing to do with it. Forty-one gifted souls, most of them gaunt and blinking, some of them weeping, one old man who kept touching his own chest as if checking that something had actually come home there. Behind them, deeper in the dark, Tam Hartwell moved like a young storm barely leashed, glass crunching under his feet, and behind *him* came Lior—no, not Lior, Dara corrected herself, not anymore, not yet again—the girl who had emptied herself out so her brother could stand upright in the world. The girl walked with Sable's hand in hers and Iven's cloak around her shoulders, and she looked at everything the way a newborn looks at fire: without a single memory to tell her whether it would hurt.

Dara looked away. Some things were too large to hold while you were also holding a sword.

* * *

The upper galleries were already coming apart. Vessic's engineers had built the Undercourt to last forever, but forever, it turned out, had depended on the harvest continuing. With the jars broken and the stolen power loosed back into the veins of the stone, the mountain seemed to have decided it no longer wished to be a prison, and prisons make poor architecture once their purpose dies.

A staircase collapsed ahead of them with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Dara stood at the front with her sword drawn—not against anything, just because the weight of it in her hand was the only prayer she'd ever learned—and shouted the column back twice before the dust settled enough to see the new route. Iven found it, of course. The disgraced mage walked with his ruined hands tucked in his sleeves and his eyes half-closed, reading the mountain's magic the way other men read tide-charts, and every time he raised one scarred finger and said *this way*, this way turned out to be true.

"You're enjoying this," Dara said, falling into step beside him on a bridge over a chasm where the ember-light rose like slow golden smoke.

"I am enjoying nothing." Iven's voice was dry as old paper. "I am merely competent, which at my age passes for joy."

Above them, far above, came the sound Dara had been listening for all day: the muffled thunder of an army routing. Vessic's host had come to seal the gates at midwinter and drink the mountain dry, and instead the mountain had drunk *them*—the re-gifted rising out of the kitchens and the kennels and the slave-galleries, deserters throwing down their pikes, ballistae turning on the throne guard because their captains' orders had stopped making sense the moment the sky filled with returning souls. Dara had heard the first screams four hours ago. Now it was mostly shouting, and running, and somewhere a bell that nobody had told to stop ringing.

"The gates will be open," she said. "An army that routs leaves doors behind it."

"An army that routs," Iven agreed, "leaves everything behind it. Including its shame. Mind the gap."

She minded the gap. She minded everything. Eleven years of hunting gifted children across three provinces had taught her exactly one useful skill for this day, and it was this: watch the exits, count the heads, keep moving.

Forty-one. She counted again at the next landing. Forty-one, plus four misfits who had never belonged anywhere else.

* * *

They came up out of the mountain through a service gate on the north face, into snow.

It fell soft and enormous, flakes the size of coins, filling the air so completely that the world beyond ten paces simply stopped existing. The battle-noise faded behind the white curtain until it sounded like a festival in another country. Dara stood in the drifts with the wind pulling at her cropped hair and realized her legs were shaking—not fear, just eleven years of held breath finally leaving her body all at once.

The freed gifted stumbled out into the snow after her, and here was the thing she would remember until she died: they stopped. All of them. They stood in the falling white and lifted their faces to it, and the ones who could do magic let it rise off their skin in small involuntary colors—blue sparks, green motes, a woman whose breath came out as tiny visible songs—and nobody burned them for it. Nobody wrote their names down. The snow didn't care what they were. It landed on the gifted and the ungifted alike, which was more justice than any court had offered in twenty years.

"We can't stay," Dara said, gently, to all of them at once. "The army's broken but armies mend. We walk tonight."

So they walked. South and west, toward the free cities, toward the valley none of them had heard of except in whispers—Hollow Valley, the place where magic couldn't follow, though Dara suspected that promise had died along with the harvest. The column strung out along a shepherd's track under the snowfall, forty-five souls moving through the white like a thread being pulled out of the mountain's hem.

Sable walked beside Lior at the rear, and Dara, glancing back more often than a soldier should, saw the thief leaning close every few minutes, murmuring. Giving pieces back. *Your name is Lior. You hate the cold. You made me laugh once on a bridge and pretended you hadn't.* Each fragment landed on the girl's face like snow landing on snow, and slowly, slowly, someone new was being assembled out of the parts of someone old. Dara did not know if that was resurrection or something stranger. She only knew that when Lior stumbled, Sable caught her before anyone else could move, as if her hands had learned the girl's weight by heart.

* * *

They made camp at dawn in the ruins of a waystation—old empire work, walls thick as a man is tall, roof half-fallen to let the sky in. Fires were lit. The gifted huddled close and began, haltingly, to tell each other their names. That was the sound of the camp, Dara thought, sitting apart with her back to a wall: not weeping, not cheering, just names, passed around the fires like bread.

Tam Hartwell came and sat near her. Up close he looked like his sister around the eyes, which was a cruel joke of the gods, since those were the eyes she'd given him back.

"You're the one who missed," he said.

Dara didn't flinch. "Once."

"Sable says you carry a list." He nodded at her breast pocket. "Names. Hunt-lists."

"It's finished," Dara said. "The hunt's dead. Vessic's running for whatever hole will take him."

"That's not what I asked."

The fire cracked. Somewhere across the camp, the old man who touched his own chest was teaching a child a song, badly, and both of them were laughing.

Dara took out the list.

Eleven years she had carried it. It had started as a captain's roster—names of the gifted registered in her district, addresses, ages, notes in her own neat hand. And then, the night she'd first been ordered to burn rather than register, it had become something else: a list of everyone she'd failed to save, written in the margins, added to in taverns and waystations and the backs of wagons, every name a debt she'd assumed she'd die owing. She had carried it through promotions she never wanted and orders she'd followed anyway, and the list had grown heavier every year, until carrying it had become the closest thing she had to a soul.

She unfolded it now, in the firelight, in front of the brother of the girl whose name sat third from the top.

And then she took out her knife.

"What are you doing?" Tam asked quietly.

"What I should have done eleven years ago." She set the blade against the paper. "You cross out the dead. That's how lists end—you strike the names of the ones you lost, and you mourn them, and the page closes." Her hand was steady. Eleven years of drill had seen to that. "But mine doesn't get to end that way. Because look."

She turned the page toward him, toward the firelight. Name after name after name, two hundred and six of them, and beside nearly every one, in the margin, small annotations in her hand: *alive. seen in Corren's Landing, autumn.* *alive. escaped east.* *alive—confirmed, the mountain.* Two hundred and six names, and the mountain had given back a hundred and nine of them tonight, and word was already moving down the column like fire through dry grass: *they're free, they're free, the jars broke and they're free.*

"So I finish it the only honest way left." Dara drew the knife's edge down the first line, slow, deliberate, striking the name through—not crossed out in grief but struck through in *grace*, the way you cancel a debt. Then the next. Then the next. The blade whispered through the ink and the firelight caught the paper and Tam watched her with those borrowed eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the scratching of the knife wasn't saying better.

Some of the names she struck, she wept for—quietly, the way soldiers weep, without changing expression, tears just falling off her jaw into the snow-melt at her boots. Some she struck laughing, short harsh barks of relief. Most she struck in silence, one after another, hour after hour, while the camp slept and the snow kept falling through the broken roof, and the list grew barer and barer until by grey morning light only a handful of lines remained unstruck—the ones still unaccounted for, scattered somewhere in the world, alive or dead, unknown.

She folded what was left of the page. It was mostly empty now. It weighed almost nothing.

"There," she said, to Tam, to the fire, to eleven years of her own reflection. "Finished."

"And now?" Tam asked.

"Now I find the rest of these names," Dara said, "and I cross them out too. Every single one. Alive, if I can manage it." She put the paper away, next to her heart, where it had always lived—but lighter now, so much lighter, a map instead of a ledger. "That's the work. Not the hunt. The other thing. The going-back-and-finding-them thing."

Tam smiled, and it was strange and new and had never been used before. "My sister would have liked you."

"She doesn't remember me."

"She will," Tam said. "Sable's giving her back a piece at a time. And when she gets to the part where four strangers became—" he paused, choosing, "—where we happened, you'll see. My sister collects people. It's the one talent the guild never put in a jar."

* * *

They broke camp at midmorning, and the column moved out south and west, forty-five strong, through a world gone white and quiet and, for the first time in Dara's adult life, unwritten—no rosters, no registers, no hunt-cries on the wind. Just the road, and the snow, and the long walk home to a valley any of them could describe and none of them could find.

Near dusk, with the mountains sinking purple behind them, a rider appeared on the road ahead—lathered horse, torn cloak, a courier's satchel bouncing at his hip. Dara's hand went to her sword on pure reflex before she saw the man was alone, unarmed, half-dead with exhaustion, and crying.

He slid from the saddle and stood swaying in the road, staring at the column of the freed, and then he pulled a folded proclamation from his satchel and held it out with both hands like an offering.

"From Corren's Landing," he gasped. "Word's outrun me by a day, they said I'd find you on this road—they said to look for the soldier with the—" he faltered, looking at her face, at the insignia she still wore because she'd forgotten to take it off, "—you're Captain Ferro?"

"Was."

"They need you in the city." He swallowed. "Vessic's fled north, but his sorcerers didn't—all seven of them, holed up in the citadel with hostages, and the city's calling for anyone who knows how the hunt thinks. They're saying—" he steadied himself, "—they're saying the woman who deserted is the only one who ever beat them at their own game. There's a price on your head, Captain. And there's a crowd at the gates chanting your name."

Behind Dara, the column had stopped. Sable stood with Lior's hand in hers; Iven raised one eyebrow; Tam watched with that brand-new smile.

Dara looked down the long white road toward the distant smudge of the city, then back at the people she'd sworn, somewhere in the night without saying it aloud, to see safely home.

"Well," she said, and slung her shield off her back. "They can chant from the walls. We all walk in together—or not at all."
