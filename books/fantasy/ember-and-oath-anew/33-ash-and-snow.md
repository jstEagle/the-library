## 33. Ash and Snow

The mountain was still breathing smoke when Tam decided to leave.

Not the smoke of burning—though there had been plenty of that, the garrison's stores going up in gouts of orange that stained the snow like wounds—but the older smoke, the deep-smelling kind, resin and cold stone and something almost sweet beneath it. The smell of a hundred years of ember-light. He had lived inside that light for twenty years, folded small in glass, and now that he stood in it with his own legs and his own lungs, he found he could not bear it.

He packed nothing. There was nothing to pack. The clothes on his back had been given to him by a freed dyer from Vell who wept while she dressed him, as if clothing a ghost. His boots were two sizes too large. He had not asked anyone's name. That was deliberate. Names were debts, and he intended to leave before he owed any more.

It was the hour before dawn, the hour he knew best—the hour when the vault's ember-light had always dimmed to its lowest ember, when the jars went quiet and even the brokers slept. He moved through the corridors of the Undercourt like a man walking through his own funeral. Scorch marks climbed the walls where Dara's deserters had turned their ballistae inward. Broken glass glittered in every gutter of the floor, ground fine underfoot into a grit that crunched like frost. Somewhere below, work crews were already prying the great harvest-engines apart, hauling out copper coils and crystal lenses that had drunk the valley's magic for a century. The sound of it carried up through the stone: a slow, patient dismantling, the sound of an age ending.

Tam kept to the shadows anyway. Old habit. A soul in a jar learns to be small.

He passed the vault itself and made himself look. The doors stood open, warped off their hinges, and inside, the racks stood empty—hundreds of empty brackets, hundreds of little rings of residue where the jars had sat, each one a pale circle on the metal like the ghost of a mouth. The freed souls had gone out of them like breath from a room. Some had found bodies waiting in the pens below; some had simply risen, formless and furious, and poured themselves into the rebellion like oil into flame. And one—*his*—had walked out on its own legs, wearing his sister's face.

He could not look long. He turned away and climbed.

The way out was not the way in. The gate where Dara had driven Lior through as merchandise had been sealed from outside by Vessic's army, and then unsealed by force, and then half-collapsed in the doing. Tam took the smugglers' stair instead, the one Iven had mapped for them in the last desperate hours—a crack in the mountain's shoulder, narrow and black, breathing cold air down onto his face like the mountain exhaling after a century of holding its breath.

Snow met him at the top.

It came down soft and enormous, flakes the size of coins, filling the pass below the mountain in slow white silence. The war above had trampled it to gray slush near the gates, but here, away from the roads, it lay deep and unbroken, and the sight of it stopped him with a pain he hadn't expected. He had never seen snow. Twenty years in glass, and before that—before that was a blur, a warm kitchen, a girl's laugh, a door closing. The valley had taken his memories when it took him, or Maren had, or the trade itself had; all he had left of his first life was a shape without details, a hunger where a childhood should have been.

He crouched and put his hand into it. Cold bit through his palm, clean and total, and he almost laughed. *This,* he thought. *I would have traded all of it for this.* One handful of snow. One morning of cold on his face. Instead they had given the valley a hundred safe years and given him a jar, and given Lior—

Given Lior everything. Given her his name and his face in her mirror, his soul coiled in her chest like a second heartbeat, his memories dissolving into hers until she could not tell which sibling she had been. She had walked through the world for twenty years being both of them, and none of them—not Maren, not the guild, not the broker who sold him—had thought that worth mentioning.

And then she had opened herself like a door and let him out, and every memory she owned had poured away with him, and now she sat somewhere behind him in the dark of the mountain, waking up a stranger among friends, knowing nothing, not even her own name—and he was the reason.

He stood. He brushed the snow from his hand as if it had burned him.

*I'm sorry* was a coin too small to pay this debt. So he wouldn't try to pay it. He would simply go, and take the debt with him, and let her build whatever life she could from the wreckage without his shadow across it. Every time she looked at him she would see what it cost. Every time he looked at her he would see the same. Better for both of them if the looking never happened at all.

That was the argument he made to himself, descending the pass alone in the falling snow, and it was a good argument, and it was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, because the truth was simpler and worse: he could not stand to be forgiven.

* * *

She was waiting for him at the tree line.

He saw her from fifty paces and stopped so hard he slid in the snow. She sat on a fallen trunk at the edge of the pines, wrapped in a gray cloak with the hood down, snow gathering in her hair—her hair, which was his hair, the same dark red-brown, though on her it fell longer now, unkempt, unwashed, a stranger's hair. Her hands were bare and folded in her lap, and she watched him come with an expression he couldn't read, because he had never learned to read her. The sister he'd known for six days and the sister she'd been for twenty years were not the same person, and neither one of them was the person sitting on the log.

"You walk loudly," she said, "for someone trying to sneak away."

"I—" His voice cracked. He hadn't spoken much since waking. "You're supposed to be resting."

"Everyone keeps saying that." She tilted her head. "Sable says I slept for three days. I don't remember sleeping. I don't remember anything, really. It's very strange." She said it lightly, the way you'd comment on weather, and that lightness frightened him more than anger would have. "Sable's giving pieces back. Little ones. This morning I got the taste of campfire bread and someone's laugh—I don't know whose. She won't tell me whose. She says I have to earn them back one at a time." A pause. "Dara told me you left your bunk before dawn. She said I should let you go, if going was what I needed." Another pause, longer. "I decided Dara is very wise about most things and wrong about this one."

"Lior." Her name came out of him like a wound. "You don't even know me."

"No," she agreed. "Tell me about it."

"You gave up *everything* for me. Your name. Your memories. Twenty years of being a person, and I—" His hands were shaking; he shoved them into his pockets. "I was in a jar. I didn't ask for any of this. I didn't ask to be bought back at that price, and I can't—every time you look at me, you'll see what it cost, and I'll see it too, and neither of us will ever be able to stop seeing it. So the kindest thing—the only thing—is for me to go, and let you become whoever you're going to become without me standing in the middle of it like a bill that's come due."

She listened to all of it without moving. Snow accumulated on her shoulders. When he finished, she was quiet for a moment, and then she said, "Sable taught me a word yesterday. *Vessel.* She used it about me, before. Before I was me again, or whoever I am now. Do you know what a vessel is, Tam?"

"A container."

"A container," she said, "is something that holds what matters and lets the rest pour through. That's what I was. For twenty years I held you, and I didn't know it, and the guild didn't tell me, and the world used me as furniture for your soul." Her voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep, like ice shifting on a lake. "So here's what I've decided, in the four days of being a person that I actually own: I am done being a vessel. Not for you. Not for the guild. Not for anybody's guilt—including yours. You do not get to leave to make yourself feel less guilty. That's just another way of using me. You'd be deciding my life is better without you in it, and you've known me for six days. You are not qualified."

He stared at her. Something between a laugh and a sob caught in his throat. "You sound like someone who's been alive longer than a week."

"I sound," she said, "like someone who got a memory back this morning. Sable gave me one while you were packing your non-existent bag. Want to hear it?"

He didn't answer. She told him anyway.

"There's a kitchen," she said. "Small. A woodstove with a loose brick, and behind the loose brick there's a tin box, and in the tin box there's honey candy that Mother thinks we don't know about. You're maybe five. You've figured out the brick trick, and you're standing on a stool with the tin in your hands, and you look over your shoulder because you heard the floor creak, and there's a girl in the doorway—younger than you, tiny, furious—and she doesn't yell. She holds out her hand. Palm up. Like a little highwayman. And you give her one candy, because you're scared of her, and also because you love her, and those felt exactly the same." Lior's voice wavered, just once, and steadied. "That girl was me. That's mine. That's the only thing I have from before, and it's yours too, and you were going to walk out of my life over a handful of snow and some guilt, and I want you to sit down on this log and think about how that would have gone for you."

Tam sat down on the log.

The snow fell. Somewhere up the pass, the mountain groaned as another piece of the harvest-engine came free, a long metallic sigh rolling down through the trees. He became aware that he was crying, quietly, the way you cry when you've forgotten how, and that she was watching him do it without pity and without judgment, the way you watch weather.

"I don't know how to be your brother," he said finally. "I don't know how. I have six days of you and a candy memory. You have one candy memory and no self. We're—we're two strangers who share a face."

"Yes," Lior said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

He turned to look at her. She was smiling—small, crooked, real.

"Everyone keeps telling me who I was," she said. "Lior Hartwell, oath-keeper, brave, self-sacrificing, gave up everything, what a saint, light a candle. And I appreciate it, I do, but none of it is mine yet. It's like wearing someone else's coat. But this—" she touched her own chest, over the heart, where the ember-mark had faded to a pale scar, "—this morning, hearing that candy memory, I felt like myself for the first time. Not the old me. Not the vessel. Just—me. Whoever that turns out to be. And you were in it. You were five years old and stealing honey candy and you shared it with a tiny terrifying girl, and that's the beginning of something. Not the continuation of something. The beginning." She held out her hand, palm up, exactly as the memory-girl had done. "Hi. I'm Lior. Apparently."

Tam looked at the offered hand for a long time.

Then he laughed—wet, broken, helpless—and dug in his pocket, and pressed something into her palm. Not candy. The compass. Maren's compass, Tam's compass, the brass worn smooth, the needle swinging wild and useless this far north where the mountain's iron confused it. "I was going to leave it," he admitted. "On your bunk. Seemed fitting. The family tradition of leaving people things instead of staying."

"We'll work on that," Lior said. She closed her fingers around the compass. "One day at a time. You steal something, I extort a toll, we call it breakfast. That's a relationship. That's practically a childhood."

"It's a protection racket."

"It's a *start*." She stood, brushing snow from her cloak, and offered him her hand again—properly this time, to pull him up. He took it. Her grip was stronger than he expected, and warm, and when he was on his feet they stood there a moment in the falling snow, two strangers with one face, holding on.

Behind them, up the pass, voices were carrying down through the trees—Sable's bright and sharp, Dara's low and amused, Iven's dry commentary threading between them. The others, coming to find them, or coming to drag them to breakfast, or simply unable to stand a morning where the family split along the seams. Lior cocked her head, listening, and something in her face shifted—recognition assembling itself out of fragments, a name here, a laugh there.

"That one's Sable," she said, pointing through the pines. "I'd know that voice anywhere. She cried over me for three days and pretends she didn't." She glanced sideways at Tam, and her crooked smile turned conspiratorial, and for one strange suspended moment he saw it—the girl in the doorway, palm out, five years old and absolutely certain she would win. "Come on, brother. They'll be worried. And I'm told I'm owed a piece of my memory tonight, and I want witnesses when I get it."

They walked back together through the ash and the snow, toward the voices, toward the fire, toward the long unfinished business of becoming—whoever they were now. Two siblings with one memory between them and forty years still ahead.

And far to the south, past the smoking passes and the broken gates, the road ran home toward a valley that had forgotten them both—and that, very soon now, was going to have to learn to remember.
