## 35. Judgment of the Guild

The valley had changed its voice.

Maren heard it before she opened her eyes on the morning of the judgment—heard it in the way the wind came down off the rim no longer muffled and dead but carrying things: birdsong from three ridges away, the creak of the well-rope, someone laughing by the eastern terraces. For ninety years the Hollow Valley had swallowed sound the way it swallowed magic, pressing everything close and small, a hand cupped over a candle flame. Now the wind moved through it like a living thing through an open door, and every sound it carried was a reminder of what they had lost and what they had been given back, all at once, by a girl who could not remember giving it.

She rose before dawn because she had always risen before dawn, and because she did not think she would sleep again anyway. Her bones ached in the old way, the honest way, and her hands shook as she dressed—not with age, though there was that too, but with the particular tremor of a body that knew what was coming and could not be reasoned out of it.

In the great hall, the ash-jars stood in their rows along the northern wall, and Maren stopped before them as she had stopped every morning for sixty years. But the jars were empty now. Every one. The souls within them had gone home weeks ago, riding Tam Hartwell's fury out of the mountain and across the free cities to whatever bodies still waited for them—and those whose bodies were long burned or buried had gone somewhere else entirely, some bright dissolution the freed mages spoke of in hushed voices, like people describing a country none of them had visited but all believed in.

Empty jars. She had spent her life guarding emptiness and calling it safety.

"You're up early, Elder."

Brannic stood in the doorway, the guild's master of grounds, a broad gray man who had dug graves for two generations of keepers and never once asked what lay beneath the floor of Maren's certainty. He held two cups of barley tea and offered her one without ceremony.

"I'm not elder," Maren said. "Not after today."

"Old habit." He drank. "The hall's filling already. Everyone came back for it. Even the ones who swore they'd left the guild behind forever."

Of course they had. When word had spread down the road—the wards fallen, the harvest ended, Lior Hartwell returned from the Undercourt with her brother alive and her own name scrubbed clean out of her head—the scattered children of the Ember Guild had turned homeward like iron filings finding a lodestone. Some came out of love. Some came out of guilt. Most, Maren suspected, came because when the world breaks open, you want to stand in the place where it broke and see it with your own eyes.

And because they wanted a judgment. She could feel it in the way people looked at her now, in the corridor, at the well, across the terraces. Not hatred—that would have been simpler. It was hunger. They wanted someone to pay for ninety years of hiding, and Maren had spent the whole walk home composing the payment in her head until it sat in her chest like a stone she had swallowed.

* * *

The guild hall had never held so many.

They filled the benches and stood along the walls and crowded the open doors, and beyond the doors, through the great arched windows that had been shuttered for a century and now stood flung wide, Maren could see people gathered on the terraces outside, unable to fit, unwilling to leave. Sunlight fell into the hall in long unbroken sheets. She kept thinking of it as an intrusion, and kept correcting herself. It was only light. Light had always been allowed here. They had simply preferred the dark, and called the preference wisdom.

Lior sat at the center of the hall on the plain wooden chair that had been the elder's seat since before Maren's time—but she did not sit like an elder. She sat like a girl in a borrowed chair, hands folded in her lap, watching the crowd with the careful, courteous attention of someone meeting a room full of strangers. Which they were. All of them. Even the ones who had known her since birth, even Sable and Iven and Dara standing near her like a wall of quiet insistence—even them, in the ways that mattered most. The journey memories had come back to her in fragments, Sable returning them piece by piece like a woman paying off a debt in coins counted out one at a time. But the deeper years, the valley years, the twenty years of being Lior Hartwell of the Hollow Valley—those were gone, spent, poured out of her in the Undercourt so that her brother could walk free.

Maren had watched her try, gently, patiently, to relearn her own childhood. Watched Brannic tell her stories of the girl who used to steal plums off the high branches and swear she'd climb down before anyone saw. Watched Lior listen with a polite smile that never reached her eyes, because how could it? A story about a stranger is only ever a story.

*You made her,* Maren thought, standing at the edge of the hall, waiting for the moment she had rehearsed a hundred times. *You didn't just sell her brother. You built her out of him, and then you hid her where she'd never have to know what she was.*

Tam stood at his sister's shoulder. He had barely left it since the mountain. He was thinner than the boy Maren remembered—or thought she remembered; her memory of Tam was itself a curated thing, trimmed and tidied by twenty years of guilt—and he carried himself with a watchfulness that no child raised in this soft valley should have needed. Sometimes his eyes found Maren across a room, and there was nothing cruel in them, which was somehow worse than cruelty. There was only the question he was too kind to ask aloud: *Did you know? Did you look at me all those years and know what you'd done?*

She had known. That was the whole of it. She had known, and she had knelt each morning before the empty jar that had once held him, and she had told herself it was grief, when it was really just arithmetic wearing grief's clothes.

Elder Corvane rose first. Old, sharp-voiced Corvane, who had argued against the founding bargain even in the years when arguing was dangerous, and who had wept alone in her quarters the night the wards fell—not from joy, Maren knew, but from vindication arriving forty years too late to matter.

"The guild is assembled," Corvane said, and her voice carried to the last terrace. "We are here to answer for ourselves. And first—" she turned, and her old eyes found Maren with terrible gentleness, "—first we must hear from the one who kept the ledger."

* * *

Maren walked to the center of the hall, and the sound of her own footsteps was very loud.

She had prepared a speech. She had written it out three times and burned it twice, and what remained was not a speech at all but a handful of sentences she had chosen the way you choose stones for a cairn—one to mark the fact of it, one to mark the weight, one to mark the place where the walking stops.

"I was born in this valley," she began. "I was sworn to this guild at fourteen, and I swore the oath believing every word of it—that we hid the gifted to save them, that the valley's silence was mercy, that the price paid at the founding was paid once and long ago and not by anyone I had to look in the face." She paused. "Twenty-two years ago, King Vessic's men came to the rim with an offer. Not an army. An offer. The valley's invisibility, renewed for a century, in exchange for one soul, delivered to the Undercourt. And there was a boy in our care whose gift was strong enough to interest them. His parents had died in the fever year. He had no one to speak for him except us."

Someone in the back made a small sound. Maren did not look to see who.

"We told ourselves it was arithmetic. One soul against four hundred. We told ourselves he would be kept, not destroyed—the brokers promised us he would be kept, and we let ourselves believe that a promise from the Undercourt was worth the air it rode on. And when the vessel was made—" her voice caught, and she let it catch; she would not smooth this over, she owed them the sound of it breaking, "—when the vessel was made to hold what we sold, we brought her here. A baby, soul-bound to her brother's stolen self, grown in a broker's glass. We named her Lior. We gave her a cot and a chore list and a place at the festival fire, and we told ourselves that keeping her safe from knowing was the same thing as loving her."

"It wasn't," said a voice—it might have been Brannic, it might have been a dozen someones—and Maren nodded.

"No. It wasn't. Love tells the truth or it isn't love. We chose the lie for twenty years because the lie was comfortable and the truth would have cost us everything we thought we were." She turned then, slowly, and faced the chair at the center of the hall, and faced the girl sitting in it. "Lior Hartwell. I don't ask your forgiveness. You don't know me well enough to forgive me, and I know that is my doing too. I ask only for judgment. Whatever you decide, the guild will carry it, and I will carry it, and we will not call it anything but what it is."

Then Maren Okonkwo, sixty-one years keeper of the Ember Guild, knelt.

Her knees cracked on the stone and she felt the cold of it rise up through her skirts, and she bowed her head, and she waited.

* * *

The silence went on so long that Maren could hear the wind moving through the open windows, could hear a bird somewhere on the southern rim singing a song that no bird had sung inside these wards in ninety years, because the wards had eaten songs too.

When Lior finally spoke, her voice was quiet, and it wavered, and Maren realized with a strange distant ache that the girl was frightened. Of course she was. They had handed her a room full of wounded people and a kneeling elder and expected her to perform justice in a language she no longer owned.

"Stand up," Lior said. "Please. I can't—I can't do this with you down there."

"Judgment requires—"

"I don't know what judgment requires." A ripple went through the hall—not anger, something rawer. "I don't remember the oaths. I don't remember the laws. I don't remember *you*, Elder Maren, and I have spent weeks trying to, because everyone looks at me like I should, like there's a shape where you're supposed to be and I keep reaching into it and finding nothing." Her hands had tightened in her lap. Tam put a hand on her shoulder and she drew a breath and steadied. "So I'll tell you what I do know. I know what it's like to find out that the people who kept you safe also kept things from you. I know what it costs. And I know—" her voice dropped, almost to herself, "—I know that when I woke up in the dark with no name, the worst part wasn't losing everything. It was learning that strangers had stayed anyway. That they refused to let me be nobody. So."

She rose from the chair. She crossed the space between them, and Maren watched her boots stop an arm's length away, and she did not raise her head.

"I'm not going to exile you," Lior said. "And I'm not going to take your staff, if you're offering it, which I think you are, because everyone keeps telling me you will."

Maren's breath went out of her. "Child—"

"Don't *child* me." No heat in it. Just weariness, and under the weariness, steel—the same steel, Maren suddenly understood, that had walked into the Undercourt with no memories and come out having unmade a king's greatest treasure. "You want punishment because punishment is easy. Punishment ends. You kneel, I sentence you, you walk out the gate, and everyone gets to feel clean, and nothing changes. The guild goes on doing exactly what it did, just with a different elder and a better story." She crouched then, down to Maren's level, and for the first time their eyes met—green eyes, Tam's eyes, the eyes Maren had seen in a broker's mirror twenty-two years ago and pretended not to recognize. "I don't want you to leave. I want you to stay and fix what you broke. With your hands. In front of everyone. For the rest of your life. That's heavier than exile, Elder. I'm choosing the heavier thing."

The hall had gone utterly still.

"Stay," Maren repeated. The word felt like a key turning in a lock she had assumed was rusted shut forever.

"Teach them," Lior said. "All of it. The ledger, the bargain, the vessel—every ugly page, out loud, to every new keeper who swears in. You don't get to burn the books anymore. You get to read them." She straightened. "That's my judgment. Not mercy. Work."

And Maren, kneeling on cold stone in a hall full of people she had lied to for a lifetime, began—to her own astonishment—to laugh. It came out ragged, half sob, and she pressed her wrist to her mouth to hold it in and failed, and around her she heard the hall exhale, a hundred voices releasing a hundred held breaths, and Brannic's big hand landed on her shoulder like a beam settling.

"Well," the old man rumbled. "That's the cruelest kindness I've ever heard."

"That's the point," said Sable from the front bench, dry-eyed and precise, though Maren noticed her fingers were white where they gripped the wood. "She learned it from watching us."

* * *

They swore no new oath that day. Lior refused that too—said an oath written in a single afternoon by guilty people was worth exactly what it sounded like, and that they would write it together, slowly, over the winter, every voice in the valley given a line. What they did instead was simpler and stranger: they ate. Long tables dragged out onto the terraces, the first open-air meal in the guild's history, under a sky that no longer pressed down like a lid. Maren sat at the end of the farthest table, where she belonged, among the newest and youngest keepers, and answered their questions until her voice gave out, and then answered more in writing, and found—to her bewilderment—that the stone in her chest had shifted, not gone, but moved to a place where she could carry it.

It was full dark when Lior found her at the terrace rail, a cup of tea in each hand, offering one the way Brannic had offered one that morning, as if kindness were just a thing you did with your hands regardless of history.

"You should know," Lior said, "I didn't do it for you."

"I know."

"I did it because I've been nobody recently, and I remember what it was like. Nobody doesn't need punishing. Nobody needs a job." She looked out past the rail, down the valley, where lights were appearing in windows that had been dark for a century—scattered, tentative, growing. "Also, Tam wants to talk to you. Tomorrow. He's been working up to it for days."

Maren's hands tightened around the cup. "What does he want?"

"He won't say. But he's been sitting with the old ledgers every night—the ones you didn't burn. The founding records." Lior turned to her, and in the lantern light her borrowed face was calm and her brother's eyes were not calm at all. "There's something in them, Maren. Something about the bargain that you haven't told us. I've seen him reading. I've seen what page he stops on."

Far below, at the valley's mouth, the wind came up the road—the open road, the remembering road—and it carried, faint and unmistakable, the sound of hoofbeats.
