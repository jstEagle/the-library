## 36. A New Oath

The valley had never looked like this.

Lior stood at the edge of the guildyard in the hour before dawn and watched the light come up over the Hollow, and it was not the light she had grown up with. For twenty years—no, for a hundred—the sun had entered this valley filtered, thinned, as though it too had been made to forget something on its way down through the wards. Now it fell whole. It fell the way light fell everywhere else in the world, gilding the ash-jar shelves through the open windows of the keeping-house, catching the dew on the long grass where no grass had ever grown so tall, laying a road of gold across the stream that ran louder than Lior remembered it running, as if the water too had spent a century speaking in whispers.

Magic lived here now. She could feel it the way she felt her own pulse: a low warm hum beneath everything, in the soil, in the stones of the guildhouse, in the old oath-stone that stood at the yard's center wearing its century of moss like a shroud it was slowly shrugging off. The moss was dying. The stone beneath was black volcanic glass, and when Lior touched it—and she touched it every morning, some new habit her emptied-and-refilled self had made—it hummed against her palm like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Behind her, the guildyard was filling.

They came from everywhere now. That was the thing she still could not quite believe, a year after the mountain, half a year since the first refugees had crept down the pass with their bundles and their burned papers and their eyes that flinched from every shadow: they *came*. Word traveled the way word always did, faster than armies, faster than grief. *There is a valley east of the salt road where they do not ask what you are. There is a guild that swears a new oath. There is a girl who opened herself like a door.*

The girl was twenty-two years old and felt sometimes like a house whose rooms had been rearranged by someone else while she slept. She knew her name because they had told it to her, and then told it again, and then told it so many times with such stubborn tenderness that it had finally taken root and become hers. She knew the taste of Sable's terrible campfire tea. She knew Dara's silence meant thinking and Iven's silence meant guilt and Tam's silence meant he was writing poetry he would never show anyone. She knew all of this the way you know a song learned from someone else's humming—by heart, but not by history.

And yet when she turned to face the gathering crowd, when she saw them arranging themselves in the rough crescent that Elder Maren had once used for the annual swearing, something in her body knew this place. Some part of her had stood here before, small and solemn, reciting words about hiding and ash and forgetting. The body remembered what the mind had surrendered. She had stopped resenting that. It was, she had decided, the closest thing to a fair trade the world had ever offered her: she had given up her past and received a future that needed her.

Maren came to stand beside her at the oath-stone.

The old woman moved slowly now, leaning on the staff she had tried to give away twice—once to Lior, once to the fire—and which had refused both times to burn or be relinquished, as if the staff itself had opinions about retirement. Her hair was white as the first frost. Her hands, which had buried ledgers and lit pyres and held a child's secret for twenty years, were steady as she arranged the pages on the stone's flat crown.

"You'll want to read it aloud yourself," Maren said. "Not me. Not ever again me."

"Maren—"

"I have sworn enough oaths for one life." The old woman's voice was dry as autumn leaves, but her eyes were wet as spring. "Most of them wrong. Let an old liar watch a young truth-teller work. It's the only penance I trust."

* * *

The crowd settled. Three hundred souls, maybe more—Lior had stopped counting, because counting them made them a number and numbers were how Vessic had thought of people, and she refused, in even the smallest arithmetic, to think like him. They sat on benches and blankets and upturned crates. Children ran the last laps of their morning games and were gathered in by mothers and fathers and the particular fierce gravity of parents who had crossed a war to keep their children unregistered, unlisted, unsold.

Near the front sat her family.

Sable wore her thief's grin like armor, but Lior had learned to read underneath it: today the grin was real, stretched wider than usual, because Sable loved ceremonies the way she loved locked doors—as invitations. Beside her, Iven Ashgrave sat with his hands folded and his back too straight, the posture of a man who had spent his life in courts and still did not know how to sit among free people without performing respectability. His disgrace had followed him out of the Undercourt like a scent; half the valley still watched him sideways. But he had rebuilt the guild's shattered archive with his own hands, cataloguing every jar-fragment and ledger-scrap, and last month he had begun teaching the children control exercises in the orchard, and Lior had heard one small girl call him *Master Ashgrave* in a voice full of uncomplicated adoration, and had seen him go white and walk into the trees afterward and stay there until dark.

Dara Ferro stood at the edge of the assembly, arms crossed, because Dara never sat if standing was possible and never stood if walking was possible. She had taken to patrolling the valley's rim each dawn—not guarding against anything anymore, there was nothing left to guard against, but because, she said, a soldier who stops walking starts rusting. Her captain's insignia was still in her pocket. Lior had seen her touch it sometimes, absently, the way you touch a scar.

And Tam.

Tam sat cross-legged at the very front, closest to the stone, the way he had insisted on sitting at every gathering since they'd come home. He was thinner than Lior imagined a brother should be—a hundred years of soul-light poured through a vessel leaves marks, and his marks were silver streaks in his dark hair and eyes that caught lamplight strangely—but he was *there*, solid and breathing and awkward, and every time Lior saw him her chest did the thing it did, the ache-that-was-not-an-ache, the feeling of a room in her heart furnished for someone she couldn't remember furnishing it for.

He raised his eyebrows at her. *Ready?*

She wasn't. She nodded anyway.

* * *

"Three hundred years ago," Lior began, and her voice carried farther than she expected—the valley itself seemed to lean in, magic lending its hum to human speech—"the Ember Guild swore its first oath. Most of you know it. Some of you fled from places where it was quoted at you like a threat."

She let the silence hold. Then she spoke the old words, and the crowd went still around her, because everyone knew them, everyone had grown up inside them or run from them:

*"We keep the ember hidden. We keep the gifted silent. We pour our light into jars of ash, we seal our names from every ledger, and we teach our children to be small. What the world will not shelter, we will bury. What the world would burn, we will extinguish ourselves. So we survive. So we endure. So we forget."*

A hundred years those words had been spoken in this yard. A hundred years of children learning to shrink. Lior had spoken them herself, eleven years old, voice cracking, proud of the weight of them—and had not known, could not have known, that the price of that survival had already been paid, that her own name had already been spent, that the smallest child in the yard was the payment.

"So we survived," she said quietly. "So we endured. And we forgot my brother."

Tam didn't flinch. They had done this work together, the two of them, in the long winter evenings—grief without erasure, anger without blame. He met her eyes and nodded once: *say it.*

"The old oath kept us alive," Lior said. "I will not dishonor the dead who kept it. My mother kept it. Maren kept it. Every keeper who tended the jars and taught the hiding and died unknown kept it, and they bought us a hundred years, and I am grateful down to the marrow of me." She drew a breath. "But gratitude is not obedience. And survival is not the same as living. We learned that in the mountain. We learned it in the burning cities. We learned it in every year we hid while the world outside got crueler and the jars got fuller."

She lifted the pages Maren had laid on the stone. Written in Iven's precise court-hand, amended in Sable's slanted scrawl, argued over in Dara's blunt marginal notes, blessed in Maren's trembling signature, and copied out at last in Lior's own hand because they had all agreed, in the end, that the oath should live in the handwriting of the one who had paid most for the old one.

"This is the oath of the Ember Guild, rewritten. Sworn for the first time under open sky. If any soul here cannot swear it, leave now with our love and no shame—we have had enough of oaths that bind the unwilling."

No one moved. Somewhere in the crowd a baby laughed, bright as a bell, and no one hushed it.

Lior read.

*"We keep the ember burning. We keep the gifted safe—not hidden, not silent, not small. We pour our light into no jars but our own lives, and we spend those lives gladly for one another. We write our names in every ledger that asks, and dare the world to come collect. What the world will not shelter, we will shelter. What the world would burn, we will stand between it and the flame. So we live. So we remember. So we remain."*

The words went out over the yard and did not echo, because the valley caught them, held them, hummed them back through the ground and the stones and the roots of the orchard trees, and Lior felt the oath land somewhere deeper than ears—in the wardless air, in the freed soil, in the glass of the old oath-stone, which rang, audibly rang, a single clear note like a bell struck by a ghost.

Then the crowd breathed, all together, three hundred lungs, and the sound was like wind coming into a house after a hundred sealed winters.

* * *

The swearing took the rest of the morning.

It was not the old way—the old way had been one voice, the elder's, speaking for everyone, the congregation silent as jars. The new way, the way they had designed in a hundred arguments around the kitchen fire, was each person speaking their own line, their own addition, one true sentence appended to the common oath. *So we remain,* each would say, *and I remain as—* and then whatever they were.

Sable went first, because of course she did. "So we remain," she said, "and I remain a thief, and this valley should count its spoons." Laughter rolled through the crowd, and Sable let it wash over her, and Lior saw—because she had learned to see—that underneath the joke Sable's hands were shaking, because Sable had spent her whole life being property and this was the first oath she had ever sworn that belonged to no one but her.

Iven: "So we remain, and I remain a teacher, and I will die before I fail another student." No laughter for that one. Just a low murmur of assent, and the small girl from the orchard shouting "You won't!" with such ferocity that Iven had to look at the sky for a moment.

Dara: "So we remain, and I remain the sword of this valley, and my list is finished." Only about forty people understood what that meant. All forty of them went quiet in a particular way, and Dara nodded once, satisfied, and stepped back.

Tam: "So we remain, and I remain my sister's brother, and I will spend a lifetime earning the price of my freedom, starting with doing the dishes." The laughter came gentler this time, and Tam grinned his crooked grin, and Lior's chest did the thing again.

Maren went near the end, staff tapping, ancient and upright. "So we remain," she said, "and I remain a keeper—one who kept wrongly, and will spend what's left of her keeping rightly. Bury me under the orchard when I go. I want to grow something useful for once."

And then the people Lior did not know. The refugee families. The smuggler with smoke-scarred hands who had guided them through the pass. The twins from Corren's Landing who had lost their parents to a pyre and who spoke their line together, voices steadying each other: "So we remain, and we remain *both*, and no one will ever make us choose which twin is real." The old ferryman. The salt-wife. The boy of maybe nine who climbed the steps to the stone, froze, looked at the crowd, looked at Lior, and said, in a whisper the whole valley somehow heard: "So we remain, and I remain magic, and nobody gets to say that like it's a bad thing ever again."

Nobody said it like a bad thing ever again. Lior would make sure of that.

When the last of them had spoken—the last was a woman of ninety who had walked out of the mountains alone and who added only, "and I remain, which is miracle enough"—Lior stepped forward to close the rite.

"I am Lior Hartwell," she said, using her whole name the way you set a foundation stone. "Youngest keeper of the Ember Guild. Last vessel of the Undercourt. Sister of Tam. Friend of thieves and soldiers and disgraced mages. Student of an elder who lied to me to save me, and who I forgive, mostly, on good days." A ripple of laughter; Maren bowed her head with theatrical humility. "I gave up every memory I had to buy my brother's freedom, and my family gave them back to me piece by piece, and I have learned this: memory is not what makes us. *Oath* makes us. What we choose, again and again, in front of witnesses, under open sky. So hear the guild's choice, and mine."

She pressed her palm flat to the black glass of the oath-stone, and the stone sang under her hand, and she spoke the final line, the one they had argued about longest, the one Sable had suggested at three in the morning with tears running into her grin:

"So we remain—and we are *done hiding.*"

The note the stone released was not a single tone this time. It was a chord, rising, spreading outward through the valley floor and up the slopes and into the bright unfiltered morning, and above the guildhouse the air shimmered once—like heat-haze, like a held breath let go—and every bird in the Hollow took flight at once, a dark joyful scattering against the blue.

* * *

Afterward, the yard dissolved into the beautiful chaos of a valley feeding three hundred people. Long tables appeared as if conjured—Iven swore it wasn't him, though his sleeves smelled faintly of ozone. Sable circulated with a tray of honey-cakes she had absolutely stolen from her own kitchen. Tam got cornered by the twins, who had decided he was going to teach them everything about vessels whether he liked it or not, and shot Lior a look of pure pleading across the crowd that she answered with a serene smile and no rescue whatsoever.

Maren found her at the oath-stone, where Lior was copying the new oath onto a fresh page for the guild archive—the old ledger had been burned, all of it, every sold name and buried debt, and the new book started blank except for this.

"You did well," Maren said.

"We did well. You wrote half of it."

"I wrote the parts about remembering. You wrote the parts about remaining." The old woman looked out over the yard, at the tables, at the children chasing each other through the tall grass that no longer feared to grow. "A hundred years I kept this valley like a tomb. It took you one year to make it a home. I find I'm not jealous. I find I'm—" she searched, and settled, with a dry smile, on— "*relieved.*"

Lior leaned her head briefly against the old woman's shoulder, the way she had decided, sometime in the winter, that daughters were allowed to do.

It was Dara who interrupted, striding up from the rim path with her patrol-walk pace, which meant either trouble or the end of trouble. Her face said both.

"Lior. Riders on the south road. Six of them, flying no colors." A pause, and something flickered across the soldier's face that Lior had never seen there before—uncertainty, almost shyness. "One of them's carrying a banner I recognize. It's the old royal hunt standard. And the rider holding it is wearing a captain's insignia."

Lior felt the yard's warmth dim by a degree. "Vessic's remnant?"

"That's the thing." Dara's hand had gone, without her noticing, to her pocket—to the insignia she carried there, the twin of the one on that banner. "Vessic's captains don't ride six strong with no escort. They don't fly colors in a valley that broke their king. Whoever this is, they're not riding to fight." She met Lior's eyes. "They're riding to surrender. Or to swear. One of the two."

Above them, the birds wheeled back down toward the trees, and the oath-stone hummed its unfinished note, and down the south road, small with distance, the riders came on.
