## 37. Hunters No More

The news came up the valley on the back of a crow.

Not a real crow—Dara had learned that much in her first weeks among the gifted. It was one of Iven's messenger-shades, a bird stitched out of smoke and intention, and it landed on the windowsill of the longhouse where she was mending a harness strap with hands that still remembered how to do nothing useful. It looked at her with eyes like struck flint, opened its beak, and spoke in a voice that was unmistakably Iven's: dry, precise, and trying very hard not to sound pleased with itself.

"Vessic is deposed. The Council of Free Cities has declared it by unanimous vote, which is to say they argued for nine days and then discovered they all agreed. The Gift Hunt is dissolved. All warrants are void. All bounties are ash." A pause in the bird's throat, a small mechanical cough. "Dara Ferro, your name appears on no list of the condemned. You are, officially, a free woman. Unofficially, you have been free since the night you missed."

The shade folded itself into cinders and drifted out the window like a small gray snowfall going the wrong way.

Dara sat very still. The harness strap hung slack between her hands. Outside, the valley was doing what it did now every morning—glowing. That was the only word for it. A year ago this place had been a hole in the world's eye, a valley where magic couldn't follow, and now magic didn't just follow; it pooled here like meltwater in spring, rising off the fields in visible ribbons when the sun hit it right, threading through the new-built training yards where children who'd spent their whole lives hiding were learning, loudly and badly, to stop.

She heard the news reach them before she saw it. A shout from the eastern yard—one of Sable's runners, a boy of maybe twelve with a stutter he was slowly unlearning—and then another shout, and then the particular roar a crowd makes when grief and joy arrive at the same door and neither will wait for the other.

Dara set down the strap. She stood. Her legs carried her outside the way they always had, without asking her opinion, because that was what twenty-two years of soldiering built into a body: legs that moved toward the sound of a crowd, hands that checked for a weapon that wasn't there anymore, because she'd given her sword to the forge in the third week, back when staying had still felt like a decision she might reverse.

* * *

They were gathered at the well by the time she crossed the yard—the whole strange population of the valley, which had swollen from forty-odd keepers to nearly three hundred souls since the mountain broke open. Freed vessels mostly, people who'd walked out of the Undercourt blinking into daylight with someone else's decades behind their eyes, and refugees from the burning cities, and a handful of deserters like herself who'd drifted in following rumors of a place where the hunt couldn't reach.

Lior Hartwell stood on the well's low stone rim, because Lior had never once in Dara's hearing used a platform when a well would do. She was thinner than the woman Dara had first seen across an arrow's distance—a hunted girl in a stolen cloak with ember-light crawling up her wrist—but she stood now the way Maren used to stand, or perhaps the way Maren had always wished she could: unhidden.

"Vessic is deposed," Lior said, and the crowd made its sound again, that doubled noise, cheering and weeping braided together. "The Hunt is dissolved. Every warrant burned. Every bounty void." She paused, and Dara saw her find faces in the crowd—Sable's, sharp-eyed and suspicious of good news as always; Tam's, standing slightly apart as he still did, as maybe he always would; Iven's, insufferably composed. "It's over," Lior said, softer. "You can put the running down now. All of you. It's over."

And the crowd believed her, because the crowd had learned what Dara had learned somewhere on the road between Corren's Landing and the mountain: that Lior Hartwell did not say things she did not mean, even when meaning them cost her everything she had.

Dara stayed at the edge. She always stayed at the edge. It wasn't shame, exactly—or it wasn't only shame. It was arithmetic. Three hundred people in this valley, and every one of them had lost someone to the Hunt. To captains. To soldiers who hadn't missed. She had worn the black-and-gold for eleven years. She had read names off lists in a clear voice so her squads wouldn't have to feel them. She had trained marksmen, signed requisitions for fire-oil, stood at parade rest while villages burned in orderly, documented fashion. The fact that she had missed—one shot, one deliberate miss, the single crack in a long wall of obedience—did not balance the ledger. Dara Ferro understood ledgers. She'd kept them professionally.

So she stood at the edge, and she clapped her hands until they stung, and when the crowd began to sing—some old valley song that Tam apparently knew and was teaching people with his rough, underused voice—she turned and walked back toward the longhouse, alone, the way she preferred her victories: quiet, and slightly apart from everyone they belonged to.

* * *

That evening she went to the forge.

It was Tam's forge now, though the guild had run it for generations before him; there was something fitting in that, Dara thought, in the brother whose soul had been smelted into a jar now working iron the honest way, with bellows and sweat. He was banking the coals for the night when she came in, and he looked up, and he didn't ask why she was there. He never asked why anyone was there. Twenty years in a jar gave a man a permanent patience with other people's silences.

"I need the fire lit again," Dara said.

Tam picked up the poker. He stirred the banked coals until they woke, orange climbing up through the gray, and heat rolled out across the anvil like a tide coming in. Then he stepped back from it and waited.

Dara reached into her collar and drew out the chain.

Her captain's insignia had survived everything: the desertion, the road, the ambush by Vessic's hounds, the mountain, the year after. She'd told herself she kept it as evidence. Proof of what she'd been, so she could never lie to herself about it. But she knew, standing in the coal-light with the little stamped disc of blackened gold swinging from its chain, that she'd kept it the way a wound keeps its splinter—not as proof, but as presence. Something in her that refused to close.

Eleven years. Two hundred and six names on warrants she'd executed or overseen. She had counted them once, drunk, in a smuggler's den in Corren's Landing, on the night before she'd followed four strangers into the mountains instead of reporting them. Two hundred and six. She'd stopped counting after that, not because the number stopped growing but because counting had started to feel like a way of making it manageable, and some things should never be manageable.

"This doesn't fix anything," she said to Tam, or to the fire, or to no one. "Burning a piece of metal. I know that. I want you to know I know that."

Tam said nothing. He leaned on the poker and watched her with his sister's eyes—no, that wasn't right anymore, and it hadn't been for a year. With his own eyes, which happened to be the same eyes, and which held the specific gravity of someone who had been owned and had come out the other side of ownership knowing exactly what forgiveness cost and refusing to hand it out cheap.

Dara lifted the chain over her head. The insignia swung once, caught the light, went dark.

Then she held it into the coals.

Gold doesn't burn. She knew that too—knew it better than most, having watched the Hunt burn things that also didn't burn, houses and letters and people. What gold does in a forge-fire is soften, and slump, and lose its shape, and that was enough. That was the whole point. She didn't need it destroyed. She needed it deformed past recognition, past any future in which she could polish it up and wear it again and be, for one nostalgic hour, Captain Ferro of the Third Hunt Company, a woman with authority and a list and the terrible clean comfort of orders.

The metal glowed. Slumped. Became a shapeless bright tear in the heart of the fire.

Dara watched until her eyes watered, and found, somewhat to her surprise, that she was not crying. She had expected to cry. She had rehearsed crying, privately, the way soldiers rehearse everything. Instead what she felt was lighter and stranger: the sensation of setting down a pack she'd carried so long her spine had grown around it. There would be a shape left where it had been. There always was. But the weight was gone, and the weight had never been hers to begin with—it had been issued to her, standard equipment, along with the sword and the list and the certainty.

"Done?" Tam asked.

"Not even close," Dara said. "But done with this."

He nodded, satisfied, and raked the cooling metal out onto the anvil with the poker, where it lay steaming—a small misshapen lump, worth nothing, worth less than nothing, the most valuable thing Dara Ferro had ever destroyed.

* * *

She found Lior at the valley's edge an hour later, because of course she did. Dara had spent a year learning this place, and the first thing she'd mapped—soldier's habit, map the perimeter—was the places people went to be alone. The well-rim for Lior. The roof of the granary for Sable. The old ward-stones for Maren, before Maren died in the autumn, gentle and unrepentant and forgiven, buried under a marker that read only *she kept us*. And the eastern ridge for anyone who wanted to look at the road.

Lior was looking at the road.

The pass below the ridge was white with early snow, and the road cut through it like a scar that hadn't decided whether to heal. Somewhere down that road was the capital, and the Council, and the machinery of a kingdom learning to walk without a usurper. Somewhere down that road were the families of the two hundred and six. Dara had written to none of them. She had drafted seventeen letters and burned eighteen.

"You're brooding," Lior said, without turning around. "You're very bad at it. You stand like a sentry even when you're sad."

"I'm not sad."

"No," Lior agreed. "You're deciding. There's a difference. Sadness sits in the chest. Deciding sits in the feet." She finally turned, and the last light off the snow caught her face, and Dara saw—as she saw every time, and it had not dulled with repetition—the faint double-exposure of her, the ghost of a brother's features laid over a sister's, the residue of a soul shared and returned. "Ask me whatever it is."

Dara looked at the road instead of at her. "The Council will want officers. For the reconstruction. Men and women who know how the Hunt worked from the inside—who can testify, who can train the new city guards differently, who can find the people the dissolution paperwork will inevitably lose." She said it flatly, report-style, the cadence of briefings. "Iven's already been summoned. He leaves next week. He asked me to come with him."

"And?"

"And I don't know." The honesty came out of her rough, unpolished, like ore straight from the ground. "A year ago I would have gone. Orders are orders; a summons is a summons. I was built for that. Point me at a duty and I'll die of it happily." She flexed her empty hands. "But I've spent a year watching this valley do the thing the kingdom never managed. Watching you take people the world threw away and just—keep them. Feed them. Teach them. Write their names down in a ledger nobody can sell." Her jaw tightened. "There are going to be more of them, Lior. The war's over but the roads aren't safe yet, and the cities are full of freed vessels with no homes and hunted kids who won't believe the Hunt is dead until someone proves it with a sword between them and anything that says otherwise. Someone has to be the proof."

Lior was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked softly against the pines.

"You're asking to stay," she said. "As what? We don't keep soldiers here. That was rather the point of the old oath, and I rewrote it myself."

"I'm not offering to be a soldier." Dara turned to face her fully now, and made herself hold those doubled eyes, because this deserved eye contact if anything ever had. "I'm offering to be the valley's sword. Not the guild's. Yours. Yours and the council of whoever you gather, and the people themselves, if they'll have me—which they might not, and I'd accept that, and I'd stay anyway, chopping wood, until they did or didn't." She swallowed. "I have one skill, Lior. I spent my whole life being pointed at people. Let me spend the rest of it standing between this place and everything that points at it. That's the whole offer. No rank. No list. No company. Just—" she gestured vaguely at the ridge, the snow, the glow rising off the valley below like heat-shimmer in winter, "—here. If here will have me."

The wind moved through the pines. Far below, a lamp was lit in the longhouse window, then another, the valley lighting itself against the dusk the way it did every night now, openly, defiantly, a constellation that dared the dark to try anything.

"You know they'll vote on it," Lior said at last. "All of them. Everyone you'd be guarding. Some of them lost people to the Hunt. Some of them lost people to *you*."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

Lior studied her for a long moment, and then something moved through her face that Dara had learned to recognize over the past year and had never gotten comfortable with: the look of a woman weighing a soul, and finding the weighing painful, and doing it anyway because mercy that doesn't look isn't mercy, it's avoidance.

"The vote is tomorrow night," Lior said. "After the swearing-in of the new intake. You'll stand in front of everyone and make this offer yourself, and they'll decide, and whatever they decide stands." She paused. "One condition. Mine."

"Name it."

"When you speak tomorrow—you tell them the number."

Dara's breath stopped. "Lior—"

"Two hundred and six," Lior said quietly. "Iven told me. Sable confirmed it—don't glare, she tells me everything, it's her love language. They deserve to hear it from you, not from rumor. Not because it condemns you. Because hiding it would be the old guild's mistake all over again, and we don't do that anymore. We don't bury ledgers. We read them aloud, and then we build something that makes the next one unnecessary." She stepped closer, and her voice gentled. "You burned the insignia tonight. I saw the smoke from here. Good. Now burn the secrecy. That's the last of the Hunt in you, Dara. The part that thought some truths were safer underground."

Dara stood very still. Below them the valley glowed. Above them the first stars were coming out over the pass, cold and indifferent and beautiful, and the road ran away south into the dark toward a kingdom that no longer wanted her and a conscience that had never let her go.

"All right," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Tomorrow. Out loud. All of it."

Lior nodded once, soldier-short, and turned back to the road, and Dara left her there and walked down into the light of the valley, carrying nothing but her name and the number, both of which she intended, from this night forward, to carry honestly.

Behind her, on the ridge, the snow kept falling on the road—the road that remembered everything, Maren had once warned, the road that remembers what valleys cannot hold. Tomorrow the valley would hold her too, or it wouldn't. Either way, for the first time in twenty-two years, Dara Ferro would sleep without a weapon within reach, waiting on no order but the voice of the people she hoped, desperately, to serve.

In the longhouse below, someone began to sing the old song again, and this time, she found, she knew the words.
