## 39. The Girl Who Left

The frost came early that year, silvering the bracken along the valley's rim before the last of the harvest had been carried in. Lior walked up through it alone, her breath pluming white, the compass riding in the pocket of her coat like a small warm heart.

She had not planned to come today. She had told herself she would wait until spring, when the ground was softer and the light kinder, when burying things felt less like ending and more like planting. But she had woken before dawn with Tam's name in her mouth—not a grief, exactly, not anymore, but a kind of attention, the way you wake when someone in the next room has said your name—and she had known that waiting was only another word for fear, and she had spent enough of her life being afraid on behalf of people who deserved better.

So: frost, and morning, and the long climb to the valley's edge.

The Hollow Valley spread below her as she gained the ridge, and she stopped, as she always stopped, to look at what it had become. A year ago—no, less than a year; the mountain still smelled of smoke then—the valley had been a held breath. Wards woven so tight that magic itself slid off the air like rain off oiled canvas. Jars of ash hidden beneath the guildhall, each one a soul the guild could not save and would not surrender. A people trained out of their own natures, generation by generation, until forgetting looked like peace.

Now the wards were gone, and the valley breathed. She could feel it from here: the low hum of the training circles down by the river, where Iven stood ankle-deep in frost-grass teaching farm children to hold a candleflame steady in wind. The lanterns strung between the guildhall and the new dormitories, each one lit by hand, each one a small declaration. Smoke from the kitchens. The sound, faint at this distance, of Dara drilling the watch on the east field—her voice carrying the way voices carry over cold air, clipped and patient and no longer apologizing for itself.

And everywhere, threaded through all of it, the emberlight. Not the stolen kind, not the harvested glow of the Undercourt's cathedral of jars, but the ordinary, stubborn radiance of people who had been given back to themselves and were learning, slowly, what to do with the return.

Lior stood on the ridge a moment longer than she needed to. It was a habit now, this looking. Sable teased her about it—that she stood at edges the way other people stood at hearths—but Sable understood better than anyone why a person might need to check, again and again, that the world was still there. That she was still there. That the shape of her life had not dissolved overnight into someone else's.

She touched the compass through the wool of her pocket and climbed the last stretch to the marker stone.

* * *

They had raised the stone in the first week after they came home, when the snow was still falling soft enough to muffle everything but their own breathing. It stood where the old boundary cairn used to be, at the exact place where the valley's edge met the road—the place where, for twenty years, the world had ended and begun again, depending which direction you were walking.

The stone was river granite, hauled up by six pairs of hands, and Dara had chiseled the words herself, because she said her hands needed honest work or they'd start remembering dishonest work instead. Two words, cut deep enough to outlast weather:

**BOTH OF US**

Not *Tam Hartwell*. Not *the brother who was taken*. Both of us. Because that was the truth they had finally learned to say without flinching: whatever Lior had been before the vessel, whatever Tam had been before the jar, the two of them had lived one life for twenty years, sharing skin and silence and a name, and neither of them had ever truly been alone in it.

Tam came up here sometimes. Less often than she did, she thought, though he'd never admit to counting. He kept to the far side of the marker, always, as if standing directly before it required a permission he hadn't finished granting himself. He was down in the valley now, in the orchard, learning grafting from old Wenna because he had decided—with the fierce, deliberate gentleness that was entirely his own—that a man who had missed twenty years of seasons owed it to himself to learn how trees were made. He had dirt under his nails these days. He laughed more easily than Lior did. She had stopped finding this strange and started finding it right: he had been kept in the dark longest; of course he loved the light loudest.

She knelt beside the stone. The frost crackled under her knees. From her pocket she drew the compass.

It was a plain thing, brass gone green at the seams, the glass clouded with age. Maren had pressed it into her hands on the morning she left—a lifetime ago, another self ago—and told her it had been Tam's, that it pointed not north but home, that the road remembers what the valley cannot hold. Lior had carried it across half the free cities. It had trembled in her palm outside Corren's Landing while families burned. It had steadied her on the Bridge of Small Mercies. It had pointed, unerringly and uselessly, toward the mountain, toward the vault, toward the mirror corridor where she had finally seen both of their faces looking back at her from one pair of eyes.

And since the day she woke emptied of herself, it had pointed nowhere at all. The needle swung slow and idle, a drunkard's wandering, because home was no longer a direction. Home was a radius. Home was wherever the four of them—or five, if you counted the way everyone counted Tam now, which everyone did—happened to be standing.

That was why it could be buried. That was why today.

She dug with her hands. The ground was hard with frost but the frost was thin, and beneath it the earth gave reluctantly, clod by clod, dark and smelling of iron and leaf-rot and the deep cold patience of soil. Her fingers went numb. She kept digging. There was a rightness to doing this with bare hands, the way there is a rightness to saying important things aloud rather than writing them down—something about the cost of it, the body's small payment, that made the act true.

When the hole was deep enough, she turned the compass over once in her palms. The needle swung, drifted, settled nowhere.

"You got me there," she said quietly. "Wherever we were. Every time." Her breath fogged the clouded glass. "I don't need pointing anymore."

She set it in the earth face-up, the way you'd lay a coin on an eye, and began to cover it.

She worked slowly. This was the part she had not let herself rush, because rushing it would have made it a disposal, and it was not a disposal. It was a thank-you. It was also, though she only admitted this halfway through the second handful of dirt, a farewell to the girl who had carried it—the frightened girl who left the valley with a warning ringing in her ears and a hole in her memory shaped like a brother, who crossed burning cities on legs that shook, who opened her own chest to the light because someone had to and because it turned out the someone was her. That girl had done everything with this compass in her pocket. That girl deserved more than a flung shovelful.

The frost melted around her kneeling warmth. By the time the last of the brass disappeared, the ground above it was dark and wet, a small island of thaw in the silvered grass.

She pressed her palm flat against it. Cold seeped up into her hand, and under the cold, faintly, the heartbeat of the valley itself—that deep slow pulse the land had found once the harvesting stopped, as if the whole hollow had been holding its breath for a century and had finally, finally exhaled.

"Both of us," she said to the stone.

The words didn't echo. They didn't need to. Some sentences are complete the moment they're spoken.

* * *

She stayed until the sun cleared the eastern ridge and the frost began to smoke off the fields below, rising in threads, the whole valley exhaling its silver. Then she shouldered her pack—it was a short trip down, but she'd brought bread and a flask of cider, because some vigils deserve provisions—and turned to face the road.

The road ran south and west, away from the valley, the same road she had walked out on with nothing but terror and a borrowed name. It dropped from the ridge in long switchbacks, disappearing into the bare woods, reappearing in the distance as a pale thread stitched across the brown hills. In winter it would be mud and ice. In the wet months, washouts. Somewhere along it stood the ruins where they had shared their letters never sent. Somewhere beyond that, Corren's Landing, rebuilding its burned quarter plank by plank. Somewhere beyond that, the mountain, its gates broken open, its garrison scattered, its cathedral of jars a cavern of glittering shards that pilgrims already traveled weeks to see.

Lior looked down that road the way you look at an old scar. Without flinching. Without longing, either.

She searched herself for the pull—the ache she remembered having once, the restless itch that had made her pace the valley's edge on festival eves, staring at the boundary stones like a bird studying glass. She had assumed, in the worst months after waking, that the restlessness had been burned away with everything else, that she had traded wanting for emptiness. But that wasn't what she found now, standing in the strengthening sun with the thaw steaming around her boots.

What she found was quiet. Not the dead quiet of the vessel years, not the held-breath quiet of the valley's warding, but the quiet of a question answered. The girl who had stared down this road and ached had been aching toward something she couldn't name because it had been taken from her before she could form the words. She had gone down that road and found it. Found him. Found them. And the road, having delivered her, had no further claim.

If she left again someday, she thought, it would be the way you leave a house you love—for a visit, for a errand, with the door unlocked behind you. Not the way you leave a prison. Not the way you leave a life that doesn't fit.

She felt no pull to follow the road. What she felt was the valley at her back, warm as a hand between her shoulder blades.

She ate her bread sitting against the marker stone, and drank the cider, and watched a pair of crows ride the thermal off the ridge in slow spirals, and thought about nothing in particular, which was itself a kind of luxury she was still learning to trust.

* * *

She heard footsteps on the path below before she saw anyone—Dara's tread, unmistakable, even cadence, weight forward—and a moment later the soldier crested the rise with an axe over her shoulder and a face that pretended surprise very badly.

"Heard you came up here," Dara said. "Wenna sent bread. Then Ivolen sent me after the bread, because apparently I'm a courier now."

"You're a courier who brought your axe."

"A courier travels armed." Dara set the wrapped loaf on the flat top of the marker stone, glanced at the fresh-turned earth, and understood immediately, because Dara always understood immediately and pretended otherwise. "Today, then."

"Today."

Dara lowered herself onto the frost-wet grass without ceremony, laid the axe across her knees, and looked down the road with the expression of a woman reading a map she no longer needed. For a while neither of them spoke. The crows wheeled. Far below, Iven's training circle released its children in a scatter of shrieks and small bright lights, sparks trailing them like mayflies.

"I burned my insignia eight months ago," Dara said at last, conversationally, to the road. "Still think about it sometimes. Not regretting it. Just—" She turned her hand over, studied the palm. "Twenty years I was somebody's instrument. You spend that long aimed at things, you forget you get to choose a direction." She nodded at the buried compass without looking at it. "Good thing to put in the ground, that. Some instruments shouldn't keep working."

"It wasn't broken."

"No," Dara agreed. "Finished, maybe. There's a difference."

Lior leaned her head briefly against the older woman's shoulder, the way she had learned to do in the months since—small contacts, freely given, each one a brick in the wall of proof that she existed to other people, that she was held in memory stronger than any ledger. Dara allowed it with the rigid stillness of a woman who had not been leaned on since childhood and had discovered, to her private astonishment, that she liked it.

"Sable's looking for you," Dara said after a moment. "She's got that look."

"What look?"

"The look she gets when she's carrying something she doesn't want to carry alone." Dara stood, brushing frost from her trousers, and slung the axe. "Down by the lantern-yard. Take your time, though." A pause, and then, gruffly, almost gently: "Take all the time you want. That's the whole point of the place, isn't it."

She crunched away down the path, even-footed, and Lior sat a while longer with the bread warming her hands and the sun climbing and the road lying quiet and claimless below her.

Then she rose, brushed the earth from her knees, touched the marker stone once—two fingers, brief, the way you touch a doorframe on leaving a room you'll return to—and started down toward the lanterns, toward whatever Sable was carrying, toward the rest of the life that was hers.

Behind her the frost finished melting. Above the buried compass the grass would grow back crooked and green by spring, and no one would be able to say why that one spot always seemed warmer than the ground around it, and no one would need to.

The valley held its dead close now, and its living closer. Lior Hartwell—who had been two people, and no one, and was at last, simply, herself—walked down into the emberlight, humming a song she couldn't remember learning, and did not look back at the road even once.
