## 40. Emberlight

The valley woke to light.

Not the thin, borrowed gray of the old years—light that had crept in apologetic, filtered through wards that drank it before it could touch the ground—but true morning, gold and unashamed, pouring down the Hollow's throat like honey off a spoon. It caught on the dew in the high meadows. It caught on the new glass windows of the guildhouse, blown by a mage from Vell who wept the first time she saw her own reflection in them. It caught, most of all, on the valley itself: on the faint shimmer that rose from the soil at dawn now, the way breath rises from a sleeping body, visible proof that the Hollow Valley was no longer hollow at all.

Sable stood at the eastern ridge with a cup of chicory going cold in her hands and watched it happen, because she did this every morning and would probably do it every morning until she died, and she had stopped pretending otherwise.

A year ago she could not have stood here. A year ago these hills had been a dead place, a blind spot stitched into the world, and Sable—whose whole trade was seeing what others hid—had found the absence of magic more unsettling than any curse. Now the valley breathed. She could feel it under her feet like a second heartbeat, warm and slow and utterly without guile, and every day it astonished her again: that a place could simply be kind.

"You're up early," said Dara Ferro, coming up the path with an axe over one shoulder and frost in her hair. The soldier moved differently now. Looser through the shoulders, as if some armor she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it was there had finally been set down. "Or you never went to bed."

"Both," Sable said. "I was working."

"At dawn."

"Memories don't keep office hours." Sable smiled into her cup. It was mostly true. Mostly what she'd been doing was sitting with a fragment she hadn't yet returned—a small thing, a sliver of Lior laughing at Tam's terrible cooking—and savoring the weight of it one last time before giving it back. That was the discipline now. She kept nothing. She held things only long enough to carry them home.

Dara leaned the axe against the fence post—the same fence post, Sable noted, that the woman had been meaning to fix since midwinter—and looked out over the valley with her. Below, the guild grounds were already stirring. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney. Somewhere a bell rang, not the crier's harsh iron but the soft bronze note Maren had cast from the melted-down ledger chains, and children came spilling out of the dormitory wing like water from a tipped bucket.

"They're loud this year," Dara said.

"They were louder last year. You've just gotten soft."

"I have gotten," Dara said with great dignity, "promoted."

"That's what I said."

Below them, the first lesson of the day was assembling itself in the east field, where the grass grew greenest over the place the ash-jars used to be buried. The jars were gone now—all of them, emptied or shattered or given back, their glass melted into the windows of the new schoolhouse so that the souls' old prison would hold light instead of people. But the field remembered. Everyone agreed the grass grew best there, though no one could say why, and it was there that Lior Hartwell taught.

* * *

Lior stood in the east field with forty children arranged in a ragged half-moon around her and a basket of river stones at her feet, and she began the way she began every lesson, the way Maren had once taught her to begin an oath: with the hands.

"Show me your ember," she said.

Forty small palms turned upward. Forty small flames bloomed—some steady, some guttering, one boy's flaring blue with excitement and scorching his own sleeve until the girl beside him patted it out without looking, the way you'd swat a fly. The air filled with warmth and the smell of singed wool and the particular bright silence of children concentrating.

"Good," Lior said. "Now put it away."

This was the hard part, and always had been. In the cities they'd fled, gifted children learned two things only: hide it or burn for it. They arrived in the valley knowing how to smother a flame but not how to hold one, how to fear their own light but not how to spend it. So Lior taught the third thing, the thing the Ember Guild had spent a century forgetting: that an ember is not a wound. That it can be banked without being buried. That the trick was never hiding—it was keeping.

She walked the line of them, correcting grips, steadying trembles. The girl with the river-stone habit—Pell, eight years old, sold twice before her cousin carried her over the mountains—held her flame cupped like a bird she was afraid to crush.

"Looser," Lior told her, folding Pell's fingers back a fraction. "It's not yours to strangle. It's yours to keep. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?" Pell asked, the way she asked everything, suspiciously, as if kindness might be a trap.

Lior considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "A strangled fire goes out," she said. "A kept fire waits. And when you need it—" she opened her own hand, and her flame rose tall and clean and white-hearted, throwing their shadows long across the dew—"it's still there."

Pell's flame steadied. Just for a moment, just a heartbeat, it burned as tall as hers.

That was when Lior felt it: the small cold touch against her mind, familiar as a knock on a door, and she laughed before it even finished arriving. "You're late, Sable."

Sable came down the slope with Dara behind her, hands raised in mock surrender. "I prefer *punctually dramatic.*"

"Class," Lior said, "this is Sable, who is going to tell you about remembering, and this is Dara, who is going to pretend she isn't going to teach you footwork later and then teach you footwork anyway."

"She always does," said a boy in the back, with the weary tone of a veteran.

Dara pointed at him. "You. Ten laps."

"He can't do laps," Lior said. "He's six."

"Then he can walk them thoughtfully."

The children laughed, and the sound went up into the bright morning air and did not echo strangely off any ward, because there were no wards, and Lior felt the laugh land somewhere deep in her chest where a year ago there had been only smooth, shining emptiness.

That was the thing about being emptied. People assumed the vessel stayed empty. But a vessel, once opened, learns what it is for—and Lior had been refilling herself ever since, deliberately, handful by handful, from the well of the world. She had relearned her name from Sable's careful mouth. Relearned Tam's face from the man himself, who wore it awkwardly, like a coat cut for someone slightly different. Relearned the road, the bridge, the burning city, the campfire where she had said the word *family* and meant it with her whole cracked-open heart. Every memory Sable carried home to her had been set back in place like a bone, and the limb worked now, mostly, though some mornings she woke reaching for a brother-shaped absence before remembering he slept two doors down.

Some things had not come back. The guild's old oath, word for word. Her mother's voice. Whatever Maren had whispered to her the night before the elder died, which Sable swore she had returned faithfully and which Lior suspected had been returned gently, edited by love. She had made peace with the gaps. A person was not a jar, sealed and complete. A person was a fire, and fires consumed and renewed and were never once, in all their burning, the same fire twice.

"All right," she called. "Stones. You know the drill."

The children groaned with theatrical agony and scrambled for the basket, because the drill was this: each of them took a stone, warmed it with their ember until it glowed faintly, and then passed it to their neighbor, who held it, and passed it on, all the way around the circle, so that by the end every stone had been touched by every hand and none of them could say whose warmth it carried.

"Why do we do this?" Lior asked, as the circle began.

"So no one can take it back," said Pell.

"So it belongs to everybody," said another.

"Because it's fun," said the six-year-old, honestly.

"Because," Lior said, "a gift that passes through many hands cannot be stolen by one. Write that on your hearts. Some of you will be alive when people forget it again. They always forget. Your job is to be the ones who remember."

* * *

Tam found Iven Ashgrave in the library, which was not surprising; Iven lived in the library the way moss lives on stone, and had to be physically extracted for meals. What was surprising was what he was reading: not one of the salvaged court grimoires, not the broker's seized ledgers, but a child's primer, its cover scribbled with crayon, open to a page of very simple diagrams.

"Don't say anything," Iven said, without looking up.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to say something insufferable about how far I've fallen, from royal sorcerer to remedial tutor."

"I was going to say," Tam said, settling into the chair across from him, "that Pell asked me yesterday why her letters swim on the page, and I didn't know what to tell her, and then I remembered you'd mentioned once that you couldn't read until you were nine, and that someone sat with you every evening until the swimming stopped."

Iven went very still. Then he closed the primer carefully, as if it might startle. "Her letters swim?"

"Like fish. She hides it. You'd know how she hides it."

"Yes," Iven said quietly. "I would know how." He reopened the book. His long court-mage's fingers, scarred now from vault-work and honest labor, smoothed the crayon marks with something close to reverence. "There are ways to teach a page to sit still. Old ones. Unglamorous ones. I'll need to build the exercises from scratch; the court never bothered, it simply discarded anyone who—" He stopped himself, the old reflex, the old accounting of the discarded. Then, deliberately, he finished the sentence differently. "It simply hadn't learned better yet. Neither had I."

"That's the most repentant thing you've said all year."

"I'm pacing myself. Repentance is a marathon." Iven looked up, and for a moment the old irony fell away entirely and left only the tired, earnest man beneath. "How is she? Today, I mean. Some days are—"

"Some days are heavy. Today's a good one. She laughed at Dara before breakfast."

"Then the world is correctly ordered." Iven pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began sketching, already murmuring, already lost. Tam rose to go, and paused at the door.

"Iven. The thing you built for the vault door. The counter-seal. You know it works. You know it's holding. You're allowed to stop checking."

"I check it every day," Iven said, not looking up, "because one day I won't, and I want the last time I checked to be as recent as possible." He glanced up, wry. "Go away, Hartwell. Some of us are working."

Tam went, smiling, and out in the corridor he passed the wall where the new oath was carved, and touched it as he passed every time, the way you'd touch a doorframe of a house you'd almost lost.

* * *

At midday the whole valley gathered—not by command, never by command anymore, but by gravity, the way water finds level. The noon meal was served in the long yard between the guildhouse and the schoolhouse, at tables built from the dismantled frames of the ash-jar shelves, planed smooth and scrubbed and oiled until no one could have guessed what they'd held. Maren's idea, that. One of her last. *Wood remembers what it's for,* she'd said. *Teach it a better one.*

Dara ate with the deserters, of whom there were now eleven, a number that had grown by ones and twos all year as word spread that the valley neither pardoned nor punished but simply asked: *what will you do with your hands now?* Two of them had been hunt-captains. One had burned names into the very ledgers they'd seized from Vessic's archives. He'd asked, his first week, if he was allowed to eat with everyone else, and Lior had said, *you're allowed to plant the west field, and after that we'll see,* and he had planted the west field, and now he was eating stew next to a woman whose family name he had once entered into a hunt registry, and neither of them pretended it was easy, and both of them kept showing up, which was the only forgiveness the valley offered and the only one it required.

Sable drifted among the tables doing what she did at every meal: listening. Not stealing—never that, not here, not ever again—but listening, the way a musician listens. Grief had a sound. So did healing, quieter, easier to miss. She heard the Vell weaver telling the story of her rescue for the fourth time that week, embellishing freely. She heard two boys arguing about whether the Undercourt's broken vault should be preserved as a monument or quarried for stone (quarried, was winning). She heard Pell, at the children's table, explaining to a newcomer with tremendous authority: *"That's Lior. She gave away everything she was to get her brother back, and then we gave it back to her, piece by piece, and that's why we don't sell anything here, not memories, not names, not anything, it's the rule, I'm basically the one who made the rule."*

Sable laughed out loud, alone, into her stew.

Later, when the plates were cleared and the afternoon lessons called, she found Lior at the well, drawing water, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its braid in the way it always did by noon.

"A moment of your time, keeper."

"You have all my moments. You're carrying half of them."

"Only until Thursday. I've got three fragments left in the case and then I'm empty, truly empty, and you'll be—" Sable hesitated. This part never got easier to say. "Whole. As whole as you're going to be. Are you ready for that? For me to stop?"

Lior set down the bucket. She thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything now, unhurried, unhurriable, a woman who had already paid the steepest price on offer and knew exactly what things cost.

"When my mother's voice comes back," she said, "it comes back because I find it, not because you hand it to me. I've decided that. The gaps are mine to fill." She smiled, and it was the smile from the campfire, the one Sable had carried through the dark months inside the mountain like a coal in cupped hands. "But come sit with me while I look for it. That part I want. That part's yours forever."

* * *

Evening came down the valley like a blessing, and with it, the ceremony.

They held it on the first night of every month, weather permitting, and weather permitting tonight: a sky rinsed clean, stars sharp as nailheads, a fat moon rising over the western rim where the old wards had flickered and failed and finally, gloriously, dissolved. The whole valley came—two hundred souls now, maybe more, the displaced and the deserted and the delivered, the ones born here and the ones who'd crawled here bleeding. They gathered in the east field, over the greenest grass, and the children lit the boundary lanterns one by one, passing the flame hand to hand down the line so that no single fire could be traced to a single keeper.

Lior stood at the center with Tam at her right hand and Sable and Dara and Iven at her left, and the four of them were not officers or elders or anything with a title; they were simply the ones who stood there, month after month, because the circle needed anchors and they had volunteered and never unvolunteered.

Maren's staff lay across two pegs at the field's edge, retired, ornamental, growing a respectable patina. No one had taken up the elder's mantle. Lior had refused it, and the guild had surprised everyone—including itself—by deciding it didn't need a single elder at all. Decisions were made in council, slowly, loudly, sometimes badly. It was messier than the old way. It was also, Dara observed monthly, the only way worth defending.

Lior raised her hands, and the valley quieted, and she spoke the words they all knew, the words the children mouthed along with her, the words carved above the library door:

*"We are the Ember Guild. We were sworn to keep the ember low—to hide the light, to bury the names, to survive what could not be fought."*

She paused, as she always paused, and let the old words hang in the cold air like smoke from a fire that was finally, fully out.

"And we are sworn anew. We keep the ember high. We speak the names. We teach the light to every hand that reaches for it. What was hidden is sheltered. What was sold is free. What was ours alone belongs to all of us, and no ledger, no king, no fear shall gather it up again—for a gift that passes through many hands cannot be stolen by one."

Two hundred voices took up the last line, and the children's voices rang brightest, and the flames in the boundary lanterns stood up straight as soldiers presenting arms, and somewhere in the dark beyond the valley the world went on mending itself, city by city, name by name, slow as spring.

Afterward, when the crowd had thinned to embers and echoes, Pell came running up the hill to the ridge where Lior stood looking out at the lights below—the guildhouse glowing, the schoolhouse glowing, the whole Hollow Valley lit from within like a jar full of dawn.

"Lior," Pell said, tugging her sleeve. "The new girl. The one who came in with the caravan today. She won't show anyone her ember. She says it's bad. She says it got her brother taken." Her small face was fierce and frightened at once. "What do I tell her?"

Lior knelt, so their eyes were level, and she thought of a valley that forgot, and a ledger with her brother's name, and a mirror corridor, and four misfits on a burning road, and a stranger waking among friends who refused to let her stay a stranger.

"Tell her," she said, "to bring it to the field tomorrow. Tell her we'll keep it together."

And far below, in the east field, the lanterns burned on—forty small flames and two hundred, passed hand to hand, belonging to no one, kept by everyone, against the coming of any winter, for as long as any of them drew breath to pass the light.
