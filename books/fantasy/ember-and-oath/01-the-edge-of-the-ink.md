## 1. The Edge of the Ink

The trouble with the Hollow Valley, as far as Tamsin Dravenmoor could tell, was that it was too easy to map.

He had thought about this often, usually while hunched over his drafting table with cold tea going colder beside him. Other cartographers — the ones whose names appeared in guild registries and gilded atlases — had to contend with rivers that wandered like drunkards, coastlines that argued with themselves, mountain passes that vanished under snow for half a decade and reappeared somewhere slightly wrong. The world beyond the boundary stones was a quarrelsome thing. It kept moving.

The valley never moved.

It lay folded into the mountains like a letter in an envelope nobody opened: eleven miles long, three wide at the shoulder, narrowing to a throat of grey stone at the north end where the river Vell went underground and didn't come back up until it was someone else's problem. Four villages. One market town, called simply Town by everyone who lived there, because in a valley this size ambition was considered a form of bad manners. Two hundred and six farmsteads, though Tamsin counted two hundred and seven if you included the ruined croft above Miller's Bend where nobody had lived since before his mentor was born. Three chapels, one mill, one bridge worth the name, and one workshop above a chandler's shop that smelled permanently of tallow and linseed oil, where Tamsin Dravenmoor had spent twenty-three years drawing the same valley over and over again.

Everyone said magic stopped at the stones.

This was not folklore, exactly. It was more like weather — a fact you lived around without examining. The valley had been hollowed out of the old kingdoms' wars some centuries back, a place where people went who wanted nothing to do with wonders, and whatever bargain or accident had made it so, the result was simple: no charms worked here, no curses took root, no hedge-witch could coax so much as a blister from her own herbs. Priests who came over the pass found their prayers landed flat as coins on a dead man's eyes. The valley was quiet the way a locked room is quiet.

Tamsin had grown up believing this the way he believed in the weight of water or the honesty of a plumb line. It was load-bearing. Take it away and everything he knew about himself would come down.

Which is why, on the morning he drew the lake, he was thinking about none of it at all.

* * *

"You've got ink on your face," Master Corvin said from the doorway.

"I always have ink on my face."

"Yes, but today you have it *arranged*. There's intent in it."

Tamsin looked up from the table. Corvin filled most doorways the way weather fills a sky — not by force, exactly, but by presence. He was sixty-odd and shaped like a question mark that had spent decades leaning over maps, with a beard he trimmed once a season whether it needed it or not, and hands so stained with iron-gall ink that they looked bruised at every joint. Forty years a surveyor in the lowlands before he'd come to the valley to teach, and he spoke of that former life the way other men spoke of a first marriage: fondly, briefly, and only when drinking.

Tamsin wiped his cheek and transferred the ink to his sleeve, which was, on balance, a lateral move.

"Better?"

"Marginally." Corvin came in and set down the morning bread and a pot of honey, because he believed no honest day's work should begin hungry, and surveyed what Tamsin had been working on since before dawn. His eyebrows did something complicated.

"That's the west fell," he said.

"It's the west fell."

"With a lake on it."

"There's a depression there," Tamsin said. "I walked it last week. The ground dips, the springs come down off the scree, the drainage has nowhere to go. I've been saying for two years the water must be pooling somewhere. So I drew where it ought to pool." He shrugged, and tried to make the shrug casual, and suspected he failed. "It's a projection. Surveyors do them all the time."

"Surveyors do projections," Corvin agreed, "and then they go and check them, and they're usually wrong, because ground is ground and doesn't care what we think." He tapped the edge of the sheet, where Tamsin had written, in his neat drafting hand: *Mere, conjectural — to be verified.* "You've even labeled it. Good habits. When do you go verify?"

"This afternoon. I promised Widow Hale I'd survey her orchard boundary first — she's convinced Farmer Ottery's moved his wall four feet onto her land, which is impossible, because stone walls don't move, but try telling her that."

"Stone walls don't move," Corvin said, "but farmers will, given a shovel and a grievance. Go. And take the good chain, not the one with the kinked link. She'll count every foot."

Tamsin rolled the map, tied it, and forgot about it, which was the last easy hour he would have for a long time.

* * *

Widow Hale's orchard dispute consumed the afternoon and a fair portion of Tamsin's patience. The wall had indeed moved four feet; Farmer Ottery had indeed moved it, and defended the move with the serene confidence of a man whose grandmother had once heard something secondhand. Tamsin measured the disputed strip twice, drew the line plain, and left both parties with a document neither wanted and both signed, because in the Hollow Valley a mapmaker's word was one of the few things still regarded as binding.

By the time he climbed the west fell, the light was going long and gold, and the shadows of the scree ran down the slope like spilled grain.

He was halfway up when he noticed the smell.

Water smells before you see it — a green, mineral coolness in the air, especially in evening. But there shouldn't have been water here. The depression was a bowl of grass and loose stone; he'd picnicked in it as a boy. He crested the rise expecting nothing, and stopped walking so abruptly that his satchel swung forward and hit him in the hip.

There was a lake.

It wasn't large — perhaps two hundred paces across, an oval, fed by a thread of spring-water spilling down from the scree. In the slanting light it lay copper and rose, perfectly still, holding the sky upside down in its skin. A few new rushes stood at the margin, thin and startled-looking, as if they weren't sure how long they'd been alive.

Tamsin stood very still.

Then, because he was who he was, he did the worst possible thing: he sat down, unrolled his map, and compared it.

The shoreline matched. Not approximately — *matched*, every curve and notch of it, including a small hook of land on the northern shore that he'd drawn partly from the contour lines and partly, if he was honest, from a feeling. He had invented that hook. Ground didn't work in feelings. And yet there it was in the world, catching the last light, exactly where his pen had decided it should be.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. Some distant part of him noted that his hands were steadier than his thoughts, which were doing something like a cartwheel down a staircase.

"All right," he said aloud, to the lake, because talking to things was a habit of solitary men. "Let's be sensible."

Sensible explanations presented themselves in order. One: he'd seen the lake before and forgotten, and memory had dressed itself up as deduction. But he'd picnicked here; boys remember water. Two: someone had dammed the springs recently — but there was no dam, no spoil heap, no cut timber, and the rushes were too young. Three: coincidence. Springs pool; pools form; shapes repeat. Any oval can resemble any other oval if you want it badly enough.

All three explanations filed past him, tipped their hats, and left. None of them believed what they were saying.

Tamsin stayed until the light died, measuring the shore with his chain, pacing the margins, sketching the hook of land from life and finding it identical to the one from imagination. On the walk home in the dark he told himself a story about telling Corvin in the morning, rehearsed several versions, and discarded all of them, because every version began with the same sentence and the sentence was: *Master, the valley made something I dreamed.*

And the valley didn't make things. The valley was the place where nothing was made — where wonder came to die politely, where the world outside checked its strangeness at the boundary stones like a traveler surrendering a knife at a chapel door.

He slept badly, in the way of men who leave lamps burning against thoughts rather than dark.

* * *

In the morning, Corvin knew.

Not about the lake — about something. Tamsin came down the stairs with the map under his arm and found the old man already at the big table, not working, just standing over a sheet of his own with his hands flat on it, the way he stood when a drawing had told him something unpleasant.

"You look terrible," Corvin observed.

"You should see the other fellow."

"The other fellow being?"

Tamsin set the map down between them. He unrolled it to the west fell, to the small blue oval and the careful label — *mere, conjectural* — and then he laid beside it the field sketch he'd made by lantern light the night before, taken from life.

Corvin looked at the first. Then the second. Then he took off the cracked spectacles he wore for close work, polished them on his cuff with great thoroughness, put them back on, and looked again, as though the fault might be optical.

"When did you draw the projection?" he asked. His voice was carefully level, the voice of a man crossing ice and testing each step.

"Four days ago. I walked the ground the week before that. There was no water. I ate lunch in the bottom of that depression when I was nine, Master. There was grass and ants and Hesta Brindle dared me to eat a dandelion whole."

"And the field sketch?"

"Last night."

Corvin sat down slowly, which he almost never did at the drafting table; sitting was for reading and despair. "Show me the hook."

Tamsin showed him the hook.

For a long moment the only sound was the chandler's boy below, hauling tallow blocks up the stairs with the rhythmic thump-and-grunt of youth performing labor loudly so adults would appreciate it.

"I want you to think very carefully," Corvin said at last, "and tell me if anyone else knows. Anyone at all. Did you show the projection to anyone? Leave it out? Mention it at the tavern? To the widow, God forbid?"

"No one. I finished it after you left. It's been in my chest since."

"Good." Corvin rubbed his eyes with ink-stained fingers, leaving faint grey crescents under them, so that he now looked less like a tired old man than a raccoon in mourning. "That's good. That's — we keep it that way."

"Master." Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "What is 'that way'? What are we keeping?"

Corvin didn't answer immediately. He rose instead, went to the window, and looked out at the street, at Town with its slate roofs steaming in the morning sun, at the fells beyond, at the boundary pass, a pale notch in the eastern ridge, visible from this window on clear days. He looked at it the way a man looks at a door he'd assumed was nailed shut and has just heard click.

"Magic stops at the stones," Tamsin said. It came out harder than he meant, an accusation. "That's the whole point of this place. That's the whole point of *me*. I came here because—" He stopped. Recalibrated. He had not come here; he had been brought, an infant, a foundling left at Corvin's door with nothing but a blanket and, according to legend, a remarkably calm disposition. "We're in the valley. Nothing works here."

"Nothing works here," Corvin agreed mildly. "No charms, no curses, no blessings worth the candle. I've watched a pyromancer strike flint for an hour and produce nothing but blisters." He turned back. "But lad — a charm is a thing you *do*. What you may have is a thing you *are*."

The words went into Tamsin like a splinter: painless on entry, and already beginning to fester.

"It's a lake," he said. "Lakes happen. Springs shift, ground settles—"

"Tamsin."

"—and any oval resembles any other oval, and I labeled it *conjectural* precisely because I knew it might be wrong—"

"*Tamsin.*" Corvin came back to the table and put one bruised hand flat over the little blue oval, covering the lake the way you'd cover a birdcage at night. "I taught you surveying for ten years. I taught you that a map is a description. Descriptions don't make the described. If you drew a lake and the hillside obeyed—" He paused, and chose the next words with the care of a man defusing something. "Then either the world has changed its rules, or we have been wrong about you for twenty-three years. And of those two possibilities, I know which one keeps me up tonight."

Tamsin looked at his own hands. Ordinary hands. Ink at the knuckle, a scar across the left thumb from a drafting knife, bitten nails. Hands that had drawn the valley two hundred times and never once been obeyed.

Or so he'd thought.

A cold little thought surfaced, the kind that rises when you stop fighting the water: *How would you know?* The valley was small and settled. Every stream, every wall, every footpath already existed, already matched some older drawing. If his pen had ever run ahead of reality, reality here had nowhere to go except along lines that had been laid down long ago. A gift like that wouldn't shout, in a place like this. It would whisper, and be mistaken for accuracy.

"Master," he said quietly, "have you ever wondered why my maps are always right?"

"Every apprentice's maps are right. That's what apprentices are *for*."

"No. I mean — right *first*. Before I measure. You've said it yourself, for years. Half a joke. 'Dravenmoor draws it true before he's walked it.' You laughed about the ford at Alderbrook. I placed it a hundred yards off from where the old surveys said, and when they cut the new road, the ford was exactly where I put it. You said I had lucky instincts."

Corvin had gone very still. "I remember."

"What if it wasn't instinct?"

The silence stretched. Below them the chandler's boy dropped something heavy and swore with impressive range. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing, or at something, or at the general idea of a changing world.

"We test it," Corvin said finally, and his voice had shifted into the register he used for dangerous terrain — measured, unhurried, giving nothing away to whatever might be listening. "Quietly. No more projections. No more drawings of things that don't exist. You'll copy old charts, and you'll copy them exactly, and you'll touch nothing original until I say otherwise." He held up a finger before Tamsin could object. "Not because I doubt you. Because if you are what that lake says you are, then you have spent your life inside a locked room holding a lit lamp, thanking your stars the room is full of water. And the moment anyone outside these hills hears of a mapmaker whose maps come *true*—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening.

"Then what?"

"Then every crown, cult, college, and conqueror from here to the sea will want to hold the hand that holds the pen." Corvin said it plainly, without drama, which made it worse. "So. We say nothing. We burn the field sketches, we shelve the projection, and tomorrow you will help me re-ink the parish boundaries like the most bored young man in the history of cartography. Can you do that?"

Tamsin looked at the lake on the page — small, blue, innocent, sitting inside its ring of mountains like a secret that hadn't learned yet to be dangerous.

"I can do that," he said.

* * *

They burned the field sketches that evening in the chandler's furnace, and Corvin watched the curls of paper catch and curl with the expression of a man burning letters from a lover he'd rather not be reminded of. Tamsin went home through streets that smelled of bread and lamp-oil and ordinary human life, and told himself the unease in his chest was just exhaustion.

But sleep, when it finally took him, brought dreams of blue ovals blooming on blank pages like stains spreading through cloth, and he woke twice in the dark with the certain, wordless feeling that somewhere out beyond the boundary stones, in the wide quarrelsome world he'd never seen, something had noticed him back.

He was still trying to shake the feeling three mornings later, when the heralds came down the pass with the king's edict nailed to their saddlebags — and the smell of smoke clinging to their cloaks like a passenger.
