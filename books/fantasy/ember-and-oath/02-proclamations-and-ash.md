## 2. Proclamations and Ash

The heralds came to Hollow's Rest on a morning so ordinary that Tamsin Dravenmoor would remember it, for the rest of his life, as the morning the world ended politely.

He was in his workshop when the first horn sounded — a long, brassy note rolling down the valley walls the way the bells of the harvest festival did, except that no festival had ever been announced with horns. He looked up from his drafting table, quill poised over a half-finished survey of the miller's new irrigation channel, and felt something cold walk down his spine.

Horns meant the crown. The crown never came to the valley. The valley existed, as far as Tamsin had understood in twenty-six years of living in it, as a kind of clerical error — a fold in the kingdom's maps where the roads forgot to go and the tax collectors forgot to follow. His mentor, old Master Corvin, liked to say that the valley was where the kingdom kept its spare air.

"Horns," said Marta from the doorway. She was the baker's daughter and Tamsin's occasional delivery girl, fourteen years old, freckled, and constitutionally incapable of entering a room without announcing its contents. "Heralds. Three of them. On horses with those pointy hats on the saddles. They're nailing something to the well-post."

"Nailing what?"

"Dunno. It's got writing on it." She paused, considering. "Big writing."

"Thank you, Marta. Your powers of observation remain unmatched."

"You're welcome." She didn't move. She was watching him with the particular patience of someone waiting to see if he'd do the interesting thing or the sensible thing. In Marta's experience, he usually did neither, which she found disappointing but reliable.

Tamsin set down his quill. He told himself he was going out because a proclamation concerned everyone and it would be strange not to look, and because Master Corvin would want to know, and because standing still while a horn rang out over your valley was the sort of behavior that invited trouble. He did not tell himself — because he did not yet know it — that some part of him had already heard the horn as a summons, the way a dog hears a whistle pitched just above human hearing.

The square of Hollow's Rest was fuller than he'd ever seen it. The whole village had turned out the way people turn out for a bear-baiting: drawn by dread, unable to look away. Three heralds sat their horses beneath the old elm, resplendent in royal blue and silver, and one of them — a broad man with a voice built like a barn door — was reading aloud from a scroll.

"—by order of Aldric, King of the Nine Valleys and the Ashreach Marches, Sovereign of the Long Road, Protector of—"

The titles went on for a while. Tamsin let them wash past him and read the posted edict instead, shouldering through the crowd until he stood close enough to touch the parchment. It was nailed up high, at a height calculated to be readable and unreachable, and the hand that had written it was beautiful — a formal chancery script, every letter upright and disciplined, the work of someone who had been trained to make words look trustworthy.

*THE EDICT OF THE HUNT*, the header read.

Tamsin read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way you reread a sentence that has rearranged itself into something worse.

*Be it known to all subjects of the Crown: that there dwell among you persons gifted with arts unnatural and forbidden. These persons are an abomination before law and gods alike. Let every such person be surrendered to the King's wardens within thirty days of this posting. Those surrendered shall be examined and, if found penitent, may be granted the King's mercy.*

*Those who conceal a gifted person, knowing them for what they are, share their crime and shall share their fate.*

*Those gifted persons who do not surrender themselves shall be sought. And being found, they shall burn.*

The last line was underlined. Underlined. As though the burning were a point of emphasis worth drawing the eye to, a flourish, a bit of craft.

Around him the crowd murmured. Old Tam Hedley spat. Someone's baby started crying, and the sound seemed to say what nobody else would.

"Burned," whispered the cobbler's wife. "For what? What have any of us done?"

"It's not for us, silly," said her neighbor. "It's for the gifted. Out there. In the cities."

"Out there," the cobbler's wife repeated, and Tamsin watched the words settle over the crowd like a comfortable blanket. *Out there.* Not here. Not in the valley where magic couldn't follow, where nothing stranger than weather had happened in living memory. The edict was a fire in someone else's house, and the whole village leaned toward it with the warm, horrible fascination of people watching smoke rise beyond their own rooftops.

Only Master Corvin, standing near the front with his arms folded and his face gray as old ash, wasn't leaning. He was very still, the way a hare is still, and when his eyes found Tamsin across the crowd they held something Tamsin had never seen in them before.

Fear.

Not for himself. Corvin feared nothing for himself; he was seventy-one years old and had once argued down a tax assessor. This was fear *for*, aimed like an arrow, and it took Tamsin a moment too long to realize the arrow was pointed at him.

Corvin jerked his chin: *home. Now.*

Tamsin went.

* * *

"The valley is not exempt." Corvin had the shutters closed and a candle lit, which in midmorning meant either that his eyes were failing again or that he wanted shadows to think in. "I've been trying to tell myself otherwise since the horn sounded, but I've read the wording four times now. 'Let every subject of the Crown.' We're subjects, boy. Distant ones, forgotten ones, but the parchment doesn't know that. Parchments never do."

"They'll never come here." Tamsin heard how young his own voice sounded. "There's nothing here. There's no road in worth the name, no garrison, no—"

"No gifted persons?" Corvin said mildly.

The silence stretched. Outside, the village was already going back to its business, because that was what villages did — they swallowed horror the way a river swallows a stone, with a ripple and then nothing. Somewhere a hammer was knocking together a cart. Somewhere Marta was probably telling everyone, in exhaustive detail, about the heralds' hats.

"Master," Tamsin said carefully, "you know I'm not—"

"I know exactly what you're not." Corvin lowered himself into his chair by the cold hearth, joints cracking like green kindling. "You're not a fire-caller. You're not a hedge-witch. You can't hex a wart off a toad. And three days ago you drew a lake on a piece of vellum, and this morning I walked past the low meadow, and there is a lake there. Exactly where you drew it. Exactly the shape. Down to the little island you put in the middle because, if I recall, you thought the composition needed balance."

"It's a coincidence."

"Tamsin."

"Springs shift. Groundwater does strange things after a wet autumn. There are entire treatises on—"

"*Tamsin.*" Corvin's voice cracked across the room like a whip, and then, immediately, gentled. That was his way: thunder first, tenderness after, always in that order, like a storm that apologized. "Sit down, boy."

Tamsin sat.

"I've watched you draw places that don't exist for eleven years," Corvin said. "Little things, mostly. A bridge on a survey that later turned out to be exactly where the flood washed out and the villagers needed one. A well. Once — do you remember? — a stand of birch trees on the north slope, and two years later, seedlings. I told myself each time: coincidence. Good judgment. The eye of a born surveyor, seeing what the land wants to become. And maybe that was true. Maybe that's all it was." He rubbed his eyes. "But I stopped believing it around the time the lake appeared, and I find that lying to oneself is much harder work when there's an edict nailed to the well-post saying what happens to people the lie is about."

"I don't feel magic." It came out smaller than Tamsin intended. "Everyone says magic feels like something. Fire in the blood, voices in the head. I feel nothing. I'm just... good at maps."

"And I am just good at arguing with tax assessors," Corvin said, "but I've never once been tempted to believe it was a gift from the gods. Listen to me. Whether what you have is magic or merely miracle-adjacent, the men on those horses don't have instruments fine enough to tell the difference. To a warden with a torch, 'just good at maps' and 'gifted' weigh exactly the same on the pyre." He leaned forward. "So we are going to be careful. No more drawings of things that don't exist. Nothing imaginative. Surveys only — real places, measured honestly. And if anyone comes asking questions, you are the dullest cartographer in the Nine Valleys, and I am your duller mentor, and between us we could bore a warden into hibernation. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, Master."

"Good." Corvin slumped back, and for a moment he looked every year of his age. "Thirty days," he murmured. "They gave the realm thirty days to turn on itself. That's the cleverness of it, you know. They barely need wardens. Neighbors will do the hunting for them, given a month and a reward."

"Is there a reward?"

"There's always a reward."

Tamsin walked home through streets that felt subtly wrong, the way a familiar room feels wrong after someone has moved a chair. People nodded to him. People smiled. But he caught, more than once, a glance that lingered a half-second too long, and he understood that Corvin was right about the cleverness of the edict: it had already changed the valley without a single warden setting foot in it. Everyone was now wondering about everyone. The village had begun quietly mapping itself.

That night he burned nothing, drew nothing, and slept badly, dreaming of lakes rising out of blank vellum.

* * *

It was raining on the fourth night when the girl came.

Tamsin was banking the workshop fire, working late by habit even though he'd touched no ink in three days — ink felt dangerous now, the way a loaded crossbow feels dangerous in a house with children. The rain came down in ropes. And through the ropes of rain, over the drumming on the roof, he heard a sound he almost didn't register: footsteps. Uneven ones. Dragging.

Then a fist against his door. Three knocks, spaced wrong — too weak, too slow, the knocking of someone who had spent their last strength on the walking and had none left for the door.

He opened it and a body fell against him.

She was perhaps nineteen, small, soaked to the skin and heavier than she should have been because her clothes were sodden and because, he realized as he caught her, she had wrapped her left arm in strips of cloth that had been white once and were now rust-brown, and the wrapping was soaked through. Her hair was dark and plastered to her face. Her lips were gray. And her eyes — she managed to get them open, and they were the most awake eyes Tamsin had ever seen on a dying person, bright and furious and terrified all at once, cataloguing him in a single sweep the way he catalogued terrain.

"You're a mapmaker," she rasped. It wasn't a question. Behind him, on the wall, his surveys hung in rows, and she'd read them in the half-second before she fell. "Guild style. Southern school. Please. Please, I need—"

"You need a healer, not a mapmaker."

"Healers are burned too, now." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and thin. "Didn't you hear? Gifted. Arts unnatural. My master could find water through stone and they—" The laugh broke off. She shuddered. "Please. Inside. Before someone sees."

Every sensible thought Tamsin owned lined up in a row and spoke in unison: *shut the door*. Corvin's voice was in there too, careful and gray: *we are going to be careful.* Thirty days. Neighbors. Rewards. A girl bleeding on his threshold in the rain, and every reason in the world to let the night swallow her back up.

He looked at her arm. At the gray lips. At the eyes that were still, even now, taking his measure — deciding whether he was rescue or another kind of trap.

He pulled her inside and barred the door.

* * *

Getting the wrappings off was the worst thing Tamsin had done in years, and he had once set Master Corvin's broken finger with no anesthetic but brandy and apology. The burn ran from her wrist to her elbow — a rope burn of fire, deliberate, dragged, the mark of something applied slowly by someone patient. He cleaned it with boiled water and the last of Corvin's honey-salve, and she bore it without a sound except at the very end, when she made a noise like a hinge and said, "Wren."

"What?"

"My name. Wren. If I pass out, I want someone to know it. They buried my master without his name. Just 'a gifted man.' Like he was a dog." Her eyes fixed on the ceiling. "I'm Wren. Cartographers' Guild, Vellum Hall, third-year apprentice. Was."

"Tamsin Dravenmoor. Also a cartographer. Also, apparently, was."

Her gaze snapped to him. "Also?"

"Also nothing. Forget I said it."

But she was too clever for that, and too feverish to be tactful. While he worked, she talked — the rambling talk of pain and exhaustion, words spilling out of her like water from a cracked jug. The Guild hall surrounded at dawn. Wardens with lists. Lists! Names, addresses, the Guild's own registry turned against it, because of course the crown had registries, of course the Guild had filed its members like specimens. Her master refusing to run, saying the archives had to be saved, saying the maps were the kingdom's memory. The fires. The archive burning, vellum curling like leaves, a hundred years of the kingdom's memory going up in an afternoon.

And then she said the thing that made Tamsin's hands go still over the bandages.

"They had a map of us," she whispered. "The wardens. A proper one. Every guildhall, every chapterhouse, every licensed surveyor's office — marked. And routes. Search routes, drawn clean, efficient, like a campaign. Whoever made it knew cartography. Real cartography. Grid references, contour intervals, our own notation system." Her breath hitched. "They used *our* notation, Tamsin Dravenmoor. Somebody who learned what I learned is drawing the hunt."

Tamsin finished tying the bandage. He did it slowly, because his hands wanted to shake and he refused to let them.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Somewhere in the village a dog barked and was silenced. And on his table, under a weight, lay the survey he'd abandoned three days ago — the honest, harmless, measured survey of real places — and beneath it, hidden, the sketch he hadn't been able to stop himself from making last night despite everything Corvin had said: a little drawing of a road. A road that led out of the valley. A road that did not exist.

He looked at Wren — asleep now, finally, her burned arm cradled against her chest, her fierce eyes shut — and he looked at the hidden sketch, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the careful life was already over. The edict had seen to that. The girl on his cot had seen to that. Concealing a gifted person shares their crime and shares their fate; he had barred the door himself.

"Master Corvin is going to be insufferable about this," he said softly, to no one.

Wren stirred. Her eyes opened a slit, fever-bright, and she smiled the smallest, sharpest smile he'd ever seen.

"Took you long enough," she murmured, and slept.

Tamsin sat down at his table, pulled the hidden sketch out from under the weight, and unstoppered his ink for the first time in four days.

If someone out there was mapping the hunt, then the hunted needed a mapmaker too.

His hand did not shake at all.
