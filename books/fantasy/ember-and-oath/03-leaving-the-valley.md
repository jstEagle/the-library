## 3. Leaving the Valley

The problem with leaving a valley that magic cannot enter, Tamsin reflected as he packed his life into a satchel the size of a loaf of bread, is that nobody in it has ever needed to teach you how to leave.

There were no roads out. There was a road, technically — a goat track that wound up past the slate quarries and thinned into a suggestion, then into a rumor, then into nothing at all. The valley's founders had been thorough about that. No road in meant no road out, and for two hundred years the Dravenmoors and the Ellerys and the forty-odd other families of Hollow Vale had agreed, with the quiet stubbornness of people who had chosen forgetfulness on purpose, that this was a feature and not a prison.

Tamsin had spent twenty-six years agreeing with them.

Now there was a girl asleep on his workbench, breathing in the shallow, careful way of someone whose ribs had been recently introduced to a boot, and her map was bleeding on his floor, and the heralds' edict was nailed to the meetinghouse door with four iron nails, and none of it, none of it, could have followed him over the boundary stones if he simply stayed.

He could stay. He could burn Wren's map in the hearth, wake his mentor, say *I found a madwoman in the lane*, and go back to drawing coastlines that would never exist and lakes that — he stopped that thought before it finished forming. The lake existed now. He had walked its shore yesterday morning with his boots sinking into real mud, while Master Corvin stood behind him saying nothing in the particular way that meant everything.

*You drew a place,* Corvin had finally said, when they were back inside and the door was barred, which was ridiculous, because no one in the valley barred doors. *Places don't come from drawings, boy. Drawings come from places.*

*Then where did the lake come from?*

And Corvin had looked at him for a long time — long enough that Tamsin had counted the cracks in the ceiling plaster, all eleven of them, twice — and said, *Pack light.*

That had been yesterday. Today the edict was on the meetinghouse door, and Corvin had not said another word about anything, and Tamsin had spent the night wrapping Wren's bleeding map in oilcloth and deciding, in the small hours, that he was not the kind of man who let a half-dead stranger be dragged back to a pyre because staying was comfortable.

He was still deciding it, honestly. Every few minutes. Deciding it again, like bailing water.

* * *

Corvin was in the kitchen when Tamsin came down, doing something to a kettle that did not need doing. His mentor was seventy-one years old and built like a fence post that had decided, over many decades, to lean slightly left, and he had taught Tamsin everything: letterforms, scale, hachures, the difference between a river that bends and a river that lies. He had never once, in twenty years, raised his voice. He didn't raise it now.

"You'll take the quarry track," he said, without turning around. "Past the third cairn there's a sheep path the shepherds use. It's ugly, but it's honest."

"Master—"

"Don't." Corvin turned. His face was doing what old faces do, which is hold very still so that nothing escapes. "Don't thank me, don't argue with me, and above all don't make a speech. I've watched you draft speeches in your margins since you were nine years old. You write them out and cross them out and keep the crossed-out ones in case you need them later. I'm telling you now: you won't need them."

Tamsin's throat did something complicated. "The edict says harboring the gifted is—"

"The edict," Corvin said, "was written by men who have never been to this valley and cannot find it on any map, which I used to think was our great good fortune." He picked up the kettle, considered it, put it down. "It occurs to me this morning that perhaps we were not hidden, Tamsin. Perhaps we were *kept*. There's a difference, and I'm too old to learn which way round it goes."

"Come with us."

"No."

"Master—"

"I said no speeches, and that goes for both directions." But his voice cracked on *both*, just slightly, like ice on a pond in late spring, and Tamsin saw — really saw, for the first time in twenty years — how afraid the old man was. Not of the edict. Of the door. Of the space beyond it where his student was about to walk and he was not going to follow.

"Why?" Tamsin asked quietly. "Truly."

Corvin was silent for a while. Outside, the valley went on being the valley: someone's rooster making an idiot of itself, the mill wheel turning, Mrs. Ellery scolding her geese in a voice audible from three fields away. All of it utterly ordinary. All of it, Tamsin understood suddenly, a kind of music you only hear when you're about to stop hearing it forever.

"Because I spent my whole life teaching you that the world ends at those stones," Corvin said at last. "And last week you proved me wrong with a lake. If I walk out that gate with you, I'll spend every mile hoping you're wrong about everything else too — hoping the edict isn't real, hoping the burning isn't real, hoping the girl's map doesn't bleed. And a man who hopes against evidence isn't a mentor anymore, boy. He's luggage." He finally looked up, and his eyes were dry and fierce and terribly kind. "You go be the evidence. I'll stay here and be the hope. Someone has to remember you exactly as you are, in case the world out there tries to redraw you."

Tamsin laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and wiped his face with his sleeve like a child, and Corvin pretended not to notice by becoming intensely interested in the kettle again.

"There's bread in the satchel already," Corvin said. "Under the spare nibs. Don't crush the nibs. And—" he hesitated, then reached into his pocket and pressed something into Tamsin's hand: a compass, brass, worn smooth, older than either of them. "It belonged to the first Corvin. It points north. That's all it does. In a family of mapmakers, sometimes that's the most useful thing an object can do — tell you one true thing and mind its own business about the rest."

Tamsin closed his hand around it. It was warm from the old man's pocket.

"Third cairn," Corvin said. "Sheep path. Go."

* * *

Wren woke when he touched her shoulder, the way soldiers and stray cats wake — all at once, with her hand already closing on a knife that wasn't there. She'd lost the knife somewhere between wherever she'd come from and his workshop; he'd found the empty sheath in her coat and decided not to ask yet.

"It's me," he said. "Tamsin. We're leaving."

"Leaving." She tested the word like a coin bitten for gold. Her eyes were gray and fever-bright and missed nothing. "Out. Past the stones?"

"That's generally what leaving means, yes."

"Don't joke." She sat up too fast and paid for it, pressing a hand to her ribs. "Sorry. Sorry. I just — everyone who jokes near me lately ends up dead or worse." She looked at him then, really looked, taking inventory the way he took inventory of a coastline: headland, bay, hazard. Whatever she concluded, she nodded once. "Your old master. Is he coming?"

"No."

Something moved across her face — pity, maybe, or recognition. "They never do," she said softly. "The ones who stayed safe their whole lives. They give you bread and a blessing and stay by the fire. It's not cowardice. Mostly it's not." She swung her legs off the bench and stood, swaying, refusing his arm with a look. "Which way?"

"Up. Through the quarries. Can you walk?"

"I can walk, run badly, and lie fluently. Between the three of us we'll manage."

They went out through the workshop's back door, past the drying racks where his maps hung like laundry — the lake among them, innocent and damning — and Tamsin did not look at the lake. Then across the garden, where Corvin's beans climbed their strings in neat soldierly rows, and through the gate, which had never once in Tamsin's life been latched.

At the gate he stopped. He couldn't help it. Behind him the valley lay in its morning colors, green and gray and smoke-blue, the church spire, the mill, the little bridge over the little river, every feature known to him down to the individual stones. He had drawn this view four hundred times. He knew it better than his own hands.

He would never see it again. He knew that too, the way you know weather coming.

"Tamsin." Wren, ten steps ahead, not unkindly.

"I'm coming."

He didn't look back. Corvin hadn't come to the gate — of course he hadn't, the stubborn old post — and somehow that made it easier. If his mentor had stood there waving, Tamsin might have broken. Instead there was only the empty garden and the beans climbing their strings and the sense, faint but unmistakable, of being watched from a kitchen window by a man pretending to fuss with a kettle.

* * *

The quarry track was exactly as ugly as promised. It switchbacked up through spoil heaps and gorse, and Wren's breath went ragged within the first mile, and they stopped more often than Tamsin liked, sitting on cold stone with the valley shrinking below them into a toy model of itself.

"So," Wren said, during the fourth stop, her head tipped back against the rock. "The lake."

"What about it?"

"You drew it. Before it was there."

Tamsin busied himself with the bread. "My master thinks I misremembered a survey."

"Your master," Wren said, "is a very kind liar." She watched him not answer. "I've seen two gifted in my life before this month. A woman in Carrowmere who could mend broken things by holding them. A boy who could make plants grow in a night. Neither of them could *make things exist*. The Guild would have — " she stopped. Started again, quieter. "The Guild would have built you a tower and never let you leave it. Or burned you. Depends which decade."

"And which am I, to you? Tower or pyre?"

"Neither." She said it simply, and it landed harder than either option would have. "You're the man who opened his door to a bleeding stranger because she was bleeding. Everything after that is details."

They walked on. The second cairn passed on their left, leaning like a drunkard. The air changed — Tamsin noticed it without knowing what he was noticing, a thinning, a brightness, as though the valley's held breath were loosening its grip degree by degree. The sheep path, when they found it, was less a path than an argument the hillside occasionally conceded to, and they followed it upward through bracken and loose shale until the third cairn rose ahead of them on a bald shoulder of moor, and beyond it —

Beyond it, the land simply continued. That was all. Hills going on into hills, a haze of distance, a hawk hanging in the wind. The world, unhidden.

But between the third cairn and the continuation ran the boundary stones: seven of them, waist-high, mossy, arranged in a shallow arc like teeth. Tamsin had seen them a hundred times on rambles and never thought twice. They were just stones. Old ones, lichen-crusted, unremarkable.

Wren stopped as if she'd hit glass.

"What is it?"

"They're *singing*," she whispered. "Can't you — no. No, you can't, can you. You've lived inside them your whole life." She was staring at the arc of stones with an expression Tamsin had only ever seen on people looking at the sea: awe with fear underneath. "It's a warding. An old one. Older than the Edicts, older than the Guild. Someone wove a null around this entire valley, big enough to swallow a town, and they anchored it in seven stones because seven is — " she shook her head. "It doesn't matter. It's beautiful work. Whoever did it spent their whole life on it and died young, probably. Nulls eat the weaver."

"Then why can't I feel it?"

"Because you're inside it. Fish don't feel water." She turned to look at him, and the fever-brightness in her eyes had sharpened into something almost fierce. "The moment you step past those stones, Tamsin Dravenmoor, you're going to feel everything. Every working, every ward, every thread of it, all at once, for the first time. Most gifted are born outside and grow up learning to ignore it, like noise. You're going to hear silence end." She paused. "It can break people. I've seen it break people. So we go slow, and you hold onto my arm, and if you fall down, you fall down and then you get up. Agreed?"

It should have frightened him. It did frighten him. But underneath the fear, Tamsin felt something else stir — the same feeling he got standing before a blank sheet of vellum, the feeling that had gotten him through twenty years of a life that fit him like a borrowed coat. The feeling of a page waiting.

"Agreed," he said.

They crossed together, her arm through his, one step, two, three — and the world *opened*.

There was no other word for it. It was like being deaf since birth and hearing music for the first time, except the music was everywhere and in everything: the warding behind them humming its long patient note; the land ahead threaded through with faint silver lines, old paths and older promises; the hawk overhead carrying a spark of wildness bright as a coal; and Wren beside him blazing, actually *blazing*, dim and guttering but unmistakably alight with whatever it was the edict called abomination and she called being alive. And beneath all of it, under everything, steady as bedrock—

Himself.

Tamsin stopped walking. The moor wind moved through the bracken. Somewhere below, impossibly far now, a rooster was still making an idiot of itself.

"Oh," he said. Very quietly. Because he had expected, insofar as he'd expected anything, to feel magic arrive in him like a guest — foreign, new, overwhelming. Instead he felt recognition. The sensation flooding through him fit him the way his own handwriting fit his hand. It was the feeling he'd had all his life at the moment a map began to come right — the line landing true, the shape resolving — except it wasn't in the pen or the paper or the eye. It was in *him*. It had always been in him. The valley hadn't kept magic out.

The valley had kept him *in*, wrapped around his gift like bandages, so that he'd grown up thinking the tightness was simply how skin felt.

"It was mine," he said aloud. Wonderingly. "All along. It was always mine. They didn't hide me from magic. They hid magic from *me*."

Wren was watching him with her head tilted, and whatever she saw in his face made her smile — the first smile he'd seen from her, crooked and tired and real. "Hello," she said. "There you are."

And Tamsin laughed — a startled, unsteady, exhilarated laugh that came up from somewhere below his ribs and surprised the hawk off its branch. The grief was still there; he could feel it sitting in his chest like a stone from the cairn, heavy and permanent, the weight of a green garden and a leaning fence-post of a man and forty families who would never know why he'd gone. But braided through it, inseparable from it, was this: the world laid open like a chart, and himself at last drawn upon it, lines landing true.

He turned once and looked back. The valley lay below in its cup of hills, small and green and utterly ordinary, and he could not see the kitchen window, but he knew anyway that the old man was there, minding his own business about everything except one true thing.

"Goodbye, Master Corvin," Tamsin said, and touched the brass compass in his pocket, and turned north.

They walked. The moor rolled out ahead of them, silver-threaded and singing, and Tamsin learned, mile by mile, to bear the noise of the world — until, near dusk, on a ridge overlooking a country he had never seen and had no chart of, Wren suddenly seized his arm hard enough to bruise.

"Down," she breathed. "Get down — smoke. Do you see it? Three fires, moving in line. That's a hunting sweep, that's the pattern they used on the Guild, that's—"

Far below, torchlight crawled across the darkening land like ink spilled on vellum. And it was heading, unhurried and certain, toward the pass they would reach by morning.
