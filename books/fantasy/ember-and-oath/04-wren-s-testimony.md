## 4. Wren's Testimony

Wren woke to the smell of ink and bread, which was a strange pairing, and to the sight of a stranger's ceiling, which was stranger still.

She lay still for a moment, taking inventory. Ribs: bruised but whole. Left arm: bandaged, someone competent had done it. Throat: raw from running and screaming in roughly equal measure. The map — her hand flew to the satchel before she'd finished the thought, and there it was, tucked against her side like a sleeping animal. Still bleeding.

Not bleeding *blood*. She'd stopped thinking of it that way around the third day of flight. It bled something else — a thin red shimmer that seeped through the leather no matter how many times she wiped it, that pooled on whatever surface she set it down and refused, absolutely refused, to dry.

"You're awake," said a voice from the doorway. "Good. I was starting to worry I'd rescued someone just to have them die decoratively on my workbench."

The man from last night — Dravenmoor, Tamsin Dravenmoor, though he'd mumbled it like an apology — stood holding a tray with two bowls of porridge and an expression of cautious hope, as if porridge were a peace offering between nations.

"I'm not hungry," Wren said, because her mother had raised her to say that first, and then she ate the entire bowl while he watched with poorly concealed satisfaction.

"Tamsin," he said again, when she'd finished. "Tamsin Dravenmoor. And you are currently occupying my workshop, my spare blanket, and most of my professional dignity."

"Wren Ashvale." She sat up too fast and the room tilted politely. "Journeyman cartographer, Third Circle of the Royal Guild." A beat. "Formerly."

"Ah," Tamsin said. He pulled a stool over and sat, elbows on knees, and she saw now what she'd been too half-dead to notice last night: he looked at her satchel the way other men looked at locked strongboxes. Not greed. Hunger of a different flavor entirely. "You came a long way to bleed on my floor."

"Not your floor specifically." She looked down at her hands. They were still shaking, three days later, and she hated them for it. "I didn't know where I was going. The map did."

"The map," Tamsin repeated, very carefully, "did."

Wren unslung the satchel and laid it on the workbench between them, and they both looked at it the way one looks at a sleeping wolf.

* * *

"It started," she said, once she'd found her voice somewhere under the porridge, "with the census."

Tamsin frowned. "Censuses are normal."

"Censuses are normal. This one wasn't." Wren wrapped her hands around the empty bowl for warmth, though the workshop wasn't cold. "Last spring, the crown ordered a full survey of the realm. Every province, every road, every river re-measured. Fine. Good practice, even. But the surveyors weren't measuring land, Tamsin. They were measuring *people*. Where the gifted live. Which villages have hedge-witches. Which midwives deliver babies that don't cry until touched with iron. It was a map of us. Drawn by us."

She watched him absorb that. He had a face built for absorbing things — long, thoughtful, with ink permanently stained into the creases of his fingers, and she thought, absurdly, *he'd never survive a week at the Guild*, where you learned to keep your hands clean and your thoughts cleaner.

"The Guild protested," she went on. "Master Ellery wrote a formal objection. Said cartography served travelers, not tribunals. It was read aloud at court." Her mouth twisted. "Then the king read his reply aloud. Except—"

She stopped. Three days of running, and this was the part that kept catching in her throat like a fishbone.

"Except?" Tamsin prompted gently.

"Except it wasn't the king." Wren set the bowl down. "I was there. We all were — the whole Third Circle, summoned to the Guildhall to hear the royal decree. And King Aldric walked in, and everyone bowed, and I looked up because I'd seen him once at a procession when I was nine and I remembered he used to smile at crowds. He didn't smile. He didn't do anything. He stood there like—" she groped for the word, "—like a coat on a hook. Like someone had stepped out of him and forgotten to take the shape along."

Tamsin had gone very still.

"And then he spoke," Wren said. "And the voice was wrong. Not disguised-wrong. *Empty* wrong. Like wind through a chimney. And he said—" she closed her eyes; the words hadn't faded, they'd only sunk deeper, "—'Every gift is a debt unpaid. Every debtor will be found. Let the maps be made true.' And then he left. Didn't look at anyone. And Master Ellery said, quietly, to Provost Hennet, 'That man has been weeping behind his eyes since midwinter,' and three days later the soldiers came."

* * *

The purge itself, Wren told him in pieces, the way you tell a story you've been carrying in your teeth.

They'd come at night, which was traditional, and in force, which was new. Not the city watch — crown wardens, gray-cloaked, carrying charts instead of torches. Charts. That was the detail that made Tamsin's breath catch audibly, and Wren nodded grimly, because yes, exactly, she'd had the same reaction.

"They knew where everyone slept. Not roughly — *precisely*. Master Ellery's house had a hidden cellar behind the hearth that even the Guild provosts weren't supposed to know about. They went straight to it. Straight through it." Her voice flattened. "They had floor plans, Tamsin. Of our own guildhall. Drawn in a hand I recognized."

Whose? His eyes asked it.

"Provost Corvane," she said. "He taught me projection geometry. Best draftsman in the Guild. Kind, in a distant way — the kind of man who remembers your name but not your face. He drew the survey templates last spring. Everyone assumed he was following orders." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "He was. Just not *their* orders."

"What happened to him?"

"He walked out of the Guildhall the night before the soldiers came. Nobody saw him go. His desk was clean. His quarters were clean. There was a chart pinned to the door of the archive, and it was a map of the whole kingdom, and every place where a gifted person lived had a small red mark on it, and the marks were arranged—" she made herself say it, "—arranged in a pattern. A sigil. I copied it before we ran. I don't know what it means. But I know a signature when I see one, and that was one."

The firelight moved across Tamsin's face. He was looking at the bleeding map on his bench, and his expression had shifted from hunger to something colder and more awake.

"How many escaped?" he asked.

"Of the Third Circle? Four. Of the whole Guild?" She shook her head. "I don't know. Less than a tenth, maybe. They burned the archive, Tamsin. Two hundred years of maps. Every chart of every border ever negotiated. Gone in a night. You don't burn an archive unless you want history to start over from a blank page."

* * *

It was full dark outside when she finally showed him the map itself.

She'd been avoiding it, partly because touching it hurt — a low, buzzing ache up the wrist, like holding a struck bell — and partly because she wasn't sure yet whether this stranger was a rescuer or simply a longer delay before the pyre. But he'd listened. He'd fed her. He'd bandaged her arm without asking questions, and when she'd flinched at the touch of the cloth he'd pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of courtesy.

So she unwrapped the oilcloth and let him see it.

Tamsin made a sound she'd only ever heard from apprentices seeing their first masterwork — a small, involuntary exhale, reverence escaping custody.

The map was old. Older than the Guild, older than the dynasty, drawn on hide that had gone soft and brown as autumn. It showed the kingdom, but not the kingdom as any living cartographer knew it — the borders were different, the rivers wandered where rivers no longer went, and in the northeast corner, where the mountains shouldered up against the sky, there was a valley. Small. Unremarkable. Except that it was drawn in a different hand than everything around it, newer, firmer, and except that from the valley's edge, a faint red shimmer was spreading outward like frost across a window.

Like blood through water.

"It belonged to Master Ellery," Wren said. "He gave it to me the night the soldiers came. Shoved it in my arms and said, 'Take it to the Hollow Valley. Find the one who can read it.' And then he went back inside." Her jaw tightened. "He went back *inside*, Tamsin. To hold the door."

"Why the Hollow Valley?"

"Because—" she gestured helplessly at the spreading stain, "—because of this. It started bleeding the day after the decree. Ellery said the valley is the one place the thing in the palace can't reach. The one blank space on its map. And he said whoever lived there would know why." She finally looked up at him. "Do you?"

Tamsin didn't answer immediately. He leaned over the map, and Wren watched his fingers hover above the parchment without touching, tracing the air along the valley's border stones, and there was something in his posture she couldn't name — recognition, maybe, or homesickness for a home he was sitting in.

"No," he said at last, and it sounded almost true. "But I think I know who could answer better than I could. My mentor. Old Maren. She's spent forty years studying why magic doesn't cross those stones." A pause. "She also spent forty years telling me I had no talent whatsoever, so if you're hoping for a wise sage, temper your expectations."

Despite everything — the burned guildhall, the dead masters, the four hundred miles in her legs — Wren felt her mouth twitch. "What *do* you do, Tamsin Dravenmoor?"

"I draw places," he said. Then, quickly, as if he'd said too much: "Places that might exist. Proposed roads, speculative harbors. It's a niche market."

"Mm," said Wren, who had spent six years among cartographers and knew what a lie of omission looked like when it was trying to hold a straight edge.

* * *

Later — much later, the candle burned to a thumb's width, the bleeding map wrapped and quarantined in a wooden chest like the dangerous relic it apparently was — Wren sat by the fire and tried to sleep and failed, and heard Tamsin moving quietly around the workshop, and finally said, to the darkness:

"She'll come here. Corvane. Or whatever he serves will. Your valley is the last blank space, and blank spaces are the one thing a mapmaker can't abide."

The movement stopped.

"I know," Tamsin said.

"You should leave. Both of us. Tonight, before—"

"I know," he said again, softer. And then, after a silence long enough for the fire to settle twice: "Wren. The sigil you copied. The pattern of red marks. May I see it?"

She fished the scrap of paper from her boot, where it had lived against her skin since the burning, and handed it over. He held it to the candlelight, and she watched his face do something complicated — dread and wonder fighting for the same ground.

"This isn't a sigil," he murmured. "Or — it is, but that's secondary. Look at the spacing. The marks aren't decorative, they're *coordinates*. Nodes in a network." His finger traced the pattern without quite touching the paper. "This is a survey grid. Someone is mapping the hunt itself, region by region, marking territory as it's cleared. It's not a signature." He looked up at her, and the firelight caught something in his eyes that was equal parts terror and terrible clarity. "It's a *legend*. A key. Whoever drew this isn't hunting the gifted, Wren. They're mapping a country where the gifted don't exist. And every red mark is a promise that the map will come true."

Outside, far off toward the valley's boundary stones, a dog began to bark — one dog, then another, then a dozen, the sound rolling down the dark hillsides like a stone down a well.

Wren was on her feet before she'd decided to stand, satchel already in her hand, three days of flight collapsing into a single instinct: *run*.

But Tamsin hadn't moved. He stood in the middle of his workshop with the scrap of paper in his fist, looking at his own neat rows of pens, his careful shelves of finished charts, his whole quiet life arranged around him like furniture in a house about to be surveyed — and when he turned to her, his voice was steady in a way she suspected cost him everything.

"Get your boots on," he said. "And tell me everything you remember about the road south. Because I think, Wren Ashvale, we're going to need a route that isn't on any map."

He reached for a fresh sheet of parchment.

And paused, hand hovering, as if he'd heard something in his own heartbeat.
