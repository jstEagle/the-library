## 5. The Road That Wasn't There

The trouble with fleeing for your life, Tamsin decided somewhere around the third hour of stumbling through the Thornwood, was that nobody had ever taught him the proper technique.

He had been taught to read contour lines by candlelight until his eyes ached. He had been taught to triangulate from three fixed points and to never, ever trust a single bearing. He had been taught that a mapmaker's first duty was honesty — *the land is what it is; your job is only to tell it true*. What he had not been taught was how to run through undergrowth in the dark while carrying a feverish girl on his back and a satchel of ink bottles that clinked with every step like a small, treasonous bell choir.

"Left," Wren croaked against his shoulder. "No — my left. Your right."

"You said left a moment ago."

"I said *my* left. I'm facing backward. It's very confusing being carried by someone who argues."

"I'm not arguing. I'm navigating." He ducked under a low branch that tried to take his hat — his good hat, the one with the wide brim he'd worn every day for six years — and felt it scrape past anyway. "There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed weakly, "and you're losing it."

He would have laughed if laughing hadn't cost breath he needed for other things, like breathing. Behind them, back down the slope toward the valley's boundary stones, torchlight flickered through the trees in a way torchlight should not flicker through trees: in formation. Four points of orange light, moving in a line, spaced evenly, the way soldiers moved when they had been told to sweep a wood and leave nothing unswept.

Hunters. Crown hunters, wearing the new seal — a crown over crossed quills, which Tamsin found almost personally insulting. He had spent his whole life believing maps were gentle things. Now they were being used as weapons of arrest, and the heralds had chosen a *quill* for their sigil.

"They've stopped," Wren murmured. "Listen."

He stopped. The wood went quiet around them — the particular, listening quiet of a forest that has noticed something wrong. No nightjars. No rustling of things small and sensible getting out of the way. Just the distant crackle of torches and, beneath it, a sound he couldn't place at first: a soft, rhythmic whispering, like paper being turned.

"What is that?" he whispered.

"That's how they hunt us," Wren said. Her voice had gone flat, the way it did when she talked about the Guild hall burning. "They have charts. Charts of everyone who ran. Someone at court is drawing the hunt as it happens — updating it, region by region. When they stop moving, it means they've found where we are *on the page*. Then they come to where we are in the world."

Tamsin stood very still in the dark, holding a half-dead cartographer's apprentice, and felt the thought settle over him like cold water.

Someone was mapping them. And wherever the map said they were, there they were.

It occurred to him — not for the first time since the boundary stones, but more sharply than ever before — that this rule could work both ways.

* * *

They reached the ravine an hour past midnight, and the ravine ended the argument about which direction to go, because neither direction was possible.

It cut across the Thornwood like a wound, forty feet deep, sheer-sided, its bottom lost in fog and the sound of water. On Tamsin's map — the real one, the surveyor's map, the honest one — the ravine was exactly where the ravine was, because he had drawn it himself two summers ago during the valley's outer survey, and the land had politely agreed to match him.

"We go along the rim," he said. "North. There's a footbridge at the old mill, maybe six miles."

"The torches are north," Wren said. "They'll be at the bridge before we are. They have horses. We have—" she looked down at herself, at her burned hands and ruined boots, "—me."

"South, then. There's a ford—"

"Fords are places, Tamsin. Places can be watched. That's rather the point of everything happening to us."

He set her down against a beech trunk, carefully, the way you set down something borrowed and precious, and crouched at the ravine's edge with his satchel open across his knees. Below, the fog breathed. Somewhere in it, water worked patiently at stone, the way water does, unhurried, certain of winning.

The torches were closer now. He could smell the pitch. And beneath the pitch, fainter, that whispering again — pages turning, ink drying, a pen moving in long confident strokes. Drawing them. Closing the circle.

Wren watched him unroll a fresh sheet and pin it to his drawing board. "What are you doing?"

"Thinking."

"With a pen in your hand?"

"I always think with a pen in my hand. It's how I know I'm thinking and not panicking." He licked his thumb and rubbed out a smudge that wasn't there yet, purely out of habit. "Wren. When we crossed the stones. You felt it too. The — the brush of it. Like walking through a spiderweb made of warm air."

She was quiet a moment. "I felt magic die behind me, if that's what you mean. Whatever the valley does, it stayed inside."

"It didn't stay inside." He heard his own voice drop, and hated how much it shook. "Wren, I drew a lake. Three nights before it existed. Every inlet. Every reed bed. The little island with the crooked pine. And then it was *there*, and Master Corvel looked at me like I'd grown a second head, and I told myself it was coincidence, because the alternative was — was *this*. Was running through a wood at midnight knowing that the thing hunting the whole kingdom might be living in my own hand."

The torchlight was nearer. He could hear voices now, low and disciplined, calling positions to one another. *Rim's clear. Check the mill path.*

"Tamsin," Wren said softly, "what are you thinking?"

He looked at the blank sheet pinned to his board. He looked at the ravine. Forty feet of impossible, honest geography, put there by no one's pen, indifferent to the wants of anyone.

And then he thought — with the strange, floating clarity that comes at the exact moment a life changes shape — *the land is what it is.*

*Unless it isn't.*

"My whole life," he said, "I've been telling the land what it is. Very quietly. In pencil first, usually."

"...Tamsin."

"And it has been *listening*."

"*Tamsin.*"

He dipped his pen.

Later — much later, in safer places, with wine and firelight and people who loved him — he would try to describe what it felt like, and he would never quite manage it. The nearest he ever came was this: it felt like speaking after years of being certain you were mute. It felt like the first word of a conversation you'd been having alone your entire life, suddenly answered.

He drew a road.

Not a suggestion of a road. Not a surveyor's careful dotted line, *path here, condition unknown*. He drew a road the way roads ought to be: broad enough for two carts, graded, graveled, switchbacking down the ravine's southern wall in long confident curves, bridging the water on a stone span, climbing the far side into the hills beyond. He drew culverts. He drew a milestone, and then, because terror makes a man whimsical, he lettered on it: *SAFE HAVEN — 12 MILES — KEEP SMILING.*

"Tamsin, what are you—" Wren began, and then the world *moved*.

There was no other word for it. The ground shivered like a struck drumhead. The fog below the rim lit from within, a soft gold glow rising up the ravine walls, and Tamsin felt the drawing leave him — felt it tear free of the nib and pour out through his arm, his chest, his teeth, a river of certainty flowing out of him into the world. His nose started bleeding. He didn't notice. The pen kept moving even after he'd finished the road, dragging itself toward the margin, hungry, wanting to draw *more* — bridges, towns, a whole country with him at the center of it, and some cold, delighted part of him wanted to let it—

Wren's burned hand closed over his wrist.

"No," she said. "That's enough. *That's enough.*"

The pen stopped. The glow faded. Tamsin came back to himself kneeling in dead leaves with blood on his lip and his heart slamming and the taste of lightning in his mouth, and Wren gripping his arm hard enough to bruise, staring at him with an expression he'd never seen on anyone: fear and wonder fighting to a draw.

Below them, where forty feet of empty air had been, there was a road.

* * *

It was, he had to admit, a very good road.

That was the thought his mind chose, absurdly, as they picked their way down the first switchback together — Wren leaning on his arm, both of them staring at the gravel under their feet as if it might apologize and vanish. The grade was perfect. The drainage ditches were perfect. The stone of the bridge, when they reached it, had that weathered gray patience of masonry that has stood for a century, moss in the joints, initials carved by some nonexistent shepherd boy. There was even a milestone. It read, in his own handwriting:

*SAFE HAVEN — 12 MILES — KEEP SMILING*

"Oh no," Tamsin said.

"It's a nice touch," Wren said faintly.

"It's a *terrible* touch. Roads don't editorialize. Roads don't have *handwriting*." He knelt and touched the stone and snatched his fingers back as if it had bitten him. It was warm. Everything was warm — the gravel, the bridge rail, the air. The road smelled faintly of his workshop. Of linseed oil and cedar shavings and the particular brand of ink he'd bought from a peddler in Aldermoor. This road was *made of him*, and now he was standing on it, and the understanding of what that meant arrived all at once, like cold water finding the seam in a boot.

If he could make a road, he could make anything. If he could make anything, then everything he'd ever drawn —

Every map. Every chart. Every careful, honest survey he'd sold to farmers and foresters and, once, to a crown surveyor who had paid too generously and smiled too long.

"Wren," he said. "The stolen chart. The one from the Emberhold network — the one Sefa's people said the hunters used to find the safehouse outside Marrowdale. I drew that. Two years ago. A routine commission."

Wren had gone very still. "You drew the chart they're using to hunt the gifted."

"I drew it as a *map of a river valley*."

"Things change," Wren said quietly, "when you draw them."

Above them, on the north rim, a horn sounded. Short, sharp, repeated twice. Then a voice, carrying clearly across the ravine in the still air:

*"Contact! Fresh works on the south wall — they've gone down! Down the new road!"*

A pause. Then another voice, younger, uncertain: *"Sir, there isn't a road on the south wall. There's never been a road on the south wall. My grandmother grazed sheep on that wall—"*

*"Then your grandmother owes the crown an explanation. Move!"*

Torchlight bloomed along the opposite rim. Boots began to pound.

Tamsin took Wren's hand and ran.

The road carried them — that was the horror and the mercy of it, it *carried* them, the switchbacks arriving exactly when needed, the footing sure, the curves banked so perfectly that even Wren, half-collapsing, could lurch along at a pace that would have been impossible on any honest ground. They plunged through the fog layer, across the bridge — the water underneath sounding startled, if water can sound startled, rushing white and confused around piers that had not been there at dusk — and up the far side, and the whole time Tamsin's mind was screaming at him in his mentor's voice.

*A mapmaker's first duty is honesty. The land is what it is; your job is only to tell it true.*

*I just lied,* he thought, lungs burning. *I lied a road into the world, and now men with torches are chasing us down my lie, and the lie is holding my weight better than the truth ever did.*

Halfway up the southern wall, Wren stumbled and went down, and he caught her, and for a moment they both just hung there in the fog, gasping.

"Listen," she wheezed. "Listen to me. You need to hear this now, because later you'll be too tired or too scared or too proud. What you did back there — that wasn't a trick. That wasn't a clever escape. That was *power*, Tamsin. Real power, the kind that gets kingdoms burned. And it liked it." She gripped his collar. "You felt it wanting more. Don't pretend you didn't. I saw your face."

He couldn't pretend. His face had presumably shown everything; his face had never once in his life successfully hidden anything, which was why he'd become a mapmaker instead of a card player.

"It wanted to keep going," he admitted. "It wanted to draw the whole ravine full of towns. It wanted—" He swallowed. "It wanted me to be the kind of man who redraws the world because he can."

"And what kind of man are you?"

Behind and above them, torches spilled over the north rim onto his beautiful, terrible road. Ahead, the southern hills rose black against a sky just beginning, very faintly, to consider dawn. Twelve miles to nothing in particular. No safe haven. He'd invented the milestone out of panic and whimsy, and now the words sat in the world like a debt he'd have to pay.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I'm going to find out somewhere that isn't here."

They ran. The road bent south, generous, obedient, leading them away from the torchlight — and Tamsin understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the fog, that the road was doing what he'd wanted without being asked. That his gift was reading him. That every careless wish, every stray fear, might now be a line waiting for a pen.

The gift was confirmed. It was loud, it was dangerous, it was visible from a mile off in every direction, and somewhere behind them a hunter was shouting for a runner to be sent to the capital with a message Tamsin could already compose in his head:

*The fugitives have made contact with a reality-shaper. Repeat: a maker. Send the surveyors. Send the special charts. Tell His Majesty the mapmaker exists.*

Dawn found them cresting the last hill, ragged, bleeding, alive. Below, in a fold of the southern hills where no fold should have been, smoke rose from chimneys of a village that was not on any map Tamsin had ever seen or drawn.

Wren stared at it. Then at him. Then at it.

"Please tell me," she said, "that you didn't do that in your sleep."

"I have never been there in my life," Tamsin said. "I have never *drawn* it in my life."

The village waited below, twelve miles early, smelling of woodsmoke and bread — and on its green, unmistakable even at this distance, a banner with a crown over crossed quills, torn down and trampled into the mud.
