## 6. Vaelith

The Ashreach mountains did not appear on any map Tamsin had ever drawn, and he had begun to suspect that this was not an accident.

For three days they had climbed through passes that seemed to rearrange themselves behind them — Wren swore the trail they'd taken that morning was gone by afternoon, replaced by scree and a stubborn stand of black pines that hadn't been there before. Tamsin had checked his sketches twice, three times, and found that the mountains refused to hold still on paper the way rivers and roads did. His pencil would draw a ridge; by evening the ridge had wandered a mile east, as if embarrassed to be caught in one place.

"They don't want to be mapped," Wren said, hunched into her borrowed coat, breath steaming. She was walking better now, color back in her face, though she still favored her left side where the burn scars pulled tight. "The old guild stories said the Ashreach were the first place magic ever slept. Maybe it's still half-asleep, and it dreams badly."

"Everything dreams badly," Tamsin said. "I once dreamed I was being chased by a goose with my mentor's face."

Wren snorted, which he counted as a victory. In the week since leaving the valley she had laughed exactly four times, and he had kept a private tally, the way he kept tallies of everything: milestones, stream crossings, the number of times his hands shook when he thought about the road he'd drawn into existence beneath their fleeing feet. The tally of laughter was the only one that made him feel anything but afraid.

They crested the pass at dusk on the third day, and the world opened up below them like a page someone had written in fire.

A valley — no, a *bowl*, a vast crater walled in by peaks the color of old iron — lay spread out beneath a sky going violet at the edges. And in the center of it, coiled around a spire of black glass like a cat around a chair leg, was a dragon.

Tamsin's first coherent thought was that every map he had ever made was a lie of omission. His second was that he should probably sit down. He did both at once, which Wren later said looked very dignified.

"That's—" he managed.

"Yes," Wren said.

"It's enormous."

"Yes."

"I drew a dragon once. When I was nine. On the back of a survey chart." He heard his own voice going thin and strange. "It was about the size of a barn cat. I remember thinking I was being generous."

"This one could eat the barn," Wren said. "And the cat. And probably the county."

They watched as the last light caught the creature's scales — not gold, not red, but a deep ember-orange gone gray at the edges, like a hearth left untended overnight. Even from this distance, even across a mile of cold air, Tamsin could see what the stories had never bothered to mention: she was *thin*. The great coils around the glass spire were magnificent the way a ruined cathedral is magnificent, all architecture and no weight behind it. Her wings, folded along her flanks, were veined with light that flickered rather than glowed.

"She's dying," Tamsin said quietly. It wasn't a question. You didn't spend your life reading terrain without learning to read everything else too.

"The fugitives' letters said the last dragon keeps the high passes," Wren murmured. "They didn't say *this*." Her hand found his sleeve — not for comfort, he suspected, but to stop him doing something foolish. It was a reasonable precaution. "We don't have to go down there. We could go around—"

"There is no 'around,'" Tamsin said. "Look at the walls of the bowl. There's one pass, and we're standing in it."

He was right, which was becoming an uncomfortable habit. The crater had exactly one entrance, and as if to underline the point, the wind shifted and carried up to them a sound like a bell the size of a cathedral being struck once, softly.

The dragon's head had turned. She was looking directly at them.

Two eyes the color of banked coals fixed on the tiny figures at the pass, and Tamsin felt them land on him with physical weight, the way sunlight through a magnifying lens lands. Not hostile. Worse than hostile: *recognizing*.

"Oh no," he said.

"What?"

"She knows we're here. She *knew* we were here. Wren, I think she knew before we did."

* * *

The descent took an hour. It felt like a funeral procession, mostly because neither of them spoke, and the mountain air had the particular silence of a place where sound goes to be judged.

Up close, Vaelith was worse and better than she'd seemed from above. Worse, because the gray in her scales wasn't weathering — it was ash. Patches of her hide had gone dull and chalky, and when she breathed, the glow in her throat stuttered like a lamp running out of oil. Better, because her eyes held nothing of death in them at all. They held *amusement*, which Tamsin found infinitely more alarming.

She did not rise. She watched them pick their way across the crater floor, and when they stopped — at a distance that felt respectful and was, he realized too late, simply the edge of her shadow — she spoke.

Her voice was not what he expected. He had braced for thunder. What he got was dry and warm and slightly cracked, like a well-loved book opening, like his mentor reading aloud on a winter evening.

"You walk like a man counting his steps," she said. "Left, right, left, right. As if the ground might cheat you."

"It has before," Tamsin said, before his caution could catch up with his mouth.

Something moved at the corner of her vast mouth. A smile, or the geological precursor of one. "And yet here you are, standing in the one place in the world the ground cannot lie about. Do you know why, mapmaker?"

"I'm not—"

"You are." The word settled over him like snowfall. "I have waited two hundred years to say it to the right person, so you will forgive me if I decline to un-say it. The mapmaker. Come down from the Ashreach at last, smelling of ink and fear and a valley that shouldn't exist." Her head lowered, bringing those coal-gold eyes level with him, close enough that he could feel heat radiating off her breath — faint heat, guttering heat, a candle-flame wearing the shape of a furnace. "Do you know what you smell like, Tamsin Dravenmoor?"

"How do you know my—"

"Everything you have ever drawn remembers you," she said simply. "And I am very old, and I listen to everything." Her nostrils flared, drawing in a long, slow breath of him. "You smell like a lake that appeared overnight. Like a road that grew out of desperation. Like a boy who was told all his life that magic stops at the valley stones, and believed it, because believing it was easier than asking why the valley needed to stop anything."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. Beside him, Wren had gone very still, the stillness of someone watching a trap close around someone else and being grateful and horrified in equal measure.

"The valley," Tamsin said carefully, "is just a valley."

"Mm." Vaelith's eyes half-lidded. "And I am just a large lizard with opinions. Shall we agree to keep each other's secrets?" She exhaled, and the breath stirred his hair and smelled, strangely, of woodsmoke and old paper. "Sit down, both of you. My fire is short tonight, and what remains of it does not care to waste itself on people who insist on standing through a long story."

There was, Tamsin discovered, no argument that survived contact with a dying dragon's invitation. They sat — Wren cross-legged and wary, Tamsin with his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield, which he only noticed he was doing when Vaelith's gaze flicked to it with unmistakable irony.

* * *

"My fire is going out," she said, without preamble, once they were settled. "Not quickly — dragons do nothing quickly, not even die — but certainly. Two hundred years ago I could have melted the iron peaks. A hundred years ago I could have boiled the lakes. Now?" A flicker ran down her throat, guttered, steadied. "Now I can keep one thing warm, and I spend nearly all of it doing that, and it is not enough, and every year it is less enough."

"Keeping what warm?" Wren asked. Her voice came out smaller than usual.

Vaelith was quiet for a moment. When she answered, the amusement had drained out of her voice entirely, and what remained was so tired that Tamsin felt it in his own bones.

"A man," she said. "A king. Or what is left of one."

The wind moved through the crater. Somewhere above, a stone cracked with the cold.

"His name is Aldric," she went on. "Though I doubt anyone has used it aloud in years. Everyone says *the crown* now, or *the throne*, or *the edict*, as if the kingdom were run by furniture. And perhaps it is. Perhaps that is the most honest description anyone has managed." Her tail shifted against the black glass spire, and the movement made a sound like a ship settling. "Long ago — longer than your guilds, longer than your borders — Aldric and I swore an oath together. A binding oath. The kind that is not words but *architecture*. He swore to protect this land and everyone in it. I swore to give him the fire to do it. Two halves of one promise."

"And something went wrong," Tamsin said quietly.

"Something went *right*," Vaelith corrected, "which is far more dangerous. Oaths want to be kept. That is their nature. But Aldric's oath was too large for one life — protect *everyone*, forever, perfectly — and so the oath began keeping itself. It reached into him and started building. A perfect protector needs no mercy, no doubt, no grief. So it burned those away, piece by piece, and wore what remained." Her eyes closed. "The thing issuing your edicts, child. The thing marching gifted children to pyres. It is not a demon and not a usurper. It is a *vow*. Aldric's own promise, grown fat and hollow, eating its maker to fund its purpose. And my fire — the little that is left — is the only wall between it and the last living part of him."

Tamsin stared at her. The scale of it pressed down on him like altitude. "Then the hunt for the gifted—"

"Is the oath protecting the kingdom from the kingdom's own magic," she said. "Perfectly. Mercilessly. Exactly as sworn." A pause. "You begin to see the problem."

"And you've spent two hundred years holding the wall," Wren whispered, "alone."

"I have spent two hundred years holding the wall," Vaelith agreed, "*and* alone, and I will thank you not to make me cry about it, because dragon tears are mildly corrosive." But the joke landed soft, and her eyes when they opened again were bright in a way that had nothing to do with fire.

* * *

It was Tamsin who finally asked the question sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"Why me?" he said. "Why 'mapmaker'? Why did you wait two hundred years to say it to *me*? I'm nobody. I drew maps of a valley nobody cared about, and until a month ago the most magical thing I'd ever done was win an argument with a goat."

"Tell her about the road," Wren said quietly.

So he did. Haltingly, staring at his own hands — the road that hadn't existed, drawn in desperation while hunters closed in, appearing beneath their boots like it had always been there, real enough to carry their weight, real enough that the hunters had stood at its edge and howled. Real enough that he had lain awake afterward feeling like a man who had discovered he'd been juggling knives his whole life without looking at his hands.

When he finished, Vaelith was silent for a long time.

"Do you know what the echo is doing right now?" she said at last. "While it burns the gifted and marches its armies? It is *redrawing the kingdom*. Slowly. Border by border, law by law, heart by heart. Every village it empties, every name it strikes from the rolls, every road it closes — it is editing. Making the land simpler. Making it match the oath's perfect design. It is the greatest cartographer alive, and it doesn't even know that's what it is." Her gaze pinned him. "And the only counterweight to a map being drawn is another map being drawn. One that comes from somewhere other than the oath. One that comes from *outside* — from a valley built to hide something from the oath's sight, by people who understood, centuries ago, that a day would come when the kingdom needed a pen it couldn't see coming."

The bottom dropped out of Tamsin's stomach.

"The valley," he said. "The Hollow Valley isn't a refuge. It's a—"

"A locked drawer," Vaelith said, "with something in it. Yes. Your ancestors were not hiding *from* magic, Tamsin Dravenmoor. They were hiding *a magician*. They just weren't told which one. Kinder that way, probably. Children grow strange when raised as weapons." Something almost gentle crossed the vast face. "You were raised as a cartographer instead. Which is to say: as yourself. It may be the kindest thing anyone has ever done for this kingdom, and none of them knew they were doing it."

Tamsin looked down at his hands again. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained, chapped from the cold, faintly trembling. Hands that had drawn a lake into being and never noticed. Hands that had drawn a road under his own fleeing feet and called it luck.

"I don't know how to control it," he said. It came out barely above a whisper. "The road — I didn't decide it. I just wanted it so badly the pencil did it. What happens when I want something *wrong*? What happens when I'm angry, or frightened, or—" He stopped, because the next word was *what happens when someone makes me*, and they both heard it anyway.

"Now you understand the weight," Vaelith said. "Good. Anyone who hands you a destiny without also handing you the fear of it is trying to use you." She lowered her head further, until her brow nearly touched the frozen ground between them, and this close Tamsin could see the fine ash-gray cracks running through her scales like faults in porcelain. "Here is what I can offer. Not training — I am a dragon, not a tutor, and my remaining strength is spoken for. But there are others. Survivors. Gifted folk who fled the purge, gathered in a hold in the western reaches, led by a woman named Sefa who trusts no one and feeds everyone. They have been waiting for a sign. For *you*, though they don't know it yet."

"And if I refuse?" Tamsin asked. "If I just — go home? Back to the valley? Draw maps of lakes and mind my own business?"

Vaelith smiled then, fully, and it was terrible and kind at once.

"Then the echo finishes redrawing the kingdom, finds your valley — it will, eventually; nothing stays hidden from a perfect map forever — and burns it last of all, because hidden things offend it most. And you will spend whatever remains of your life knowing that the one person who could have drawn a different ending chose to draw none." She straightened, and the effort cost her visibly; the glow in her throat dimmed and took a long moment to return. "But you won't refuse. I've watched you since the pass, mapmaker. You haven't stopped counting your steps, but you haven't stopped *walking* either. That is the whole of courage, as far as I've ever determined. The rest is commentary."

The light was failing fast now, true dark pooling in the crater's corners. Wren had wrapped her arms around her knees, and Tamsin saw her lips moving silently — rehearsing, maybe, or praying, or tallying something of her own.

"One more thing," Vaelith said, as they rose stiffly to their feet. "The road west. Through the Ironmark. You'll need provisions, and the pass is watched since the burning at Thornford." Her eyes glittered. "You'll want to draw yourselves a safer way."

"I told you," Tamsin said, "I can't control—"

"No," Vaelith agreed placidly. "But you can *practice*. Tonight, by my fire, with me watching. Because tomorrow, mapmaker, you begin learning the truth that will save or damn us all: that every line you draw is a promise, and the kingdom is full of promises already kept, and yours must be stronger." She curled her great head back onto the black glass spire, and the ember-light of her throat painted them both orange and trembling. "Sleep first. Even destinies keep decent hours."

As they spread their bedrolls near the warmth of her — actual warmth, the first either of them had felt in days — Tamsin lay staring up at the wheeling stars and felt the weight settle over him, vast and specific, like the dragon's shadow at noon.

Somewhere south, a king made of a broken vow was redrawing the world.

And here in the ashes of the mountains, a dying fire and a frightened cartographer were all that stood between the oath and the ending it had planned.

"Tamsin," Wren whispered across the dark.

"Hm?"

"When you practice tonight — draw us something small." A pause. "Please. Nothing with teeth."

He smiled into the cold, and reached for his satchel, and found his hands had already stopped shaking.
