## 7. The Fugitive Network

Wren had learned, in the weeks since the Cartographers' Guild burned, that fear came in flavors. There was the sharp kind — hunters' boots on stairs, the smell of pitch and torches. And there was the slow kind, which was worse: the flavor of walking for days beside a stranger who drew impossible things and never once seemed frightened of them.

Tamsin Dravenmoor was currently humming.

He was crouched at the edge of a stream, sketching a bridge onto a scrap of parchment with the easy contentment of a man drawing flowers, while Wren kept watch on the treeline with her hands shaking around a knife she barely knew how to use.

"You could hum *quieter*," she said.

"Sorry." He didn't sound sorry. "It's nearly done. Look."

She didn't want to look. Looking at Tamsin's drawings had stopped feeling safe somewhere around the time a road had grown out of bare hillside beneath their feet like a root pushing through soil. But she looked anyway, because that was the other thing she'd learned: you always looked.

The bridge was drawn in his careful surveyor's hand — stone, arched, mossy at the joints, exactly the sort of bridge that belonged over this particular stream. Except there was no bridge over the stream. There had never been a bridge over the stream.

By the time Wren finished counting to forty, there was.

Stone. Arched. Mossy at the joints. A heron was already standing on it, regarding them with the offended dignity of a bird whose fishing spot had been renovated without consultation.

"That's going to get us killed," Wren said flatly. "You know that, don't you? Somewhere out there, someone is going to notice a brand-new bridge on a stream nobody bridges."

"It's a *good* bridge," Tamsin said, wounded.

"It's a *loud* bridge."

He opened his mouth to argue, and then closed it, because he'd been doing more listening than arguing since the road. Since the moment he'd felt magic move through him and recognized it the way you recognize your own reflection in dark water — late, and badly, and all at once.

"We're close," he said instead, folding the parchment away. "Aren't we? You said three days past the Ashreach passes."

Wren looked north, where the mountains went up like a wall someone had built out of spite. Somewhere up there, if half the rumors were true, was a refuge called the Emberhold — a place where the gifted weren't burned. She'd been following those rumors since the night she'd run from the Guildhall with a bleeding map clutched to her chest and Master Corvin's last words in her ears: *"Find the Emberhold. Tell them what the king said."*

"If it exists," she said. "If it hasn't moved. If they let us in."

"You don't sound hopeful."

"I sound *surviving*," Wren said. "Hope is for people who aren't worth money dead."

Tamsin winced. He still did that — flinched at the arithmetic of the hunt, as though he hadn't spent his whole life in a valley where the worst news arrived a season late. She should have found it naive. Instead she'd found herself guarding it, the way you guard a candle flame with your whole hand cupped around it.

They crossed the good, loud bridge. The heron watched them go.

* * *

The Emberhold did not announce itself.

That was the first thing Wren understood about it. They climbed through two days of scree and pine, following instructions that weren't instructions — a cairn shaped wrong, a pine split by lightning leaning east, a hollow stone with a coal-scar inside — until Wren began to suspect the refuge was either a myth or a test. Then the mountain simply opened.

There was no door. There was a seam in the rock, wide as a wagon, and standing in it a woman with close-cropped hair, burn scars laddering one forearm, and the posture of someone who had been expecting them for two days and was annoyed at being right.

"You're late," the woman said.

"The passes were—" Tamsin began.

"I know what the passes were. I drew them." She stepped aside. "In. Before something with wings or warrants sees you."

Inside, the mountain was warm.

That was the second thing Wren understood, and it undid her more than the door had. Warmth — real warmth, woodsmoke and bread and tallow, the heat of many bodies living together on purpose. The seam opened into a cavern worked into halls and stairways, lamplit, hung with drying herbs and laundry, loud with voices. Children ran past hauling a rope. An old man argued cheerfully with a woman twice his size about whether a stew wanted thyme or *more* thyme. Someone was playing a fiddle badly and with tremendous commitment.

Wren stood in the entrance with her heart hammering and realized she was waiting for the trap. For the doors to seal, for the masks to come off, for the hunters to step out smiling. That was what safety had taught her: that it was bait.

"You can put the knife down," the scarred woman said. "Nobody here needs stabbing today. I'm Sefa. I run this place, which means I'm the one who decides who stays, so try to be interesting."

"I'm Wren," Wren said. "I was apprentice to Master Corvin, of the Royal Cartographers' Guild. Before it burned."

Something shifted behind Sefa's eyes — not softness exactly; more like a ledger being updated. "Corvin's girl. He wrote to us, years back. Said if anything ever happened to him, find the girl with the steady hands." Her gaze flicked over Wren, cataloguing: the burns on her own arms, old ones, the way she held her weight off her left ankle. "And him?"

"Tamsin Dravenmoor," Tamsin said. "Cartographer. Sort of."

"Sort of?"

"He draws places," Wren said, because Tamsin seemed constitutionally unable to say it himself, "and then the places happen."

The fiddle stopped.

It wasn't dramatic — no record scratch of destiny, no gasp. Just a small silence spreading outward through the hall like ink through water, conversation dimming room by room as the words traveled, until the whole cavern was looking at the tall, tired young man from the Hollow Valley who had never in his life been looked at by more than four people at once.

"Oh," Tamsin said faintly.

Sefa's expression didn't change, but her voice dropped into something quieter and harder. "Say that again. Slowly."

"He draws places," Wren repeated. "And then the places happen. A road. Two bridges. A spring when we were dying of thirst. He doesn't mean to. He didn't even know he was gifted until—"

"Until recently," Tamsin supplied.

"Until *extremely* recently," Wren said. "He thought magic stopped at the edge of his valley. Everyone there thinks that. It's rather the point of the valley."

Now Sefa did go still — a different stillness, the stillness of a hunter hearing a sound in brush. "Hollow Valley," she said. "You're from *inside*."

"Born and raised."

"And you walked out."

"Carrying her," Tamsin said, nodding at Wren. "Mostly."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — one short, sharp bark with no humor in it at all. "Do you know how many people have died trying to find this place? Do you know how many *gifted* people have died within ten miles of these passes, because the gift leaks and the hunters' instruments taste it?" She stepped closer. "And you strolled out of a valley that hides itself from magic, carrying a fugitive, and the mountains just... let you in."

"They've been very accommodating," Tamsin agreed cautiously.

"He's not joking," Wren said. "I've checked. He's never joking. It's exhausting."

* * *

They fed them first. Sefa was pragmatic about most things, but food was doctrine: no decisions before bread. So Wren sat on a bench in the great hall with a bowl of stew — thyme, decisively — and let the warmth soak into her bones while the hall slowly refilled with its noise, and tried to remember the last time she'd eaten sitting down without counting the exits.

Three exits, she noted. Two tunnels, one chimney flue, load-bearing pillars at intervals of—

"Wren." Tamsin nudged her. "Eat."

"I am eating."

"You're eating and *surveying*."

"Habit," she said, and then, because the stew was good and the hall was warm and something in her chest had gone loose and dangerous, she added: "Master Corvin used to say a cartographer eats with her boots on. In case the map changes under her."

Around them, the Emberhold went about its evening. A girl of maybe nine sat nearby practicing letters by scratching them into a slate with a fingertip that glowed faintly gold — glowing slate, ordinary chalk, the letters shining like embers. Nobody paid her any attention. A boy two benches down was having an argument conducted entirely in whispers with a moth the size of Wren's hand, and losing. An elderly woman mended a net that appeared to be mending itself slightly faster than she could work.

Wren watched all of it and felt something crack quietly in her chest.

This was what she'd imagined, on the bad nights. Not glory, not rescue — just *this*. A hall where a glowing child was a child, not kindling. She had told herself the Emberhold was probably a myth, because hoping otherwise hurt too much, and now here it was smelling of thyme, and hope turned out to feel less like triumph and more like grief arriving early.

"You're crying into the stew," Tamsin observed.

"I'm *seasoning* it."

"Ah," he said gravely. "Like the thyme question."

She laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh — and Sefa chose that moment to sit down across from them with her own bowl, which apparently concluded the welcoming ceremony.

"Here's how it works," Sefa said, without preamble. "Everyone works. Everyone learns. Nobody leaves the passes without my word, because the hunters have instruments that taste gift-work like sharks taste blood, and every fool who wanders off to 'just check on something' brings them a mile closer to four hundred people." She pointed her spoon at Tamsin. "Especially you."

"Me?"

"You especially. Your gift isn't quiet, Dravenmoor. Quiet gifts are a fever-cold, a dream that comes true, a lock that opens sad. Yours builds *bridges*. Bridges are visible from ridgelines. Every drawing you make is a flare." She leaned forward. "So rule one: nothing gets drawn inside the Emberhold. Nothing. You feel the itch, you come find me, we walk out to the scree fields, and you draw rocks. Boring rocks. Rocks nobody will ever cross a mountain to inspect. Understood?"

Tamsin looked, for a moment, like a man being told the sea was off-limits. But he nodded. "Understood."

"Rule two: you tell me everything about the valley. How it hides. What the boundary stones do. Whether others like you are still in there." Her voice flattened. "Because if the crown ever cracks that valley, it won't be a refuge anymore. It'll be a quarry."

The warmth in Wren's chest cooled several degrees. She saw Tamsin arrive at the same place — the understanding that the Emberhold's welcome had a shape, and the shape was *useful*.

"And me?" Wren asked.

Sefa turned to her, and for the first time something almost gentle crossed the scarred face. "Corvin's girl gets a desk, dry ink, and whatever's left of our records. We've got maps coming out of our ears and nobody trained to read them. Congratulations. You're the most educated person in a mountain."

It shouldn't have made Wren's throat tight. It did anyway.

* * *

They gave Tamsin a cell with a bed and a view of solid rock, and by the third morning the whole refuge had formed its opinions of him, the way small places do.

The opinions were not unanimous.

Half of them adored him. The children adored him most of all — he'd started teaching letters and surveying to a cluster of them in the afternoons, using a stick and the dusty floor of a side gallery, and the children had decided that anyone who could make a mountain explain itself was the finest entertainment since the moth boy. The old net-mender declared him "polite, which is rarer than magic." Even the fiddler had written a song, mostly about the bridge and the heron, which was inaccurate but affectionate.

The other half watched him.

Wren noticed it at meals — the way conversations dipped when Tamsin sat down, the way certain faces arranged themselves into careful blankness. She caught fragments. *Valley-born.* *Never burned for it.* *Where was he when the Guildhall went up? Where was any of them?* And once, sharper, from a gaunt man with frost-white eyes who had been smuggled out of a border town in a grain cart: "Easy to be gentle, growing up where the edicts never reached."

Tamsin heard it too. Wren watched him hear it — watched him set down his spoon, open his mouth, close it again, and finally say, mildly, "That's true. It did never reach us. I used to think that meant we were safe. Now I think it meant we were hidden, and I'm still working out who hid us, and why, and what it cost everyone outside." He picked his spoon back up. "I'm sorry. It's a poor answer. It's the only honest one I have."

The gaunt man stared at him a long moment, then grunted, which Wren would later learn was roughly a medal.

But the distrust didn't vanish; it just went underground, like water through limestone. And Wren, who had survived the Guild purge by learning to read rooms faster than people read her, understood something she didn't say aloud: belonging here was real, and it was rationed. The Emberhold loved generously and trusted carefully, and Tamsin — valley-born, gift-loud, useful beyond measure — sat precisely on the fault line between the two.

He took it better than she expected. One evening, on the gallery ledge where the mountain opened onto a shelf of night sky, she found him sketching — nothing, just lines, no intention in them, the way a musician runs scales — and she sat down beside him and said, "They'll come around."

"Some of them already have."

"Some of them want to use you and some of them resent you and both halves are right," Wren said. "Welcome to being a person other people need. It doesn't get more comfortable. You just get better shoes."

Tamsin laughed — really laughed, head tipped back toward the stars — and for a moment the fault line didn't matter, and the mountain was just warm, and Wren let herself believe, briefly, recklessly, that they might actually be safe.

Which was, of course, when Sefa found them.

She came up the gallery stairs fast, no lamp, which was itself an alarm — Sefa moved like a lit lamp only when things were calm. Her face in the starlight was grim as a closed gate.

"Maproom," she said. "Both of you. Now."

"What is it?" Wren was already on her feet.

"A courier got through tonight. Half-dead, like you were." Sefa's jaw tightened. "He brought papers out of the capital. Survey schedules. Deployment orders." She paused, and Wren watched her decide to say the rest plainly, because Sefa always did. "The crown isn't hunting the gifted blind, Wren. They're hunting by map. Region by region, grid by grid, someone in that palace is charting every gift-work signature in the kingdom — and crossing them off."

The cold went through Wren like well-water.

"There's more," Sefa said. "The courier saw the survey charts. He said the hand that drew them was familiar. Guild-trained. Master-level." Her eyes settled on Wren, steady and terrible. "He said it looked like Corvin's work."

"*Corvin's dead*," Wren said. "I watched the Guildhall burn. I carried his last map out of the fire. He—"

"I know what you carried," Sefa said quietly. "That's why you need to see this. Because either the dead are drawing for the crown—" she turned toward the stairs, "—or somebody learned to draw just like him. Come on. Both of you. Bring your steady hands."

Below them, the Emberhold glowed and murmured, four hundred lives stacked warm in the dark of the mountain. Above them, the stars wheeled on, indifferent as ink.

Wren followed Sefa down, and the fragile belonging in her chest pulled tight as a drawn line — the first line, she would think later, of a new map. One she hadn't drawn yet.

One that was already being drawn against them.
