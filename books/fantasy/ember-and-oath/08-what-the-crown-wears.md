## 8. What the Crown Wears

Sefa Emberhale had learned long ago that the difference between a rumor and intelligence was about three days and a great deal of running.

The rumor arrived on a Tuesday, in the mouth of a tinker named Odo who smelled of tin and panic. The intelligence arrived three days later, in the mouth of a court defector who smelled of velvet and worse panic, and who collapsed in the Emberhold's entry tunnel because he had never once in his life walked downhill for six hours.

"Get him up," Sefa said, crouching beside the man. "Gently. He's worth more conscious."

The defector — Lord Perrin Vasse, formerly Undersecretary of the Royal Seal, a title so ornate it sounded like an apology — was hauled to his feet by two of her people and deposited in the map room, which was the only room in the Emberhold large enough to hold both a table and an argument.

Sefa followed, drying her hands on her apron out of habit. She'd been rendering fat for candles when Odo's runner came down the mountain. Leadership, she had found, was mostly deciding which fires to tend literally and which figuratively, and today the literal ones could wait.

Lord Vasse sat in the offered chair like a man sitting on a horse for the first time: correctly, painfully, and with the constant expectation of disaster. He was perhaps fifty, soft at the middle, with the kind of face that had spent decades agreeing with people more powerful than itself. His hands, though — his hands were steady, folded neatly on his knee, and Sefa trusted hands more than faces.

"You're the one they call Sefa," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm the one who runs this hole in the mountain," she said. "Which makes me whatever you need me to be. Talk."

He talked.

* * *

Sefa listened the way she did everything: completely, quietly, and while doing something else with her hands. She sat by the table and sharpened a pencil that didn't need sharpening, because Lord Vasse spoke better to someone not staring at him, and because a sharp pencil is a comfort in uncertain times.

"The edict," Vasse said, "was read from the palace balcony on the first of Thawmonth. I stood in the courtyard below. I have heard King Aldric speak perhaps four hundred times in my career. Investitures, declarations of war, the opening of the granary courts. A king's voice is a tool, Mistress Sefa, and I know this particular tool the way a carpenter knows his own hammer." He paused. "That was not his hammer."

"Meaning what? An impostor?"

"No. The face was his. The height, the hands, the grey coming in at the temples — all his. But the voice was wrong. Too smooth. Aldric has a rasp; he took an arrow through the throat at Redford Bridge twenty years ago and never fully healed. Every herald in the capital knows the rasp. That day there was no rasp. And the words—" Vasse's composure flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind. "Aldric was never eloquent. He was a soldier-king. Plain speech, plain oaths. The man on the balcony spoke for eleven minutes without drawing breath, and every sentence was beautiful, and every sentence made my skin crawl. *The gifted are a rot in the root of the realm.* Eleven minutes. Then he smiled at the crowd, and it was a smile I had seen before, but never on him."

"Where had you seen it?"

Vasse looked at her hands, then at her face, as if deciding how much truth a rebel leader could carry. "On a cat," he said. "Watching a bird it couldn't reach yet."

Nobody laughed. Sefa appreciated that. In her experience the moments you most needed laughter were the moments it would kill you.

"And since then?" she asked.

"Since then the palace has changed. Not the stones — the *air*. Servants walk softly and are dismissed without cause. Three chamberlains in two months. The king holds audiences, but no one meets his eyes, because those who do come away gray-faced and won't say why. One of them — a maid, Maret, sweet girl — told me the king thanked her for her service and then said, in a voice like hers, in *her own voice*, 'You will be needed soon.' She fled that night. I fled the week after, when the survey office began requisitioning maps of the border provinces. All of them. Every chart, every county roll, going up to the sealed wing where no one is permitted." He swallowed. "Mistress Sefa, I served the crown for thirty years. I am telling you the thing on that throne wears Aldric like a coat. And I believe it is getting warmer inside the coat."

* * *

Afterward, Sefa stood alone in the map room for a long moment, letting the shape of it settle over her the way snow settles over a roof — silently, and with the eventual promise of collapse.

She had built the Emberhold on pragmatism. That was her reputation and she wore it like armor: Sefa Emberhale, who counted stores twice, who rationed hope along with flour, who had told more than one gifted child that magic wouldn't save them but soup might. She did not deal in monsters. Monsters were what frightened people invented when the arithmetic got too hard.

But the arithmetic here was simple, and it pointed at something with teeth.

The hunt was organized. That was the part that had been keeping her up at night even before Vasse arrived. Burnings were old hatred, and old hatred was sloppy — mobs, torches, luck. This was different. The purges moved province to province in an order that made no sense by population or by guild strength, but made perfect sense if you laid them out on a map, which Sefa had done, which was why the map room's wall now bore a chart of the kingdom studded with black pins, and why the pins traced a spiral. Slow, patient, tightening. Someone was sweeping the country the way you'd sweep a floor. Starting at the edges. Working inward.

And now: a voice that wasn't the king's. A survey office feeding charts into a sealed wing. A smile off a cat.

She went to find the dragon.

* * *

Vaelith kept to the high caverns, past the hot springs, where the rock was veined with something that glowed faintly gold and warm as bread. You always knew you were getting close because the air started to smell of woodsmoke and, oddly, of libraries.

Sefa climbed the last stair and found the dragon coiled around the cavern's central pillar with her eyes closed, looking less like the last of her kind and more like an enormous cat who had decided the sun was adequate. Her scales were the color of cooling embers — deep red shot through with grey, more grey every month, Sefa suspected, though she'd stopped counting. Dying fire had a smell. So did dying dragons. Both were in the air tonight.

"You've brought news," Vaelith said without opening her eyes. "I can tell because your footsteps have opinions."

"My footsteps always have opinions."

"Yes, but usually they keep them to themselves." One amber eye opened, ancient and amused. "Sit, spymistress. Or stand and pace, if pacing helps. It does help some people. It does not help me, but I have learned to tolerate the habits of short-lived things."

Sefa paced. She gave Vasse's testimony whole, sparing nothing — the missing rasp, the eleven minutes, the maid and the borrowed voice, the sealed wing full of maps. When she finished, the dragon was silent long enough that the sound of the hot springs filled the cavern, patient as breathing.

"You already know something," Sefa said. "Don't you. You've known since the mountains."

"I have known since before your grandmother's grandmother was a disappointment to her mother," Vaelith said. "Though knowing a thing and being able to prove it are different animals, and being believed is a third animal entirely, usually dead." She uncoiled slightly, and the glow of the veins in the rock caught her scales so that for a moment she seemed lit from within — a memory of fire rather than its reality. "Tell me, Sefa Emberhale. What do your people say a broken oath becomes?"

"A broken oath?" Sefa frowned. "Becomes shame. Becomes war, sometimes. Becomes—"

"In your tongue, in your small human categories, yes. Shame, war, regret. But oaths are not empty things. An oath spoken truly is a rope thrown across a chasm. It holds weight. And when the one who swore it breaks it — when the weight comes down and the rope snaps — the rope does not simply vanish. It frays. It hangs in the air between what was promised and what was done. And given enough power behind the breaking, enough grief, enough *will*—" The amber eye fixed on her. "It can pull itself together. Into a shape. Into a purpose. Into a thing that exists only to finish what was abandoned. To complete the vow by force, since it cannot be completed by faith."

The cavern felt colder than it had a moment ago, though Sefa knew that was nonsense; the springs kept these halls warm year-round.

"You're saying the king broke an oath," she said slowly, "and the broken pieces of it got up and started walking around wearing him."

"I am saying something older and worse than that, and I will say it plainly because you are a woman who prefers plainly." Vaelith rose, unfolding by degrees, until her head was near the ceiling and her shadow covered Sefa entire. Up close, the dragon's eyes held a sorrow so old it had worn smooth, like a stone in a river. "Twenty-two years ago, I swore an oath with Prince Aldric of the realm, in this very range, with fire and blood and the old words. It was a vow of protection — his line to the land, the land to his line, dominion held in trust. Stewardship, we called it then, before the word fell out of fashion among kings. And then Aldric came to the throne, and the crown came with counselors, and counselors came with wars, and somewhere in the years of smoke he did the one thing the oath forbade above all others." Her voice dropped, and it rumbled through Sefa's ribs. "He used the gift-taken children. The ones the guilds found and the crown collected. He fed their gift into the realm's defenses. Broke the vow to protect the kingdom, which is the way all such vows break — not with villains, but with reasons."

Sefa thought of the pyres. Of the lists. Of the children marched in files with their wrists bound, and the heralds calling it law. Something in her chest went very quiet and very hard.

"So the thing on the throne," she said, "is his broken word."

"It is his word, walking. An echo given flesh by the size of what was shattered. It wants only one thing, because it was made of only one thing: completion. Finish the oath. Fulfill the vow. And because the vow was sworn in blood and fire, it interprets fulfillment the old way — purge the gifted, gather the gift, seal the realm, make the promise true by making everything else false." The dragon lowered her head, and her breath stirred Sefa's hair, warm and smelling of ash. "It is not evil, spymistress. That is the worst of it. Evil can be reasoned with, sometimes, or at least surprised. It is a *promise*. And promises are the most stubborn creatures in creation."

"How do we kill a promise?"

"You don't." Vaelith settled back onto the warm stone, folding herself away again, smaller and older. "You unmake it. You return it to the mouth that spoke it. But that—" and here the great eye closed again, and something in the set of her jaw, if dragons have jaws that set, told Sefa the rest before the words did, "that is a conversation for another night, and it will cost more than either of us wishes to pay."

* * *

Sefa went back down through the tunnels with the lantern held high and her mind moving faster than her feet.

In the common hall, the evening meal was underway — forty-odd fugitives at long tables, the clatter of spoons, someone's terrible attempt at a song being shouted down by general acclamation. She paused at the doorway and let herself look at them, really look, the way she did on nights when the news was bad. The glasswright whose hands shook unless she was working. The twins who lit the lamps by pointing at them. Old Master Corvin, who could hear rain three provinces off and complained about everyone's weather. Tamsin Dravenmoor sat near the end with Wren, sketching on the tabletop with a finger in spilled ale, and Wren was laughing at whatever he'd drawn, and for a moment the boy looked like a person instead of a survivor.

*This,* Sefa thought, *is what the thing on the throne is spiraling toward. Pin by pin.*

"Tamsin," she said, before she could decide against it. "Wren. A word."

They followed her to the map room, where the black pins caught the lamplight like a swarm of frozen flies. Tamsin looked at the wall chart the way he looked at everything — hungrily, professionally, with the total absorption of a man reading a letter addressed to him personally.

"The spiral," he said quietly. "I saw it yesterday. I didn't want to say anything."

"Why not?"

"Because maps don't lie, but people lie to themselves about maps all the time." He touched the outermost pin, gently, the way you'd touch a scar. "This isn't a search pattern. Searches wander. This is a *drawing*. Whoever's making it knows exactly what they're doing, and what they're doing is tracing something. I just can't tell what yet."

*A circle,* Sefa thought, remembering the dragon's words. *A rope pulled tight.* But she said only: "Then keep looking. Both of you. The court defector says the survey office is feeding charts to the palace — every map of every province. If the enemy thinks in cartography, then cartography is our weapon too, and you two are the best I have."

Tamsin blinked at her. "We're the only two you have."

"That's what 'best' means in wartime," Sefa said, and was rewarded — barely — by Wren's crooked half-smile. Gentle humor, she'd found, was like salt: cheap, and it made everything else go further.

She left them to the pins and the lamplight and climbed back toward her own narrow bunk, and it was only halfway up the stairs that she admitted what was actually bothering her — the thing under the intelligence like a stone under a floorboard.

Vaelith had said *you return it to the mouth that spoke it.* And the dragon had flinched. Dragons, Sefa suspected, did not flinch at the cost to others. They flinched at the cost to themselves.

Whatever the unbinding required, Vaelith intended to pay it personally, and soon.

Down in the map room, faint through the rock, she could hear Tamsin's voice, low and certain, explaining the spiral to Wren. And beneath it, so faint she might have imagined it — though she was quite sure she didn't — the sound of the mountain itself humming, like a rope beginning, very slowly, to sing.
