## 9. First Blood on the Map

The morning the Emberhold burned began, as so many disasters do, with something small and domestic: Sefa arguing with her brother about porridge.

"You've taken the last of the honey again," Dain said, holding up his bowl like evidence at a trial.

"I have not *taken* it," Sefa said, spooning another generous dollop into her own bowl without a flicker of shame. "I have *redistributed* it. That's what leaders do."

"That's what thieves do."

"Leaders and thieves differ only in paperwork." She glanced over at Tamsin and Wren, who sat on the long bench by the hearth with their own bowls, and winked. "Ask your mapmaker there. He'll know. Half of cartography is deciding which things to leave off the page."

Tamsin smiled into his porridge, because it was true, and because for eleven days now he had been almost — *almost* — happy.

That was the thing about the Emberhold that unsettled him most, looking back. Not the hidden doors behind the waterfall, or the lanterns that burned without oil, or the woman who could coax tomatoes out of stone in midwinter. It was how quickly it had felt like home. Eleven days, and he already knew which floorboards creaked, which of the children snuck down to the kitchens after lights-out, where Wren liked to sit to read with her bad leg stretched toward the fire. Eleven days, and the word *fugitive* had started to feel less like a sentence and more like a description of everyone, together, in one warm room.

He should have known better. He was, after all, a professional student of places. He knew that every place has an edge, and that edges are where things get in.

* * *

It was Wren who found the chart.

She came limping into the map room — a converted grain store at the eastern end of the hold, all candle-smoke and paper smell, his favorite room in the world — carrying a rolled parchment the way you carry a dead bird. Carefully. Like it might still mean something.

"Tamsin." Her voice was flat in a way he'd learned to dread. "You need to look at this."

"What is it?"

"It was in the supply cache. The one near the mill ruins. Sefa sent me to check it because I'm quiet and she says I have 'a suspicious face,' which I've decided to take as a compliment." She laid the roll on his table and unbound the cord with fingers that were not quite steady. "There was a body nearby. Crown livery. He'd been dead two days, maybe three. Cold enough that the crows hadn't—"

She stopped herself. Unrolled the chart.

Tamsin looked down at it, and the bottom fell out of the morning.

He knew the hand immediately. Of course he did. It was his own.

It was a survey of the Ashreach foothills — the passes, the watercourses, the old shepherd roads — drawn in the careful, disciplined style Master Ellery had beaten into him back when Tamsin still believed maps were just maps. He'd drawn it in the first days out of the valley, before the road-that-wasn't-there, before Vaelith, before any of it. A practice piece. A way of keeping his hands busy while Wren slept off her fever in a ditch-side barn. He remembered drawing it. He remembered being proud of it.

And there, in the lower right corner, where a proper cartographer signs his work, someone had written in a neat official hand:

*Property of the Royal Survey. Recovered from fugitive T. Dravenmoor. Route intelligence appended.*

Below that, in red ink, someone else had added annotations. A trail of small crosses marched across the foothills like wounds — search patterns, sweep lines, dates. And each cross converged, step by patient step, on a single point in the mountains.

A point marked with a tiny, precise symbol.

A waterfall.

"They found my chart," Tamsin said. His voice sounded very far away. "I lost it when we — when the hunters nearly had us at the ford. I thought it went in the river."

"It did go in the river," Wren said quietly. "Someone fished it out. Someone who knew exactly what they were holding." She tapped the red crosses. "This isn't a patrol map, Tamsin. This is a hunt. Look at the dates. They've been walking your drawing for three weeks. Every pass you drew, they've walked. Every stream. You didn't just make them a picture of these mountains. You made them a *key*."

Tamsin stared at the waterfall symbol until it blurred.

Because here was the thing — the terrible, unspeakable thing — and it was this: he had drawn those mountains beautifully. He had drawn them *well*. Every contour true, every distance honest, every shortcut lovingly rendered, because that was what he did, that was the only thing he had ever been able to do, and he had done it *so well* that a stranger could take his paper and use it to find the one place in the world where people like Wren had managed to hide.

His gift didn't just make roads appear. Apparently it also made doors open. For anyone. In either direction.

"We have to tell Sefa," he said.

"Yes."

"Now."

"Yes," Wren said again, and caught his sleeve as he turned. "Tamsin. Whatever happens next — it isn't your fault."

"That's a very kind lie," he said, "and I'd thank you not to waste it on me twice."

* * *

Sefa took the news the way she took everything: standing up, arms crossed, jaw set like a fortification.

"How long?" she asked.

"The sweep line reached the eastern ridge four days ago," Tamsin said. "If they followed the watercourse the way I drew it — and they would, it's the fastest route, I made sure of that —"

"Then they're close." Sefa was already moving, snapping orders across the great hall. "Rouse everyone. Pack light, nothing that can't be carried in ten minutes. Torv, get the children up and dressed. Maren, the tunnel under the north wall — check it's clear. We drill for this. We *drill* for this." She turned back to Tamsin, and for a moment the commander's mask slipped, and underneath it was just a tired woman with ash-colored eyes. "How many exits does your chart show them?"

"All of them," Tamsin said. "I drew the whole approach. Waterfall, both cliff paths, the goat track behind the ridge. I drew it *accurately*, Sefa. That was the whole problem with me before it was the whole problem with the kingdom."

"Then we don't use any of them." She said it simply, like arithmetic. "We use the tunnel. The one thing you never saw, because I never showed you." And then, seeing his face: "Don't. Don't do that thing where you swallow yourself. There'll be time for guilt later, if we live to schedule it. Right now I need your hands and your head, not your penance. Can you draw us a delay? Something between us and them?"

Tamsin's stomach turned over. "I — yes. I think so. But Sefa, every time I draw something big, it's loud. The road, the river — Vaelith says the land remembers it. If they have anyone gifted with them, they'll feel it like a shout."

"Then make it quiet," Sefa said. "Make it small. You're the cleverest man in this room, Dravenmoor, and this room contains a woman who grows tomatoes out of rock. Figure it out."

So he sat down at his table in the middle of an evacuation, with the Emberhold's people streaming past him with bundles and frightened children, and he drew.

Small, he told himself. Quiet. He drew a rockfall — not a wall, not a fortress, just a slope giving way, a natural thing, the kind of thing mountains did on their own sometimes. He drew it across the goat track, two miles east, timed to nothing in particular. A pebble's worth of magic. A cough, not a scream.

The pencil moved. The paper warmed under his hand the way it always did now, faintly, like a sleeping animal. Somewhere two miles east, stone shifted against stone with a sound like a long sigh.

"There," he said, and hated how much better that made him feel, and hated himself for hating it.

Dain clapped him on the shoulder as he passed with an armload of blankets. "Good man. Rockfall. Very tidy." He grinned — Dain grinned at everything, that was his whole personality, a big sunburned grin and hands that were always fixing something. "When this is over, you're drawing me a house. Big kitchen. South-facing."

"Dain," Sefa called, "stop flirting with the mapmaker and get the wagons moving."

"I'm not flirting, I'm commissioning!" But he went, laughing, weaving through the crowd, pausing to swing a small girl onto his shoulders because that was Dain too, always, everyone's brother, the Emberhold's loudest and warmest heartbeat —

Tamsin would remember that. Later, he would remember all of it, in perfect detail, because that was the other curse of being what he was: he never forgot a line he'd drawn, or a person, or the exact angle of the light at the moment everything broke.

* * *

They came an hour before dusk, and they did not come by the goat track.

Later — much later, in the ashes, piecing it together — Tamsin understood. The rockfall had worked. It had slowed them exactly as designed. But the hunters had a chart too, and charts show more than one road, and the men who carried his drawing had walked every line of it until they knew his mountains better than he did. While he'd spent his magic on the small quiet delay, they had simply gone around. Through the cliff path. The one he'd drawn so helpfully, with such loving attention to gradient and footing.

The first thing through the waterfall was fire.

It came in a glass flask, arcing through the curtain of falling water, and burst against the granary wall, and the dry autumn timber went up like a struck match. Then another flask. Then shouting — boots, steel, the screams of people who had drilled for this day and discovered that drilling is not the same as believing.

"North tunnel! North tunnel, GO!" Sefa's voice cut through everything, iron and fury. "Maren, take the children! Torv, rear guard! Nobody fights if they can run!"

Tamsin ran. Everyone ran. The great hall filled with smoke; the lanterns that burned without oil guttered and died one by one, as if the hold itself were exhaling its last small magics. He found Wren in the crowd, grabbed her arm, kept her upright as they shoved through the press of bodies toward the north wall, toward the dark mouth of the tunnel Maren was holding open with both hands and a face like carved grief.

Behind them, the roof beams came down in the great hall.

And Dain — Dain was not behind them. Dain had gone *back*.

Tamsin saw it happen, the way you see everything in a disaster: in fragments, each one lit and separate. Dain emerging from the smoke with two children bundled under his arms, one on each hip, grinning even now, even through the ash on his teeth. Dain setting them down at the tunnel mouth, ruffling the girl's hair, turning back for the third child he could hear crying somewhere in the burning east wing. Sefa screaming his name — not *brother*, just his name, over and over, the way you call anything you cannot bear to lose. The east wing doorway collapsing inward in a bloom of orange.

The third child came out. Maren carried her, coughing, singed, alive.

Dain did not come out.

* * *

They stood in the cold outside the tunnel's far end, maybe forty of them where there had been ninety, watching the mountain glow orange against the night sky. The Emberhold had been built into the rock itself, and rock does not burn, but everything human inside it did, and the fire found the vents and chimneys and breathed out of the mountainside in a dozen places, so that the whole ridge seemed to be exhaling embers.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Sefa walked away from the group, up the slope a little way, and stood with her back to all of them, and Tamsin watched her shoulders and waited for them to shake, and they never did. That was somehow worse.

Wren found him sitting against a boulder, staring at his own hands.

In his right hand, still, absurdly, he held the pencil.

"You couldn't have known," Wren said.

"I drew them a map," Tamsin said. His voice came out strange and level, like someone else's. "Every door. Every path. I drew it well, Wren. I drew it the best I've ever drawn anything, because drawing well is the only good thing about me, and it turns out it was never good at all. It was just — accurate. Accuracy doesn't care whose side it's on." He looked up at the burning ridge. "Vaelith told me. The first day. She said the kingdom was being redrawn, and I thought she meant the echo-king. I thought I was the counterweight. The hero with the magic pencil." He laughed, and it came out broken. "I'm not the counterweight. I'm the ink. Anyone can dip a pen in me."

Wren lowered herself down beside him, bad leg first, the way she always did, and said nothing for a while. Below them, Sefa still stood facing the fire.

"She's going to hate me," Tamsin said.

"No," Wren said. "She's going to hate them. That's the difference between her and you — you turn the knife inward first. She'll aim it where it belongs." A pause. "Eventually."

"And what am I supposed to aim at? My own hands?" He flexed his fingers around the pencil. "I keep thinking — every person I've met since I left the valley, I've drawn something near them. The road. The mountains. The Emberhold's approaches. It's all in my hand somewhere, Wren. Everything they need to find everyone I love. What if there are other copies? What if the cache wasn't the only one? What if—" He stopped, because the thought arrived fully formed, cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts do.

The valley.

Master Ellery. The workshop. The lake he'd drawn that appeared overnight, the first crack in everything. The Hollow Valley, the one place magic couldn't follow — except that it could, because magic had lived there all along, curled up inside a boy with a pencil, and Tamsin had written down every path in and out of it a hundred times over. He'd mapped the valley the way other people memorized prayers.

"Oh no," he said aloud.

Wren went still. "What?"

But he was already on his feet, already digging through his satchel, already counting — the valley surveys, the boundary stones, the passes, the one road in, the one road out, all of it, years of it, meticulous, beautiful, *accurate* — and the fire on the mountain painted his hands orange as he counted, and the number he arrived at was the same every time.

All of it. He'd drawn all of it.

From the slope above, Sefa turned at last from the burning ridge. Her face was wet and her voice was not.

"Mapmaker," she said. "Get over here. We need to talk about what you're going to do next — and this time, you're going to tell me *everything* you've ever put on paper."
