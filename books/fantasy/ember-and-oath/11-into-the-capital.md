## 11. Into the Capital

The road to Corvane was paved with lies, and Wren had written most of them.

She'd spent three days at the Emberhold forging surveyor's credentials — the wax seals alone had taken two attempts and one small fire that Sefa still hadn't let her live down. Now she sat in a cart bed stacked with genuine-looking instruments: brass theodolites, measuring chains, rolled charts with official annotations in her best imitation of Guild script. Beside her, Tamsin wore a crown livery coat two sizes too big and kept tugging at the collar like it was trying to strangle him.

"Stop fidgeting," Wren murmured. "Surveyors don't squirm."

"Surveyors also don't usually feel like they're wearing a stolen skin." He plucked at the coat's silver-and-black braid. "This belonged to someone, Wren. Someone who probably got burned."

"It belonged to a man who deserted," Sefa had told them when she handed the coats over, her voice flat as slate. "He left the night his own sister was taken. If he's out there, I hope he's freezing without it." She'd looked at Tamsin then, and something in her face had softened by perhaps a single degree — which, from Sefa, was practically an embrace. "You're wearing his shame so his sister doesn't wear his fate. Make it mean something."

The gates of Corvane rose out of the morning haze like the jaw of some enormous patient beast. Wren had seen them before, as an apprentice — had walked through them a hundred times with her satchel of charts and her head full of contour lines and her whole future arranged like a well-drawn map. Back then the guards at the gate had been bored. Boredom, she was learning, was a luxury.

Now they checked papers. Now they checked faces against broadsheets pinned to a board, each one bearing a charcoal sketch and the word GIFTED stamped beneath in red.

Wren kept her eyes forward and her breathing even. Beside her, Tamsin had gone very still — the particular stillness of a man pretending very hard to be furniture.

"Business?" The guard was young, with the raw knuckles of someone new to the work.

"Boundary resurvey for the Office of Crown Lands," Wren said, and passed up the credentials. "The old surveys predate the river shift at Marlow's Bend. His Majesty requires accurate borders." She let a note of professional boredom creep into her voice. "You wouldn't believe the disputes a mislabeled stream causes."

The guard glanced at the seal. Glanced at Tamsin. Glanced back at the board of sketches — where, thank every merciful star, neither of their faces hung.

"River shifted, did it?"

"Rivers do that. It's rather their defining feature."

Something almost like a smile flickered across the young guard's face before duty smothered it. He waved them through. "Welcome to Corvane. Keep your instruments registered with the Survey Office within three days or you'll be fined."

"Of course," Wren said. "We wouldn't dream of being unregistered."

* * *

Inside the walls, the city had changed, and the change was written everywhere in the language Wren knew best.

They had redrawn it. That was the first thing she understood, riding the cart down the Processional toward the surveyors' quarter. New boundary stones stood at intersections where none had been before, each carved with the crowned sigil and a district number. Streets she remembered as tangled, organic, alive — the kind of streets that grew over centuries like root systems — had been straightened. Not physically. On paper. The city maps posted at every corner showed clean geometric districts, tidy grids imposed over the crooked truth, and people navigated by the paper version now, because the criers announced it, because the law required it, because the alternative was to be noticed.

"They've mapped the city into obedience," Tamsin said quietly beside her. He was staring at one of the posted charts with an expression Wren couldn't read — horror and recognition tangled together. "Look at the scale bars. Look at the notation style. That's Guild work. That's *our* work."

"I know."

"No, Wren, I mean—" He lowered his voice until it barely cleared the clop of the cart horse. "I mean those are corrections made by someone who learned the same conventions I did. The same hand habits. Whoever drew these didn't just study cartography. They loved it once. You can tell. You can always tell when a map was made with love, even when it's being used for this."

Wren said nothing, because her throat had closed. She had loved the Guild. She had loved the smell of fresh vellum and the way Master Ellery used to hum when he inked coastlines. And she had run from the smoke of its burning while her teachers —

Don't. Not here. Not on an open street with red-stamped broadsheets fluttering on every post.

They found lodging above a chandler's shop in the surveyors' quarter, paid for with forged coin that Wren had aged convincingly in a pouch of ash. From the window, if you leaned out and looked east past the rooftops, you could see the pyre grounds.

Wren made herself not look. Then made herself look, because not looking felt like a betrayal of everyone whose names she'd memorized off the Guild's death lists.

* * *

The Survey Office itself was a cathedral of bureaucracy, all vaulted ceilings and echoing footsteps and clerks who moved like clockwork. Wren presented their credentials at the registration desk with her heart hammering a rhythm entirely unsuited to her bored expression.

The clerk — a thin woman with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on her nose — examined the seals for a long, long moment.

"Marlow's Bend resurvey," she said. "That assignment was issued six weeks ago. Where have you been?"

"Waiting out the spring floods," Wren said smoothly. "No point surveying a riverbed that's underwater."

"Hm." The clerk stamped the papers. "You'll report findings within the month. And be advised—" her voice dropped, became procedural, deadened, "—in accordance with the Edict of Purity, all survey data is subject to review by the Office of Loyalty. Any anomalies in population distribution are to be flagged. Unregistered settlements. Unlicensed gatherings. Persons exhibiting irregular characteristics."

"Irregular characteristics," Tamsin repeated carefully.

"The edict uses the term 'gifted,'" the clerk said, not looking up. "The forms use 'irregular.' It's less alarming on official documents." She slid a ledger across the desk. "Sign here. And here. Initial there."

Wren signed. Her forgery hand held steady, though inside she was screaming. *Anomalies in population distribution.* They were using surveyors — her profession, her calling, the thing she'd given her whole life to — as hunting hounds. Every map drawn under crown commission was now a net.

And then, as they turned to leave, the clerk added, almost as an afterthought:

"You've arrived at an eventful time. There's a procession tomorrow. The Office calls it a Purification Convoy." Her pen scratched on. "Surveyors are requested to document it. Attendance is compulsory for crown personnel." She finally looked up, and behind the spectacles her eyes were red-rimmed and utterly empty, the eyes of someone who had filed too many forms about terrible things. "You'll want the eastern viewing stand. Bring your sketchbooks."

* * *

They brought their sketchbooks.

Wren stood at the eastern viewing stand the next morning between two junior surveyors who chatted amiably about grain tariffs, and she watched the convoy come down the Processional, and she understood with total clarity why the Office wanted it documented.

It wasn't a procession. It was an inventory.

Twenty-three children, ranging in age from what looked like seven to perhaps fourteen, marched in rows between mounted guards. Their wrists were bound with cord. Their mouths were bound with cloth — no, Wren realized with a fresh wave of nausea, not cloth. Wax-sealed gags, official issue, stamped with the crown sigil. Because gifted children might speak words that worked, and the edict accounted for that.

The crowd along the Processional was silent. Not hostile — silent, in the specific way of people watching something they cannot stop and cannot bear and have decided to survive by not seeing. A woman near the front pressed her hand flat against her mouth. A man turned his face to a shop window and studied hats with desperate intensity.

At the head of the column rode a banner-bearer carrying the new standard — the crowned sigil, but beneath it, stitched in thread that seemed to drink the light, a second emblem Wren didn't recognize. A circle bisected by a line. An oath-mark.

Tamsin's hand found hers on the railing. His grip was ice.

"That mark," he breathed. "Vaelith described it. The oath-echo's sigil. It's on the standard, Wren. It's flying over the king's own city."

The children passed directly below the stand. One of them — a girl, small, dark-haired, with a smudge of soot on her cheek that looked like it came from burning her own drawings, her own maps, her own little works of impossible art — looked up. Straight up. At Wren.

Her eyes were the color of deep water, and they were not afraid. That was the worst part. They were not afraid; they were *furious*, and beneath the fury, buried deep, was a question: *Where is everyone? Why is no one stopping this?*

Wren's sketchbook was open in her hands. Compulsory documentation. Her pencil moved without her permission, recording the column, the guards, the wax gags — and then, in the margin, tiny, hidden beneath her thumb, she drew the girl's eyes. Just the eyes. So that somewhere, on some page, someone would know they had been furious and unafraid and asking.

The convoy turned toward the pyre grounds. The crowd began, cautiously, to breathe again.

One of the junior surveyors beside them sighed and said, "Terrible business, but the law's the law," in the tone of a man discussing weather, and Wren had to walk away immediately or do something that would get them both killed.

* * *

She found Tamsin at the lodgings, sitting on the floor with his back against the bed, his surveyor's coat pooled around him like shed skin. He was holding one of his own maps — an old one, drawn in the valley, a careful rendering of the lake that had appeared exactly as he drew it, the map that had started everything.

"I keep thinking about what I'd have done," he said, without looking up, "if I'd grown up here. If the valley hadn't hidden me. Would I have been on that road today? Would my mentor have had to watch me march past some viewing stand while clerks documented it?"

"You'd have escaped," Wren said. "You're too stubborn to burn quietly."

"Wren." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and hard at once. "There were twenty-three of them. Children. And the whole city watched, and the law *made* them watch, made it compulsory, made documentation of it a job requirement — and everyone will go home tonight and eat supper and sleep, because it was legal. Every step of it was legal. Somebody *drafted* those gag orders. Somebody designed the convoy route. Somebody chose the viewing stands." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever heard from him. "They're using my gift's cousins to build the cages, Wren. Maps and laws. The two things people trust most, because both of them promise to tell the truth."

Wren sat down beside him on the cold floorboards. For a while neither spoke. Below them, the chandler's shop went about its evening; the smell of tallow drifted up the stairs, ordinary and warm and obscene in its ordinariness.

"When I was an apprentice," she said finally, "Master Ellery told me that a map is never neutral. Every map serves somebody. He said our job was to make sure we knew who, and to be able to answer for it." She took the old valley map gently from Tamsin's hands and smoothed it flat between them. "The echo-king knows that. Better than we did. He's weaponized the truth-telling. But that means—" she tapped the parchment, "—it can be turned back. Every net is also a web, Tamsin. Every route they've mapped, every district they've numbered, every convoy schedule — it's all written down. And anything written down can be read. By us."

Tamsin stared at her. Slowly, the horror in his face rearranged itself into something sharper. Something with edges.

"The convoy schedules," he said.

"Filed at the Survey Office. I saw the archive room when we registered. Third floor, west wing." She allowed herself a thin smile. "And surveyors have legitimate access to convoy documentation. We're supposed to be documenting them, after all. Compulsory attendance cuts both ways."

"We could find out where the next convoy goes. When. How many guards."

"We could find out everything." Wren folded the old map and pressed it back into his hands. "Sefa's people need routes into the capital. The network needs warning of raids before they happen — and every raid is preceded by a survey flag, an anomaly report. We're standing inside the machine, Tamsin. We're holding its pens."

He laughed then — a real laugh, startled out of him, tired and fierce. "When did you become the dangerous one?"

"I was always the dangerous one. You were the one who made roads appear out of desperation. I just forge paperwork." She stood and pulled him up. "Come on. We have legitimate access to an archive full of the enemy's secrets, and a deadline of one month before anyone wonders why our riverbed survey is taking so long."

They worked by candlelight that night, drafting a plausible resurvey of Marlow's Bend — real measurements, real notes, meticulous enough to survive inspection — while underneath it, in Wren's smallest, sharpest hand, they built the true map: guard rotations copied from posted rosters, convoy timetables glimpsed on clerks' desks, the geometry of the pyre grounds sketched from memory and rage.

It was nearly dawn when Tamsin set down his pen and flexed his cramping fingers.

"There," he said softly, looking at the hidden layer beneath the false one. "A map that serves somebody."

"The right somebody," Wren said.

But sleep didn't come easily, and when it finally took her, she dreamed of the girl with the soot-smudged cheek and the deep-water eyes. In the dream the girl wasn't marching. She was drawing — furiously, joyfully, on any surface she could find — and the drawings were coming true around her, flowers bursting through cobblestones, chains unraveling into songbirds, and the whole gray city waking up wondering what had happened to it.

Wren woke with the question still burning in her chest, the same question the girl's eyes had asked on the Processional:

*Why is no one stopping this?*

Soon, she promised the dream. Soon.

Two days later, copying rosters in the archive's third-floor reading room, Wren looked up from her desk and felt the blood leave her face.

Across the room, helping a clerk carry a stack of anomaly reports, was a woman she knew. Broad shoulders, ink-burned fingertips, a scar splitting her left eyebrow — a scar Wren herself had accidentally caused, ten years ago, with a carelessly swung compass arm.

Bree Tanner. Former guildmate. Fellow apprentice. The girl who'd shared her bread and her bunk and her dreams of mapping the northern coast.

Who had, according to the last whisper Wren heard before fleeing the Guild's burning, taken the crown's coin.

Bree looked up.

Their eyes met across the reading room, over a sea of anomaly reports and condemned children's names, and Wren watched recognition bloom on her old friend's face like fire catching dry paper.
