## 12. The King in the Mirror Room

The surveyor's badge itched.

Tamsin had worn a great many disguises in his life — most of them accidental, all of them involving aprons and ink stains — but he had never before worn a lie pinned to his chest in brass. *Crown Surveyor's Office, Third District.* The letters were stamped deep and official, and every time he caught sight of them reflected in a shop window he felt the urge to scrub his hands.

"You're scratching at it like it's a flea," Wren murmured beside him. She was dressed as a junior surveyor too, her burned hands hidden beneath long gloves that she'd insisted were regulation, though Tamsin suspected no regulation anywhere required gloves quite that elegant. "Stop it. You look guilty."

"I *am* guilty."

"Of impersonating a surveyor. Which is ironic, given that you're the only real surveyor either of us knows." She adjusted her collar with her chin, hands staying demurely folded the way they'd practiced. "Besides, we have papers. Forged ones. Very good forged ones."

"I made them," Tamsin said. "I know exactly how good they are. That's the problem. I also know the three mistakes I didn't have time to fix."

"The watermark is fine."

"The watermark is fine. The ink weight on the countersignature is wrong. Crown offices use a gall-and-oak blend; I used what Sefa had, which is walnut. Anyone who handles documents for a living will feel the difference under their thumb before they ever see it."

Wren considered this. "So don't let anyone touch them."

"That was my plan, yes."

"And your backup plan?"

Tamsin watched a pair of actual crown surveyors cross the plaza ahead of them, their satchels bouncing, arguing cheerfully about drainage easements. Ordinary men. Men with pensions, probably. He felt a sudden, absurd kinship with them and an even more absurd urge to ask about the easements.

"My backup plan," he said, "is the same as everyone else's in this city. Don't get caught."

* * *

Getting into the palace turned out to be insultingly easy, which was the first thing that frightened him.

Sefa's people had spent three weeks building the way in: a routine resurvey of the eastern terraces, ordered by a clerk who owed the network a favor, scheduled for the one afternoon when the terrace garrison changed shifts. The paperwork waved them past two checkpoints. A bored guard glanced at Tamsin's badge, then at his face, then visibly decided that whatever a surveyor was, it wasn't worth the effort of thinking about.

"Terrace work's pointless," the guard said, waving them through. "Whole east side's coming down next year anyway. King's orders."

"Coming down?" Wren asked, with the precise note of professional curiosity she'd rehearsed.

"Redrawing the grounds. Walls moved, gardens moved, the fountain going where the orangery is. Nobody knows why." The guard shrugged. "King wants what King wants."

Tamsin kept his face bland and his breathing even, but something cold slid down his spine and settled there, familiar as an old scar. Redrawing. Moving walls that had stood for two hundred years. Not building — *rearranging*, the way a child drags furniture around a dollhouse.

He'd seen that kind of change before. Every morning, in fact, in the first hour after he woke, when he checked the maps he'd drawn and waited to see which lines the world had obeyed overnight.

They crossed the eastern terraces, unrolled their instruments, took readings they didn't need from corners nobody cared about, and made themselves beautifully boring for two hours while the shift changed and the patrols thinned. Wren was better at boring than he was. She could stand in one spot measuring nothing with the patience of a heron, and once she even struck up a conversation with a passing gardener about rose blight that was so convincing Tamsin nearly believed she cared.

Then the bell rang the quarter hour, the last patrol rounded the far colonnade, and there was only the door.

It was set into the base of the old watchtower, iron-banded, unmarked except for a keyhole shaped like nothing Tamsin had ever picked. Sefa's people had gotten them the key — a real one, bought at a price Tamsin didn't want to think about, paid by someone whose name would never be written down. It fit as if the lock had been waiting for it.

Beyond the door, stairs went down.

"Palaces go *up*," Wren whispered. "Palaces are famous for going up. Towers, spires, balconies for dramatic speeches. Why does this one go down?"

"Because whatever's down here isn't for showing off," Tamsin said. He lit the small shuttered lantern they'd brought and held it low. The stairs were old — older than the palace above them, older than the dynasty, by the look of the stone. The steps were worn in the middle, hollowed by centuries of feet, and yet there was no dust. None at all. As though the stairs were swept daily by someone very thorough, or as though dust simply declined to settle here.

The air changed as they descended. Tamsin had spent twenty-six years in the Hollow Valley, the one place magic couldn't reach, and he had thought he knew what absence felt like — that flat, quiet nothing, like a room with the windows painted shut. This was the opposite. The air below the palace was *thick*. Every breath felt like drinking. His skin prickled the way it had the day he'd stepped over the valley's boundary stones and felt, for the first time, the vast humming attention of the world outside.

Except this wasn't the world's attention. This was something focused. Something with its attention fixed on a single point far below, the way a lens focuses light.

"Do you feel that?" Wren whispered.

"Yes."

"It feels like—" She stopped, searching. Her gloved hand found his sleeve. "Like being looked at. By something patient."

The stairs ended at a corridor lined with closed doors, each one sealed with lead poured into the hinges and locks — not locked, *sealed*, the way you'd seal a tomb or a plague house. And at the corridor's end, one door stood slightly ajar.

Not forced. Not broken. Simply left open, the way a host leaves a door open for an expected guest.

Tamsin stood in front of it for a long moment. "We can still turn around," he said, without meaning it, and knowing Wren knew he didn't mean it.

"We came to see the king," Wren said. "Or the thing wearing him. Whichever answers."

She pushed the door, and it swung inward without a sound, and they stepped into the mirror room.

* * *

He had expected grandeur. Palaces traded in grandeur the way harbors traded in fish. What he got instead was a round chamber, modest-sized, its walls paneled in dark wood gone silver with age, and mirrors.

Mirrors everywhere. Not ornamental ones — no gilt frames, no beveled edges, no vanity. These were plain, heavy plates of glass in plain heavy frames, mounted floor to ceiling, wall to wall, angled inward, so that wherever you stood in the room you were surrounded by dozens of reflections of yourself, each one slightly different, each one catching the lantern light at a different angle until the whole chamber seemed to flicker like a candle seen through water.

And in the center of the room, in a high-backed chair facing away from the door, sat a man.

"Aldric," Tamsin breathed, before he could stop himself.

The name fell into the silence and did not echo. The mirrors drank it.

The man didn't move. He sat very straight, the way kings sit in portraits, hands resting on the chair's arms — and Tamsin's eye, trained by a lifetime of reading terrain, read him the way he'd read a landscape, and what he saw made his stomach drop.

The man was *fading*. There was no better word for it. His left side was solid — a broad shoulder, a strong hand, a boot heel planted firmly on the stone. But his right side, the side nearest the far mirrors, was wrong. The hand on that armrest was gray as ash, and where the light touched it, it seemed almost translucent, as though you might see the chair through the palm. The edge of his jaw on that side had the soft, blurred quality of charcoal rubbed with a thumb. Half of him was a man. Half of him was the memory of a man, drawn by someone who was running out of ink.

And he was whispering.

Tamsin crept closer, lantern shuttered to a thread of light, Wren a step behind him with her breath held so carefully it barely moved her shoulders. The whisper resolved word by word, a low, cracked repetition, the sound of a man praying in a language he no longer believed in:

"—finish the oath. Finish the oath. Finish the—"

Up close, Tamsin could see the rest. The king's solid eye was open, fixed on nothing, red-rimmed and exhausted. His ash-gray eye was closed. His lips moved around the words like a man chewing gravel. There was a circlet on his brow — not the great crown of state, but a thin band, plain gold, and where it touched his skin the flesh was darkest, as though the metal were drinking from him slowly, sip by sip, year by year.

*Half-consumed,* Vaelith had said. *Her fire is the dam holding back the flood.*

Tamsin had imagined a monster. He had prepared himself for a monster — had rehearsed horror the way soldiers rehearse battle, grimly, in the dark. He had not prepared himself for this: a tired man in a chair, whispering to himself in a room full of mirrors, being eaten alive so gradually that the kingdom above mistook his consumption for policy.

Pity arrived in Tamsin's chest before fear could stop it, and pity was worse. Fear you could fight. Pity just sat there, warm and awful, refusing to leave.

"He's still in there," Tamsin whispered. "Wren. Whatever the echo is — he's still *in there*. Look at his face. That's not a monster's face. That's a man trying to remember why he started."

"Don't," Wren breathed. "Don't feel sorry for it. Sefa's brother is dead. The pyres are real. Whatever's left of him in there, it's driving the machine."

"I know." And he did know. But knowing didn't drain the pity out; it only mixed it with anger until he couldn't tell which feeling was which, and both of them together were heavier than either alone.

The whispering stopped.

Tamsin froze. Wren's hand closed on his arm hard enough to bruise.

In the mirrors, the reflections shifted. All of them. At once. Dozens of Aldrics turning their heads in perfect unison, the solid eye and the ash eye both opening now, both fixing — not on Wren, not on the door —

On Tamsin.

Every reflection smiled.

It was not a cruel smile. That was the horror of it. It was a *delighted* smile, warm and welcoming, the smile of a man greeting a guest he had been setting a place for. The ash-gray half of the king's face moved stiffly, the smile dragging the blurred cheek up like a curtain, and when he spoke, his voice was two voices braided together — the tired man's whisper underneath, and over it something smooth and resonant, something that sounded the way a signature looks:

"There you are."

Tamsin's whole body went cold. "Me?"

"The mapmaker." The king rose from the chair — slowly, joints cracking like green wood, the ash side moving a half-beat behind the solid side, out of sync with itself. He turned, and up close, in the flicker of forty mirrors, Tamsin could see that the gray half of his face wasn't featureless after all. There were lines in it. Faint, fine lines, like the strokes of a pen. Like *hatching*. Like something drawn rather than born, rendered in shades of graphite and grief.

"I've been waiting," the echo-king said, and the smile widened, gentle as an uncle's. "You took the long road. Through the mountains. Past the dragon." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "How is Vaelith? Still burning? Still *almost*?"

"Stay behind me," Tamsin said to Wren, and hated how thin his voice came out.

"She can't hurt me. Neither can you." The king spread his hands — one flesh, one ash — and the mirrors multiplied the gesture into a forest of open palms. "That's the tragedy of you, mapmaker. You could redraw these walls into a garden, the floor into a river, and it wouldn't touch me. I'm not *made* of places." The solid eye glittered. "I'm made of a promise. And promises are so much harder to unmake than stone."

"Then why am I here?" Tamsin asked. His heart was slamming, but some stubborn cartographer part of his mind was working even now, cataloguing, mapping — the angles of the mirrors, the position of the door, the exact shade of gray at the king's jaw. *If you can see it, you can draw it. If you can draw it—*

"Because I wanted to meet you before the bargain," the echo-king said simply. "Before the offer. Before the choice. I find choices go more smoothly when both parties know each other's faces." He tilted his head, and in forty mirrors, forty kings tilted their heads, and the tenderness in the motion was the worst thing Tamsin had ever seen. "You pity me. I can see it. Good. Hold onto that. You'll need it, when you understand what I'm going to ask of you."

Behind him, Wren made a small sound — and Tamsin realized, with a lurch, that in the nearest mirror, her reflection was wrong. Blurred at the edges. Faint lines crossing her face like hatching, like ink, like a figure being *sketched in*.

The echo-king followed his gaze and chuckled, low and warm.

"Ah. Yes. I should mention — the chart you drew. The one that was stolen from you." The smile again, patient, terrible. "Your blood was on it. Just a little. Enough. I've been studying you, mapmaker. Line by line. And I've begun to return the favor."

The mirrors flickered — and for one heartbeat, in every glass surface in the room, Tamsin saw not the chamber at all, but a map. A vast, exquisite, half-finished map, and at its center, rendered in fine gray strokes, a figure with his own face, surrounded by careful annotations in a hand he recognized as his own.

Then the mirrors showed the room again, and the king was standing very close now, close enough that Tamsin could smell ash and old paper, and the two-voiced whisper brushed his ear like a draft under a door:

"Go home, Tamsin Dravenmoor. Sleep. We'll speak again soon — you and I have so much to *draw* together."

The lantern guttered out.

When light returned — Wren's shaking hands striking flint, cursing in three dialects — the chair was empty, the whispering had resumed somewhere impossibly far away, and the door stood open onto a corridor that looked, somehow, longer than it had before.

Neither of them spoke until they were up the stairs and out under the open sky of the terraces, where the evening air tasted like freedom and roses and lie.

"Tamsin." Wren's voice was steady, which meant she was terrified; she only sounded calm when she was scared enough to need the armor. "What did it mean? *Studying you?*"

But Tamsin was looking at his own hands in the fading light, turning them over, checking the lines of his palms as though expecting to find hatching there too — and what he said was:

"Wren. Where's Sefa right now? Exactly. What survey office? What street?" His blood had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the evening. "Because if it has my chart, it doesn't just know where I've been. It knows everywhere I've *drawn*. And I drew the Emberhold. And I drew the capital cell. And I drew—" His voice cracked. "*Everyone.*"

Far across the city, a bell began to ring — not the quarter hour, but fast and repeated, the alarm pattern Sefa had drilled into them until they could hear it in their sleep.

Wren was already running.
