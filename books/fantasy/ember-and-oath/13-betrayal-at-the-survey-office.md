## 13. Betrayal at the Survey Office

Wren had learned three things about the capital in the four days since they'd slipped through its gates disguised as crown surveyors.

First: the city smelled like tallow and ambition in equal measure. Second: the bread was better than anything she'd eaten on the road, which felt like a personal insult given everything else going wrong. And third — the most important third — the Survey Office kept its records in a cellar that flooded every spring, which meant half the guild's old charts were water-stained, misfiled, or eaten by something the clerks called "the archive mouse" and Wren suspected was actually a very fat rat with opinions.

She stood at the counter of the Survey Office's public reading room, wearing a surveyor's gray coat two sizes too big for her, her hair pinned up under a cap, and her heart doing something acrobatic and unwise behind her ribs.

"Name and commission," said the clerk, who had the complexion of a man who hadn't seen daylight since the last coronation.

"Wren Ashfell," she said, because lying had gotten easier and that frightened her more than the edict did. "Commissioned out of Marrowdale. We're verifying boundary stones along the eastern approach."

The clerk squinted at her papers — good papers, forged by a woman in the Emberhold named Ivet who charged three eggs per document and was worth every one. He stamped them without really looking.

"You'll want the cellar," he said. "Everything pre-Edict is down there. Mind the mouse."

*Everything pre-Edict.* That was what Sefa wanted. The purge of the Cartographers' Guild had happened fast — faster than a purge should, if it were only fear driving it. Someone had known exactly which maps to burn, exactly which cartographers to take, exactly where the gifted hid their work. Wren had watched the guildhall go up in flames from a rooftop two streets away, and in the weeks since, she'd turned one question over and over like a stone in her pocket:

*Who told them?*

The answer, she hoped, was filed somewhere in this damp cellar, in the shape of a requisition, a delivery record, a name.

She descended the stairs into air that tasted of mildew and old ink, and for a moment — just a moment — she was home. The guildhall's map room had smelled like this. Master Corvin used to say you could tell a cartographer's age by how much of the smell lived in their clothes. Wren had been seventeen when the guild burned. She'd been carrying a case of coastal charts out the back door when the front door came down, and she had run, and she had not stopped running until she collapsed at the door of a stranger in a valley where magic couldn't follow.

A stranger whose maps came true. Whom she had led straight into all of this.

*Don't,* she told herself firmly. *Guilt later. Requisition forms now.*

* * *

The cellar was long and low, lit by oil lamps that hissed like small angry geese. Rows of shelving stretched into the gloom, and here and there other researchers hunched over documents like crows over roadkill. Wren found the section marked GUILD RECORDS — CARTOGRAPHERS' and began to pull files.

It took her an hour to find the first crack.

A delivery ledger, dated nine days before the Edict of the Hunt was posted. A private courier service — Hallow & Sons — paid by an account Wren didn't recognize, to carry *fourteen sealed cases* from the guildhall annex to a residence on Cordwainer Street. Fourteen cases. The guild's most sensitive holdings — the surveys of gifted enclaves, the hidden-atlas fragments, the charts that showed where magic pooled and gathered — had been moved out of the guildhall *before* anyone knew there was danger.

Someone had emptied the vault before setting the fire. Which meant someone inside had helped them pack.

Wren copied the account number onto a scrap of paper with hands that weren't quite steady, tucked it into her boot, and was reaching for the next ledger when the lamp nearest her guttered — and a voice behind her said, warm as a hearthfire and twice as treacherous:

"Well. If it isn't little Wren Quillfeather."

Wren's whole body went cold, then hot, then cold again, in roughly the space of a heartbeat.

She knew that voice. Everyone from the guild knew that voice. Dannel Petch had sat two desks down from her for six years, a round-faced boy with ink-stained cuffs who could copy a coastline so precisely it made master cartographers weep, and who had once traded Wren his afternoon pastry for three weeks of her geometry notes and then complained the notes were *too thorough.*

She turned around slowly.

Dannel Petch stood in the aisle holding a file under one arm, and he was not round-faced anymore. Hunger had carved him down to angles. But the eyes were the same — quick, clever, always calculating what a thing was worth — and they went over her gray coat, her cap, her forged papers, with the unhurried precision of a man reading a legend on a map.

"Fancy coat," he said. "Surveyor, are we? Out of—" he tilted his head, reading upside-down from memory, because of course he remembered, he remembered *everything*, "—Marrowdale. Is there even a survey office in Marrowdale?"

"There is now," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Dannel. You're looking well."

"I'm looking employed," he corrected. "Which in this city is better." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice into something almost confiding. "You know, when the heralds posted the list of names, yours was on it. Third page. Under 'apprentices, unaccounted for.' I thought, poor Wren, probably dead in a ditch somewhere. And here you are. Alive. In the capital. In the *cellar.* Looking for—" his gaze flicked, casual as a knife going into a sheath, "—guild records."

"The Edict requires historical verification of boundary claims," Wren recited. "I'm following procedure."

"Mm." Dannel smiled. It was the smile he'd worn whenever he caught another apprentice cheating off him — pleased, proprietary, patient. "Here's my problem, Wren. Procedure doesn't pay. Informing pays. There's a bounty schedule now. Did you know? Gifted apprentices, unaccounted-for category, forty crowns. Forty crowns is a year of rent and a new coat and *bread that isn't rationed.*"

"Dannel—"

"And the thing is," he went on, conversationally, horribly, "you were never gifted. Not really. You were *adjacent.* You carried the cases. So maybe you're worth less. Maybe you're worth more — because adjacent people know things. They know where the gifted *go.*" His eyes glittered. "So let's make it easy. Who are you working with? Where are you staying? Give me something real, and I'll report you as cooperative. Cooperative gets you a hearing instead of a pyre. That's a kindness, Wren. I'm offering you a kindness."

And the worst part — the part that would keep her awake for years — was that some tired, starving corner of her brain whispered: *he means it. He thinks this is friendship.*

"I need to check something upstairs," Wren said. "My commission seal. One moment."

"Wren."

"One *moment,* Dannel."

She walked. She did not run — running confirmed everything — but she walked with the particular briskness of a person with entirely legitimate business elsewhere, up the stairs, past the clerk, out the door, around the corner, and then she ran.

* * *

Sefa had established the capital cell in a dyer's loft above a tannery, on the theory — as she'd explained with a completely straight face — that no one searching for fugitives would voluntarily enter a building that smelled like that. She'd been right so far. The loft held six people: Sefa; her brother Tammas, who did the cooking and was the gentlest soul Wren had ever met; Ivet the forger; two brothers named Osk and Pell who could pass unheard through any crowd in the city; and a nervous former priest called Brother Aldous who had joined after refusing to read the names of the condemned aloud.

Wren came through the roof hatch at a dead sprint, breathless, and found Sefa mending a net by lamplight while Tammas stirred something that smelled miraculous.

"They have my name," Wren gasped. "On a list. And Dannel Petch — my old guildmate — he's an informer, he recognized me, he offered to — Sefa, we have to move, tonight, now—"

Sefa set down the net. She had a way of going very still when bad news arrived, like a heron deciding whether the pond was safe. "How sure are you that he followed you?"

"He didn't. I'd have seen him. I checked."

"You checked." Sefa's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "Wren. Sit down. Breathe. Tell me all of it."

Wren told her all of it — the ledger, the fourteen cases, the account number, Dannel's face in the lamplight, the bounty schedule, the word *cooperative.* When she finished, Sefa looked at the scrap of paper from her boot for a long moment.

"Cordwainer Street," she said. "That's a noble address. This confirms what Tamsin's been saying — the hunt was planned before the edict. Someone packed the vault first." She folded the paper away. "Good work. Terrible luck. Both at once, that's usually how it goes."

"We should leave tonight," Wren said. "All of us. Split up, scatter to the fallback points—"

"And abandon the capital cell? The children's route runs through here in three days. Forty-one kids coming down from the northern parishes, and this loft is the last safe house before the river." Sefa shook her head. "One informer saw your face in a cellar. That's a thread, not a noose. We cut the thread. Tomorrow, Osk moves you and Tamsin to the chandler's on Bell Lane. Tonight, we finish the supply inventory, and nobody panics."

Tammas ladled stew into bowls and pressed one into Wren's hands. "Eat," he said. "Frightened people who don't eat make frightened decisions."

It was such a Tammas thing to say that Wren almost laughed, and almost cried, and settled for eating. The stew was lamb and barley and too much rosemary, because Tammas believed rosemary cured despair, and sitting there in the reeking loft with the lamplight warm and six fugitives passing bread, Wren thought: *this is what we're fighting for. This exact thing. A bowl of stew among people who shouldn't trust each other and do anyway.*

She should have enjoyed it more. She would spend a long time wishing she had.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, when the lamps had burned low and even the rats were asleep.

Wren woke to the sound she'd heard once before and had prayed never to hear again: the front door of a building giving way. Not broken — *opened*, smoothly, by someone who had a key or had bought one, and then boots, many boots, on the stairs.

"Sefa." She was already shaking her shoulder. "Sefa, they're here."

Sefa was awake instantly, and she was magnificent — no panic, just motion, waking the others, kicking the lamp out, pressing the escape route into every hand. "Roof, across the dye-house, down the rain barrel, alley, split at the corner — Osk takes Wren and Tamsin, Aldous with me, everyone else the river route. *Go.*"

But the loft had one stair and one roof hatch, and the hunters had thought of both.

The hatch banged open above them as they reached for it, and lantern light poured down, and a voice — Dannel's voice, breathless with the excitement of a man collecting his forty crowns — called out: "There! All of them! Don't let the tall one reach the window!"

What followed took perhaps ninety seconds and lasted, in Wren's memory, approximately a decade. Osk got the hatch open anyway, into the faces of two hunters, and fought like a man possessed of eight elbows. Pell threw the stew pot — full pot, still hot — and the screaming was educational. Tammas put his enormous gentle body between the hunters and Brother Aldous and did not move until they broke a chair over him, and even then he mostly leaned.

Sefa fought the way she did everything: efficiently, without wasted motion, and with a flat expression that suggested she was filing grievances for later. She got Aldous to the window. She got Wren boosted halfway to the roof.

And then a hunter got a rope around her ankle, and pulled.

"No!" Wren's fingers were on the roof tiles, Tamsin's hand reaching down for hers — when had Tamsin arrived, he must have come the moment Osk fetched him, he was white-faced in the pre-dawn gray — and below her Sefa twisted, struck, nearly freed herself, and then three hunters landed on her at once and it stopped mattering how well she fought.

"*Go,*" Sefa snarled up at them, hair in her face, arms pinned. "Bell Lane. Chandler's. *Go, you idiot children,* this is an order—"

The last thing Wren saw of her was Sefa being dragged toward the stairs, still giving orders, still furious, still *unafraid* — and then the roof swallowed Wren whole and the cold air of the city took her, and she was running across wet tiles with Tamsin beside her and Osk bleeding behind them, and somewhere below, the dyer's loft filled with lantern light and shouting and the sound of Tammas not fighting anymore.

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, in a bell tower — Osk's idea, on the principle that hunters search cellars and attics but rarely climb. They sat in the dark among the ropes and the pigeons, and the bells hung silent above them like sleeping giants, and Wren shook.

Tamsin put his coat around her shoulders. He didn't say *it's not your fault.* He was learning, slowly, the hard-won wisdom of not saying useless things. Instead he said, "Tell me what happened. From the beginning. Everything matters right now."

So she told him. The ledger. The fourteen cases. Cordwainer Street. Dannel's face, and the pastry he'd once owed her, and the bounty schedule recited like poetry. And Tamsin listened the way he listened to everything — completely, hungrily, like a man mapping terrain — and when she finished he was quiet a moment.

"The vault was emptied before the fire," he said at last. "By someone with guild access. Someone who knew what to take." He looked at her, and his eyes in the dark were steady and kind and terrible. "Wren. Who else had access to the annex?"

And Wren opened her mouth to say *no one, no one I trusted, no one who mattered* — and stopped.

Because there had been one other person with annex keys. One other apprentice who stayed late. One other person who had asked her, three days before the fire, casually, over cold pastry: *where does the guild keep the enclave surveys, exactly? Not the public ones. The real ones.*

She had answered him. She had drawn him a little diagram on the back of a napkin, laughing, because it was Dannel, because Dannel was harmless, because Dannel had owed her a pastry for three weeks and she'd thought that meant something.

"Oh," Wren said. Very quietly. To the pigeons, to the dark, to no one. "Oh, it was me. It was always me. I was the thread. I showed him where to pull."

Tamsin's hand closed over hers in the dark. "Then we pull back," he said simply. "Sefa's alive. Captured isn't finished. And tomorrow morning, you and I are going to find out what happens to prisoners before execution day — because if they're holding her for the pyre grounds, then we have a date, and a place, and a map." Something flickered across his face then, grim and bright at once, the look he got when the gift woke in his fingertips. "And I am very, very good with maps."

Above them, the great bells slept on. Below, the city stirred toward a dawn that would bring proclamations, and posters, and a name newly added to the lists of the condemned.

*Sefa Vane,* it would read. *For harboring the gifted. Execution at the pyre grounds, seven days hence.*

Seven days. Wren counted them in the dark like heartbeats, and swore, on everything the guild had ever taught her about promises, that she would use every single one.
