## 14. The Echo's Bargain

They did not drag him to the palace in chains, which Tamsin found, in a grim and distant way, almost flattering.

They came at dusk, three days after the Emberhold burned, when he and Wren were sheltering in the cellar of a dyer's shop in the low town — a cellar that smelled of vinegar and old grief. There was no battering of doors, no shouting. Just a knock above them, polite as a neighbor asking to borrow sugar, and then the trapdoor opening, and lamplight falling down the stairs along with the shadow of a man in the gray-and-gold livery of the royal survey.

"Master Dravenmoor," the man said, reading from a card as though Tamsin were an appointment. "His Majesty requests the pleasure of your company."

Wren was on her feet before he could stop her, a dye-stained chisel held out like a dagger. "He's not going anywhere with you."

"No," the surveyor agreed mildly. "He isn't going anywhere at all. That is rather the point." He stepped aside, and Tamsin saw the street behind him: not empty, not full of soldiers, but simply *full* — of people. Shopkeepers, cart-drivers, a woman holding a loaf of bread. All of them standing perfectly still, all of them with the same faintly puzzled look, like sleepers who had wandered out of bed and couldn't remember why.

And every one of them had a thin line drawn across their throat. Not blood. Ink. A fine black line, precise as a surveyor's rule, that hadn't been there a moment before.

Tamsin's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the bedrock of the city itself. He knew that line. He had *made* lines like it, once, marking boundaries on charts of places that didn't exist yet.

"You've been mapping people," he said quietly.

The surveyor smiled with a mouth that moved a half-beat too late, like a puppet remembering its cues. "His Majesty prefers the term *surveying*. One cannot govern what one has not measured." The smile widened by exactly one tooth. "You of all people should understand, Master Dravenmoor. You taught us how."

* * *

The mirror room was not where they'd taken him the first time — that had been a cell, bare and honest in its cruelty. This was worse. This was a long gallery hung with mirrors on both sides, dozens of them, tall as doors, each one framed in tarnished silver, and Tamsin's reflection walked beside him in every single one.

Except that his reflections were wrong.

In the first mirror, he was older. In the second, he stood in the Hollow Valley, mentor's workshop behind him, though he'd left it months ago and the glass showed it whole and unburned. In the third, Wren walked at his side — except the reflection-Wren was smiling, and the real Wren was miles away, and the reflected version turned its head to watch him pass with eyes that were not hers.

"Don't look at them," said a voice ahead, dry as old paper. "It only encourages them."

The echo-king sat at the far end of the gallery in a chair that might once have been a throne but had been demoted to furniture, positioned before a single enormous mirror draped in black cloth. Aldric — or the thing wearing Aldric — looked smaller than Tamsin remembered from Wren's terrified descriptions. Smaller, and grayer, like a man made of ash pressed hard enough to hold a shape. His crown sat slightly askew, and there was something pitiful in that, something almost human, and Tamsin hated himself for noticing.

"You came quietly," the echo said. Its voice was the king's voice, Tamsin realized — or nearly. It was the king's voice played back by someone who had heard it secondhand and gotten the warmth slightly wrong. "I appreciate that. So few people come quietly. They always want to make speeches first."

"I had one prepared," Tamsin admitted. "Something about tyranny and the indomitable spirit of the gifted. I can deliver it if you'd like."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered across the ash-gray face. Not a smile. Something older and sadder, like a memory of a smile that had died before Tamsin was born.

"There it is," the echo said softly. "The valley humor. Your mentor's, isn't it? Master Corvin Hale, who taught you that maps are promises." It tilted its head. "Do you know what I promised, mapmaker? Once?"

"I know what you are," Tamsin said. "An oath that broke. A vow the king made and couldn't keep, walking around wearing his face."

"And yet here we are, having a civilized conversation instead of a burning." The echo gestured, and one of the mirrors — the one showing the Hollow Valley — rippled like pond water. "Because I am not my reputation. My reputation is fire and edicts and children marched to pyres, and yes, all of that is true, and none of it is *personal*, which is more than most kings can say. What I want—" the gray hands folded, patient, reasonable, "—is to finish."

"Finish the oath," Tamsin said. "So I've heard. From a man made of ash, whispering in a sealed room."

"Rumors travel fast when you build roads for them." The echo rose, and the movement was wrong in a way Tamsin couldn't name — smooth where joints should bend, fluid where bones should creak. It crossed the gallery and stopped before the valley-mirror, gazing at the little workshop, the boundary stones, the lake Tamsin had once drawn into being without knowing it. "Look at it. Do you know what this place is, Tamsin Dravenmoor? It is a hole in my map. A blank space the size of a county, sitting in the middle of everything I have measured. Every gifted soul in the kingdom who escapes me eventually finds their way there, because magic cannot follow, and so my beautiful, complete survey has a wound in it that will never close." It turned. "I could burn it. I nearly have. But burning a blank space proves nothing. Filling it — *that* would be finishing."

"So draw it yourself," Tamsin said. "You're clearly capable. You've got half the capital wearing ink collars."

"Ink follows rules," the echo said, with the first note of genuine frustration, and for an instant the mirrors all along the gallery shivered. "My lines bind what exists. They do not make. Yours—" it pointed one gray finger at him, and Tamsin felt it like a compass needle finding north, "—yours *make*. A road where no road was. Walls become rivers. I have read the reports. I confess I didn't believe them. Then I saw the pyre grounds." Something that might have been hunger crossed the ash face. "You don't draw what is, mapmaker. You draw what *will be*. Do you understand how rare that is? How *lonely*? I have spent thirty years alone inside a dead man's promise, and you are the first thing I have ever met that is anything like me."

"I'm nothing like you."

"We are both promises," the echo said simply. "The difference is that yours haven't broken yet."

It let that sit. Outside the draped mirror, somewhere deep in the palace, a bell rang the hour, muffled and distant, and Tamsin thought absurdly of Wren — was she safe, had Sefa's people gotten her out, was she right now doing something reckless and brave and completely predictable — and the echo watched him think it, and smiled its half-late smile, and Tamsin understood that the thinking had been allowed. Encouraged. Bait.

"Here is the bargain," the echo said. "Draw me the hidden places. The Emberhold's sisters, the waystations, the cellars and hollows where your fugitive network shelters. Draw them truly, as only you can, and I will strike the Hollow Valley from my survey forever. Unmapped. Unmeasured. Blank by *decree* rather than by accident — which, for a thing made of law, is the closest I come to mercy. Your mentor lives. The valley lives. Everyone you loved before you ever met a dragon keeps breathing." It spread its gray hands. "Or refuse, and I take the valley anyway, slowly, and everyone in it, and you will have refused to save them for the sake of people who will die regardless. I am told that is called principle. It looks, from where I stand, remarkably like arithmetic done badly."

The gallery was very quiet. Tamsin became aware that his heart was hammering, and that the echo could probably hear it, and hated that too.

"You're asking me to betray every gifted person in the kingdom."

"I'm asking you to choose which blank space survives. I'm told that's what growing up is." The echo drifted closer, and up close Tamsin could see that its eyes were the worst part — not cold, not cruel, but *tired*, tired the way only something that had been awake inside a vow for thirty years could be tired. "You will refuse. I know. They always refuse; it's in the reports. Refusal is practically a genre." It sighed, and the sigh stirred dust that had no business existing in a body that wasn't breathing. "But I wanted you to hear it from me rather than from the fire. I find that matters, lately. I don't know why. Another thing that's wearing out, I suppose."

"Then you know my answer."

"I knew it before you arrived." The echo turned back toward the great draped mirror. "Refuse, then. Beautifully, if you like. Make it a speech."

"I refuse," Tamsin said. And then, because the echo had asked for beauty and he'd never been able to resist a request well-made: "You're a broken promise wearing a dead man's crown, and every line you've ever drawn is a lie about what people are for. People aren't territory. They're not blank spaces waiting to be filled. And if you touch one hair on the valley—"

"Yes, yes." The echo waved a hand, almost fond. "Indomitable spirit. Very good. Thank you." And it pulled the black cloth from the mirror.

Tamsin had expected the king. Instead the mirror showed a chart.

It was *him*. Rendered in the fine black linework of a royal survey — the slope of his shoulders as contour lines, the scar on his knuckle marked as a landmark, the small hesitation in his stance rendered as a dotted line labeled *UNRESOLVED*. Rivers of ink ran where his veins should be. And across the whole of it, in the neat hand of the crown's cartographers, a title:

**SURVEY OF THE PERSON KNOWN AS TAMSIN DRAVENMOOR. SCALE: COMPLETE.**

"That's not possible," Tamsin said. His voice sounded far away. "That's not how the gift works. Only I can—"

"Only *you* can unmake what you make," the echo agreed pleasantly. "But I never needed you to make this one." It reached into its sleeve and drew out a folded sheet of paper, worn soft at the creases, and Tamsin knew it before it was fully open — knew the coastlines, the road networks, the careful little symbols for water and wood. His own work. The chart stolen from the Emberhold, the one that had led the hunters to Sefa's door, the one whose guilt had been sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone for weeks.

"Do you know why I wanted the original?" the echo said, smoothing it flat. "Not a copy. The original. Because a map is a promise, mapmaker — your dragon taught you that much, I believe — and a promise carries the maker in it. Sweat in the paper. Blood, if you're careless with a pen knife." It turned the chart over, and there, near the bottom margin, was a tiny brown fleck Tamsin had never noticed, the ghost of a nick from a drafting blade three years ago. "One drop. That's all a person is, to a surveyor. One drop, properly indexed."

The echo pressed its gray thumb against the fleck.

The gallery changed.

Every mirror along the walls filled at once — not with reflections now, but with *lines*. Contour lines crawling over Tamsin's reflection like ivy. Boundary markers blooming along the line of his spine. In one glass, a small symbol appeared over his heart and began, patiently, to be labeled. He felt each one land — not pain, exactly, but *presence*, the sensation of being measured, of being known down to the inch by something that intended to own the measurement.

"What are you doing," he whispered. It wasn't a question. It was a prayer said backwards.

"Finishing," the echo said. "You wouldn't draw me the gifted, so I shall draw myself *you*. And a man who holds the map of Tamsin Dravenmoor holds everything Tamsin Dravenmoor knows. Every refuge. Every password. Every mile of every road the network walks." It rolled up the stolen chart with terrible gentleness, like a man handling a love letter. "You see the elegance. I don't need your betrayal. I only need your *self*, and you brought it with you. You've been carrying it to me for weeks. Every step you took running away was a step on my survey line."

Tamsin lunged for the chart. The echo didn't even step back — two surveyors in gray livery simply materialized on either side of him, or perhaps they had always been there, or perhaps they were lines that had just finished drawing themselves, and their hands closed on his arms with a grip like ruled margins.

"The valley," Tamsin gasped. "You promised—"

"I promised nothing," the echo said. "I *offered*. Offers require acceptance. You declined. And now, since you're being surveyed anyway, let us begin with the region you know best." It lifted the black cloth again, and beneath it the great mirror filled with the image of the Hollow Valley — and across the valley, slow and deliberate as a signature, the echo began to draw a line.

A boundary line. A new edge, where no edge had ever been.

"Chapter one," the echo said, almost tenderly, dipping its pen. "*The place called Hollow, and what it contains.* Hold still, Master Dravenmoor. This next part goes easier if you don't struggle."

Far away — beyond the palace, beyond the city walls, past the mountains where a dying dragon kept her guttering candle of fire — Tamsin felt something answer the pen's first stroke. A pull, faint and horrible, like a thread tied around his ribs being tested for weight.

And in the deepest part of him, under the fear, under the guilt, something old and stubborn and *his* whispered: *not yet. Not like this.*

The line kept drawing.
