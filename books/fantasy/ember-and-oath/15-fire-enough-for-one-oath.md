## 15. Fire Enough for One Oath

Vaelith had lived long enough to know that the hardest truths were never spoken in grand halls or upon battlefields. They were spoken in caves, over cold tea, to children who had no idea what they were asking.

The cave was old even by her standards. She had hollowed it herself, three centuries past, when the Ashreach still smoked and she had been young enough to think fire was infinite. Now her flame guttered in the great basin of her chest like a lamp left burning in an empty house, and the cave walls — once black and glassy with dragonfire — had gone dull as old slate.

She heard them coming long before they reached her: the boy's uneven footfalls on the scree, the girl's lighter ones beside him, and behind them both the soft, determined tread of Sefa's people carrying supplies they did not know would not be needed tonight.

*The mapmaker,* she thought, and something in her ancient chest turned over, the way a key turns in a lock that has been rusted shut for years. *He has come at last.*

She did not rise. Rising took effort now, and effort fed the fire, and the fire was not hers to spend freely anymore. It belonged to someone else — had belonged to someone else for thirty years, though he did not know it, though *she* had only truly understood it in the last decade, when the guttering began in earnest and she finally admitted what the leaking meant.

They came around the last bend of the passage, torchlight spilling ahead of them, and Tamsin Dravenmoor stopped dead, the way mortals always did. Even diminished — even folded down into this cavern like a ship beached in a dry dock, wings furled, scales gone the gray of ash that has cooled too long — a dragon was still a dragon.

"You're smaller than I imagined," said Wren, because Wren was the sort of person who said such things, and Vaelith found, to her own mild surprise, that she liked her for it.

"And you are ruder than I imagined," Vaelith replied, her voice rolling out of her like distant thunder deciding whether to bother. "We are even."

Tamsin recovered first. He always would, she suspected; it was in the set of his shoulders, that stubbornness of men who have spent their lives being wrong about themselves in interesting ways. He bowed — awkwardly, a valley bow, the kind given to village elders rather than dragons — and set down his satchel.

"Vaelith. Sefa said you asked for me."

"I asked for the mapmaker." She let her gaze settle on him, heavy as weather. "You have not yet decided if that is you. I am patient. I have been patient for thirty years. A few more hours will not kill me." A dry rumble, almost a laugh. "Though it may come close."

Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled by fear. Good. Fear that could laugh was fear that could work.

"Sit," Vaelith said. "Both of you. What I have to tell you is long, and it is ugly, and neither of you is going to enjoy it. But you came here for truth, and truth is the one thing left in this kingdom that no one has thought to burn."

*

She began, as all true stories must begin, with an oath.

"There was a king before Aldric," she said. "His name was Edran, and he was a good man and a bad ruler, which is a combination more common than either flatterers or historians care to admit. Under his reign the gifted were licensed, catalogued, taxed, and occasionally disappeared. He called it order. The gifted called it a cage with very nice bars.

"Aldric was his ward. A boy from the borderlands, sharp-eyed, stubborn, raised in a household where a hedge-witch was family and a storm-caller was a neighbor. He grew up believing what the valley folk believe — that magic is just another neighbor, sometimes annoying, occasionally dangerous, but *yours*. Part of the household of the world."

Tamsin shifted. Vaelith watched him shift, and knew exactly which word had caught him. *Valley.* She let it sit. There would be time.

"When Edran died without an heir, the council looked for a successor who would not simply continue the cage. They found Aldric. And Aldric, twenty-three years old and full of the particular arrogance of young men who have never been truly afraid, swore an oath before the assembled realm."

She closed her eyes. She did not need to remember the words; she had helped write them.

"*I swear by my crown, my blood, and my breath: so long as I reign, no gift shall be hunted, no gift shall be bound, and no power of mine shall ever be turned against the gifted of this realm. This I vow until my dying day, and beyond it.*"

The cave was very quiet. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the mountain's throat and made a low moan, like the mountain itself remembering.

"He meant it," Vaelith said. "That is the tragedy, mapmaker, and you must hold onto it, because everything else I tell you will make you want to put it down. He meant every word. He was crowned on a midsummer morning with the sun through the eastern windows, and the gifted of the realm wept in the streets, and I —" her voice caught, a sound like gravel sliding — "I flew the length of the kingdom that night, lighting the beacon-fires on every peak, because I believed the age of cages was over.

"The oath needed a witness of sufficient weight. That is how oaths of state work — they are not words, they are *structures*, load-bearing things, and they require foundations. The crown was one foundation. The blood royal was another. And the third..." She opened one eye, vast and amber and very tired. "The third was me. The last dragon. The oldest living thing in the realm. My fire became the third pillar of his oath.

"For eleven years, it was the finest thing I was ever part of. Better than any hoard. Better than any flight. The guilds flourished. The pyres went cold. Children born with gifts were kissed instead of drowned. I thought, *this is what fire is for.* Not burning. Keeping warm."

She paused. In the silence, Tamsin spoke, quietly, the way people speak when they already dread the answer.

"What happened?"

"Aldric happened," Vaelith said. "Aldric, who discovered that meaning an oath and keeping one are separated by a distance wider than the Ashreach. Eleven years in, the northern lords rebelled, and at their head marched a storm-caller — a woman who could pull lightning out of a clear sky, who had been his friend, who had taught him to fish when he was nine. He faced her across a field at Carrowmere, and she called the storm down on his army, and four thousand men died in the space of an hour.

"He won. He always won; that was the problem with Aldric. And standing in that field, in the rain, among the dead, something broke in him. Not his heart — hearts mend, messily but they mend. His *oath* broke. Not the letter of it. The letter stood. But inside, in the place where a man keeps his vows, he wished — with his whole soul, with the full weight of a crowned and blooded king — that he had never sworn it. He wished he could turn his power against the gifted. He wished the oath had never been spoken."

Her fire flickered low. Both humans leaned forward without realizing it, drawn like moths, and Vaelith thought, not for the first time, that it was a strange joke of the universe that the creatures most drawn to her dying light were the two who would have to carry what it bought.

"An oath sworn on crown, blood, and fire does not simply sit there when its keeper turns against it," she said. "It *answers*. The wish of a king is a powerful thing. The wish of a king breaking his own vow is —" she searched for the word and did not find a gentle one — "*generative*. The oath tore loose from his heart and stood up. It took his shape, because it had been made of his voice. It took his crown, because it had been sworn on the crown. And it began doing the one thing Aldric had wished for in his darkest moment: hunting the gift out of the world.

"It calls itself the king. The court believes it is the king. Half the time, I suspect, *it* believes it is the king. But it is not. It is the shape of a broken promise, walking around wearing a man's face, and it will not stop until every gifted thing in the realm is ash — including, at the end, the man whose wish made it. Because an echo, mapmaker, cannot abide its source. When there is nothing left to hunt, it will go home and finish the job."

"And Aldric?" Wren whispered. "The real one? Where is he?"

"In the palace," Vaelith said. "In the mirror chamber, behind seven seals, where the echo keeps him the way a spider keeps a fly — alive, because the oath needs its keeper alive to remain binding, and imprisoned, because the echo cannot risk him changing his mind." Her voice dropped. "He is half-consumed. Thirty years of being eaten slowly, from the inside, by your own worst moment. He whispers *finish the oath* because the echo pours those words into him, and some nights I am told he believes them. Some nights he begs for it. That is what consumption looks like when the devouring thing is made of your own regret."

Tamsin had gone very pale. "And your fire. You said —"

"I said my fire was the third pillar." She drew a breath that stirred the dust of three centuries. "When the oath broke and the echo rose, the structure should have collapsed entirely. Oaths do not survive their own betrayal. But a pillar of fire does not break, mapmaker. It *burns*. For thirty years my fire has held the broken oath together — barely, badly, the way a dam holds back a flood with cracks running through it. Every year the cracks widen. Every year I burn a little dimmer, because the fire is going somewhere. It is going into the wall between Aldric and the thing that eats him.

"If my fire dies before the oath is unmade, the wall falls, and the echo consumes him whole — and then, with a king's full power and no remnant of conscience left inside it, it does to the world what it has been doing to him. Slowly, and then all at once."

The wind moaned overhead again. Wren had wrapped her arms around herself. Tamsin sat motionless, and Vaelith, who had watched kings and cowards and heroes for three hundred years, recognized the look on his face: a man doing arithmetic he did not want to finish.

"So unmake it," he said. "Break the oath. How?"

"Ah." She let the word hang. Gentle humor was a kindness she could still afford, and she spent it carefully. "There is the ugly part. An oath can be ended three ways. It can be kept until the keeper dies — not available; the echo intends to keep him alive indefinitely, the way a miser keeps a coin. It can be broken by force — but force is what created it, and striking it with force feeds it. Or it can be *unsworn*: unwound, line by line, word by word, by the ones who swore it, in the presence of the pillar that anchors it."

"By Aldric," Tamsin said.

"By Aldric, yes. But he cannot. He is half-eaten, sealed away, and the echo holds his tongue. Which leaves—" she spread one vast wing, joints crackling like green wood, and let them see how the membrane had gone thin and gray as parchment, translucent at the edges, "—the other party to the oath. The witness. The pillar."

Understanding arrived on Tamsin's face the way dawn arrives over mountains: slowly, and then all at once, and then it was everywhere and there was no undoing it.

"No," he said.

"My fire must burn the old words away," Vaelith said. "All of them. Crown-oath, blood-oath, fire-oath, root and branch, the whole rotten beautiful structure. And a fire burns only what it touches, mapmaker. To unbind Aldric, the oath must be consumed entire — and I am the last log on that particular pyre."

"You'll die." Wren's voice cracked on the second word.

"I will *end*," Vaelith corrected, with the pedantry of the very old. "It is not the same thing, though I grant the distinction will comfort no one. Dragons do not die the way you do. We go out. Like lamps. And if the wick is spent in good service, the dark afterward is not so frightening. I have been afraid of many things in three hundred years, child. Running out of reasons to burn is the only one that ever kept me awake."

Tamsin was on his feet. Of course he was. Valley-born, valley-stubborn, a man who had spent his life drawing lines around places to keep them safe. "There has to be another way. Another anchor. Another — I could draw one. I could *map* one, a new pillar, a new —"

"Sit down, mapmaker."

"I can fix this —"

"*Sit.*" The word came out with a thread of real fire in it, the first she had allowed herself in weeks, and it filled the cave like a struck bell. Tamsin sat. Wren grabbed his sleeve, whether to steady him or herself, Vaelith could not tell.

"Listen to me," she said, softer now, because softness was the harder discipline. "You cannot redraw what you do not understand, and you do not yet understand what you are. That is the next truth, and it is yours, and I will not take it from you tonight. But hear this much: the echo knows about you. It has known since the Emberhold burned. It has your blood in a stolen chart, and it is learning to use it, and every hour you spend refusing to understand your own gift is an hour it spends understanding it better. Whatever you feel about my ending — grief, guilt, fury — set it on the shelf. There is work first. There is a man in a mirror chamber who has spent thirty years whispering *finish the oath* to himself, and there is a kingdom being repainted gray, and there is a boy from a hidden valley who draws the future and doesn't know why."

She lowered her great head until her eyes were level with his, amber and ancient and, despite everything, warm.

"I swore an oath once that the age of cages was over," she said. "I intend to keep it. Even if keeping it is the last thing I do. Especially then."

Tamsin's jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "How long? How long does your fire have?"

Vaelith considered lying, found she had no taste for it — not with these two, not anymore — and answered honestly.

"With careful spending? A season. Perhaps two. But the echo is accelerating, and it has your chart, and it has begun mapping *you*." She saw the flinch and did not soften it. "So the honest answer is: less than a season. Less, probably, than anyone wants. Which means the question in front of us is not *whether* I burn the oath away. That was settled thirty years ago, the night a good man broke his own beautiful promise. The question is whether we reach the mirror chamber while there is still enough of me left to light it."

Silence again. Then Wren, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand in the brisk, furious way of people who refuse to cry properly, said: "Then we'd better not waste time sitting in a cave."

"No," Vaelith agreed, and felt — genuinely, unexpectedly — the ghost of a smile move through her old bones. "No, we had better not."

They gathered their torches and their courage and their terrible new knowledge, and turned toward the cave mouth, where the night air of the Ashreach came down cold and clean. But at the threshold Tamsin stopped, half-turned, and looked back at her with an expression she had seen once before, long ago, on a young king's face at his coronation: the look of a person about to say something reckless and meaning it completely.

"Vaelith. When we get there. When you —" He couldn't finish it. Good. Some sentences deserved to fail.

"When I go out," she said, "the last light in the world will be the one you carry forward. Make it a good map, mapmaker. Draw us somewhere worth arriving."

He nodded once, and then they were gone, footsteps fading down the scree, and Vaelith settled back into the dark of her cave alone.

Alone — except for the fire. Her fire, guttering, faithful, thirty years spent holding up the sky for a man who didn't know her name anymore. She banked it carefully, the way she had learned to, feeding it just enough, hoarding the rest against the day it would all be needed at once.

Far to the south, faint as a bruise on the horizon, the sky over the capital glowed the color of old ash.

*Soon,* she thought, closing her eyes. *Burn fast, little echo. Burn loud. I am saving my light for you.*

And in the deep of the mountain, the last dragon began, very quietly, to count what remained of her fire — and to plan how to spend every last ember of it in a single, final, magnificent blaze.
