## 16. Rescue at the Pyre Grounds

The morning of Sefa's execution dawned clear and cold, the kind of morning that would have been beautiful anywhere else in the world.

Tamsin stood at the window of a safehouse three streets from the pyre grounds, watching frost form on the glass, and thought about how he had once believed mornings were neutral things. In the Hollow Valley, a morning was just a morning — mist on the lake, bread in the oven, his mentor grumbling about ink freezing in the well. Mornings did not carry executions. They did not have schedules for burning people.

"Stop doing that," Wren said from behind him.

"Doing what?"

"Standing at windows looking like a man composing his own elegy. It's depressing and also you're silhouetted against the light like a target." Wren tossed him an apple. "Eat. You'll need your hands steady."

Tamsin caught it, mostly. "My hands are always steady."

"Your hands shook so badly drawing the river last week that we now own a waterfall that wasn't there before."

"That was *intentional*."

"The miller disagrees. The miller's chickens disagree even more loudly."

Despite everything — despite the burned Emberhold, despite Sefa in a cell beneath the Survey Office, despite the echo-king wearing Aldric's face like a borrowed coat — Tamsin felt his mouth twitch. That was Wren's particular gift, one no edict could hunt: the ability to make the end of the world feel survivable, one absurd complaint at a time.

He bit into the apple. It was sour. He ate it anyway.

* * *

They went over the plan one final time around the safehouse table, which was really a door laid across two barrels, because everything in the capital's fugitive network was improvised from things that had once been something else. The network had been gutted after Wren's escape from the Survey Office — cells rolled up, couriers taken, safehouses burned — but what remained had gathered here, in a tanner's attic that smelled powerfully of ambition and slightly less powerfully of leather.

There were eleven of them left in the capital cell. Eleven, against a garrison, against the pyre grounds, against whatever the thing in the palace sent to watch its executions.

"Guards first," said Corvin, a broad-shouldered former soldier who had lost an ear to the Edict's first sweep and gained a permanent scowl. "Walls second. Then the crowd. Then the pyres themselves. Mapmaker, can you do it?"

Tamsin looked down at the map spread across the barrel-door. It was the pyre grounds as he'd drawn them from memory and Wren's testimony: a great cobbled square ringed by the old city wall, the execution platform at its center, six iron pyres arranged in a crescent behind it. Five were empty. One, this afternoon, would not be.

"I can redraw it," he said. "I've done walls before. Roads. Rivers."

"Not all at once," Corvin said. "Not under pressure. Not with hunters in the crowd looking for exactly your kind of trouble."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "Not all at once."

Corvin waited. Wren waited. Everyone waited, with the particular patience of people who had learned that Tamsin Dravenmoor arrived at his conclusions the way a cartographer arrives at a coastline — slowly, and then all at once.

"But I don't have to do it all at once," Tamsin said. "That's the thing about maps. Nobody reads a whole map at once. You read it line by line. So I'll redraw it line by line. A wall becomes a road — that's one line. The road becomes a river — that's another. Each change is small. Each change is nothing, to anyone watching. Until suddenly the square isn't a square anymore, and by then it's too late to unsee it."

"And if you falter halfway through?" Corvin asked. "If the wall is half-road and half-wall when the guards come?"

Tamsin thought of Vaelith, her fire guttering in the mountain dark. *Every map is a promise,* she had told him. *And promises can be unmade — but only by the one who made them.*

"Then I finish it," he said. "That's the whole point of me, Corvin. I finish lines."

* * *

They entered the pyre grounds with the crowd, which was the only way in and nearly the worst way to be anywhere.

The Edict required public executions — *that the lawless may witness the wages of lawlessness*, the proclamation read, in language Tamsin now recognized as the echo's handwriting: grand, hollow, and hungry. So the square filled. Shopkeepers with their sleeves pulled low. Mothers who told their children to look away and then didn't look away themselves. Old men who remembered the last purge, forty years gone, and stood with their hats off as though at a funeral — which, Tamsin supposed, they were.

He and Wren pressed toward the eastern edge of the square, where the old city wall rose fifteen feet of gray stone. Above it, the watchtowers. Along the crescent of pyres, soldiers in crown black, pikes at rest. On the platform, a herald in silver-trimmed robes, reading names from a scroll with the enthusiasm of a man reciting a laundry list.

Sefa was brought out at midday.

Tamsin had prepared himself for chains, for bruises, for the particular brokenness that captivity worked into people. What he had not prepared himself for was that Sefa walked upright. Her wrists were bound. Her hair, always severe, had been half-torn loose. But she crossed that platform the way she had crossed the Emberhold's great hall — like a woman inspecting premises she intended to reclaim.

She found the crowd with her eyes. Found *him*, somehow, in the press of bodies, thirty yards and a thousand risks away. And she lifted her chin, very slightly, in the smallest possible nod.

*Get on with it,* the nod said. *You're late.*

Tamsin's fury, which he had been carrying since the Emberhold burned like a coal wrapped in cloth, finally caught open flame.

* * *

The herald began the formal charges. Tamsin stopped listening. He took out his map.

It was folded small against his chest, hidden beneath his coat, and it was the most dangerous thing in the kingdom: the pyre grounds, drawn in his own hand, in his own blood-tempered ink. He opened it against his forearm, angled so only he could see, and found the first line.

The eastern wall.

He had drawn it as a wall because it was a wall. But a line was a line, and a line did not care what it became. He set his pen — a stub of charcoal, nothing finer, nothing that would glint — to the paper, and he drew.

He drew a gate where there had been stone. Then a road through the gate. Then the road widening, curving, running west along where the wall's foundation had been.

The magic came up through him like water finding a channel — familiar now, after weeks of practice, but never gentle. It never got gentle. It felt, every time, like being briefly larger than his own body, like the world leaning close to ask *are you sure?*

*I'm sure,* he answered, and pressed the line home.

Above the heads of the crowd, the eastern wall shivered.

It did not crumble — crumbling was loud, and loud was death. It *changed*. Stone flowed like poured cream, reorganizing itself into cobbles, into a graded roadway, into a wide gate standing open onto the eastern hills. The transition made almost no sound at all — a sigh, a whisper, the sound of a held breath released. But the crowd felt it. Tamsin watched it move through them like wind through wheat: heads turning, murmurs rising, a woman dropping her basket of onions and not noticing.

On the platform, the herald faltered mid-sentence.

"Now," Tamsin breathed.

Corvin moved. That was the signal and the sum of it — Corvin, at the western edge, bellowing "FIRE! The pyres! She's lighting them early!" — and the crowd, which had been balanced on a knife-edge of dread all day, broke like a dam.

It was not a stampede. It was worse and better than that: it was eleven fugitives, seeded through ten thousand frightened people, each shouting different directions, each starting different panics. *The bridge! The bridge is out!* *Soldiers on the north road!* *Run east, east, the gate's open!* And the crowd, given five contradictory exits, chose the truest one — the impossible new gate in the east, because fear loves a door.

The garrison commander on the platform screamed orders. Half his soldiers turned to face the fleeing thousands; the other half stared at the wall-that-was-not-a-wall with the expressions of men quietly revising their understanding of reality.

Tamsin drew the second line.

The road he'd made became a river.

This was the part he'd argued with himself about for two nights — the violence of it, the sheer presumption. A wall becoming a road displaced nothing. A road becoming a river displaced *everything*: cobbles into current, ground into water, the solid certainty of the world into motion. He had lain awake imagining the people who would fall in, the carts that would founder, the chaos.

But the river was shallow. He had drawn it shallow, knee-deep and fast, a barrier and not a drowning. And when it came — when the new-made water burst from the transformed roadway and sheeted across the square in a glittering arc — it swept the soldiers' formation apart without sweeping a single civilian off their feet. The crowd knew the ground. The soldiers did not. That was the whole difference, and Tamsin clung to it: *the crowd knows the ground.* He had drawn the river to remember the paths between the cobbles, the high places, the way home. The water carried people toward safety and soldiers into confusion, and if that was violence, it was violence with a conscience.

"Mapmaker!" Wren shouted over the roar. "Pyres!"

Tamsin was already moving, Wren at his shoulder, cutting through the panicked press toward the crescent. The soldiers nearest the pyres had abandoned their posts to fight the river; the pyre holding Sefa stood guarded by exactly two men, both staring eastward at the impossible water.

Wren took the first one's knees out from under him with a length of chain. Tamsin, who had never struck anyone in his life before this month, drove his shoulder into the second and discovered that terror is an excellent teacher of leverage. The guard went down into the shallows, spluttering.

"Sefa!" Wren was already climbing the pyre, sawing at the ropes binding her to the stake. "We're getting you out, don't say anything, I know you're going to say something—"

"You're late," Sefa said hoarsely.

"See? *Knew* it."

Tamsin kept watch, pen in one hand, heart hammering against the map against his ribs. Across the square, the garrison was regrouping. Worse — at the far edge, near the northern tower, he saw them: four figures in gray, unmoving amid the chaos, and even at this distance he could feel it, the wrongness, the *hunger*. Hunters. Echo-touched. And they were not looking at the crowd or the river or the fire.

They were looking at him.

*Tamsin,* one of them mouthed, though no sound reached him. *We see you, mapmaker.*

His blood went cold. His blood-chart — the stolen chart, the one the echo had taken, the one laced with his blood. The echo knew his work. Knew his hand. Could probably smell it across a crowded square.

"Sefa, can you run?" he called.

"I've been sitting in a cell for nine days waiting to run," Sefa said, hitting the ground beside him. Her legs buckled; Wren caught her; her eyes, fever-bright, swept the transformed square — the gate, the river, the crowd pouring through — with a general's assessment. "You did *this*?"

"Line by line," Tamsin said.

"It's the ugliest piece of urban planning I've ever seen." She gripped his arm. "It's perfect. North wall next. We need a way out that isn't the gate — they'll seal the gate."

"They're already—" Tamsin started, and then the world tilted.

It came through the map. Through *him*. A pressure, vast and patient, pressing against the lines of his drawing like a thumb testing wet clay. The echo. Somewhere in the palace, the thing wearing Aldric's crown had picked up the blood-chart, and it was reading him — reading the square he'd redrawn, reading the river, reading the signature of his intent written in every transformed stone.

And it began, gently, horribly, to edit.

The river's far bank curled upward, trying to become a wall again — *his* wall, inverted, a trap closing around the fleeing crowd. The new gate's arch thickened, darkened, reaching for the shape of a prison door.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to the echo, to the map, to the sky. "This one's mine."

He dropped to his knees in the shallows, map flat on the water's skin, and redrew faster than he had ever drawn — not fighting the echo's edits but *outpacing* them, adding lines ahead of the corruption, giving the river tributaries the echo hadn't mapped, splitting the gate into three arches so no single edit could close them all. It was like wrestling a tide with a pencil. Sweat ran into his eyes. His vision blurred. Somewhere behind him Wren was shouting his name, and Sefa was shouting strategy, and none of it mattered, because there was only the line, only the next line, only—

A shadow fell across the square.

Not a cloud. The sun was high and hard. This shadow came from the north, from beyond the wall, and it was shaped like wings.

The crowd saw it and screamed anew. The soldiers saw it and fell to their knees. The four gray hunters looked up, and for the first time, Tamsin saw fear move across their faces.

Vaelith came over the wall like the last page of an old book — vast and worn and burning at the edges. Her scales had gone dull as ash, her wings beat with terrible effort, and her fire — her fire was a candleflame now, a coal, a single stubborn ember in a cavernous throat. But when she opened her jaws above the pyre grounds and let that ember out, it did not need to be large. It needed only to be *hers*.

The flame struck the six iron pyres and unmade them — not melted, *unmade*, iron remembering itself as ore, as earth, as nothing at all. The execution platform followed, timber sighing back into forest-shape and then into shapelessness. Where the pyres had stood, there was only scorched ground and the imprint of a promise revoked.

Every map is a promise, she had told him. Apparently some promises could be burned.

"*Go,*" Vaelith's voice rolled across the square, ancient and cracked and absolutely certain. "*Little king's hounds. Tell your master the mapmaker is claimed.*"

The hunters fled. The garrison, wisely, decided that dragons fell outside their pay grade. And Tamsin knelt in his own river, shaking, laughing, crying, while Wren hauled him up by the collar and Sefa — free, upright, furious, alive — looked at the dragon, the river, the ruined pyre grounds, and the two bedraggled young men responsible, and delivered her verdict:

"We're leaving. Now. Before either of you does anything else sentimental."

* * *

They went out through the third arch — the one the echo had never managed to close — and up into the western hills as the sun went down behind them, painting the flooded square gold. Behind them, the capital seethed and rang with alarm bells. Ahead, the fugitive network's scattered survivors would be waiting at the rally points, counting heads, mourning their dead, and beginning, in the stubborn way of such people, to plan again.

Wren walked beside Tamsin in comfortable silence for a long while. Then:

"You wrestled a god with a pencil today."

"A pencil and a very good map."

"You kneed a soldier into a river."

"He started it."

"And a dragon showed up and burned the pyres, which — I want to be clear — was not in the plan."

"She improvises," Tamsin said.

Sefa, limping ahead on Wren's arm, snorted. "The whole kingdom improvises now. That's what we're building, whether the crown likes it or not." She glanced back at Tamsin, and for a moment the general's mask slipped, and underneath it was simply a woman whose brother had died and whose rescue had cost a square, a river, and probably a kingdom's worth of consequences. "Thank you," she said, gruffly, as though the words cost more than the battle.

Tamsin was still warm from it — from all of it, the fury transmuted at last into something with purpose and shape — when the rider found them.

One of the network's couriers, foam-flecked horse, a face gray as ash. She slid from the saddle before the horse fully stopped.

"Tamsin Dravenmoor?" she gasped. "There's word from the south. From the valley." Her eyes found his, and he felt the world tilt again, the way it had when the echo pressed against his map — but colder, much colder, and this time there was no pencil in his hand.

"The Hollow Valley's burning," she said. "The echo-king found it. It's been burning since dawn."
