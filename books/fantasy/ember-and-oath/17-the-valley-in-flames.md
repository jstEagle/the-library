## 17. The Valley in Flames

The pigeon arrived at dawn, and it was the wrongness of it that woke Tamsin before the bird did.

He had been sleeping badly since the pyre grounds — since he had torn a royal square open like wet paper and poured a river through it, since Sefa had come out of her chains spitting fury and grief and, once they were safely away, an unexpected hug that had cracked two of his ribs and something in his chest besides. His body still ached from all of it. But it wasn't pain that pulled him up out of his bunk in the safehouse's cellar loft. It was silence.

The Emberhold's survivors had scattered to three safehouses after the burning, and this one — a decommissioned granary outside the village of Marrow's End — kept pigeons. Wren fed them every morning at first light, cooing at them in a voice she used for no one else, and the cooing was as reliable as sunrise.

This morning: no cooing.

Tamsin came down the ladder into gray light and found Wren standing very still in the middle of the dovecote, a message tube in her hands, unopened. Her face was the color of old candle wax.

"Wren?"

She didn't look at him. "It came in wrong," she said. "The seal. It's not one of ours."

"Then don't open it."

"I have to." Her thumb moved over the wax without touching it, hovering, the way people hover over a burn they already know is there. "Look at the paper, Tamsin."

He looked. The message tube was standard guild issue — he'd carried a hundred of them himself, back when carrying them was the most dangerous thing he did. But the parchment visible at the tube's mouth was wrong. It was chart-paper. Heavy, gridded, ruled for elevation lines. Nobody sent messages on chart-paper.

"Open it," he said, and hated how steady his voice came out, because inside he was already running.

* * *

The message was written in a neat surveyor's hand, the kind taught in every cartography hall in the kingdom, and it was addressed to him by name.

*Tamsin Dravenmoor, sometime of the Hollow Valley.*

*You will want to see what your ink has done.*

Beneath the words was a map. Not a copy — an original, drawn in brown-red ink that Tamsin knew in his stomach before his mind caught up, because he knew the hand. He knew the *pressure*. He had drawn it himself, months ago, back when the Emberhold was whole and Sefa had asked him to chart the valley roads for the network's couriers, and he had worked late into the night with a cut on his thumb he hadn't bothered to bandage properly, because who bothers bandaging a papercut when there is work to do?

His blood was on that chart. A smear at the margin, small as a freckle. And someone had taken it — taken the stolen chart from the ruins of the Emberhold, or taken it before the ruins, which was worse — and used it the way Vaelith had warned him blood could be used. As a thread. As a scent. As a line leading home.

Because the map on the chart was the Hollow Valley.

Every boundary stone. Every switchback trail. The millpond where he'd learned to swim. The crooked chimney of Master Ellery's cottage. The workshop with its south-facing window, drawn in his own hand, annotated in his own shorthand — and across all of it, laid over it like a shroud, fresh marks in a different ink. Gray. The gray of the echo-king's dominion, the color Wren had described from the capital, the color that ate borders and left nothing but a single obedient dominion behind.

The gray marks were moving inward. Slowly. Methodically. Like a noose being walked closed.

And beneath the map, in that same neat surveyor's hand:

*You have until the last line is drawn. Come watch, if you like. I find that makers always come to watch.*

— A.

"A," Tamsin said aloud. "Aldric. Or whatever wears him."

"It's a trap," Wren said. Obviously, immediately, the way she said everything, and then, quieter, because she was Wren and she had earned the right to say the second part too: "It's also true. Look at the dates in the margin. The gray started four days ago. That's — Tamsin, that's when we were pulling Sefa off the pyre grounds. While you were redrawing the square, he was reading your blood."

Tamsin sat down on a feed barrel because his legs had opinions of their own.

* * *

By midmorning everyone in the granary had read the letter twice and said the obvious things, and Tamsin had heard all of them and agreed with none of them in his heart.

Sefa said: "It's bait. You go, you die, and the valley burns anyway. We've lost enough." She said it with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and nobody argued with Sefa's jaw, except that Tamsin noticed she didn't look at him while she said it, and Sefa always looked at people when she meant it.

Wren said: "We could send word ahead. Riders. Warn them to evacuate." And then she bit her lip, because they both knew what warning meant — the valley couldn't evacuate. That was the entire point of the Hollow Valley. Magic stopped at its boundary stones. Its people had spent generations building lives on the certainty that nothing uncanny would ever reach them, which meant no one there had ever learned to run.

Old Bram, the network's quartermaster, a man shaped like a question mark and twice as bent, said: "Even if you could get there in time, boy, what would you do? Fight the king's army with a pencil?"

And Tamsin, who had been quiet through all of it, finally said: "Yes. Probably."

Which ended the argument for about ten seconds.

"You can't redraw an army," Sefa said.

"I redrew a royal square."

"You redrew a *square*, Tamsin. Stone and cobbles and a river you could argue was already mostly there. This is—" She stopped. Unfolded her arms. And when she looked at him at last, what was in her face wasn't anger. It was the thing under anger, which in Sefa was almost always fear wearing armor. "Your brother died because your chart led them to us. I forgave you for that because you didn't know. Because none of us knew what blood could do. But now you know. Now you *know*, and if you draw one line toward that valley, you're telling him exactly where you are and exactly what you are, and he will use you until there's nothing left to use. I watched him do it to a whole kingdom. Don't volunteer."

"I'm not volunteering to be used," Tamsin said. "I'm volunteering to be *there*. There's a difference."

"Is there?" she asked, and it wasn't cruel. It was a real question, the kind she saved for things that mattered, and it sat in the room like a stone none of them could lift.

* * *

He went looking for answers in the only place he'd ever found them, which was a mountainside cave that smelled of hot stone and old grief.

Vaelith was smaller than she had been. Tamsin noticed it the way you notice a fire dying — not all at once, but in the spaces between glances. When they'd met in the Ashreach she had filled the cavern like weather. Now she lay curled around herself like a banked hearth, scales gone dull along her spine, and the light in her throat — the guttering candle of her, the last true fire in the world — flickered longer between flares than it used to.

She heard him coming, of course. She probably heard him thinking.

"The valley," she said, before he'd cleared the entrance. Her voice was gravel and embers. "You got the letter."

"How did you—"

"Because you walk like a man carrying a map he wishes he'd never drawn." One amber eye opened, fixed on him, ancient and tired and fond. "Sit, mapmaker. Your legs want to pace and pacing won't help either of us."

Tamsin sat on the warm stone near her jaw. For a while neither of them spoke. Outside, wind moved through the pines, and somewhere below, a hawk complained about the morning.

"When I swore the oath with Aldric," Vaelith said at last, "we were young in the way that only the powerful are young. We believed a vow could hold a kingdom together like mortar holds stone. And it did — for a while. But a vow is a map too, Tamsin. Did you know that? A promise about where things are and where they must stay. And when Aldric broke his — when he chose the crown over the oath — the promise didn't die. Promises made in dragonfire don't die. It just... stopped knowing what it was for. It became a map with no territory. An oath hunting for anything it could bind." The fire in her throat pulsed, dimmed. "It has been binding ever since. And now it has found the one place in the world built to be unbindable, and it is going to erase it, and it wants you to watch, because erasing your valley is how it means to finish erasing *you*."

"That's what Sefa's afraid of."

"Sefa is afraid of the right things. It's why she's alive." The eye blinked, slow. "But she is afraid you'll be used, and I think you already know something she doesn't. Say it. Saying things makes them smaller. Usually."

Tamsin looked at his hands. Ink-stained, calloused, a scar across the left thumb where the papercut had been — the cut whose blood had threaded home to everything he loved.

"If I go," he said slowly, working it out as the words came, "and I draw — really draw, the way I drew the road, the way I drew the river through the square — then yes, he learns me. Every line teaches him my hand. But Vaelith... he's already learning me. He has my blood. He has my chart. Hiding doesn't slow him down; it just means I'm not there when the valley needs..." He stopped. Started again. "Master Ellery taught me that a map isn't the territory. It's a *promise* about the territory. And I made promises to that valley my whole life. I promised it I'd chart every trail. I promised it I'd never let it be forgotten. I just never thought keeping those promises would look like this."

"And if saving it costs you the gift? If drawing against him spends you the way fire is spending me?"

Tamsin thought about the blank page waiting at the end of every mapmaker's life anyway. He thought about Wren teaching pigeons to carry hope in tubes. He thought about Sefa's arms around his ribs, cracking them, forgiving him.

"Then it costs," he said. "Vaelith — you're planning something. I can smell the planning. Dragons are bad liars and worse at hiding it."

The great head lifted, and for a moment the old awe came back, the mountain-that-decides-to-move feeling, and Vaelith smiled the way dragons smile, which is to say with far too many teeth and, somehow, warmth.

"There is a way to end the echo," she said. "Not to fight it. To *unmake the oath it grew from*. But the oath lives in the mirror chamber, in the heart of the palace, behind wards that were old when I was young, and my fire — what's left of it — is the only flame that can burn the old words away. Which means I must come to the capital. Which means a flight I may not survive, carrying passengers I may fail, at the end of a road your enemy is watching." Her eye gleamed. "Unless, of course, we arrive somewhere he isn't watching. Unless someone could simply... draw us a better road."

Tamsin felt it land in his chest like a struck bell.

"You want me to map us into the palace."

"I want you to map us into the *story*," Vaelith said, "before he finishes mapping us out of it. But first—" and here her voice gentled, which from a dragon was more alarming than any roar, "—first you have to decide what you owe a valley that was built to hide you from yourself. Go and see it burn, if seeing is what you need. Grief keeps. It will keep until the work is done. But choose *now*, mapmaker, so that what you carry to the mirror chamber is purpose and not regret. Regret is heavy. I have carried some for three hundred years. Take it from me: pack lighter."

* * *

They flew low and fast, Vaelith hugging the terrain like a memory, and even dying she was faster than anything the crown had in its stables. They reached the ridge above the Hollow Valley on the second evening, and Tamsin slid down from her shoulders into the bracken and walked the last mile alone, because some things you do alone.

From the ridge, he could see everything.

The valley lay in its bowl of hills the way it had lain for all of his life — green, ordered, small, beloved. The millpond caught the sunset. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, domestic threads. For one wild, merciful moment he thought: *it's fine, the letter was a lie, he never came—*

And then he saw the gray.

It stood at the valley's edges like frost. A ring of it, patient and total, where the boundary stones were — and the stones themselves, he realized with a lurch, were *changing*. Their old carved sigils, the ward-marks older than anyone's grandmother, were fading. Being overwritten. The echo-king wasn't marching an army in. He was doing what Tamsin did. He was *redrawing the border*, line by careful line, and each line he finished let the gray seep further in, and inside the shrinking circle the valley went quiet in a way valleys should never be quiet. No dogs barking. No evening bells. Just smoke, rising straight up in air that had forgotten how to move.

Down in the workshop district — he could see it from here, he had mapped it a thousand times, he could count the chimneys — a light burned in the south-facing window of Master Ellery's cottage. One light. Steady. Stubborn.

"He stayed," Tamsin whispered. "You stubborn, brilliant old man. You *stayed*."

Vaelith landed behind him without sound, which should not have been possible for something her size, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the smoke wasn't already saying.

Tamsin knelt in the bracken and took out his kit with hands that shook and then, deliberately, stopped shaking. He spread a fresh sheet of chart-paper on his knee. He did not dip his pen in ink.

He pressed his thumb — the scarred one — against the nib instead, hard, until it beaded red, and he thought about what he owed, and to whom, and what a promise weighed when it was written in blood by someone who finally understood what blood was for.

"One line," he said to Vaelith. "Just one. Not to fight him. To buy them time to run. A road out of the valley that doesn't exist yet — wide enough for everyone, ending somewhere the gray hasn't touched. He'll feel me doing it. Let him. Let him learn that I don't draw walls anymore." His voice cracked, and he let it. "I draw *doors*."

He put the pen to the paper.

Far below, in the fading light, the south-facing window of a cottage in a burning valley flickered — and then, impossibly, answered: three short flashes, the old guild signal, the one every apprentice learned before they learned letters.

*Come in. All clear. Hurry.*

Tamsin stared at it, heart slamming. Master Ellery wasn't signaling a warning. He was signaling an *invitation* — which meant the old man knew. Knew Tamsin was on the ridge, knew what was coming, knew what Tamsin's pen could do, and was telling him, in the oldest language they shared:

*I see you. Get down here. Bring your maps.*

Below, the gray ring tightened another inch, and somewhere in the valley a bell began to ring — ragged, human, urgent — and Tamsin Dravenmoor drew his first line.
