## 18. What Tamsin Dravenmoor Is

The valley was still burning when Tamsin reached the boundary stones.

He had felt it before anyone told him — a pulling behind his sternum, like a thread drawn taut through his chest and yanked eastward. Wren had found him standing in the middle of the Emberhold's ruined courtyard with his hand pressed flat against his own ribs, saying nothing, and had simply begun saddling the horses. That was the thing about Wren. She never asked the question he couldn't answer. She just started packing.

Now the smoke rose over the ridge in a gray column so wide it looked like the sky itself had been wounded. The Hollow Valley — the place that had made him, that had hidden him, that he had believed for twenty-six years was the safest pocket of ground in the kingdom — was on fire.

"Stop," Wren said quietly.

"I'm not running."

"You're walking like a man who intends to walk straight into a pyre." She caught his sleeve. Her fingers were ink-stained; they always were, even now, even after everything. "Tamsin. It's done. Whatever was there to save is gone."

"It wasn't gone when it started."

"No," she agreed. "But you weren't there when it started, and you can't be un-absent now." She said it gently, the way she said most hard things, as though kindness were simply a more efficient way of being honest. "Come and look at it anyway. But come and *look*, not throw yourself in."

So they walked up together, horse and horse, and crested the ridge at dusk, and Tamsin Dravenmoor looked down at the ashes of his whole life.

* * *

It was smaller than he remembered.

That was the first absurd thought, and he almost laughed at it, because grief does that — it hands you the wrong feeling first, like a servant who brings the wrong coat. The valley where he'd spent every year of his life, the valley of green terraces and slow brown river and the workshop with its crooked chimney, looked from this height like something a child could cup in two hands. A little fold of earth. A wrinkle in the world. He had thought it vast because he had been small when he learned its shape, and he had never once re-measured himself against it.

The fire had taken the eastern woods first. He could see the black skeleton of the old mill. The workshop — Master Corren's workshop, *his* workshop, the one with the south window that caught the morning light exactly right for mixing pigments — was a charred square of foundation. The chimney still stood, which seemed like a private joke at his expense. Everything else about him burned, but the crooked chimney endured.

And the boundary stones. He searched for them out of habit before he understood what he was doing, and found them: seven gray sentinels around the valley's rim, older than any record, the stones that kept magic out. They were untouched. Of course they were. They had done their work perfectly for centuries.

They had kept magic out of the valley.

They had never once kept him *in*.

Tamsin sat down slowly on the cold grass of the ridge, and Wren sat beside him without a word, and the fire crackled far below like a hearth heard through a wall.

"I used to think," he said, "that I was the exception. The one ordinary thing in an extraordinary family of maps." He turned his satchel over in his lap. Inside it were the charts that had survived the Emberhold's burning — the road that hadn't existed until he drew it, the river he'd pulled out of the ground beneath the hunters' feet, the surveyor's credentials he'd forged for the capital. Evidence, all of it. Confessions, all of it. "I thought the gift came *to* me when I crossed the stones. Like catching a fever. Bad luck. Something that happened to me."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at those stones and doing arithmetic." He laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothing. "Wren. Magic stops at the valley's edge. Everyone knows it. The stones have held for six hundred years. Nothing gifted has ever been born inside them — the guild records say so, the dragon says so, everyone says so."

Wren went very still beside him. He watched her arrive at it a heartbeat after he had, watched her eyes widen and her mouth form the shape of the conclusion before she let any sound out.

"So either the stones failed for the first time in six hundred years," Tamsin said, "for one boy, in one valley, by accident—"

"—or they didn't fail," Wren finished softly. "Or they did exactly what they were built to do."

"A valley where magic cannot follow." He said the old phrase slowly, tasting it like an unfamiliar spice. Every child in the Hollow Valley learned it as comfort. *No witch can find you here. No curse can reach you here.* A refuge. A hiding place. "I always heard it as a wall keeping danger out. But walls keep things in too, don't they? Or—" he stopped, started again. "You don't build a hiding place unless there's something worth hiding."

Below them, a beam collapsed in the ruins with a sound like a distant door closing.

"They knew," he whispered. "Corren knew. My mother knew, maybe. Maybe the whole valley knew and drew straws over who got to raise the mapmaker's son and pretend he was ordinary." He thought of his mentor's face the morning the lake appeared — the lake Tamsin had drawn the evening before, every inlet exact, every depth sounding correct to the foot. Corren had gone white. Not amazed-white. *Recognizing*-white. The white of a man seeing a debt come due after twenty years of pretending it was forgiven. "He looked at me that day like he'd been waiting for it. Like he'd promised someone."

"Tamsin—"

"He wouldn't come with us." His voice cracked on it. When they'd fled, old Corren had stood in his doorway with his arms folded and refused, absolutely refused, to look back at them as they rode away — and Tamsin had carried that refusal like a stone ever since, turning it over, wondering if it was disappointment or cowardice or love. Now he set it down and looked at it properly for the first time. "He wasn't refusing me. He was staying at his post. You don't leave the hiding place empty. Someone has to stand at the door and keep looking ordinary." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "Gods. He stayed so I could be *boring*. Twenty years of telling me my lines were crooked and my scale bars were sloppy, and all along he was — he was teaching me craft so thoroughly I'd never notice I had power. Because a craftsman asks *is it accurate*. Only a sorcerer asks *what will it do*."

Wren put her arm through his. She smelled of woodsmoke and cheap ink and the particular iron tang of fear sweated out over many miles, and it was the best smell in the world, because it meant she was still there.

"So that's what I am," Tamsin said. "Not a man magic found on the road. Not an accident of geography." He looked down at the burning valley, at the stones that had hidden him, at the small black square where he'd learned to draw. "I'm the reason the valley exists. Or the valley is the reason I exist. Either way—" he exhaled, long and shaking, "—either way, I was never running *from* anything. I've been running *as* something this whole time. And I am so tired of pretending I don't know what."

* * *

They made camp on the ridge, because neither of them could bear to go back down the road while the valley smoked, and because Vaelith had told them to wait for her signal and neither of them trusted themselves to interpret silence correctly yet.

Sefa arrived near midnight.

She came up the trail alone, leading three horses, moving with that economical anger that was simply how she walked — Sefa, who had lost her brother to a fire lit by a chart in Tamsin's own hand, who had led the fugitives of the Emberhold out of the ashes and somehow, impossibly, kept them a network instead of a mob. She dropped the reins, looked at the burning valley, and then looked at Tamsin with an expression he had learned to dread and had lately learned to need: the expression of a woman deciding whether to tell you the truth.

"The echo-king used your blood-chart," she said. No preamble. Sefa didn't believe in preambles. "We recovered a fragment from a dead hunter. Your blood in the ink, your line-work underneath. It found the valley the way a hound finds a scent." She paused. "I'm not telling you this to hurt you."

"I know."

"But I need you to hear the rest of it, because you're going to want to drown in the first part." She crouched in front of him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the soot still ground into the creases of her knuckles. "The echo took your chart and burned a valley. One valley. My brother. One man. The gifted children in the capital. Dozens. If you spend tonight cataloguing every cost of your own hands, you'll never lift them again, and we need them lifted. So grieve — I'll sit with you while you do, I'll even be patient, which you know costs me — but you grieve *forward*. Understood?"

"That's a terrible eulogy technique."

"It's a survival technique. Eulogies are for people with time." But her voice gentled on it, and she stayed crouched there a moment longer, and then she sat down heavily in the grass on his other side, so that he was flanked — Wren on the left, Sefa on the right, the two poles of his strange new life, ink and iron.

For a while nobody spoke. The fire below settled into embers. Somewhere an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"My mother named me for a river," Tamsin said at last. It came out of nowhere and he let it. "Tamsin. There's a river in the old tongue — Tam, the slow one, the one that goes around obstacles instead of through them. She thought it was funny. A cartographer's son named for water, because water's the only thing that draws its own map." He shook his head. "I used to think the valley shaped me. Made me careful, made me quiet, made me a man who measures twice. And it did. But it wasn't shaping an ordinary man into a safe shape. It was shaping *this*—" he spread his hands, ink-stained, burn-blistered, trembling slightly, "—into something that could survive being what it is. The patience isn't a cage, Sefa. It's a scabbard."

"Did you just call yourself a sword?" Sefa asked. "Because if you've started calling yourself a sword I'm going to need to lie down."

"A map," Tamsin said. "I was going to say a map. Maps are patient things. They wait hundreds of years in archives for the exact moment someone needs to know the way." He looked at her. "Vaelith said the kingdom is being redrawn. The echo-king is erasing borders, folding everything into one gray dominion, unmaking every promise this land ever made to itself. And the only counter to a false map is a true one. That's why it's me. That was always why it was me. The oath broke, and the world grew a scar over the break, and the scar grew a valley, and the valley grew—" he touched his own chest, wonderingly, "—me. I'm not collateral. I'm the answer the question took six hundred years to write."

Wren made a small sound. When he glanced at her she was crying silently and trying to hide it by looking very intently at the fire, and when she noticed him noticing she said, thickly, "You're allowed to say 'I'm a person with a gift,' you know. You don't have to be a geological event."

"I'm both," Tamsin said. "It's very exhausting."

That startled a laugh out of both of them — wet, ragged, real — and Sefa thumped him on the shoulder hard enough to mean *welcome back from wherever you went*.

* * *

Near dawn, the sky above the eastern peaks turned the color of hammered copper, and Vaelith came.

She flew low and slow these days — the great wings beat half as often as they should, and the fire that guttered along her throat flickered like a lamp running dry. Even dying, even diminished, she blotted out the stars as she passed, and when she landed on the ridge before them the ground remembered what it was to be humble. Up close, her scales had gone the gray of old ash, except along her spine, where a thread of ember-light still ran like a fuse waiting for a spark.

She looked at the smoking valley for a long time. Dragons, Tamsin had learned, did not rush their looking.

"So," she said at last. Her voice was deep enough to feel in the teeth. "The hollow place burns. I swore an oath on that ground once, little mapmaker. Did you know that? Before your guilds, before your kingdoms wore their current shapes. Aldric and I, and a third whose name I will not speak tonight, and the vow we swore there was meant to hold a kingdom's heart shut against exactly the thing now wearing its crown." Her ember-lit eye fixed on him, ancient and terrible and, strangely, kind. "When the oath broke, the breaking had to go somewhere. Power does not vanish. It pools. It hides. It waits in the one place in the world where nothing can touch it and grow."

"The valley," Tamsin said.

"The valley. And what grew in the valley." She lowered her great head until her breath stirred his hair — warm, smelling of coal and rain. "I have watched you since the Ashreach, child. I named you mapmaker because I thought you would need the title before you needed the truth. The truth is larger. You are not a man the magic found. You are the magic's own answer, grown patient in the dark, taught to draw straight lines by a good old man who loved you enough to lie to you for twenty years." A rumble moved through her, and it took Tamsin a moment to recognize it as a dragon's laugh. "He wrote to me once, you know. Your Corren. Thirty years ago, asking whether the wardens' work was done or whether the seed should stay planted. I told him: let the boy be boring a while longer. The world will make its claim soon enough."

"You — he *wrote* to you—"

"Dragons get letters. It's one of the few perks." She straightened, and the humor drained out of her like water from a cracked bowl, leaving the gravity beneath. "Hear me now, Tamsin Dravenmoor, because the hour is short and my fire shorter. The echo-king has your blood. With it, it can map *you* — find you anywhere, anticipate you, and worse, begin to rewrite you, the way it rewrites borders. Every day you remain scattered, it tightens its grip on your line. And my fire — what remains of it — must burn the old oath away in the mirror chamber, or Aldric dies inside his own broken word and the echo wears him forever."

"So we fly," Tamsin said. "Tonight. Now."

"Now," Vaelith agreed. Then she paused, and her head tilted with something almost like delicacy. "But first — say it. Not to me. To yourself. I have flown with heroes who died mid-battle because they were still arguing with their own names. Say what you are, and mean it, and then mount up."

Tamsin looked past her, one last time, at the valley. The fires had burned down to a dull red glow along the terraces, like a forge at rest. The crooked chimney stood black against the dawn. Somewhere under that ash was the lake he had dreamed into being, the workshop, the south window, the boy who had believed he was ordinary and had been so carefully, so lovingly deceived. He would carry none of it forward in his hands. He would carry all of it in his lines, the way a map carries a country — reduced, essential, true.

"I'm Tamsin Dravenmoor," he said, and his voice didn't shake. "Son of the Hollow Valley, apprentice of Corren, friend of Wren, debtor to Sefa, and student of the last dragon in the world. I was born magic and raised to be a mapmaker, and it turns out those were the same lesson. I'm not running anymore. I'm drawing." He turned to Vaelith, and found he was smiling, tired and fierce and certain. "Show me where the lines need redrawing."

The dragon's ember-thread flared — briefly, brilliantly, a candle remembering it had once been a star.

"Hold tight to the one with the ink," Vaelith said, lowering her shoulder. "We fly for the capital. And little mapmaker — when we get there, the echo will show you a version of yourself you'll barely recognize. Remember, standing in front of it, that you already know who you are."

"That's the trick, isn't it," Tamsin said, swinging up onto the warm gray scales behind her shoulders, Wren's arms locking around his waist, Sefa shouting orders to the network below. "Everything, in the end, is knowing where you are on the map."

Vaelith gathered herself, wings opening against the brightening sky like the pages of an enormous book.

"Hold on," she said. "We're going off the edge of it."
