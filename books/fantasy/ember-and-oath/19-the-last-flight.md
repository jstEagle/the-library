## 19. The Last Flight

Wren had always assumed that if she ever rode a dragon, it would feel like triumph. Songs said so. The old ballads all agreed: fire above, wind beneath, the rider's heart lifted like a lark.

The songs had not mentioned the smell.

Vaelith smelled like a hearth the morning after a house fire — char and ash and something underneath, older, like sun-warmed stone in a place no one had lived for a thousand years. Wren sat wedged between two of the great neck-spines, knees pressed against scales gone dull as old coins, and tried very hard to think about anything except how high up they were.

"You're gripping my third spine hard enough to crack it," Vaelith rumbled. Her voice came from everywhere, from the hollow of her chest and the long throat ahead of them, resonant as a bell rung in an empty cathedral. "I have carried knights in full plate who were gentler."

"Knights in full plate hadn't spent three days watching the world turn gray," Wren said.

Below them, the kingdom was coming apart at its edges. Not burning — burning would have been easier to look at. Burning had color, had heat, had a shape you could fight. This was worse. From this height Wren could see the borders of the map rewriting themselves, the green of the Weald fading to a flat, patient gray, rivers straightening like someone had taken a ruler to them, villages blurring into neat identical clusters. The echo-king was redrawing the realm, and he was doing it the way a clerk does sums: thoroughly, without passion, without mercy.

"He's fast," Tamsin said beside her. He lay half across Vaelith's shoulder, chin on his folded arms, staring down at the graying land with an expression Wren had learned to dread — the one where his eyes went distant and his fingers twitched, sketching invisible lines against the scale beneath him. "Faster than I thought he'd be. Look at the Thornmark border. That was still contested yesterday."

"It is not contested now," Vaelith said. "Nothing will be contested soon. That is rather the point of him." She banked, slow and careful, and Wren felt the enormous effort of it in the way her wingbeats stuttered — once, twice — before finding their rhythm again. Three days ago her fire had been a guttering candle. Now it was a candle in a drafty room, cupped by desperate hands.

"How are you doing?" Tamsin asked quietly. "Honestly."

"I am dying, mapmaker. I have been dying since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. What has changed is the schedule." A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, thin and gray as everything below. "Ask me again when we land. If we land."

"That's comforting," Wren muttered.

"I did not become the last dragon by being comforting. I became the last dragon by being right, and being right is rarely comfortable for anyone involved."

Tamsin huffed something that might have been a laugh. Wren was grateful for it. In the three days since the valley burned — since they'd stood on a ridge and watched the smoke rise from the only home Tamsin had ever known — she hadn't heard him laugh at all. She'd heard him not-sleep, and not-eat, and sit by the fire drawing the same small map of the valley over and over until Sefa gently took the charcoal out of his hand.

Sefa wasn't with them. Someone had to hold what remained of the network together, and Sefa had looked at Tamsin across the ashes of the Emberhold's second burning and said, "You go do the impossible thing. I'll do the merely difficult ones." Then she'd gripped Wren's shoulder hard enough to bruise and said, quieter, "Bring him back. Both of you. I've buried enough people this season."

So now it was the three of them: a dying dragon, a mapmaker whose gift was spending itself like a candle burned at both ends, and Wren, who had survived the Cartographers' Guild purge by hiding under a floorboard for two days and had been running ever since. She kept waiting to feel like the wrong person for this. She'd felt like the wrong person for everything since the edict came down. But nobody had offered to swap her out, and there was no time to train a replacement, and besides — she knew the capital. She knew the survey offices, the guild archives, the mirror chamber's location in the palace's oldest wing, which every apprentice learned as a ghost story and which she now understood had never been a story about ghosts at all.

The light was going. It went early these days; the echo's gray drank the sunset like wine.

"We'll have to fly through the dark," Vaelith said. "I would prefer not to. My eyes are not what they were, and neither, frankly, is the rest of me."

"Can you rest?" Tamsin asked.

"There is a hollow in the Grayfell crags. Old. Mine, once, in the way that a grave belongs to the person in it." A pause, long enough that Wren wondered if she'd finished speaking. "We will rest there. And then, at first light — or whatever passes for first light under that thing's sky — we fly the last stretch."

* * *

The hollow was exactly as cheerful as advertised, which is to say it was a cave. But it was dry, and the walls held the day's warmth, and when Vaelith coiled herself at the entrance with her head resting on her forepaws like an enormous exhausted hound, Wren found a niche that fit her back and let herself slide down it until she was sitting.

Tamsin didn't sit. He stood at the cave mouth, looking north toward the capital, and Wren watched him take out the stub of charcoal he apparently kept hidden from himself the way other men hid flasks of drink.

"Don't," she said.

He glanced back. "I'm not going to draw anything. I just think better with it in my hand."

"You've been drawing in your sleep. Sefa told me. Little maps, on the stone, on your own arm. Your gift is nearly gone, Tam, and every scrap you spend—"

"—is a scrap spent on something that matters." He turned fully around then, and in the dim light his face was hollowed and stubborn and so tired that Wren's anger collapsed into something softer. "Vaelith says her fire can burn the oath away. But someone has to redraw it while it burns. Line by line. A vow of dominion into a vow of stewardship. Do you know how many words are in a royal oath? I counted. There are ninety-one. I've been practicing the substitutions for three days." He held up his forearm, where faint charcoal ghosts of phrases overlapped each other like sediment: *to hold* struck through, *to keep* written above it. *To bind* struck through, *to serve*. *Mine* struck through, again and again and again, *ours* written in its place until the skin was gray with it.

Wren crossed the cave and sat down next to him at the mouth, shoulder to shoulder. Below, far off, the capital glowed — not with lamplight but with that flat, sourceless gray luminescence, like a city seen through fogged glass.

"Ninety-one words," she said. "And you're going to rewrite them while a dragon burns the original out of reality, inside a sealed mirror chamber, guarded by the thing wearing the king's face."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"It sounds bad in every register, Tam. I've tried singing it."

That got the laugh out of him — small, cracked, real. He knocked his shoulder against hers. "You've gotten braver since the floorboards."

"I haven't gotten braver. I've gotten tired of running. It's different. Braver implies you stop being afraid." She pulled her knees up. "I'm still afraid of basically everything. I'm afraid of the echo, and I'm afraid your plan won't work, and I'm privately afraid that if it does work, you'll be standing in that chamber with nothing left — no gift, no maps, no valley — and you won't know who you are anymore."

Tamsin was quiet for a while. Out in the dark, wind moved through the crags with a sound almost like breathing.

"Sefa asked me something, before we left," he said finally. "She asked if I'd do it all again. The road, the Emberhold, all of it — knowing the valley would burn." His thumb moved over the charcoal stub, turning it. "And I wanted to lie, because the true answer felt like a betrayal of everyone I grew up with. But the true answer is yes. Because if I'd stayed, I'd have died never knowing what I was. And the people I met on the road—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way he talked when he was mapping something delicate. "You don't find belonging on parchment, Wren. I spent twenty years trying. You find it walking next to someone who's also afraid of basically everything."

Wren discovered her eyes were stinging. "That was almost poetic. Are you feeling well?"

"Dying dragon, doomed kingdom, imminent apocalypse. I've decided to live a little."

From the cave entrance came a sound like a landslide clearing its throat, which was Vaelith laughing.

* * *

Wren woke to silence, and knew immediately that something was wrong, because Vaelith's breathing filled any space she occupied the way a bell fills a tower. The silence meant the dragon had gone outside.

She found her at the crag's edge, head raised, staring north. The gray glow over the capital had changed. It pulsed now, slow and steady, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

"The borders are closed," Vaelith said, without turning. "All of them. An hour ago the last open road into the city sealed itself. He knows we're coming."

"Of course he knows. He has Tamsin's blood-chart." Wren's stomach tightened around the old guilt. The stolen chart — drawn in Tamsin's own hand, carrying his own blood from a cut palm, the night hunters had used it to find the Emberhold. The echo had taken it from the ruins and had been mapping *him* with it ever since. Every step Tamsin took, the echo could read.

"Yes. And yet." Vaelith's tail moved, a slow sweep of dust. "Come here, little scribe. Look at what I'm looking at."

Wren climbed up onto the warm stone beside her foreleg and looked. At first she saw nothing but the pulsing gray. Then she saw it: threads of gold, hair-fine, moving through the gray like veins through marble. They radiated outward from the capital in every direction — the rewritten borders, the new roads, the straightened rivers. All of it anchored, all of it fed, from the center.

From the mirror chamber.

"Every line he draws runs back to the oath," Vaelith said. "He cannot help it. He *is* the oath — a vow of dominion given legs and a crown. Cut the anchor and the whole web goes slack." Her voice dropped, and for the first time since Wren had known her, the ancient dragon sounded old. Not weary. Old, the way bedrock is old. "My fire will cut it. What remains of it. Enough, I think, for one oath. It was always going to be enough for exactly one."

"Vaelith—"

"Do not." Gentle, but final, like a door closing. "I have flown nine hundred years, little scribe. I have outlived my clutch, my mate, my riders, and every promise I made them. Tomorrow I get to keep the last one. Do you understand what a gift that is? Most creatures die mid-sentence." She lowered her great head until one ancient eye — gold shot through with gray, like autumn light failing — regarded Wren directly. "But I have a request, and it is not a small one."

"Anything."

"When the fire takes me — and it will be quick, dragons do not linger — Tamsin will want to stop. He will want to grieve instead of work, because he is kind, and kindness hesitates. You must not let him. Ninety-one words, Wren. He rewrites them while the old ones burn, or the burning takes Aldric too, and everything we have done ends as nothing but a very dramatic funeral." The eye blinked, slow. "Can you do that? Can you be cruel to him for five minutes, in service of everything?"

Wren thought of Sefa's grip on her shoulder. *Bring him back.* She thought of the pyre grounds, of children marching, of every gray mile spreading under that pulsing light.

"I can be cruel to anyone for five minutes," she said. "It's the other hours that frighten me."

Vaelith laughed again, softly this time, and smoke curled up into the dark. "Good. Then we are ready, as much as anyone has ever been ready for the end of anything."

* * *

They flew the last stretch in the hour before dawn, such dawn as remained.

The gray had eaten even the stars near the capital. Vaelith flew low, skimming the dead-flat fields, wings beating in that labored rhythm that Wren had stopped pretending not to count. Behind them the sky was still honest — a thin band of true darkness, a few stubborn stars — and Wren kept turning to look at it, memorizing it, the way you memorize a face at a departure gate.

Tamsin sat forward against her spines, charcoal in hand despite everything, murmuring his ninety-one words like a rosary. Wren listened, and corrected him twice — he kept transposing *stewardship* and *wardship*, and the difference mattered, Vaelith had been very clear that it mattered — and somewhere past the ruined watchtowers of the outer ring, he reached back without looking and found her hand and held it.

"For luck," he said.

"You don't believe in luck. You believe in surveying."

"I believe in you," he said simply, "and you once told me you survive on luck and spite."

"That's accurate."

"Then hold on to both. We're going to need them."

The capital rose out of the gray like a tooth. Its walls were wrong — too smooth, too uniform, the crenellations melted into clean unbroken lines, as if someone had taken an eraser to seven hundred years of history. And above the palace, at the heart of it, the gray light gathered and pulsed, and pulsed, and pulsed.

Vaelith rose in one last effortful climb, banking wide of the walls, and Wren felt the dragon gather herself — not just muscle and wing, but will, nine hundred years of it funneling toward a single sealed chamber in the oldest wing of a stolen palace.

"Mapmaker," Vaelith said. "Scribe. When we strike the roof of the mirror chamber, there will be no second pass. Whatever happens after, happens in the space we make, and it happens *now*." A pause. Wind screamed past. Far below, tiny figures spilled from barracks gates, pointing up. Somewhere, alarm bells that should have been bronze rang flat and toneless. "I want you both to know that of all the creatures I have carried — and I have carried kings — you two argued the least and complained the most, and I have loved you for it."

Tamsin's grip on Wren's hand went tight.

"Vaelith," he started, "when this is over—"

"When this is over," she said, "you will build me a grove, and you will argue with Wren about its dimensions, and I will not be there to enjoy it, and that is *fine*. Some promises are made to be kept by the living." Her wings beat once, twice, gathering the last of everything. Ahead, the palace roof rushed closer, and the pulse of gray light quickened like a heart that knew it was about to be touched. "Now. Ninety-one words, mapmaker. Say them with me one last time."

And as the dawn that wasn't dawn broke gray over the capital, the last dragon in the world folded her wings and fell toward the heart of the thing wearing the crown, and Wren — terrified, grieving, certain — began to recite.
