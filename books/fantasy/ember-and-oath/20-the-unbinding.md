## 20. The Unbinding

The mirror chamber had no door.

That was the first thing Tamsin understood about it, standing in the corridor beneath the palace with Wren's hand white-knuckled around his sleeve: there was no door because the thing inside did not believe anyone should ever leave. The wall before them was seamless gray stone, and set into it — floor to ceiling, edge to edge — was a single mirror so old and so vast that its silver had begun to tarnish in rivers, like a map of a country that no longer existed.

"He's in there," Wren whispered. "Aldric. Sefa said the defectors called it the room without hinges."

"Then we'll have to be impolite." Tamsin pressed his palm flat against the glass. It was warm. That was the worst part — not cold like dead silver ought to be, but warm, like skin, like something sleeping under a blanket of its own reflection. "Vaelith," he said quietly, "whenever you're ready."

The fire came first as light.

It poured down the corridor behind them — not the roaring furnace-terror of the stories, but a thin, guttering gold, the last ember of the last dragon carried in the cupped hands of a girl who had walked beside him since she collapsed bleeding at his door. Wren held it the way you hold a candle through wind: hunched over it, shielding it with her whole body, her burned hands wrapped in linen that was already scorching.

"It won't last," Vaelith had told them on the flight, her voice like a landslide heard from very far away. "I have perhaps one flame left in me, mapmaker. Spend it well. I would rather it went to unmaking a lie than to warming my own death."

Now that flame stood in the corridor and made their shadows enormous, and Tamsin looked at his own shadow thrown against the mirror — a tall thin shape with ink-stained fingers — and thought, absurdly, of his mentor back in the Hollow Valley, who used to say that a cartographer's first duty was honesty about scale.

All right, he thought. Honest scale. One man, one girl, one dying dragon's spark, against an oath old enough to have forgotten it was ever words.

He drew a breath. He took out his pen.

* * *

The glass recognized him before he touched it again. The tarnish-rivers in the silver began to move, flowing backward, gathering, and the surface cleared the way a fever breaks — all at once, and showing what had been underneath the whole time.

The chamber beyond was round and small and terribly ordinary. A table. A chair. A window painted shut. And on the table, spread open and weighted down with a king's signet ring, lay a document written on vellum gone brown as autumn.

And kneeling beside the table, forehead nearly touching the floor, was a man made half of ash.

"Aldric," Tamsin breathed.

The king raised his head. What was left of his face was gaunt and gray and gentle, and his eyes were the eyes Tamsin had seen in the vision weeks ago — hollowed, exhausted, lit from somewhere very far back by a stubborn coal of a self that refused, after everything, to go out. His lips moved. Through the glass, sound came warped, like a voice underwater:

"*Finish the oath.*"

"No," Tamsin said. "I've come to do something worse. I've come to *edit* it."

The echo arrived the way weather arrives.

It did not come through the door — there was no door — it simply condensed out of the mirror itself, rising off the silver like steam, assembling itself into the shape of a king: broad shoulders, crowned head, a face that was Aldric's face worn smooth of every human thing, the way a coin wears smooth in ten thousand pockets. When it smiled, Tamsin felt the smile land on his skin like frost.

*Mapmaker.* Its voice filled the corridor and the chamber both, one voice in two places. *You came. I hoped you would. I have been so looking forward to finishing your chart.*

Tamsin's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with fear. "My chart?"

*You bled on your own work, little cartographer.* The echo drifted closer to the glass, and where its reflection passed, the tarnish returned, spreading like rot. *Blood is ink to things like us. Every line you drew for us, we learned you. Your roads. Your shortcuts. The valley where you hid.* It tilted its smooth head. *We are burning it now, incidentally. We thought you'd want to know.*

Wren made a small broken sound. Tamsin didn't let himself hear it. Later, he told himself. Grieve later. There was a discipline to mapping, he was learning, and the first rule was: *the territory does not care about your feelings. Work anyway.*

"You found the valley," he said, "because I bled on a map. Fine. Then understand this." He lifted the pen, and the echo's attention snapped to it like a hawk to a hare. "Everything I draw, I can redraw. You're made of an oath. An oath is just words that someone promised too hard. And words—" he smiled, and it was not a kind smile, though it was, he hoped, a warm one underneath, "—words are my whole profession."

*You cannot destroy me,* the echo said, and for the first time something flickered under the smoothness. Something quick and frightened. *I am his vow. To destroy me you must destroy him.*

"I know," Tamsin said softly. "That's why I'm not going to destroy you."

He turned to Wren. "Fire. Now. On my mark — and Wren? Whatever happens, whatever you see — keep burning until I say stop. Even if I scream. *Especially* if I scream."

"That's a horrible instruction," Wren said, "and I'm going to follow it, and I want you to know I hate you a little."

"Noted. Mark."

She opened her scorched hands, and Vaelith's last flame rose off her palms like a bird leaving a nest — small, gold, trembling, and absolutely certain. It slid through the mirror as though the glass were water, and settled above the vellum on the table in the chamber beyond, waiting, a candle-flame the size of a promise.

Tamsin put his pen to the mirror.

* * *

Here is what no one had told him, because no one had ever tried it: writing on the world feels like being written on.

The first stroke went through the silver and into the oath beneath, and Tamsin felt it in his bones, in his teeth, in the scar-tissue of every choice he'd ever made. The vellum in the far chamber glowed where his line landed, and letters surfaced in the brown old script — the original words, the ones Aldric had spoken over a broken kingdom twenty years ago, swearing, in grief and fury, that no power would ever again threaten his people, that he would root out magic itself, that he would make the realm *safe*, safe, safe, safe, the word repeated until it stopped meaning anything and became only hunger.

*I will burn the gifted from my borders,* the old vow read. *I will hunt them to the last child. My realm will be clean.*

"There it is," Tamsin whispered. "There's the poison in the well."

*Careful, mapmaker,* the echo murmured, circling behind him inside the glass, its reflection pacing his. *Every word you change, you change him. Cut out the vow and you cut out the man. They grew together. Would you like to see how deep the roots go?*

And it showed him.

The mirror flooded with images — Aldric young, kneeling at a graveside; Aldric swearing the oath with Vaelith's fire on his tongue, because oaths need witnesses and dragons were the oldest witnesses of all; Aldric waking the next morning with something new sitting behind his eyes, patient and smooth, whispering *more, safer, cleaner* — and Tamsin saw the trap of it, the horrible elegant trap. The echo wasn't a parasite. It was a promise. You couldn't kill a promise. You could only keep it or change it.

"So don't cut the roots," Tamsin said aloud, mostly to himself, in the voice his mentor used for talking himself across a bad bridge. "Transplant them."

He began to write.

Not over the old words — *through* them, each new letter laid into the grooves the old ones had worn, the way a road follows a riverbed because the riverbed is already there. Where the vow said *burn*, he wrote *warm*. Where it said *hunt*, he wrote *shelter*. Where it said *clean*, that terrible sterile word, he wrote *tended*, and felt the word resist him like a mule resisting a halter, twenty years of momentum pushing back up his arm and into his chest.

The pain started in his fingertips.

It climbed. It climbed the way floodwater climbs — deceptively, then all at once. Each rewritten word cost him something he could feel leaving: the memory of the smell of the Hollow Valley after rain; the exact blue of the lake he'd drawn into being at nineteen; the name of the boundary stone his mentor had leaned against while refusing to look back. The gift was paying for the edit out of his own ledger, and the ledger was long, and the echo knew it.

*Stop,* the echo crooned, watching him bleed words into the world. *Look at yourself. You are erasing your own country to save a stranger. Is that stewardship, mapmaker? Or is it just another man deciding what gets to exist?*

Tamsin laughed — a ragged, gasping sound, because laughing hurt, everything hurt — and kept writing.

"That's the difference between us," he managed. "You never asked anyone. I'm asking *him*."

And through the glass, through the ash and the ruin of him, King Aldric looked up.

* * *

Later — much later, when Wren told the story in the schoolroom, and always got this part slightly wrong — people would ask how a bound man, half-consumed, kneeling in a sealed room, could help. And Wren would shrug and say, *you've clearly never watched someone decide*, which was true and also not the whole truth.

The whole truth was this: Aldric had spent twenty years screaming inside his own skull, and no one had ever once addressed the scream. Everyone fought the echo. Everyone pitied the king. No one had knelt down next to the king and said, *your vow was love, it was only ever love, it just curdled — and curdled milk can still be made into cheese, give me a moment, I know that sounds mad.*

Tamsin wrote the last lines kneeling, because his legs had gone, pressing the pen against the glass with both hands while Wren held him upright under the shoulders and swore steadily and creatively at the echo, which was doing more for morale than either of them acknowledged.

The final passage read:

*I will keep my realm as a keeper keeps a garden — not clean, but tended. Not safe, but loved. The gifted are my people. The strange is my soil. Nothing in my borders shall be burned for being other than I expected, for I am a king, and a king is a servant with a crown, and I am done mistaking the crown for the hand that holds it.*

The old words underneath began to smoke.

Vaelith's flame, waiting all this while with the patience of a creature that had lived four thousand years, leaned down and touched the vellum, and the original oath caught — not with roar but with a soft golden sigh, the way a hearth catches, the way a home catches, warmth eating the old cold words line by line while the new ones sank into the parchment like seeds into spring ground.

The echo screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a promise coming apart, of a rope fraying strand by strand, of twenty years of *safe safe safe* collapsing into a question it had never once asked itself: *safe for whom?* It thrashed against the inside of the mirror, its smooth face cracking like lake ice, and beneath the cracks Tamsin saw — he would remember this all his life — relief. Underneath all that polish, the thing had been tired. Promises get tired too, when no one lets them rest.

"You kept your vow," Tamsin told it, panting, bleeding from the nose, smiling anyway. "You kept it so hard you forgot what it was *for*. So I'm giving it back. Go on. Be finished."

The echo looked at him with Aldric's eyes one last time — and bowed, actually bowed, a king acknowledging a debt — and came apart into gold dust and morning light, and the light went up through the painted-shut window as though windows had never been shut at all.

* * *

Silence. Then a sound Tamsin had not dared hope for: coughing. Wet, human, undignified coughing, followed by a voice cracked and rusted saying, "Is — is someone there? There was a dragon. There was a dragon and then there was — oh. Oh, no. How long. How long has it *been*."

Tamsin got his legs under him — barely — and pressed his hand to the glass one final time. It was cool now. Dead silver, honest silver. And in it he caught his own reflection and stopped, because something was missing. Not from his face. From *behind* his eyes. The sense he'd carried since the valley, faint as a second heartbeat, of lines waiting to be drawn, of places leaning toward him wanting to exist.

He reached, instinctively, for the map of the palace in his satchel — the real one, ink on paper — and pushed, the way he'd pushed a hundred times.

Nothing. Warm paper. Just paper.

"Oh," he said quietly. He found he was smiling. "Oh, good."

"Tamsin?" Wren's arm was still around him, and her voice wobbled. "Tamsin, your hands —"

He looked down. His fingertips were gray as ash, the color bleached out of them, and they ached the way old scars ache before rain, and he suspected they always would. A fair price. He flexed them, winced, and decided they'd learn to hold a pen again even if the pens only ever wrote things that stayed written.

The mirror shivered. Not with magic — with plain mechanical weight, stone grinding on stone — and a seam appeared in the wall where no seam had been, and swung inward, and the room without hinges opened onto the corridor like a fist unclenching.

King Aldric stood in the doorway.

He was thin as winter and dressed in ash-colored robes and he was crying without any attempt at royal composure, great silent gulping tears, and when he saw Tamsin he tried to kneel, and Tamsin — whose legs were also barely working — caught him, and the two of them ended up in an extremely undignified heap in the doorway of the most feared room in the kingdom, holding each other up, laughing and sobbing in roughly equal measure.

"The valley," Aldric rasped against Tamsin's shoulder. "Your valley. Tell me I'm not too late. Tell me there's still time —"

Tamsin went still.

Because he had felt it, hadn't he, in the moment the gift left him — the last thing it had done, spending itself down to nothing, snapping taut like a cut rope — and he had been so busy surviving that he hadn't let himself read the message in it. But a mapmaker reads. It's the whole profession.

The rope had pointed north. Toward home. Toward smoke.

"Wren," Tamsin said, hauling the king upright, his voice steadier than he felt, steadier than it had any right to be. "Get Sefa's people. Get every runner in the network. The echo mapped my blood to find the valley, and the edict's officers will still be carrying out orders no one's rescinded — a king waking up doesn't stop fires already lit." He grabbed his satchel, the ordinary satchel, full of ordinary maps that would never come true, and slung it on. "We ride tonight. I'm going home one last time — not to save it."

Aldric stared at him. "Then to do what?"

Tamsin looked at his gray fingertips, and thought of a mentor who wouldn't look back, and a lake that had risen exactly as drawn, and a life built in a place designed to hide a boy from what he was — and found, to his own surprise, that his eyes were dry.

"To bury it properly," he said. "And then to build something better on the ashes. I hear the king's rather good at founding things now."

Behind them, unnoticed, on the table in the empty mirror chamber, the re-written oath lay glowing softly in the dark — and in the margin, in handwriting no one in the room possessed, one final line had appeared, small and neat, like a surveyor's note:

*Boundaries subject to revision. All maps are drafts.*
