## 21. Ashes and Atonement

The first thing Aldric did as a free man was weep into his hands for the better part of an hour, and the second thing he did was ask for paper.

Not parchment. Not the heavy vellum of royal decrees, stamped with seals that had signed death warrants in a voice not entirely his own. Paper — the cheap, fibrous kind surveyors used, the kind Tamsin Dravenmoor had pressed into his shaking hands before leaving the palace with ink still under his fingernails.

"Write it yourself," the mapmaker had said. "Every word. If you let the chancellery draft your pardon, they'll bury exceptions in it like seeds in winter soil. Exceptions grow."

So Aldric sat at a plain table in a plain room — he had refused the throne room; he could not yet look at the throne without hearing that other voice echo off its gilding — and wrote:

*Let it be known that no person shall be seized, tried, or burned for the gift of magic within these borders, now or hereafter.*

He stopped. Read it back. The words looked small on the page, absurdly small, as though six lines of ink could outweigh ten years of pyres. He thought of the children marched to the pyre grounds, of the smoke that had hung over the capital like weather. He thought of Vaelith, whose fire had burned itself to nothing so that he could sit here, whole, weeping, holding a pen.

Six lines would have to be enough. They would have to become more than enough.

He added: *And let all edicts issued under the sign of the Hunt be struck from the law entire, root and branch, as though they had never been spoken.*

Then, because his hand was steady and because lying to himself was one indulgence he had sworn off along with the crown's comfortable lies, he added a third line in smaller script:

*As though they had never been spoken — knowing full well they were spoken, and by me, and that no decree can unmake what memory holds.*

He signed it *Aldric*, only that. No regnal number. No style or title. When the chancellery scribes saw it, three of them fainted and one resigned on the spot, which Aldric privately considered a fair average.

* * *

Sefa Emberhold read the pardon four times, looking for the trap.

She found none, which was somehow worse. Sefa had spent nine years learning that documents from the crown were traps with good penmanship. She turned the page toward the window light, held it at an angle, checked for watermarks that might dissolve the words overnight. Nothing. Just the king's plain signature and, beneath it, a postscript in the same hand:

*To the survivor Sefa: I do not ask forgiveness. I ask for a seat at the council I am founding, because justice written without the burned will burn again. Come or don't. But come.*

"He signs his name like a man apologizing," said Wren, peering over her shoulder. The young cartographer had taken to haunting Sefa's doorway since the rescue, ostensibly to deliver reports, actually because the Emberhold's common room reminded her too much of the guildhall she'd lost. "Look at the descender on the R. It's practically groveling."

"It should grovel," Sefa said. Then, because Wren was eighteen and had earned gentleness: "It's a start, is what it is."

"A start." Wren made a face. "My master used to say every map is a promise. Is this a promise?"

Sefa folded the pardon carefully and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, where she kept things she hadn't decided to trust yet. "It's a survey," she said. "Of ground nobody's mapped before. And I've never once trusted a map I didn't help draw."

She went to find Tamsin.

* * *

Aldric expected anger from the survivors, and received it, and deserved it, and did not flinch from it — mostly. What he had not expected was the questions.

They came to the palace in ones and twos at first, then dozens: the gifted who had hidden in cellars and hedgerows and hollow hills while his edicts hunted them. A woman who could coax fruit trees into bearing out of season. Twin boys who spoke to crows. An old man whose gift was so modest — he could warm tea with his palms — that he had been arrested anyway, because the Hunt had stopped distinguishing degrees long ago and simply burned whatever it caught.

They stood in his audience chamber — he had finally consented to use it, because meeting them in a corridor felt like an apology whispered through a wall — and they asked him things no courtier had ever dared.

"Why didn't you stop it?" asked the tea-warming old man, whose name was Hobben and who had buried two daughters under false names.

"I tried," Aldric began.

"You didn't try hard enough to die trying," Hobben said, and the chamber went very quiet, and Aldric found he could not argue. There had been a night, early in the binding, when the echo's voice had been thin and he had been strong, when he might have thrown himself from the highest tower and shattered the oath with his own death. He had not. He had been afraid. And his fear had bought the echo ten years and the pyres a thousand lives.

"No," Aldric said at last. "I didn't. I'm sorry is a small word for it. I'll spend the rest of my life making it larger."

Hobben studied him for a long moment. Then the old man held out his hands, palms up, and warmed them at nothing, the way he'd done for his daughters on cold nights. "Tea's better warm," he said. "So is mercy. Don't let yours go cold, boy. I'll be watching."

It was not forgiveness. Aldric understood that. It was something rarer and harder: supervision. He would take it.

* * *

The council met for the first time in the autumn, in a hall that had once stored the Hunt's records — Sefa had insisted on that particular cruelty of symbolism, and Aldric had agreed before she finished suggesting it. The ledgers of the condemned were stacked in the corner, awaiting the shredders, and the smell of old ash seemed to live in the walls no matter how many times the windows were opened.

Nine seats. Three for the crown's remnant, six for the gifted and their allies. Sefa took hers at the far end, arms crossed, and watched Aldric struggle through the opening formalities with the expression of a woman grading an exam she expected him to fail.

He did not fail, exactly. But when he proposed that the council's first act be a public accounting — every name the Hunt had taken, read aloud in the capital square, one by one, for as many days as it took — Sefa raised her hand.

"Amendment," she said. "We read the names. All of them. But we don't stop there. After each name, we say what they loved. My brother Bren — you'll find him in your ledgers, Your Majesty, entry four hundred and eleven — Bren loved bad poetry and worse cooking, and he once tried to teach a goat to swim. That goes in the record too. They don't get to be just names. Names are what the Hunt left us. We're putting the rest back."

The hall was silent. Aldric bowed his head. "Amendment accepted," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and nobody pretended not to notice, and nobody minded.

The reading took nineteen days. On the fourth day, rain fell, and no one left the square. On the eleventh day, a woman heard her son's name and what he loved — carved whistles, herons, the smell of cut grass — and fainted, and was caught by six strangers before she touched the ground. On the nineteenth day, when the last name was read, the crowd did not cheer. They stood together in the quiet for a long time, and then, slowly, they began to talk to one another — survivors to neighbors, neighbors to strangers — and Sefa, watching from the council balcony, understood that this was the sound of a country beginning to knit.

"The law bends," Aldric said beside her. He sounded tired and astonished, like a man watching a river change course.

"The law was bent wrong for ten years," Sefa said. "This is just it straightening. Don't congratulate yourself yet, Your Majesty. Wait until we've rewritten the succession laws, the guild charters, and the tax rolls. Then you may have one small congratulation. A modest one."

Aldric laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound, as if the mechanism hadn't been oiled in a decade. Sefa decided, grudgingly, that she didn't hate it.

* * *

They buried Vaelith's ashes on the first clear day of winter, beneath a hillside that existed on no map but the one Tamsin drew for the occasion.

That was the strange grief of it, Sefa thought, standing bareheaded in the cold with the others: the mapmaker's gift was gone. Spent, poured out line by line in the mirror chamber, traded away to unbind a king most of them had never wanted saved. The grove Tamsin sketched that morning would remain only ink and intention — a drawing of rowans and white birches arranged in a spiral, with a clearing at the center where a stone could rest. So instead of letting the land oblige, they planted it themselves. Every fugitive, every survivor, every councilor who could be spared came with a sapling in numb fingers, and by dusk the spiral stood real and crooked and imperfectly spaced, which Sefa thought suited a dragon's memorial far better than any magically perfect version ever could.

Tamsin set the stone himself. It was plain granite, and Wren had lettered it, and it read:

*VAELITH*
*Who kept her word longer than the world deserved.*
*Fire enough for one oath, and she gave it.*

"She'd have hated the fuss," Tamsin said. His voice was rough. Since the unbinding he had seemed diminished in some way no one could name — smaller, quieter, a man standing in a house with the furniture rearranged. But his eyes, Sefa noticed, were clearer than they had been since the Emberhold. "She'd have called this a hedge maze and offered to burn it down as a kindness."

"She'd have been right," said Wren, wiping her nose. "Rowans in a spiral? Who arranges trees in a spiral?"

"Someone who misses being able to make the world listen," Tamsin said, and the joke landed soft and sad, and Wren put her arm through his, and they stood like that while the light went gold and low over the young trees.

Sefa stayed after the others drifted back down the hill. She stood before the stone a while, arms crossed against the cold, and found herself talking the way people do at graves, low and unsentimental.

"I didn't know you," she said. "You knew my brother, I think. Bren wrote about you — the dragon who answered when he sent up sparks from the ridge. He thought you were a story. He told the children at the Emberhold you were a story." She paused. "You weren't a story. Neither was he. Somebody ought to have said that sooner, to both of you."

The wind moved through the saplings. It made a sound like pages turning.

Sefa nodded once, as if a bargain had been struck, and went down the hill to where the others waited, and to the work, which was endless, and which was hers.

* * *

In the weeks that followed, the kingdom learned its new shape the slow way — not redrawn by any gift, but argued into place, petition by petition, in a hall that smelled faintly of ash and always would.

Aldric announced, at the winter session's opening, that he would not name an heir from the old bloodlines. "The old bloodlines gave us the old oaths," he said. "The next sovereign will be chosen by this council, from among this council, when this council judges the time right. Perhaps from among the gifted. Perhaps a farmer's daughter who warms tea. The crown has been worn by a ghost long enough. Let it next be worn by someone alive."

The chancellery fainted again, en masse. Sefa moved the motion to make his announcement legally binding before anyone could recover, and it passed, and that was that.

On the last day of the year, Aldric came to find her in the council hall after hours, where she sat surrounded by petitions and a supper of bread and cheese that had seen better days.

"You could rest," he said. "The edict is dead. The hunt is pardoned. You've won."

"Won." Sefa laughed shortly. "Your Majesty, I've spent nine years winning nothing but tomorrows. I don't intend to stop collecting them now that they're legal." She pushed a petition across the table. "Rebuilding stipends for the border villages the echo grayed out. Sign it tomorrow."

"I'll sign it tonight." He took up the pen, then hesitated. "Sefa. May I ask you something honest?"

"You may attempt it."

"When you look at me — when any of you look at me — what do you see? The man, or the thing that wore him?"

Sefa considered him for a long moment. The lamplight caught the new lines around his eyes, the ones ten years of captivity had carved and three months of freedom had not yet softened.

"I see a man doing his penance in public," she said at last. "Which is more than the thing ever did. And I see a council seat I intend to keep until this country doesn't need guarding from its own kings — which will be never, so settle in." She slid the bread toward him. "Now sit down and share my terrible supper, and tell me what you loved, before all this. Since we're putting the rest back."

Aldric sat. And after a moment, quietly, haltingly, like a man reading names from a ledger he'd sealed shut long ago, he began to answer.

Outside the hall's high windows, snow began to fall over the capital — soft, unhurried, falling equally on the palace and the pyre grounds and the young spiral of trees on the far hill, where come spring, the rowans would leaf out green over a dragon's stone, and the world, redrawn by no magic at all, would go on learning to keep its promises.
