## 22. The Blank Page

The first map Tamsin Dravenmoor drew after the Unbinding was of the harbor at dawn.

He hadn't meant to draw it. Old habits die harder than oaths, and his hands still woke before he did, reaching for the pen and ink that lived in the drawer beside his bed the way other men kept knives. So there he sat at a window overlooking the gray-gold water, sketching the fishing boats as they came in with the tide, the gulls wheeling over the masts, the fishwife Marta arguing cheerfully with a boy about the price of cod — and when he finished, he sat back and waited.

Nothing happened.

The boats stayed where they were. The gulls did not rearrange themselves into the shape he'd drawn. Marta went on winning her argument, but no more thoroughly than she would have anyway. The morning simply continued, indifferent to his ink, and Tamsin sat very still for a long moment, feeling for something — grief, loss, the phantom weight of a gift amputated.

What he felt instead was relief so profound it made him laugh out loud, alone in his room like a madman, startling the gulls after all.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Wren said that evening, not looking up from the ledger she was balancing. "The staring."

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring at your hands like they owe you money." She set down her pen. In the two years since the pyre grounds, Wren had grown into her shoulders and out of her flinches, mostly; she still checked exits when she entered a room, but now it was habit rather than terror, the way some people always knew where their cup was. "They don't, by the way. I've asked them. They insist they're square with me."

"It's just strange," Tamsin said. "Two years, and I keep waiting for it to come back. And every time it doesn't, I feel—" He searched for the word. "Lighter. Is that terrible? Everyone assumed I'd mourn it. *I* assumed I'd mourn it."

Wren considered him across the table, in the schoolroom that had once been a smuggler's warehouse and still smelled faintly of salt and old rope no amount of scrubbing could reach. Chalk dust hung in the lamplight. Somewhere upstairs, one of their students was supposed to be asleep and was audibly not.

"Everyone assumed Sefa would mourn being a general," Wren said. "She gardens now. She talks to the beans. I've heard her." She shrugged. "People spend so long carrying a thing that setting it down feels like losing an arm. But arms get tired too."

"That's either very wise or nonsense."

"With me it's usually both."

Tamsin laughed, and the sound of it — easy, unguarded, the laugh of a man who was not waiting for hunters at the door — reminded him how far the road had been. He thought sometimes about telling his students about that road: the valley he'd fled, the dragon who'd carried them through the dark on wings that were already ash, the king they'd unmade and remade in a room full of mirrors. But the truth was the students didn't need the story. They needed what the story had bought, which was this: a school where gifted children learned that their gifts were tools, not verdicts. Where a girl who dreamed true could sleep without chains. Where a boy whose touch mended broken things was taught anatomy instead of being marched to a pyre.

Aldric's council had built shelters. Sefa had built laws, stubbornly, line by line, the way she did everything. But it was Wren who had stood in the ruins of the Cartographers' Guild hall — the guild that had burned its own apprentices rather than surrender them, Wren among the few who'd crawled out — and said, *We should teach them to draw.*

And Tamsin, who had once believed magic stopped at the edge of a valley, had said yes.

* * *

The trouble with teaching cartography to the gifted, Tamsin discovered, was that half of them didn't want maps. They wanted weapons.

He understood. He'd been that desperate once, on a hillside with hunters behind him, scratching a road into existence with a shaking hand. Power that reshapes the world is intoxicating right up until it burns down everything you love — and then it is only heavy.

So on the first day of every term, he gave each student a blank page and told them the same thing his mentor Maren had told him, back in the valley, back when he'd thought he was only drawing:

"A map is not the territory. A map is a promise. It says: *here is the world, and here is how to move through it.* Now — what do you promise?"

The first year, a girl named Odile — named, she claimed proudly, after a heroine in a novel nobody admitted reading — had drawn a map of the school with all the teachers' bedrooms marked X. Tamsin had confiscated it, graded it *excellent survey work, deplorable ethics*, and made her copy the coastline of the Ashreach fifty times while reciting the difference between *recording* and *commanding*. She was now his best student and insufferable about it.

The second year, a boy arrived who could make ink move. Not draw — the ink moved itself, crawling into shapes on the page while he watched, frightened, as though his own hand were haunted. Tamsin had sat with him for three evenings doing nothing but watching the ink, naming what it made, until the boy stopped being afraid of it. On the fourth evening the ink drew a small dog, and the boy laughed for the first time since arriving, and Tamsin had gone home and told Wren, *that's the whole job, that's all of it,* and Wren had said, *yes, now fix the roof, it leaks.*

That was the shape of his life now, and he loved it with a completeness that occasionally embarrassed him.

* * *

In spring, a letter came from the capital bearing the royal seal, which no longer terrified anyone but still made Wren raise an eyebrow.

"His Majesty requests," Tamsin read aloud, "the pleasure of your attendance at the dedication of the Vaelith Memorial Grove." He paused. "'Bring your students. He insists. He underlined it.'"

"Underlined?"

"Twice. There's also a postscript in different handwriting that says 'he has been practicing his speech for a month, please come, for all our sakes.' That one's signed Sefa."

The grove lay on a hill above the capital, where the pyre grounds used to be. Aldric had ordered the stakes torn out root and timber and the ground turned and planted, and for two years he had come himself, in plain clothes, to dig. The court found this eccentric. The court had learned not to say so.

They came by river, Tamsin and Wren and eleven students crammed against the rail to watch the banks go by. Odile sketched the whole journey, and Tamsin checked her work afterward and found it accurate — merely accurate, beautifully, ordinarily accurate, a record and nothing more — and felt that familiar lightness again.

At the grove, Aldric met them at the gate. He was grayer than he'd been, and moved more carefully, a man rebuilt from wreckage who never quite trusted the mortar. But his eyes were his own; they had been since the mirror chamber, since the last fire burned the old oath away and left him weeping on the floor, mortal and hollowed and free.

"Mapmaker," he said, and bowed — actually bowed, to Tamsin's enduring discomfort.

"Your Majesty."

"Aldric. We've been over this."

"Aldric," Tamsin conceded, as he conceded every time, knowing the king would ask again next time, because asking was part of how Aldric paid his debts — small courtesies, endlessly repeated, penance in installments.

The grove was young still — birch and rowan and one oak grown unnaturally fast, because Sefa had quietly hired a gifted gardener and saw nothing wrong with modest miracles. At its center stood a stone, and on the stone, carved deep:

*VAELITH, LAST OF HER KIND.*
*SHE BURNED SO THAT A PROMISE COULD BE KEPT.*
*LET EVERY MAP MADE HERE BE WORTH HER FIRE.*

Aldric spoke — briefly, thank the stars, though Wren mouthed along with two sentences she'd clearly memorized from leaked drafts. Then the students were released onto the hillside like a flock of delighted crows, and Sefa appeared at Tamsin's elbow with two cups of cider and the expression of a woman about to enjoy herself.

"So," she said. "Odile's applied for the survey corps."

"She mentioned it."

"The *royal* survey corps. Mapping the borderlands. Alone. At nineteen."

"She's twenty," Tamsin said. "And she's the best I've ever taught. And if I tell her no, she'll go anyway and be insufferable about that too." He sipped his cider. "I wrote her a recommendation. I also gave her three rules."

Sefa waited.

"One: a map is a promise, so keep your promises. Two: never draw anything you wouldn't want to meet. Three—" he smiled— "write to Wren every week, because Wren worries."

"Wren does worry," Wren confirmed, materializing with the timing of long practice. "It's load-bearing."

They stood together on the hill in the spring light, the four of them — king, general, teacher, teacher — watching children run between trees that would outlive them all. Tamsin thought of the Hollow Valley, far away now, green and quiet and empty. He'd gone back once, three years ago, walked the boundary stones, visited Maren's grave. It had been beautiful. It had been enough. It had not been home, and standing there among the stones he'd finally understood that it never had been — that the valley had been a hiding place he mistook for a self, and that he'd left his actual home scattered along a road: a girl with burn-scarred hands, a dragon's dying fire, a rebel's stubborn justice, a king learning to dig.

Home wasn't a place on parchment. It was the people at his table.

"Well," said Aldric, when the silence had gone on comfortably long. "I suppose you'll want to get back. Term starts soon?"

"Three days."

"And the new intake? Sefa says you have another ink-mover. And twins who share dreams."

"And a boy who can find anything that's lost," Tamsin said. "Which is going to be a discipline problem, but a manageable one."

Aldric smiled — a real smile, worn smooth at the edges but real. "You know," he said, "when I swore my oath, forty years ago, I swore to protect this kingdom. I thought that meant walls and edicts and a crown." He looked out over the grove, at the students, at the stone with the dragon's name. "Turns out it meant this. Teachers. Gardens. People who write letters and worry about roofs."

"Don't let the council hear you talk like that," Sefa said. "They'll put it on a monument."

"They can put it on a monument if they spell my name right."

Walking back down the hill toward the boats, Wren fell into step beside Tamsin, and for a while neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of fluency after all these years.

"You're doing it again," she said finally.

"What?"

"The face. The soft one. Like a man counting his blessings and finding the arithmetic works."

"I'm allowed a soft face. I earned a soft face."

"Mm." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "For what it's worth — I don't think the gift's gone."

Tamsin stopped walking.

Wren kept going three steps before noticing, then turned, hands in her pockets, entirely too calm. "Think about it. Your maps stopped coming true the moment you redrew the oath. You spent yourself on it — everyone said so, even Vaelith's ashes seemed to say so. But spent isn't the same as gone. Ink comes back. Wells refill. And lately—" she nodded back up the hill, toward the grove, toward the oak that had grown too fast, toward the students— "lately I've noticed things. Small things. The garden at the school blooming a month early. That storm last autumn that split around us like we'd drawn it a detour. Little mercies, everywhere you've been living for two years."

"That's coincidence."

"Probably," Wren agreed. "Almost certainly. But you taught me that a map is a promise, Tamsin. And I think maybe you're still keeping one — without meaning to, without paying for it, the way promises are supposed to be kept. Quietly. Just by being somewhere and meaning well." Her eyes glinted. "Or maybe the world just likes you now. Hard to say. Science is inconclusive."

Tamsin looked at her, and then up at the hill, and felt something turn over gently in his chest — not the old hungry power, nothing so loud. Something smaller. Something like a seed deciding.

"If it's true," he said slowly, "then it's not a gift anymore. It's just... a habit. The way Sefa talks to beans."

"The best magic usually is."

They walked on together down toward the river, where eleven students were already embarking on the catastrophic loading of supplies, and behind them the grove stood green and new on ground that had once known only fire, and ahead of them the road ran home — not drawn on any page, needing no ink at all, held up instead by the oldest cartography there was: people, promising each other the way forward, one honest mile at a time.

That night, back at the school, Tamsin took out a fresh sheet of paper, dipped his pen, and — just to see — drew a single line.

It stayed exactly where he put it.

He smiled, blew the ink dry, and pinned it above the door of the schoolroom, where every student would pass beneath it on their way in:

*A map is a promise. Make good ones.*
