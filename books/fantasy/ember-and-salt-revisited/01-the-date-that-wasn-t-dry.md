## 1. The Date That Wasn't Dry

There are three rules in the sealed archive of the Temple of the Quiet Flame, and I had broken two of them before supper.

Rule one: no ink of your own within reach of the collection. I kept a pot hidden under my tongue's worth of discipline and a stub of pencil behind my ear, because copying by pencil first and inking after was the only way I'd ever finish my quota before my hands gave out. Rule two: no questions about what you copy. Rule three — the one I hadn't broken yet, though the night was young — *do not read ahead*.

I want it on the record that I resented all three equally.

My name is Juniper Sterling, and for six years I have been indentured to this temple for a debt my father incurred buying medicine he was too proud to ask for help with. He died anyway. The debt lived on, as debts do. They're hardier than people. So every morning I climbed the ninety-one steps to the archive — I counted once, out of spite, and now I can't stop counting — and every night I descended them again with ink-stained fingers and the particular emptiness of someone who has spent ten hours making other people's words beautiful.

Tonight's assignment sat in front of me under a brass reading cage: a confiscated book. *The Ember Reckoning*, the catalog slip said, provenance unknown, recovered from a smuggler's cache at Thornfell. Prophecy, which meant heresy, which meant the temple didn't burn it only because burning things tends to make people curious about why. Instead they copied it. Full transcription, every word, so the Archivists could study the error of it safely.

I was that transcription. I was the temple's hands, and hands don't get opinions.

The book itself was ugly. Not badly made — badly made things don't survive a century in a smuggler's cellar — but ugly the way old things are ugly, all stained vellum and a cover stitched with thread gone the color of dried blood. The hand that wrote it was confident, looping, the kind of script that belongs to someone who never doubted a letter in their life. I hated it professionally. Nobody's that sure. Certainty in a scribe is just arrogance with better posture.

I worked through the opening chapters without incident. Stars and their meanings. A king who would not be crowned. The usual prophetic soup: vague enough to fit anything, specific enough to frighten someone. I transcribed *the mountain shall open its ledger* and did not wonder about it, per rule two. I transcribed *a debt of blood, three generations deep* and kept my pencil moving.

Then I reached the passage about Thornfell, and the moon above it, and the thirteenth turn.

And there, in the middle of a line written — according to the catalog slip, according to the vellum, according to everything — a hundred years ago, I found tonight's date.

Not approximately. Not in some round prophetic way, like *when the harvest moon rises over a house of salt*. The actual date. The day, the month, the year. Written out in full, the way I write dates in the margin of every page I copy, because that's how I was trained, because margins are where a scribe leaves proof they were present.

It was in the margin.

In my style of dating.

I told myself it was coincidence. There are only so many ways to arrange a date; any trained scribe would land in the same neighborhood. I told myself that, and I turned the page, and I found my name.

*Juniper Sterling.*

Not Juniper. Not Sterling. Both. Together. In the middle of a sentence about the heir of a sold soul coming down to Thornfell to settle accounts.

Here is the thing about being a scribe: you spend your whole life learning to see writing as evidence. Every hand has a fingerprint. The way an *r* tails off. Whether the crossbar of a *t* slants up or lies flat. I have copied ten thousand pages in six years, and I know my own hand the way you know your own face in a mirror — including the flaws, especially the flaws. My *J* dips too low. Always has. Master Ondrey beat it out of me for a month and gave up, saying some letters keep their own counsel.

The *J* in *Juniper*, there in the century-old prophecy, dipped too low.

I sat very still. The archive lamp hissed. Somewhere below me, the temple went about its evening — sandals on stone, the clink of the supper bell being polished, someone laughing in the refectory, the ordinary noise of an ordinary evening that I had spent six years wishing would be anything but.

I reached out and touched the name.

The ink smeared under my thumb.

I want to describe what that moment was like, properly, because I've thought about it since and language keeps failing me. It wasn't fear, not at first. It was smaller than fear. It was the feeling of a stair that isn't where your foot expects it to be — that half-second of freefall when the world admits it has been lying to you about something fundamental. A book written a hundred years ago does not smear. Ink dries. That is the entire covenant between ink and time. You write, it dries, it endures. Everything I do, everything this temple does, rests on that covenant holding.

Under my thumb, the *J* of my own name was wet.

I looked at my finger. Black ink, fresh, smelling faintly of iron and something else — something warm, almost like embers, like the air over a banked fire. I wiped it on my robe and left a gray ghost there, and I sat back, and I did the thing I always do when the world misbehaves: I looked for the clerical error. Because there is always a clerical error. Someone forged the page recently. Someone planted the book. Someone in the temple was playing a joke on the indentured girl with the crooked *J*.

Except the vellum was right. Old vellum has a feel — supple, thin, faintly cool, like touching the ear of a sleeping animal. This was that. And the surrounding text had faded the way century-old iron-gall fades, browned and ghostly, while the date and my name sat black and wet and new in the middle of it, like a hole cut in an old coat and patched with cloth from tomorrow.

*Do not read ahead,* said rule three.

I read ahead.

I'm not proud of it. But you put a person's name in a forbidden book along with a date, and the date is today, and the ink is still wet — you don't get to then expect obedience. Curiosity isn't a virtue in a scribe. It's a disease. I caught it years ago and I've managed it ever since, and that night I let it run.

The passage continued:

*On this night the heir comes down of their own unbought will, and the Court shall count them willing, and the account shall close. Let none warn them. Let none turn them aside. What is written in wet ink cannot be unwritten.*

*Juniper Sterling. The name is the key. The key turns tonight.*

Below it, a final line, smaller, cramped against the edge of the page as if the writer had run out of room or nerve:

*—and forgive me what I set down here, for I had no other way to say it.*

That last line was in a different hand. Older-looking. Shakier. And it, too, was wet.

I closed the book. I actually closed it, pressed the covers together, and put both palms flat on top like I was keeping something in, and I breathed, and I made a list. Lists are how I pray. One: the book is a forgery, recent, malicious. Two: report it to Archivist Veyne at dawn. Three: do not, under any circumstances, tell anyone about the name, because the last indentured scribe who claimed supernatural attention got reassigned to the tannery, and I have smelled the tannery.

Four: go home. Finish the page tomorrow. Ordinary evening. Ordinary life.

I remember thinking that last one with real relief. Isn't that a terrible thing to admit? My life was a debt and ninety-one steps and ten hours a day of other people's words, and I resented every hour of it, and when the universe cracked open and put my name in a century-old book, my first instinct was to crawl back into the resentment because at least it was mine.

The bell began to toll.

Vespers. Except — I counted, because I count — it tolled thirteen times. Vespers tolls nine. Thirteen is the death knell, and nobody had died, and the thirteenth stroke came out wrong somehow, low and resonant, less like a bell and more like a door swinging shut somewhere very large and very far beneath me.

The lamps guttered. All of them, in order, from the door toward me, like footsteps.

And the archive door — the heavy iron-banded door that leads to the corridor, to the ninety-one steps, to the refectory and the tannery-smell and Archivist Veyne's disapproving cough — swung open onto darkness.

Not corridor-dark. Not lampless-night dark. Mountain dark. The kind of dark that has weight and temperature and a mineral smell, cold stone and salt and something faintly burning underneath it, like a forge lit in a cave. Air moved through the doorway, and it moved *upward*, out of the earth, warm as breath.

I stood there with my hand still on the closed book and understood, in the slow horrible way one understands these things, that the corridor was not on the other side of that door anymore. That maybe it hadn't been for some time. That the bell had tolled thirteen times because something below was counting, and it had finished counting, and it had found me.

"Okay," I said aloud, to the empty archive, because sarcasm is also how I pray. "Clerical error. Right."

The darkness said nothing, but the warmth of it reached across the floor and touched my feet like a tide coming in.

I looked down at the book under my hands. At the reading cage. At the lamp, relighting itself now, flame steadying, everything returning to normal except the door, except the door, except the door.

And here is the part I will be ashamed of until I die, whatever I am by the end of this: my second thought — after the fear, after the disbelief — was *good*. Some traitorous corner of me, the corner that had counted those steps out of spite for six years, heard the mountain breathing and thought, *finally, something happens.*

Then the proper terror arrived, fashionably late, and I grabbed the book — rule-breaking, evidence, weapon, I didn't know which — tucked it under my arm, and stepped toward the doorway, because the alternative was standing alone in a sealed archive while the lamps went out one more time.

The last thing I saw of my life was the transcription page, half-finished, my neat marginal note of today's date sitting there in my own ink, dry and safe and utterly useless.

The last thing I felt was the threshold under my foot, giving way like the top step that isn't there.

* * *

Falling, if you can call it that, takes longer when there's nothing to hit. I fell through dark that smelled of salt and hot metal, clutching a heretical book to my chest like a child's doll, and I had time — a long, insulting amount of time — to think about the refectory. About the bad stew I'd skipped. About Sister Maren, who saved me the crusty end of the loaf because she said I chewed like a scholar. About the ninety-one steps, each one, all of them, the ones near the top where the stone dips and collects rainwater, the one on the landing that creaks in the key of something mournful.

All the ordinary furniture of a life I'd spent six years resenting. It's remarkable how fast resentment converts to grief when you think the shop's closing.

I thought about my sister Wren, who was probably at home right now doing sums by candlelight, believing her boring older sibling was boring safely upstairs among the archives. I thought: *if I get out of this, I will never complain about the steps again.* I thought: *if I get out of this, I'll complain constantly, but lovingly.*

Then the dark ended the way dark does — all at once, rudely — and I was lying on gravel and old ties of rusted rail, under a sky thick with stars, and the stars were wrong. Not missing. Wrong. Arranged like a letter written by someone who knows the alphabet but not the language.

Somewhere down the slope, a lantern came alive, and a voice — dry, amused, unhurried, the voice of someone who has been waiting a long time and knows exactly how to make it look casual — said:

"Well. You're early."

I sat up in the gravel with a stolen prophecy under my arm and a century of questions in my throat, and I did not yet know that the man holding the lantern was going to be either my salvation or the betrayer the book had promised.

The lantern came closer. I reached for the only weapon I had.

"I'm a scribe," I said. "I know forgery when I see it. And whoever wrote that book — they're still writing."
