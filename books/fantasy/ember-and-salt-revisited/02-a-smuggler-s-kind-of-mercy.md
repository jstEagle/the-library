## 2. A Smuggler's Kind of Mercy

The lantern stopped about six feet away, which told me two things before the man behind it said anything else. First, that he knew exactly how far a frightened scribe could lunge. Second, that he'd done this before.

"Forgery," he repeated, tasting the word. "That's your opening line. Not *help*, not *who are you*, not even a good scream. Forgery." He crouched, setting the lantern on the gravel between us like a man laying down cards he intended to win with. "The temple trains you people strange."

"The temple trains us thorough," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, given that my hands were still shaking and my knees had gone to gravel-paste. "Who are you?"

"Rook Ashvale." He said it the way other men say *nothing you can do about it*. He was hard to look at directly — not because he was ugly or beautiful, but because he was built out of ordinary parts arranged just slightly wrong, like a face copied from memory by someone who'd only half been paying attention. Grey stubble, road-worn coat, boots that had outlived several owners including possibly himself. "And you're Juniper Sterling."

My stomach dropped through the mountain. "I never told you my name."

"You didn't have to." He nodded at the book under my arm. "It's in there, isn't it? Circled. In ink that hasn't dried."

I didn't answer. Which was an answer.

He sighed and sat down on a rock across from me, unhurried, settling in like the night had all the time in the world and he'd already negotiated a discount on it. "Then let's skip the part where you pretend this is a normal evening and I pretend I'm a friendly shepherd. You copied a forbidden prophecy in a sealed archive. The archive opened onto Thornfell's back slope instead of a corridor. And now you're sitting on smuggler's road number nine — which, incidentally, is closed, has been closed for years, so whatever else happened tonight, you're also trespassing."

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I've been waiting here for three days," Rook Ashvale said, "for someone who was supposed to arrive tonight. And you're early."

*You're early.* He'd said it already, but hearing it twice made it worse. Early implied a schedule. A schedule implied an author.

I looked past him, finally letting myself see where I actually was, and my chest went tight in a way that had nothing to do with fear. I knew this road. Of course I knew it — every apprentice scribe sent down from the temple to buy salt and lamp-oil learned the smuggler paths along Thornfell's flank, because the temple paid late and the salt merchants accepted gossip as currency. This was the switchback below the old watchstone, the one with the split boulder shaped like a broken tooth. I'd napped against that boulder. I'd kissed a salt-cart boy against that boulder, once, badly, both of us laughing too much to make it work.

Except the watchstone wasn't there anymore. Where it should have stood there was only a stump of carved granite, furred with moss a foot deep, wearing lichen like a shroud. And the stars —

I'm a scribe. I keep calendars for a living. I have drawn the wheel of the sky more times than I've drawn my own name. And the stars above Thornfell were wrong. Not dimmed, not clouded — *rotated*, the way a page looks when someone has bound it into the wrong book. The Serpent's Eye sat where the Ploughman's Yoke should be. Winter constellations hung over what my skin insisted was a summer night.

"What year is it?" I asked. It came out very small.

Rook tilted his head. "You first."

"That's not — you can't bargain with me, I don't even know—"

"Year of the Salt Tithe," he said. "One hundred and nineteen."

I did the arithmetic because I couldn't not do it, because numbers were the last solid thing I owned. One hundred and nineteen. The prophecy book had been confiscated one hundred and eighteen years ago. Its colophon claimed it was written a century before *that*.

"That's impossible," I said.

"Mm." He picked up the lantern and stood. "Everything about tonight is impossible. That's rather the point of it. Are you going to sit in the gravel all night, or are you going to walk with me while we discuss the book?"

"I'm not giving you the book."

"I haven't asked yet." He started down the switchback without checking whether I followed, which was either confidence or a trap, and with smugglers those are frequently the same thing. "But we'll get there."

* * *

We walked. I kept three steps behind him and the book pressed between my ribs and my arm, and I catalogued him the way I catalogue everything, because cataloguing is how I keep the world from dissolving. Coat: good leather, old mending, no temple marks, no guild seals. Boots: salt-cured, southern make. Hands: ink-stained at the edges — actual ink, working ink, the kind you get from keeping ledgers, not letters. A smuggler who does his own books. That was worth knowing.

The path let out onto a shelf of rock overlooking the valley, and I stopped walking, because the valley was wrong too.

Thornfell town should have been there. Lamplight, smoke, the temple's bell-tower with its crooked copper roof I'd complained about for six years. Instead: foundations. Roofless walls gone soft-edged with moss. A single tower standing like a pulled tooth, and no copper on it at all.

"There's no town," I whispered.

"There's a town," Rook said. "Just not the one you're missing. Come on. There's tea. Tea helps. Everything helps less than people claim, but tea helps some."

* * *

His camp was a hollow beneath the split-tooth boulder — my boulder, the kissing boulder, now ancient — with a fire ring, two bedrolls, and a traveling writing case that made my fingers itch with professional envy. He boiled water over a small flame and handed me a tin cup, and I wrapped both hands around it and pretended the warmth was enough.

"Now," he said. "The book. Read me the passage. The one with your name in it."

"Why?"

"Because I need to hear it in your voice," he said, "to know if you understand what you're carrying."

So I read it. I know it by heart now; I'll know it until I die, whichever flavor of death that turns out to be. *When the moon wears thirteen turns and the thirteenth falls upon the copyist's hand, the heir of the sold soul shall come down to the Ember Court, guided by the betrayer Ashvale, who will deliver her unto the mountain.*

I left out the name. He noticed.

"And the name?" he asked quietly.

"You know it."

"I want to hear whether you say it like a stranger or like something you've already lost."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I said, "Juniper Sterling," and hated how it sounded — like a signature on a document I hadn't read.

Rook nodded slowly and fed a stick to the fire. "A hundred and eighteen years ago," he said, "a woman came down this same road with a man named Ashvale guiding her. She carried nothing but a bundle of winter clothes and a debt she couldn't say out loud. He delivered her to the Ember Court beneath Thornfell, exactly as written. Every word of it."

"You're quoting the prophecy."

"I'm quoting history," he said. "They happen to match. That's the part that should worry you."

The tea had gone cold in my hands. I hadn't noticed drinking it. "The betrayer Ashvale," I said. "That's you."

"It's my name."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight doing unflattering things to his copied-from-memory face. "Here's my offer, scribe, and I'll only make it once. I can take you to the Ember Court. I know roads under that mountain that the Court itself has forgotten, because I helped forget them. I can get you in front of whoever keeps their ledgers, and you can find out what your grandmother bought with her soul and whether any of it can be returned. That passage costs you one thing: the book stays with me when we go in."

"Why do you want it?"

"Insurance."

"Against whom?"

"Everyone," he said simply. "Including you. Including, if it comes to it, myself."

I turned the cup in my hands. Wary alliance, the situation practically announced itself, and I wanted to laugh at how neatly it fit the genre — the lost scribe, the guide with a guilty name, the bargain struck by firelight. Except this wasn't a genre. This was my grandmother, whose funeral I'd attended at age nine, whose hands smelled of bread and lamp oil, whose name might be carved into a ledger under a mountain.

And the prophecy had named its betrayer. *Guided by the betrayer Ashvale.* If the book was true, then trusting him was the trap. But if the book was false — and I was increasingly, professionally certain that no legitimate prophecy used ink that wet — then the book was bait, and bait implies a fisher, and I would much rather stand next to the hook than dangle from it.

Either way, I needed him. That was the elegant cruelty of it. He'd known that before I woke up.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"I copy the book first. Every page, into my own hand, before you keep it. If I'm walking into a mountain on the strength of a document, I'm keeping a transcript."

Something moved across his face — quick, unreadable, gone. Something that looked almost like grief, or guilt, or recognition. "You really are a scribe," he said.

"It's not flattery. It's terms."

"Terms accepted." He put out his hand, and after a moment I shook it. His grip was dry and careful, like a man handling paper he was afraid of tearing.

* * *

He slept. I didn't. I sat by the low fire with the prophecy open on my knees and my writing case beside me, and I copied, because copying is the only prayer I know.

Page by page, the book gave up its habits to me. The hand was confident, practiced, a little too smooth — prophecy hands usually tremble, they're written in haste or terror, but this one wrote like a clerk with a deadline. The parchment was even. The margins were disciplined. And somewhere around the third hour, copying a passage about the thirteenth turn of the moon, my pen slowed and stopped.

Because I recognized the letterforms.

Not the style — the specific hand. The way the ascenders leaned. The peculiar hooked *r*. I'd seen it before, a hundred times, in the temple's donation ledgers, in the tithe rolls, in the margin of a hymnal that had belonged to—

No. That was impossible. That hand belonged to a woman dead sixty years, whose ledger entries I'd studied as an exercise in historical paleography, whose handwriting I could reproduce from memory better than my own.

I looked across the dying fire at Rook Ashvale, asleep with his back to the boulder, his face slack and older in sleep than waking, and I thought: *he's been waiting a hundred years for me, and he knows my name, and the betrayer in the prophecy is his.*

And then I looked down at the page, at my grandmother's handwriting claiming to foretell my birth, and I understood that I was holding either a miracle or a forgery, and that in my experience the two look nearly identical until you check the ink.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would check the ink.

Above the split-tooth boulder, through the gap in the stars-that-weren't, the moon hung thin and hungry — and on its lower edge, faint as a watermark, sat a sliver of light that no calendar I had ever copied had room for.

The thirteenth turn. Already begun.
