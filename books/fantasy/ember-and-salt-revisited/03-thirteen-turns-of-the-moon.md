## 3. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

I woke to the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, which was strange, because the archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular despair of confiscated books. Then I remembered: the door had opened onto mountain dark, and I had walked into it, because apparently my survival instincts were as overworked and underpaid as the rest of me.

The road I'd woken on was one I knew. That was the worst part. The smuggler's path above Thornfell — the one temple novices weren't supposed to know existed but all of us did, because Brother Callum used it to fetch contraband pepper — wound along the same scree slope, past the same leaning cairn, down toward the same salt-stained switchbacks. Except the cairn had grown a second story. The switchbacks had multiplied like sins. And when Rook Ashvale unrolled his star-chart across a flat stone and pointed at it with a stub of charcoal, he said words no mapmaker should ever have to say.

"This is Thornfell's sky," he said. "A hundred years out of date."

"That's impossible."

"Most things are." He tapped the chart. "The Wandering Sisters sit three degrees east of where they should. The Salt King's Belt has drifted. Either the heavens are drunk, or you've come a long way, scribe."

"I came from the archive. Yesterday. Tonight. Whatever." My hands were still ink-stained. Of course they were. A century, and the first thing the universe preserved was my bad habit of chewing my pen.

Rook watched me with the patience of a man who had learned that watching was cheaper than talking. He was hard to age — thirty, maybe, or fifty wearing thirty's coat — with a face like a cliff that had decided against weathering. His cloak was good wool gone threadbare at the edges, the way clothes get when someone keeps them far longer than clothes should last.

"You said you were waiting for me," I said.

"I said I'd been waiting for the book." He nodded at the prophecy volume, which I had, in a moment of either wisdom or stupidity, clutched through sleep and waking and whatever the mountain dark had been. "You're just what it was wrapped in."

That stung more than it should have. "It's a confiscated text. Temple property. I was copying it for the index."

"Copying it." Something moved behind his eyes — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "And did you copy the part about tonight?"

"There's nothing in it about tonight. It was written a hundred years ago."

"Read it again."

I didn't want to. I read it anyway, by the grey pre-dawn light, my thumb finding the page before my courage did. *On the night the moon shows its thirteenth sliver, the debtor's blood shall come down the mountain.* And beneath it, in ink that had smeared under my thumb not twelve hours ago: *Juniper Sterling.*

"It's a trick," I said. "Someone wrote my name in later. Confiscated books get defaced all the time — novices, bored clerks—"

"With ink that wasn't dry?"

"With ink that *wasn't dry*," I admitted, and hated how small my voice came out.

Rook rolled up his chart. "Here's my offer, scribe. I know the roads under Thornfell — the real ones, the ones the Court uses. I'll take you to the Ember Court. In exchange, when this is done, the book comes to me."

"Why do you want it?"

"Because I've been carrying its words around for a long time," he said, "and I'd like to hold the thing itself for once."

I should have refused. Every instinct I'd honed in the archive — where the most dangerous objects were always the ones people wanted too much — said refuse, run, become very small and very literate somewhere far away. But the alternative was staying on this mountainside with a star-chart that called me a century late, and I have never met a problem that running from made smaller.

"All right," I said. "But if you're the betrayer the prophecy names—"

"If I'm the betrayer," Rook said, standing, "you'll know soon enough. Betrayers don't usually announce their schedule."

* * *

We walked down through the day, and the mountain grew teeth as we went. Thornfell from the temple side was all soft shoulders and sheep pasture; from here it was salt and black stone, the rock weeping white crusts like an old wound that never quite closes. And above it all hung the moon.

I noticed it at dusk, or what passed for dusk under that wrong sky. It was nearly full — one sliver shy, the way a moon should be two nights before the turning. But there was a second shadow on its face, thin as a paring knife, sitting where no shadow had any business sitting. A thirteenth sliver. No calendar I had ever copied accounted for it. No almanac, no ephemeris, no marginal note in any ledger Brother Callum ever made me index.

"The moon has thirteen turns now?" I asked.

"The moon has always had thirteen turns here," Rook said. "Everyone else just agreed not to count them. Calendars are a kind of treaty, scribe. The Ember Court broke it a long time ago, and the sky's been keeping honest books ever since."

"And the thirteenth turn finishes tomorrow."

"Tomorrow night. When it does—" He stopped walking. We stood on a ledge above the first of the salt tunnels, a black mouth breathing cold air, and Rook looked at me with something I hadn't seen on him before. Not calculation. Pity, maybe, or its close cousin.

"When it does, the debt comes due. That's the terms. A soul sold beneath the mountain belongs to the Court until it's reclaimed. If the debtor doesn't come for it before the thirteenth turn completes, the debt passes down the bloodline. To whoever carries the name."

My mouth went dry, and not from the salt wind. "Passes down. You mean—"

"I mean the Court is patient, but it isn't generous. A debt unpaid for a century doesn't die. It inherits."

I thought of my mother. I thought of my sister, Wren, who was eleven and could not sit still through a single indexing lesson and who I had promised, only last week, that I'd buy her a ribbon from the market when my indenture ended. Somewhere a century away, Wren was presumably still waiting for ribbons, unaware that her older sibling had fallen out of time onto a mountainside to stand between her and a Court that kept accounts in souls.

"Whose soul," I said. "Sold beneath the mountain. Whose?"

Rook was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence. Then he said, "You already know. You copied the book. The entry's in the back — the Court's mark, the sale record. Read the name."

I opened the book with hands that did not feel like mine. The sale record was written in a formal hand, cramped and old, the kind of script temple clerks used for wills and condemnations. I read the seller's line first — *sold by Maren Sterling, of her own free will* — and then the sold line, and the world tilted sideways, gently, the way it does when you faint sitting down and have time to be embarrassed about it.

*Maren Sterling.*

My grandmother's name.

Except my grandmother hadn't been sold anywhere. My grandmother had *run off*. That was the family story, worn smooth as a river stone from telling: Maren Sterling, restless even as a girl, who walked out one famine winter and never came back, who wrote once from the coast and then not at all, who became less a person than a cautionary tale our mother deployed whenever any of us talked about leaving. *Your grandmother ran off. Remember that, when the road looks sweet.*

She hadn't run off. She had walked down a mountain like this one, in a famine winter, and sold herself to keep the rest of us fed — and the family had spent a hundred years telling her story wrong, telling it in the only shape that let them live with it. Running off was a wound you could resent. This was a wound you'd have to thank her for, and some debts are too heavy to carry with gratitude.

"She bought medicine," I said. I don't know how I knew. Maybe the ledger-hand told me; maybe grandmothers leave instructions in their handwriting. "During the famine. She sold herself to buy medicine, and everyone thought she abandoned us."

"Does it matter why?" Rook asked softly. "A sale's a sale, under the mountain."

"Yes," I said. "It matters enormously. It matters to the terms, and to the price, and to every clause she signed. I'm a scribe, Ashvale. Words are the only weapon I own, and I intend to read every word she left behind."

He looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him turn toward the tunnel mouth without another word.

* * *

Far below, in a hall where the walls were salt and the light came from nowhere, the Ember Court convened its evening review.

The Auditor did not rage. Rage was inefficient. The Auditor sat at a desk of veined white stone and turned the century-old contract over in hands that had handled ten thousand such documents, and found, as it had always known it would find, a flaw.

*Sold by Maren Sterling, of her own free will.* True, and insufficient. For Maren Sterling had been a grandmother, and grandmothers do not wholly own themselves; part of her had been promised already, in lullabies and saved sweets and the fierce silent arithmetic of love, to a granddaughter not yet born. She had sold a soul encumbered by devotion. The claim was imperfect. The Court could not force what it could not fully own.

But imperfection had a remedy, and the remedy was walking down the mountain right now, clutching a forged book like a talisman, believing in a thirteenth moon.

The Auditor dipped its pen. It did not write an order. It wrote an invitation — the most binding kind of document there is, because no one guards against an invitation. It required only one thing of Juniper Sterling: arrival. Willingness would follow. Willingness always did, given the right terms, the right grief, the right hour.

The pen scratched. Somewhere above, the thirteenth sliver of moon brightened by a degree no calendar would ever record.

The Auditor filed the invitation and waited, as the Court had waited a hundred years, for the signature to come to it.
