## 5. The Court That Keeps Accounts

I have held this office for four hundred years, and in that time I have learned one truth above all others: souls are not stolen. They are *filed*.

The Ember Court does not rage. Rage is for creatures with hearts to burn out of them. We have ledgers instead, and a ledger never raises its voice; it simply waits for you to be wrong on paper.

My name — and I use the word loosely, the way one uses "today" loosely for any day the sun happens to be up — is Auditor Vess. I was not appointed. I was *amortized*. There is a difference, and the difference matters to no one but me, which is why I mention it only once per century, and this is that century, so consider yourself honored.

On the night the thirteenth sliver appeared above Thornfell, I was reviewing a contract older than most of my staff's grudges.

* * *

The contract lay open on my desk in the Counting-Floor, which is not a floor at all but a vast descending spiral of desks, each one lower than the last, each one dealing with debts slightly more eternal than the one above it. Mine is near the bottom. The air down here smells of hot copper and old promises. The light comes from the ledgers themselves, because paper written in good faith glows faintly amber, and paper written in bad faith glows brighter still. Hypocrisy is very illuminating. It is practically our primary fuel source.

This particular contract had been filed under Famine Winters, Desperate Measures, Subsection: Grandmothers. A rich subsection. We keep it well-stocked.

*Item:* One soul, whole and undivided, sold by Maren Sterling of the salt-farm holdings, in exchange for medicine sufficient to carry her family through the winter of the Grey Rot.

*Consideration rendered:* Delivered. The Court honors its side of every bargain. This is not generosity; it is hygiene. A Court that welched would soon find no one willing to sell anything at all, and then where would we be? Broke. The horror.

*Term:* One hundred years of kept time, after which the soul reverts to the general inventory or transfers by bloodline clause, whichever arrives first.

Standard. Clean. I had already begun drafting the transfer notice to the granddaughter — bloodline clauses are my favorite kind, they save us all the trouble of finding new debtors — when my pen stopped.

A pen stopping mid-word is, in the Ember Court, the equivalent of a scream.

I read the line again. Then I did something I do perhaps twice a decade: I fetched the original from the vault myself, rather than sending a clerk, because clerks read what they expect to read, and I needed someone who would read what was actually there.

There it was, in the seller's own hand, cramped and hurried and honest in the way only terrified people manage: a marginal note, initialed, witnessed by nobody, legally worthless and yet — this is the part that ruined my evening — *legally relevant.*

*"What I have left over, I leave to Juniper."*

* * *

Let me explain the problem, because the problem is beautiful in the way that structural failures are beautiful, if you enjoy watching things collapse along their fault lines.

Maren Sterling sold her soul. Fine. Souls are commodities; we buy them by the bushel. But she did not own her soul entire. She had already promised a portion of it — a mother's portion, the portion that lives in the naming and the hoping and the leaving-things-in-wills — to her granddaughter's future. Every grandmother does this. It is an unwritten covenant, unwritten precisely because it predates writing, and the Ember Court has spent four centuries trying to get it into writing so we can tax it. We have failed. Some covenants resist ink.

So the sale was flawed. Not void — void contracts are a mercy, and we do not deal in mercies — but *flawed*. We held a soul with a lien on it. A third-party claim, unregistered, unquantified, sitting inside our asset like a stone inside a loaf of bread.

Could we enforce anyway? I ran the numbers. Of course I ran the numbers; running the numbers is breathing, down here. The answer was no. Oh, we could hold the soul for its term — possession is nine-tenths of the law and the tenth tenth is paperwork — but the *transfer* to the bloodline, the thing that makes century-long holdings profitable, required the claim-holder's consent. And the claim-holder was a girl who did not know she held a claim, who had never signed anything, who was at this moment — I checked, I always check — copying confiscated books in a temple archive with ink on her fingers and resentment in her heart.

You cannot force a signature. This is the one commandment of the Counting-Floor. Coerced consent is null; a soul taken by force depreciates the moment it enters inventory; we have tried, in less hygienic centuries, and the losses were catastrophic. Everything of value beneath Thornfell must arrive *willingly*. Willingness is our currency, our ore, our salt.

Which meant the girl had to come to us. Down the mountain, past the wards, into the galleries, to the counting-floor, pen in hand, of her own free will, believing — and here is the delicate part — believing it was her own idea.

I want to be clear about what I felt, reading that flawed contract by the light of its own bad faith. I did not feel triumph. Triumph is warm, and nothing down here is warm. What I felt was the particular satisfaction of a problem with a known solution. The solution was authorship.

* * *

I convened the Quorum of Clerks — eleven of them, ranked by how long they could go without blinking — and laid out the situation without editorializing, because editorializing is for the upper floors, where they still pretend to have opinions.

"The Sterling contract is imperfect," I said. "We cannot compel the heir. We must attract her."

Clerk Second, whose specialty is calendars, observed that the moon over Thornfell had been persuaded to show a thirteenth sliver. This had cost us three favors and a minor deity's patience, and it bought us urgency: a deadline makes even sensible people stupid.

Clerk Fifth, whose specialty is prophecy — yes, we have a prophecy department; no, the prophecies are not true; that is rather the point of the department — presented the draft. A book. Old-looking. A passage concerning a soul beneath Thornfell, a date circled, a name written plain: *Juniper.* The date was tonight. The ink, Clerk Fifth assured me, would still be wet when she found it. Wet ink is important. Dry ink informs; wet ink *accuses*. A person who touches their own name in a century-old book and feels it smear will believe almost anything afterward, chiefly that they were always meant for this.

"Forgery," said Clerk Ninth, approvingly, the way another institution might say *masterpiece.*

"Prophesy," I corrected. "In-house terminology. We are not forging; we are *pre-writing*."

The distinction matters to me, though I admit it matters to no one else. A forgery pretends to be something it isn't. Our prophecy is exactly what it appears to be — a document designed to produce a specific reader response — and it will do so perfectly, and it will be believed as fate, and fate is simply paperwork that arrived before the explanation did.

There remained the matter of the guide. An heir cannot simply wander down a mountain; she must be *delivered*, and delivery requires a courier with a hundred years of credibility and no remaining scruples to complicate the invoice.

"Rook Ashvale," I said.

The Quorum went quiet in the way that means *we thought you'd never ask.*

Ashvale had carried Maren Sterling down these tunnels a century ago. He had been paid in years — we extended his youth on a rolling stipend, one decade at a time, each renewal contingent on performance — and he had spent that youth hating us with a consistency I can only describe as professional. He thought his hatred made him free. It made him *predictable*, which is better. A man who hates his employer never leaves, because leaving would mean admitting the years were wasted, and no one admits that. Not smugglers. Not auditors.

He would offer her passage. He would mean to help her. He might even, in his sentimental way, believe he was helping her. It changes nothing. A road is a road regardless of the intentions of the man walking it, and all roads beneath Thornfell end at a desk.

* * *

There was one further complication, and I record it here because I record everything, because recording everything is the whole of my function and nearly the whole of my personality.

The kept soul — Maren Sterling, neither living nor dead, shelved in the salt galleries at aisle ninety-one, between a drowned sailor and a bishop who regretted nothing, which we found unsettling — had begun, in recent decades, to remember. Kept souls usually blur. They forget their names first, then their faces, then their grievances, until they are smooth as river stones and easy to store. Maren had refused to blur. She had spent her century holding one name intact the way a miser holds a coin: *Juniper, Juniper, Juniper,* worn bright from handling.

This was inconvenient. A soul that remembers its heir might warn her. Might tell her not to sign. Might, in the worst case, make her *loved*, and love is the only solvent we have ever found for a signature.

I considered having her blurred by force. I drafted the order. I sat with it through one full rotation of the counting-bells.

Then I set it aside, and I will tell you why, and I ask you not to mistake it for mercy. If the girl came down expecting a monster and found her grandmother instead, she would come to the counting-floor already grieving, and grieving people sign quickly, to make the hurting stop. A warned girl runs. A loved girl signs. I filed the order under Pending, which is where the Court keeps the things it is ashamed of, and the Pending file is very large, and it burns very brightly, and we heat the upper floors with it.

* * *

By the third bell of that night, everything was in motion. The prophecy sat in a temple archive, arranged to be found. Ashvale waited on the smuggler's road with a lantern and a hundred years of rehearsed regret. The moon displayed its thirteenth sliver like a shopkeeper displaying bait. And the flawed contract lay on my desk, one signature short of complete.

I dipped my pen. I began the transfer notice:

*Item: One soul, whole and undivided—*

And stopped. Amended. *Whole* was no longer accurate; it had never been accurate; that was the entire flaw. I crossed it out and wrote, in my smallest, most precise hand:

*Item: One soul, mostly whole, remainder promised elsewhere, claimant inbound.*

*Claimant inbound.* Four words, and beneath them the whole architecture of the next nine days: the descent, the galleries, the meeting, the signing. We did not need her soul tonight. We did not need her soul tomorrow. We needed only her arrival, and her arrival was already written, in a book she had not yet opened, in ink that had not yet dried.

People imagine that a court like ours must hunger, must scheme, must lie awake wanting. Nothing so strenuous. We lie awake *balancing*. Somewhere above us, a scribe was touching her own name and finding it wet, and her vertigo was entering our books even now, as goodwill received, as willingness accrued, as interest beginning — gently, gently — to compound.

I blotted the page. The blotter came away amber.

Somewhere far above, a temple bell tolled, and a door that should have opened onto a corridor opened onto mountain dark instead.

Right on schedule. We do keep accounts, after all.

Of everything.
