## 6. Salt in the Wound

The Ember Court did not keep its souls in cells. It kept them in *shelves*.

That was the first thing I understood, standing in the salt galleries with Rook's lantern guttering beside me and my breath fogging in air that tasted of old pennies. The tunnels branched off the counting-floor like the ribs of some enormous buried animal, and every rib was lined with niches — thousands of them, cut into the white salt walls, each one holding a small glass vessel no bigger than a spice jar. Each jar held a curl of light. Not firelight. Not moonlight. Something warmer and more patient, like a coal that had decided not to go out.

I had spent six years copying confiscated books in a sealed archive. I knew what an inventory looked like when it wanted to look like something else.

"They're labeled," I said. My voice came out flat, which was how I knew I was frightened. When I'm truly terrified I get very calm and very pedantic, like a clerk at her own funeral.

"Everything down here is labeled," Rook said. "Labels are how they love you."

He said it lightly, but his hand on the lantern handle had gone white-knuckled, and I filed that away with everything else I'd filed about Rook Ashvale: the man who'd been waiting for me, who could recite prophecy on sight, who walked these tunnels like he'd memorized them in his sleep. Rescuer or betrayer. The jury was still deliberating, and the foreman was me, and I didn't trust the foreman either.

We walked. The jars watched us go by, if light can watch. Some of them flickered as we passed, the way a sleeper stirs when a door opens somewhere far off in the house.

I wasn't looking for my grandmother's name. That's what I told myself, anyway. I was looking for the ledgers — the debt-records carved into the salt itself that I'd begun reading on the way down, because apparently even in hell I couldn't stop proofreading. But my feet had opinions of their own, and they carried me deeper into the galleries, past niches whose labels grew older, the script shifting from the Court's crisp clerical hand to older, cruder carvings, until—

Until I saw a name I knew from a kitchen table. From burnt porridge and mended socks and a woman's voice singing off-key on purpose to make my sister laugh.

**MAREN STERLING. KEPT TIME: 100 YEARS, 4 MONTHS, 11 DAYS.**

The label was carved fresh. The salt around it hadn't yellowed. Which meant someone had re-cut it recently, which meant someone down here was still doing bookkeeping on a soul that had been sitting on this shelf for a century, which meant—

"Juniper."

Not from Rook. From the niche.

The light in the jar had changed. It wasn't a curl anymore; it had gathered itself into a shape, small and bright, and the shape had a voice, and the voice was my grandmother's, and it said my name the way she used to say it when I'd stayed out past dark on the smugglers' road as a girl — half scold, half relief, all love.

"Maren," I whispered. Because you don't call your grandmother by her name, except when she's been dead for a hundred years and alive for all of them, and then you don't know what else to call her.

"Oh, child." The light pressed against the glass like a palm against a window. "Oh, you shouldn't have come. You shouldn't have— who told you? Who brought you here?"

"I did," Rook said quietly.

The light flared. For one moment it wasn't warm at all; it was the color of a struck match, of a lamp thrown, of fury with nowhere to burn. "You," my grandmother said, and there was a century in that word. "*You.*"

"You know him?" I asked, stupidly, because obviously she knew him, because everyone in this mountain seemed to know everyone, and I was the only person in Thornfell who'd just found out their whole life was a footnote in someone else's ledger.

"He carried me here," she said. "A hundred years ago. He stood at that door and he took the coin and he watched them put me in the jar, and he has been young ever since, hasn't he, Rook Ashvale? Hasn't he?"

Rook set the lantern down. Slowly. Like a man disarming himself in front of a judge.

"Yes," he said. "Hasn't changed a day. Haven't slept a full night either."

"*Good,*" said my grandmother, and the light shuddered, and I realized she was crying, or whatever light does instead of crying, and that she was furious, and that both of those things were true at once, and that I had inherited exactly that.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my grandmother Maren Sterling, before tonight:

She ran off. That was the story. She ran off in the famine winter — the hard one, the one the old-timers still measured other winters against — and she never came back, and my mother grew up telling it like a wound she liked to press: *she chose the road over us.* My mother said it without heat, the way you say things so often they lose their temperature. And I grew up resenting a woman I'd never met for abandoning a family I loved, and I built a little shrine of grudges to her, and I lit it every time my own life felt too small.

Here is what actually happened, as the light in the jar told it, in a voice that kept breaking and reforming like water finding its level:

The famine winter, my mother — then seven years old — took the fever. The lung-fever, the kind that emptied whole houses. Medicine existed. Medicine cost more than our house, our name, and three generations of Sterling stubbornness combined. My grandmother went to the smugglers' road because honest doors were all shut, and she found Rook Ashvale, young then, hungry then, and Rook knew a buyer. Everyone knows a buyer, eventually. That's the whole trade.

The Ember Court bought souls the way merchants buy futures: cheap when the seller is desperate, dear when the buyer is patient. They offered enough medicine to save a village, let alone a child. All they asked was one soul, and my grandmother, being a practical woman in an impractical position, asked the only question that mattered: *whose?*

They said hers. She signed. She drank the medicine down her daughter's throat herself, spoon by spoon, and then she walked into the mountain and did not come out.

"The contract said my soul," she told me. "My whole soul, freely given. And that's what I gave them, Juniper, every scrap of it, and I have sat in this salt for a hundred years listening to them count." A pause. The light dimmed, gathered, steadied. "But a soul isn't a purse you can empty clean. There are threads. Things you've promised to people who aren't in the room. I had promised—" the light flickered, almost shyly, "—I had promised part of myself to you. Before you were ever born. Grandmothers do that. We stitch ourselves into the future. Nobody warns you it counts as collateral."

I thought of the ledger clause I'd found in the tunnels. *The debtor's heir may void the sale, at a price.* I thought of the price: *a name, freely surrendered.*

"Don't," my grandmother said, and now her voice had gone sharp, the kitchen-sharp, the *get your elbows off my table* sharp. "Whatever you're thinking. Whatever clause you've found, whatever clever scribe thing you've worked out — don't pay it. Do you hear me?"

"It's my name or your freedom, Gran."

"It's neither! That's the trick, child, that's always the trick — they show you two doors and both of them open into their pocket. A hundred years in a jar teaches you to smell a rigged game." The light pressed harder against the glass. "You think it's a coincidence that a book in a sealed archive opened its mouth and swallowed you? You think fate circled your name in wet ink?"

I went cold. Colder than the salt air.

"What do you mean, wet ink?"

"The Court doesn't rage, Juniper. It doesn't chase. It *files*. If it wants something, it doesn't send soldiers — it sends paperwork dressed up as destiny. And I have heard them, down here, through the salt, talking. Counting. Planning." Her voice dropped, and the drop frightened me more than any shout. "They've been waiting a hundred years for an heir to walk in willingly. Willingness is the whole contract. Everything else is decoration."

"The prophecy," I said slowly. "The date. My name."

"A forgery," she said. "Or near enough. Ask yourself, child — you're the scribe, not me — ask yourself who benefits from a prophecy that delivers a willing signer to their counting-floor on the exact night the moon turns thirteenth."

I am a scribe to the bone. I have spent six years learning to read hands the way other people read faces — the pressure of a pen, the lie in a flourished capital, the truth in a tired descender. And standing in that salt gallery, I felt the whole beautiful architecture of my supposed destiny shift under me like ice on a thawing pond, and I heard, very clearly, in the archive of my own memory, the sound of my own thumb smearing ink that should have been a century dry.

Ink stays wet for hours. Not decades. Never centuries.

Somebody had written that passage recently. Somebody had written *me.*

"Get me out of here," I said to Rook, and my voice didn't shake, which surprised us both. "Not yet. First — Gran, listen to me. I'm not leaving without you, and I'm not signing anything, and I need to see the original contract. The true one. The one from a hundred years ago, in your hand."

"Why?" my grandmother asked.

"Because," I said, "if they forged a prophecy, they may have forged other things. And if there's one thing I know about forgers—" I looked at the jar, at the light that was my grandmother, at the hundred years of kept time carved neat as a receipt above her head, "—it's that they're always in a hurry. And hurried hands make mistakes. And mistakes," I said, feeling something in my chest that wasn't dread for the first time in days, something hotter and more useful, "are how debts get voided."

Behind me, Rook picked up the lantern. His face, when I turned, wore an expression I hadn't seen on it before — not the smuggler's grin, not the guilty flinch. Something older. Something like hope, handled carefully, the way you handle a thing you've dropped too many times.

"There's a copy of every contract in the Auditor's counting-floor," he said. "Locked behind the great ledger. And the moon finishes its turn tomorrow night."

"Then we'd better start reading," I said.

My grandmother laughed — a real laugh, rusty and cracked and a hundred years out of practice, and it echoed down the salt galleries and made the little lights in their jars flicker, all together, like a field of candles catching a wind.

"That's my grandchild," she said. "Now get me out of this jar, and mind the third step on your way up. It's loose. It's been loose for a hundred years and nobody down here fixes anything."

* * *

We climbed back toward the counting-floor in silence, Rook ahead with the lantern, me behind with my grandmother's words turning over and over like a page I couldn't stop rereading.

*A forgery. Or near enough.*

If the prophecy was bait, then the trap had an author, and authors can be caught the way all liars are caught: by their own handwriting. I had the forged book in my pack. Tomorrow, behind the great ledger, I would have the true contracts. Ink against ink. Hand against hand.

And if the Court had written my fate in wet ink —

Then I would answer them in the same medium.

I just needed a pen.
