## 7. The Betrayer's Part

There are three kinds of silence on a smuggler's road. The kind that means nothing's coming. The kind that means something's coming and you'd best be still. And the kind that sits down across the fire from you, wraps its hands around a tin cup, and waits for you to explain yourself.

The third kind was sitting across from me now, wearing Juniper Sterling's face.

I'd built the fire small — habit, not kindness. Small fires lie about how many people are traveling, and I've spent a century preferring my lies portable. The salt tunnels were two days' walk south, close enough that the wind off Thornfell carried that faint mineral bite it always does, like the mountain licking its lips. The kid sat with the prophecy book in their lap like it was a sleeping animal they hadn't decided whether to trust.

I knew what was coming. I'd known since the archive door opened onto the wrong century and the Court's stipend had warmed in my pocket like a fever breaking. A hundred years of waiting ends all at once or not at all, and tonight it was ending at once.

"You keep looking at me," Juniper said. "Like you're doing arithmetic."

"I'm always doing arithmetic. It's the looking you should worry about."

"What are you counting?"

"Debts." I turned the cup in my hands. "Mine, mostly."

* * *

Let me tell you how a man becomes the villain of a prophecy without ever meaning to be in the book at all.

Her name was Wren Sterling before it was anything else. Grandmother, great-grandmother — the family tree has grown some rings since, but she was Wren when I met her, nineteen years old and carrying a bundle of dried nettle and a sister who coughed like the winter meant to keep her. Famine year. The kind of year where the temple granaries stayed locked and the rich bought bread by the loaf and sold it by the crumb.

She came to me because I was the only fool on the salt road who'd take coin from a poor woman. That's the smuggler's whole theology, if you want it: everyone pays, the question is only what. She paid in the only currency she had left.

"The Ember Court buys," she said. "Everyone says so. Everyone says they pay fair."

Everyone says that because no one who's sold has come back to argue. I told her as much. I was twenty-six and clever and I thought warning her absolved me. She said her sister would be dead by thaw. She said she'd heard the Court kept what it bought *safe*. Safe. You could hear the word doing work it wasn't built for.

I carried her down myself. That was the job — the Court doesn't send invitations, it sends intermediaries, and intermediaries need someone who knows the ways under Thornfell. I walked her through the salt galleries with a lantern and a speech I'd rehearsed about how this was her choice, freely made, and every step of the way I could feel the speech rotting in my mouth because she held my sleeve the whole descent like I was the last solid thing in the world.

They gave her a chair in a counting-room full of ledgers. They gave her ink. They gave her terms read aloud in a voice like a clerk reading a shipping manifest, and when she hesitated — one breath, one single human hesitation — the Auditor looked up and said, "Your sister's cough worsens in cold air. We can conclude whenever you're ready."

That's how they do it. No chains. No knives. Just arithmetic with a person's life on one side of the scale.

She signed. I carried her name back up the mountain in a sealed ledger-page, and the Court paid me, and here is the part I need you to understand, kid, because everything else follows from it: they didn't pay me once.

They paid me *forever*.

A stipend. Quarterly. Delivered by crow or courier or sometimes just appearing in my pack like a magician's trick, always enough, never more than enough. And with the coin came the other thing — the years sliding off me like rain off waxed canvas. I was twenty-six when I carried Wren Sterling down. I am — well. Do the arithmetic yourself. The Court keeps its instruments sharp, and an instrument doesn't age out of usefulness.

The contract said *deliver the heir*. Not *if an heir comes*. Not *when*. *Deliver.* So I waited. I watched the Sterling line the way a shepherd watches a flock he doesn't own, counting lambs, noting names, sending my reports. And every quarter the coin came, and every year my face in the river stayed young, and every decade I hated myself in some new and inventive way, which is the closest thing to growth a man like me gets.

* * *

Juniper had gone quiet somewhere in the middle of it. The fire popped. Down the valley, Thornfell sat against the stars like a closed fist.

"You're him," they said finally. "In the book. The betrayer."

"I'm in the book?"

"'One who walks both roads will lead the heir beneath the stone, and there betray them.'" Their voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a blade by the flat. "I copied that passage with my own hand. Twice, because the archivist wanted a fair copy. I've been trying to figure out all night whether that's you."

"And have you figured it?"

"I figured out you waited until we were two days from the mountain to tell me any of this."

Fair. More than fair. I set the cup down. "Would you have walked with me if I'd opened with it? Day one: hello, I'm Rook Ashvale, I sold your grandmother to a hole in the ground a hundred years ago, care to share a fire?"

"That's not—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "You didn't sell her. She sold herself. You carried her."

"There it is. The distinction that lets a man sleep." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Kid, I've had a century to polish that distinction. I could show you where I keep it, next to the one about how I warned her first, and the one about how refusing the job would've just meant she found another smuggler, a worse one. I've got a whole shelf of them. None of them hold weight. I've checked."

Juniper hugged their knees, the book still in their lap, and for a long moment neither of us said anything, and the wind did the talking, the way wind does over salt flats — saying nothing, endlessly.

"Why tell me now?" they asked at last.

"Because tomorrow you go down there, and down there everything I haven't told you becomes a knife somebody else can pick up. The Court reads people, Juniper. That's all they do. They'll open you like a ledger and find every entry, and if the entry says *the guide withheld*, they'll use it. 'He never told you he bound himself to deliver you. What else is he withholding? Trust the ledger instead. Sign with us. He's the betrayer; we're merely the collectors.'" I shrugged. "I'd rather you hear it from me ugly than from them polished."

"And the stipend. If you help me instead of delivering me—"

"They stop paying. The years catch up." I said it lightly, the way you say things you've rehearsed until the rehearsal wears thin. "I've done the sums. A hundred years of interest arriving at once. There are men who'd call that fair."

"Is that why you're offering the other road?"

And there it was. My exit, laid out neat as a pickpocket's palmed coin. Because yes — I had an offer ready. I'd had it ready for weeks, turning it over, sanding its edges. The smuggler's road. Out. Not through the Court's front door with its contracts and clauses, but under it, around it, the ways even the Auditors don't map because mapping them would be admitting they exist. Out of Thornfell's shadow, out of the prophecy's margins, out. No soul reclaimed, no debt paid, no name surrendered. Just running — the one craft I can teach in full confidence.

"No soul, no price," I said. "You don't get your grandmother back. But you don't lose your name either. Your sister never owes a copper. The moon turns its thirteenth sliver over an empty mountain and the Court files its little complaint into the dark. You live. That's the offer. That's the whole offer, and I'll tell you honestly: it's the only clean thing I've brought anyone in a hundred years."

Juniper looked at me for a long time. Firelight does funny things to faces — gives everyone a second face underneath, flickering.

"You hate it down there," they said. Not a question.

"I hate what I did going down there. The place is just rock and arithmetic."

"And my grandmother. Is she—" Their voice caught, steadied. Scribe discipline; I'd seen it hold worse cracks than this. "When you carried her down. Was she afraid?"

"Yes." No point lying. Lies are for people with more time. "And brave. Both at once, the whole way. She made a joke about the dark, near the bottom. I've remembered it every year since and I still couldn't tell you what it was. Isn't that a hell of a thing. A hundred years of memory and the joke's the part that wore away."

Juniper's eyes were wet but nothing fell. "If I run," they said slowly, "she stays. A hundred more years. Two hundred. However long the Court keeps accounts open."

"If you stay, you might lose more than she already lost. That's the arithmetic they want you doing. Don't do their arithmetic for free."

"But you're doing yours. The years catching up. You're offering me the road that costs you least."

I opened my mouth to deny it and found, to my considerable irritation, nothing there. The kid had gone through my shelf of polished distinctions like a customs officer through a false-bottomed crate.

"It costs me plenty," I managed. "Running with you means running forever. The Court doesn't forgive delivery failures. It amortizes them."

"Then why offer it at all?"

"Because it's the only way you walk out of this with your name still yours." I leaned forward, and the firelight gave my second face too, and I let Juniper see all of it — the twenty-six-year-old under the unaged skin, the man who'd been carrying a woman's sleeve-grip in his memory since before their grandmother was born. "Listen to me. I know what I am in that book. Maybe I wrote my part walking down that tunnel, maybe the Court wrote it later and called it prophecy. Either way, the betrayer's part is *leading you in*. So here's the one thing a betrayer can give you that nobody else can: the choice to not be led. Take the road. Hate me if it helps. Live anyway."

Juniper was quiet a long moment. Then they looked down at the book in their lap, and something shifted in their face — not softening, sharpening. The look of a scribe who's found a smudged word in a fair copy and can't yet prove it's wrong but knows, in the spine-deep way scribes know, that it is.

"Rook," they said. "When you carried her down. The contract she signed. Did you see the original?"

"I saw it signed. Why?"

"Because the copy in this book says the sale was made in *her hand alone*." Juniper's fingers had gone still on the parchment, the way they went still in the archive, I'd learn later, right before they found the wet date. "But she told me herself — no. She told my mother, who told me. Wren Sterling had already promised part of herself away. To her granddaughter's future. Before she ever met you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere under the mountain, I'd have sworn I heard a pen scratch.

"Then the contract's flawed," I said slowly. "And the Court doesn't buy flawed goods. Which means—"

"Which means the prophecy isn't a hundred years old." Juniper lifted the book, and the firelight caught the ink, and I saw what they saw: something in the hand that was too sure of itself, too *expectant*, a page written by someone who already knew how the story ended. "Which means somebody forged it. Recently. Somebody who needed me to believe I was fated."

I reached for the book, and Juniper — bless them, curse them — pulled it back.

"Not yet," they said. "First you're going to tell me everything the Court's ink looks like. Every quirk of their hand. Because if I'm going to accuse a court of forgery, I intend to prove it like a scribe."

Outside, over Thornfell, the moon hung with its impossible thirteenth sliver, one turn from full.

One turn from too late.
