## 8. Forged in Wet Ink

I didn't sleep. I want to say that was a choice, but honestly, the Ember Court doesn't believe in night so much as it believes in *billable hours*, and the salt galleries glowed with that pale, patient light that makes everything look like it's waiting for you to make a mistake.

So I made tea instead. Rook had a battered kettle and a hoard of smuggled leaf he claimed was "acquired, not stolen," which is the sort of distinction that keeps a man's conscience fed while his hands stay dirty. We sat in a niche off the third gallery where the walls were carved with ledger-script — columns of names, dates, debts — and I spread my two books out between us like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The prophecy book on the left. The Court's own ledgers on the right.

"You're going to read all night," Rook said. It wasn't a question. It had the tone of a man who'd watched someone do this before, a century ago, and knew how it ended.

"I'm going to *compare* all night," I said. "Reading is what you do when you trust the text."

"And you don't."

"Rook." I looked up at him. "I've spent nine years copying other people's words for the temple because my grandmother's debt ate our family's money before I could walk. I have copied psalms, land grants, confessionals, one extremely scandalous series of letters between a bishop and a beekeeper. You learn things. The first thing you learn is that every document wants something from you. The second thing you learn is how to catch it wanting."

He settled back against the salt wall with the expression of a man deciding to be entertained before he decided to be afraid. "Go on, then, scribe. Catch it wanting."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about forgery: it is almost never caught by the lie itself. Lies are easy. Lies are smooth and confident and they sit on the page like they belong there. Forgeries are caught by the *habits* — the small, unconscious truths a forger can't help committing, the way a counterfeit coin still has to be struck by a real die.

I started with the parchment.

The prophecy book claimed to be a century old. Its colophon said as much, in that formal temple hand: *Set down in the year of the Long Frost, at Thornfell, by an unnamed scribe.* And the parchment *looked* old. It had that soft, foxed, tea-stained quality that makes collectors weep and forgers rich.

But here's the thing about genuine age: it's uneven. A hundred years of damp and handling doesn't stain a page uniformly; it stains it *honestly*. Edges darken more than centers. The side of a page facing a window fades differently than the side facing a wall. Genuine old parchment is a map of its own neglect.

This parchment was aged the way a scribe ages parchment: evenly. Beautifully. Someone had brushed it with something — weak tea, probably, or a tincture of oak gall — working corner to corner with the patience of a saint or a liar. Under the gallery light, held at an angle, I could see the brush strokes. Faint as breath on glass, but there. Nine parallel sweeps per page, left to right, top to bottom.

Nobody's century does that. Somebody's *afternoon* does.

"What?" Rook said, because I'd made a sound. A small sound. The kind of sound you make when the floor of your understanding gives way and you discover the basement goes deeper.

"The parchment's younger than me," I said. "Look." I tilted the page toward him. He squinted. Smugglers, I was learning, have excellent eyes for cargo and terrible eyes for craft. "See the sweeps? That's artificial aging. Applied. Uniform. This book wasn't written a hundred years ago. It wasn't written ten years ago. I'd stake my indenture on it being younger than my sister."

Rook was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, the entertainment was gone from his voice. "The date. Your name. Tonight."

"Tonight," I agreed. "Which means whoever wrote it knew I'd be reading it tonight. Which means—" My thumb found the passage again, the one that had started all this, the wet-ink smear that had pulled me out of my life and down a mountain. I pressed my thumb to it, half expecting it to come away black.

It came away dry. Of course it did. It had been dry for days. But I remembered the smear. I remembered the vertigo of it, the sense that time itself had been caught mid-sentence.

Except now I knew better. Now I knew what a fresh ink smear looks like versus an old one, because I've made ten thousand of both. Old ink smears *gray*, flaking into the fiber. Fresh ink smears *black*, wet and whole. What I'd felt under my thumb that first night hadn't been ink that hadn't dried in a century.

It had been ink applied recently enough to still be tacky. Someone had touched up that page. Recently. Possibly *days* ago. Possibly while I sat in the archive three shelves away, cataloguing confiscations.

"They put it in front of me," I said. My voice sounded strange to my own ears, flat and far away. "The temple didn't randomly assign me that book. Or if they did, someone arranged the randomness. The date, the name, the wet ink — it was staged. All of it. A little theater of destiny."

* * *

I should have stopped there. Any sensible person would have stopped there, curled up next to a smuggler, and grieved for a while over the death of their own significance. Because that's what it was, underneath the fear. If the prophecy was forged, then I wasn't chosen. I was *targeted*. There's a difference, and the difference is the difference between being struck by lightning and being struck by a man with a hammer who read your address off a list.

But I'm a scribe to the bone, and a scribe doesn't stop at the first lie. A scribe pulls the thread until the whole garment confesses.

So I moved to the hand.

Every writer has a hand the way every person has a walk. You can disguise it for a line or two, maybe a page if you're good and your wrist is strong. But disguise is effort, and effort leaks. The pressure varies. The letterforms drift toward the forger's natural habits in the last words of long passages, when fatigue erodes discipline.

The prophecy book was written in a beautiful, archaic hand — all angular ascenders and formal serifs, the style of a century past. And it held. Page after page, it held perfectly. No drift. No fatigue. No human wrist behind it.

At first I thought: *a master.* Then I thought: *no.*

A master's hand holds because a master's hand is relaxed. This hand held because it was *copied* — drawn, not written. I measured the letters against each other, the way I'd been trained to authenticate temple charters. Identical. Not similar. *Identical.* Every *thorn* rune crossed at precisely the same angle. Every terminal flick exactly the same length, to the hair's breadth, across ninety pages.

No living hand does that. Living hands breathe. These letters had been traced from a template, slowly, carefully, by someone who cared more about consistency than speed — which meant they weren't trying to pass a casual glance. They were building an artifact. Something designed to be examined and believed.

And then the ink.

This was the part that made me laugh, actually — a short, ugly laugh that echoed down the salt gallery and came back sounding like accusation. Oak gall ink, proper ink, the kind used a century ago, darkens as it ages. Iron oxidizes. A genuinely old manuscript sits brown-black on the page, sunk into the fiber, part of the parchment itself. New ink sits *on* the page, glossy, sitting proud of the surface like frost on a fence rail.

The prophecy book's body text was properly aged. Brown-black, sunken, perfect.

The name — *Juniper Sterling* — and the circled date were darker. Fresher. Sitting proud.

Someone had written the body first, aged it, and then added my name later with different ink. Two inks. Two sessions. One purpose.

"It's not a prophecy," I said to Rook, and my voice cracked on the word like cheap pottery. "It's an *invoice*. Look at it — the aging, the tracing, the two-stage inking. Someone manufactured a century-old destiny and delivered it to a debt-indentured scribe who had no family, no leverage, and no reason to doubt that the world might have plans for her. They wrote me a fate the way you'd write a bill of lading. And I signed for it. I walked into the mountain holding it like a lantern."

Rook had gone very still. In my experience, large men going very still is either very good or very bad, and usually you find out which afterward.

"The Court," he said quietly. "You're saying the Court wrote it."

"I'm saying the Court *commissioned* it. Their Auditor couldn't force a flawed claim — your grandmother's contract, the one she signed without fully owning her soul. So they needed me to arrive willingly. To sign. And what makes a person sign willingly? Not threats. Threats make people run. You need them to feel *chosen*. You need them to think the universe has been waiting a hundred years for them specifically." I tapped the forged page. "So they wrote the universe waiting. In ink that hadn't dried yet, because nothing says 'destiny' like catching time mid-signature."

* * *

We sat with that for a long while. The salt galleries hummed their faint, mineral hum. Somewhere below us, the Court went on counting whatever it is Courts count at night.

"So the thirteenth turn," Rook said finally. "Tomorrow. Is it real?"

"Oh, the deadline's real. Deadlines always are — that's the first rule of coercion, dress the trap in legitimate paperwork." I turned the forged page toward the ledger wall, where the Court's own carved records ran in unbroken columns. "But here's what they didn't plan for."

"Enlighten me."

"They forged a prophecy. But they filed it against real ledgers. Real contracts. Real clauses." I stood, and my knees cracked, and I realized I was grinning, and I couldn't stop. "Grandmother's sale has a flaw in it — the Auditor's own finding. A woman who didn't fully own what she sold. And flawed contracts have remedies. Remedies are *written down*, Rook. The Court lives by its books. Which means the Court can be *beaten* by its books."

"That's not how power works," Rook said. "Power doesn't lose arguments to paperwork."

"No," I agreed. "Power loses to *better* paperwork. Ask any empire."

I gathered the two books into my arms — the forged fate in one hand, the true ledgers in the other — and felt something settle in my chest that I hadn't felt since the archive door opened onto mountain dark. Not hope, exactly. Hope is soft. This was harder. This was the specific, cold clarity of a scribe who has finally been handed a document worth fighting.

They wanted me to believe I was fated. Fine. Two could play at authorship.

They had written me a story with a circled date and a willing signature. Tomorrow, on the Court's own counting-floor, I was going to write back — in ink that would take a century to dry, in a hand no template could trace, amending their precious ledger with a clause they'd been arrogant enough to carve into their own walls.

The moon hung above the salt shafts, one turn shy of full, its impossible thirteenth sliver thin as a paring knife.

"One more thing," Rook said as I turned toward the counting-floor stairs. "Whatever you're planning — the Court reads everything. Every document that enters that room gets read, weighed, filed."

"Good," I said. "Let them read it. I'm a temple scribe, Rook. Reading is what I do when I trust the text."

I started down the stairs, carrying my forgery in one arm and their own law in the other, and behind me the moon slid another degree toward morning.
