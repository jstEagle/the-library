## 9. The Thirteenth Turn

The counting-floor of the Ember Court is not what I expected, which is to say it is exactly what I should have expected: a vast circular chamber of black salt, polished until the walls give back your reflection doubled and dimmed, and in the center, a desk. Not a throne. A desk. Of course a desk. You don't rule a place like this by sitting above people; you rule it by making them stand before your paperwork.

The Auditor sat behind it, quill already in hand, as though I were an appointment it had made weeks ago. Perhaps it had. Its face was the kind of face you forget while looking at it — pleasant, gray, unremarkable — and its voice, when it spoke, was the voice of every clerk who has ever told you the fee cannot be waived.

"Juniper Sterling," it said. "You arrive one turn early. Punctuality will be noted."

"Don't bother," I said. "I'm not staying long enough for it to matter."

Behind me, Rook made a small sound — half warning, half prayer. My grandmother stood on my other side, her hands folded the way she'd folded them over bread dough and ledgers and my sister's hair, a century out of place and utterly herself. The Court had brought her up from the salt galleries for the occasion, because the Court believed in staging things. It thought theater was leverage.

It wasn't wrong, exactly. It just hadn't counted on me knowing how the trick was done.

"The terms are simple," the Auditor said, and slid a single sheet across the desk. The parchment was fine. The ink was fresh. I recognized the hand immediately, because it was mine — or close enough that most people would never know the difference. *A name, freely surrendered,* it read, in letters that looked like they'd been written by someone who meant them. "Sign, and your grandmother walks free tonight. Refuse, and at the moon's thirteenth turn the debt transfers to the next of your blood. A younger sister, I believe. Small. Fond of climbing things she shouldn't."

Every clever thing I'd rehearsed in the tunnels went out of me like breath through a cracked window. That's what fear does; it doesn't argue, it just empties the room.

But Wren climbed things she shouldn't. Wren fell off those things and got up laughing with grass stains on her knees. And my grandmother had spent a hundred years in the dark so that wouldn't happen to any of us.

"No," I said.

The Auditor tilted its forgettable head. "No to signing?"

"No to both roads." I put my palms flat on the desk, and the black salt was cold under my hands, colder than the archive floor where all this started. "You've offered me two doors, Auditor. Sign away my name, or run and let the debt fall on a child. But you wrote this contract a hundred years ago, and contracts have clauses, and I have spent my whole life reading other people's handwriting. So here's what's actually going to happen."

I reached into my coat and set the prophecy book on the desk between us. Then, beside it, the pages I'd copied from the debt-ledgers in the salt tunnels — my own fair hand, transcribed line for line, because a scribe never enters a negotiation without her sources.

"Clause nine," I said. "Of the original sale. In my grandmother's hand, witnessed by yours." I turned to the page. "*Should the sale be found imperfect — should the seller not wholly own that which was sold — then the debtor's heir may void the instrument entire, upon payment of a fee to be assessed by the Court.*"

"You cannot assess the fee yourself," the Auditor said smoothly. "That authority rests—"

"With the Court. Yes. I know. Which is why it's convenient," I said, "that the Court already ruled on this. Last night. In writing."

I let that sit. Let it breathe. Somewhere above us, through a thousand feet of mountain, the moon was dragging its thirteenth sliver toward completion, and I could feel the whole room leaning toward it like water finding a drain.

"Your own ledger entry, Auditor. Dated yesterday. *Claim found imperfect; seller did not wholly own the soul sold; remedy available to heir per clause nine.* Your handwriting. Your seal. You wrote down your own confession because your rules require you to write everything down, and your rules bind you tighter than they bind anyone else. That's the whole architecture of this place, isn't it? Power as bookkeeping. Well." I smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "I'm a better bookkeeper than you."

For the first time, the Auditor's pen stopped moving.

"You forged the prophecy," I went on, and now the words came fast, the way they do when you've finally stopped being afraid and started being furious. "Wet ink on old parchment. A date circled a century after the book was bound. My name in a dead man's margins. You didn't foresee me coming — you *authored* me coming. You needed me willing. You needed me to walk in here believing I was fate's errand girl, because a coerced signature is void under your own law and you knew it. Everything — the book, the smuggler, the thirteenth moon — was a form you needed signed."

Rook made a noise behind me that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Told you," he murmured. "Scribe to the bone."

"So I decline the fee," I said. "Both fees. I won't surrender my name, and I won't leave her here. Instead I invoke clause nine, and I amend the record." I pulled the ledger transcription toward me and uncapped my own ink — good ink, temple ink, the honest brown-black of oak galls, nothing wet about it — and I began to write.

*Let it be entered,* I wrote, *that the soul of Maren Sterling was held in keeping by the Ember Court for one hundred years, less eleven days. Let it be entered further that such keeping constitutes payment rendered against all claims outstanding. One hundred years of a woman's warmth, her labor, her memory, her love, withheld from the living world — the Court itself shall assess no price higher.*

The Auditor moved. Fast, for a thing made of procedure — its hand came across the desk toward my page, and Rook caught its wrist, and my grandmother caught its other arm, and for one absurd moment the three of us stood there wrestling a bureaucrat over a piece of paper, which is the truest picture of power I have ever witnessed.

"You cannot alter a Court record," the Auditor said, and its voice had lost its smoothness. Underneath it was something older and hungrier, something that had been feeding on owed things since before my grandmother's grandmother was born. "The record alters *you*."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm using a pen and not a sword. Write it down — oh wait. You can't stop writing anything down. It's the rule."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn: the Auditor's own quill lifted from its holder. On its own. Because the Court's law required that all amendments be entered in the Auditor's hand, and an amendment validly invoked must be entered, and there was no exception written anywhere in a thousand years of carved salt for amendments the Court disliked. Somewhere beneath Thornfell, some ancient clerk had been thorough, and thoroughness — not courage, not magic, not love — was what saved us.

The pen touched the ledger wall. It wrote my words in the Auditor's gray hand, stroke by stroke, and the Auditor watched it do it, and its forgettable face forgot how to be calm.

*Struck:* the pen wrote across the original sale, one clean line through a century of theft. *Paid in full: one hundred years, kept.*

The counting-floor shuddered. The black salt walls cracked along seams no one had dusted in centuries, and light came through — real light, gray-gold, morning-colored, from a sun none of us had seen in days. The debt of blood, the transfer, the thirteenth turn: all of it unwinding like thread pulled from a bad seam, because a debt that is struck from the record was never owed at all.

"It will not hold," the Auditor whispered. "Fees. There are always fees."

"Yes," I agreed, capping my ink. "There are. And I expect you'll collect yours however you can, because that's what you are. But you'll collect it from me, and only me, and you'll do it *after* we walk out of here. Those are my terms. Enter them."

The pen, traitor to its master, wrote: *Terms accepted.*

My grandmother's hand found mine. Hers was warm. After a hundred years in the salt, it was still warm.

"Now," I said to the Auditor, as the cracks of morning widened overhead, "about that fee."

* * *

We climbed toward the light, and the mountain let us, groaning its ledgers shut behind us. Rook went first, mapping the collapse as it happened, swearing cheerfully at stones he'd known a century ago under different names. My grandmother kept pace beside me, asking questions — about Wren, about the farm, about whether the plum tree still stood — hoarding answers like a woman who intended to spend every one of her restored years wisely.

Only at the threshold, where the mountain's dark met the true gray dawn, did the Auditor's voice follow us one last time, dry as a filing stamp:

"The fee is assessed. It will be collected at sunrise."

I stepped into the light, and waited to learn what I had lost.

Somewhere behind me, faintly, I heard Rook say my name — and heard it land strangely, like a word spoken in a language only he still remembered.
