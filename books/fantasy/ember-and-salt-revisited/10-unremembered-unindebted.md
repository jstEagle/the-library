## 10. Unremembered, Unindebted

The Auditor's pen finished the last stroke, and the mountain exhaled.

I don't know how else to describe it. The salt galleries shuddered once — a long, grinding sigh through stone that had held its breath for a hundred years — and somewhere far above us, the thirteenth sliver of moon slid out of existence like a coin palmed by a conjurer. The ledgers on the walls went still. Not quiet; they had always been quiet. *Still.* The way a ledger goes still when its accounts are closed.

The Auditor looked at the amendment I'd forced into its book with something I can only call professional disappointment. It did not rage. It was not capable of rage. It was capable only of *procedure*, and procedure had failed it for the first time in a century.

"The entry stands," it said, in a voice like a stamp coming down on wax. "The soul of Maren Sterling is discharged. One century of kept time, entered as payment rendered. The debt is void."

"Good," said my grandmother, who was standing beside me with her hand on my arm, her hand warm and human and trembling, because she was alive now, or as close to alive as a woman could be after a hundred years of being an entry in someone else's arithmetic. She was old. Properly old, the way she should have been — silver-haired, stooped, with laugh lines carved deep around eyes that had spent a century watching ink dry. "Then we're done here."

"Not entirely." The Auditor turned to a fresh page. It did this without hurry, because hurry was not in its nature, and I understood then — with the particular clarity of a scribe watching someone else make an error they cannot stop — what was coming. "The Court does not render services gratis. An amendment requires a fee. You invoked clause seven of your own accord, debtor's heir. The schedule of fees is fixed."

"I know," I said. "A name, freely surrendered."

My grandmother's grip tightened. "Juniper—"

"It's all right," I told her, and the strange thing, the thing I will carry longer than anything else from that mountain, is that it genuinely was. Somewhere between the forged prophecy and the counting-floor, between Rook's confession and my grandmother's tears, I had stopped being afraid of losing my name. A name, after all, is just ink. And I had learned, down here in the dark where power was nothing but bookkeeping, that ink could be struck through. Amended. Rewritten.

"You'll forget," my grandmother said. Her voice cracked on it. "Everyone will. Your mother. Wren—" My sister. The debt would have passed to Wren at moonrise tomorrow, and my grandmother knew it as well as I did, and that was why she'd begged me not to pay, and why I was paying anyway. "You'll walk out of here and we won't know you. We won't know *why* we're grieving."

"Grieve anyway," I said. "Grief doesn't need a name attached. Trust me — I've read enough funeral records to know the good ones never mention the deceased by name. It's always 'beloved child' and 'devoted sibling.' People remember the shape of you long after they've lost the word for it."

"That is not how memory works," said the Auditor, with the tone of a functionary correcting a clerical error.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. But it's how love works, and you wouldn't know, because you've never loved anything except balanced columns." I turned back to my grandmother and took both her hands. They were cold. Mountain-cold. But they were hers, and they were free, and in a few hours she would walk out of Thornfell into a morning a hundred years late, and she would get to grow old properly, in a world with medicine in it, with her family in it, even if her family didn't know why their hearts caught every time a stranger with familiar eyes passed them in the market.

"Maren Sterling," I said. "You sold everything so Wren and I would exist. So the Sterlings would survive one winter and then all the winters after. You don't get to feel guilty about this. That's an order. I'm the eldest grandchild; I outrank you."

She laughed, wet and broken. "You sound exactly like your mother."

"I know. Terrifying, isn't it?" I kissed her forehead. Salt taste. Always salt, down here. "Go home. Grow old. Be insufferable about it."

The Auditor uncapped its pen.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the fee: a sensation like a page being turned inside my own chest. Here is what I remember after: everything else.

That's the joke of it, the beautiful, vicious joke. The Court took my name and left me *everything else*. Every memory, every feeling, every scar — intact. It simply removed the word that tied them together. I remembered being eight and falling out of the temple's cherry tree; I no longer knew whose childhood that was. I remembered loving my sister fiercely, protectively, with a love that had teeth; I no longer knew her face. Somewhere in Millbrook there was a family waking up with a hole shaped like a person in the middle of it, and no word to write on the hole.

The Court thought that was cruelty. The Court, I think, fundamentally misunderstood what it had taken. You cannot erase a self by striking through its label. I was still the person who had read a forged prophecy and refused to believe it. Still the person who had out-audited an Auditor. The name was gone. The *handwriting* remained.

Rook Ashvale found me at the mouth of the tunnel, where the mountain dark gave way to a grey pre-dawn that smelled like rain and pine and a century I'd missed.

He looked older, suddenly. Or maybe he'd always looked that way and the Court's stipend had been doing the work, and now that his contract was struck through along with everything else, time was catching up to him at interest. His hair had gone grey at the temples overnight. His hands, those quick smuggler's hands, were steady.

"Well," he said. "That's done, then."

"That's done."

We stood there while the sky went from grey to the color of a fresh page. Behind us, Thornfell sat silent, its ledgers dark, its thirteenth moon dissolved. Ahead of us, the road wound down toward the valley, toward Millbrook, toward a house where a very old woman was going to arrive on her family's doorstep with no explanation anyone would believe.

"They won't know me," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd felt the absence already — a whole constellation of people, dimmed.

"No." Rook shifted his pack on his shoulder. "They won't."

"And you do."

"I do." He said it carefully, the way a man handles something breakable. "Funny thing about that. The Court took your name out of every mind that holds it. Mine included, mostly — I wake up some mornings and have to work at it. But it can't take what I learned before the fee came due. And before the fee came due, I spent a hundred years memorizing the sound of it, because I knew — some part of me knew — that someday it would be the only copy in the world." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet, which he would deny to his grave. "*Juniper.*"

It landed strange. Like hearing your own voice played back on a recording. Familiar and foreign at once.

"Do it again," I said.

"Juniper."

"Huh." I tried it myself. My mouth made the shape. Something behind my ribs stirred, like a banked fire remembering it was a fire. "It fits. That's the hell of it. It actually fits."

"So what now?" Rook asked. "You could go down to the valley. Knock on doors. Watch people you love look at you like a stranger. Or—" he jerked his chin at the road, the open road, the smuggler's road he'd walked for a hundred years alone, "—there are other roads. Roads that need relearning, since apparently I'm mortal again and the maps have all changed. Roads that could use a second pair of legs. Someone who reads contracts. Someone who," and here his voice did something complicated, "someone I owe a century to."

I looked down the mountain. Somewhere down there, my grandmother was walking into a morning that owed her sixty years. Somewhere down there, my mother would feed a stranger soup and feel inexplicably like crying, and my sister Wren would dream that night of a person she couldn't name and wake up comforted. They would be all right. Grief without a name is still grief, but it's also still love, and love keeps.

And me? I was unremembered and unindebted, which meant I was the freest creature in the valley, possibly in the world. The Ember Court had taken my name and accidentally given me the one thing it could never buy, borrow, or ledger: a blank page.

"I'm keeping the name," I said. "Juniper. Even if nobody else can hold onto it, I can. First entry in the new book." I pulled the prophecy book from my pack — the forgery, the trap, the bait — and tore out the last blank leaf. On it, in my best scribe's hand, the hand that had beaten an Auditor at its own game, I wrote:

*Juniper. Debts: none. Belongings: one name (self-made), one road (unmapped), one companion (disputed).*

"Disputed?" Rook said, reading over my shoulder.

"You confessed to a century of smuggling and betrayal and then offered to walk me out of a mountain. Forgive me if I need references."

He laughed — really laughed, the first honest sound either of us had made in that place — and shouldered his pack, and we started down the road together as the sun cleared Thornfell and lit the valley gold.

Behind us, the mountain kept its silence. Ahead of us, the morning kept its promises.

And in my pocket, against my heart, the page stayed blank below that first line — waiting, the way all good books wait, to find out what happens next.
