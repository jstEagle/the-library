## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth by the time Odile Eriksen reached the third column of the autumn ledger, and still the numbers refused to lie to her.

That was the trouble with numbers. They were honest in a way nothing else was.

She sat in the counting-room beneath the Saltglass Guildhall with her sleeves pushed to her elbows and her ink-stained fingers moving down the page in small, precise strokes. Around her, the guild's inventory slept in its shelves and vaults: jars of bottled grief sealed with grey wax; stoppered vials of borrowed years, each one faintly warm to the touch, like a bird that had just been held; names cut loose from their owners and folded into slips of paper that whispered when the wind found them. The Saltglass Guild traded in impossible things, and impossible things required impeccable bookkeeping. A grief unaccounted for could curdle into something worse than sorrow. A borrowed year left off the books would come due at the worst possible hour, the way all debts did.

So Odile balanced. Every night, by candlelight, while the guild above her slept.

*Three measures of quiet, sold to the widow at Candlemaker's Row.* She wrote the entry clean. *One year, lent against the birthright of a farmer's second son, interest compounded at the turn of the season.* Clean again. Her pen scratched in the silence like a small animal moving through dry grass, and she found — as she did most nights — a wary, guarded contentment settling over her shoulders like a shawl. This was her life now. Ledgers and candle stubs and the soft clink of the night-watch's keys overhead. No hedge-cottage. No mistress. No fire.

She did not think about the fire. She was very good at not thinking about the fire.

The gift, of course, made the work easier, which was precisely why she hid it.

It was a small thing, her gift. Small enough to deny, if she ever had to. When she looked at an object for longer than a breath, she saw what it truly was — not its shape or its color but its *substance*, the truth underneath, laid bare the way a ledger laid bare a merchant's fortunes. The bottled grief on shelf nine looked like grey water to any eye, but to hers it showed as a slow storm behind glass, all thunderheads and drowned light. The vial of borrowed years shimmered with other people's mornings. The cut names were the worst; they looked like torn cloth, frayed at every edge, and she had learned not to look at them too long because they always seemed to be looking back.

Her mistress had called it *clear-seeing*, and had said it was a humble gift, a clerk's gift, nothing to burn a witch for.

Her mistress had said a great many comforting things, right up until the cottage burned.

Odile dipped her pen, blinked the memory away, and turned to the final folio of the quarter: the guild's receipts from outside traders, the odd lots and stranger consignments that came through the Salt Gate under seal. This was her favorite section, if she was honest with herself, though she'd have bitten her tongue bloody before admitting it to Guildmaster Corvane. It read like a catalogue of everything the world was too frightened to want openly.

*A jar of last words, spoken and unsold, from the house of a dying poet.*

*A length of shadow, cut from a hanged man, provenance disputed.*

*Six hours of a child's laughter, traded by a mother who could no longer bear the sound since the fever took the child's sister.*

She catalogued each one with the same steady hand, and each one opened itself to her sight like a flower deciding whether to trust the sun. The poet's last words glowed ember-orange in her mind's eye. The hanged man's shadow was cold, so cold it made her teeth ache to look at it, a black hole in the shape of a man with his arms still raised. The laughter — she paused over that one, pen hovering — the laughter was thin and gold and full of gaps, like sunlight through a moth-eaten curtain.

*Clean entry,* she wrote. *Provenance verified. Seal intact.*

Outside, the harbor fog pressed against the high windows of the counting-room, turning the moonlight to milk. Somewhere beyond it, the city of Vhalen slept — the fish-stink of the docks, the bell-towers, the palace on its hill with its windows burning yellow all night because kings, presumably, did not need to sleep. Odile had lived in Vhalen four years now and had never once seen the king. Almost no one had. He kept to the palace since the queen died, and the criers spoke his proclamations, and his coin bore a profile so worn by handling that it might have been anyone.

She thought nothing of this. Why would she? Kings were weather. Kings happened to you the way rain happened to you, and you carried your umbrella and got on with your ledgers.

The discrepancy appeared on the fourth-to-last page.

At first she took it for a smudge. The ink sat strangely on the parchment, darker than the surrounding entries, as though it had been written with a different hand or a different pen or simply a different *weight*. She tilted the candle closer. The wax guttered. And because she was Odile Eriksen, and because she had never once in four years failed to look directly at anything placed before her, she looked.

The entry showed her what it truly was.

It was not written in ink. Underneath the black surface of it, visible only to her gift, the letters were made of *absence* — a writing scraped out of the page rather than set upon it, the way a scar is a story told in skin that isn't skin anymore. Whoever had entered it had used something that ate. Something that took the parchment's own substance and spent it, the way a fire spends wood.

Her mouth went dry. She read it anyway.

*Received, by private courier, under royal seal: payment rendered in full to the Crown's account — one crown's worth of quiet.*

She read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves. They did not resolve into sense.

"A crown's worth of quiet," she said aloud, just to hear how foolish it sounded in her own voice. The candle flame leaned toward her, listening. She blew it out out of pure nerves, then felt ridiculous sitting in the dark, and lit it again with hands that were not quite steady.

Because here was the thing — here was the thing that made her stomach fold over on itself like wet paper. The Saltglass Guild *sold* quiet. Sold it by the measure, by the jar, by the long grey ounce. Quiet was one of their staple goods, along with grief and years and forgotten lullabies. But the guild *sold*. It did not *buy*. Not from the crown. Especially not from the crown. The crown taxed them, regulated them, occasionally raided them when a magistrate needed to look vigorous. The crown did not walk down into the dark with its hat in its hands and purchase *quiet* — and certainly not a crown's worth of it, which was not even a unit. There was no unit. She had memorized the guild's entire schedule of measures in her first month. There was no such thing as a crown's worth of anything.

And yet the entry balanced. That was the part that chilled her most, when she forced herself to look at it plainly. The sums on either side of it reconciled perfectly. Whatever had been paid, whatever had been received, the ledger accepted it without complaint, the way a body accepts a knife, cleanly, between the ribs.

Someone had wanted this entry to exist. Someone had wanted it to exist *and* to be invisible — except to a clear-seer, and there were perhaps six clear-seers in all of Vhalen, and two of them were dead, and one of them was her.

She sat back. The chair creaked. Above her, the night-watch's keys jingled past and faded, and the fog breathed against the glass, and Odile Eriksen did the thing she was best at in all the world besides arithmetic: she thought.

Options, laid out like columns. First: report it to Guildmaster Corvane in the morning. He was a dried-apple of a man, honest in the way of men who had never been tempted by anything larger than a second helping. He would frown, he would summon the senior factors, he would make noise. Noise was the one thing the guild could not afford. The guild survived on being dull. On being *beneath notice*. An entry involving the crown's own seal, an entry that could not be explained — that was not noise, that was a bonfire, and Odile knew with a certainty that came from somewhere below her ribs what bonfires meant for people like her.

Second: ignore it. Turn the page. Balance the rest and go home to her narrow room above the chandlery and sleep, and let the entry sit in the closed ledger until some other clerk found it, some other year, some other lifetime.

Third —

Third was the option she didn't have a name for yet. Third was the cold little voice that sounded, annoyingly, like her dead mistress: *You looked at it, girl. It looked back. You don't get to pretend you didn't see.*

She pulled the folio toward her and examined the entry once more, slowly, letting her gift range over it the way a tongue ranges over a sore tooth. The scraped-out letters. The false ink laid over them like a bandage. And beneath both, faint as a watermark, something else — a residue, a trace of whatever had been spent to write it. She concentrated until her eyes ached.

It felt like the hanged man's shadow. Cold, and vast, and patient. But more than that — and this was the detail that finally, truly frightened her — it felt *hungry*. The bottled griefs were sad. The borrowed years were wistful. Even the cut names were only lost. This residue was none of those things. It was appetite given handwriting. It was the feeling of standing in a doorway and realizing the darkness inside the room is not empty but *attentive*.

The candle flame stood very straight, as if afraid to move.

"Right," Odile whispered, to the candle, to the ledger, to the attentive dark. "Right."

She did what a careful woman does. She copied the entry — word for word, sum for sum, including the strange weight of the ink — into the back pages of her own pocket-book, the one she kept for private accounts, the one that never left her person. Then she closed the guild ledger, squared its corners, and returned it to its shelf with hands that only trembled a little.

Then she sat back down, because the night's work was not finished, and Odile Eriksen finished her work. That was the whole architecture of her survival: be exact, be dull, be indispensable, be invisible. She dipped her pen. She turned to the next folio.

But the contentment was gone. She understood that now, sitting in the little circle of candlelight with the fog pressing at the windows and the impossible inventory sleeping on its shelves around her. Four years she had built this life, grain by grain, entry by entry — a life small enough to hide inside, ordered enough to trust. And one line of scraped-out writing had cracked it down the middle the way ice cracks on a pond: quietly, and then all at once, and then you are in the water.

*A crown's worth of quiet.*

What kind of thing needed quiet so badly it would pay a kingdom for it? What kind of thing had a kingdom to pay with?

She wrote the next entry. Her pen did not shake. She was proud of that, later, in the way one is proud of small survivals.

Near dawn, when the fog began to turn the grey of dishwater and the first gulls started their arguments outside, Odile snuffed the candle, gathered her cloak and her pocket-book, and climbed the stairs into the waking guildhall. The floorboards were cold through her boots. In the great room, the day-clerks' desks waited in rows like headstones, and the morning light came through the salt-frosted windows in pale bars.

On the notice board by the door, where the guild posted tariffs and tide-tables and the occasional warning about counterfeit grief, someone had nailed a fresh broadsheet. The paste was still wet at the corners. It bore the royal arms, and it was printed in letters large enough to read from across the room, large enough, Odile thought distantly, to read from across a square, from across a crowd, from the back of a mob.

She crossed the room. She read it.

By order of His Majesty, a census of the gifted. All persons possessing unnatural faculties shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who conceal their gifts —

She stopped reading. Not because the rest was hard to guess. Because her gift, unbidden, unasked, had reached out and shown her what the broadsheet truly was — and underneath the ink and the royal arms and the official cadence of the type, it was made of the same thing as the ledger entry. The same scraped absence. The same cold, patient hunger, wearing the shape of law.

The paper fluttered slightly in the draft from the door, as if breathing.

Odile stood very still in the grey morning light of the Saltglass Guildhall, with her pocket-book against her ribs and her heart going like a hammer in a locked room, and understood that the entry she had found in the dark had not been a curiosity. It had been a warning. The thing that paid for quiet had run out of patience with it.

Thirty days.

She turned and walked back down toward the counting-room, unhurried, a dull and exact and invisible clerk, already counting in her head what she would need to carry and what she must leave behind — and which book, above all others, she could not leave to burn.
