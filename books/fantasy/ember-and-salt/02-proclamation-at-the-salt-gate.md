## 2. Proclamation at the Salt Gate

The bells began before dawn.

Odile heard them through the shutters of her narrow room above the counting-house — not the guild's own bells, which rang the hours in their familiar patient pattern, but the cathedral bells across the city, all of them at once, a sound like iron rain. She lay still in her cot and listened and felt the unease settle into her chest the way cold water settles into boots.

She had gone to sleep over the ledger of impossible things. She had dreamed, absurdly, of columns that would not balance, of ink crawling off the page like insects, of a payment made in the king's own name for *a crown's worth of quiet*. Waking did not burn the dream away. It sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Downstairs, the clerks were already gathering at the windows. Odile dressed quickly — wool stockings, the grey dress, her hair pinned back with the plain bone comb that had been her mistress's — and came down into the long room where forty desks stood in rows beneath the smoke-stained rafters of the Saltglass Guild.

Nobody was working. That alone told her how wrong the morning was. In six years she had never seen the counting-house idle while candles burned unattended and ledgers lay open to the damp air.

"Listen," whispered Pellam, the oldest clerk, a man whose spine had curved into the shape of his chair. He was leaning toward the open window, one hand cupped behind his ear.

And then she heard it: voices, many voices, moving through the streets below in a slow tide. The criers.

*"By order of His Majesty the King — by order of His Majesty the King — let it be known throughout the city of Vhalen and all its dominions—"*

The proclamation rolled up the street like something heavy being dragged. Odile pushed through the cluster of clerks to the window and looked down into Salt Gate Square, where three criers in royal blue stood on the steps of the fountain, brass horns in hand, and the crowd gathered around them the way water gathers around a stone — slowly, then all at once.

*"—that the practice of the Gifted Arts is hereby declared treason against the crown. All persons possessing unnatural power, whether by birth, bargain, or study, shall present themselves at any garrison within nine days to be entered upon the Register of Gifts. Those who register shall be marked, taxed, and bound to the king's service."*

A pause. The crier in the middle lifted his horn higher.

*"Those who do not register shall be sought. And those who are found shall burn."*

The words went through the square like a blade through wet cloth. Somewhere a woman began to cry. Somewhere else a man laughed — one short, disbelieving bark — as though the whole thing were a play he'd wandered into and couldn't find the exit from.

Odile stood very still at the window with her hands flat against the sill, and beneath the fear, beneath even the old instinct to count — nine days, three criers, forty desks, one gift to hide — she felt something colder take root.

*They will come with lists,* she thought. *Lists are my trade. I know exactly what a list can do.*

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane did not shout. He never shouted. He simply appeared at the head of the counting-house an hour later, still in his nightclothes under his coat, his face the color of tallow, and said in the voice he used for correcting entries:

"Every clerk at their desk. Now."

They worked all day, and what they worked at was destruction.

Corvane moved down the rows of the deep inventory vault beneath the counting-house with a candle in one hand and a ledger in the other, calling out entries, and the clerks carried the items up two at a time and fed them to the great iron stove in the corner of the main room, the one usually reserved for warming sealing wax.

Odile catalogued as they burned. It was her place; no one questioned it; her hand did not shake, or only shook where no one could see.

*Bottled grief, vintage of the flood year, twelve flasks.* Into the fire. The room grew strange and heavy as the glass cracked, and two of the younger clerks wept without knowing why, grief escaping its bottles for one last unowned moment before the flame took it.

*Borrowed years, loose, in a purse of eel-skin, quantity unknown.* Into the fire. The stove flame leapt green and then gold, and for a heartbeat everyone in the room felt young, felt the specific ache of being seventeen again, and then it passed and left them all older-feeling than before.

*A name, cut loose from its owner, provenance withheld.* Corvane held this one himself, a slip of vellum with letters that squirmed when looked at directly, and he fed it to the flames with an expression Odile had never seen on him before. It took her a moment to recognize it, because she had only ever seen it in mirrors on bad nights.

It was fear.

"Master," she said quietly, when the vault was empty and the stove roared and the room smelled of burnt wonders. "The Register. If they come here — if they audit us—"

"They will audit us." Corvane wiped ash from his hands with a linen cloth, methodically, finger by finger. "That is what this is, girl. A hunt conducted by clerks. They will want our books. So our books will show a respectable house trading in salt, dye, and licensed curiosities, nothing more. You will see to it. You are the best hand with figures I have ever employed, and I am telling you now—" he fixed her with eyes gone pale and hard, "—the best thing you can do with whatever you carry inside you is to carry it smaller. Smaller than small. Carry it like a coin you must never spend. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, master," Odile said.

But she thought of the entry she had found three nights ago, the one that shouldn't exist, the payment in the king's own name, and she thought: *someone spent a crown's worth of quiet already. Someone bought silence at the very top of the world. And now the crown hunts the gifted with fire.*

She did not believe those two things were separate. She was a bookkeeper. She believed everything balanced, eventually, and somewhere there was a column where this horror was entered, and she meant, someday, to find it.

* * *

It happened on the third evening after the proclamation.

His name was Joreth Lunn. He was twenty-two years old, second-desk in the imports row, with ink permanently stained into the creases of his knuckles and a habit of humming when he multiplied large sums. He was not particularly clever and not particularly brave, and he was kind to the sweep-boy, and he had once covered for Odile when she mis-entered a shipment of bottled lullabies, saying the error was his.

He also, as far as anyone in the counting-house knew, had no gift at all.

The hunters came at dusk — four of them in the king's blue, with a fifth man behind them in grey robes, carrying a long forked instrument of black iron that Odile's eyes refused to look at directly. Her gift flared before she could stop it, that unwanted sight of things-as-they-truly-are, and what she saw made her breath catch: the forked rod was *hungry*. It drank the air around it. It was a thing built to find what hid.

The grey-robed man walked the rows of desks slowly, and the fork trembled in his grip like a dowsing wand, and Odile pressed both hands flat against her own desk and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than the soldiers did: *smaller than small. Be paper. Be arithmetic. Be nothing but a girl who counts.*

The fork passed her desk. It dipped — a fraction, a twitch, hardly anything — and the grey man paused, and Odile stopped breathing.

Then Joreth Lunn knocked over his inkwell.

It should have been nothing. A clumsy boy, a startled moment, a spreading pool of black across his day-book. But the grey-robed man turned toward the sound, and the fork in his hands *leapt*, strained forward like a dog scenting meat, and the ink on Joreth's spilled page began to move — rising off the paper in fine curling threads, hanging in the air, forming shapes. Little figures. A tiny horse of ink galloped across the ceiling beams. A tiny tree of ink blossomed above the import ledgers.

Joreth stood up so fast his chair went over backward. "I didn't — I never registered because there's nothing, it's only ink, it's only ever been ink, I can't do anything *else*—"

"Ink that obeys," said the grey-robed man softly, "is ink that obeys someone. Take him."

"No," Joreth said, and then louder, and then he was screaming it, the word tearing out of him over and over as the soldiers took his arms, as the little ink-horse dissolved sadly back into droplets, as his half-finished ledgers slid off the overturned desk and fanned across the floor. "No — please — Master Corvane! Master Corvane, tell them, tell them I'm *registered*, tell them I'm in your books—"

Corvane stood at the head of the room with his hands folded behind his back, and he did not move, and he did not speak, and Odile understood in one sick drop of comprehension that the guildmaster had already decided, three days ago, that Joreth Lunn was an entry to be written off. A loss. Balanced against the survival of forty other clerks.

They dragged Joreth past her desk. His fingers caught hers for half a second — ink-stained knuckles, the boy who hummed when he multiplied — and then he was gone, out the door, into the dark, still screaming, and the door closed, and the counting-house was silent except for the tick of the great regulator clock and the drip of spilled ink off the edge of a desk.

Nobody looked at anybody. That was the worst part. Forty people who had worked beside him for years, all suddenly fascinated by their own pages.

Odile looked down at her hands. They were steady. She hated them for it.

* * *

She packed by candlelight, in her narrow room, with the shutter closed and a towel pushed against the gap beneath the door.

She did not pack much. Two dresses, stockings, the bone comb, a knife with a wrapped handle, her strongbox of saved wages — eleven crowns and a handful of silver, the exact sum of four years of careful living. Money was a language too, and she spoke it fluently.

The rest of the space in her satchel went to the grimoire.

It had belonged to Mistress Hessa, the hedge-witch who had taken her in at thirteen, fed her, taught her letters and numbers and the small honest uses of a small dishonest gift — and who had died in the fire. Odile had pulled the book from the ashes herself, three years ago, her palms blistering, and had carried it from town to town until she reached Vhalen, and had kept it ever since wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her trunk, never opened, never read, because opening it meant remembering, and remembering meant—

She unwrapped it now. The cover was scorched leather, soft as skin. Inside, Hessa's handwriting marched in its crabbed brown ink: remedies, charms of finding, charms of hiding, and in the last third of the book, the pages Odile had never let herself study — the ones about power, about what a gift becomes if you feed it, about the difference between a candle and a conflagration.

*I should leave it,* she thought. *Burn it, even. Corvane is right. Carry it smaller than small.*

But her hands were already wrapping it again, already stowing it, and she recognized the truth even as she lied to herself about it: she would rather be caught with it than live without it. It was the last thing Hessa had touched that still smelled of her — woodsmoke and rosemary and lamp-oil — and Odile had lost enough to fires.

She sat on the edge of the cot a moment longer and made herself do the accounting. It was how she steadied herself; it was the only prayer she had ever learned.

*Nine days since the proclamation, minus three. Six remain. One fellow clerk taken, fate presumed. One guildmaster complicit by silence. One entry in the guild's books that points upward, toward the crown, toward a purchase of quiet that no one has explained. Assets: eleven crowns, one grimoire, one gift — small, hidden, and now worth a death. Liabilities: everything.*

She blew out the candle and stood in the dark and listened to the city. Somewhere distant, a mob was singing a loyal song outside a tavern. Somewhere closer, boots passed in the street, unhurried, in pairs. The hunt had begun, and it would not stop at hedge-witches and ink-singers. Hunts like this never did. First they came for the gifted, and then for those who sheltered them, and then for anyone the lists said had ever *looked* at them wrongly. She had read enough history to know how the columns added up.

Odile slung the satchel across her body, put on her hood, and left a single coin on the sill of her window — payment owed to the sweep-boy for the mornings he'd brought her water, because debts were debts even at the end of the world, especially at the end of the world.

She went down the back stairs, past the dark counting-house where Joreth's spilled ink had dried to a stain shaped like nothing at all, and out through the yard gate into the fog of the dockside streets.

She did not know where she was going. She knew only what she was carrying: a dead woman's book, a living woman's guilt, and a secret about the king that she had found in a ledger and did not yet understand.

Behind her, the Saltgate Guild slept on, its dangerous inventory ash, its books clean, its conscience balanced to the penny.

Ahead of her, the fog was full of hunters.

Odile pulled the hood lower, counted her heartbeats until they slowed — one, two, three, the old trick, the accountant's rosary — and walked into the dark to see what the dying men of this city would sell her.
