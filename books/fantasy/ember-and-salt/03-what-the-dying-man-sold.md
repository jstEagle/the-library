## 3. What the Dying Man Sold

The fog came off the harbor like something poured, thick and grey and tasting of brine and tar, and Odile moved through it the way she had learned to move through the guild's back rooms: quickly, quietly, and with her eyes down.

She had been running for two days.

Not well — she was a ledger-keeper, not a fugitive, and her body knew the difference. Her calves burned from cobbles and stairways. Her lungs carried a rasp that no amount of salt air would cure. She had slept four hours in the loft of a tannery that smelled of rendered fat and old blood, and she had dreamed of the proclamation, of the crier's voice cracking across the Salt Gate square like a whip:

*By order of the Crown: all persons gifted with unlicensed arts shall present themselves for registration within seven days. Those who do not shall be taken. Those who resist shall be burned.*

*Burned.* The word had a clean sound to it, official, inked and sealed. That was the horror of it — not rage but paperwork. Somewhere in the palace a clerk had written *burned* in a fair hand, and another clerk had checked it, and a third had stamped it, and now it was law.

She kept to the dockside because the docks were blind. Fog pooled between the warehouses in standing drifts; ropes and crates broke sightlines into fragments; and half the people who worked the water's edge owed the Saltglass Guild money, which meant they owed *her* money, which meant they would look at her face and see an account, not a bounty. It was thin protection. It was the only kind she had.

Under her arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, rode her mistress's grimoire. *Maren Eriksen's book*, though Maren had never been her mother despite the surname she'd given the orphan girl, and the book had never been gentle despite its soft leather cover. She had not opened it since the fire. She did not intend to open it now. She only intended to keep carrying it, the way you carry a debt you cannot pay and cannot put down.

*Registration,* she thought bitterly, ducking beneath a crane arm furred with wet rope. *I balance books. I know what things truly are. Shall I write that in the census column? Occupation: clerk. Peculiarity: I see the truth of objects when I touch them. Value of property: one forbidden book, one stolen life.*

Her gift — she made herself think the word plainly, because euphemism was how the crown won — her gift was small. That was what she'd always told herself. Small enough to hide. When she laid her hand on a thing, she knew what it was. Not what it looked like; what it *was*. She could hold a guild seal and feel whether it had been forged. She could take a merchant's coin and know if grief had passed through it. Once, balancing the accounts of a widow who sold bottled sorrow by the dram, she had touched a vial and felt, distinct as a voice, a man's last words to his daughter. She had put the vial down and gone outside and been sick into the harbor, and come back in, and finished the ledger, because that was what Odile Eriksen did. She finished the ledger.

Small gift. Hideable gift. Except the crown had stopped asking how big.

A bell began to ring somewhere behind her — not the harbor bell, which tolled slow and deep for weather, but a quick bright handbell, the kind hunters used to flush game. Voices answered it. Boots on stone, more than two pairs, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew the fog better than she did.

Odile stopped breathing, then made herself breathe again, slowly, because panic was loud even when the mouth was shut.

They were between her and the south stairs. The only other way off the quay was through the salt-sheds — long low buildings where fish was packed in barrels of coarse crystal — or down the water stair itself, into black water she couldn't swim in winter clothes with a book under her arm.

She chose the sheds.

Inside, the dark was total and the smell was enormous — salt so dense it was almost a texture, laying on her tongue, stinging the raw skin under her eyes. Barrels stood in ranks like a buried army. She felt her way along them, palm flat on the wood, and her gift woke without permission, whispering to her of each barrel's truth: *herring, herring, herring, herring, nothing, nothing, empty, herring—*

And then, faintly, from the far end of the shed, in the direction the hunters' voices were drifting toward: blood.

She didn't need her gift for that. Blood has a smell iron can't disguise, and under the salt it bloomed like a flower under snow. But her gift confirmed it anyway, reaching out through the dark before she could stop it, touching whatever lay beyond the last barrel and reporting back with terrible clarity: *a man, dying, alone, afraid.*

Every sensible part of her said *walk away*. Sensible parts had kept her alive this long. Sensible parts had finished ledgers while widows wept. She took one step toward the door and the fog outside swallowed a shout — closer now, inside the yard — and the sensible door closed, and the foolish one opened, and she went through it, because there are moments when hiding behind a barrel feels less safe than kneeling beside a bleeding stranger, and she would never afterward be able to explain why.

He was slumped against the wall with his legs out straight, a big man gone soft around the middle, wearing the good grey coat of a senior guildsman with the Saltglass clasp torn off. His left side was soaked black. A crossbow bolt stood in him below the ribs — hunter's iron, she saw at once, because her gift told her so the moment her knee touched the floor beside him: *cold iron, blessed iron, made to drink magic, and it is drinking him.*

"Tammas Vey," she breathed, before she could stop herself.

His eyes found her in the dark. They were clear, which was worse than glazed. Dying men with clear eyes have already done their arithmetic.

"The ledger-girl," he said. His voice was gravel in a sack. "Eriksen. The quiet one. Third desk by the window." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; she watched it cost him, watched his whole frame shudder around the bolt. "Of course. Of course it's you. Everyone else ran days ago."

"You should be lying down."

"I should be dead. I'm negotiating the interval." He caught her wrist with shocking strength. His hand was cold and slick. "Listen. They're in the yard. You have perhaps ninety heartbeats, and I have fewer, so we will do business quickly, you and I. I know your habit, girl. You don't take anything without writing it down. So write this down."

"I'm not taking anything—"

"You're taking everything." His grip tightened. "Everything I am is about to go into the ground unsold, and I have spent forty years in the Guild of Impossible Things, and I will not — *will not* — die holding inventory." He pulled her closer. Up close she could smell the iron on his breath, a smell like a struck nail. "One secret. The last one. Worth more than everything the guild ever traded, and I'm selling it to you for the price of a promise. Do you hear me? A promise. That's my price. Nothing else. Swear it first, then hear it, because if you hear it first you'll run, and if you run, it dies with me, and then it doesn't matter what they burn."

Outside, a boot scraped on stone. A voice, muffled by fog and planking: "Check the sheds. He went to ground somewhere."

Ninety heartbeats. Odile counted hers going too fast.

"What promise?" she whispered.

"That you'll finish it." His eyes held hers, and in them she saw the thing she had seen once in a dying fire, once in a drowning cat she'd pulled from a canal, the plain animal refusal to stop. "Whatever it costs. Whatever it makes you. You finish it. Swear."

"I swear," she said, because there was no other word in any language, and because she was already, she realized, crying, and had been for some time.

Something in his shoulders let go — not collapse, exactly. Settlement. An account closed.

"Good girl." He drew a breath that whistled. "Here is what I sold, three weeks ago, to a buyer whose name I wrote nowhere and will carry into the ground: I sold a year. One borrowed year, top grade, drawn off a healthy donor, the finest stock we handle. And the payment—" he coughed, wetly, "—the payment came signed with the privy seal. The king's own name. For delivery to the palace. The item purchased—" his voice dropped, and the fog outside seemed to lean in to listen, "—was entered in our books as *a crown's worth of quiet*."

"I know," Odile said. "I found the entry. I audited it. I thought it was a cipher, a smuggling code—"

"It's not a code." Tammas Vey smiled, and it was the worst thing she had ever seen, that smile, gentle and certain on a dying man's face. "It's a description. We deal in impossible things, girl. Grief in bottles. Years in jars. Names cut loose and folded into paper. And the crown bought *quiet* — silence, stillness, the cessation of a thing — a crown's worth of it. What do you imagine goes quiet, in a palace, when a king stops being a king?"

The bell rang again, nearer. Under the floorboards, through the soles of her shoes, she felt boots crossing the yard.

"I don't understand," she said.

"No," he agreed. "You will. Here's the shape of it, and then you run. Three weeks ago the king's own shadow-brother — no. No names. Names are currency and I'm nearly bankrupt." He gathered himself, and she leaned in, and his whisper was the wind through a keyhole. "The man on the throne is not the king. Something else wears him. Stitched together out of bought magic — years, strength, *quiet* — patched over the place where the true king used to be. I delivered the year myself. I stood in the receiving room. I looked at him." His hand spasmed on her wrist. "Girl, I have sold wonders to monsters for forty years and I know goods when I see them. That thing looked back at me with the king's eyes, and behind the eyes there was nothing. A shop window dressed for a customer who never comes."

"That's madness," she said automatically. "That's treason. That's—"

"That's the reason they're hunting the gifted." His voice hardened, and for a moment he sounded like the senior guildsman who had terrified her in her first week, correcting her columns. "Think. Use the head you keep ledgers with. Why now? Why a hunt, why registration, why fire — in the same season the crown starts buying raw magic by the jarful? They're not hunting the gifted, girl. They're *harvesting* us. Every witch they burn, every hedge-mage dragged to the pyres — you think the iron and the flame destroy what's in them? Iron drinks. That's what it's *for*. Everything they take goes into the vaults, and out of the vaults into *him*, and he grows, and the hunt grows with him, and there is no registration list, there is only a shopping list."

The world tilted very slightly, the way a floor tilts when a ship takes swell. Odile knelt in fish-salt and blood in the dark and felt her whole understanding of the last six days turn over like a page.

Her mistress. Maren Eriksen, burned in her own cottage — no. *She* had lit that fire, that was her sin, her secret, the thing she carried wrapped tighter than the grimoire — but the hunters had come because of it anyway, had traced the blaze, had asked questions in the village, and the questions had gone up the chain, and the chain had ended in a proclamation. She had thought herself a spark blown loose from someone else's catastrophe. She was beginning to understand there were no loose sparks anymore. There was only the harvest, and the hands that gathered.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why sell it to me? You could have—"

"Sold it to whom?" He laughed again, softly, terribly. "To the guild? The guildmaster burned his own vault rather than let the crown read his shelves. To another guildsman? Every one of them is running, or dead, or—" he tapped the bolt in his side, "—or spending their inventory. No. It needed someone who keeps accounts. Someone who audits. Because here is the last of it, the part I held back until you swore: the thing wearing the king was *made*, and things that are made have entries. Costs. Sources. A trail of payments, girl, a ledger line running from every stolen witch to the throne room, and nobody in this kingdom can read a trail like a clerk who hides her gift behind neat columns." His grip loosened at last, gently, like a father letting go of a child's hand at a gate. "You found one entry that shouldn't exist. Find the rest. Follow the money. Finish it."

"I don't know how," she said. "I'm a clerk. I balance books. I don't — I can't fight kings."

"No," he agreed. His eyes were going dimmer now, the arithmetic finally done. "But you can count. Counting brought down bigger men than him." His breath rattled. "The promise, Eriksen. Say it back."

"I promise I'll finish it."

"Whatever it costs."

"Whatever it costs," she repeated, and hated him, and loved him, and would carry those three words the rest of her life like a stone in the shoe.

"Good." He smiled again, smaller now. "Then get out. Back wall, third board, loose. There's a boatman called Dree who runs night cargo past the tide-gate; tell him Vey settled his account. And girl—" his hand flapped weakly at the air, dismissing her, blessing her, "—when they ask who told you, tell them a dying man sold cheap. He always was bad at business."

She got the board loose with shaking fingers. Cold air and fog poured through the gap. Behind her, Tammas Vey arranged himself against the barrels with the dignity of a man setting his affairs in order, and when she looked back once from the gap in the wall, he lifted two fingers — a clerk's salute, the sign they used across the counting-room to mean *entry received, account open* — and she lifted hers back, and then she was through, and running, and the shed was behind her.

She heard the door bang open as she reached the water stair. Heard voices, sharp, triumphant. Heard — she would always believe she heard, though the fog distorted everything and mercy might have invented it — heard Tammas Vey say, clearly, conversationally, "Gentlemen. Your timing is impeccable. I've just concluded a sale."

Then the fog took her, and the stair, and the black water slapping the pilings, and she ran along the strand with the grimoire under her arm and the promise in her chest like a second heartbeat, heavy, irregular, hers.

* * *

Dree the boatman took her past the tide-gate in a scow that smelled of eel and lamp-oil, and asked no questions, because Vey had settled his account, and whatever else could be said of the Saltglass Guild, its debts were honored. She crouched among the cargo with her arms around her knees and watched the city recede — the towers, the palace on its hill with its windows burning yellow and warm, one light in the high tower that she now knew, would always know, was a shop window dressed for no one.

*A crown's worth of quiet.*

She opened the ledger she kept in her head — the real one, the one no crown could seize — and made the entry in the dark, in the eel-smell, with salt spray drying white on her lips.

*Received: one secret, final stock of Tammas Vey, deceased. Price: one promise, payable in full, terms unknown.*
*Contents: the king is not the king. The hunt is a harvest. There is a trail of payments leading to the throne.*
*Balance outstanding: everything.*

The scow bumped the far shore. Ahead of her, past the mudflats, the south road ran away into hills full of fog and distance and the first grey suggestion of dawn, and somewhere on that road, she did not yet know, were a disgraced soldier with borrowed strength, a child who talked to drowned things, and a woman who forged souls — but the road knew, roads being patient, and it waited for her the way all debts wait.

Odile Eriksen climbed the muddy bank, settled the oilcloth bundle under her arm, and started walking.

Behind her, across the water, the palace light in the high tower went out — and then, a moment later, lit again, as though something had turned over in its sleep and opened one eye to check the ledgers of the world.

She walked faster.
