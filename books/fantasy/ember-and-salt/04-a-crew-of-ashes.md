## 4. A Crew of Ashes

The Salt Gate closed behind Odile at dawn, and she did not look back.

She wanted to. Her whole body ached with the wanting — to see the crooked skyline of Saltmere one last time, the guildhouse roof where she'd spent three years balancing other people's wonders, the narrow window of her attic room where her mistress's grimoire now sat wrapped in oilcloth against her spine like a second heart. But looking back was how they caught you. Tammas Vey had told her that with his last wet breath, and dead men's advice was the only kind worth taking whole.

So she walked. The coast road north was pale gravel and gorse, and the fog came off the sea in long gray fingers that touched her face like questions. *Where are you going, girl? What are you carrying? Who did you leave behind?*

She had no answers she liked.

Her feet found the rhythm of flight easily enough — she'd been practicing for years without knowing it, keeping her head down in the guildhall, keeping her gift folded small and dull as an unpolished coin. *Seeing what things truly are.* That was all it was. Not fire, not storm, not any of the grand and burning gifts the criers had named from the Salt Gate steps yesterday while a clerk she'd shared bread with was dragged through the crowd by his hair. Just seeing. A ledger-keeper's magic. She had always assumed it was too small to matter.

The proclamation had corrected her assumption. Small things, the crown had apparently decided, were still things. And things could be burned.

By midmorning the fog burned off and the road emptied, which should have been a comfort and instead felt like standing in an open field during lightning weather. Other travelers had taken the same advice she had: go early, go quiet, go alone. Alone was safer. Alone meant no one to sell you, no one to mourn you, no one whose screaming would draw the hunters to your hiding place.

Alone meant no one at all.

Odile counted her coins by the roadside out of habit — forty-one coppers, two silver bits, and a guild token Tammas had pressed into her palm along with his secret, good for one favor at any Saltglass counting-house still loyal to the old ways. She was doing the arithmetic of survival — forty-one coppers bought perhaps nine days of bread if she slept rough — when she heard the cart.

It came up behind her slow, its axle singing a flat note, drawn by a mule so old and gray it seemed assembled mostly from patience. On the bench sat a woman of indeterminate age wearing a widow's black gone green at the seams, and beside her, wrapped in sacking against the cold, a child.

The woman looked at Odile the way Odile herself looked at suspicious ledger entries. Thoroughly. With professional interest.

"You're walking like someone counting," the woman said. "Counting what?"

"Coins," Odile said. "And days."

"Mm." The woman clicked her tongue and the mule stopped, though Odile hadn't seen her pull the reins. "Days are the wrong currency this season. Get on. You can count better sitting down."

"I didn't say I was going where you're going."

"You didn't say you weren't. And your left hand hasn't left that bundle on your back since I first saw you. Nobody guards bread like that." The woman's eyes were the color of old ink. "That's a book, or something that ought to be. Get on, book-girl. The road's not safe for people who smell of paper and fear."

Every sensible part of Odile said *walk on*. Sensible parts had kept her alive for three years. But Tammas Vey had also been sensible, right up until a hunter's iron opened him like a letter, and his blood was still under her fingernails in the way that blood gets there even when you've washed.

She got on the cart.

* * *

The child was called Sefa, and Sefa did not speak so much as emit observations, in a voice like water over stones. "You have a river in you," the child announced ten minutes into the ride, staring at Odile with the frank horror of the very young. "A small one. It goes around and around."

"That's called a heartbeat," Odile said.

"No." Sefa shook their head — Odile couldn't tell if the child was boy or girl and suspected the child couldn't either, or cared to. "The river's under that. It's yours but it isn't. It counts things."

Odile's hand went, before she could stop it, to the grimoire.

"Not the book," Sefa said, almost pitying. "You."

Mother Quill — for that was the ink-eyed woman, and she offered the name the way other people offered a knife, handle-first but visible — laughed low in her throat. "Leave her be, sprat. Some folk don't want their insides read before breakfast."

"She glows," Sefa said, undeterred. "Not bright. Like a banked coal."

"And what do I glow like?" Mother Quill asked, mild as milk.

Sefa considered. "Like a page with writing on it that keeps changing."

"Hm," said Mother Quill, and said nothing else for a mile.

They traveled north through country that grew progressively less tended — hedgerows collapsing into scrub, fields gone to thistle, a shrine to some road-saint toppled into a ditch with its painted face peeling toward the sky. The Gift-Hunt's shadow lay over everything, Odile realized. Not soldiers yet, not here, but the absence of them: villages where people watched the road and then carefully watched away, doors that stayed shut past reasonable hours, a hanged man outside one hamlet with a wooden sign nailed above him reading GIFTED in letters that had been cut by someone in a hurry and a fury.

Sefa looked at the hanged man for a long time.

"Don't," Mother Quill said quietly.

"He's still talking," Sefa said. "They always talk for a while. He says he wasn't. He says he just knew things about fish." The child's small hands curled in the sacking. "He wants someone to tell his daughter he wasn't."

Odile looked at the child — really looked, with the plain eyes she used for ledgers — and saw what she should have seen at once: a hunted thing, small and exhausted, running on nothing. "How long since you've eaten?" she asked, and dug out her bread before anyone answered.

That was the moment, though she wouldn't know it for years. Not the getting on the cart. The bread.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a tollhouse, its roof half fallen, its little iron stove still sound. Mother Quill built a fire with a economy that suggested long practice at building fires that must be small, warm, and deniable. And it was there, over barley tea and the last of Odile's bread, that the third member of their accidental company stepped out of the dark and nearly died for it.

Odile saw him first — her gift flaring before her mind caught up, showing her the truth of the shape at the edge of the firelight. And the truth was strange. Most people, to her sight, were simple: flesh and want and a small flicker of whatever they carried inside. This man was a contradiction. He was enormous — a head taller than any soldier she'd seen, shoulders like a doorframe, hands that could palm her skull — and underneath the enormity he was *empty*, hollowed like a looted house, and the bigness sat on him the way a rich coat sits on a beggar. Borrowed. Every ounce of it borrowed.

"Bran," Mother Quill said, unsurprised. "You're late."

"Roads are slow when you're checking every shadow twice." He came into the light, and up close the falseness of his size was worse; his wrists were thin where the sleeves rode back, and there was a tremor in his left hand he kept trying to hide by making a fist. His eyes went around the circle — Quill, Sefa, and then Odile, where they stopped. "New?"

"Book-girl," Quill said. "Guild clerk, or was. Runs from the hunt like the rest of us."

"Wasn't hunting me," the big man said. "Hunting my kind doesn't need a proclamation. We've been hunted by professionals for years." He lowered himself to sit by the fire, and the ground seemed to brace itself. "Brannic," he said to Odile. "Late of the Ninth Lances. Currently of nowhere."

"What did you do?" Odile asked, because she was tired and frightened and it slipped out.

Quill snorted. Brannic didn't. He looked into the fire with an expression she would come to know well and hate: the look of a man reading a bill he already memorized.

"Took a strength-not-strength off a hedge-charlatan in Vail Cross, the night before a battle we were losing. Paid him everything I had. Held the ford." A shrug that moved his whole impossible frame. "Six hundred men crossed home because of it. Then the army found out what I'd done, and six hundred men decided they'd won the battle themselves, honestly, and I was a cheat and a freak and worse than a deserter, because at least deserters don't win." The tremor in his hand got worse; he pressed it flat against his knee. "They're right, mind. It is cheating. It's just that the alternative was dying."

"How much did you take?" Odile asked. Ledger instinct. Assets against liabilities.

His eyes came up, surprised. "Enough," he said, which was an answer shaped like a wall.

"He took years," Sefa said softly, from the child's nest of sacking. "I can hear them. They're loud. Like coins in a big purse, but they spend fast."

"Sefa." Quill's voice had an edge.

"It's true. He's almost empty. There's only a little left and it's going *fast*, faster since yesterday, and underneath there's hardly any of him at all—"

"*Sefa.*"

The child shut up. Brannic stared into the fire, jaw working, and Odile understood suddenly and completely why he checked every shadow twice, why he'd come into the firelight at all when staying dark was safer. He was spending himself down to the wick, and he knew it, and he'd come looking for these two anyway.

Nobody had ever taught Odile what to do with information like that. Ledgers didn't weep. So she did the only thing she knew: she reached into her pack, took out her tally-book, and said, "Then we budget."

Three faces turned to her.

"If you've got — call it reserves, call it coin, it doesn't matter — and you're spending faster than you meant to, then we plan around it. When to spend, when to hoard, what it buys us. That's all accounting is. Deciding what runs out last." She heard her own voice go flat and professional and was grateful for it. "I kept books for the Saltglass Guild for three years. Impossible things, traded daily. Your strength is just another entry. Give me the numbers and I'll keep you alive longer than you would on your own."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You're offering to manage me," Brannic said slowly, "like an account."

"I'm offering to keep the books on how you die," Odile said. "Someone should. You clearly weren't going to."

And Brannic — huge, ruined, borrowed Brannic — started to laugh. It was a rusty sound, a hinge unused for years, and it cracked halfway through into something else that he disguised badly as coughing. "Quill," he managed. "Where did you find her?"

"On the road," Quill said, "walking like someone counting." She poured a fourth cup of barley tea and pushed it across the stones to Odile with one ink-stained finger. "Drink, book-girl. You're one of us now, gods help the lot of us."

"I'm not," Odile said. "One of you. I'm traveling in the same direction. Temporarily."

"Of course," said Mother Quill, in the tone of a woman entering figures into a ledger she fully expected to balance.

* * *

They moved north together for four days, and Odile learned the shape of them the way she learned anything: by observation, entry, and reluctant revision.

Mother Quill drove the cart and spoke maybe fifty words a day, but Odile noticed that every village they passed through, the woman could produce papers — travel writs, widow's certificates, a bill of sale for the mule — that were flawless. Not forged-looking. *Flawless.* Once, at a checkpoint outside Ferrow Bridge where a bored sergeant actually read the documents, Odile watched with her gift open and saw the truth: the papers weren't paper at all, not entirely. They were names. Quill had written names into them — real ones, taken from somewhere, worn like coats — and the sergeant's own eyes slid off the forgery because the forgery believed in itself harder than he did.

Afterward Odile asked, "Whose name was on my travel writ?"

"A dead girl's," Quill said. "She won't miss it."

"Where did you get it?"

Quill looked at her for a long moment, ink eyes unreadable. "Book-girl," she said finally, "every trade has stock. Ask me again when you've paid for something."

Sefa talked to drowned things. That was the plain fact of it, and Odile stopped doubting it the second morning, when the child knelt at a rain-barrel behind a waystation for an hour and then reported, matter-of-factly, that a woman named Aldith Marn had drowned in a well in Ferrow Bridge eleven years ago and her wedding ring was still in the silt, third stone from the left, and would someone please get it because her husband had never stopped looking. They got it. The husband cried on the doorstep of the waystation and gave them eggs.

Sefa heard the dead in water the way Odile heard numbers: constantly, involuntarily, in a language nobody else was fluent in. And the child clung — to Quill's sleeve, to Brannic's enormous finger, and, with a gradualness that Odile resisted noticing, to her.

"You count in your sleep," Sefa informed her on the third night. "Your lips move. Numbers."

"Ledger habit."

"When the bad thing happened to you — the fire—" and Odile went cold all over, because she had never told them about any fire, "—the numbers stopped. For a whole year, the river in you says. You counted nothing. Isn't that strange? Counting is how you hold on."

"Go to sleep, Sefa."

But the child's words followed her down into sleep, and the sleep was full of smoke.

Because yes. There had been a fire. Mistress Ysolde's cottage, three winters ago, burned to its foundation stones in a single night, and Odile Eriksen had walked out of the ashes with a grimoire and a story about a lamp knocked over, and no one had ever questioned it, because who questions a grieving apprentice? She had told the story so many times it had grooves worn in it, smooth as a stair. Lamp. Wind. Tragic.

The story was load-bearing. If she pulled it out, she did not know what of her would stand.

So she kept it, and kept the others at the distance of a cart's width, and told herself the lie about temporary.

* * *

On the fifth day the road changed.

Odile felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in her feet, a sense of walking on something aware. The old highway they'd joined that morning was ancient beyond reckoning, paved in stones worn round as river pebbles, and where most roads ran straight or bent with the land, this one *wandered*. It curved when nothing obliged it to curve. It climbed a hill that no surveyor would have chosen. And the hedges along it were grown into shapes that, if you looked too long, resolved into faces.

"The Old North Road," Brannic said grimly, when Odile asked. "My grandmother swore it was dug up from somewhere older and laid down wrong. Soldiers hated marching it. Said it rearranged itself behind you."

"It doesn't rearrange," Quill said. "It *remembers*. Different thing, worse thing." She reined the mule to a stop and sat looking down the long green tunnel of it, and for the first time since Odile had met her, Mother Quill looked uncertain. "Roads like this show you to yourself. That's the old stories and the old stories are usually advertisements, but this one—" She exhaled. "There was a time I'd have said let's walk around. Three days east, the river way."

"But?" Odile said.

"But there's a hunter's patrol on the river way. Sefa heard it off a ditch this morning. Four men and a leashed finder-beast." Quill's jaw tightened. "So it's the remembering road, or it's iron. Choose."

They chose the road, because four men and a beast was a certainty and a story was only a story.

They walked into the green tunnel as the afternoon slanted gold, and the road took them the way the sea takes swimmers — gently at first, then with intent. The hedges' faces grew more distinct. The stones underfoot warmed like skin. And Odile began to feel it working on her, a tide pulling at the sealed door inside her chest where she kept the fire, the real fire, the one that was not a lamp and not wind.

Beside her, Brannic had gone pale under his sunburn, staring at his own borrowed hands as though the road were showing him the original owner. Ahead, Sefa had begun to hum — a drowning song, low and bubbling. And Mother Quill walked with her eyes fixed straight ahead and her lips moving, and Odile, catching it with her gift before she could close it, saw that the old woman was *reciting names*, dozens of them, holding them up between herself and the road like a shield made of other people's souls.

The road noticed Odile noticing.

She felt it turn its attention on her the way a clerk turns a fresh page, and somewhere far ahead, where the gold light thickened into something like a doorway, she saw the shimmer begin — the road preparing to show them their truest selves, whether they had paid for the viewing or not.

And deep under her breastbone, behind three years of smooth-worn lies, the fire stirred and asked, in Mistress Ysolde's voice, *are you ready to enter me into the record?*

Odile's step faltered.

The road, patiently, began to open the ledger of her.
