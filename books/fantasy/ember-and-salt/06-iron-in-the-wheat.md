## 6. Iron in the Wheat

Brannic had learned to count his strength the way a miser counts coins, and on the morning they reached the wheat country he had less than he pretended.

It was always like this after the road. The Road That Remembers had taken something from each of them — he'd felt it pull at him as they walked its length, tugging loose the truths he kept buried, and though he'd shown it nothing worse than a soldier's shame, the showing itself had cost him. Borrowed strength did not replenish. That was the first law of it, the one the flesh-mongers in the war camps never put in their pretty contracts: what you spend, you do not get back. What you are given, you pay for later, with interest.

He was paying now. His knees ached in a way knees shouldn't ache for a man of thirty-two. His hands, big enough to crack walnuts whole, trembled faintly around the strap of his pack, and he hid the trembling by gripping harder.

Ahead of him on the track, Odile walked with her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, and Sefa skipped along the wheel-ruts, and Mother Quill brought up the rear with her ink-stained fingers laced behind her back, humming something old and off-key.

Four of them. A ragged little company of ash and secrets, walking through gold.

The wheat came up past Brannic's waist on either side of the track, heavy-headed and dry, rustling with a sound like rain that never fell. It smelled of dust and sweetness and the hot green breath of high summer. Somewhere ahead there was supposed to be a farmstead — Quill's map said so, a place called Harrow's End where a widow sold milk and eggs and asked no questions, one of the quiet stops on the smugglers' road south.

"Two days of this," Sefa announced, to no one and everyone. "Then the sea again. The drowned ones say the coast is kind this time of year."

"The drowned ones," Brannic said, "should mind their own business."

"They say *you* should eat more." She looked back at him, her small face solemn under the tangle of her hair. "They know about spending."

He grunted. Children and dead people, both of them too sharp-eyed for comfort.

Odile glanced back too, and he saw her do the thing she did — the flicker of her eyes over him, quick and assessing, like a clerk tallying stock. He'd caught her at it before. She counted everything: rations, miles, the coins in Quill's false bottom, the hours between hunter patrols. He'd thought at first it was just the habit of a guild clerk, but he'd come to understand it was more than habit. It was armor. The girl had been burned by magic — he'd seen the way she flinched from her own reflection some mornings — and she trusted numbers because numbers never lied to you, never grew teeth, never asked you to be something you weren't.

He liked her for it. He'd have died for any of them by now, he suspected, which was inconvenient, because he'd already planned on dying for exactly one thing, and he hadn't expected to add three more names to the list.

The farmstead appeared an hour past noon: a low stone house with a thatched roof going soft at the ridge, a barn with its door sagging, a well with a crooked crane-arm, and fields beyond stitched with bean-rows. Chickens scattered as they came up the lane. No dogs. Brannic noticed that first, the way he noticed all wrongness — no dogs, and the gate unlatched, and the widow's washing still strung between two poles, shirts gone stiff and yellow in the sun.

Nobody left washing out for days unless they meant to come back to it.

"Wait here," he said.

Quill raised an eyebrow. "You're giving orders now?"

"I'm giving sense." He shifted the pack off his shoulders, and the relief of it nearly made him groan. "Sefa stays with you. Odile—"

"I'm coming with you," Odile said.

He wanted to argue. He looked at her instead — thin, pale, a ledger-clerk in a man's coat with a hedge-witch's grimoire hidden against her ribs — and saw the set of her jaw and knew arguing would cost more time than it saved.

"Stay behind me," he said. "Touch nothing."

* * *

The house was empty in the way that made the skin crawl. Not looted-empty. *Left*-empty. A bowl of cherries on the table had gone to sugar and flies. A doll lay in the corner of the main room, face-down, one arm torn off. In the hearth, ashes cold for days, and scratched into the soot-black stone above them, deep and deliberate, a mark Brannic knew from the war:

A circle with a slash through it. The Gift-Hunt's brand. *Cleared.*

"They took them," Odile whispered. She stood in the doorway, not crossing the threshold, her eyes moving over the room the way they moved over a page. "Or they ran. Or—" She stopped. Her gaze snagged on the doll, and something went through her face, quick and swallowed down.

"Widow was on Quill's list as neutral," Brannic said. "Quiet hands. Paid her tolls same as anyone."

"Paid them until someone decided she didn't have to anymore." Odile crouched by the hearth, studying the mark without touching it. "This is fresh. Three days, maybe four. And look—" she pointed, not quite touching, "—the scratches at the edge. More than one hand made this. They let the new men practice."

Soldiers practicing cruelty on a hearthstone. Brannic had seen it before, in a dozen burned villages across two wars. It never stopped making his jaw ache.

"We should go around," he said. "Sleep in the field, move at dawn."

"There's water in the well," Odile said quietly. "And we haven't drunk properly since yesterday. And Sefa's feet are bleeding through her boots — I've been watching her walk on the outside edges for two days because she doesn't want to slow us down."

He stared at her. "You've been counting her steps?"

"I count everything." She said it without apology. "The well is forty paces from the treeline. We can draw water, fill skins, and be gone inside ten minutes if we're disciplined. But we should assume the hunters who marked this place will come back. They always come back. Hunters patrol their cleared ground like creditors checking collateral." She paused, and something dry and grim crossed her face. "Believe me. I know how creditors think."

So they drew the water. And Brannic, who had survived two wars by breaking every rule of caution at least once, let himself believe — just this once, just for an hour — that the girl with the ledger might be right that the world could be managed like an account.

That was the hour the iron came.

* * *

He heard the horses first. Four sets of hooves, maybe five, moving at a walk along the far side of the wheat, coming up the cart-track toward the farmstead from the north. Brannic was at the well when the sound reached him, and every hair on his arms rose, and the old war-cold slid down his spine like a blade laid flat against skin.

"Sefa," he breathed. "Inside. Now. Quiet as drowning."

She was already moving — the child had a genius for vanishing — but she'd left her boots by the well-bucket. Little cracked boots, stuffed with moss to pad the holes, sitting in plain view on the stones.

And the wheat parted.

There were six of them. Brannic counted without wanting to: six riders in the king's grey, faces wrapped, long hunting-spears with iron caps, and at the front a broad man on a dun mare with a badge on his chest that Brannic recognized and wished to every god he didn't — the crowned eye. A *seeker*. Not common hunt-braves but a seeker-captain, one of the usurper's own, gifted himself or worse, and his eyes were already moving across the yard, reading it: the wet bucket, the disturbed dust, the small boots.

The seeker's gaze found the boots and stopped.

"*Well-well,*" the man said, and his voice carried the pleasant, unhurried tone of a man reading a debt notice aloud. "*Little feet.*"

Everything after that happened fast, the way bad things do.

Brannic moved. There was no decision in it — decision was for slower moments. His body simply spent what it had. He crossed the yard in four strides that ate ground like a landslide, hit the nearest rider's spear-arm with his shoulder, and felt the bone go with a crunch he heard through the man's scream. The rider came off his horse and Brannic turned the falling man into a shield as the second spear came down; the iron head punched through the man's back and out the chest and Brannic wrenched sideways, tearing it free, and threw the body into the third horse's legs.

Horses screamed. Wheat flattened. Somewhere behind him Sefa was screaming too, high and thin, and then Odile's voice cutting through it, hard as a whip-crack: "*Sefa! The barn! RUN!*"

He got one more of them — a fist like a millstone into a jaw, the man dropping like cut rope — and then the seeker-captain came off his mare, and the air around him *bent*, and Brannic understood with a soldier's old, sick clarity that this one was not going to fall to fists.

The captain's hand closed on empty air and the air became a wall. Brannic hit it at a run and the world rang like a struck bell and he went down into the dirt with his ears bleeding.

Get up. Get up, you useless—

He got up. Of course he got up. That was the whole curse of him, the whole contract: he got up, and the strength came, and the strength was not his, and every drop of it was being drawn out of a purse with a hole in the bottom.

Sefa was running for the barn. One of the remaining hunters wheeled his horse and leveled his spear at her small, flying figure, and Brannic did the arithmetic he'd sworn never to do again, the arithmetic that had gotten his whole company killed at Redford Bridge, and the answer came out the same as it always came out.

He spent.

He crossed the yard faster than anything human should move, and took the spear meant for a child full in the meat of his own shoulder, snapping the shaft, and used the broken half to open the rider from knee to hip, and then the yard tilted, and the sky was under him, and the wheat was very golden against a very blue sky, and somewhere far away a child was crying his name.

*Brannic. Brannic. BRANNIC.*

Dark, then. Warm dark, like the bottom of a river.

* * *

He surfaced to pain and voices and the smell of blood and crushed wheat.

"—not asking you to heal him, I'm telling you what he needs, and what he needs is *time*, and time is the one thing we don't—"

"He'll die if we move him."

"Then we make the moving cheap. Everything has a cost, Quill. You taught me that. So we find the cheapest cost and we *pay it*."

Brannic opened his eyes. Sky, going amber at the edges. Odile's face above him, smudged with his blood, her hands pressed hard on his shoulder where someone had bound it with stripped shirt-linen gone red through twice. Behind her, dusk, and the farmstead silent, and the bodies of hunters dragged into a row by the fence like entries in a grim inventory.

"How many," he rasped.

"Three dead, three fled, one horse taken." She said it flatly, the way she read figures. Then her voice cracked despite her. "Don't talk. Save your strength."

"Don't have any," he said, and tried to laugh, and it came out as a cough that hurt all the way down. "That's rather the point of me, girl. Spent purse. You're looking at the last coin."

"Stop it." Her hands pressed harder. "Quill says the wound will kill you if it festers. Sefa says the drowned things say—" she faltered, steadied. "Sefa says your strength isn't healing you anymore. That it's *gone quiet*. Whatever you borrowed, it's almost paid back. Isn't it?"

He looked at her. Lying to her seemed suddenly both easy and obscene.

"How much?" she demanded. "In numbers, soldier. You're talking to a clerk. Give me the account."

He breathed. The pain came in waves, patient as tide.

"A week," he said. "Maybe two, if I spend nothing. After that—" he moved his ruined hand vaguely, taking in his own body, "—the loan comes due. All of it. Every mile I ever ran, every wall I ever broke, everything I lifted that wasn't mine to lift. It collects."

Silence. Above them the first stars were pricking through the amber.

"That's why you walk like an old man," Odile said softly. "That's why you eat like a starving man and sleep like a dead one. You've been dying by inches this whole time, and you let us walk beside you without saying a word."

"You had enough ledgers," Brannic said. "Didn't want to be one more entry in the loss column."

Her eyes were bright and furious. "You absolute fool. You're not a *loss*. You're—" she stopped, jaw working, and he watched her rebuild herself out of habit, brick by brick, the clerk's calm sliding over the girl underneath. "Fine. Fine. Then we treat this like what it is. An audit."

"*An audit.*"

"You have a week of reserves. I intend to see none of it wasted." She was already pulling his pack over, digging through it with brisk, bloody fingers. "Which means we don't run tonight. Which means we need to know exactly when those three riders come back, and from which direction, and how many friends they bring. Hunters patrol their cleared ground. You said it yourself — they come back like creditors. So we map them."

She sat back on her heels, and in the last light her eyes had gone strange and steady, the eyes of a woman reading a page no one else could see.

"Six riders came today. Three broke and ran — wounded men run home, they don't wander. Their captain will report a fight, casualties, a child escaped. Seekers don't forgive failure, so they'll return reinforced, but reinforcements take time to muster and provision — call it two days, three at most. They'll come up the north track again, because the south approach is marsh and they'll not drag horses through it twice. They'll search the house first, then the barn, then work outward in a sweep-line through the wheat, because that's how sweep-lines are drilled and drill is all most of these men have." She ticked each item off on her fingers like closing columns. "Which means there is a window tomorrow night, after their scouts pass and before the main sweep, when the north track is empty. We cross it, we take the marsh path south, and we're a rumor by dawn."

Brannic stared at her. Beside her, Mother Quill had gone very still, ink-stained fingers pressed to her mouth. From the barn doorway, Sefa peered out with her enormous eyes, and said, with the terrible gravity of children:

"The drowned ones say she counts like the tide counts. They're scared of her a little."

"She should be scared of herself," Quill murmured, and there was something in her voice Brannic couldn't name — wonder, or warning, or both.

Odile ignored them all. She was looking at Brannic, waiting, her bloody hands still steady on his shoulder, and he understood suddenly that she wasn't asking permission. She was presenting the accounts and expecting his signature.

"Well, soldier?" she said. "Do we balance?"

He laughed — really laughed, even though it tore at the wound — and gripped her wrist with his good hand.

"Aye," he said. "We balance."

* * *

They moved at moonrise, and it went almost exactly as she'd reckoned, which frightened Brannic more than the hunters had.

The scout passed on the north track an hour after dark — one rider, walking his horse, bored, alone. Odile counted his pace by the sound of the hooves and gave them the signal on the exact beat she'd promised. They crossed the track in single file, Brannic half-carried between Quill and Odile, his shoulder screaming, his legs answering only because he told them stories about duty. The marsh took them at midnight, black water and whispering reeds, and Sefa walked ahead of them all like a little lantern, the drowned things parting the paths for her, murmuring their directions through her lips in voices like wind over wrecks.

Dawn found them on the far side, on a low hill with the wheat country burning gold behind them and the coast a grey line far ahead.

Brannic sat propped against a milestone, white as whey, and watched Odile stand at the crest with the wind pulling her coat, doing what she always did: counting. Miles. Rations. Days. He wondered if she'd counted him yet — truly counted him, entered him honestly in whatever book she kept behind her eyes. One disgraced soldier, strength borrowed and nearly spent, value declining daily.

But she'd looked at him last night like he was worth saving, and she'd mapped death itself into columns and beaten it, and when Sefa curled up against his good side and slept, and Quill stood watch with her back straight as a quill-stroke, he thought: *this is what I bought with all that stolen strength, in the end. This. Worth every coin.*

"Two days to the coast," Odile said, sitting down beside him, opening her ledger across her knees. "If we're careful. And Brannic—" she glanced at him, and for once there was no armor in her face at all, just the girl, tired and fierce and young. "Thank you. For Sefa. For the spear."

"Any clerk would've done the same."

"No," she said. "No, they wouldn't." She bent her head over the page and began to write, and he watched her pen move, recording the day — the cost, the loss, the survival — and he thought that whatever she was writing him down as, he hoped she'd be generous.

It was Quill, at midmorning, who brought the news up the hill from the road below, where she'd gone to listen at a shrine. She came back fast, her face grey under its brown, and the words fell out of her like dropped coins.

"There's a proclamation posted at the crossroads shrine. Fresh ink." She swallowed. "The Guild of Quiet Hands has been named. Traitors to the crown. Every sanctuary, every neutral house, every safe-road on the smugglers' network — seized or burned. And there's a reward posted, Odile. A *great* reward." Her eyes found the girl's face. "For a runaway clerk of the Saltglass Guild. Wanted alive."

The wind moved the wheat, and Odile's pen stopped mid-word.

"They know my name," she said quietly.
