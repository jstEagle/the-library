## 8. Sefa Hears the Sea Count

The sea did not speak in words. It never had.

It spoke the way rain spoke against a window, the way a mother's breathing sounded through a wall — in rhythms that meant something if you lay very still and let them into your chest. Sefa knew this the way other children knew their own names. She had known it before she could walk, back when she was small enough to fit inside a fisherman's boot and the tide came up to kiss her toes and tell her things.

*Count with us,* the sea said now. *Count with us, little counter.*

They had made camp in the ribs of an old ship, beached and broken on a grey shingle beach below cliffs the color of old teeth. Mother Quill said it was called the Graveyard of Second Chances, because smugglers used to run ships aground here on purpose to cheat their insurers, and half the wrecks still held something worth finding. Sefa thought it was called that because of the drowned ones. There were so many here. They pooled in the shallows like oil, like spilled ink, like all the dark things at the bottom of a cup when you drink too fast.

She sat cross-legged on the sand with her hands flat on the wet stones, and she listened.

Brannic was up the beach, pretending to mend a net he had found, though his hands shook and Sefa could hear — not hear, *feel*, the way you feel weather coming — how tired he was. Tired down in the place where borrowed things sit. Odile was in the wreck's belly with the candle and the ledgers, always the ledgers, scratching her little numbers like a bird pecking at ice. And Mother Quill walked the tideline with her skirts gathered up, looking for nothing, which is what she did when she was thinking about her daughter.

Sefa did not look at any of them. Looking made the listening harder, and tonight the listening mattered, because tonight the sea was counting.

*One,* said the water over her knuckles. *Two. Three.*

It counted the way it always counted: everything it had ever taken. Every hull, every sailor, every coin spilled from a split purse, every name the waves had closed over. The drowned ones kept the count. That was their work now, their whole work, forever — they remembered what the sea took, because the sea itself forgot. The sea only wanted. The drowned ones remembered.

Usually the count was long and slow and sad and beautiful, like a lullaby sung by someone who had forgotten how lullabies ended. But tonight there was a wrongness in it. Tonight the count stumbled.

*Four hundred and eleven thousand and six,* murmured a voice just under the surface, a woman's voice, full of weed and patience. *Four hundred and eleven thousand and seven — no. No, little one. Strike the seventh. Strike him out.*

Sefa opened her eyes. "Struck out?" she asked, because you were allowed to ask the drowned things questions; they liked it, it meant you were listening properly. "Who's struck out?"

The shallows went still. Even the foam seemed to hold its breath. Then, all along the tideline, the drowned ones began to whisper at once, the way a congregation whispers when the wrong person walks through the church door.

*Him,* they said. *The one who was taken out of the count. The one who was never allowed to drown, never allowed to float, never allowed to be anything at all. The uncounted one. He has been walking on the land wearing a shape stitched from what was stolen, and the sea remembers him even though nothing else does, because the sea forgets nothing except by accident, and this was no accident.*

Sefa's toes curled in the cold sand. She was nine years old and she had spoken to drowned things since before she could say *mama*, and they had never once frightened her, not even the one with the anchor chain, not even the ones from the deep trenches where the fish had lights on their faces. But this frightened her, a little, the way a door opening in an empty house frightens you — not because of what comes through, but because of what it means about the house.

"Tell me," she said. "Tell me about the uncounted one."

And the sea told her.

* * *

She did not understand all of it. Some of it came as pictures instead of words, and some of it came as feelings with no pictures at all, and some of it came as a number, which was the worst, because numbers were supposed to mean that somebody had counted, and this number meant nobody ever had.

There had been two princes born on one night, the drowned ones said. Twins. The midwife washed them both in the same basin. One cried loud and one cried soft, and the soft-crying one was born second, and being born second was the whole of his crime.

Their father looked at the second one and saw nothing. Not hatred — hatred would have been something. He saw nothing, the way you see nothing when you look at a chair. A king needed one heir. Two was an embarrassment, a mistake of arithmetic, and kings do not keep mistakes.

So they wrote him out.

Not killed — killing leaves a body, and a body leaves a grave, and a grave leaves a story. They did it cleaner than that. They paid the priests to strike his name from the baptismal rolls. They paid the chroniclers to begin the year's history with the words *the king was delivered of a single son*. They paid the midwife, and when money was not enough for the midwife, they paid her differently. They paid everyone. It cost the treasury a fortune, the drowned ones said, and the drowned ones knew about fortunes, because most of theirs were at the bottom of the sea.

The boy grew up in the palace like a ghost grows up in a house. Fed, clothed, taught his letters — but never named aloud, never painted, never presented. Servants were instructed not to see him. When ambassadors visited, he was locked in a room with the windows shuttered. His brother, the loud-crying one, the heir, loved him — this part came through as a feeling, sharp and aching, like a splinter under a fingernail. The heir used to sneak him sweets. Used to call him by a secret name, a nursery name, the only name he had. Used to swear, with a child's terrible solemnity, *when I am king I will make them all see you.*

But then the heir became king, and a king is a busy thing, and promises made in nurseries are made in a country that stops existing.

And the uncounted one grew, and learned, and watched, and understood that he had been given the one punishment worse than death: he had been made into a bookkeeping error.

That phrase — *bookkeeping error* — did not come from the drowned ones. That came later, from Odile. But the feeling of it came from the sea, cold and precise, and Sefa hugged her knees when it touched her.

Because here was the thing the drowned ones whispered last, the thing that made the whole tideline shiver: the uncounted one had found magic. Or magic had found him — it goes to the overlooked, sometimes, pours itself into empty places the way water fills a hole. And he had learned that names could be cut loose, that years could be borrowed, that a man could be unwritten — and he had gone to his brother's court not with an army but with a ledger of his own, and he had begun, entry by entry, to take everything his brother had.

His face first. Then his voice. Then his years, drunk down like wine. Then his name, which was the crown jewel of the collection, the final entry, the one that made the theft complete — because a king without a name cannot be a king, and a thief with a king's name can wear the throne like a coat.

*He sits there now,* the drowned ones whispered. *Stitched together out of stolen magic, wearing his brother's shape, and he is not wicked the way storms are wicked or sharks are wicked. He is wicked the way a wound is wicked — because something was taken from him first, and he never learned any way to fill the hole except by taking.*

*And here is the mercy in it, little counter, and here is the horror:* *he can be unmade the same way he was made. What was erased can be erased again. But what was erased can also be written back.*

Sefa sat on the cold shingle until the moon was high, and the drowned ones sank back into their counting, four hundred and eleven thousand and six, and she got up on legs gone numb and went to find Odile.

* * *

Odile was where Odile always was: bent over the books by candlelight, quill moving, lips slightly parted in the expression she wore when the numbers were talking to her. Up close, in the flicker, she looked older than she probably was and younger than she acted, and there was a smudge of ink on her cheekbone like a beauty mark drawn by someone careless.

"Sefa." She didn't look up. "You're freezing. Where's your blanket?"

"The sea told me something."

That made the quill stop. Odile looked up, and Sefa watched her do the thing she always did — the quick flicker of her eyes, the little pause, like she was checking Sefa over the way she checked the ledgers, line by line, looking for errors. Sefa didn't mind. Being checked carefully was its own kind of being cared about. Brannic checked people by standing between them and danger. Mother Quill checked people by forging them papers. Odile checked people by reading them like accounts, and Sefa had decided long ago that this was love too, just in a different handwriting.

"Sit down," Odile said, and moved the candle closer, and pulled her cloak off and put it around Sefa's shoulders without being asked, which was also love in a different handwriting. "Tell me slowly. Everything. Leave nothing out — you know I hate gaps in an account."

So Sefa told her. About the count that stumbled. About the two princes in one basin. About the paying of the priests and the chroniclers and the midwife. About the sweets smuggled by a brother who forgot. About the magic that pours into empty places. About the taking, entry by entry, face first and name last.

Odile listened without moving. That was the strange and wonderful thing about Odile: other people, when you told them something enormous, did things with their faces — gasped, frowned, clutched at themselves. Odile went still, like a pond going glassy, and you could feel her thinking underneath, huge and fast, like fish under ice.

When Sefa finished, the silence lasted long enough for the candle to gutter and need its wick trimmed.

"Say the last part again," Odile said quietly. "About how he was made."

"He was *written out*," Sefa said. "Paid and struck and erased. Every record. Every memory. He isn't hiding, Odile. He's *unwritten*. Nobody knows he exists because he made it so nobody could know."

"And the last part. The mercy-horror part."

"What was erased can be erased again. But what was erased can also be—" Sefa screwed up her face, reaching for the exact shape of the whisper. "*Written back.*"

Odile stood up so fast her stool tipped over. She caught it, absently, without looking at it, the way she did everything absent and exact at once, and began to pace the cramped length of the wreck's belly, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, her arms folded hard across her chest.

"That's why the records," she said, half to Sefa and half to the numbers in her head. "That's why the Gift-Hunt isn't really about hunting. Registration. Rolls. Lists of names. He's not collecting the gifted — he's collecting *entries*. Everything he takes gets written into his own book somewhere, I'd stake my life on it, because that's how the magic works, that's how *all* of this works — Tammas's secrets, Quill's names, the bottled grief in the guild vaults — it's all bookkeeping, it's all *ledger-work*, and he—"

She stopped pacing. Sefa watched the color come into her face and then go out of it again, like a tide doing something complicated.

"He erased himself a kingdom," Odile said softly. "And nobody noticed, because noticing requires a record, and he owns all the records." She laughed, one short unhappy note. "I balance books for a living, Sefa. Do you know what I'd have found, auditing that court? Nothing. Perfectly clean accounts. The cleanest accounts in the world are the ones where the crime is the accounting."

"But Odile." Sefa pulled the cloak tighter. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "The drowned ones said he wasn't wicked like a shark. They said he was wicked like a wound. They said—" She hesitated. "They said something was taken from him first."

"I heard."

"He was a baby in a basin. He didn't do anything. He just cried soft instead of loud." Sefa's eyes were hot and she didn't fully know why, except that she spent her days listening to the dead and the drowned and she knew — she *knew*, the way the sea knew — what it was to be somewhere nobody had counted you. Before Odile. Before Brannic and Quill. Those months alone after the flood, when she had talked to drowned things because nothing living would listen. "Nobody should be struck out. Even if he's done horrible things. Even if he's the reason they're burning people. He was a *baby*, Odile."

Odile crouched down in front of her. Up close her eyes were wet-bright in the candlelight, and she reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind Sefa's ear, gentle as closing a book you didn't want to end.

"You're right," she said. "Both things are true at once, and I hate it, but you're right. He's a monster and he's a wound and those aren't different facts, they're the same fact seen from two sides." Her jaw tightened. "And it means we can't just kill him. Killing an unwritten man might not even work — you can't cut a throat that's already been cut from every record. But if Sefa's right, if erasure is both his method and his weakness..."

"Then write him back," Sefa whispered.

"Then write him back," Odile agreed. "Name and all. Every stolen year returned to its owner. Undo the oldest entry first." She exhaled, long and shaky, and for a moment she looked very young and very scared and very determined, all three at once, like a knot of three different colored threads. "Which means we don't need an army. We need—" She stopped herself, and something moved behind her eyes, some thought she wasn't ready to say out loud yet, and Sefa, who heard true things for a living, heard it anyway, unspoken: *we need someone willing to pay whatever it costs.*

Outside, boots on shingle: Brannic's heavy tread, and Mother Quill's lighter one beside it, coming back from the tideline. Odile squeezed Sefa's hand once, quick, and stood, and by the time the canvas flap lifted she was already reaching for her ledgers with her face arranged into something calmer.

"Sefa's had word from the sea," she said. "You'll want to sit down. Both of you. It's about the king — and it's worse than we thought, and better than we hoped, and I need paper. A lot of paper."

Mother Quill raised one grey eyebrow. "Child," she said to Sefa, settling onto a crate with the air of a duchess claiming a throne, "what exactly did the drowned ones tell you?"

Sefa looked around at them — Brannic filling the doorway with his tired bigness, Quill sharp-eyed and silver, Odile already uncapping the ink — and felt the feeling she always felt lately and hadn't had a name for until this moment, warm and terrifying, like a lit candle carried through wind.

"They told me about a man nobody ever counted," she said. "And I think we're going to have to count him."
