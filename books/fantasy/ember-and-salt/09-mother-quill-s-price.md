## 9. Mother Quill's Price

The counting-house of the Quiet Hands smelled of tallow and old paper, and Mother Quill had always found that comforting. Paper held what flesh could not. Paper kept its promises. She ran her thumb along the edge of the blank writ she'd been given and felt the fibers hum with potential — a thing not yet lying, a thing waiting to be told what truth it would carry.

"You understand the terms," said Vess, the guildmistress on duty, a narrow woman whose eyes were the color of dishwater and whose hands never stopped moving, folding, unfolding, smoothing.

"I wrote half of them," Quill said. "Thirty years ago. Before you could hold a quill."

"Terms change."

"Truths don't." Quill set the blank writ down on the scarred oak table between them. Around them, the sanctuary murmured with its nightly business: a courier whispering the price of a bishop's sins into a client's ear; two archivists arguing in the corner about whether a memory of a smell counted as scent or sentiment for tariff purposes. Somewhere below, in the vaults where the guild kept the things it traded, something sighed. The vaults did that. Everyone pretended not to hear it.

Vess spread her hands flat on the table. "You want court credentials for four fugitives. Sealed, signed, verifiable under the king's own scrutiny. There is no ink alive that can fake that. There is only *substitution*."

"And substitution costs," Quill said.

"It costs *weight*," Vess corrected. "You know how this works better than I do, old woman. The seal doesn't care about gold. It cares about equivalence. To forge a document the crown's own sorcerers will read as true, we must feed it something true. Something of equal weight to what it will be asked to carry." Her fingers stilled, finally, folded together like dead birds. "A life would do it. A name, freely given. Or—"

"A memory," Quill finished. "Of sufficient weight."

They both knew what memories carried sufficient weight. Not first kisses. Not the taste of a particular summer. The guild's tariffs were written in grief, because grief was the only currency heavy enough to move the machinery of lies that great institutions ran on. A man could sell his wedding day and barely cover a merchant's charter. To forge passage into a palace wrapped around a hungry god —

"How long have they been with you?" Vess asked quietly. "The girl, the soldier, the child?"

"Long enough."

"Long enough to love them."

Quill looked at her hands. They were old hands, ink-stained at every knuckle, the right forefinger permanently crooked from sixty years of holding pens. Forgers' hands. Liars' hands. Hands that had written other people's souls onto clean paper so many times she sometimes woke in the night unsure which name was hers.

"They're not mine," she said. "I'm theirs. There's a difference. It matters in the accounting."

Vess almost smiled. "Does it? In your experience, does love ever balance cleanly?"

"No," Quill said. "Love is the one entry that never balances. That's rather the point of it. You put in everything and the ledger shows a loss on every line, forever, and you sign your name anyway." She straightened her spine, feeling every one of her seventy-one years click into place like coins into a strongbox. "Show me the scale."

* * *

The weighing chamber was beneath the counting-house, down stairs worn into shallow bowls by generations of feet. It was a round room with a domed ceiling painted with faded constellations no astronomer had ever charted — the guild had painted their own sky up there, centuries ago, the constellations of debts and payments. In the center stood the scale itself: not brass and chains but a slab of black glass balanced on a pillar of salt, wide enough to lie down on. Saltglass. The guild's namesake. It remembered everything placed upon it and forgot nothing, which was why it made such a terrible altar and such an honest ledger.

Quill had lain on it once before, forty years ago, when she'd sold a rival forger her own memory of her mother's voice. She'd gotten a good price. She'd spent thirty years afterward trying to remember why the word *lullaby* made her cry.

She sat on the edge of the glass now, cold through her skirts, while Vess lit the four candles at the compass points. The flames burned greenish, the way candles burned down here, feeding as they did on the residue of everything traded in this room.

"The credentials require three weights," Vess said, unrolling a sheet of instructions written in the guild's cipher. "First, a seal of identity — proof you are who your papers say. That's light work. A childhood will cover it. Second, a seal of office — proof you hold the position claimed, revenue auditor's clerk under the Chancellery. Heavier. Ten years of a trade, perhaps. Third—" Vess paused, and Quill watched her decide whether to say the next part gently or plainly. Vess chose plainly; she always had. "Third is the seal of the crown itself. The writ must pass inspection by whatever sits behind the king's eyes. And that thing eats magic for breakfast and suspicion for supper. To fool it, we need a weight it will recognize. Grief. True grief, freshly owned, freely surrendered."

"Freshly owned," Quill repeated. "Meaning I can't sell an old sorrow."

"An old sorrow has been discounted by time. The scale knows. It wants something still bleeding."

Quill nodded slowly. She had known, walking down those stairs, exactly what she'd brought with her. She'd carried it wrapped in oilcloth against her heart since the night the fire took her daughter — no, not fire, that was the story she told, the story even Odile believed, *the fever took my Marisol when she was nine*, and fever was true enough, fever of the mind, the burning that came after, when the guild had offered her the choice they offered every grieving parent of sufficient skill:

We can cut it out. The worst hour. The sight of her face going slack. We can take the whole of it — the face, the voice, the weight of her in your arms — and pay you enough to vanish, to start again, to forget you ever—

No. No, she had refused then. She had kept every scrap of it, the whole ruinous treasure, and paid her own way out of the guild's debt with twenty years of forgery work instead. She had chosen to carry it. That was the whole point of carrying grief: nobody could make you set it down. It was the last property a person truly owned.

And now she was choosing to set it down anyway.

Because Brannic had maybe a season left in him before his borrowed strength burned him hollow, and he spent what remained of it carrying Sefa over fences like she weighed nothing. Because Sefa talked to drowned things and slept curled against Odile's side like the child had finally found a harbor. Because Odile — quiet, watchful Odile, who kept everyone else's accounts and never her own, who flinched at open flame and thought nobody noticed — Odile had looked at the plan to infiltrate the palace and said, *I'll go in first,* and meant it, meant to walk alone into the mouth of the thing that ate kings.

You didn't let family walk into the fire without doing everything in your power to make the road shorter.

"Mother Quill?" Vess's voice. "The scale is ready. If you wish to reconsider—"

"What is it you say in the new tariffs?" Quill asked, swinging her legs up onto the glass. "Full disclosure, no recourse, the seller bears all subsequent poverty of spirit?"

"We say it more politely than that."

"Say it anyway. I like knowing the shape of the trap I'm walking into."

Vess recited the words. They were old words, older than the guild, probably older than writing — the same shape of words spoken over marriages and hangings and coronations, the formal acknowledgment that what was being done could not be undone. Quill listened with professional appreciation. Good contract language. Tight. No loopholes for regret to wriggle through.

When Vess finished, Quill lay back fully on the glass and closed her eyes.

Marisol had been born in the month of the plum rains, in a house with a leaking roof, and she had come into the world furious about it, red-faced and yelling, and Quill had laughed until her stitches hurt. Marisol had a gap between her front teeth she was proud of. Marisol had a way of tilting her head when she was deciding whether to trust someone, a little birdlike cock, watching you sideways. Marisol had drawn a picture of the two of them holding hands under a sun with eyelashes, and Quill had kept it folded inside her false-bottomed ledger box for thirty years.

The face. God, the face. Nine years old and missing her front teeth too, grown into the gap she'd inherited, squinting against the sun in the orchard the last summer, freckles coming in across her nose like a spilled handful of cloves—

"Begin," Quill said.

The glass went warm beneath her. Warm as a living cheek. She heard the candles gutter, heard Vess step back, heard the salt pillar beneath the slab begin to sing — a low harmonic, the sound of a debt being entered.

She held on as long as she could. That was the ritual within the ritual; everyone who sold to the saltglass tried to hold on, to feel the extraction happen, to witness their own loss. Some part of her, the part that had spent a lifetime forging identities, wanted to at least be present at the funeral.

She felt it lift away. Not torn — *drawn*, like thread from a spool, smooth and endless, and the strange thing was that it didn't hurt. That was the obscenity of it. The guild's machines were merciful in the taking and merciless in the aftermath. She lay on the warm glass and felt her daughter leave her, the face going first, then the tilt of the head, then the sound of her laugh, and Quill's hands rose without her permission and pressed themselves flat against her own sternum, pressing down on nothing, pressing on a wound that hadn't happened yet.

Then it was done, and the candles burned steady yellow again, and Vess's face swam above her, wet-eyed, saying something Quill couldn't hear over the ringing silence where a lifetime of love used to be.

* * *

She woke in the guest room above the counting-house with the credentials finished on the desk beside her — three documents, sealed in wax the color of dried blood, perfect. She examined them with a forger's eye and found them flawless, and understood dimly that some of the perfection was hers, that the scale had paid her change in craft, sharpening what it had dulled.

She knew things still. She knew she had had a daughter. The fact survived like a headstone survives — *here lies Marisol, 9 years, beloved* — but the ground beneath it was empty. When she reached for the face, she found the shape of a reaching. When she reached for the laugh, she found the notation of laughter, the way a musician might find a rest marked in a score. *Here, something dear once sounded.*

It was bearable. That was the horror of it. The guild took payment precisely so that it would be bearable, and there was no cruelty quite like mercy administered by contract.

She went downstairs on unsteady legs and found them in the common room, the three of them, pretending not to wait for her. Brannic stood by the window with his arms crossed, filling the frame the way he filled every frame, gray at the temples and tired in the bones and hiding both. Sefa was on the floor drawing in spilled salt — little spirals, the shapes the drowned things taught her. Odile sat at a corner table with a ledger open, because of course she did, balancing figures that needed no balancing, keeping her hands busy so her fear couldn't use them.

"There," Quill said, laying the credentials on the table. "Three sets. Revenue audit clerks, second rank, Chancellery seal. Even the palace's own sorcerers will taste truth on them."

Odile looked at the documents, then up at her, and Quill saw the moment the girl understood — saw her gaze drop to Quill's hands, which were trembling, and travel to her face, which was wrong somehow, smoothed in one place and ravaged in another, the topography of a fresh amputation.

"What did it cost?" Odile asked.

"Nothing I wasn't glad to spend."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that stays true," Quill said. She lowered herself into a chair, and her body chose that moment to inform her of the full invoice: her joints ached as though she'd walked forty years in an afternoon, and behind her eyes sat a headache like a nail driven in slow. "Listen to me, girl, because I'll say this once. In the guild we have a saying: *grief is currency, and love is the debt it services*. Everything I did tonight, I did because you three are the finest bad investment I ever made. I expect no return. I expect the account to stay open until I die owing more than I started with. That is what family is — the one ledger you keep gladly in the red."

Sefa had stopped drawing. The child looked up at her with those tide-gray eyes that saw too much, and said, very softly, "Something's missing from you. The sea took something. Like it takes things from sailors."

"Yes, little fish," Quill said. "The sea and I have done some trading."

Brannic crossed the room and put his hand on her shoulder — carefully, the way he touched everything now, rationing even his gentleness. He didn't ask. Soldiers knew when not to ask. But his hand stayed there, heavy and warm, and that was answer enough.

Odile hadn't moved. She stared at Quill with an expression Quill recognized from the mirror of her own younger face: the arithmetic of guilt, running numbers that could never balance. *She did this for us. She paid with something irreplaceable. How do I enter that? What do I owe?*

"Don't," Quill told her gently. "Whatever sum you're calculating, stop. This isn't a loan. There's no repayment schedule. There's just the next thing that needs doing, and the next, and us doing them together." She tapped the credentials with one crooked finger. "Tomorrow you walk into that palace wearing my work. Every line of these papers is true except the ones that matter, which is the definition of good forgery, and possibly of most lives. So. Auditor's clerk. Do you know how a revenue audit actually works?"

"I... balance books. I can learn the forms."

"You'll do more than learn the forms. You'll think like an auditor. Kings hide things, but treasuries hide worse. Follow the money past the money — every impossible payment, every entry that shouldn't exist, every coin spent on *quiet*. The thing on the throne has to account for what it steals, even to itself. Especially to itself. Find the ledger it keeps, and you find the wound underneath the crown." She smiled, and knew the smile didn't reach anything anymore, and gave it freely regardless. "That's your inheritance from me, along with the paperwork. Guilt is useless; method is everything."

Outside, the fog came off the harbor and pressed against the windows, and somewhere in the city above them the Gift-Hunt drums began their evening rounds, and the four of them sat close around one candle like people around a campfire, because that was what they were.

Later, when the others had gone up, Odile lingered in the doorway. "The thing you sold," she said. "Will you tell me what it was? Someday? When you want to?"

Quill considered the question with the last of her strength. Someday, perhaps, she would want to — would need to, the way one needs a witness to prove a thing happened. But not yet. Not while the wound was still learning its own shape.

"Someday," she said. "Now sleep, girl. Tomorrow you become a lie so convincing that even you'll believe it. Rest while you're still allowed to be only yourself."

Odile nodded and slipped away, and the stair creaked under her weight the way honest stairs do, and Mother Quill sat alone with the candle and took out the folded paper she had kept for thirty years — the drawing of two figures holding hands under a sun with eyelashes.

She looked at it for a long time.

She could read the signature. She knew the artist's name. She knew it was her daughter's hand, knew the year, knew the house, knew the rain. But the figures were just figures now, and the faces were smudges of charcoal where meaning used to live, and when she pressed the paper to her chest she felt the warmth of the candle at her back and nothing else, and she wept the way professionals weep — silently, economically, without letting it interfere with tomorrow's work.

Then she blew out the candle, and in the dark she said the daughter's name aloud, once, to prove she still could.

"Marisol."

The word hung in the empty room, pointing at nothing, a key with no lock left in the world.

Down in the harbor fog, a bell buoy rang twice, and far off, toward the palace hill, something vast turned over in its sleep and dreamed of ledgers — and of a girl with ink-stained fingers who would soon be standing in its counting-room, reading accounts that were never meant for mortal eyes.
