## 10. Court of Paper Faces

The papers Mother Quill forged were beautiful. That was the first thing Odile noticed, turning them over in the grey light before dawn: the way the wax seal caught the candleflame like a drop of frozen blood, the way the ink of the crown's chancery hand flowed with that particular self-satisfied loop the royal scriveners all learned from the same master. Quill had even aged the vellum properly — tea and smoke and a fortnight pressed beneath a stone — so that the auditor's commission looked as though it had been sitting in some provincial office for years, forgotten and routine.

"Routine is the whole trick of it," Quill had said, pressing the folio into Odile's hands. Her fingers were steady; her eyes were not. "Nobody looks hard at a tax man. Nobody *wants* to look at a tax man. You are the most boring thing in any room you enter, girl. Be grateful for it."

*You are the most boring thing in any room.* Odile repeated it to herself now, standing in the queue at the Salt Gate while guards in the king's livery checked papers with the bored efficiency of men who had done this ten thousand times. She wore Quill's disguise the way she wore everything: close and unremarkable. A clerk's grey coat, mended at the elbows. Spectacles with plain glass, because nothing said *harmless* like spectacles. Her hair bound up under a cap, her ledgers — real ones, real numbers, because Quill insisted a forgery was only as good as its truest detail — strapped to her back.

Brannic stood three places behind her in the line, dressed as a carter, his borrowed strength folded down small inside him like a fist inside a glove. He did not look at her. They had agreed not to look at each other. Sefa was already inside, or would be by evening, carried through the water-gates in a fish-cart among the day's catch, whispering to the drowned things in the palace cisterns. And Mother Quill —

Mother Quill was not coming. That price had been paid already, though none of them spoke of it.

"Name and purpose," said the guard.

"Odile Marsh," she said, using the name on her papers. "Auditor, Second Circuit, Office of the Royal Exchequer." She let her voice go flat and dry as old bread. "Quarterly assessment of customs shortfalls."

The guard glanced at the seal. Glanced at her face. Saw exactly what Quill had built him to see: a tired woman of no particular age, whose job was arithmetic, whose life was columns.

"Next," he said, and waved her through.

The gate swallowed her, and the palace rose ahead.

* * *

She had expected grandeur. What she had not expected was the smell.

Palaces, in the ballads, smelled of roses and beeswax. This one smelled of salt and cold iron and, beneath it, something faintly sweet and wrong — like fruit just past rotting, like a candle burning something it should not burn. The corridors were white marble shot through with veins of grey, and the veins were not veins. Odile's gift woke the moment she crossed the threshold, the way it always did, showing her things as they truly were, and what it showed her made her stomach turn over slowly, quietly, like a boat taking on water.

The marble was bone. Not metaphorically. Somewhere far below, something vast had been ground fine and mixed into the plaster, and the walls drank light the way living things drink water.

*Don't look,* she told herself. *Auditors don't see anything. Auditors count.*

So she counted. She counted doorways and steps and guards — forty-one between the gate and the exchequer offices, changing watch at intervals of two hundred breaths, which meant the patrol patterns could be mapped, which meant Brannic's route in and out would hold. She counted candles and found them wrong: they burned without shortening. She counted the servants who glided past with trays and lowered eyes, and her gift showed her their shadows lagging half a step behind their bodies, like dogs that had forgotten whom they followed.

The exchequer offices themselves were almost disappointingly ordinary. Ink and paper, clerks scratching away, the dry rustle of the empire counting itself. Odile was given a desk near a window that overlooked the inner court, a pot of thin ink, and four days' worth of ledgers to reconcile. The head clerk, a stooped man named Ferren with ink-stained fingers and the haunted courtesy of someone who had survived too many purges, explained her duties twice and then left her alone, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in weeks.

For two days she was, genuinely, an auditor. It was almost restful. Numbers did not lie to her; that was the mercy of them. Where magic twisted and gifts obscured, arithmetic stood still and told the truth in its flat, faithful voice. She found discrepancies easily — the palace books were riddled with them, great silent hemorrhages of gold flowing toward a department that did not appear in any index, an office with no name receiving sums that could have fed a city. When she asked Ferren about it, mildly, professionally, the old man went grey.

"Don't," he said simply. "Whatever entry you think you've found, lose it. Copy over it. There was a woman here, before you, third circuit, very clever—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Odile copied over the entry, and understood that the palace's accounts were not kept to record money. They were kept to record obedience.

But on the third day, the king held court.

* * *

It was announced the way weather is announced — a change in the air, a hush rolling through the corridors ahead of the fact. All clerks of good standing were summoned to the gallery above the throne room to witness His Majesty's justice, a formality revived in recent months, Ferren whispered, since the king had grown fond of being seen.

Odile took her place among the rows of grey and brown clerks, quill in hand, commissioned to take notes no one would ever read. Below her, the throne room opened like the mouth of something patient. Banners hung from the vaulted ceiling — the crowned stag of the Eriksen dynasty, wait, no, that was her own name, she corrected herself, the crowned stag of the house of Aldemar — and the light came down through windows of glass so old it had gone green as deep water.

And then the king entered, and every thought in Odile's head went quiet at once.

He was not what she had feared. That was the horror of it. She had braced herself for a monster — scales, or shadow, or a face that slid. Instead there was only a tall man in white and gold, moving with the measured grace of long practice, his beard silvered, his features handsome in the worn way of old coins. He smiled at the right moments. He pronounced judgment on a smuggler and a minor lord with the same mild voice. The courtiers loved him, or performed loving him, and the performance was seamless.

If Odile had been only a woman, only a clerk, only eyes, she would have seen nothing amiss at all.

But she was never only anything. Her gift rose in her like water finding its level, and she saw.

The man on the throne was hollow.

Not empty — hollow, the way a bell is hollow, the way a well is hollow. There was a shape seated there wearing a king the way a coat is worn, and inside the shape there was nothing but a thin, exhausted residue of a person, stretched paper-thin over the vastness that filled him. She could see the seams. She could see where the stolen years had been stitched in — great crude sutures of light running down the figure's arms and throat, drinking, always drinking, drawing something out of the man-shaped vessel and feeding it upward into—

Into the thing wrapped around him.

That was the part that stopped her breath. The king was not the worst of it. The king was the *anchor*, the fixed point, the nail in the world's wood. Wrapped around him, threaded through the rafters and the banners and the green glass of the windows, coiled through the marrow of the palace itself, was something else. Something that had no name she knew and no shape her gift could hold. It was vast the way the sea is vast, and hungry the way winter is hungry, and it was *paying attention.*

To her.

There was no dramatic moment. No turning of a head, no narrowing of eyes. The king continued pronouncing judgments in his mild voice. But Odile felt the attention settle over her like a hand laid flat on the back of her neck — vast, curious, faintly amused, the way a child is curious about a moth that has gotten indoors. Her gift had touched it. Her seeing had brushed against its seeing, and somewhere in the coiled dark of the palace's bones, something had noticed the small warm flicker of a talent that should have registered nowhere, that was hidden under forged papers and plain glass spectacles and the most boring name in the kingdom.

*Interesting,* the attention seemed to say. Not alarmed. Not yet. Just interested, the way a ledger-keeper is interested in a column that doesn't quite sum.

Odile bent over her notes. She wrote: *His Majesty heard petitioners this day, and was gracious.* Her hand did not shake. She made it not shake the way she made entries balance — through sheer disciplined will, through the flat faithfulness of habit. Beneath the table, out of sight, her other hand pressed hard against her thigh, nails through wool, pain used as an anchor the way sailors use a rock in a storm.

Below, the smuggler was sentenced. Ten years' labor. The minor lord lost his lands. The king rose, and when he rose, Odile saw — only for a heartbeat, only because her gift was open and screaming — the true king underneath. A flicker. A man's face surfacing through deep water for one instant: exhausted, bewildered, *pleading*. His mouth shaped a word. No sound came out. Then the vastness closed over him again like ice forming across a pond, and the thing wearing his shape smiled graciously at his court and withdrew, trailing hunger behind him like a bridal train.

Odile sat in the gallery until the room emptied, writing nonsense in a steady hand, waiting for her legs to remember how to stand.

*Pity* was the last thing she had expected to feel. Terror, yes — terror she had prepared for, terror she carried like a second spine. But pity crept in under the fear and settled beside it, unbearable, because she had seen the man beneath. She had seen him drowning in his own body, mouthing a word no one heard, and she understood suddenly, completely, why Tammas Vey had spent his last breath on this secret. Not because it was dangerous. Because it was *sad*. The saddest thing in the kingdom, enthroned in gold, applauded daily by people who could not see him drowning.

She blew out her breath slowly. She closed her notebook. She rose with the other clerks and filed out into the corridor, one grey thread among hundreds, and no one looked at her at all.

Except, at the end of the hall, one guard did look. Briefly. Curiously. The way one looks at a moth.

* * *

That evening, in the narrow cell they'd assigned her in the clerks' quarters, Odile wrote her report in the ledger-code she and Brannic had devised — innocuous figures that read, to anyone else, as customs assessments, but to a trained eye spelled out everything: the patrols, the nameless office bleeding gold, the seams in the king, the drowned man pleading beneath the surface. She worked by the light of a stub of candle, and when she finished she sat for a long time with her hands flat on the page, listening to the palace breathe around her.

Because it did breathe. Now that she knew what to listen for, she could feel it — a slow tidal rhythm in the walls, in the salt-and-rot sweetness of the air, the vastness below drawing its long sustenance up through the bones of the building. Every stolen year, every bottled grief the Saltglass Guild had ever sold, every name cut loose from its owner — she would have wagered her life it all drained here, to feed the shape of a king.

A soft knock at the door. Two, then one. Brannic's signal.

She opened it a hand's width. He stood in the corridor dressed as a palace carter, hay dust on his shoulders, his face carefully blank.

"Well?" he murmured.

"He's in there," Odile whispered. "The real king. Alive. Drowning. And the thing wearing him—" She stopped, steadied herself. "Brannic, it noticed me. Not suspected. *Noticed.* Like a man noticing a fly. It's curious."

Brannic was quiet a moment. In the torchlight his scarred face looked older than his years, and very tired. "Curious is bad?"

"Curious is the beginning of every hunt I've ever read about." She pressed the coded pages into his hands. "Get this to Sefa's contact at the fish-market. Then get yourself out of the palace tonight, both routes, because if it decides to look closer—"

"It won't find you," Brannic said. "Quill built you out of boredom. You're the most forgettable woman in the kingdom."

"That's what worries me," Odile said. "Boredom is a disguise. So is interest."

He left. She barred the door and sat back down at the little desk, and blew out her candle, and sat in the dark listening to the palace breathe.

And in the dark, very softly, from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls, from the floor beneath her cot, from the space behind her own eyes — she heard it. Not words. Something older than words. A sensation, vast and patient and terribly gentle, like a finger tracing the spine of a book it has decided to read:

*I know what you are, little ledger. Come and see me properly. I keep such interesting accounts.*

The candle stub, cold and dead on the desk, lit itself.

Odile did not scream. She was, after all, an auditor. She watched the small flame burn with a steadiness it had no fuel for, and she understood that her disguise had lasted exactly as long as it needed to — and that tomorrow, or the day after, the king would summon the Second Circuit's new auditor for a private review of the quarterly accounts.

She lay down in the dark beside the burning candle and did not sleep, and began, methodically, to plan what she would say to a monster when it asked her to dinner.
