## 15. What Odile Lit

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old smoke.

They had been walking for hours — down through the weeping seams of the palace district, past cisterns where the water moved with a light of its own, along service tunnels so old that the mortar between their bricks had been laid by hands that predated the crown itself. Sefa walked ahead with one hand trailing the wall, listening to the pipes the way other children listened to birdsong. Brannic limped behind her, favoring the leg he'd wrenched hauling Mother Quill's satchel out of a hunter's reach. The satchel was all they had left of her. That, and the memory of her voice saying *go*, said in the tone she used for prices that were final.

Odile carried it. She had insisted. It was heavy with ink pots and blank soul-paper and the little brass stamps Quill had used to press names into being, and Odile wanted its weight on her shoulder like a penance she could count.

They stopped at last in a disused salt cellar — a low brick vault where the Guild had once stored its cruder stock before the trade went refined and bottled. Salt still furred the walls in gray blooms, and the air bit gently at the nose, clean as a wound washed with brine. There was an iron brazier against one wall, half full of ancient charcoal. Brannic struck sparks from his knife and flint until a small red light bloomed, and the three of them arranged themselves around it the way people arrange themselves around anything warm when the world has gone cold: without discussion, without hope of much, but needing it anyway.

Sefa sat cross-legged closest to the coals, her drowned-things whispering somewhere under her breath, a tide-sound. Brannic leaned against the wall with his sword across his knees, though they both knew he hadn't the strength left to lift it twice. And Odile sat with the satchel in her lap and did not open it, because opening it meant admitting that Quill wasn't coming back for it tonight, and there were still hours left in which she could refuse to know that.

The silence lasted until it couldn't.

"You counted them," Sefa said suddenly. She wasn't looking at Odile. She was looking at the fire. "In the tunnel. When the hunters came. You were counting."

"I count when I'm frightened." Odile heard her own voice come out flat, clerk-flat, the voice she used over ledgers. "It's a habit."

"It saved us." Brannic's voice was rough gravel. He didn't look at her either. All three of them were looking anywhere but at each other, which was how grief spoke in their little family — sideways, oblique, in the grammar of things not said. "The patrol patterns. You read those tunnels like a ledger and you got us out. Quill knew what she was doing when she held that door. She bought us exactly enough."

"She bought us with herself." The words came out of Odile before she could stop them, and once they started, the rest came like water through a cracked dam, and she was too tired to stopper it. "Because I led us down that route. Because I trusted the contact at the Quiet Hands. Because I decoded Tammas's clue and I decided — *I* decided — that we would go to the Emberhold, that we would do this thing, and I never once asked whether the price was one any of us could pay. I keep accounts. It is the one thing I am actually good at. And I kept this account badly."

"Odile—" Brannic began.

"The betrayal wasn't yours." She said it fast, before kindness could interrupt. "You're going to be gentle with me. Both of you. You've been gentle with me since the road, since the farmstead, since the day I found you all, and I have let you, and every hour of your gentleness has been built on a lie of omission so large that I can't fit through a door with it anymore."

Sefa turned to look at her then. The child's eyes were pale as sea-glass, and in the coal-light they seemed lit from underneath, the way drowned things are lit from underneath. "What lie?"

Odile set the satchel down. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees the way she did when balancing a column of numbers that refused to sum, and she made herself say it looking at the fire, because she could not yet say it looking at them.

"My mistress's name was Hesta Vane. She took me in when I was eleven years old, off a farm where my father had decided my gift was the devil's arithmetic and my mother had decided not to argue. Hesta taught me herbs and hedge-craft and how to keep my head down, and she taught me most of all that a gift is not a weapon unless fear makes it one." A breath. The salt air stung her throat. "She was wrong about that last part. Or I proved her wrong. One of the two."

The charcoal shifted in the brazier with a sound like a sigh.

"When I was fourteen, my gift grew. Seeing what things truly are — it deepened. I stopped seeing Hesta's cottage as a cottage. I saw the rot in its beams, the lies in its walls, the years stacked inside its stones like coins in a hoard. Everything true, everything bare, everything stripped of the softness that lets a person live in a place and love it. And I saw — " her voice caught, and she pushed through it, clerk to the end, itemizing her own crime — "I saw what I was becoming. Something that looked at the world and could never again see anything but its bones. I was afraid of myself. I was fourteen and I was afraid of myself, and Hesta told me fear was a door and I could choose not to walk through it, and I hated her a little for saying it, because it meant the choice was mine."

Brannic had gone very still. Even Sefa's tide-whispering had stopped.

"There was a night in autumn. I don't remember which. I remember the smell of the stew and the rain starting and Hesta telling me, gently, that my lessons would need to go deeper now, that the gift wouldn't stay small just because I wished it small, and something in me — " Odile's hands curled into fists on her knees. "Something in me *broke loose*. Not on purpose. But not entirely by accident either. Some part of me wanted the fear gone, wanted the whole unbearable seeing of things gone, wanted the cottage and the lessons and the woman who kept telling me the truth — gone. And my gift answered. Fire answers want. Nobody ever tells you that. Everyone talks about fire answering anger, but it's want that calls it, want is the bell and the flame comes running."

"You lit it," Sefa said softly. Not a question. Children always cut straight to the bone; adults build flesh over the truth and call it mercy.

"I lit it." The words tasted of ash even now, six years later. "The cottage burned in eleven minutes. I stood in the yard and watched and I could *see* it — that's the curse of my gift, I could see the fire truly, see it eating, see it joyful, see Hesta's years burning up in it like paper currency, and some part of me understood even then that I was watching the exact thing I would become if I let fear keep spending me. I ran. I ran to the city and I buried myself in ledgers because numbers are honest and cold and they never ask you what you are. And then Tammas died in my arms and gave me his secret, and I told you all I was running from the Gift-Hunt, and that was true, but it was only half the truth. The other half is that I've been running from that yard for six years. Every mile of this road, every step toward the Emberhold — I told myself it was for the king, for the hunted, for us. It was also a girl trying to burn down her own guilt by proxy. And look what the wanting has cost. Look at who paid."

The silence afterward was enormous. In it, Odile heard everything she feared: the rustle of Brannic shifting his weight, deciding; Sefa's small breaths; the tick of cooling salt falling from the walls. She stared into the coals and waited to be weighed and found wanting, the way she had been weighing herself for six years, and she found, distantly, that some part of her was relieved — *relieved* — to finally be caught. A debt acknowledged is a debt that can at least be entered in the book.

Brannic moved first.

He didn't speak. He hauled himself off the wall, crossed the little vault with his bad leg dragging, and sat down beside her — heavily, the way he did everything now, spending strength he didn't have. Then he put one huge scarred hand on top of both her fists, engulfing them, and left it there.

"I burned a village," he said.

Odile's head came up.

"Not with magic. With orders. I was twenty-two and a sergeant and very good at following them, and the village was full of smugglers, allegedly, and I stood at the gate and made sure no one left while it burned, and afterward they told me the smuggling was a fiction someone senior had needed for his ledgers." His mouth twisted. "So you see, we are both clerks of a kind. I kept his accounts with my body. The difference between us, Odile Eriksen, is that you were fourteen and afraid, and I was twenty-two and obedient, and obedience is a choice you make with your eyes open." He squeezed her fists, gentle as a man handling stolen glass. "You ask what kind of person lights a fire out of fear. I'll tell you what kind. The kind who spends six years carrying the dead woman's grimoire instead of selling it. The kind who counts patrol patterns to keep a drowned-child alive. The kind who walks toward the monster's vault because someone has to balance the account. Fear lit your fire. But it isn't fear that's been driving you since. I've marched beside fear. Fear runs *away*. You run *toward*."

"That doesn't unburn her," Odile whispered.

"No," Brannic agreed. "Nothing does. That's the salt in the wound and you'll carry it the rest of your life, same as I carry mine. But hear me, because I don't say soft things often and I'll not repeat them: you confessed when no one forced you. You confessed when lying would have been easier and kinder to yourself. Do you know how rare that is? I served under men who rewrote battles in their memoirs. I've met guildsmen who could forge a soul's name and couldn't forge one honest sentence about themselves." He almost smiled. "Quill could. That's why we loved her. And you — you're cut from the same cloth. Honest thread. Expensive stuff. Hard to work with. Worth every coin."

Sefa crawled across the floor then, on her knees, the way she moved when she was being serious, and wedged herself against Odile's side, small and bony and warm as a banked coal.

"Hesta knew," the child said.

Odile looked down at her. "What?"

"The drowned ones remember fires. Water keeps what fire takes, sometimes — the smoke goes up but the crying goes down, and rivers carry it to the sea, and the sea tells Sefa." Sefa's sea-glass eyes fixed on her with terrible gentleness. "Your mistress's river remembers her. She cried going down, but not the way the burned usually cry. Not angry. Not surprised." The child searched for the words, frowning, dredging them up from deep water. "She cried like someone leaving a letter unfinished. Like someone who knew the fire was coming and had already forgiven the hand that lit it. She wrote you a letter, the river says. In her heart, before the end. It said: *the girl will think she is the fire. Tell her she is the one who carries water.*"

Odile broke.

Not gracefully — she was past grace, past ledgers, past every defense her careful double-entry life had built. She folded forward over her knees and sobbed into the salt-dark, six years of it, the yard and the smoke and the road and Quill's door and Tammas's blood cooling under her hands, all of it pouring out at last into the two people who should have, by every right and rule she'd ever believed, drawn away from her. Instead Brannic's arm came around her shoulders, heavy as a beam but steady as one, and Sefa pressed her small forehead against Odile's arm and hummed a drowned lullaby in a key no living throat should know, and the salt cellar held them the way old things hold grief: without judgment, without hurry, having seen worse.

When it was spent, Odile sat back, wrung out and lighter, hollowed in the way a house is hollowed after a flood — damaged, but standing, and strangely clean.

"I keep waiting for you to leave," she said hoarsely. "I've been waiting since the road. I keep the books of this family like a woman counting the days until foreclosure."

"We're not leaving." Sefa said it simply, the way children state the weather.

"You chose us," Brannic said. "On the road, when it showed you the worst of yourself, you stayed. At the farmstead, when I fell, you mapped our survival out like arithmetic and it *worked*. In the vault today, when the hunters flooded in, you got Sefa out through a gap I'd have sworn didn't exist. Whatever you lit back then, Odile — what you've built since is bigger. Fires end. What you built doesn't. We're the proof. We're sitting in it."

"And Quill?" Odile's voice cracked on the name. "What do we owe her? What does the account say?"

Brannic was quiet a moment. The coal-light moved on his ruined face.

"It says we finish it," he said finally. "It says we go to the Emberhold, or whatever door leads to it, and we unmake the thing that took her, and we bring her home or we bring her honor, and either way we pay what's owed. That's what family means. It's not the ones who don't cost you. It's the ones worth the cost."

Sefa lifted her head suddenly, tilting it, listening to something far below hearing.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"The pipes." The child's eyes had gone wide and bright. "The old water-ways under the palace. They're singing. They've been singing all night, but now they're singing *words*." She scrambled closer to the brazier, snatched a stick, and began scratching in the salt crust on the floor — quick, urgent strokes, letters learned from Quill's soul-paper samples. "A woman's voice. Down among the dungeons. She's teaching the water lore — binding-lore, unbinding-lore — and the water is carrying it up through the pipes, and she wants it written down, she says, *she says write it down and give it to the girl with the ledgers—*"

Odile went cold from scalp to sole.

"*Quill,*" she breathed. "She's alive. She's in the cells under the palace, and she's — " understanding struck her like a bell — "she's passing us the rite. Through the water. Through Sefa. Write it down, Sefa. Write down every word."

And in the salt-furred dark, by the light of a fire a guilty girl had once feared more than anything in the world, the child knelt and wrote, and the water sang, and Odile Eriksen bent close to read what the drowned carried home.
