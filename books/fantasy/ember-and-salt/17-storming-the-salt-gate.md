## 17. Storming the Salt Gate

Brannic had spent eleven years learning how much a body could carry before it broke, and he had thought himself an expert on the subject.

He had been wrong. The city was teaching him now.

It began at dawn, the way fires begin — with one small wrongness that no one thought to stamp out. A baker in the Tallowmarket refused to open his shutters for the criers. When the hunters came to make him kneel and swear he had no gift, his daughter stood in front of him, a girl of perhaps nine with flour on her hands, and said, *He's my father,* as if that were answer enough. It should have been. The hunter raised his iron anyway, and the street — the whole gray, sleepy, bread-smelling street — came off its hinges.

By noon it was not a riot. Riots have shape, purpose, edges. This was something older and less governable: a city remembering that it outnumbered its masters ten thousand to one. Brannic watched from a rooftop above the Salt Gate as the crowd moved like weather, like tide, like the slow grinding of a glacier that has decided, after centuries, to become a river. They carried hammers and fish-gaffs and the heavy iron keys of their own front doors. They carried each other. Somewhere in the crush a drum had started up, and it did not stop.

"They're turning on everyone," Sefa said beside him, small hands gripping the roof's edge. "Not just gifted folk. Everyone." The child's voice held no triumph. That was the thing about Sefa — even drowned things spoke through her gently. "The criers are saying anyone who *sheltered* the gifted. Anyone who *traded* with them. Anyone who *looked at them wrong*."

"Good," Brannic said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

Below them, the Salt Gate — the great arched throat through which every cart of salt, grief, and borrowed years had passed for three hundred years — was burning. Not the gate itself; the *tollhouse*. Hunters' cloaks went into the fire one after another, peeled off captured men who wept or begged or, worse, went silent with the particular dignity of people who still believed they were right.

Odile stood at the roof's far edge with the wind pulling at her hair, and she was not watching the fire. She was watching the dungeons.

Brannic followed her gaze. Beneath the gate's eastern tower, half-swallowed by the hill itself, the old guild cells ran down into the dark like roots. He could not see them from here. He did not need to. He knew Mother Quill was down there the way you know a stone is in your boot — a small, constant, unbearable pressure with every step.

"She's alive," Odile said. Not a hope. An accounting. "The contact in the undercity confirmed it this morning. They haven't executed her because they're still trying to get the rite out of her. Which means they'll keep trying until she gives them nothing left to try with." Her jaw tightened. "Which means we have days. Maybe hours, once she stops being useful."

"Then we go tonight," Brannic said.

Odile turned. In the firelight from below her face was all planes and shadow, and for a moment he saw the clerk she had been — someone who believed the world could be balanced, debits against credits, if only you kept the columns straight. He wondered when she'd stopped believing it. He wondered if she ever had.

"You can't," she said quietly. "Brannic. You know what you are now. What's left of what you are."

He did know. That was the trouble with spending yourself — the ledger never lied, even when you did. Six years ago he had taken a soldier's bargain: strength on loan, courage by the hour, paid back with interest out of the only currency he owned. He had spent it freely at first, then carefully, then desperately, and now there was so little left that he could feel the edges of himself going thin, like paper held up to a lamp. Some mornings he woke and could not remember his mother's voice, and he understood that it wasn't forgetting. It was *payment*. The magic took memories too, in the lean years. It took whatever was loose.

There was maybe one expenditure left in him. One night of what he had been. Perhaps two, if he was greedy, and greed would kill him slower and worse.

"I know what I am," he said. "I'm the strongest man in this city for exactly as long as it takes to get her out. After that I'm an ordinary soldier with bad knees and a good memory for fortifications. Fortunately—" he tapped the side of his head, "—the memory is the part that matters. I walked those cells for four years, Odile. I *guarded* them. I know which wall is rotten, which lock lies, which turnkey drinks."

"And after?"

"After," Brannic said, "you won't need me to be strong anymore."

She stared at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic — he could see it happening behind her eyes, the same cold beautiful bookkeeping she brought to everything, and he loved her for it and hated it, because he knew she would arrive at the true sum. She always did.

"That's not a plan," she said finally. "That's a price."

"Everything's a price. You taught me that." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was real. "You think I've been saving it? All these months, hoarding the last of myself for some better occasion?" He shook his head. "There is no better occasion. There's her. She traded her daughter's face so we could walk into the palace. You think I'm going to let the crown keep her in a hole while I die slowly of *prudence*?"

Sefa made a small sound. Brannic looked down and found the child crying without any noise at all, tears running into the collar of her coat, and something in his chest cracked along a line he hadn't known was there.

"Hey." He crouched, joints protesting — ordinary joints, already. "None of that. I'm not dying tonight. Tonight I'm the strongest man in the city. Say it back."

"You're not *supposed* to—" Sefa's voice broke, and the drowned things stirred around her; he felt them like cold water rising, all the patient dead of the harbor pressing close to comfort her, and even they seemed to be looking at him with pity. "The sea says people who spend themselves don't come back. The sea knows. People sink."

"Sefa." He took her small hand in his big one. "The sea also takes everything eventually. Every ship, every sailor, every name. The trick isn't holding on. The trick is choosing what you spend it *on*." He glanced up at Odile, standing rigid against the smoke-colored sky. "You two taught me that. I used to think my strength was mine. It never was. It was always borrowed. The only thing that was ever actually mine was what I chose to do with it."

* * *

They went in at the change of watch, because Brannic had chosen the hour the way he'd once chosen breaches: when the old shift was drunk on relief and the new one was sober but resentful, and neither wanted to be the one who noticed anything.

The city helped. That was the miracle none of them had planned for. The uprising had not died with the daylight; it had gone underground and sideways, and now it seethed through the streets like water finding cracks. Fires burned on the headlands where signal beacons had stood unlit for a generation. The garrison was stretched along the walls trying to hold back a population that had simply stopped obeying, and the dungeons beneath the Salt Gate were guarded by fourteen men who kept looking over their shoulders at the glow on the horizon and wondering if anyone was coming to relieve them.

No one was coming. Brannic had made sure of that, with forged orders in Mother Quill's own style — she would have wept with pride, or laughed, or both — redirecting reinforcements to the harbor warehouses where a phantom weapons cache waited to be found.

He went over the wall first, alone, because the last of his borrowed strength deserved to be spent honestly. He climbed the sheer outer face of the eastern tower with his fingers finding holds that should not have existed, hauling his body upward on arms that sang with power like struck bells, and at the top he paused, breathing hard not from effort but from the sheer *glory* of it — the wind, the burning city, the blood moving through him like a river in flood — and let himself feel, for ten full seconds, what he had been.

Then he dropped into the yard among the guards, and the night became very simple.

He had fought in wars. He had forgotten most of what the loan-shark magic had taken from him, but not this — the grammar of violence, the way a fight is a sentence and you must know where it ends before you begin speaking. Four men in the yard. He put the first through the timber door of the guardroom and did not check whether the door or the man fared worse. The second swung a halberd and Brannic caught the shaft and folded it closed like a book. The third and fourth ran, which was wise, and he let them, because fear traveled faster than soldiers and fear was on his side tonight.

The inner door was oak and iron, locked with a mechanism he had oiled a hundred times as a young trooper, and he hit it once, at the junction of beam and frame, and the whole thing came out of the wall.

Below, the cells breathed cold. Torches in brackets every twenty feet, guttering in air that had nowhere to go. Brannic descended into the dark of the place he had once patrolled from the other side, and the strangeness of it sat in his stomach like swallowed ice. He remembered the sounds these corridors made at night. He had told himself, back then, that the people screaming behind the doors deserved it — that the Gift-Hunt was justice, that the gifted were a rot in the kingdom's wood. He had needed to believe it, the way a man needs to believe the animal he slaughters doesn't suffer.

He opened every door anyway. All of them. He didn't sort the guilty from the innocent, because there was no such sorting, and because his hands were busy and his heart was done with arithmetic that ended in other people's chains.

Most of the cells held thieves and debtors and one furious smuggler who tried to recruit him before he'd finished picking the lock. Two held children with frightened, flickering eyes — a boy whose skin shimmered faintly like oil on water, a girl who simply would not stop humming, though Brannic suspected the humming was doing something, because the torches burned steadier near her. He sent them all up toward the surface and the waiting hands of Odile's undercity contacts, and he did not count them, because counting was Odile's gift and his was simpler: he lifted what needed lifting, broke what needed breaking, and asked nothing of the world in return except one more corridor.

Mother Quill was at the bottom.

Of course she was at the bottom. The deep cells were for the ones the crown feared, and the crown had good reason to fear a woman who could forge a soul's name well enough to fool God.

She sat on the stone floor with her back against the wall, and Brannic's heart stumbled, because she looked *small*. Mother Quill, who filled every room she entered like weather, like a verdict — reduced to a gray bundle in a gray cell, wrists chained above her head to a ring in the stone, hair come loose from its pins, one eye swollen shut. But the other eye found him in the torchlight and sharpened instantly, and her mouth curved into something that was absolutely not a smile of gratitude.

"Well," she rasped. "Took you long enough. Did you bring my tools?"

"I brought my hands."

"Then they'll have to do." She flexed her ruined fingers. "The ring bolt's set in lead. They weren't taking chances. Can you—"

He took the chain in both fists and pulled, and the ring tore out of the wall in a cough of dust and lead shavings, and Mother Quill swayed forward into him, and for one moment — one only — she pressed her forehead against his chest and stayed there, breathing.

Then she straightened, and she was Mother Quill again, terrible and precise.

"The interrogators," she said, voice low and rapid as they moved up the corridor, him half-carrying her, her refusing to be carried entirely. "Listen to me, Brannic, this matters. The usurper came himself. Twice. Not a shadow-projection, not a messenger — *him*, in the flesh, sitting across from me like a merchant at market. And he asked questions no tyrant asks. He asked about the *rite*. About whether unbinding hurts the one who performs it. About whether the bond could be severed *without* sacrifice." Her swollen eye gleamed. "Do you understand what that means? He's afraid of it. Something wearing that much stolen power, and he's afraid of a page of lore. Because somewhere under all those borrowed years, there's still a man who remembers what it cost to build himself."

"He offered Odile a partnership," Brannic said grimly. "In the palace. She refused."

"Mm. And he let her walk away." Mother Quill's good eye narrowed. "That's not mercy, boy. That's a fisherman leaving a line in the water. Mark me — he wants her. A gift like hers, that sees through his stitching? He needs her either bound to him or broken. And he will be very patient about which."

They reached the foot of the stair, and the world above them shuddered. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Somewhere overhead, muffled by tons of hill and stone, came a sound like the sky tearing — and beneath it, impossibly, a roar of human voices, thousands strong, rolling down through the rock like surf heard from the bottom of a well.

"The gate," Brannic breathed. "They've reached the gate."

"Then we have minutes, not hours." Mother Quill gripped his arm with fingers that felt like iron wire despite everything. "Go. Get me to the light."

* * *

He got her to the light.

He got her through the collapsed guardroom, through the yard where the fire had spread to the stables and horses screamed, through the postern tunnel he remembered from a smuggling scandal six years old, out onto the hillside where the city burned below them like a fallen constellation — and it was there, in the open air, with Mother Quill leaning on his arm and the uprising roaring through the streets like a second tide, that the loan came due.

He felt it the way a candle feels the last of the wick. One moment he was walking, the strongest man in the city, holding her upright through sheer borrowed thunder. The next, his legs informed him, politely and without drama, that they were finished. His knees met the grass. The night tilted. The fires smeared into long golden streaks, and the drumming of the crowd became the drumming of blood in his ears, and then that faded too, and there was a strange quiet, and Mother Quill was saying his name in a voice stripped of all its armor.

"Brannic. *Boy.* Look at me."

He looked at her. It was difficult. Everything was very far away and very close at once.

"There you are." Her hands framed his face, cool and certain. "Stay with me. Stay *here*."

"Spent it all," he managed. His mouth barely worked. "Every... last coin. Worth it. Tell her—" He had to stop. Swallow. Start again. "Tell Odile. The ledgers. She was right. Everything's a price. I just... picked mine."

"Don't you dare write your own eulogy in my presence," Mother Quill snapped, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of the sentence. "You listen to me. You are not permitted to die on a hillside like a discharged debt. Do you hear me? We are *not finished*. There is a king to unmake and a kingdom to rebuild and someone has to carry things, that's all you've ever been *for*, you enormous sentimental ox, and I forbid—"

But he was smiling. He could feel it on his face, lopsided and stupid, and he couldn't stop it, because through the fading of everything he could hear the city — the real city, the living one, thousands of ordinary voices shouting a name that wasn't the usurper's, hammering at the gates of a palace built on a vault full of stolen years — and it sounded, to his failing ears, exactly like family.

*Sefa had been right. The sea takes everything.*

*But look what it got spent on.*

Darkness came up gentle, like deep water, like sleep after a long march, and the last thing he knew was Mother Quill's grip on his hand — tight, furious, refusing — and her voice, still talking, still forbidding, following him down:

"—and if you leave us now I will forge you such a *name*, Brannic Vael, I will write you back into the world so thoroughly you'll be ashamed to stay dead—"

* * *

He woke to candlelight and the smell of tallow and salt, in a room he did not know, with Sefa asleep in a chair beside him and her small hand wrapped around two of his fingers.

His body felt like a house after the furniture has been sold — empty, echoing, honest. No thunder in the blood. No borrowed bells. Just a tired soldier's pulse, steady and plain and entirely, unmistakably, his own.

Alive, then. Against all arithmetic, alive — emptied to the last coin, but the coin-purse itself somehow still breathing. He would pay for that miracle later, he suspected. There was always a later payment.

On the table beside the bed lay a scrap of paper in Odile's precise clerk's hand, weighted down with a candlestub, clearly left for whenever he woke. He read it twice, because the first time his eyes wouldn't hold still.

*We have her route. The Emberhold opens from the undercity — Sefa's drowned ones showed us the way. Quill is forging our faces even now. Rest if you can. But if you wake before dawn — the palace gates fall tonight, and Odile means to be standing in front of the throne when they do.*

*Come home to us. Either way, come home.*

Brannic lay in the candlelight, listening to the distant roar of a city tearing its masters down, and began — slowly, joint by joint, muscle by ordinary muscle — to get up.
