## 21. New Entries

The counting-house had been a chandler's shop once, and it still smelled faintly of tallow and honey when the morning sun warmed the front windows. Odile had scrubbed the floors herself, on her knees with a bucket of salt water and river sand, working the old wax out of the grain of the boards until they showed pale and honest, like something newly made. That had been three weeks ago. Now the floor was scuffed again — good scuffs, earned ones — and the smell of the place had changed to ink and paper and Sefa's ever-present scent of brine, as if the sea had followed her indoors and decided to stay.

Odile stood at the long counter they'd built from salvaged ship-timber and ran her thumb along its edge. Smooth. She'd sanded it four times because Brannic kept finding splinters, and he kept finding them because he refused to say anything until one drew blood.

"Third shelf's bowing," he said now from the doorway, ducking under the lintel out of habit even though it had been raised twice for his height. He carried a crate of paper against his chest like an infant. His voice was rougher than it used to be — everything about him was thinner since the dungeons, since he'd spent the last of his borrowed strength breaking Mother Quill's cell open and walked out of Saltgate prison a man made entirely of his own bones. But his hands were steady. They had been steady every day since, and Odile had stopped checking them each morning only last week.

"Then we'll brace it," she said. "Everything in this city bows. We prop it up and keep working."

He grunted, which from Brannic was a sonnet, and set the crate down. The paper inside was good stock — cream-colored, rag-pressed, bought with the first coin the crown had paid them. *For services rendered in the matter of the restoration of His Majesty,* the writ had said, in language so careful it practically apologized. The king who came back small had insisted on paying them himself, coin by counted coin, refusing to let the treasury round the sum or delay it. "I spent twenty years being rounded down," he'd told Odile, in a voice like dry leaves, "by a brother I never knew wanted my shape. I will not do it to anyone else."

She thought of him often. The true king, restored, was a slight man with a seam of white through his hair where the unraveling had gone deepest, and he flinched at mirrors, and he ate his meals like a man who had forgotten food could taste of anything. But he signed his own name now, slowly, letter by letter, the way a child learns — because for twenty years no one had written it anywhere, and the muscles of it had to be taught back into his hand. When Odile had watched him practice it on scrap parchment, tongue between his teeth, she had understood something she couldn't yet say aloud: that a name is not a thing you have. It is a thing you do.

The bell over the door rang.

Their first client of the day was a woman in a fishmonger's apron, wringing her cap in both hands, and behind her a boy of perhaps ten who looked at the floor the way people look at deep water. Odile knew the look. It was the look of someone who had spent the winter learning to make themselves small enough to be overlooked.

"Mistress Eriksen?" the woman said. "They said — the criers said there was a place. For names."

"There's a place." Odile pulled out the great ledger — volume one, its spine already softening from use — and opened it flat on the counter. "Sit, both of you. There's tea if you want it. Tell me the name."

The woman sat. Her hands wouldn't stop moving. "My mother," she said. "Maren Ostergaard. She had the gift of — well, she could sweeten water. Just water. Nothing else. But when the Hunt came she ran north and they caught her at—" Her voice snagged. "They burned the record. Everything. Where she was born, who her people were. As if she'd never—" She pressed the cap against her mouth.

"As if she'd never been," Odile finished quietly. She dipped her pen. "Then we'll write her back. Maren Ostergaard. Born where?"

The story took an hour. It always did. The names came slowly at first and then all at once, the way floodwater comes — Maren's village, her mother's name, the daughter's own childhood memories, the particular way Maren had hummed while she worked, off-key, always the same song. Odile wrote it all. Not just the entry but the margins, the small truths that made a person more than a line in a register. She had learned this from Mother Quill, who said that a forged soul needed weight or it would blow away, and that a remembered soul needed the same.

When it was done, Odile read it aloud, all of it, and the woman cried, and the boy — who had not spoken once — leaned over the counter and touched the wet ink very carefully with one finger, as if checking whether it was real.

"It's real," Odile told him. "That's the whole point of the place."

* * *

At midday Sefa came in from the harbor with her sleeves rolled and her hair stiff with salt, carrying a covered pail that sloshed. She was eleven now by anyone's guess, though the drowned things insisted she was older than that, and she had grown half a head taller since the throne room. She kicked the door shut with her heel and set the pail on the counter with the gravity of a magistrate presenting evidence.

"Found another one," she announced. "Down past the breakwater. Old wreck, older than the Guild, older than the road." She lifted the cloth. Inside, cradled in wet kelp, lay a slate tablet furred with barnacle, its surface scratched with letters worn nearly smooth.

Odile bent close. The gift was gone — had been gone since the night she poured it out, since she'd named an erased twin back into existence until the false crown crumbled to salt between her hands — but she didn't need magic to read a list. She'd never needed magic for that. It was a manifest. A ship's roll. And along the bottom, in a different hand, cramped and hurried: names struck through, one after another, deliberately, thoroughly, the way a clerk strikes through a bad debt.

"They were erasing people even then," Sefa said. "Out at sea. Before the Hunt, before any of it." She looked up at Odile with those grey eyes that saw further underwater than most people saw across a room. "The drowned ones keep asking about you, by the way. They want to know if you're happy."

Odile laughed — startled out of her, bright and unguarded. "What did you tell them?"

"That you argue with your pen when it skips. They said that means yes."

* * *

Mother Quill arrived in the late afternoon, as she always did, precisely when the light turned gold and made her silver hair look deliberate rather than aged. She moved slowly these days — the dungeons had taken more than Brannic's strength; they had taken months of her, and some mornings her hands shook too badly to hold a pen, and on those mornings she dictated and Odile wrote and neither of them remarked on it. She carried a basket of plums, because she always carried something, because empty hands were for people with nothing left to give.

She surveyed the shop with the expression of a general reviewing troops. "The sign's crooked."

"The sign is charming."

"The sign says *Eriksen & Company, Counting-House*, and it hangs two degrees off true, which tells every forger in the city that the proprietor has no eye." She set the plums down and straightened her sleeves. "Fortunately the proprietor has me."

"You could hang it yourself."

"I could forge the seal of the Lord High Admiral," Quill said, "but I choose not to, on principle. Hanging signs is beneath my dignity and within your capability. Also," and here her voice softened by a degree so small that only family would catch it, "the light is better from inside. I like to watch you work."

Odile pretended to be very busy with the ledger. Some kindnesses were too large to look at directly.

Quill settled onto the stool reserved for her — the tall one, near the window, where she could see the street — and began, as she did every day, to review the day's entries. This was their ritual now, the three of them plus whoever drifted through: the audit. Not of coin. Of names. Every person recorded, every erasure reversed, cross-checked against the palace archives the restored king had thrown open without hesitation, against parish books smuggled out of burned villages, against Sefa's drowned witnesses and Brannic's soldier's memory of muster rolls. Somewhere in the city, other houses were doing similar work — the Guild of Quiet Hands had come out of hiding after the Hunt collapsed, blinking, and offered their network of secret-keepers to the effort — but this house was the first, and the others took their method from it, and Odile tried not to think about that too hard because it made her hands shake in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

"Entry forty-one," Quill read. "Tamsin Hollow, midwife, gift of easing pain. Recorded by her granddaughter. Good. Entry forty-two — hmm." She tapped the page. "You've given Wren Aldous two birthdates."

"He has two birthdates. His village recorded one, the army recorded another, and he swears by a third. I wrote down all of them and let posterity sort it out."

Quill looked at her over her spectacles for a long moment. Then she smiled — the rare one, the whole face one, the one Odile had seen maybe six times. "Your mistress would have called that sloppy bookkeeping."

"My mistress," Odile said, "called a great many things sloppy that turned out to be mercy."

It was easier to say now. *Her mistress.* The hedge-witch whose cottage had burned on a night eleven years ago, on a night Odile had lit the fire herself, half-accident and half-terror, afraid of the power growing in her own hands. She had confessed it all in the dark beneath the palace, cornered and certain she was about to die, and Brannic had said — she could still hear it, would hear it the rest of her life — *"You were a child who was scared. We've all burned something. You're still at this table."* And Sefa had taken her hand, small and salt-crusted, and said nothing at all, which had somehow been the loudest forgiveness in the room.

The guilt hadn't vanished. She suspected it never fully would. But it had changed shape — from a thing that owned her into a thing she carried, the way the ledger carried its entries: honestly, in full, available for inspection. That was what she'd learned from all of it, from Tammas Vey dying in the fog and the road that remembered and the king who came back small. You didn't get to be innocent. You got to be *accounted for*. And accounted-for was its own kind of clean.

* * *

Brannic cooked dinner, because Brannic cooking dinner was the arrangement and no one dared amend it. He filled the little back room with the smell of onions and river fish and the particular burnt edge that meant he'd gotten distracted arguing with Sefa about whether eels had opinions. They ate at the counter because the table wasn't finished, elbows bumping, passing the bread, and outside the window Saltgate went gold and then blue and the lamps came on one by one like slow stars.

"Letter from the palace," Brannic said, producing it from somewhere in his coat. "Came by runner, not post. Royal runner. Sefa made him show his boots."

"Why his boots?"

"Palace runners wear issued boots. Forgers wear good ones." He shrugged. "Quill's rules save time."

Odile broke the seal. The handwriting was the slow, laborious hand she knew, each letter built like a man laying stones.

*Mistress Eriksen — The archive work proceeds. Today I found my own coronation record, and beside it, in the same year, a christening record for a second prince, and the ink of the erasure over it is different from the ink around it. Someone was careless once, twenty years ago, and I have lived in that carelessness ever since. I tell you this because you will understand what it means: the erasures are not perfect. They are only thorough. Thoroughness can be undone by patience, and patience is what your house sells.*

*I have a request, and it is larger than the last one. There are thousands of names. Perhaps tens of thousands. The treasury can fund five more houses — ten, if you insist — but houses need keepers, and keepers need training, and I know of exactly one person who can teach frightened people how to write the truth down so it holds.*

*Come and build the school. Or don't, and build it here, in your chandler's shop, and I will send the students to you. Either way — the first class should begin soon. Winter is coming, and winters are when people forget things.*

*— Aldric, who is trying to be a king and mostly managing to be a man*

Odile read it twice. Then she passed it down the counter without a word, and watched Brannic read it, and Sefa standing on her stool to read it over his shoulder, and Mother Quill take it last and hold it at arm's length and make a sound that in anyone else would have been a laugh.

"Well?" Brannic said.

"Well what?"

"Are we doing it? Ten houses. A school. A whole—" he gestured with his fork at the shop, the street, the world, "—all of it."

Odile looked around the counter. At Brannic, hollowed and rebuilt, eating his own cooking and liking it. At Sefa, dripping on the stool, already whispering something to the pail of seawater she refused to explain. At Quill, folding the king's letter into precise quarters as if filing it in some inner archive of her own. At the great ledger lying open, forty-two names written back into the world, forty-three waiting for tomorrow.

Once, she had balanced books for a guild that traded in impossible things, and told herself the numbers were all she was. Once, she had a gift that let her see what things truly were, and she had traded it away to free a stranger, and woken the next morning expecting to feel amputated — and instead had felt, for the first time since the fire, *whole*. Because it turned out the seeing had never really lived in her. It had lived between them. In the crew of ashes, in the found thing none of them had planned, in the space where four damaged people kept choosing each other daily, without magic, without destiny, without anything but the stubborn arithmetic of showing up.

"We're doing it," she said. "But we start here. First class in this room, eight students, and Quill teaches forgery-detection because I refuse to raise a generation of clerks who can't spot a false seal."

"Naturally," said Quill.

"And Brannic teaches the patrol-mapping. Ledger-logic for staying alive."

"If there's chalk," he said.

"Sefa teaches—" Odile paused. "Sefa, what do you teach?"

Sefa considered this with the seriousness of a judge. "Listening," she said finally. "Nobody knows how anymore. Even before the Hunt. Especially the grown-ups."

"Listening," Odile agreed, and reached for the ledger, and turned to a fresh page.

The candle flame stood straight in the still air of the shop. She dipped her pen, and at the top of the page, in her best clerk's hand — the hand of a runaway apprentice who had become a keeper of names — she wrote:

*Volume One, continued. Entry the first of the school of Saltgate: all names welcome, all accounts kept, nothing erased, nothing forgotten.*

Below it, after a moment, she added one more line, small, in the margin where the true things went:

*Begun by O. Eriksen, who lit a fire once, and has spent every day since learning what else hands are for.*

She blotted the page, and the ink dried dark and permanent, and downstairs in the streets of Saltgate someone was singing — off-key, always the same song — and Odile blew out the candle and went to join her family in the noise.
