## 22. The Last Page of the Ledger

The counting-house smelled of tallow and ink and, faintly, of the sea that came up the hill on windy nights — salt threaded through everything now, the way it had been threaded through the guild books of her old life. Odile sat alone at the long table with the first volume of the new house's ledgers open before her, and she did not write.

She had been sitting there since the lamps were lit below, listening to the sounds of the house settle around her: Brannic's slow tread overhead as he carried Sefa's cot to the window where the child liked to hear the harbor; Mother Quill's voice, low and patient, teaching a trembling young woman in the front room how to sign her own name for the first time; the clatter of the kettle; laughter that broke off and started again, uncertain as a fledgling's wings but growing bolder every day.

Three weeks since the Salt Gate opened. Three weeks since the crown crumbled to white dust on the throne room floor and a small, gray king woke weeping in the wreckage of his brother's hunger. Three weeks, and still Odile could not make herself close this book.

It was not an ordinary ledger. She had built it that way deliberately, entry by careful entry, in the months since they'd hung the shingle over the door — *Eriksen & Company, Counting-House* — though there was no company yet, only five people and whatever strays the Gift-Hunt had scattered into their arms. The first pages held ordinary accounts: two coppers owed to the baker, a debt of gratitude recorded because gratitude was also currency here, a list of names recovered from the palace archives, names the old regime had cut loose from their owners like threads snipped from a coat. Every name had been entered twice — once in ink, once read aloud at supper so it lived in more than paper. Paper burned. Paper could be seized. But a name spoken nightly over soup, argued over, laughed over, belonged to everyone who had heard it.

Tonight the book lay open to its final page, and Odile dipped her pen, and her hand would not move.

*You're stalling,* said the part of her that sounded like Tammas Vey, that dying man's dry humor preserved forever in her memory like a pressed flower. *You've balanced impossible books your whole life, girl. What's one more entry?*

But it wasn't one more entry. It was the last page of the first volume, and she had known from the beginning what belonged on it. She had known since the night she confessed to Brannic and Sefa beside a dying fire in the undercity, since the moment they chose her anyway. The true account. Everything. Including the parts no auditor would ever volunteer.

She looked down at her hands. They were just hands now. No gift shimmered beneath the skin when she touched an object and asked it silently what it truly was. That sense — the seeing, the knowing, the quiet certainty that had let her look at a bottled grief and know whose it was, look at a forged soul-name and know the truth underneath — was gone, poured out of her into the bond between two brothers until the false crown came apart like wet sand. Some mornings she reached for it out of habit, the way a tongue seeks a missing tooth, and found only silence, and found, strangely, that the silence did not hurt.

What remained was skill, and memory, and the people downstairs.

Odile straightened her spine, the way her mistress had taught her — *a crooked spine makes crooked numbers, girl* — and began to write.

---

*Final entry, first volume, Eriksen & Company.*

*I am Odile Eriksen, formerly apprentice to Maren Solvang, hedge-witch of the Fenmarsh road, deceased eleven years past. I record here the true account of her death, which I have told to no one entire, and which belongs in this book because this book exists to hold what others tried to erase.*

*I killed her.*

*Not with malice. Not even, I think, entirely with my hands. I was fourteen, and my gift was growing faster than my control, and Mistress Maren had begun to speak of binding it — not cruelly, never cruelly, but she spoke of it the way one speaks of lopping a branch that threatens the roof. I heard only the lopping. I was afraid of her, and more afraid of myself, and one night when she lit the stove and turned her back, I felt my power rise in me like floodwater against a door, and I did not hold it down. I let it go. The fire took the cottage in four breaths. She did not scream. I have always hated most that she did not scream — that she must have understood, even then, even burning, what I had done and why, and loved me enough to be silent about it.*

*I ran. I hid my gift inside ledgers and arithmetic for eleven years, telling myself that hiding was safety, that safety was virtue. It was not. Hiding was the first stone of the road that led to the Emberhold, because a world where the gifted must conceal themselves is a world where the gifted can be hunted, and a world where they can be hunted is a world where something hungry can wear a king's face and call itself order.*

*Here follows the rest, briefly, for the rest is already written elsewhere in these pages: the hunt, the fog, Tammas Vey dying among the fish crates and selling me his secret for a promise I intend to keep until I die. The road that showed me my fire. Brannic spending his borrowed strength like a man spending his own blood, because it was, in the end, his to spend. Sefa hearing the drowned count their dead and finding a living brother in the count. Mother Quill trading away her daughter's face so that strangers might walk safely through a gate — and getting that face back, piece by piece, from the daughters of other women who remembered it for her, because love is the one debt that compounds in the debtor's favor.*

*The vault. The unraveling king. The rite. My choice.*

*I poured out my gift freely, and I record here that I do not regret it, and that this absence of regret frightens me less than it once did. What I learned in the throne room, with the roof coming down around us and a made thing screaming itself back into being a man, is this: power taken alone corrupts, but power given away becomes something else entirely. It became mercy. It became a twin brother remembered. It became a crown of salt.*

*And then I came home to people who knew the worst of me — who knew I had burned the only mother I had — and they set a place for me at the table anyway. Brannic said, "A debt you confess isn't a debt that owns you." Sefa said nothing, but put her hand in mine, which is the child's way of balancing any account. And Mother Quill, who has forgotten her daughter's face and remembers mine instead, told me that forgiveness is not forgetting. It is writing the truth down and choosing to keep the book open.*

*So: I was a runaway, a liar, a killer, a clerk, a rebel, an unmaker. I am all of those things still, in the way that a ledger holds every entry ever made in it. And I am also this: the keeper of this house, where the formerly hunted come to have their names written back into the world.*

*Account closed. Account carried forward.*

*Carried forward into hands that are only hands, and are enough.*

---

Her pen stopped. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and the wax pooled in the saucer like something molten and holy. Outside, the wind had come up off the water, and somewhere below a shutter knocked its small wooden knuckle against the frame.

Odile read the entry through once, the way she read every account — hunting for errors, for evasions, for the small beautiful lies that books tell when their keepers grow tired. There were none. It was terrible and true and complete, and her eyes had gone hot somewhere around the fourth paragraph, and she let them, because no one was watching, and because she had spent eleven years not crying and had earned the interest.

Then she took the sealing wax — real wax, bought dear from a chandler who didn't ask questions — and held it to the flame until it wept red. She let it fall across the corner of the final page, and pressed her thumb into it, and held it there while it cooled, her print rising in the hardening wax like a small pink island in a red sea. Not a sigil. Not a mark of power. Just a thumbprint, the oldest signature there was, proof that a particular human hand had been here and meant it.

She closed the volume. Its covers met with a sound like a door shutting gently on a warm room.

* * *

The stairs creaked — they always creaked, third step from the bottom, and Brannic kept promising to fix them and Sefa kept secretly preventing it, because the creak was a warning system and also, the child said, "the house talking." Odile descended through the smell of bread and lamp oil and woodsmoke, carrying the closed ledger against her chest like a sleeping animal.

The front room was full. It was always full now, though "full" was a generous word for a space meant to be a shop and serving instead as kitchen, schoolroom, sanctuary, and on Thursdays, when Mother Quill hosted the naming circle, something closer to a church. Tonight it held Brannic, enormous and unhurried, kneading dough with the concentration he'd once given to battlefield formations; Sefa cross-legged on the table itself, forbidden and permitted simultaneously, sorting shells into piles only she understood; Mother Quill by the window with three newcomers, a fisherman's widow and her two sons, walking them through the words of their own names as if teaching a prayer.

"—and when you can say it without flinching," Quill was saying, "it's yours again. No one can take a thing you say whole."

"Odile!" Sefa spotted her first, because Sefa always spotted her first, some drowned-born instinct for the people who mattered. "Did you finish it? Did you finish the book?"

"I finished the book."

"Read us the last line."

Brannic looked up, flour to his elbows, and Quill turned from the window, and the widow and her sons went quiet, sensing — as people in this house quickly learned to sense — that something was being weighed.

Odile stood in the doorway with the ledger in her arms and considered lying, or deflecting, or simply saying *accounts balanced* and reaching for the bread. Eleven years ago she would have. Six months ago she would have.

Instead she opened the book to the last page, holding it so the lamplight fell across her own cramped, careful hand, and read aloud:

"*Account closed. Account carried forward.*"

"Carried forward where?" Sefa demanded.

"Into volume two." Odile shut the cover. "Which starts tomorrow, and whose first entry will be the widow's sons, if they're willing."

The younger boy's chin came up. His mother's hand found his shoulder. "We're willing," she said, in the voice of a woman deciding to live.

"Then it's settled." Brannic thumped the dough down like a gavel. "Supper first. Nothing gets written on an empty stomach — house rule."

"Since when?" Odile asked.

"Since I invented it just now." He grinned, and the grin had lost its soldier's edge somewhere over the winter and become something simpler and more dangerous to his dignity: happiness.

They crowded toward the table. Sefa claimed Odile's sleeve and towed her, chattering about a shell that had whispered to her of a shipwreck full of unclaimed names — *a whole cargo of them, Odile, whole families, nobody's written them down in sixty years* — and Odile listened and nodded and felt the familiar ache behind her ribs that was not grief anymore, or not only grief, but the specific tenderness of a person who knows exactly how much work remains and is no longer afraid of it.

Mother Quill caught her arm as they sat. The old woman's eyes, which had seen too much to be surprised by anything, rested on the closed ledger with something like ceremony.

"You wrote it all," she said. Not a question.

"All of it."

"Including the fire."

"Including the fire."

Quill squeezed her wrist once, hard, the way she did instead of embracing anyone. "Then it's a true book. We'll build the house on it." She raised her voice to the room. "Sit down, all of you, before Brannic takes a spoon to someone."

Lamps were lit. Bread was broken. The widow's sons told the story of their father's boat, and Sefa promised to ask the harbor what it remembered of him, and Brannic laughed at something, and the noise rose the way it did every night now — layered, imperfect, alive, the sound of a family that had assembled itself out of wreckage and refused, on principle, to stop making noise.

Odile sat in the middle of it with bread in one hand and no magic anywhere in her body, and understood that this was the balance she had been working toward her whole life without knowing it. Everything given accounted for. Everything taken repaid. Nothing hidden, nothing erased, and the book — the true book — sealed and shelved and safe.

Tomorrow there would be names to write. Tomorrow there would be a shipwreck, and a second volume, and the third step that still creaked, and all the ordinary miraculous labor of putting the world back together one entry at a time.

Tonight there was only this: the candle upstairs blown out, the ledger closed, and the noise of her family rising around her like warmth off a hearth —

— and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the front door opening, and a voice she did not know saying, hesitantly, into the lamplight:

"I was told — they said this is the place where you write people back. Is that true? Is this the place?"

Odile set down her bread, and reached for a fresh page.
