## 2. What the Gods Bought

The sigil came at dawn, the way debts always do.

I was in my room above the counting-house, three floors of ledgers below me and a cup of cold chicory on the sill, when my right hand began to burn. Not the ache of old ink-stains or the cramp of too many hours with a quill. This was heat from underneath, as if something had been written into me years ago and had only now decided to dry.

I turned my hand over. The mark sat in the meat of my palm, where an oath-taker's brand goes: a circle broken by a vertical line, the old sign of the Quiet One, the god who stopped answering before I was old enough to know his name. Except now it was wet and red and new, and it pulsed like a second heartbeat.

"Vesper," said my own mouth.

Not me. My voice, but pitched wrong — deeper, slower, like a man speaking through a wall of water. My jaw moved without my permission. The chicory cup rattled against the sill.

"You are the door."

"I'm a bookkeeper," I said out loud, to the empty room, because arguing with gods is a habit you pick up even when they're silent for thirty years.

"The door does not choose its hinges." The voice left my throat raw. Then it was gone, and my hand was mine again, and the sigil stayed, angry and awake, like a coal that refused to die down.

I sat on the edge of my cot for a long time, listening to Greyhollow wake up below — gulls screaming over the fish-market, the winch-groan of the harbor cranes, someone's cart axle complaining on the cobbles. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world where gods kept their silence and men kept their books, and everything balanced.

Except nothing balanced anymore. The dead had spoken through our séance-table two nights ago. And now my god had spoken through *me*, uninvited, unpaid, without so much as a contract. In the Gilded Ledger we had a term for that. We called it theft.

* * *

Ondrell was waiting in her office when I came down, which meant she'd been told about my hand before I'd finished dressing. News travels fast in a guild that trades in impossible things; impossible things are our whole inventory.

She sat behind a desk of black walnut older than the guild itself, small and neat and gray as a ledger's margin, her spectacles catching the lamplight. On the desk lay the account book for the Fairweather file — my file. My oath, my wages, my debts. She tapped one long fingernail against it.

"Show me the hand."

I showed her. She looked at the sigil for a long moment, and I watched her do the arithmetic behind her eyes — the same arithmetic she did over every asset and every liability. I knew what I was in that equation. I'd been a line item since I was nine years old and she'd bought my oath off a drowning priest for the price of a funeral.

"A door," she said. "That's what it called you?"

"It called me a hinge, actually. Doors get more respect."

"Don't be clever, Vesper. Cleverness is a cost with no return." She removed her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, which she only did when she was about to say something that would hurt. "There were men here last night. Ashen Choir. They paid a retainer for information regarding marked individuals. I gave them your name."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something, ordinary and obscene.

"You sold me."

"I sold *information*, which is what this guild sells. You are not property, Vesper, whatever the oath says." She put the spectacles back on. "You are a debt. And the Choir pays better than the god who holds your paper ever has. He's been in arrears for thirty years. Frankly, I'm surprised he finally made a payment."

I looked at my burning hand. "This is a payment?"

"It's a *down payment*. There's a difference, and you of all people should know it." She slid a folded paper across the desk. "They'll come within the hour. The Choir doesn't knock. Go out the coal-chute, take the ferryman's stairs, and consider your contract with this guild dissolved by act of war. I'll write it up as a casualty. It's cleaner."

"Why?" I asked. "Thirty years you've held my leash. Why cut it loose now?"

For just a moment, something moved behind Ondrell's eyes that wasn't arithmetic. Something tired.

"Because I've read every ledger this guild owns," she said, "and in forty years I have never once seen a column labeled *mercy*. But I can balance around a missing entry. Go, Vesper. And don't come back. Dead assets depreciate."

* * *

I went out through the coal-chute with nothing but the clothes I stood in, my satchel of working tools, and one item of inventory I had no right to: the bottled summer.

It sat in my coat pocket like a captured sunbeam — a stoppered glass vial, warm to the touch, full of light the color of wheat fields and late afternoons. Marta Hale had paid it to us to ask the dead about her son. The dead had answered. Her son was alive, and north, and the answer had cost the world its silence. By every law of the guild, the summer belonged to the house.

By every law of mine, it belonged to her son. I intended to deliver it.

Greyhollow was already hunting me. I could feel it in the way the market went quiet as I passed, in the ash-gray hoods at the ends of streets, in the chalk marks appearing on doorposts — the Choir marking territory the way their god once marked souls. They wanted their door opened. They hadn't yet understood that doors don't get asked. They get *broken*.

So I did what any sensible fugitive does. I found the one person in Greyhollow who knows everywhere, and I prepared to pay an outrageous fee.

Maren Ostwick kept a map-shop on Tallow Lane, wedged between a chandler and a shop that sold nothing but keys. She was seventy if she was a day, half-blind in one eye, and rumored to have charted coastlines that no longer existed. When I slipped through her door and barred it behind me, she didn't even look up from her drafting table.

"Choir's chalk is on six houses between here and the harbor," she said. "You're either running from them or recruiting for them, and you don't have the shoulders for recruitment."

"They want me to open something. I want to close everything." I set the bottled summer on her table. "One question. Name your price."

She looked at the vial then, and her good eye went wide, and for a moment Maren Ostwick looked like a girl remembering a field. "Where did you get summer? It's been wintering early for years. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it."

"That's the question I need answered. Why is it getting colder? Where does the cold come from?"

Maren was quiet a long time. Then she rolled out a map — not one of hers, older, the vellum gone soft as cloth — and weighted its corners with inkpots. It showed the northern reaches past the wintering wastes, and there, where the mountains should have been, was a blank space. Not unmapped. *Erased*. Someone had scraped the vellum clean, and beneath the scraping, faint as breath on glass, ghost-lines of roads and rivers still showed.

"There was a country there," she said. "Forty years ago. Vhalen. You won't find it in any archive, because when it went, everything about it went — records, songs, gravestones, memories. People woke up one morning with a hole in their heads shaped like a nation." She tapped the scraped space. "The cold comes from there. Winter's been leaking out of that wound for decades, and now it's sealing shut for good. When it closes, the leak stops — and so does everything else still inside."

"And the way out?"

"The Ember Gate. Last door out of Vhalen. If it still stands." She looked up at me, and her voice dropped. "Here's the strange part, bookkeeper. That country was erased so thoroughly that nobody should be able to remember the Gate's location. But you asked me the question like a man who already knows the road. Like a man who's walked it."

"I've never been north of Greyhollow."

"Mm." Maren began copying the ghost-lines onto fresh paper, quick and certain, her pen finding roads she supposedly couldn't remember. "Then it's odd that your hand knew where to point when I mentioned the name. Look at it."

I looked. The sigil in my palm had shifted — the broken circle now oriented north, like a compass needle, like a finger pointing home to a place I had never been.

Outside, somewhere down Tallow Lane, a bell began to ring. Not a church bell. An ash-bell, cracked and discordant, the sound the Choir used to call a hunt.

Maren folded the map into my hands and pushed the bottled summer back after it. "Keep your currency. You'll need it where you're going — summer buys miracles in a frozen country." She was already snuffing lamps, opening a back door onto the alley. "Run north, bookkeeper. And Vesper — when you find that Gate, ask yourself why a god who's been silent for thirty years chose *you* to be his door. Doors aren't chosen at random. They're built that way."

I ran. Behind me the ash-bell rang, and ahead of me the map pointed north into a blank space that my bones insisted was home — and in my pocket, a stolen summer burned warmer with every step, as if it knew it was going back where it came from.
