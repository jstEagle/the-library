## 3. The Country That Wasn't

The wastes did not want him there.

Vesper felt it the way a ledger feels an unbalanced column — a wrongness in the arithmetic of the land, snow that fell upward some mornings, rivers that ran backward toward their sources as if ashamed. He walked north out of Greyhollow with the bottled summer strapped against his chest, warm through three layers of wool, and every mile the cold grew less like weather and more like opinion.

*You are walking in the right direction,* said the god.

He didn't answer. He'd stopped answering on the second day, when it had spoken through his own mouth while he slept — he'd woken to find frost on his lips shaped like words, and the words had been *closer now* in a voice that was his voice but older, hungrier, worn smooth like a stone other things had been dragged across.

*You were always walking this way,* the god said. *Even before you knew there was a way.*

"Shut up," Vesper told the wind, and the wind carried it obediently north, which felt like a small betrayal.

* * *

On the ninth night he dreamed of a village.

Not Greyhollow, not any port he'd worked. A village of low stone houses with turf roofs, smoke rising straight in windless air, a well at the center with a wooden cover carved into the shape of a sleeping fox. He knew the well. He knew that if you lifted the cover quietly enough, you could hear the water dreaming.

He woke with tears frozen on his cheeks and no idea whose they were.

*They are yours,* the god said gently, and gentleness from it was worse than hunger. *That is the problem.*

He built a fire and took out the map the cartographer had sold him — a scrap of hide, inked in a hand that shook. The cartographer had been drunk, or honest, or both. *"There's no road to Vhalen,"* she'd said. *"There never was. You walk until the maps stop arguing with each other, and then you're close."*

He looked at the hide now. The blank space where Vhalen should have been had begun, faintly, to fill itself in — hills, a river, a cluster of marks that might have been houses. Ink bleeding in from nowhere. His hands were shaking too.

*You remember the lullaby,* the god said. It wasn't a question.

He did. Three notes rising, two falling, a word at the end that meant *sleep, the door is watched.* He'd hummed it for years without knowing where he'd learned it, the way you carry a splinter so deep the flesh grows around it. He'd assumed it was something from before the guild, before the ledgers, some half-life he'd lived and lost.

*It is not half anything,* the god said. *Keep walking.*

* * *

The border announced itself by absence.

One step: wind, scree, the grey light of a country ending. Next step: stillness so complete his ears rang. The snow here lay undisturbed — not fresh, just untouched, as though nothing had fallen or flown or walked in forty years. And the light came from everywhere and nowhere, the color of the inside of a closed eyelid.

At his feet, half-buried: a milestone. He knelt and brushed it clear. The inscription had been chiseled off, deliberately, but beneath the gouges his thumb found grooves that fit his fingers like a lock fits its key.

*VHAL-* said the stone, one syllable surviving, and Vesper sat down hard in the snow.

"I don't know this place."

*You know it better than anywhere,* the god said. *You were made here. Do you truly think a guild would take a foundling with no name and no history and teach him impossible accounting out of charity? They kept you because you were already impossible. A boy from a country that doesn't exist, carrying debts no one could collect. You were collateral, Vesper. You have been collateral your whole life.*

"That's not—" He stopped. Because the memories were coming now whether he wanted them or not, arriving like refugees: turf roofs. The fox-well. A woman's hands, flour to the wrist, singing three notes rising and two falling. A man with a smuggler's scarred palms pressing a swaddled bundle into the dark of a wagon and whispering *the door is watched, sleep.* Fire on a horizon that was not sunset. Running. Always running south.

His name — his first name, the one before Fairweather — surfaced whole, and he clamped his mouth shut around it like a man holding back vomit. Some things you do not say aloud in a place like this. Names are currency here. He knew that professionally.

"They erased it," he said hoarsely. "The whole country. From maps. From memory."

*From memory,* the god agreed. *But not from you. You were outside when they pulled the thread. A loose end. A splinter of Vhalen walking around wearing a man's shape.* A pause, and then, with something almost like tenderness: *And a door, little splinter, needs only be pushed.*

* * *

They came at dusk-that-wasn't-dusk, out of the featureless light: nine figures in ash-grey robes, faces masked with porcelain painted to look like closed eyes. The Ashen Choir. They did not draw weapons. They didn't need to; the air around them hummed with held breath, with prayer compressed into something weaponizable.

"Door," said the foremost, in a voice like a hymn played backward. "The Gate answers only to what was born behind it. Open yourself, and it opens. Be our key, and be rewarded with purpose everlasting."

"I have a purpose," Vesper said. "I keep accounts."

"The accounts are settled." The masked face tilted. "Your guild sold you. Your god bought you. Everyone has a claim on you, door. It is only mercy to let them collect."

And the god rose in him then — all the way up, no longer a voice but a pressure, a flood, filling his mouth and his hands and the space behind his eyes. *Open,* it said, through his own throat, in his own stolen voice. *Let me through. I have waited forty years in the dark between prayers. I will make you a prophet. I will make you a country. Open.*

His right hand — the ledger-hand, the branded one — began to move without him, tracing the sigil in the air, and the air remembered it, and somewhere ahead in the white silence something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

The Choir sank to its knees. The porcelain masks smiled.

Vesper stood at the center of it, a puppet of two masters, and understood with terrible clarity what he was: not a man, not anymore. A hinge. An instrument. A page in someone else's ledger, and both of them writing.

*Unless,* whispered something very small and very much his own, buried deep beneath the flood — the voice that balanced columns, that kept the books, that had spent thirty years asking one question of every impossible entry:

*Who holds the pen?*

Ahead, through the parting light, the Ember Gate showed itself — a seam of fire in the face of the world, older than the erasure, waiting to see who would claim it.

Vesper opened his eyes.
