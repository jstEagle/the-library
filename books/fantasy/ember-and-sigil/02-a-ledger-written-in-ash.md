## 2. A Ledger Written in Ash

The Ledgerhouse kept its dead accounts the way a city kept its dead: below ground, in ordered rows, with names on the doors and no expectation of visitors.

Sebastian Brightwater had been given the audit as a kindness, or what passed for kindness in the Ledgerhouse. *Third Subvault, western shelves, accounts older than the current charter,* Guildmaster Corvane had said that morning, sliding a brass key across his desk like a man dealing cards he'd already counted. *Nothing down there will bite you. If it does, note it in triplicate.* The junior clerks had smirked into their inkwells. Auditing the deep archive was work for the newest pair of hands, and Sebastian's were the newest by three weeks.

He didn't mind. He had discovered, in his twenty-two years and one flight from everything he knew, that he was happiest when his hands were full of someone else's order.

The Third Subvault smelled of cold stone and tallow and something faintly sweet, like fruit going over in a locked room. Lamplight fell in yellow pools along the aisle, and beyond the pools the dark was absolute—the kind of dark that made you aware of your own breathing. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling, iron-banded, each shelf crowded with ledgers bound in leather, vellum, bark-cloth, one unsettling volume in scaled hide that Sebastian had decided not to look at too closely. Every spine was lettered in the guild's precise hand: dates, regions, class of trade.

*Class of trade.* That was the phrase that had first made him love this place, back when he was still telling himself he'd only stopped here to rest. Bottled storms, class four. Borrowed years, class two—restricted. Names not yet earned, class seven, and gods help you if you misfiled one. In Maren Thistlewood's cottage, magic had been a hedge-row craft: charms muttered over churn butter, fevers sweated out with nettle and willow-bark and a word or two that cost you a nosebleed. Here, magic was *inventory*. It had weights and measures, provenance and liability, a column for spoilage. It was the first thing Sebastian had ever seen that made the impossible feel like it could be held still long enough to be trusted.

He wanted to be trusted like that. He suspected it would take years, and he had already learned not to want things loudly.

So he worked. He pulled ledgers one at a time, checked them against the master manifest, noted discrepancies in his own neat hand. Most volumes were dull in the way treasure is dull to anyone but its keeper: forty pages of grain futures paid in weather-favors; a dispute over a river's course settled with nine years of someone's childhood; an entire ledger devoted to a single lullaby sold eleven times and never once delivered. He catalogued. He cross-referenced. Somewhere around the fourth hour, with lamplight pooling and dust drifting through it like slow snow, Sebastian realized he was humming, and stopped, and then—glancing at the dark at either end of the aisle—started again, quieter.

It was on shelf fourteen, wedged behind a run of customs manifests so boring they had probably never been read twice, that he found the ledger that did not belong.

He noticed it first by touch. The other volumes gave up their dust in a dry whisper against his fingers; this one was *warm*. Not sun-warm. Hearth-warm. The warmth of something recently alive.

He drew it out and turned it in the light.

It was small—a clerk's pocket ledger, the kind carried into field negotiations—and it was sealed shut. Not with wax; with ash. Someone had packed the page-edges with fine gray powder until the book became a single solid block, then pressed a sigil into the ash while it set. Sebastian knew sigils. He'd spent six years learning them from a woman who could draw a warding-circle blindfolded in a rainstorm, and the mark on this seal was nothing she had ever taught him. It was a border-sign, or the ghost of one: a line crossed by a line crossed by a line, drawn so that your eye kept trying to follow it and kept arriving somewhere else.

The label on the spine had been scraped off. Under the scrape, in the raking light, he could just make out the ghost of older letters: *COVENANT ACCO—*

The rest was gone.

Sebastian stood very still in the lamplight and listened to the subvault breathe. Stone ticked as it cooled. Somewhere far above, the great counting-floor murmured like a hive. Nothing else.

Here is what a sensible clerk would have done: logged the anomaly, reported the unlisted volume, requested instruction. Sebastian had been a sensible clerk for three weeks and it fit him like a borrowed coat—close enough to pass, wrong in every seam. Because he had fled one life already, and he knew the taste of doors closed in his face, and there was a part of him—hungry, disloyal, *his*—that looked at a ledger the guild had hidden behind its most boring books and thought: *that is mine. That is mine the way I am mine, and no one has ever asked my permission to keep things from me.*

He broke the seal.

It was easier than it should have been. His thumb found the sigil's crossing-point and pressed, and the ash sighed apart like a crust on snow, powdering his fingers gray. The warmth breathed out at him, faintly scented—woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke something mineral and old, like the air off a forge that has been cold for a century.

The pages inside were written in ash. Not *with* ash—in ash. Each character sat slightly raised off the page, granular, gray-black, and as he watched, the letters at the top of the first page faded even as new ones darkened at the bottom, the way coals die at one end of a fire while they catch at the other. The whole book was one slow, continuous burning. Whatever wrote it was writing it *now*, and had been writing it for a very long time, and would go on writing it until the fuel ran out.

Sebastian began, with hands that were almost steady, to read.

---

*Paid: one season's true dawn, held in escrow, to the Wardens of the North Reach — that the sky may stay lit above Harrow Fen.*

*Paid: the last word of a dying language, spoken once and surrendered — that the sky may stay lit above the Salt Steps.*

*Paid: three hundred unnamed graves, their grief rendered down — that the sky may stay lit.*

*Received: nothing. This is the covenant's portion. We pay to keep the sky lit, and we are paid in the keeping.*

---

He turned pages. The entries went back and back, the handwriting shifting through centuries of clerical hands—flowery, cramped, brutal-efficient—but always in ash, always the same refrain threading through like a warp-thread: *to keep the sky lit. To keep the sky lit. To keep the sky lit.*

And then, near the middle of the book, the refrain changed.

---

*Paid: one-third portion, diverted per amendment — that the sky may stay lit above the Emberlands also.*

*Amendment struck. Diversion ends. The Emberlands' share reverts to the human realm, per signatory instruction.*

*Note appended by Auditor Vess: the signatories did not both instruct this. One signature is forged. I have said so. I have been told to say less.*

*Paid: one auditor's memory of this entry — that the sky may stay lit, and that she may keep her position, and her tongue.*

---

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. He turned faster now, ash smearing his fingertips, little gray ghosts of letters transferring themselves onto his skin so that his hands looked like they'd been rummaging in a cold hearth.

The entries after Auditor Vess grew sparser. The payments grew stranger. A decade of rainfall from one valley, bought and banked. The color blue, for one generation, from a fishing village whose descendants still painted their boats gray without knowing why. And threaded through all of it, growing more frequent as the pages neared the present, entries that did not say *paid* at all:

---

*Drawn: 4 marks, covenant-current, source undisclosed.*

*Drawn: 11 marks, covenant-current, source undisclosed.*

*Drawn: drawn drawn drawn —*

---

That last line wasn't in any clerk's hand. It was smeared, frantic, the ash dragged sideways as if the writer's hand had shaken apart mid-word. After it, the page was blank except for a single fresh line, still cooling, still faintly warm when he held his palm above it:

*Drawn: [amount redacted], source: the anchor itself. Note: the anchor is waking up. Note: whoever reads this, count the stars tonight. Count them, and remember what number you get.*

Sebastian closed the book.

The sound it made was soft—ash on ash—and somehow that was worse than a slam. He stood in the lamplight holding a warm dead-dying book against his chest and made himself think like a clerk, because thinking like a clerk was the only discipline he owned.

Facts. One: the Ledgerhouse held an account of the covenant between the realms, which was supposed to be a matter of legend, a story hedge-witches told children about why the stars burned steady and the wards between worlds held. Two: the account showed payments—enormous, impossible payments—made continuously, secretly, *by someone*, for longer than the guild had existed. Three: those payments had been diverted, a third of them, away from the Emberlands, on a forged signature, generations ago. Four: someone was drawing against the covenant now. Siphoning it. Regularly. In amounts worth redacting.

Five: the anchor was waking up. He didn't know what the anchor was. Some animal part of him, some part older than his apprenticeship, older than his name maybe, whispered that he didn't want to know.

Six: *count the stars tonight.*

He looked up, though of course there were no stars down here, only lamplight and dark. He thought about the border wards he'd felt humming along the north road when he'd come to the city, a sensation like standing near an enormous struck bell that never finished ringing. Everyone felt it. Nobody talked about it. It was simply the world, the way gravity was the world.

*We pay to keep the sky lit, and we are paid in the keeping.*

Who collected the payment? Who had signed? What was covenant-current worth on the open market—and who in the Ledgerhouse had been spending it?

Somewhere above him, muffled through feet of stone, the counting-floor bell rang the change of watch. Sebastian started so badly he nearly dropped the ledger. He looked down at himself: ash to the wrists, ash smudged where he'd gripped the book, a fine gray dusting on his waistcoat front like the residue of a fire he'd crawled out of.

A sensible clerk would report this. Would carry the volume up to Corvane's office and lay it on the desk and say *sir, this was misfiled*, and watch the Guildmaster's face, and learn something from whatever crossed it.

Sebastian looked at the ledger. The ledger, warm as a sleeping bird, seemed to look back.

He wrapped it in his inventory cloak, tucked it under his arm, and told himself—as he climbed the stairs, as he nodded to the door-warden, as he crossed the counting-floor with his heart going like a hammer in a bell—that he was taking it up to report. That this was exactly what a diligent auditor would do. That the quickening in his blood was diligence.

He was lying, and some quiet, starved part of him knew it, and didn't care. For the first time since he'd run from Maren Thistlewood's cottage with a knapsack and a bleeding heart, Sebastian Brightwater had something that was *his*—a secret, a burden, a thread that, if he pulled it, might unravel everything, including the careful lie of a life he'd built here.

He reached his narrow room under the eaves as the light failed. Through his one window, the evening sky over the city was doing what it always did: kindling, one star at a time, in the old reliable order. The Shepherd first, low in the west. Then the Sisters, three bright points above the harbor. Then the rest, hundreds, thousands, the whole patient machinery of the lit sky that someone, somewhere, according to a book of cooling ash, was paying for in pieces of the world.

Sebastian sat on his cot with the ledger in his lap and counted.

Shepherd. Sisters. The Miller's Wheel, turning. The Wolf, the Lamp, the long pale scatter of the Ember Road running south toward the border no one crossed anymore.

He counted twice, because the first time he got a number that frightened him, and the second time he got the same number, and it was not the number of stars there were supposed to be.

There were fewer.

Only a few—three missing, maybe four, gaps so small that no one who hadn't been told to count would ever notice—but the constellations were wrong, subtly, terribly wrong, like a face with the eyes moved a finger's width apart. The sky of his childhood had hung in the cottage window every night of his life. He knew it the way he knew his own hands.

Someone had already begun spending it.

In his lap, the ledger warmed another degree. When he opened it to the last page, a new line was cooling there, written in ash that was still settling, addressed—to his certain, sick knowledge—to no one but him:

*You counted. Good. Now find me before they do. I am the part of the covenant that remembers being a person, and I am so tired, and the one who is drawing against us sits three floors above your head.*

Sebastian looked up, through the floor, through stone and timber and the busy arithmetic of the Ledgerhouse, toward the office of Guildmaster Corvane.

Outside, another star went quietly out.
