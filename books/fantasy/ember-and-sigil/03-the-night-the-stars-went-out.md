## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Sebastian Brightwater was balancing the quarter's accounts on bottled thunder when the first star went out.

He did not see it happen. He was three floors below ground in the Ledgerhouse vaults, in a room with no windows, surrounded by shelves of storm-glass that hummed like struck bells. The ledger open before him recorded a shipment of forty-one thunderstorms consigned from the Stormreach coast—each one captured at sea by weather-wrights who charged by the hour and the danger, each one logged, weighed, appraised. Forty-one entries. Forty-one small blue-white lights glowing in their glass prisons along the wall, casting restless shadows that crawled across the ceiling like things trying to escape.

He liked this work. That was the strange part, the part he never said aloud. Three years ago he had been Maren Thistlewood's apprentice, sleeping in a lean-to that smelled of ash and goat, copying charms onto scraps of birchbark while she muttered about the ingratitude of herbs. Now he had a desk. A lamp that burned oil, not tallow. A chair that did not wobble. He had colleagues who called him *Ledgerman Brightwater* without irony, and a supervisor who had once told him his hand was the neatest in the eastern annex, and he had carried that sentence around for two weeks like a coin too valuable to spend.

Belonging, he had learned, was mostly bookkeeping. You showed up. You made your entries. You balanced what could be balanced, and you wrote *discrepancy* beside what could not, and you came back the next day. No one revoked you if your columns were clean.

He was entering the appraisal for storm thirty-seven—a fat, sullen thing that had already cracked its glass once and been re-bottled at a surcharge—when the humming changed.

All forty-one storms went quiet at once.

Sebastian's pen stopped. In three years he had never heard the vault silent. The storms murmured even in their sleep; it was the sound of the place, the sound he'd come to think of as the building breathing. Now nothing. Forty-one glass cells full of light and pressure and fury, and not one whisper out of any of them.

"Thirty-seven," he said softly, because talking to the inventory was a habit he'd picked up from Maren and never shaken. "What's got into you?"

The light inside thirty-seven's glass dimmed. Not flickered—*dimmed*, the way a candle dims when something drinks the air. Then thirty-six. Then thirty-five, down the shelf, a wave of failing glow rolling toward him through the dark, and Sebastian stood up so fast his chair went over backward, and he thought: *they're afraid.*

Bottled storms did not feel fear. That was the entire point of bottling them. He had written that in an appendix once, in his neat, careful hand: *Subject exhibits no affective response to external stimuli.* He had believed it. He had believed most things, then.

He was halfway up the stairs when the lamps died.

Not the vault lamps—the whole city. He knew it because the stairwell's high slit window, which showed a slice of night sky over the rooftops of Vellum Row, went absolutely black. Not cloudy-black, not smoke-black. Black like ink poured across glass. He pressed his face to the slit and looked up and saw nothing where everything should have been. No stars. No moon. No far lantern-glow of the watchtowers. The sky above Emberfall had been erased.

And beneath the silence of the storms, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, he heard the wards fail.

Everyone in the city heard it, though they would describe it differently all their lives. To some it was tearing silk. To some it was a scream stretched too long to be a scream. To Sebastian, standing alone in a dark stairwell with forty-one terrified storms below him, it sounded exactly like a page being ripped out of a very large book—and he knew, with the cold certainty of a man who has spent years learning what things cost, that somewhere an entry had just been voided. Something enormous had been removed from an account, and the whole world had felt the pen scratch through it.

The vault door banged open behind him. Lamplight—real lamplight, someone running with a hand-lantern from the upper floors.

"Brightwater?" It was Hesk, the night auditor, an old man with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like gravel in a sack. "You down there? Stars are gone. All of them. Guildmaster's ordered the vaults sealed and every clerk to their station."

"The storms," Sebastian said. His own voice sounded far away. "They've gone quiet. All of them at once. That shouldn't be possible."

Hesk paused on the stair. In the lantern light his face did something complicated—fear, yes, but underneath it something else, something that looked horribly like recognition. Like a man hearing a noise he'd been told to expect.

"They'll quiet down," Hesk said. "Storms know better than to make noise tonight." And he kept climbing, taking the lantern with him, leaving Sebastian in the dark with the words *tonight* ringing in his ears like a struck coin.

*Tonight.* As if there had been other nights planned. As if this one were on a schedule.

Sebastian stood in the blackness a long moment. Below him, in the vault, forty-one bottled thunderstorms held their breath. Above him, the sky where the stars had been was a wound that would not close.

He should have gone to his station. Every clerk in the Ledgerhouse was going to their station; that was what belonging meant; you showed up and made your entries. Instead he found himself thinking of a sealed ledger he had catalogued eleven days ago, bound in gray leather gone stiff as bark, its pages written in cooling ash, its entries listing payments made—to whom? to what?—*to keep the sky lit.*

Eleven days ago he had shelved it and told himself it was old business. Dead accounts. Nothing to do with him.

Now the sky was unlit, and the storms were silent, and somewhere overhead a page had been torn out, and Sebastian Brightwater climbed the stairs slowly, feeling for the first time since he'd fled Maren Thistlewood's lean-to the shape of the trap he had walked into with his neat handwriting and his careful columns.

He had found that ledger. He had read it. And he had said nothing.

* * *

Odile Vane was eating cold sausage on the Northgate wall when the stars went out, and her first thought—gods forgive her—was that someone in the kitchens had finally poisoned her.

The darkness hit like a blow. One heartbeat the sky was its ordinary spill of stars, the river of them bright over the Emberlands' distant hills; the next, nothing. Her hand went to her sword before her mind caught up, and she was on her feet with half-chewed sausage still in her mouth when the sound came.

It rolled across the plain from the east like a wave of pressure, a long ripping note that she felt in her teeth and her breastbone and the fillings of her back teeth. Along the wall, forty soldiers flinched as one. Down in the gatehouse, dogs began to howl. Behind her, the city of Emberfall lit up window by window like a body breaking out in hives.

"Wards," said Sergeant Brakka beside her. The old woman's voice was flat, which was how Odile knew she was badly frightened; Brakka only went flat when the fear was too big for anything else. "That was the border wards. All of them. At once."

"That's impossible."

"Yes, Captain."

Odile spat out the sausage and looked east, toward the line of beacon towers that marked the boundary between the human realm and the Emberlands. In peacetime you could see their glow on clear nights, a string of low red embers stitched along the horizon, the covenant made visible: *this far, and no farther, and safe on both sides of it.*

The horizon was black. Not one ember. Not one.

For six hundred years, since the signing of the covenant, the wards had held. Through wars and plagues and the Year of Two Winters, they had held—not because anyone maintained them, particularly, but because they were simply *there*, like gravity, like the color of the sky. Generations of children grew up knowing the border was real the way they knew their own names. And now Odile Vane, captain of the Northgate, holder of a commission signed by the Lord Protector himself, was staring at a horizon with no embers on it, listening to the last vibration of a sound like the world's largest piece of silk being torn in half.

"Light the signal fires," she said. "All of them. And get runners to the garrison—I want every off-duty spear on the wall within the hour."

"Captain—" Brakka hesitated. "Signal protocol says we report to the Ledgerhouse liaison first. Ward-failure is a guild matter. It's in the charter."

"I know what's in the charter, Sergeant. Light the fires."

Brakka went. Odile stayed at the parapet, hands flat on cold stone, and made herself look at the darkness until her eyes watered. She was thirty-four years old and she had served the city since she was fifteen, and in nineteen years she had learned one rule above all others: when the impossible happens, the paperwork has already been filed. Someone, somewhere, knew this was coming. The question—the only question that mattered—was whether they had failed to stop it or declined to.

She didn't have long to wonder.

The refugees started arriving before midnight.

At first it was stragglers—farm families from the near villages, carts piled with whatever could be grabbed, children asleep against their mothers' shoulders because children can sleep through anything. Then the stream thickened. By the second bell after midnight there were hundreds on the north road, and by the third, thousands, a river of humanity flowing toward the gates out of the black countryside, and Odile stood on the wall and gave the order to open the gates, because whatever else was true, she was not going to be the captain who locked people out on the night the stars died.

They came with stories. That was the worst of it. Soldiers could handle wounds and fire and even panic, but the stories got under the skin and stayed.

A drover named Pell, hands still shaking around a cup of the tea Odile's sergeants were passing down the lines: *"It came out of the ground, Captain. Out of the ground like smoke. We always thought the wards were up in the air, but it came up from under, and where it passed the well water tasted of cinders, and Old Marta's cow just lay down and died with its eyes open."*

A woman with a baby and no husband and a voice scraped raw: *"The ember-folk came to our village first. Before it happened. They stood at the fence line and they weren't raiding, they were begging—we couldn't understand their words but we understood the begging—and then the sky went out and they ran back east, and I swear on my mother's grave, Captain, they looked frightened of something behind them more than anything ahead."*

An old man, a hedge-charmer by his faded cloak, who caught Odile's arm with fingers surprisingly strong: *"The stars didn't go out, girl. They were put out. There's a difference. A candle gutters, but a snuffed flame means a hand, and a hand means a reason."* And when she asked what reason, he only looked at her with wet white eyes and said, *"Ask the ones who sell the sky. Ask what they've been buying."*

By dawn the Northgate yard held four thousand souls and the count was climbing. Odile had organized them into rows, registered the heads of households in a borrowed muster-book, set water boiling and latrine trenches dug. Her sergeants moved among the refugees with the grim competence of veterans, and if their eyes kept sliding upward to the starless sky, well, so did everyone's.

It was near morning when the Ledgerhouse liaison finally arrived—an under-clerk named Fess, pale and precise, carrying a leather folio and an expression of profound apology.

"Captain Vane. The guild extends its regards and its assurance that the matter of the ward failure is in hand. Provisional supply contracts for refugee relief are available at standard rates; I'm authorized to negotiate terms on behalf of the—"

"You're authorized," Odile said, "to tell me when the guild first learned the wards might fail."

Fess blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The wards held for six hundred years and failed in one night, and your guildmaster had relief contracts drafted fast enough to bring them to me before sunrise. Paper takes time, Under-clerk. Ink dries. Someone sat at a desk and wrote these terms *before* the sky went dark, or I'm a hedge-witch's grandmother." She stepped closer, and let him see how little sleep and how much anger were in her face. "So. When did the Ledgerhouse know?"

Something flickered behind Fess's careful expression—that same terrible flicker Odile had seen on Hesk's face in a stairwell she would never know about. Recognition. Confirmation. The look of a man whose orders did not cover this question.

"I'm sure I don't know what you mean, Captain. The guild serves the realm in all things." He held out the folio. "Shall we discuss grain prices?"

Odile looked at the folio. She looked at the thousands of people camped in her yard, sleeping in the open under a sky with no stars, and she thought about the old charmer's words: *a snuffed flame means a hand, and a hand means a reason.*

"Not tonight," she said, and turned away, and did not see the under-clerk's gaze follow her, nor the small note he scratched in his own ledger before he left:

*Northgate captain asks wrong questions. Recommend flagging file.*

* * *

Sebastian did not go to his station either.

He told himself he was going. He climbed out of the dark stairwell into chaos—the great hall of the Ledgerhouse ablaze with emergency lamps, clerks running with ledgers clutched to their chests like infants, messengers hammering up the grand stair to the Guildmaster's tower, the great brass doors chained shut against a city gone mad outside. He got as far as the eastern annex. He sat down at his desk. He dipped his pen.

Then he looked at the shelf where the sealed ledger lived.

It was supposed to be in Restricted Holdings, sub-vault C, under triple seal. Eleven days ago, cataloguing a bequest of dead clients' effects, he had found it misfiled among ordinary estate papers—deliberately misfiled, he understood now, buried in the mundane the way a knife is buried in a haystack. He had read three pages before the ash-script's chill drove him back, and he had reshelved it and told himself the story every clerk tells: *not my account, not my problem, someone above my pay grade owns this truth.*

The stars were gone now. The wards were torn. Refugees were flooding the Northgate, and somewhere in this building, in some office with a good lamp and a comfortable chair, someone had known.

Sebastian stood up. His legs carried him past his desk, past the neat stacks of his immaculate files, down the corridor to sub-vault C, where his own countersignature—his own name, the name Maren Thistlewood had given him, the only inheritance he'd ever trusted—opened the outer seal.

The inner seal was already broken.

He stood in the doorway a long time, lamp raised, looking at the empty cradle of velvet where the ash-ledger had lain, at the fine gray dust on the velvet, at the single drop of wax on the floor that was still, impossibly, warm.

Someone had taken it tonight. Within hours of the stars going out. Which meant whoever took it had known exactly where it was—which meant they had known who had found it and misfiled it back—or rather, who had found it and *reported finding nothing at all.*

His name was on the access log. His countersignature, in his own careful hand, dated eleven days ago.

Sebastian Brightwater closed the vault door gently, the way you close a door on a room where something has died, and went back to his desk, and sat down, and made no entries at all.

Outside, under a sky scrubbed of every star, the city of Emberfall burned ten thousand candles against the dark, and none of them helped, and all of them knew it.
