## 4. What the Guild Knew

Sebastian did not sleep. He had told himself he would—one hour, perhaps two, curled in the narrow cot above the counting room with the ash ledger locked beneath his floorboard—but sleep refused him the way a creditor refuses mercy. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the entries again: *paid to keep the sky lit*, written in cooling ash that smudged when he breathed too near, as though the words themselves were still burning down to nothing.

By the fourth bell of morning he gave up pretending and dressed. The Ledgerhouse woke early; it always had. He could hear the clerks below shuffling their ledgers, the soft percussion of quills, the clink of the assay scales weighing out impossible things—three drops of a river's memory, half an hour borrowed from a dying man's last summer, a jar of silence harvested from a cathedral before its bells were cast. Ordinary commerce. Honest theft, at least, the kind the guild admitted to.

The ash ledger was not honest anything.

He carried it up through the house's spine, past the Hall of Instruments where bottled storms murmured in their glass like sleeping wasps, past the Registry of Names where unearned titles waited in drawers for people worthy of them. The ledger sat against his chest inside his coat, warm as a coal that would not die, and with every stair he climbed he felt it grow heavier—not in weight but in meaning, the way a secret does.

Guildmaster Corvane received petitioners in the Map Room, a chamber Sebastian had entered only twice in four years. It was a long room lit by a single skylight, and every wall was covered in maps so old their ink had gone the color of dried blood. Not maps of the city or the realm—maps of the border. Dozens of them. Centuries of them. The line between the human lands and the Emberlands drawn and redrawn and drawn again, each version annotated in a different hand, some in ink, some in something darker.

Corvane stood at the far wall with his back to the door, a thin man made thinner by his robes, gray as paper, gray as ash. He did not turn around.

"You've brought it," he said.

Sebastian's mouth went dry. "Guildmaster. I came to report an irregularity in the deep archives. A ledger without a catalog entry. Unregistered holdings are a violation of—"

"I know what's in it, Brightwater." Corvane turned then, and his face was exactly as it always was: patient, mild, the face of a man who had spent forty years listening to other people panic and found it mildly restful. "You may as well put it on the table."

Sebastian set the ledger down on the great oak table that dominated the room, and immediately regretted how loud the sound was. Like a gavel. Like a verdict.

"Where did you find it?" Corvane asked.

"The sealed stacks. Sub-level three. Behind the fireproof cabinets."

"Mm." Corvane crossed the room and looked down at the ledger without touching it, the way one looks at a snake one has known for years. "And you read it."

"Some of it. The entries are—" Sebastian stopped, steadied himself. He was a bookkeeper; precision was his armor. "The entries are written in ash. Cooling ash. They record payments, disbursements, transfers of... I don't know what the unit of account is. Power, I think. And they're all marked *to keep the sky lit*. Except the earliest ones. The earliest ones say something else."

"Say it."

"*To hold the covenant.*" Sebastian's voice cracked slightly on the second word, and he hated himself for it. "Guildmaster, what covenant?"

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. Then he drew off his rings—one, two, three, setting them on the table beside the ledger with small precise clicks—and laid his bare hand flat upon the cover.

The ash stirred.

It rose from between the pages like breath from a dying fire, curling upward, and where it settled across the table it formed words. Sebastian read them upside down and felt the floor tilt beneath his certainties:

*Let there be a border, and let the border be kept. Let the sky of the small realm be lit by the fire of the great, and let the fire of the great be fed by the faith of the small. So long as both keep faith, both keep light.*

"That is the first page," Corvane said quietly. "Written before this guild had a name. Before the city had walls. We were founded to administer it, Brightwater. Do you understand? The Ledgerhouse is not a merchant concern that happens to deal in wonders. It is the *accounting office of the covenant*. Bottled storms, borrowed years—all of it, all of our trade, grew up around the original business like ivy around a pillar. We trade in impossible things because the first impossible thing was entrusted to us."

Sebastian stared at the ash-words until they faded. His mind, faithful old servant, was already doing what it always did: sorting, filing, reconciling. "Then the stars going dark—"

"Is a default." Corvane said it without drama, without grief, the way he announced a bad quarter. "The Emberlands have stopped paying into the sky. Or we have stopped paying into the fire. Or someone has been skimming from both sides for a very long time, and the account has finally run dry." He looked up, and for the first time Sebastian saw something in the guildmaster's face that frightened him more than anger would have. It was uncertainty. "I do not know which. That is the truth, boy, and I will thank you to remember I gave it to you plainly."

"Someone's been skimming." Sebastian heard his own voice as if from a distance. "You said that like it's a hypothesis. You've known. How long have you known?"

"Three years." Corvane began putting his rings back on, one finger at a time. "There is a discrepancy in the covenant accounts. Small. Fractional. Less than a tenth of one percent per annum. But compounding." He fastened the last ring and flexed his hand. "A tenth of a percent of a covenant, siphoned year over year for decades, is enough to gutter a sky. Someone inside this house has been bleeding the border, drop by drop, and laundering the loss through legitimate trades so cleanly that even I could not find the seam. Until now I assumed they were patient. Now I know they were simply waiting for the balance to fail."

"And you didn't tell anyone?" The betrayal arrived all at once, hot and total, and Sebastian heard it rise in his voice like floodwater. "The wards failed, Guildmaster. People died at the gates. There are refugees in the mud outside our walls who watched their farms burn because the border came apart, and you sat in this room for *three years* knowing the accounts were rotten and said nothing?"

"To whom?" For the first time Corvane raised his voice, and the maps on the walls seemed to lean in. "To the Council of Wardens, who lease their authority from us? To the Crown, whose treasury we quietly own? Tell me, Brightwater—who do you warn, when the only institution capable of understanding the crime is the institution committing it?" He exhaled slowly, and the mildness returned, worse than the shouting. "I told no one because telling no one was the only way to keep the search quiet. If the thief knew I was looking, they would have burned the ledgers. All of them. Every record of the covenant, everywhere, and then no one could ever repair it. My silence was stewardship."

"Your silence was arithmetic."

"Yes," Corvane agreed, without shame. "It usually is."

Sebastian looked down at the ledger, at the faint ghost of the ash-words still clinging to the table, and felt something shift in his chest—a hairline crack running through the four years of careful, grateful belonging. He had fled Maren Thistlewood's crooked little cottage believing the world was divided into people who used you and people who sheltered you, and he had chosen the Ledgerhouse because it seemed to be made entirely of the second kind. Ledgers did not lie. Numbers did not flatter. He had built a life on the belief that here, at least, everything was written down honestly.

And the founding document of the whole edifice was written in ash that vanished when you touched it.

"Why show me this?" he asked. "Why not just take the ledger back and lock me out of sub-level three?"

"Because you found it when forty senior auditors did not." Corvane studied him with those pale, weighing eyes. "Because you have a talent for seeing what accounts want to hide, and I am running out of people I can trust to look. And because—" he paused, and chose the next words with visible care, "—because the covenant cannot be repaired by bookkeeping alone. It requires a sigil. A living one. And living sigils are rare enough that when one turns up in my employ, I make certain to keep him close."

The room went very cold, or Sebastian did.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Don't you?" Corvane smiled, and it was almost kind. "Your master wrote to me when she sent you south. Maren Thistlewood. She asked me to give you honest work and never ask what you were. I have honored that request for four years. Perhaps honor it a while longer by not making me explain things you already feel in your blood."

*In your blood.* Sebastian's hand moved before he decided to move it—up to his left forearm, where the mark sat beneath his sleeve, the birthmark shaped like nothing and everything, the one Maren had traced with her thumb on cold mornings and called *just a stain, love, just a stain.* He had shown it to no one in the city. No one.

He hadn't needed to.

"I'll ask you one thing," Sebastian said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Who is stealing from the covenant?"

"If I knew, I would already be standing over their corpse arranging the paperwork." Corvane gathered the ledger from the table and held it against his chest, and for a moment, just a moment, he looked less like a guildmaster and more like a man carrying something heavy uphill in the dark. "Find me the seam, Brightwater. Reconcile the covenant accounts. You have my authority to open any holding, any vault, any record in this house. Consider it your true apprenticeship—the one you were always meant for."

"And if I find the seam and it runs through *you*?"

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—amusement, perhaps, or respect, or the particular pleasure of a chess player watching a pawn promote itself. "Then I will expect you to do your duty," he said, "and write it down."

Sebastian bowed, because four years of habit is a strong rope, and walked to the door. His hand was on the latch when Corvane spoke again, softly, to the maps rather than to him.

"Brightwater. The Tallyman will see you home."

The Tallyman. Corvane's enforcer, who wore no face that anyone could describe afterward, who collected debts for the guild in the outer provinces and was spoken of only in the passive voice. Sebastian turned. "I know the way home."

"I'm sure you do." Corvane was already turning back to his wall of borders, his gray robe blending into the gray parchment, a man dissolving into his own records. "But the streets are unsettled since the stars went out. Refugees. Panic. It would ease my mind considerably to know you arrived safely."

It took Sebastian the length of the corridor, all three flights down, past the murmuring storms and the sleeping names, to understand what had just happened. He stood in the counting room among the honest ledgers and the dishonest daylight and understood it fully: he had not been summoned. He had been *audited*. And the finding was entered somewhere he would never see, in a book he would never read, under a heading he could not have imagined a week ago—

*Watched: asset, uncertain provenance, potentially irreplaceable.*

Outside the window, the sky hung starless and wrong, and somewhere beyond the city walls the refugees were still coming, and somewhere in the house a faceless man was pulling on his coat to walk a hedge-witch's runaway home through the crowd.

Sebastian pressed his palm flat against his forearm, over the mark, and felt—as he had never allowed himself to feel it before—a faint warmth there, like a coal refusing to go out.

*Living sigils are rare,* Corvane had said. *When one turns up in my employ, I keep him close.*

Close, Sebastian thought, was another word for caged.

That night, for the first time in four years, he checked the window latch twice, and slept with his boots on.
