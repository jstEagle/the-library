## 5. The Apprentice Who Ran Twice

The night Sebastian ran from Maren Thistlewood for the second time, it was raining the way rain falls in the Marches—sideways, with intent, as if the sky itself had a grudge against travelers.

He had packed badly. That was the thing he remembered most clearly afterward, in the long years of ledgers and lamplight: not her face when she caught him at the gate, not the sigil-knife glinting on her belt, but the sheer incompetence of his packing. Three shirts rolled into a knot. A half-loaf of bread he'd forgotten to wrap. His mother's ring—the only thing he had of her, a plain band of gray metal that never warmed no matter how long he wore it—shoved loose into his pocket where it clinked against a tin of salve like a small, accusing bell.

He was seventeen, and he had been planning this flight for four months, and he had packed like a child.

* * *

Maren Thistlewood's cottage sat at the crook of the Drownwater road where the marsh began its slow argument with the hills. It was built of fieldstone and turf and one wall of salvaged ship-timber that Maren swore came from a vessel that had sailed off the edge of an old map. Chickens muttered under the eaves. A row of drying herbs turned the kitchen air green and bitter. Everything about the place was small, stubborn, and precisely maintained—which described Maren too.

She had taken him in when he was nine, delivered by a carter who claimed to have found him asleep in a hay wagon outside Corrow Ford with no name but the one stitched into his collar: *Sebastian*. No surname. No letter. No coin. Just the name, embroidered in thread so fine the carter said it must have cost more than the boy did.

For eight years she had taught him what hedge-witches teach: which mushrooms lie, how to bargain with river-spirits without blinking first, how to mend a fever with willowbark and stubbornness. And other things she taught without teaching—how to move quietly, how to notice when a stranger's shadow fell wrong, how to read a room the way you'd read weather. He had thought those were just the habits of an old woman who lived alone in a dangerous country.

He was packing his bag by candlelight when he heard the floorboard sigh behind him, and knew she'd been awake the whole time.

"You'll want the oilcloth," Maren said from the doorway, "if you're going to cross the Drownwater in this."

Sebastian didn't turn around. His ears burned. "I wasn't—I'm going to bed."

"With your boots on and your bag packed." She crossed the room, took the knotted shirts out of his hands, and began repacking them with brisk, wordless efficiency, the way she did everything. Oilcloth over the bread. The salve nested to keep the tin from rattling. She held up the ring between two fingers, looked at it a moment longer than she needed to, and put it in the inner pocket, the one that buttoned.

"I could have done that," he said.

"You could have done it worse," she agreed. "Sit down, boy. If you're going to run away properly, we're going to do it properly."

That was the other thing he remembered: she hadn't tried to stop him. Not once, in all the months of his secret scheming—not when he'd asked careful questions about the road south, not when he'd begun hoarding coins from the herb-money, not even now, standing in her kitchen while the rain hammered the turf roof like a fist demanding entry. She had known. She had always known. And she had let him get ready.

"Why?" he asked. It came out smaller than he meant. "Why won't you stop me?"

Maren set the bag down by the door and stood looking at it, her back to him. She was not old, exactly—hedge-witches age strangely, worn smooth rather than worn out—but there were nights when the weight of her showed, and this was one of them. Her shoulders had the look of a woman carrying something heavy down a very long stair.

"Because I promised someone I wouldn't keep you past seventeen," she said. "And because keeping you would be the cruelest thing I could do, though I've been tempted every day for eight years."

"Tempted to what?"

"To tell you." She turned then, and her eyes in the candlelight were the color of bog-iron. "Sit, Sebastian. There are things you should have before you go, and things I should say, and if I don't say them tonight I never will, because I am a coward in exactly one particular and it is this one."

He sat. The bench creaked. Outside, the chickens had gone silent, which they only did when something walked the yard—and nothing ever walked their yard but Maren herself.

* * *

She gave him three things.

The first was money—more than he'd saved, folded into an oilskin pouch. "There's a man at the Ledgerhouse door called Ferren, a porter with a bad knee. Tell him Thistlewood sent you and he'll walk you through the intake. The guild takes people like you; it's the one honest thing about them. They don't ask where you came from. They ask what you can count." Her mouth twisted. "You can count better than anyone I've met, and I include myself."

"The Ledgerhouse." He'd heard of it the way children hear of the sea—vast, glittering, impossibly far away. "You want me to join a *guild*?"

"I want you somewhere with walls and rules and ten thousand witnesses," Maren said, "because there are fewer places in this world harder to make a person disappear than inside a ledger. Every hour of your life accounted for, initialed, filed. You'll hate it. It will also keep you alive."

"Alive from *what*?" His voice cracked on the last word, seventeen years old and furious with the shapelessness of it. "You keep saying things like that. *Keep you safe. Keep you hidden.* Hidden from who? What did I do? Whose son am I? Why is there a name in my collar and none anywhere else?"

Maren took a slow breath, and he watched her decide—watched the truth rise in her like water in a well and be pushed back down, bucket by bucket, by whatever promise she'd made.

"That's the second thing," she said instead, and drew the sigil-knife from her belt.

He had seen it a thousand times. It hung at her hip the way a wedding ring sits on a hand—so constant it became invisible. A short blade, dark metal, the handle wrapped in leather gone black with age, and along the flat of the blade a line of characters that were not letters in any alphabet he knew. They seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them. He had assumed, all these years, that it was a witch's tool, a spirit-cutter, a thing for her trade.

She wrapped his hand around the handle. The metal was warm. It was always warm; he'd forgotten that, living beside it. Warm as skin.

"When you get where you're going," she said, "you do not show this to anyone. You do not let anyone see the marks. You carry it the way you carry your own heartbeat—inside, unremarked, or you will die for it. Do you understand me? Not might. *Will.*"

"Maren—"

"*Do you understand?*"

"Yes," he whispered.

She nodded once, satisfied, and released his hand. The knife felt heavier than any tool had a right to feel. It felt, he thought wildly, like being handed a debt.

"And the third thing?" he asked, because his mouth had always outrun his sense.

Maren looked at him for a long time. Rain. Candleflame. The green smell of the herbs and the iron smell of the knife. Somewhere in the marsh a bittern boomed, once, like a giant clearing its throat underground.

"The third thing," she said, "is that you were never my apprentice."

The words landed wrong, sideways, and for a moment he simply didn't understand them, the way you don't understand a blow until the bruise comes up.

"What?"

"You were never my apprentice. Apprentices are chosen for talent. I chose nothing." She reached out and, for the first time in eight years, touched his face—her thumb rough against his cheekbone, brief as a struck match. "I was given a charge. A child to hide and raise and keep breathing until it was time for him to run. Your whole life here—the lessons, the chores, the moving the chickens twice when the strangers came asking—none of it was training, Sebastian. It was camouflage. I painted you into the background of my small life so thoroughly that even you believed you belonged there."

The kitchen tilted. He gripped the bench. "Believed—"

"You belong everywhere," she said fiercely, and her voice broke on it, this woman who never broke, "and that is precisely the problem, and one day you'll understand why, and I pray to every god I've ever cheated that I'm dead before you do, because if I'm alive when you understand, you'll come back here and ask me why I lied to you for eight years, and I don't—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them she was Maren again, flint and turf and stubbornness. "Go out the back. Follow the drainage cut to the ford; the road's watched. Ferren at the Ledgerhouse. Don't write to me. Don't come back. And Sebastian—"

He was already at the door, the knife against his ribs, the rain waiting. He looked back.

"When you find out what you are," Maren Thistlewood said, "remember that I loved you before I knew what you were. That part was never a lie. It was the only part that wasn't."

He ran. Of course he ran. He was seventeen and furious and frightened, and the road south was black and streaming, and behind him the cottage light shrank to a coal and went out.

It was eleven years before he understood what she had meant. Eleven years of counting impossible things in the vaults of the Ledgerhouse before he opened a sealed ledger written in cooling ash and learned what it costs to keep a sky lit—and what, and who, it had been purchased with.

But that night, running through the rain with a warm knife against his heart and a name in his collar and no other name anywhere in the world, Sebastian Brightwater—who was not yet Brightwater, who was still just *Sebastian*, the boy with no surname—believed only this:

That he had finally escaped the person who had been hiding him.

He had no idea he was running straight into the arms of the people who had arranged the hiding in the first place.

* * *

The Drownwater ford nearly killed him, which he supposed was traditional. The water came up to his chest and pulled like a living thing, and he lost the bread, and he laughed—actually laughed, alone in the flood in the dark—because losing the bread felt like the last straw of the old life, the small life, the camouflage life, washing away downstream toward the sea.

On the far bank he wrung out his shirts and walked south through the gray beginning of morning, and the sigil-knife rode warm against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and he did not look back.

He told himself he was running toward something. Freedom, the city, a future of his own choosing. And in a way it was true. But the truth underneath the truth—the one that took eleven years and a book bound in ash to surface—was simpler and colder:

Maren had pointed him at the Ledgerhouse the way a hunter points a dog. Someone, somewhere, had decided that the safest place in the world for Sebastian-whatever-he-was was behind the guild's walls, among its ten thousand witnesses and its meticulous, initialed, filed hours. His escape had been scheduled. His refuge had been assigned. Even his rebellion, the one act he'd believed was entirely his own, had been anticipated, approved, and provisioned with oilcloth and coin.

He had not run away twice.

He had been released twice.

And the question that would wake him, years later, in his narrow bed above the counting-room, sweating in the dark with the stars going out beyond the window, was the one Maren had refused to answer with her well full of truth and her bucket in her hands:

*Hidden from whom—and who had paid her to do the hiding?*

He would learn the answer soon enough. It was already walking toward him through the ruined borderlands, wearing a queen's grief like a borrowed cloak, and it already knew his name—even the part of it that had never been stitched into anyone's collar.
