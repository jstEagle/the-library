## 6. An Exile at the Gate

Sarelle had worn a hundred faces in her long exile, but never one so small as this.

The refugee's body she had borrowed—a dead girl from the Gray Country, starved and gray-lipped—was a poor fit for a queen. The girl had been nineteen, perhaps twenty, with hands that had known only threshing work and a spine bent early by carrying water. Sarelle kept the girl's name close, as courtesy demanded: Wren. Wren of the ash-fields. Wren, who had died three days ago with her family around her and no priest to sing her across.

*I will do better with your face than you were allowed to do with it,* Sarelle promised her silently, hunching her shoulders into the girl's stoop and letting her gait go uneven, one hip hitching on every third step. *I will make your death mean something.*

Around her, the road to Vael Corren—the human city whose true name was not written on any map, only traded in whispers through the Ledgerhouse—seethed with the displaced. They came in families and fragments of families, in carts pulled by oxen too thin to complain, on feet wrapped in rags because boots had been sold or eaten or burned for warmth. They came from the border villages where the wards had failed, from farms where the soil had gone strange and cold overnight, from places where the stars had simply stopped being there when people looked up.

They all looked up. That was the thing that struck her, walking among them. Every few minutes, some new arrival would tilt their head back and search the blank sky with naked, animal bewilderment, as if the stars might be embarrassed at their absence and return if only someone caught them out. Children asked questions their parents could not answer. An old man near the front of the line wept without sound, his face turned upward, tears cutting clean tracks through the dust.

*Good,* Sarelle thought, and hated herself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway. *Let them look up. Let them feel what my people have felt for two hundred years under a sky that gives nothing back.*

The queue for the city gates stretched half a mile through the mud. Above the walls, the human wards glowed their usual sullen amber—dimmer than they should have been, Sarelle noted, dimmer than they'd been even a decade ago, when she had last come creeping to this city in another stolen shape. The great sigil-stones along the walltop pulsed like tired hearts. Whatever had broken on the night the stars went out, it had not finished breaking. It was bleeding out slowly, and everyone on this road could feel it the way animals feel weather.

She had crossed four hundred miles of dead country to be here. She had eaten things she did not wish to remember and burned memories as fuel—small ones first, the taste of her mother's honey-cakes, then larger ones, the sound of her sister laughing—and now she stood in a mud-splattered line of dying farmers wearing a dead girl's face, hunting a single man.

Not a man, precisely. A sigil. A living covenant, walking around in a clerk's body, filing papers in the guild that had helped break her people.

*Sebastian Brightwater.* She turned the name over the way she'd once turned coins of pure ember-gold, feeling its weight, testing whether it rang true. Maren Thistlewood's letters had been maddeningly spare—the hedge-witch wrote as though paper cost blood—but she had given Sarelle enough. The boy who ran. The boy who catalogued impossible things and wanted desperately, pathetically, to belong. The boy who did not know what he was.

The gate loomed closer. Human soldiers worked the line, checking papers, turning away those without coin or craft. Sarelle had prepared for this: Wren had been a dyer's daughter, and dye-work was scarce enough in a war-frightened city to be waved through. She had rehearsed the girl's flat border accent until it lived comfortably in her borrowed throat.

What she had not prepared for was the smell.

It hit her fifty yards from the gate—a thread of scent beneath the mud and smoke and unwashed humanity, so faint she almost dismissed it, so impossible she nearly laughed aloud. Ozone and hot iron. Cinnamon and struck flint. The smell of a forge where the metal being worked was not iron but daylight itself.

Sigil-blood. Fresh. Close.

Her heart—if this stolen body's heart could be called hers—began to hammer. She scanned the crowd with eyes that were not her own, and there, thirty feet ahead, standing apart from the refugees as though he weren't sure he had permission to stand anywhere: a young man in a Ledgerhouse clerk's coat, holding a ledger against his chest like a shield, directing the gate-captain's soldiers with anxious little gestures. He was pale, dark-haired, unremarkable in every way except one.

He was glowing.

Not visibly. No human eye would have seen it. But Sarelle had spent two centuries learning to read the light of living things, and Sebastian Brightwater burned like a coal wrapped in wet cloth—damped down, hidden, smothered deliberately by warding charms stitched into the lining of that sensible gray coat. Someone had tried very hard to make him invisible.

Someone had failed.

*There you are,* she thought, and felt the old hunger rise in her, the queen's hunger, the exile's hunger, the hunger of a woman who had traded everything for a chance to undo one mistake. *There you are, little page. Little unfinished sentence.*

And beneath the hunger, quieter and more dangerous, something else. Because she watched him work—watched him check a farmer's tally-mark, watched him argue gently with a soldier about a family split between lines, watched him give up his own place in some invisible hierarchy of clerks so a widow with twins could pass first—and she saw it, plain as fire.

He wanted to matter. He was starving for it. This boy who carried a covenant in his veins had been fed scraps of purpose his whole life, and he was so hungry he didn't know he was hungry, and he would follow anyone who made him feel real.

*Just as I once followed,* she thought. *Just as I once burned.*

She knew exactly how to ruin him. That was the terrible part. She knew it the way she knew her own name—which is to say, imperfectly, painfully, and with increasing difficulty—for exile ate names too, hers most of all. Two hundred years of trading pieces of herself to survive, and Sarelle sometimes woke in strange bodies wondering which parts of her remained, and whether the woman who had broken the covenant would recognize the creature who meant to mend it.

She joined the line moving toward the clerk's station and let Wren's stoop settle deeper into her spine.

* * *

"Name?" His voice was hoarse. He'd been shouting over the crowd for hours; she could hear the weariness fraying it.

"Wren," she said, in the girl's flat border vowels. "Wren Ashfell. Dyer, out of Marrow's Bend."

He wrote it down. She watched his hand—ink-stained, precise, each letter formed with the care of a man who believed records were sacred—and felt a flicker of something uncomfortably like tenderness. *He still believes in ledgers,* she thought. *Poor lamb. He doesn't yet know what's written in them.*

"Purpose in the city?"

"Work. My family's—" she let the pause crack, just slightly, "—my family didn't make it across the Gray Country."

His pen stopped. He looked up at her, and his eyes were brown and ordinary and full of a grief he clearly hadn't been given permission to feel, and Sarelle understood in that moment that ruining him would be the easiest thing she had ever done.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it. He glanced past her at the line, then lowered his voice. "There's a relief house on Candlemaker's Row, third door past the shrine. Tell them Brightwater sent you—they'll feed you before they ask questions." A ghost of a smile. "I'll note it in the ledger. That usually helps."

*Brightwater.* He gave his name away like alms. Like it cost him nothing. Like it wasn't the single most valuable object in either realm.

"Thank you," she said softly. And then, because the hunger was patient but not infinitely patient, and because she had crossed four hundred miles and burned her mother's honey-cakes for this: "You've a good heart, master clerk. But you look like a man carrying something heavy. Heavier than ink."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Wariness, quick as a fish turning. "Just a long day," he said.

"Mm." She leaned fractionally closer, and let a whisper of her true self bleed through Wren's voice—not enough to reveal, just enough to resonate, the way one struck bell answers another. "Longer than you know, I think."

His hand went still on the ledger. Slowly, he looked at her again, really looked, and she watched him see it—the way one sees a word one has read a thousand times suddenly appear misspelled. The wrongness of her. The heat underneath the gray.

"You're not—" he began, and stopped himself, glancing at the soldiers. When he spoke again it was barely breath. "What are you?"

"A refugee," Sarelle said, with Wren's flat honesty. "Same as you, in the end. We're all fleeing something." She held his gaze and let the ember show, just a coal's worth, just enough. "But I think you and I are fleeing the same thing, master clerk. I think we're both running from what we are."

The color left his face in stages, like a tide going out. His hand drifted toward the ledger—not writing now, hiding, an instinctive curl of the body around the record. Around the truth. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed gently. "That's rather the tragedy, isn't it? You don't. And the people who do know aren't telling you." She straightened, letting Wren's stoop fall away for one unguarded instant, and for that instant she let him see her—not the queen, not yet, but the exile, the survivor, the woman who had also been lied to by everyone she trusted. "Candlemaker's Row. Third door past the shrine. Come find me when the questions you can't ask get loud enough. I promise you answers, Sebastian Brightwater. Real ones. The kind they don't keep in vaults."

"How do you know my—"

"The gate's backing up," said a soldier, bored and brutal, and prodded her forward with a spear-butt.

She went. At the shadow of the gatehouse arch she paused, one hand on the ancient stone—and felt it, faint and far below, the great slow pulse of the Borderstone itself, wounded and weakening, calling out across the miles to anything that could hear. Calling, perhaps, to the boy behind her.

She did not look back. Looking back was for people with nothing ahead of them.

* * *

The relief house on Candlemaker's Row smelled of barley soup and wet wool, and Sarelle sat in its corner with a bowl she did not eat, watching the door.

Wren's body needed food; she forced down enough to keep the disguise stable. But most of her attention was elsewhere—inward, into the quiet vault where she kept the last of her true self, taking inventory. She had perhaps six strong workings left before she burned through what remained of herself. One to reach the Borderstone country alive. One to shield them both when the guild's hunters came—and they would come; Corvane was many things, but careless about assets was not one of them. One, if it came to it, to die properly, in her own shape, facing whatever needed facing.

And one—the last one, the one she had saved for two hundred years—to open the Borderstone's lock, if the boy's blood proved insufficient.

If it proved sufficient, she would need nothing at all. Just him. Just his name, freely given, written in the old way.

*Freely given.* She turned that phrase over, and it cut her. Nothing about what she intended was free. She was going to take a lonely, grieving, desperate young man and offer him exactly the thing he could not refuse—purpose, belonging, the chance to matter—and she was going to call it choice. She had done it before. That was how the covenant broke the first time: not with armies, but with an idealistic queen and a desperate people and words like *freely* spoken by mouths that had no other option.

*This time will be different,* she told herself. *This time I tell him the truth.*

*Most of it,* whispered the older, colder voice. *Enough of it. Truth is a currency too, and you have so little left to spend.*

The door opened at dusk, letting in a knife of cold air and the noise of the street. Sarelle did not look up immediately. She had learned patience in exile the way other people learn languages—slowly, painfully, completely.

But she knew it was him before she raised her eyes. The soup steam bent slightly toward the door, the way flame leans toward a draft. Toward heat. Toward him.

Sebastian Brightwater stood in the doorway of the relief house, hat in his hands, looking like a man who had argued himself into and out of a decision eleven times on the walk over. His gaze swept the room, found her, and stuck.

She smiled Wren's small, sad smile, and gestured to the bench across from her.

He came. Of course he came. He was starving, and she had set out bread.

"You said answers," he said, sitting, keeping the bench's edge between them like a border of his own. His voice shook only a little. "About what I am. About—" he glanced around, lowered his voice, "—about the ledgers. About why the stars went out."

"I did."

"And who are you? Truly?"

Sarelle set down the spoon. Beneath the table, out of sight, she let her hand shift—just for a heartbeat—from Wren's chapped farm-girl fingers to something older, something crowned in ember-light, and then back again. His breath caught. That was enough. That was the hook set.

"My name," she said, "is a thing I sold a long time ago, and I'm not sure I want it back yet. But my people call me the Queen of Ash, and my enemies call me worse, and the truth—" she leaned forward, and let him see, finally, fully, the fire banked two centuries deep behind a dead girl's eyes, "—the truth is that I broke the world, Sebastian Brightwater. And I have walked four hundred miles through the corpse of my own mistake to find the only creature in existence capable of mending it."

The relief house clattered on around them—bowls, voices, a baby crying—but between the two of them the silence went absolute, taut as a wire.

"So," said the ember-queen in exile, in a dead girl's voice, offering the whole burning truth like a merchant offers terms, "shall we talk trade?"
