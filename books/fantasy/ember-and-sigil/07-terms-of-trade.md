## 7. Terms of Trade

Sebastian did not sleep. He sat in the narrow room the Ledgerhouse kept for junior clerks—a cot, a desk, a window that looked out on the guild's inner courtyard and nothing else—and he watched the sky where stars should have been.

It was not merely dark. Darkness had a texture; he knew it from cellars and storm-shuttered nights. This was different. This was absence with intention behind it, a black so complete it seemed less like something missing than something removed. He kept thinking of the ash ledger, of entries written in cooling cinders: *paid to keep the sky lit*. As if the light overhead had always been a service rendered. As if someone had stopped paying.

His hands would not stay still. He turned his quill over and over, then set it down, then picked it up again. In the end he did what he always did when the world grew too large: he made a list.

*What I know:*

*The covenant between realms is real. It is written down. The Ledgerhouse holds the record.*

*Someone has been drawing against it for years—siphoning, Corvane said, though he said it the way a man mentions weather.*

*The stars went out the same night the wards failed. Those two facts are one fact.*

*I am involved. I do not know how. I am afraid to know how.*

He looked at the last line for a long time. Then, because honesty was the only discipline he had never broken, he added:

*I am afraid to know how much I want to be involved.*

A knock at the door—soft, three beats, no urgency. Guild messengers knocked like debt collectors. Servants knocked like they were apologizing for existing. This knock belonged to neither.

Sebastian opened the door on a woman he did not know: gray-cloaked, travel-worn, her hood pushed back to reveal dark hair shot through with strands that caught the lamplight like cooling embers. Her eyes were amber, but not the flat amber of honey or resin. They moved. Something lived behind them at a depth that made his skin prickle.

"You're the clerk," she said. Not a question. "The one who found the ash ledger."

"I'm many clerks," Sebastian said. "We're all interchangeable by design."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "May I come in? Or would you rather conduct this in the courtyard, where your masters can watch?"

That decided him, though he couldn't have said why. He stepped aside.

She moved through the little room the way water moves through a channel—taking its shape without being shaped by it. She looked at his list, still lying on the desk, and he snatched it up too late. She didn't gloat. She simply nodded as if he'd confirmed something she'd already entered into her own accounts.

"My name is Sarelle," she said. "I came through the north gate two nights ago, with the third wave of refugees. I burned my papers myself before crossing—I don't enter places carrying proof of who I am." She folded her hands. "You're wondering what I am."

"The thought occurred to me."

"I'm from the Emberlands. From the court that used to be, before the exile. And before you reach for the alarm cord, consider: if I meant you harm, I'd hardly announce my origin to a guild clerk. Your people have been killing mine on sight since the wards fell. They call us *ashborn* now. It's shorter than *demon*, apparently."

"Is it true?" Sebastian asked. "What they're saying at the gates. That the Emberlands are marching."

"Parts of it. Armies move when borders break—that's not malice, that's gravity." She leaned forward, and the lamplight did something strange in her eyes, a slow churn like banked fire deciding whether to become flame. "But I didn't come to talk about armies. I came to talk about ledgers. Yours specifically."

"I don't keep personal accounts."

"Everyone keeps personal accounts. Most people just refuse to audit them." She reached into her cloak and produced something wrapped in oilcloth, setting it on the desk between them with the care of a surgeon laying out an instrument. "Unwrap it."

He hesitated. Then, because curiosity had always been the loudest voice in him, he unwrapped it.

Inside was a shard of stone no bigger than his palm, black shot through with veins of dull red. The moment his fingers touched it, every hair on his arms rose, and somewhere behind his sternum something *answered*—a resonance, low and vast, like striking a bell buried under a mountain. The shard hummed against his skin. His name rose unbidden to his lips, and he bit down on it before it escaped.

Sarelle watched him with an expression he could not read—hunger and pity fighting for the same ground.

"That is a fragment of the Borderstone," she said quietly. "The anchor of the covenant. It has been dead for a hundred years. It has been dead for everyone." She paused. "Except you."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low.

She told him things in the careful order of someone who had rehearsed this conversation across long miles: that the covenant was older than either realm's oldest kingdom; that it was not a wall but a *wound closed with stitching*, two worlds sewn together along a seam of shared magic; that the Ledgerhouse had been founded, in its first incarnation, as the covenant's custodian—the keeper of terms, the collector of what each side owed the other.

"And then?" Sebastian asked.

"And then keeping became owning. Custody became inventory. Somewhere in the last century, the guild learned it could skim the covenant—draw small amounts of border-magic off the top, bottle it, sell it. Borrowed years. Bottled storms. Names not yet earned." Her voice hardened. "Your vaults are full of our inheritance, clerk. Sold back to us at interest."

"That's—" He started to defend the guild and found he had nothing to defend it *with*. He thought of the ash ledger. *Paid to keep the sky lit.* Paid to whom? By whom? And where, in all those meticulous columns, was the entry for what was taken?

"You've seen the records," Sarelle said. "I can tell. You have the look of a man who found a number that doesn't add up and hasn't slept since."

"The ledger was sealed. Written in ash. I don't even know if what I read was real."

"It was real. It's the covenant's own accounting—it writes itself when the terms are honored, and it writes in ash when they're broken. Ash is what remains when something burns that should not have." She drew a breath. "Here are my terms, Sebastian Brightwater. I will give you the truth of the covenant—all of it, everything the guild has hidden, including what you are and why a hedge-witch spent twenty years hiding you in plain sight. In exchange, you help me reach the Borderstone. I can't reforging it alone. Neither can you. But together—"

"Together we rewrite it," he said. "And the guild loses its monopoly on scarcity. And the Emberlands get their border back. And I get—" He stopped.

"Yes?" Sarelle said softly. "What do you get?"

And there it was, the trap in his own chest. Because she had said *what you are*, and she had looked at him when she said it, and some starving part of him had heard *you matter, you were always meant to matter, here is the proof.* After years of being interchangeable by design. After a lifetime of being someone's charge instead of someone's choice.

He recognized the shape of the offer. That was the worst part. He'd fled Maren Thistlewood partly because she'd arranged his whole life around a purpose he'd never agreed to. And here was another woman with another plan, offering him meaning on terms he hadn't set.

"I get to be useful," he said carefully, "to someone who needs me to be exactly what I am. Which is either the truest thing anyone's ever offered me or the most sophisticated lie I've ever been sold. I genuinely can't tell which."

Sarelle held his gaze, and to her credit—for a given value of credit—she didn't flinch. "Both of those can be true at once," she said. "I'm not asking for your faith. I'm asking for your signature. Read the contract first. That's more than your guildmaster ever gave you."

"I need time."

"You have less of it than you think. But yes." She rose, rewrapped the Borderstone fragment, and left it on his desk. "Keep that. Consider it collateral. If I'm lying, it's worthless to you anyway. If I'm telling the truth, you'll want proof that fits in your pocket." At the door she paused. "One more thing. Corvane will make you an offer soon—probably tonight, probably through channels that feel like friendship. When he does, remember: protection purchased with silence is just rent paid on a smaller and smaller cell."

Then she was gone, and the room felt emptier than it had before she filled it.

* * *

Corvane's offer came, as predicted, within the hour—but not through channels that felt like friendship. Through channels that felt like Corvane.

Two stewards arrived to escort Sebastian up to the Guildmaster's office, which had never once happened in four years of service. Junior clerks were summoned; they were not *escorted*. The distinction sat in his stomach like swallowed ice the entire way up the spiral stair.

The office occupied the tower's crown, and Corvane had opened the shutters despite the starless night. Moonless dark pressed against the glass. On the desk lay the ash ledger itself, open, its pages breathing faint heat.

"You met with the exile," Corvane said, without looking up. "In your room. Ninety-one minutes. I mention the duration because precision matters to you, and I want you to understand that it matters to me as well."

Sebastian's mouth went dry. "You have watchers on junior clerks?"

"I have watchers on *anything that touches the covenant*. You became such a thing the moment you opened a sealed ledger you had no clearance for." Now he looked up, and his eyes were mild, almost kind, which was somehow worse than anger. "Sit."

Sebastian sat.

"I'm going to be brief, because unlike our visitor, I don't deal in theater." Corvane laced his fingers. "The situation is dangerous. There are factions in this city who will learn what you are—she clearly already knows—and they will not offer you tea and a contract. They will offer you a knife and a barrel. My offer is simple: continued protection, full access to the archives, and in time, a seat at the table where decisions about the border are actually made. In exchange: silence. You saw nothing in the ash ledger. You met no one at the gate. You are a clerk who catalogs storms, full stop."

"And the siphoning?" Sebastian heard himself ask. "The payments 'to keep the sky lit'? Does the seat at the table include learning who's been spending the covenant like coin?"

Corvane's expression didn't change, but the temperature of the room did—or seemed to. "The guild's finances are the guild's business. That has been true for three hundred years and will remain true long after both of us are dust." He slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. On it, in Corvane's own hand, were terms of almost insulting simplicity: *protection, access, advancement* on one line; *silence* on the other. A place for a signature at the bottom.

"No truth, then," Sebastian said. "Just safety."

"Safety *is* the truth most people want. The rest is decoration." Corvane leaned back. "You're young, Brightwater, and the exile has spoken to the part of you that wants to be extraordinary. I understand the temptation. But extraordinary men die in ditches while ordinary men inherit functioning institutions. Sign, keep your head down, and in ten years you'll be auditing the very ledgers she wants to burn. Power deferred is power secured."

Sebastian looked at the paper. Then at the ash ledger, its pages still radiating that faint, wrong warmth. Then at the window, where the starless dark stood like a held breath.

"May I take the night to consider?"

Corvane studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he evidently decided it was acceptable, because he nodded once. "Until dawn. The stewards will see you back." As Sebastian reached the door: "Brightwater. The exile is using you. Whatever she told you about what you are—ask yourself why she waited until the wards fell to come collect you. People who value you don't store you until they need you."

The words followed him down the stairs like a second shadow, because they were true, or true enough, and the cruelest lies are built from load-bearing truths.

* * *

Back in his room, Sebastian laid the two offers side by side on his desk, because lists were how he thought and this was, whatever else it was, a problem of bookkeeping.

*Offer one:* Truth, purpose, belonging—in exchange for service to a queen whose motives he could not verify, whose people had every reason to hate the guild, and who had admitted, with disarming candor, that both gratitude and deception might be in the mix. Collateral provided: one dead stone that sang against his skin.

*Offer two:* Safety, status, continuity—in exchange for complicity in whatever had put out the stars. Collateral provided: none needed, because the threat was implicit in the world outside the walls.

He picked up the Borderstone fragment. It hummed. Behind his sternum, that buried bell rang again, and this time he let himself listen to it properly—not as danger, not as temptation, but as *information*. The stone wasn't calling to him. It was calling to something in him, something that had been there all along, patient as sediment. Twenty-six years of cataloging bottled storms and borrowed years, and the whole time the rarest item in any ledger had been sitting behind his own ribs.

*You were never my apprentice,* Maren had said, the last time he'd seen her, rain streaming down both their faces on the road out of the fens. *You were my charge.*

He'd hated her for it. He was beginning to suspect he'd misunderstood it.

Sebastian took out a fresh sheet of paper. Under *What I know*, he wrote:

*Both of them are right about the other one. That's the trap.*

Under that:

*Neither of them has asked me what I want the covenant to be. They've only asked me to restore it or protect it. Nobody has asked what it should say.*

He stared at that sentence for a long time. It felt important in the way a loose thread feels important—insignificant-looking, structurally vital.

Because here was the thing neither Sarelle nor Corvane had considered, the variable absent from both their arithmetic: a living sigil was not a key to be used. A sigil was *writing*. And writing, any clerk worth his salt knew, was not the same as signing. Before anything got signed—before terms were accepted, before names were given—he intended to read the original covenant himself. Every clause. Every payment. Every entry in ash.

Not the exile's version. Not the Guildmaster's version. The document.

Which meant the deep vault. Which meant breaking into the most secure repository on the continent, using knowledge only a junior clerk would have: which locks were ceremonial, which guards were rotationally blind, and which of the Ledgerhouse's thousand protections had been designed to stop thieves from getting *in* rather than clerks from getting *out*.

Sebastian blew out his lamp, tucked the Borderstone fragment into his coat, and pulled the guild seal from his belt—the small brass stamp that let him sign for shipments of impossible things.

Tonight, he would use it to sign for the impossiblest thing of all.

He was halfway to the door when he heard, faint through the floorboards, the sound of boots in the corridor below—too many boots, moving with purpose, and beneath them a voice giving orders in a tone he'd never heard from a guild steward: flat, patient, and utterly without a face.

Somewhere in the Ledgerhouse, something had begun counting.
