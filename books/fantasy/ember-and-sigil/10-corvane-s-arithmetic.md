## 10. Corvane's Arithmetic

Guildmaster Corvane had always believed that most people mistook cruelty for complexity. They imagined that men who did terrible things must be terrible men—twisted, ravening, delighting in ruin. It was a comforting fiction, and like most comforting fictions, it was profitable to those who understood the truth: that great harm was almost never done by monsters, but by accountants.

He was, he reflected as he climbed the spiral stair to his private counting room, an excellent accountant.

The room at the top of the Ledgerhouse had no name, because named things could be subpoenaed, and unnamed things belonged to whoever kept them. It was a small chamber, octagonal, windowless except for a single skylight of smoked glass through which no star had shone for six nights now. The walls were lined not with books but with instruments: astrolabes of blackened brass, orreries with too many rings, a pendulum that swung in patterns no clockmaker could explain, marking time in some currency other than hours.

Corvane poured himself a measure of plum brandy—the good bottle, the one he saved for nights when arithmetic demanded celebration—and sat down at his desk, where a single ledger lay open beneath a lamp of ember-glass.

It was not the ash ledger. That one he kept elsewhere, wrapped in oilcloth, in a place even the Tallyman did not know. This was his own book, in his own hand, begun forty years ago on the night he first signed his indenture papers and understood, with the clarity of the young, exactly what the Ledgerhouse was for.

*Owning things,* he had written then, in a boy's careful script, *is only the beginning. Owning scarcity is forever.*

He turned the pages now with something like affection. Here was the drought of his thirtieth year, when he had bought up every granary contract in three provinces and sold the grain back to the farmers who grew it, at interest, until they owned him their farms. Here was the plague winter, when he had cornered the market on grave-digging. Here was the year of the comet, when fear itself had become a commodity and the guild had sold protective charms by the barrelful—charms that did nothing, priced against a terror that was real.

Each entry was balanced to the copper. Each one had made the world worse, in the small and local sense. And each one had made the world *safer*, in the large and only meaningful sense, because a man who owns the grain does not need to burn the fields, and a city that owes him does not riot, and a kingdom deep in debt keeps its wars small and its borders quiet.

Scarcity, properly managed, was not cruelty. Scarcity was *civilization*. Abundance was what happened before the fighting started.

He drank his brandy and looked up through the smoked glass at the starless sky, and permitted himself, for the fourth time in six nights, a small and private smile.

* * *

They would call it a catastrophe. He knew that already; he had read catastrophes the way other men read weather. Within a week the temples would be full, within a month the ballads would be written, and within a year the whole affair would be folded into the shape of a morality tale—*the night the stars went out, and how we suffered.* Suffering made excellent collateral. Grief was the most reliable currency ever minted, because no one counterfeited their own.

But catastrophe implied accident, and there had been nothing accidental about it. That was the first thing to understand, and the thing no one would ever be permitted to understand. The border wards between the human realm and the Emberlands had not failed. They had been *harvested*.

Forty years of withdrawals. Small ones. Careful ones. A ward-stone here, siphoned a thimbleful at a time through a guild seal pressed to wet stone. A covenant-thread there, teased loose and wound onto a spool, sold quietly to buyers who never asked why the Emberlands' fire could be carried cold. He had inherited the operation from his predecessor, who had inherited it from hers, all the way back to the founding generation—the men and women who had signed the original covenant and then, being merchants to the marrow, had immediately begun looking for ways to trade against it.

The ash ledger recorded it all. Payments made *to keep the sky lit*—that was the euphemism the founders had chosen, and Corvane appreciated its elegance. Because that was precisely what they were doing. The covenant between realms was a lamp, and the guild had spent four decades selling off the oil a drop at a time, and the lamp had finally guttered, and now—

Now came the arithmetic.

He opened a fresh page and wrote the date at the top, though dates were becoming slippery things without stars to count them by. Then he set out the figures, as he did each morning, the way a pious man says his prayers.

*Item:* One broken border. Cost, in immediate terms: panic, refugee pressure on the eastern gates, disruption of cross-realm trade (negligible; he had quietly closed those accounts months ago), and the risk of open war if the Emberlands chose to invade rather than starve.

*Item:* One realm's worth of scarcity. Ember-goods cut off at the source. Everwarm cloth, sunfruit, healing resins, the thousand small miracles the border had leaked into human markets for nine hundred years—all of it gone, or nearly gone, and every remaining scrap now held in guild vaults.

*Item:* Price discovery. This was the beautiful part. When something became rare, its price did not simply rise—it *revealed itself*. For nine hundred years, ember-warmth had been nearly free, and so no one had known what it was worth. Now they would find out. Every hearth in the city would learn, this winter, the true price of warmth, and the guild would be holding the scale.

He totaled the column. Then, because he was honest with himself in a way he wished more men were capable of, he entered the costs on the other side of the ledger.

*Cost:* Some number of deaths. Refugees turned away from the gates in the cold. Villages in the Gray Country abandoned. He did not flinch from the figure—he had estimated it years ago, when the plan was first drawn, and revised it each season as the harvests of ward-power improved. The number was large. It was also finite, bounded, and *known*, which distinguished it absolutely from the alternative.

Because that was the argument he would make, someday, when it was safe to make it—the argument he had already made to himself a thousand times, in the small hours, and never once lost. The old border had been a lie of abundance. Nine centuries of free fire flowing across a line in the world, unpriced, ungoverned, and everyone pretending it would last forever. And what had abundance bought? Dependence. Two realms so knotted together that neither could survive the other's misfortune. The Emberlands' population had tripled on imported grain; the human realm's winters had softened until no one remembered how to endure one. The covenant hadn't been peace. It had been a debt compounding in the dark, waiting for a collector.

Someone, eventually, was going to break that border. If not the guild, then war, or famine, or the slow rot of two civilizations that had forgotten how to stand alone. The collapse was coming the way winter is coming. All Corvane had done was choose the date, and the survivors, and the price list.

That was stewardship. That was the word he used, privately, and meant. A shepherd kills a fraction of the flock to save the rest; a forester burns a clearing so the forest lives; a merchant breaks a border so that two realms might learn, at last, what everything truly costs—and learn it under his management, gently, over decades, instead of in a single night of fire.

He was not destroying the old order. He was *underwriting* it.

Corvane finished the brandy and allowed himself the smile again, longer this time.

* * *

A knock at the stair door: two soft raps, then one. The Tallyman did not knock any other way, having no face to arrange into courtesy.

"Enter."

The Tallyman entered the way it always did, without seeming to cross the threshold—a tall absence in a gray coat, its features smooth as an unwritten page. Guild-craft, that facelessness. Corvane had paid a great deal for it, decades ago, and considered it the finest purchase of his career. You could bribe a man, blackmail him, seduce or terrify him, but you could never be entirely sure of him, because a man has a face and a face has loyalties. The Tallyman had none. It was pure function, like a well-made lock.

"The Brightwater boy," Corvane said. "Report."

"He found the ash ledger," the Tallyman said. Its voice was dry paper. "Six weeks ago. He copied three entries into his personal book and returned them to the shelf. He has told no one."

Corvane had expected this, and it still settled in his chest like a swallowed stone. "And since?"

"He watches. He counts. He says nothing." A pause, which for the Tallyman was the equivalent of a raised eyebrow. "He is afraid. But he is more curious than afraid."

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "That is what I was counting on."

Because here was the second thing to understand, the thing the founders had grasped and he had refined: you did not hide a living sigil by locking it away. Locked things got found. You hid it in plain sight, in the one place no one would think to look—in the guild itself, keeping its books, balancing its columns, learning to love the very instrument of his own concealment. Maren Thistlewood had raised him gentle and half-blind; the Ledgerhouse had finished the work, teaching him that everything, even wonder, could be entered in a column and carried forward. A sigil who thought like a clerk would never burn like one.

And now the stars were out, and the boy had found the ash ledger, and he had said nothing—which meant he was ready for the third lesson, the one Corvane had been saving for forty years:

That knowing was not the same as choosing. That a secret kept is a debt incurred. That Sebastian Brightwater had been drawing wages from the guild's silence for six weeks now, whether he knew it or not, and debts, in the Ledgerhouse, always came due.

"What of the queen?" Corvane asked.

"Sarelle crossed the eastern gate two days ago, disguised. She has made contact with the boy." The Tallyman tilted its blank head. "She told him the truth about the covenant. Or a truth."

"Of course she did. Truth is her best bait—she learned that the hard way, a long time ago." Corvane rose and went to the window, looking down through the smoked glass at the city below, where ten thousand hearths were burning fuel the guild owned at prices the guild was setting. Somewhere down there, a frightened clerk with sigil-marked blood was deciding whom to trust, believing the choice was still his.

Let him choose. Choice was the whole point. A covenant written under coercion would not hold—the founders had proven that at cost—and so the boy must come to the Borderstone freely, persuaded, certain that the decision was his own. Corvane needed Sebastian Brightwater to want to write the new terms. Needed him to believe that managed scarcity was mercy, that a priced miracle was safer than a free one, that the guild's hand on the scale was the only thing standing between two realms and the dark.

The boy wanted, more than anything, to belong. Corvane intended to offer him belonging on excellent terms.

"If she moves him toward the Borderstone, do not stop them," he said. "Follow. Protect, if you must. I want him arriving alive, willing, and convinced the idea was his."

"And if the queen tells him about me?"

"She will tell him about the guild. She will not tell him about the *arithmetic*." Corvane turned back to his desk, to the neat columns of cost and gain, death and stability, all balanced to the copper. "No one believes arithmetic until they've done it themselves. Let him count the refugees at the gate. Let him count the empty granaries. Let him arrive at my figures by his own road, and he'll defend them better than I ever could."

The Tallyman waited. There was one question left, and Corvane answered it unprompted, because he respected thoroughness even in a faceless thing.

"And Odile Vane?"

"The gate-captain visited the ward-failure sites today. She found our seals in the stone." He had watched the report come in an hour ago, and felt not alarm but a kind of pride—she was good, Vane, exactly as good as he'd hoped when he'd recommended her promotion eight years ago. Good officers were an asset like any other. "She hasn't reported it yet. She's deciding what she is. Give her room. A loyal soldier who discovers treason in her own house doesn't run to the enemy—she runs to the evidence. Let her gather it. Evidence is just inventory, and inventory can be seized."

He closed his ledger, squared it on the desk, and set the plum brandy bottle back behind the false panel where it lived. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the smoked glass like a customer waiting for the shop to open.

"One more thing," Corvane said. "Send word to the vault-keepers. The anchor-book is to be moved to the Borderstone within the week. Quietly. If the boy is going to sign, he should sign on-site."

The Tallyman inclined its smooth head and was gone, and Corvane stood alone in his counting room doing the last sum of the night, the one he never wrote down:

Two realms, nine hundred years of borrowed fire, one broken border, one living sigil, one exiled queen, one doubting captain—and himself, the only man in the world who could see the whole column and call it balance.

It was good arithmetic. It had always been good arithmetic. In a few weeks, when the boy put his name to the new covenant and the guild's ownership of scarcity was ratified in blood older than kingdoms, history would agree.

Below him, the city slept badly in the cold, ten thousand fires burning on credit.

And far to the east, past the dead fields of the Gray Country, the Borderstone waited in the dark—half-buried, mountain-sized, patient as an unpaid bill—for the hand that would write upon it next.

Corvane blew out the lamp.

In the darkness, the pendulum swung on, measuring out time in a currency only he was rich enough to spend.
