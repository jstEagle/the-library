## 14. Hunters in the Hollow Hills

The hollow hills had teeth.

Sebastian felt them before he saw them—a wrongness in the ground, a hollowness beneath his boots, as though the land itself had been scooped out and left to heal badly. The Gray Country stretched around them in every direction: fields of ash-pale grass that crumbled at a touch, fences dissolved to rust-colored stains, a farmhouse with its roof caved in and its door still politely shut. Nothing grew here. Nothing rotted, either. The land between the wards was simply *paused*, held like a breath that no one remembered to release.

"Three days," Sarelle said, walking beside him with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves. In the dead light of the starless sky she looked less like an exiled queen and more like what she'd claimed to be at the gate—a refugee, tired and sharp-eyed and carrying everything she owned in two pockets. "Three days since the stars went out, and this is what's left where the wards used to run."

"It wasn't always like this?"

"No." Something moved behind her eyes, some old grief she kept on a short leash. "When the covenant held, this was orchard country. Apples. Honey. My people traded glass for cider along this road for four hundred years." She touched one of the ruined fence posts as they passed, gently, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "I helped break it because I thought freedom would look different than this."

Sebastian said nothing. He'd heard the confession three nights ago, by firelight, delivered flat and unadorned while Odile sharpened her sword and pretended not to listen. Shame offered honestly. He still didn't know if it made things better or worse that she'd done it herself—that the ember-queen hadn't been deposed by traitors but had walked into exile carrying her own crime like a stone in her chest.

What he knew was this: she hadn't lied about the way forward. Maren's sigil-knife, warm against his ribs, pulled faintly toward the Borderstone like a compass needle made of guilt. And so they walked.

Odile ranged ahead, twenty paces out, moving the way soldiers move through bad country—weight on the balls of her feet, head never still. She'd been tracking them since the shrine, or rather she'd stopped pretending she wasn't. Three days ago she had stood over Sebastian with her blade drawn and her oath burning in her throat, and then she'd sheathed it and said, "I'm coming with you. Not for you. For the truth." She hadn't spoken much since. Loyal soldiers, Sebastian was learning, grieve their institutions the way other people grieve parents: quietly, angrily, and without any hope of getting back what they lost.

The hills closed around them near midday—or what passed for midday under a sky with nothing in it. The light didn't come from anywhere anymore; it simply *was*, gray and sourceless, casting shadows that pointed in contradictory directions. The road narrowed between two slopes of bare earth, and the grass grew sparser, and Sebastian noticed that the silence had changed quality.

It wasn't empty silence. It was *attentive* silence.

He stopped. "Sarelle."

"I know." Her hand came out of her sleeve already glowing, a coal-dim orange light cupped in her palm like a stolen sunrise. "Since the second hill. Someone is counting us."

*Counting.* The word landed strangely, and then Sebastian understood why, and cold walked down his spine. "The Tallyman."

"The Tallyman," Sarelle agreed.

They'd heard the stories from refugees at the gate, back when the world still had stars. Corvane's enforcer had no face—not scarred, not masked, but *absent*, smooth as the inside of a ledger's spine—and wherever guild interests required arithmetic rather than argument, the Tallyman went to make the sums come out right. Debts collected. Assets liquidated. Discrepancies resolved.

Sebastian was, he understood now, a discrepancy.

"Off the road," Odile called back, low and urgent. She'd frozen mid-stride, one fist raised. "Down. Now."

Sebastian didn't question it. He dropped into the ditch beside the road, pulling Sarelle with him, and a heartbeat later the world tore open.

It began as a sound—the sound of tearing silk, vast and intimate, the same sound the border wards had made when they failed. Then the air above the road split along a seam Sebastian couldn't see, and figures stepped through it the way you'd step through a curtain: six of them, seven, eight, gray-cloaked, moving with the terrible economy of people who have done violence so often it has become bookkeeping. They fanned out across the road without a word. No commands. None needed.

And at their center, unhurried, came the Tallyman.

Sebastian had expected a monster. What he saw was worse: a figure of perfectly ordinary proportions, perfectly ordinary dress, a guild clerk's gray coat with the Ledgerhouse sigil stitched at the collar. Only where a face should have been there was smoothness, featureless as unwritten paper—and yet Sebastian felt, with absolute certainty, that he was being *read*. Measured. Entered into a column somewhere.

A voice came from the Tallyman, and it did not come from a mouth. It seemed to arrive directly behind Sebastian's ears, patient and dry, like ink drying.

**Item: one living sigil. Condition: unclaimed. Item: one exiled sovereign. Condition: delinquent. Item: one captain of the gate watch. Condition:** a pause, delicate as a knife turning, **defected.**

Odile rose out of the ditch with her sword drawn and her voice like iron dragged over flint. "I am Captain Odile Vane of the Gate Watch, sworn to the city and the crown, and I am placing all of you under arrest for crimes against the realm."

One of the gray cloaks tilted its head, almost curious.

**Objection noted,** said the Tallyman. **Overruled.**

The ambush broke like a dam.

* * *

Later, Sebastian would try to remember the fight as a sequence of events, and fail. Memory under violence doesn't file itself in order. What he retained were fragments:

Sarelle's hand closing on his wrist and *pulling*, fire blooming off her shoulders as she turned his stumble into a sprint—uphill, away from the road, into the teeth of the hills.

Gray cloaks flowing after them, silent, one of them running *wrong*, limbs folding at angles that made Sebastian's stomach lurch.

Odile turning to face three of them alone, alone by choice, planting herself in the narrow ground like a door deciding to be a wall. He heard her shout something—"Run, you idiot, *run*"—and then steel met whatever gray cloaks were made of, and the sound was not the sound of swords. It was the sound of a pen scratching, endless and furious.

And above it all, behind his ears, the Tallyman's voice, keeping count.

**Two minutes of pursuit expended. Acceptable.**

They ran. The hills swallowed them. Sebastian's lungs burned with air that tasted of ash and old apples, and Maren's knife pulsed against his ribs like a second heartbeat, faster now, alarmed. Behind them the sounds of Odile's battle receded—did not stop, but receded, which was somehow more frightening, because it meant she was still fighting, still holding, still spending herself as currency to buy them seconds.

Then the ground opened.

Not a pit—a hollow, one of the hills' namesake cavities, hidden under a skin of pale grass until Sarelle's weight found its edge and the earth sighed and gave. They slid together down a chute of crumbling soil into darkness, tumbling, and landed hard in a space that smelled of roots and rain that had fallen years ago and never dried.

Above them, muffled, the scratching sound continued. Then footsteps crossed the broken ground overhead. Stopped.

**Item: two fugitives. Location: probable. Expenditure to date: acceptable.**

Silence. Longer. Sebastian pressed his hand over his own mouth. Beside him Sarelle had gone utterly still, her fire smothered to nothing, her breathing slowed to almost invisibility—he hadn't known people could do that.

Then, distantly, a scream that was not Odile's, followed by the tearing-silk sound again, fading. The gray cloaks were leaving. Or relocating. With the Tallyman, Sebastian suspected, there was no meaningful difference.

They waited until waiting itself became unbearable, and then longer.

"They'll circle back," Sarelle whispered at last. "That thing doesn't abandon an entry in its ledger. It renegotiates terms."

"Odile." Sebastian's voice cracked on the name. "She was—she stayed behind—"

"I know." Sarelle's hand found his shoulder in the dark. For once there was nothing predatory in her touch, nothing calculated. Just warmth, banked low, like coals at dawn. "I know, Sebastian."

They climbed out of the hollow on shaking legs. The road above was empty. Scorch marks stitched across the grass in patterns Sebastian's eye, treacherously trained on ledgers, almost recognized as script. And thirty paces off, slumped against a ruined stone wall with her sword across her knees and blood sheeting down her left arm—

Odile Vane sat up.

"Oh good," she rasped. "You're alive. Now help me up, because I don't intend to die sitting down in front of guild property."

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a chapel that had belonged to no god Sebastian could name—round-walled, roofless, its altar a plain block of stone worn smooth by centuries of anonymous hands. Sarelle bound Odile's arm in strips cut from her own cloak, working with the brisk competence of someone who had dressed wounds in wars Sebastian could only guess at. The cut beneath the bandages was clean but deep, a line drawn from elbow to wrist, precise as a stroke of a pen.

"That's not a wound," Odile said, watching the queen work. "That's a *notation*. It was showing me where I'd be entered in its books if it let me live."

"Why did it let you live?" Sebastian asked.

Odile was quiet for a moment. Firelight—Sarelle's firelight, cupped in a lantern of fused glass she'd carried from another life—moved across her face, and Sebastian watched a woman conduct a final audit of herself.

"Because it wanted me to deliver a message," she said finally. "It stopped fighting. Mid-strike. Looked at me with that—nothing it has instead of a face—and said:" her voice flattened into a dry imitation, voiceless and patient, "*'The Ledgerhouse offers Captain Vane reinstatement, full honors, and command of the eastern gates. Terms: the sigil, delivered alive, within six days.'*" She flexed her bandaged hand. "Then it bowed. Actually bowed. And walked through a hole in the world."

"And you said?" Sarelle asked softly.

"I said I'd consider it." Odile met both their stares without flinching. "Because saying no outright would have gotten me killed on the spot, and dead captains can't defect. I've been thinking about that word for three days. Defection. Treason. Everyone who ever taught me to hold a line would call what I'm about to say treason." She reached to her belt and drew something from a leather case: her oath-scroll, the gate watch's seal in green wax, the document she'd sworn her life upon at nineteen years old with her father watching from the stands.

She held it over the fire.

"I swore to protect the city," she said. "All of it. Every soul inside the walls and every soul trying to get in. And I have spent three weeks learning that the institution I gave my oath to sold the sky, starved the borderlands, and sent a faceless clerk to collect a boy like a debt." The scroll's edge began to brown. "An oath given to thieves isn't an oath. It's collateral. And I'm calling the loan due."

The green wax caught, bubbled, and the scroll went up in a breath of flame. Odile watched it burn with an expression Sebastian couldn't read—grief and relief wearing each other's clothes—until nothing remained but ash on her gauntlets, which she brushed away with great care, like closing a ledger.

"Well," she said. "That's done. Where's the Borderstone, and how many of them will be waiting?"

Sarelle smiled—small, real, the first true smile Sebastian had seen from her. "Two days east. And all of them."

"Good." Odile rolled her wounded shoulder, winced, and grinned like a woman stepping off a cliff on purpose. "I hate fair fights."

Sebastian laughed—actually laughed, a startled, cracked sound that surprised all three of them—and then pressed his hand to his ribs, where Maren's sigil-knife had gone suddenly, urgently warm.

Warmer than it had ever been.

Warm enough, he realized, looking east into the sourceless dark, that it was no longer pulling toward the Borderstone.

It was pulling *away* from it. Toward the hills' deep heart, where something old waited among the roots—and where, faint but unmistakable, someone was calling his name.

A name no one should have known. A name he had never told anyone in the Gray Country at all.

"*Sebastian,*" said the dark between the hills, in a voice like cooling ash. "Come and finish your lessons."
