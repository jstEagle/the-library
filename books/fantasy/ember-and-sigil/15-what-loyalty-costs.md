## 15. What Loyalty Costs

The shrine had no name anymore. Whatever god it had once honored had been scraped from the altar stone so thoroughly that only the shape of the scraping remained—a hollow where devotion used to sit, like a socket where a tooth had been. Rain came through the broken roof in threads and pooled in the hollow, and by the light of Sarelle's banked ember-fire the water looked like a bowl of dark glass holding a reflection of a sky that still had no stars.

Sebastian sat against the far wall with Maren's sigil-knife across his knees and watched Odile Vane clean a sword that did not need cleaning.

She had been at it for an hour. The blade was bright enough already to shave with; he could see his own warped face in it, stretched and pale. But her hands needed work, and so she gave them work, drawing the whetstone along the steel in long, patient strokes, *shhhk, shhhk*, the sound filling the ruined nave like a slow heartbeat.

Every few strokes, she glanced at the door.

"You'll wear a groove in that edge," Sebastian said.

"I'll wear a groove in something," Odile said. "Better the sword than my teeth." She set the blade down across her knees—a mirror image of him, he realized, both of them cradling metal—and looked at the fire instead of at him. "Tallyman's hounds lost us at the river crossing. That buys us until morning. Maybe midmorning if the rain keeps up and washes our trail."

"And after midmorning?"

"After midmorning I stop making promises I can't keep."

Sarelle slept—or pretended to—curled near the embers with her cloak drawn up and her crown's absence more visible than any crown would have been. Even sleeping, even disguised in refugee wool with ash rubbed into her hair, she managed to look like someone who had been robbed of a throne rather than a purse. Her breathing was too even. Sebastian had spent three weeks on the road with her now, and he had learned that Sarelle's breathing was always too even when she was awake and listening.

He didn't mind. He was getting used to being listened to by people who pretended not to be.

*Shhhk,* went the whetstone.

*Shhhk.*

"Odile," he said.

"Don't." She didn't look up. "Whatever sentence starts with my name right now, don't finish it. I'm not ready for it, and you're not ready to say it, and we've got maybe six hours of peace left. Let's not spend them bleeding."

"It's about the ledger."

The whetstone stopped.

In the silence Sebastian heard everything else: the rain threading through the roof, the pop of the ember-fire, the wind moving through the Gray Country outside with that low, hollow note it made everywhere now, as if the land itself were an empty bottle someone kept blowing across. Somewhere beyond the hills, the Borderstone waited under its shroud of dead earth, mountain-sized, half-buried, humming a frequency he felt in his back teeth whenever he let himself be quiet.

And somewhere behind them, the Tallyman walked without hurry, because the Tallyman never hurried. Things that were certain didn't need to.

Odile laid the sword down. Carefully, the way she did everything that mattered—as if setting down a weapon and picking one up were the same gesture performed at different speeds.

"The ash ledger," she said. "The one Corvane showed you. Payments made *to keep the sky lit*."

"Yes."

"How long?"

Sebastian had rehearsed this confession for eleven days. He had composed versions of it on the road, in the gray rain, walking beside Sarelle while she pretended not to watch him struggle. He had imagined Odile's anger, planned his defenses, drafted justifications the way he'd once drafted catalog entries—precise, orderly, each clause balanced against the next.

All of it dissolved now, and what was left was the simple thing underneath, which was:

"Six weeks," he said. "Before the stars went out."

The fire popped. Sarelle's too-even breathing did not change, which meant she was listening with every part of herself except the parts that needed air.

Odile nodded slowly, once, twice—the nod of a woman counting something. "Six weeks. You found a ledger written in cooling ash that said the guild was paying to hold up the sky, and you sat on it for six weeks, and then the sky went out."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the question he'd dreaded most, because there was no good answer, and worse, there was an honest one. He turned the sigil-knife over in his hands. Maren's knife. Its handle was worn smooth by forty years of her grip, and it fit his hand almost perfectly, which was its own small grief.

"Because I wanted to belong somewhere," he said. "That's the whole ugly truth of it. You know what I was before the Ledgerhouse. A hedge-witch's boy who ran away because I thought ledgers were a kind of magic. And they are—they're the most honest magic there is, everything weighed, everything written, nothing hidden. That's what I believed. And then I opened that ledger and saw that the Ledgerhouse hides the biggest entry it owns, and do you know what I did?" He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I closed it. I put it back on the shelf. Because Corvane was kind to me. Because I had a desk with my name on it. Because after twenty-two years of being somebody's charge, somebody's problem, somebody's secret—I finally had a *place*, and I was afraid that asking the wrong questions was how places get taken away."

"Corvane knew you'd found it," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"He ordered me watched the same night. I didn't know that yet, but yes. He told me the ledger was old business. Settled accounts. He said—" Sebastian stopped, and made himself say it exactly, because exactness was the only penance he had. "He said, *Some entries exist so that others can be balanced, Brightwater. A good clerk knows which pages to leave unopened.* And I thanked him. I thanked him and went back to cataloging bottled thunderstorms."

Odile picked up the sword again. Not to clean it—to hold it. Her thumb moved along the flat of the blade, back and forth, a small restless motion completely unlike her.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I stood gate-watch during the Vellum Riots. You remember those?"

"Before my time at the House."

"Grain prices. The guild had cornered the winter stores—this was years before your time, when Corvane was just Guildmaster-elect—and the east quarter starved for a month while warehouses sat full. People died on the steps of buildings full of food. And I stood at the gate with a spear and kept the hungry away from the hoard, because that was my oath. Keep the gate. Protect the city's order." She looked up at him, and her eyes in the ember-light were the color of wet slate. "I told myself for ten years that order was the same thing as justice. Then the stars went out, and I started finding guild seals burned into the ward-stones, and I have spent every night since asking myself whether I've been a soldier or a lock. Whether the difference matters. Whether it ever mattered."

"And what did you decide?"

"I decided locks don't get to confess." She pointed the sword at him—not threateningly; conversationally, the way another person might point a finger. "But people do. So go on. There's more. There's always more with you clerks; you can't write a short entry to save your life."

Sebastian took a breath.

"There's more," he admitted. "The night before the stars went out, I went back to the vault. I couldn't sleep. I told myself it was an audit discrepancy—I'd found a page reference in the ash ledger that didn't resolve, a codex number for a volume that wasn't in any index. And I went down to find it." He looked at the fire. "I found the deep vault door open, Odile. Unlocked. Someone wanted it found. And I got as far as the antechamber, and I saw the anchor-case, and I—" His hands had gone cold around the knife. "I read the first page. Just the first page. And then I ran. I ran and I told myself I'd imagined it, and two nights later the stars went out, and I let myself keep believing I'd imagined it, because the alternative was that I had held the truth of the whole world in my hands and chosen my desk over everyone alive."

"What was on the page?"

"A name." His voice came out very quiet. "My mother's name. In a book bound in—"

"In skin," Sarelle said.

They both turned. She was sitting up now, cloak fallen from her shoulders, watching them with eyes that held their own small fires. She hadn't been asleep at all, then. Or she had woken at *ledger*, the way soldiers woke at *enemy*.

"You read the first page," Sarelle said, "and you saw her name, and you understood what the book was made of, and you ran. Yes. I know. I've known since the waystation, since you told Maren what you'd seen. I was waiting to hear whether you'd tell the captain."

"That's why you've been letting me lead us toward the Borderstone," Sebastian said slowly. "You already knew I'd touched it. You knew the book would let me touch it again."

"I knew a living sigil who has already brushed against his own anchor will be drawn back to it whether he chooses it or not," Sarelle said. "Yes. Forgive me. Exiles learn patience the way other people learn languages—out of necessity, and never perfectly." She rose and crossed to the fire, and for a moment Sebastian braced for the queen, the predator, the woman who had identified him by his blood within minutes of meeting him.

Instead she knelt. On the wet stone floor of a nameless shrine, the last ember-queen of the Emberlands knelt so that her eyes were level with his, and said:

"I helped break the covenant, Sebastian. Three hundred years ago, with my own hands, believing I was freeing my people. Everything that has burned since is my arithmetic. So believe me when I tell you I know precisely what a guilty conscience weighs, and yours is heavier than it needs to be. You were a clerk. You were afraid. You were *young*. Corvane built an empire on men exactly like you, and he built it knowing they would stay silent, and he built it so that staying silent would feel like prudence." She reached out and closed Sebastian's fingers tighter around the knife's handle. "Silence is his currency. Confession is the only coin that ruins him. Do you understand? Every truth spoken aloud in front of witnesses is a withdrawal he cannot recover."

Odile snorted softly. "She's recruiting you."

"She's *right*," Sebastian said, and was surprised to find that he meant it, and more surprised by what came out of his mouth next, which was: "Both of you. Before we go any further—if we're going to stand at that stone together, you should know what you're standing with. All of it. No more sealed ledgers among the three of us."

So he told them.

He told Odile about the apprenticeship—about Maren Thistlewood, and the word *charge*, and the way his whole childhood had been a hiding place arranged by people who loved him and lied to him in the same breath. He told her about the covenant, as much of it as Sarelle had taught him on the road: two realms, one border, a bargain renewed each generation by a name freely given. He told her what he suspected the guild had been doing with the siphoned power—bottling it, portioning it, selling borrowed years and lucked dice and weather in glass—and he watched her face as the arithmetic completed itself behind her eyes, the same arithmetic Corvane performed so reasonably, arriving at the opposite sum.

And Odile told them things too. About the orders she'd received the night the stars fell—orders that predated the fall by hours, routing extra garrisons to the gates, doubling the granary guard. About the courier who'd come from the Ledgerhouse with sealed instructions before the wards tore, and how she had signed for them without reading them, because signing for things without reading them was simply what loyal officers did.

"I helped them stage it," Odile said. "Maybe not knowingly. But the city was ready for refugees before there were refugees. Somebody fed me my own obedience, and I swallowed it, and I signed the chit." She laughed, short and bitter. "A lock, confessing. Sarelle's right. It does ruin something. I can feel it ruining."

"What does it feel like?" Sebastian asked.

"Cold," she said. "And lighter. Both at once. Like cutting off a hand that was numb anyway."

Outside, the rain thickened. The wind found the broken roof and played it, a low moaning chord, and in the hollow of the scraped altar the dark water trembled. Sarelle fed the fire another splinter of pew—one of the few things the shrine had left to give—and the flames took it with a color that wasn't quite orange, wasn't quite gold, the color of her homeland burning at the end of every story she never finished telling.

"We rest in watches," Odile said, standing, rolling her shoulders like a woman putting her uniform back on from the inside. "I take first. Sigil takes second. Your Majesty third, if your Majesty sleeps at all."

"I sleep," Sarelle said, "the way exiles sleep. Lightly, and armed."

Sebastian stayed by the wall a while longer after they settled, turning Maren's knife, feeling the confession sitting in his chest like a coal that had finally been taken out of the fire and set down where it could do no harm. It didn't feel like absolution. Absolution, he suspected, would require the stars to come back, and the dead to unburn, and a certain page in a certain book to be unwritten. But it felt like something adjacent to it. It felt like the difference between a locked room and an open one.

Belonging, he thought. He had spent his whole life trying to buy it—with obedience, with silence, with a well-kept desk and a well-kept mouth. And here in a roofless shrine in the dead heart of the Gray Country, having confessed the worst thing he'd ever done to a traitor and a queen, he felt more like he belonged than he ever had behind the Ledgerhouse walls.

Truth was the price. Nobody had ever put it in a catalog.

He was nearly asleep when Odile's hand closed on his shoulder—hard, immediate, all the softness gone out of her.

"Up," she whispered. "Lights. Down the valley road. Two lanterns, moving wrong—too steady, too spaced. Hounds don't carry lanterns." Her jaw tightened, and in the ember-light Sebastian watched the last of the lock die and the soldier take its place, fully awake, fully committed, past all arithmetic of treason or oath.

"But men do," she said. "And the Tallyman doesn't send men ahead unless he wants to talk first."

* * *

They doused the fire and killed the lights and took positions among the fallen pillars, and Sebastian crouched in the wet dark with Maren's knife in his fist and his heart going like a hammer on a nail, and watched the two lanterns come up the valley road at their terrible, unhurried pace.

They stopped a hundred paces out. One lantern was set down on the road. A voice carried across the rain—not loud, pitched with perfect economy, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it.

"Sebastian Brightwater," the Tallyman called. "Guildmaster Corvane extends final terms. He asks only that you hear the numbers."

Beside him in the dark, he felt Odile go very still, and on his other side Sarelle's ember-eyes flared like twin coals being blown upon, and Sebastian understood, with the strange clarity that arrives only at the edge of ruin, that whatever the Tallyman said next would be reasonable.

That was the horror of Corvane. It was always reasonable.

"Send him in," Sebastian heard himself say. "Let's hear the numbers."
