## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The hemlock was in the red.

Ines Galloway knew it before the wine touched his tongue—knew it from the smell, faint and green, like a meadow that had learned to lie. But protocol was protocol. He lifted the crystal goblet in front of the assembled court, met the eyes of no one, and drank.

Bitterness first. Then the numbness, cool and creeping, starting at the tip of his tongue and spreading toward his throat like frost climbing a windowpane. Water hemlock, cut fresh and steeped too long by someone who knew their craft but not their chemistry. A careless dose. Enough to stop a king's heart in an hour; enough to stop Ines's for perhaps a minute and a half, if he let it run its course.

He set the goblet down with a soft click and stood very still, hands folded, while the poison walked through him looking for a door he'd long since bricked over.

Around him, the great hall went on glittering. Candlelight ran along silver plate and the gilded shoulders of courtiers who had never once looked directly at the man in the plain grey coat standing beside the king's table. That was the arrangement. Tasters were furniture that swallowed. Furniture did not make eye contact, did not speak unless addressed, and certainly did not die dramatically at state dinners—that would be gauche, and the crown of Vellamare prided itself on never being gauche about anything, including murder.

The numbness reached his jaw. His heartbeat slowed, deliberate as a clock winding down. Somewhere behind his ribs, his body made its quiet argument with the toxin, and somewhere deeper than ribs—in whatever place the physicians called constitution and Ines privately called the thing that wouldn't let him die—the argument was won. It always was. It had been won eleven times before this one, and each time cost a little more of the evening.

His heart stuttered, paused, resumed.

He exhaled through his nose, took the decanter, and poured its contents into the spittoon basin at his hip with the ceremony of a man emptying dishwater. Then he turned to the Master of Ceremonies, a round man named Pellwick whose face was currently performing several emotions at once.

"The red is spoiled," Ines said. "I'd send for the cellarman."

Pellwick's eyes flicked to the basin, to Ines's colorless lips, back to the basin. "Spoiled," he repeated.

"Badly." Ines kept his voice level, pitched low enough to carry only to the table. "There will be others checking the rest of the service tonight. I'd start with anything opened after the fourth bell."

For three seconds, the Master of Ceremonies looked at him—actually looked, which happened perhaps twice a year—and Ines watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes: dead taster, dead king, dead dynasty, or spoiled wine and an awkward conversation with the cellar staff. The arithmetic resolved the way it always did.

"See to it," Pellwick told a footman, and the machine of the court rolled on, and Ines Galloway stepped back into his proper place among the candlesticks.

* * *

They gave him a medal once. Eleven years ago, after the night he caught the arsenic in the consommé. A small silver thing on a ribbon, presented by a chamberlain who mispronounced his name, awarded in a corridor because the throne room was being polished. Ines still had it in a drawer under his socks. He'd thought, briefly, that it might change something—that being the man who saved the king's life would buy him a warmer room, a name spoken correctly, a single person in the palace who would notice if he stopped coming to supper.

It had bought him a slightly better room. That was all. The court had absorbed the event the way sand absorbs rain, and Ines had learned the lesson underneath the lesson: invisibility wasn't a punishment the court inflicted on him. It was a service he provided. Kings slept better knowing their taster was nobody. A somebody might have ambitions. A somebody might be bribed, flattered, threatened. Nobody could be trusted precisely because nobody wanted anything.

And so Ines had spent twenty years becoming excellent at wanting nothing, and he was good at it, mostly, except on nights like this one, when he walked back through the servants' corridors with hemlock still numbing his gums and felt the strange double weight in his chest: pride and its shadow, walking hand in hand.

Pride, because he had done it again. Eleven poisons, eleven saves, and not one of them acknowledged beyond a line in a ledger.

The shadow, because he had done it again, and there was no one—not one living soul—to tell.

He let himself into his rooms on the third floor of the east wing, a space the size of a generous cupboard, and lit the lamp. Bread, hard cheese, the heel of a dried sausage. He ate standing up, the way he always did, tasting nothing much, and was halfway through the cheese when someone knocked.

Three knocks. Soft. Wrong. Servants knocked twice and announced themselves; guards knocked like they were breaking the door down. Three soft knocks belonged to no category Ines had ever needed, which meant whoever was on the other side had come for him specifically, and that was a short list, and none of them were good.

He put down the sausage. "It's open," he said, because locking his door had never once stopped anyone who mattered.

The woman who slipped inside was small, grey-cloaked, and moved like someone who had spent her life being told she was taking up too much hallway. She had ink on three fingers and the particular exhausted brightness of a person running on conviction instead of sleep. She looked around his room—the narrow bed, the drawer of medals, the single window overlooking the kitchen gardens—and something in her expression softened in a way Ines distrusted immediately.

"You're the Galloway boy," she said.

"No one here is a boy," Ines said. "You have the wrong room. And you shouldn't be in this wing after the ninth bell."

"My name is Odile Vane." She said it the way people announce themselves when they expect the name to do work. It didn't. "I'm a reader at the Athenaeum. Was. The details of my current employment are complicated." She pulled a bundle from beneath her cloak—a book wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle with age. "I need you to look at something, and then I need you to decide very quickly whether you're going to laugh at me or listen."

"I'm going to call the guard," Ines said, and made no move to do it, and hated himself a little for how obvious that made him.

"You're going to want to see the page first." She unwrapped the book on his bed.

It was old. Not palace-old, with its careful fake patina, but genuinely old—leather worn to suede at the corners, pages the color of weak tea, hand-lettered in a script that predated the current alphabet by a century or more. Marginalia crawled up the edges in at least four different hands, generations of readers arguing with the text. Odile turned the pages with the tenderness of someone handling a sleeping animal until she reached a page marked with a pressed sprig of some mountain flower, brown and flat as a fossil.

She turned the book toward him. "Read it."

"I don't read Old Script."

"I'll translate. Listen, then." She bent close to the page, and her voice changed—lower, slower, the voice of a woman reading scripture she didn't fully believe in. "*When the crown grows teeth and the mountain keeps its ledger, the Galloway boy shall stand between the table and the grave, and the wine shall not take him, for what sleeps below the Spire has already paid his debt.*"

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

Ines found he was holding the cheese knife. He hadn't picked it up. "That's a coincidence," he said. "Galloway is a common name in the river provinces."

"It isn't, actually. I checked the census rolls. There are four Galloways registered in the capital and none of them are tasters." Odile's eyes came up to his, and they were sharper than the rest of her suggested. "But that's not the part that matters. Look at the margin. The date."

Beside the passage, in faded brown ink, someone had written a string of numerals in the old reckoning. Ines had served the court long enough to convert old reckoning in his head—it was expected of even the invisible staff, on the rare occasions anyone deigned to hand them a schedule.

Tomorrow's date.

"That," Ines said slowly, "could mean anything. A feast day. An anniversary. Whoever wrote that book has been dead a hundred years; they weren't writing about my supper."

"They wrote about the wine," Odile said quietly. "The wine that doesn't take you. Tonight. Which means either this book is a hundred-year-old fraud of remarkable specificity—" she tapped the marginal note, "—or someone a century ago knew exactly what you would be doing on this exact evening, in this exact room, and cared enough to write it down."

The numbness had left Ines's tongue. He wished it hadn't. He wanted, suddenly and unreasonably, to taste something—anything—to confirm the world was still ordinary. "People write nonsense about tasters. We're half superstition already. Poison-proof men, devil-tongued men. There's a whole genre."

"There is," Odile agreed. "I've read all of it. This isn't it." She turned another page, and her finger stopped beneath a line of script, and for the first time since entering his room, the scholar hesitated. Whatever was written there, she seemed to be deciding whether to say it aloud.

"Don't," Ines said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"Whatever it is, don't. I've had a long evening. I drank hemlock four hours ago. I would like to finish my sausage and go to sleep and tomorrow I will report you to the gate captain, and this will be an odd story I tell myself on bad nights."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then, gently, almost apologetically, she read:

"*And the boy shall know the song his mother sang, though the mother is ash and the song was buried with her, for the soul remembers what the child was made to forget.*"

The room went very quiet.

Ines's mother had been dead for twenty-three years. She had been dead for twenty-three years, and in all that time he had told exactly one person about her—one cellmate, one winter, one confession traded for warmth—and he had never told anyone the lullaby. Had never sung it, never hummed it, had barely allowed himself to think it, because thinking it opened a door in his chest that he had nailed shut at nine years old on the far side of the Thornfell mountains, in the dark, with snow coming down, when everything else was taken and this one small thing—

This one small thing he had kept.

Except he hadn't. Apparently. Because a dead scholar's book, a hundred years old, sitting on a taster's narrow bed, claimed to know it.

"What song," Ines said. His voice came out steady, which he counted as the finest performance of his career.

Odile Vane studied his face with the patience of a woman who had spent years coaxing secrets out of reluctant manuscripts. Then she closed the book, wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and held it against her chest like a shield.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight you should sleep on it, and tomorrow you should meet me where I can show you the rest. Because the passage continues, Master Galloway, and the next lines describe a flight through the Thornfell passes—a route, it says, that only one living person knows." She moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "And the lines after that describe armies. Armies marching on the free cities, led by a man who wants what sleeps below the Spire."

"And what sleeps below the Spire?"

Odile opened the door onto the dark corridor. When she answered, she didn't turn around.

"According to the book? You do."
