## 2. What the Book Remembers

Odile Vane's rooms above the chandler's shop smelled of tallow and old paper, and Ines had decided within the first minute that he hated both.

"Sit," she said, not looking up from the table. She'd cleared it of everything except the book—a slab of vellum-bound something, its covers dark with age, its pages guarded by brass clasps that had been pried open so recently the metal still showed bright scratches. "And don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to touch anything."

"You were reaching."

He withdrew his hand. Outside the narrow window, the capital went about its evening: cart wheels on cobbles, a fishwife shouting prices, the bell of the low quarter ringing the hour. A city he'd saved last night with nothing more than his tongue and a swallow of poisoned wine, and which would never know it. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement.

Odile turned a page with the delicacy of a surgeon. "The scribe who made this was called Aldous Merrin. Court scholar, a hundred years dead. He wrote in cipher for the parts that mattered and plain hand for the parts he thought no one would ever read." Her finger traced a column of cramped script. "Last night I showed you the passage naming you. Today I've broken the surrounding cipher. You're going to want to sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"Ines." She looked up then, and her eyes behind their smudged spectacles were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "Sit down."

He sat. The stool wobbled. Everything in this room wobbled; Odile Vane had been expelled from the Royal Athenaeum two years ago for heresy or forgery or asking too many questions—in the retellings, the crime changed—and her lodgings reflected a woman who expected to flee at any hour. One packed satchel by the door. A bedroll instead of a bed. A single cup on the shelf, because a second cup implied company that stayed.

She turned the book toward him.

The page was dense with Merrin's cipher, but threaded through it ran lines of plain script, ink gone brown as dried blood:

*The Galloway boy flees north through Thornfell in the winter of his ninth year. He passes the Hanging Stones by moonless night. He crosses the frozen fall at Greywater where the ice sings under his weight. He is pursued, and he is not caught, for the mountain itself has been paid to look away.*

Ines read it twice. His mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with hemlock.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's a smuggler's route. The Hanging Stones, the frozen fall—those are passes my father used. Passes *I* used, later. Nobody writes those down. That's the entire point of them."

"Merrin didn't learn them from a smuggler." Odile's voice was very even, the voice of someone handling a live coal. "Read the next line."

*What the boy fled with, he does not know he carries. What was taken from him sleeps below the Spire, in the vault of names, and it will sleep until the mountain wakes.*

"The Spire," Ines said flatly. "The old watchtower on Thornfell's shoulder? There's nothing below the Spire. I've drunk in the tavern at its base. It's a cellar and some bad wine."

"The Spire isn't a tower, Ines. It's a chimney." She tapped the page. "A vent for what's underneath. And every account, every fragment, every half-mad marginal note I've chased for six years says the same thing: there is a court beneath Thornfell. Old beyond old. And it buys."

"Buys what?"

"Souls." She said it the way she might have said *grain* or *tin*. "Small pieces. Pledged in desperation, collected in full. And according to Merrin, a piece of yours has been sitting in their vault since you were nine years old."

Ines laughed. It came out wrong—too loud, too short. "I think I'd remember selling my soul."

"Would you?" Odile removed her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a scholar's pause, buying time. "You were nine. You were fleeing through a mountain pass in winter with your mother. People make bargains in extremity that they bury so deep they build whole lives on top of the grave." She set the spectacles back on. "You told me yourself you can't remember your mother's face."

The words landed like a swallowed stone.

Because it was true, and he had never said it aloud to anyone, and he could not now reconstruct having said it. Except—he must have. Some night over wine, some debrief after a tasting, some moment when Odile's questions had slipped past his guard the way questions did when you forgot she was a scholar first and a friend only incidentally.

"No," he said carefully. "I remember her hands. I remember her voice. Faces go. Faces go for everyone."

"Faces go," Odile agreed. "But listen to me. Merrin's cipher has a final layer. A verification, he called it—the proof that the prophecy concerns a real child and not an allegory. I cracked it an hour before I sent for you." She turned the page.

The bottom third of the leaf held a single passage, transcribed in a different hand—smaller, shakier, as if written by someone listening rather than composing:

*The proof is a song. The mother sang it on the ice at Greywater, and the ice carried it down, and the court below heard it and knew the child above all other children. The song has no name. It goes:*

And then, in that small shaking hand, a string of notes rendered in an old notation Ines almost recognized—until he realized he didn't need to recognize it at all, because the melody rose off the page and into his skull fully formed, note for note, the way a scar rises when weather changes.

*Sleep, my ember, the fire keeps.*
*Down where the deep roots creep,*
*I have hidden what is mine to keep,*
*sleep, my ember, the fire keeps.*

The room tilted.

Not metaphorically. The floor leaned, the candle flame stretched long and yellow, and Ines gripped the edge of the table because the alternative was gripping nothing. He could hear it—not remembered, *heard*—a woman's voice, low and smoke-roughened, singing against wind. Snow ticking against wool. The groan of ice under boots. He was nine years old and his mother's mittened hand was around his and the world was white noise and cold light and—

"*Stop,*" he said, and didn't know if he'd said it aloud.

Odile had risen. Her hand hovered near his shoulder without landing. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing." He was not breathing well. He pushed back from the table, put distance between himself and the book, and stood with his back against the chandler's wall, among the smells of tallow and honey, breathing. "That's a trick. Some scholar's trick. You found a lullaby from the region, from the era, and you—"

"Ines. Sing me another verse."

"There isn't another verse. It's four lines. It was always four lines." The words left him before he understood what he'd admitted. He heard them hang in the air, damning. Four lines. Always four lines. As if *always* were a thing he could know.

Odile sat back down slowly. She didn't gloat. He wished to God she would gloat; gloating he could fight.

"My mother died when I was eleven," he said, to the wall, because the wall asked fewer questions. "Fever, in the free city where we landed. I was told this. I was told this by the people who took me in, and I believed it, because children believe the shape of the story they're handed." His jaw worked. "I don't remember the fever. I don't remember a funeral. I remember her hands and her voice and four lines of a song, and I have spent twenty years assuming grief simply ate the rest."

"And now?"

"Now I think the story I was handed had holes in it." He turned. The book sat on the table like a wound. "You said the court below *buys*. What does a piece of a soul buy, exactly?"

"Survival," Odile said quietly. "Passage. A mountain paid to look away while a boy and his mother cross through the worst winter in a generation. And perhaps—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide to say it, watched her choose truth over mercy the way she always did, eventually, "—perhaps forgetting. Perhaps the price included the memory of the bargain itself. Which would explain why you remember everything *around* your childhood and almost nothing *of* it. The Hanging Stones, yes. Greywater, yes. Your mother's face—"

"Gone."

"—and the years between nine and eleven, gone. The fever, the funeral, gone. You weren't robbed by grief, Ines. You were *edited*."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog began barking at nothing, a thin frantic sound, and Ines thought: dogs bark at things coming up out of the ground sometimes. Old stories said so. He'd always filed those stories with the rest of the nonsense a smuggler collects and never spends.

He crossed back to the table. Looked down at the passage. *It will sleep until the mountain wakes.* 

"What wakes it?"

"Merrin doesn't say. But the usurper's inquisitors burned three archives in the southern provinces this spring, and every one of them held copies of Merrin's work. They're not hunting the book, Ines. They're hunting the *subject*. Someone above ground knows what sleeps below the Spire, and wants it before anyone else can reach it." She met his eyes. "They'll come here. For the book, and then for you. Days, not weeks—I've seen how they move."

"So I run. I'm good at running. I know passes the inquisitors' maps have never heard of."

"And leave the thing they want sitting in a vault under a mountain, powering whatever they intend to power with it?" Odile shook her head. "A soul isn't a trinket. Merrin's notes call it an ember—the seed of what you were meant to become. In the wrong hands, it's not just yours anymore. It's a key."

Outside, the evening bells rang again, though it hadn't been an hour. Ines counted the intervals out of habit, the way he counted everything—drips, footsteps, heartbeats—and the count was wrong. Off by eleven minutes. He frowned.

The dog had stopped barking.

"Odile," he said slowly. "How many entrances does this building have?"

Her face changed. Twenty years of reading courts and poisoners and men who lied for a living had taught Ines what fear looked like when it arrived ahead of understanding, and it looked exactly like Odile Vane going very still over a hundred-year-old book.

"Two," she said. "Front stairs, and the chandler's yard."

"Then we have a problem." He was already moving, snuffing the candle, sliding the book into the satchel by the door with a thief's economy he hadn't known he possessed until this moment. "Because the yard gate was open when I came in. I assumed carelessness."

"It's never open. Marta chains it at dusk."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the chandler's door opening, and a voice—oily, official, patient—asking Marta the chandler whether anyone had visited the scholar upstairs tonight.

Ines blew out the last candle and took Odile's arm in the dark.

"Back way," he whispered. "Now. And Odile—if we get out of this city, you're telling me everything else that book remembers. Every word."

In the blackness, he felt her nod.

* * *

They went down the back stair with the book between them, past Marta's startled face, into the yard where the chain hung cut and gleaming, and Ines understood, with the cold certainty of a man who has tasted poison often enough to know its arrival, that the life he had built out of being nobody had just ended.

Somewhere behind them, up the front stairs, a fist began knocking on Odile's door—polite, measured, inevitable.

The mountains were three days north.

Ines ran.
