## 3. Roads Through Thornfell

The inquisitors came at dawn, which told Ines they were professionals. Amateurs came at midnight, when fear made men loud. Professionals came when the guards were changing, when the kitchens were half-staffed, when a man who had spent nine years making himself furniture would be hardest to find and easiest to kill.

He was already on the roof.

He'd heard them in the courtyard below his window—the particular cadence of boots that didn't pretend to be anything but boots—and he'd taken the servant's stair to the laundry line with the calm of a man fetching linens. Now he lay flat against cold slate tiles, watching four grey-cloaked figures enter the servants' wing through the door he'd left politely unlocked. Let them search his room. There was nothing there. That was the whole art of Ines Galloway: nothing anywhere, no letters, no keepsakes, no name worth carving into anything.

Except, of course, in a dead scholar's book.

*The Galloway boy.* Odile Vane's voice still itched in his ear from last night, that fevered whisper over candlelight: *It names you. It names tomorrow.*

Tomorrow had arrived wearing grey cloaks.

He gave them an hour. Long enough to tear apart his mattress and find the hollow where a braver man would have hidden something. Then he went down the far side of the palace, through the tanner's yard where nobody asked questions because everybody had answers they liked better, and out through the fish gate, carrying a market basket he'd filled with turnips for authenticity.

By noon he was three miles past the walls, and by dusk the capital was a smudge of woodsmoke behind him, and the Thornfell mountains stood up ahead like the jawbone of some god that had died mid-sentence.

Everyone knew the royal road through Thornfell. Everyone knew the two great passes, the toll stations, the garrisons. That was precisely why Ines didn't use them. He knew a third way—a smuggler's cut called the Shepherd's Tooth—because nine years ago a dying smuggler had traded it to him for a cup of water and someone to hold his hand while he stopped. It was the only inheritance Ines had ever accepted, and he'd never once told anyone it existed.

Some things you kept even when you had nothing else.

* * *

The pass began as a goat track and got worse. By the second day it was a ledge barely wider than his shoulders, stitched along the mountain's flank above a valley full of cloud. The air tasted of stone and old snow. Ines moved the way he did everything: carefully, unremarkably, a man whose survival depended on never being interesting.

He thought about Odile Vane more than he wanted to. Her hands shaking around that book. *Your soul sleeps below the Spire,* she'd said, as if souls were coins left under a mattress. He'd laughed at her. He remembers laughing. What he remembered less clearly was why his hands had been so steady afterward, the way hands go steady after a blow lands—not from courage, but because the worst thing has already happened and all that's left is arithmetic.

A boy sells something he doesn't understand to survive the mountain. A man spends nine years not asking what.

Below him, on the valley floor, he saw the army.

Not marching—camping. Fires in their hundreds, laid out in the neat geometry of the usurper's legions, the black-and-silver banners he'd seen only from the palace ramparts before. They were moving toward the free cities. Toward everything east of Thornfell. And strung out behind them, on the switchback road he could see from this height, supply wagons and—his stomach tightened—wagons with cages.

He didn't let himself think about what the cages were for. Prophecy-zealots took prisoners. So did inquisitors. Neither group was famous for needing a reason.

He climbed faster.

* * *

He found her on the fourth day, or she found him—it was hard to say who ambushed whom.

He'd stopped at a spring beneath an overhang, cupping water in his hands, when a voice said, "One more step and I put a knife in your thigh," from somewhere above and behind him, which meant she'd been watching him walk into this spot for a long time and had chosen the moment with professional patience.

Ines stayed very still. "I'm just drinking."

"Nobody's just drinking in Thornfell."

"Fair." He turned his head slowly. She was wedged into a crack in the rock twenty feet up: young, mud-colored clothes, a soldier's short hair growing out ragged, a knife held low and correct. Desertion was written on her the way livery was written on palace guards—in the shoulders, in the flinching readiness. "You're a long way from any banner."

"And you're a long way from anywhere at all." She came down the rock in a controlled slide, knife still loose in her grip, and then she saw his face properly for the first time, and the knife changed its mind about being casual. "Saints' teeth."

"I get that sometimes."

"You're him." She said it flatly, not with awe—with the weary disgust of a woman confirming bad paperwork. "The sketches. They posted them through the whole column three days ago. *The Galloway boy, take alive, reward of titles and land.*" She spat. "Half my company deserted over that bounty alone."

"Then I'm sorry for your company."

"Don't be. They were the usurper's." She lowered the knife a fraction, studying him with eyes that had done a lot of counting. "Dara," she said finally, as if giving a name were a tactical concession. "Dara Vess. Late of the Third Legion, which no longer exists because I burned its muster roll and walked out."

"Why?"

She looked at him like he'd asked why water was wet. "Because of the cages."

Ah. So she'd seen them too.

"They're not taking zealots," Dara said. "They're taking anyone the prophecy-people have ever touched. Villages that keep shrines. Book-sellers. Children who can recite verses." Her jaw worked. "There's a wagon master who pays by the head. I decided I wasn't going to be paid by the head anymore."

Ines crouched and drank again, buying time. Every instinct he owned said: leave her. She's a liability with a knife and a conscience, and consciences are heavier than armor. Nine years of invisibility whispered their usual sermon: *alone is safe, alone is quiet, alone is alive.*

But she'd seen the sketches and hadn't sold him yet. That was data. And she knew the disposition of the usurper's armies, which was more than he knew, and the passes below were filling with grey cloaks hunting a Galloway boy.

"The road down," he said. "Which way were you heading?"

"Away."

"That's not a direction in Thornfell."

"It's the only direction I've trusted in a year." But she was looking at him differently now—not at the face from the sketch, but at the man drinking water like it might be poisoned, checking the wind, mapping exits out of habit. "You know a way through. Don't you. Nobody walks this high without knowing a way through."

Ines weighed it. The Shepherd's Tooth had been his alone for nine years. Saying it aloud felt like opening a vein.

"There's a pass," he said. "It's not on maps because I've never put it on one."

"And you'd show me."

"I'd show someone who knows how to be quiet and doesn't want me dead. Those are rare qualifications lately."

Dara almost smiled. It looked like it hurt her. "I had a squad once. Twelve people. I remember all their names." She shouldered her pack. "You don't have to trust me. Just walk ahead of me where I can see you, and we'll call it even."

That, Ines thought, was the most honest offer anyone had made him in years.

* * *

They walked three days together before either of them said anything true.

It happened at night, in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, with a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Dara had produced salt meat from her pack and split it without being asked—an act so ordinary it landed on Ines like a blow. When had anyone last fed him without wanting something? The palace fed him daily, and every meal was a transaction: his tongue for their safety.

"My mother used to sing," he heard himself say, and immediately regretted it, because he had never told anyone that, because he had buried it so deep he'd assumed it was gone.

Dara didn't pounce. She just turned the meat on her knife and waited, the way soldiers wait, understanding that silence is a door people open themselves.

"I don't remember the song," Ines said. "I remember that there was one. Isn't that a strange thing? To lose the song and keep the losing."

"No," Dara said quietly. "I remember my whole muster roll and I can't remember my sister's face. Same disease."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing.

"The book says my soul is under the Spire," Ines said. It sounded insane in the open air, away from Odile's candlelit certainty. "Sold. When I was a child. And the usurper wants it, and there are men in grey cloaks who want it, and zealots who want to crown it, and I spent nine years making sure nobody would ever look twice at me, and now I'm the most looked-at boy in the kingdom."

"What will you do?"

"I'm going to steal it back." He said it and was surprised to find it was true—that somewhere between the rooftop and this fire, the arithmetic had finished, and the answer was theft. "It's mine. Whatever it is. It's the only thing I've ever owned."

Dara nodded slowly, and then she did something he didn't expect. She reached over and added another stick to the fire, making it slightly larger, slightly easier to see.

"If they're hunting you," she said, "then every day you run is a day you're worth more than their whole payroll. You're not invisible anymore, Galloway. Might as well be warm."

He stared at the flames. Invisible had been his armor for nine years. He had built it stick by stick, lie by lie, and it had kept him alive, and it had also kept him *nothing*—a tongue, a function, a piece of furniture that tasted soup.

And here was a deserter he'd known four days, feeding the fire so he could be seen.

"We move at first light," he said. "There's a place past the divide—a free city called Cinderhollow. If anyone knows what's written in that book, they'll be there."

"Why there?"

"Because it's where people go who don't exist," Ines said. "And I hear it has excellent thieves."

Dara snorted, and for the first time since the capital, Ines slept without dreaming of grey cloaks.

He dreamed instead of a lullaby he couldn't hear, sung by a voice he couldn't see, in a room beneath a mountain—and woke at dawn with the strange, certain feeling that somewhere ahead of him, someone else remembered the words.
