## 4. The Thief Who Knew Him

Wren had three rules for safehouses: never sleep in the same one twice in a month, never tell anyone the full address, and never open the door before dawn.

She broke the third rule at half past midnight because the knock came in fours and a half — two quick, two slow, one dragged — and only one person alive knew that rhythm, because she'd invented it with him when they were seven years old, tapping it through a crack in a wall that no longer existed.

She stood with her hand on the bolt for a long time, telling herself it was coincidence. Smugglers had rhythms. Soldiers had rhythms. Half the underworld tapped out codes on doors like children drumming on tables.

Then the knock came again, impatient now, and underneath it a voice said, "I know you're awake. The lamp's lit."

Ines Galloway's voice. Older, rougher, sanded down by years of swallowing other people's poisons — but hers.

Wren opened the door.

* * *

He looked worse than the sketches. The wanted posters made him gaunt and dramatic, all sharp angles and hunted eyes, a man shaped by prophecy. The real thing was smaller than that, wearier, a man who'd learned to fold himself into doorways so thoroughly he'd started to believe he belonged there. His coat was good but mended badly. His boots were wrong for the mountains. He flinched at the light from her lamp the way a man flinches at a debt collector.

Behind him crowded two others: a tall woman with a deserter's cropped hair and a soldier's way of standing — weight even, hands loose, always half-turned toward the dark — and an older scholar wrapped in a travel cloak that had been fine twenty years ago, clutching a book to her chest like a breastplate.

"You're supposed to be dead," Wren said.

"Everyone I've ever met is supposed to be dead," Ines said. "It's how I know I'm popular."

The deserter snorted. The scholar didn't; she was staring past Wren into the room, cataloguing exits, Wren guessed, or maybe just cataloguing Wren herself.

"You brought people," Wren said. "To my house. That you found by following me. Which I did not invite."

"There wasn't time for invitations." He glanced down the street, and she saw it then — the real fear under the dryness, banked but not buried. "Can we come in or do you want to have this argument in the open?"

She wanted the argument. She wanted it very much, wanted to stand in the doorway and make him say her name — his name for her, the old one, the one nobody living should know — before she gave him anything, including shelter.

But the street was empty in the particular way streets are empty when someone has paid for them to be empty, and Wren had survived thirty years in the free cities by knowing which silences were natural.

She stepped aside. "Two minutes. Then everyone explains everything."

* * *

The safehouse was a cobbler's attic above a shop that hadn't opened in a decade, rented in the name of a woman who had never existed. Wren put water on to boil because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and made them sit on the floor around the cold hearth because the chairs were all hers and she wasn't sharing.

The deserter introduced herself as Dara Vess, late of the usurper's Third Column, currently of nowhere. The scholar was Odile Vane, formerly of the Royal Archive, formerly employed, formerly respectable. She set the book on the floor between them with the care of a priest laying down relics.

"I know what that is," Wren said.

Odile's eyebrows went up. "Do you."

"It's been following me for a month. Copies of pages, mostly. A man in Corvane offered me forty crowns to steal the original from a collector's vault." She shrugged. "I took the job. Then I read the pages and decided some books are bad luck, and bad luck is the one client I don't work for."

"And yet," Odile said softly, "here you are. In a city on the road to Thornfell. Holding a safehouse for a man named in its margins."

"That's not—" Wren stopped. Looked at Ines. He was watching her with the careful blankness of a man who has already heard something tonight that broke him and is waiting to find out if she'll break him again.

"He told us nothing," Dara said. "For the record. We're not here because of anything he said."

"No," Wren agreed. "You're here because of what the book says. And what does the book say about me?"

"Nothing," Odile said. "That's rather the point. It says a great deal about him."

* * *

They told her. All of it, in pieces, interrupting each other — the hemlock, the date in the margin, the lullaby, the passage about the soul sleeping below the Spire, the inquisitors burning through the capital like a fever through a body. Wren listened with her arms crossed and her face arranged into professional boredom, and inside she was doing arithmetic.

A boy sold to the court beneath the mountain. A flight through Thornfell. A mother's lullaby that no archive could have known.

She'd known since the knock. She'd known since the rhythm, four-and-a-half, tap-tap-tap-tap-taaap, the code of two children who'd lived in the row houses below the quarry road, whose mothers had both gone into the mountain in the same winter and neither come back. But knowing a thing and saying it were different trades entirely, and Wren had spent her whole life learning the difference.

"So," she said, when they finished. "You're the Galloway boy."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Recognition, arriving late, like thunder after lightning. "You've heard that name."

"Once or twice."

"From whom?"

"Does it matter?"

"Yes," he said, and his voice cracked open a little, the blankness failing. "It matters more than almost anything. Say it. Say the whole thing, if you know it."

And there it was. The moment she'd been dreading since the door, the reason she'd nearly left him on the step. Because she knew what came next, and she knew what it would cost him, and she knew — this was the worst part — that she was going to do it anyway, because she'd spent twenty-five years carrying a name that belonged to someone else and she was tired, bone-tired, of being its only keeper.

"Wren," Dara said quietly, watching her face. "What is it?"

"The book's wrong about one thing," Wren said. "Or incomplete. It calls him the Galloway boy, like Galloway was the whole of him." She looked at Ines. "Galloway was your mother's name. Your father's was Ashgrave. And you weren't Ines then, not exactly — your mother called you—"

"Don't." His voice came out flat and hard. "Don't do this. You read it somewhere. Someone talked. There are a hundred ways you could—"

"Ines." She used the new name deliberately, a hand raised palm-out. "Your mother sang you a song about a wren building a nest in a spire. Do you remember why?"

His jaw tightened. Silence.

"Because you couldn't sleep unless she sang it, and she got tired of singing the same six lines every night, so she started changing the words, and one night she sang about a wren instead of whatever bird it was originally, and you laughed so hard you fell out of bed. And after that she never sang the original again. She said the song belonged to you now, and you'd renamed it yourself, the way you renamed everything." Wren's throat was tight. "You were a renaming child. You renamed our street. You renamed the neighbor's dog. You renamed me."

The fire had gone out of him. He sat very still, the way men sit when the ground has opened under them and they haven't decided whether to fall.

"What was my name?" he asked. Barely a sound.

"Tam," Wren said. "Tam Ashgrave. And I'm not Wren because of any bird. I'm Wren because you named me. We were five. You said I was small and loud and lived in everybody's walls, and I hit you, and you said see, exactly, and we were friends until the mountain took us both." She swallowed. "Different doors, same mountain. Your mother went under for debt. Mine went under for theft. Neither came back. You ran north. I stayed and got good at taking things, and every year on the first frost I go back to the quarry road and check whether the row houses are still standing, and every year they're a little less standing, and every year I think — someday he'll come back, and I'll be the only person alive who knows his name, and isn't that the cruelest kind of inheritance."

Nobody spoke. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere below, the dead cobbler's shop settled against the night.

"That's not possible," Ines said finally. "I don't remember any of it. Not the street, not the dog, not—" His hand rose halfway to his mouth and stopped. "Not the song. I know the song. Odile read it to me from a book written before I was born, and I knew it, I knew it line by line, and I told myself it was coincidence, a common melody, anything—" He looked at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him. "Why would I remember the song and nothing else? Why would I remember nothing else?"

"Because you paid," Wren said gently. "That's what they take first, down there. Haven't you figured that out yet? They don't sell you your life for free. They sell it to you in installments, and the interest is everything that made the life worth buying. You kept the song because it was the last thing you held onto going through the door. Everything else went into their vault." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Gods, Tam. You walked out of that mountain with one memory and built a whole man around it, and you did such a good job that you convinced yourself the man was the original."

"Don't call me that."

"Which? Tam?"

"Either." He was on his feet now, moving, a caged animal testing walls. "You don't get to just — hand me a childhood. I've spent twenty years making sure I didn't have one. Do you know what that's worth? Do you know what it costs a man to have no past? Nobody can leverage it. Nobody can hunt it. Nobody can—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "Nobody can take it away twice."

Dara stood too, slowly, the way you'd approach a spooked horse. "Ines."

"He's not real," Ines said. "Tam Ashgrave. He died in the mountain. What's left is Ines Galloway, and Ines Galloway doesn't need a—"

"He hums it," Dara said suddenly.

Everyone turned.

"On the road," Dara said. "When he thinks I'm asleep. Six notes over and over. I thought it was nothing. A habit." Her voice gentled. "It's not nothing, is it. It's her. It's the part of her they let you keep."

Ines sat back down. It wasn't a decision; his legs simply ended their argument with him. He put his head in his hands, and for a long moment the only sound in the attic was the kettle, and beneath it, faintly, involuntarily, six notes hummed against his palms like a pulse.

Wren watched him and grieved. That was the thing nobody warned you about, finding someone you'd mourned: you didn't get the reunion. You got a stranger wearing the shape of your grief, and you had to introduce yourself to him, and watch him flinch from his own name, and understand that the boy you remembered was realer to you than to anyone else alive — including him. She had carried Tam Ashgrave for twenty-five years like a stolen jewel she couldn't fence and couldn't return. Now the owner was sitting in front of her, refusing delivery.

She sat down beside him. Didn't touch him. Just close enough that he'd know she was there.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the dog was called Turnip. You renamed him Duke. He bit you. You deserved it."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh, strangled at birth. "Turnip."

"Duke."

"Why would I—" He shook his head. "Why would I rename a dog that had already bitten me?"

"Because you were a renaming child," Wren said. "You thought names were things you could fix. You thought if you called a thing something better, it might become better." She looked at the book on the floor, at the prophecy coiled in its margins like a snake in grass. "You were wrong about that. Mostly."

Odile leaned forward. "There's more in the passage. About the Spire. About what sleeps below it, and what wakes when it's taken back."

"I know what sleeps below it," Wren said. "I've been dreaming about it since Corvane. A little ember, in a vault, wearing his name." She met Ines's eyes. "The question isn't whether it's yours. The question is whether you want it back — and what they'll do to keep it."

Before he could answer, the bell above the dead shop's door chimed.

All four of them went still. Nobody had keys to that door except Wren, and Wren's keys were in her pocket, and Wren was upstairs.

Through the floorboards, soft as snowfall, came the sound of many feet arranging themselves outside. Patient. Practiced. A lot of them.

"They found me," Odile whispered, and pulled the book against her chest. "The zealots. I led them straight to—" She looked up, stricken, at each of them in turn. "I led them everywhere."
