## 5. Zealots at the Gate

Odile Vane had spent thirty years learning to read the past, and in all that time she had never once managed to read the present. It was why, when the knocking began at the safehouse door at dusk—soft, patient, wrong—she was the last of them to understand what it meant.

She was at the table with the prophecy book open under a shuttered lamp, transcribing marginalia into her notebook by candlelight because the lamp oil was Wren's and Wren counted everything. Ines stood at the window slit with his back to the room, watching the street below in the way he watched everything: as if he were tasting it for poison. Dara was cleaning a sword that had no business being clean already. Wren was asleep in the corner, boots on, which Odile had learned was not carelessness but readiness.

The knock came again. Three soft raps. A pause. Three more.

"That's a signal," Dara said quietly, without looking up from the blade.

"It's a knock," Odile said.

"It's *their* knock." Ines turned from the window. His face in the lamplight was the face of a man doing arithmetic he hated. "Three and three. The beggars' cant. It means *we know you're in there*."

Wren was awake between one breath and the next, sitting up with her knife already loose in her hand, and Odile marveled—as she marveled nightly—at how quickly that girl moved through the world, like water finding a crack.

"Who's we?" Wren asked.

"The people who've been asking after us since the fish market," Ines said. "The ones who aren't wearing uniforms. I told you someone was counting doors."

"You said maybe."

"I said *someone was counting doors*, Wren."

Below, in the street, voices had begun to gather. Not many at first—a handful, then more, the way water rises in a hold before you notice the hole. Odile went to the window slit beside Ines and looked down through the gap.

They carried candles. That was the detail that lodged in her chest like a fishhook: dozens of small flames moving through the blue evening, held up by ordinary faces—shopkeepers' faces, washerwomen's faces, a boy of perhaps twelve holding his candle with both hands as though it were something holy. And they were singing. Low, at first, almost gentle. A hymn without words she recognized until she did recognize it, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

It was the lullaby. The one from the book. The one only Ines's dead mother had sung, set now to harmony, passed mouth to mouth through the free city like a contagion.

"They've made it a song," Odile whispered.

"They've made *me* a song," Ines said. He did not sound flattered. He sounded like a man listening to his own funeral bells.

* * *

By full dark there were two hundred of them, and the safehouse door shook on its hinges when they pressed against it—not to break it, Odile realized, which was somehow worse, but to lean their weight against it in prayer, as if the building itself might open and deliver its miracle.

She sat at the table with the book and forced herself to think. This was what she was for. Not swords, not locks, not Wren's talent for vanishing through walls. Thinking. Thirty years of thinking in archives while better braver people did whatever it was brave people did.

"Talk to me," she said. "All of you. Quickly."

"Front door's done," Dara said. "Two hundred bodies, even peaceful ones, is a wall."

"Roof?" Wren said. "There's a drainage run behind the chimney. I've used it before. It lets out into the tannery yard."

"Tannery yard has one gate," Ines said. "And I'd bet my tongue—which used to be worth something—that there are more of them there. They're not trying to catch us. They're trying to *herd* us somewhere they can kneel at us."

"So we don't go where they want," Odile said slowly. "We go where nobody wants. Where would that be?"

Wren and Ines looked at each other, and Odile watched an entire conversation happen between them in the space of a second—the kind of shorthand that forms between people who grew up in the same darkness, even if only one of them remembered it.

"The cellars," they said together.

"There's a well shaft," Wren said, "under the old wine cellar. My maps say it drops into the deep tunnels. The ones from before the city. Before the mountain, maybe."

"Nobody goes down there," Ines said. "That's the point of them."

"And the point of us," Odile said, closing the book, "is going where nobody goes."

From below came a new sound: singing turning to chanting, chanting turning to hammering, and a voice crying out over all of it, young and fever-bright:

*"Bring forth the Galloway boy! Bring forth the ember of the Spire! Crown him, crown him, crown the king beneath the mountain!"*

Ines closed his eyes. "They keep saying that," he said. "King beneath the mountain. Like it's a good thing."

* * *

The well shaft was exactly as terrible as it sounded.

Dara went first with a rope and a lamp, testing each iron rung set into the stone, calling up "firm," "firm," "loose, skip it," in a soldier's flat voice that steadied them all more than any comfort could have. Wren went second, light as a rumor. Then Odile, whose knees informed her at length and in detail what they thought of this plan. Ines came last, and before he descended he paused at the lip of the shaft and looked back up at the cellar ceiling, through which the muffled thunder of two hundred worshippers rolled like weather.

"Odile," he said.

"Yes?"

"When we're down here. When it's quiet. You're going to tell me the rest of it. What the book says. All of it, not the parts you've been reading aloud."

"I've read you everything relevant—"

"You've read me everything *kind*," Ines said. "I taste for a living. Don't try to sweeten my cup."

Then he dropped into the dark beside her, and there was nothing to do but climb down after him.

* * *

The deep tunnels were older than the free cities, older than the kingdom, older than anything Odile had a name for. The lamp showed walls of black stone veined with something that glimmered faintly when the flame passed, like the memory of gold. The air was warm. That was the worst part, Odile decided—the warmth, faint and steady, rising from somewhere far below, as if the whole mountain were a sleeping animal and they were walking down its throat.

They walked for hours. Dara counted paces and marked turns with chalk. Wren navigated by a map drawn from a childhood she claimed not to trust, muttering "left at the split, no—right, it was right, I can *hear* it was right"—and every time she corrected herself, Ines nodded as if some part of him agreed.

Finally they stopped in a wide chamber where a broken stair climbed toward a collapsed archway, and Dara declared it defensible, and they ate hard bread and dried apples in silence.

It was Ines who broke it. "Well? I'm listening."

Odile set down her bread. She had rehearsed this speech a hundred times across three decades of silence, and now that it was demanded of her, every word of it crumbled.

"The passage you haven't heard," she said, "because I haven't spoken it, because speaking it felt like—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a verse about the breaking. About what happens when the soul comes out of the Spire. The book gives two readings. In the first, the Galloway boy takes his soul back whole, and the power in it crowns him. King beneath the mountain. The zealots have that half right."

"And the second reading?" Dara asked.

"In the second, the soul is destroyed. Shattered. And the verse says—" Odile's voice failed her, and she hated herself for it, and pushed through anyway. "The verse says the shattering will be paid for in the blood of everyone who stands near him. Everyone who helped him. The book names it *the tithe of friends*."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Odile sat in it and let it press on her, because she deserved the pressing.

"That's why you didn't say it," Ines said at last. Not angry. Worse—understanding.

"That's why I didn't say it."

"You've known since the capital."

"Since before the capital. Since I first translated it, twenty years ago, when it was an academic curiosity and I was a fool who thought prophecies stayed in books." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you understand what I did, Ines? I found the book. I brought it into the light. I read it aloud in a room with a man who later sold what he learned to the usurper's people. Every inquisitor hunting you, every zealot singing that lullaby in the street—they are all downstream of me. I opened the book, and the hunt walked out of it."

"Odile." Dara's voice, low and even. "You didn't write it."

"I translated it. There's no difference. A key doesn't get to say it isn't guilty of the theft."

Wren, curled against the warm black wall, spoke without opening her eyes. "My mother sold me to the mountain for six silver pieces and a sack of flour. Should I have burned the flour?"

"That's not—"

"It's exactly that," Wren said. "Things get used badly. Doesn't make the using yours."

But Odile barely heard her, because Ines had leaned forward into the lamplight, and his eyes had gone to the place past her shoulder, to the broken stair and the collapsed archway beyond it, and his face had changed.

"What's below us," he said quietly. "How far does this tunnel system go?"

"Down," Wren said. "All the way down. Why?"

"Because it's warm," Ines said. "It's been warm since the second hour of walking, and tunnels don't breathe, Odile. Mountains don't breathe." He rose, slow, and put his palm flat against the black stone, and when he took it away his hand was shining faintly, as if the rock had left a little of itself on his skin. "Something down there knows we're coming. Something down there has been waiting a long time for the boy named in the book—and I don't think it's the usurper."

Far below them, so deep it might have been imagination, something answered: a single note, sustained, patient, unmistakably a voice.

It was humming the lullaby.
