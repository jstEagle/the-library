## 6. Beneath the Mountain

Wren's maps were drawn on the backs of playing cards, forty-one of them, strung together with waxed thread like a grisly rosary. She dealt them out on the tunnel floor by lamplight and studied them the way a gambler studies a losing hand.

"Okay," she said. "So. Some of these are wrong."

"Some," Odile repeated.

"I was seven." Wren tapped a card showing a staircase that spiraled into what appeared to be a fish. "The mountain looked different when you're that tall. Also I may have drawn some of these from memory of *dreams*, which in my defense were very vivid dreams."

Dara leaned over her shoulder. "This one's a picture of a dog."

"That's the Long Gallery."

"It's a dog."

"The Long Gallery is shaped like a dog. You'll see."

Ines said nothing. He had spent three days underground now, following a thief's childhood nightmares through passages that smelled of cold stone and older cold things, and he had learned that arguing with Wren about the map was like arguing with the weather. You endured it. You kept walking.

They descended.

* * *

The tunnels changed on the fourth day.

It wasn't dramatic. There was no wall of heat, no sudden gate. The stone simply began to be *finished*. Rough-hewn walls gave way to fitted blocks; the fitted blocks gave way to tile; the tile gave way to mosaic, tiny glass squares laid in patterns of vines and birds, still bright after who knew how many centuries, because nothing down here rotted or faded or died.

"Someone keeps this place," Dara murmured, running a thumb along a mosaic wren—Wren snorted at that—"like a house."

"Someone *lives* here," Odile corrected. She hadn't slept much. She walked with the prophecy book strapped to her chest like armor, and consulted it more than she'd admit. "The court below isn't a ruin. It's a *household*. It has staff. It has accounts." Her voice did something complicated on the word *accounts*.

"What kind of accounts?" Ines asked, though he already knew. He'd known since the lullaby passage. He'd known since he was six years old and something in the dark had asked him what he would give to survive the winter, and he—a starving child with a dead mother and no name anyone wanted—had answered too quickly.

Odile didn't answer him. That was its own answer.

* * *

They heard the market before they saw it: a sound like wind chimes made of teeth, thousands of small ringing notes overlapping, rising from a crevasse ahead where the tunnel opened without warning into—

Light.

Not torchlight. Not the gray seep of glowmoss. Light like a city at festival, gold and warm, pouring up out of the earth, and Ines stood at the edge of the gallery with his hand pressed flat against the stone and felt his breath go strange in his chest.

Below them, carved into the mountain's hollow heart, was a city.

Terraces of white stone descending in rings toward a central spire—the Spire, the actual one, a needle of pale crystal rising from a plaza far below, so tall it nearly brushed the cavern roof. Bridges arced between terraces. Figures moved along them, robed and unhurried, and the light came from everywhere and nowhere, from lanterns that burned without flame and windows that glowed like paper screens.

And the sound—that chiming, that endless delicate ringing—came from the bridges themselves, from the figures crossing them, because every figure carried light. Small lights. Held in cages of glass and silver, slung at their hips like purses, worn at their throats like jewelry.

Embers. Hundreds of embers, each one a soft pulsing point of gold, each one chiming faintly as it moved.

"Oh," Wren said, very quietly. "Oh, I remember this part."

"You've *been* here?" Dara's hand had found her sword hilt and stayed there.

"I lived here." Wren's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reading an inventory. "Before. I don't remember leaving. I remember the bridges. I remember being carried across one, and the man carrying me humming, and—" she stopped. Started again. "I remember being for sale."

Nobody spoke. Below, the market chimed on, beautiful and terrible, a thousand sold souls singing their small notes as they changed hands.

"They're currency," Odile said. Her scholar's voice, the lecture voice, but stripped of all its comfort. "That's what the book meant. The court below doesn't hoard souls—it *spends* them. They're coin. A soul is the only thing down here that holds its value forever, because it can't be counterfeited and it can't die. Everything else in this economy is built on top of them." She swallowed. "I thought that was metaphor. Prophecies love metaphor. It's never metaphor."

Ines wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at the Spire.

At its base, where the crystal met the plaza, the light was different. Warmer. Thicker. And arranged along the Spire's lower third, set into the crystal like jewels into a crown, were embers of a different order—not purse-lights, not jewelry, but great slow-burning cores, each one the size of a heart, each one wired into the structure with veins of silver that ran up the Spire's height and vanished into the rock above.

Into the mountain. Into the passes above the mountain. Into the war-road the usurper's armies were marching even now.

"What are those?" Dara asked.

Odile turned pages with shaking hands. "The engines. The book calls them the *hearthstones of conquest*. The usurper's machines—the ones that broke Vhalen's walls in a night, the ones the deserters talk about—they don't run on coal or oil. They run on this. The Spire is a bank, and the army is spending it." She looked up at Ines, and her face was the face of a woman who has just understood the size of a debt. "Your soul isn't sitting in a vault, Ines. It's *invested*."

* * *

They went down. There was no other direction.

Wren led them through service corridors she swore she'd never walked and then navigated flawlessly, her body remembering what her mind had sold off, and Ines watched her do it and felt something crack quietly in his chest. This was what the mountain took. Not just souls. Maps of yourself, torn out and traded, and the body left walking around the hole.

They reached a terrace overlooking the exchange floor—a vast hall beneath the Spire where the trading happened—and Ines saw the ledger.

He didn't mean to see it. But there it was: a book the size of a door, chained open on a lectern of black glass, its pages turning themselves, and every page filled with names and numbers and small glowing marks where the seals should be. A clerk stood beside it, robed in white, feeding entries into the binding with hands that moved like water.

And as they watched, hidden in the shadow of a colonnade, a buyer approached the clerk. A soldier's bearing under civilian robes. He laid something on the lectern—a sealed contract, military wax—and pointed to a line in the ledger.

The clerk drew out an ember.

Held it up to the light, turning it, appraising it, the way a moneylender turns a coin. And the ember chimed—once, softly, a note like a struck glass—and Ines felt the sound go through him like a hook behind his sternum, because he knew that note. He had heard it before. Every soul down here sang in the same key, the key of the court's founding bargain, and somewhere in that hall—

"There," Wren breathed.

She was pointing at the clerk's tray. At the corner of it, apart from the stock being traded, sat a small iron casket, unadorned, its lid pierced with ventilation holes like a songbird's cage. And through the holes leaked a light that Ines recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting.

Small. Steady. Stubbornly, stubbornly gold.

His.

He was moving before he decided to move. Dara caught his arm and he shook her off and caught himself at the colonnade's edge, crouched, staring, and the ember in the cage pulsed—faster, brighter, once, twice—as if it had seen him. As if it knew.

"It knows me," he whispered.

"Of course it does," Wren said. Her eyes were wet and furious. "It's *you*. It's the part of you that was six years old and scared and still said yes so your mother's lullaby would have someone left to sing it. That's what they've got in a box, Ines. That's what's powering the machines that are going to burn the free cities." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "And we're going to take it back."

Below them, the clerk finished the sale. The soldier pocketed his purchase—an ember, someone's whole self, bought for the price of a supply contract—and walked away humming.

And in the ledger, the pages turned, and turned, and stopped.

On a page near the front. On a name written in a child's uneven hand, witnessed in ink the color of dried blood, dated twenty-three years ago to the season.

The clerk looked up.

Not at the ledger. At the colonnade. At the shadows where four people stood very still.

Its face, when it turned toward them, was patient and polite and utterly without eyes, and it smiled the smile of a merchant greeting a customer it has been expecting for a very long time.

"Galloway," it said, and its voice was the chime of every soul in the room at once. "We've held your account open. We so rarely get to close one."
