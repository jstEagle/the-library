## 7. The Price of Names

Dara had learned to read a room by its exits. It was the first thing the army taught you—before drill, before discipline, before they taught you how to die in neat rows—and it was the last thing she'd kept when she deserted. So when the door at the far end of the antechamber ground open on counterweights older than any kingdom, she counted them: one way in, one way out, and four people standing between.

The vault door itself was smaller than she expected. A circle of black stone set into the wall, ringed with script that moved when you weren't looking directly at it, like insects under ice. Wren stood close to it, arms crossed, chewing the inside of her cheek. Odile had her nose in the prophecy book, which was less reading than praying with extra steps. And Ines—

Ines was listening to the door.

That was the thing about him Dara still hadn't gotten used to. Most people filled silence like water filling a boot, spilling everywhere just because there was room. Ines went quiet the way other people drew weapons. He tilted his head toward the black stone, eyes half-closed, and after a long moment he said, "It's asking."

"It's a door," Dara said.

"It's asking," he repeated. "For payment. Equal weight." He looked back at her, and there was something in his face she recognized from the road—the look he got right before he did something stupid and necessary. "A memory. One that weighs as much as what's behind it."

"That's not how doors work."

"Most doors don't have my soul behind them." He said it lightly, the way he said everything that mattered, and Dara felt her jaw tighten. She hated that tone. She'd heard officers use it. *Nothing to see here, soldier, keep marching.* It was the voice men used when they'd already decided and wanted you to feel foolish for objecting.

Odile looked up from the book. "The text supports this. 'And the price of return shall be paid in the coin of the self; nothing borrowed, nothing given, only taken from the taker.'" Her finger trembled slightly on the page. "I thought it was metaphor. Prophets love metaphor."

"Doors love metaphor too," Dara said. "Ask any siege engineer."

Wren snorted. Then stopped, because Ines had stepped forward, and something in the air changed—a pressure, like the moment before thunder.

* * *

They argued first. That was the thing Dara would remember afterward: they argued, and for a while it almost worked.

"There has to be another way," she said. They'd retreated to the edge of the antechamber, out of the door's hearing—or what they pretended was its hearing. The black stone pulsed faintly, patient as debt. "There's always another way. We haven't tried the walls. We haven't tried going around through the lower galleries. Wren, you said the maps showed service tunnels—"

"The service tunnels are behind the vault," Wren said. Not unkindly. That was worse. "I told you. The whole court is built around it. The Spire isn't a building with a vault in it, Dara. It's a vault with a city grown over it."

"Then we find whoever built the locks. There were builders. Builders have families, and families have grudges, and grudges have keys—"

"Dara." Ines's voice was quiet. "I've been weighing this since the antechamber opened. There's no other way. There's just the way that costs me, and I'm the only one here it costs."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower, because shouting at him would be easy and easy wasn't going to save anyone. "You don't get to decide that alone. That's the whole point of—" She gestured at the four of them, at the torchlight and the dust and the absurd fact of the four of them being alive this far beneath the mountain. "Of us. You don't carry things alone anymore. That was the deal. Nobody wrote it down, but it was the deal."

Something flickered across his face—pain, quickly filed away. "The deal doesn't cover this."

"The deal covers everything. That's what makes it a deal and not an arrangement."

Odile made a small sound, half laugh, half wince, and turned back to her book as if the argument had become too private to witness.

* * *

Here was what Dara knew about loss, and why she couldn't let this go:

She had lost a brother to the usurper's conscription—not death, worse, he'd come home two years later with the same face and none of the same person inside it, smiling at their mother like a stranger doing an impression. She had watched a man be emptied and refilled with obedience, and everyone around him had called it survival. *At least he came back,* they said. *At least he's alive.*

He wasn't. He was a coat his body kept wearing.

And now Ines stood in front of a door that ate memories, talking about equal weight, and Dara could do the arithmetic he was pretending not to do. His mother was dead. His childhood was ash and a smuggler's pass and a lullaby he'd only just learned he remembered. What was left of the boy named Galloway lived in exactly three places: his own head, Wren's stories, and a book of prophecy written by a madman a century ago. If he fed his mother's face to that door, the boy wouldn't be diminished. He'd be deleted. A page torn out, and the binding sewn shut so smooth you'd never know a page was missing.

Except Dara would know. That was the cruelty of it. Everyone else would get a seamless Ines, and she'd walk around forever knowing there was a hole where his mother should be, watching him not-grieve someone he'd loved, and never being able to tell him what he'd lost because he'd have no shape left to lose it in.

"You're thinking about your brother," Ines said.

She blinked. "What?"

"You've gone somewhere. Your hands are fists. You do that when you're somewhere else." He said it gently, and that gentleness was unbearable, because it proved exactly what she was afraid of—that he saw people, really saw them, and had no idea what it would cost him to stop seeing himself.

"My brother came home empty," she said. "Everyone clapped."

"This isn't that."

"No. This is you volunteering for it. Which is worse. At least the army took my brother. You're handing yourself over and calling it strategy."

"It *is* strategy." For the first time his voice cracked its calm, and Dara felt a terrible satisfaction at the crack, and hated herself for it. "One memory against a war. One memory against the usurper's machines going dark, against the free cities holding, against—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Against all of it. You want to talk deals? That's the best deal anyone's ever offered me. My whole life has been people taking pieces of me for free. This is the first time I get to choose the price."

"And if the price is the wrong one?"

"Then it's mine to pay wrong."

"No," Dara said, and her voice came out flat and hard, parade-ground flat, and she didn't soften it. "No, it isn't. Because we're not letting you go through that door alone either. Whatever you feed it, we carry. Wren carries the lullaby. Odile carries the book. I'll carry whatever's left. So it's not yours to pay wrong, Ines, because we're the ones who have to live in the house you build out of whatever you don't remember."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere above them, through a hundred feet of stone, the court below made sounds like a slow heartbeat. The black door pulsed. Patient. Doors always were; they had nothing but time and you had so little.

* * *

It was Wren who broke it, and she broke it the way she broke everything—sideways, fast, and aimed at the truth.

"He's going to do it whether we argue or not." She hopped down off the ledge she'd been perched on. "I've known him since before either of us had names worth keeping. He does the stupid necessary thing. Always has. When the mountain took us, he gave them his fear so I could keep mine—he doesn't remember that either, but I do, and it's the same face he's making now. So let's stop pretending we're deciding. Let's decide what happens after."

"Wren," Odile warned.

"No. Listen." Wren turned to Dara, and her voice dropped its thief's flippancy entirely. "You're right. It's a hole and it won't heal clean. But here's what you're missing: he's offering his mother's face. His mother's *face*, Dara. Not her voice. Not the lullaby. Not the name. Memories aren't stones, they're nets—cut one thread and the net mostly holds. He'll lose her face and keep everything else, and we'll keep it for him too. I remember her. I was six, and I remember she sang with her eyes shut and she had flour on her hands most days, and I will tell him that every morning if he needs it. That's the deal you were talking about. That's us carrying it."

Dara looked at her. Then at Odile, who nodded slowly, closing the book like a verdict. Then at Ines, who was watching her with an expression she refused to name, because naming it would make it harder to say what she had to say next.

"One condition," she said.

Ines raised an eyebrow.

"You tell the door what you're giving before you give it. Out loud. To us. You say her name—you said once you didn't remember it, but you do, I've seen you almost reach it in your sleep. You say it, and you describe her, one time, while you still can. So that when it's gone, we hold the true version. Not Wren's six-year-old version. Yours." Her throat tightened. "If you're going to spend it, then it gets spent in front of witnesses. That's not negotiable."

For a moment he didn't move. Then he laughed—short, cracked, real—and rubbed a hand over his face. "You're impossible."

"I'm a deserter. Impossible is the job description."

He turned to the door. The black stone brightened at his approach, script crawling faster, hungry and courteous at once. He squared his shoulders the way soldiers did before walls, and Dara stepped up beside him—close enough to touch, not touching, because some things you witnessed rather than held.

"My mother's name," Ines began, and his voice shook on the first word and steadied on the second, "was Maren. She had flour on her hands most days, and she sang with her eyes shut, and her face was—"

He stopped.

Not hesitating. Stopped, the way a clock stops, and Dara saw his pupils blow wide and fixed on something past the door, past the wall, deep in the dark of the vault—and the black stone flared white, and every piece of moving script on it reversed direction at once.

"Ines?" Wren's voice, sharp.

"It's open," he whispered. "Dara—it was never locked. It's been *waiting* for me."

The circle of stone swung inward without a sound, and cold light poured out of the vault like water finding a crack—and in that light, something small and burning turned to look at them.
