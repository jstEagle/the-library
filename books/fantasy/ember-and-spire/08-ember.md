## 8. Ember

The vault-keeper's price was a memory of equal weight, and Ines had known, walking down through the gilded dark, which one he would pay.

His mother's face.

He had carried it thirty years the way you carry a coal in cupped hands—carefully, painfully, because letting it go meant the cold got in. He had built his whole careful, unremarkable life around that small heat. And now he stood before a counter of black glass in a vault beneath the world, about to set it down.

"You don't have to do this." Dara's voice came from behind him, low and rough. She had been saying it since the tunnels. She said it now like she was still hoping to be heard. "There's another way. There has to be."

"There isn't," Odile said quietly. "I've read every account of the court below. The vault doesn't barter. It weighs."

"Then we find something lighter. A year of his life. A—" Dara stopped. Her jaw worked. "Not her face, Ines. Not the only thing you have left of her."

Ines turned. Wren leaned against the wall, arms crossed, watching him with those quick thief's eyes that had already seen this coming and made peace with it before any of them. Odile stood with the prophecy book open against her chest like a shield. And Dara—Dara looked at him the way soldiers look at a comrade stepping onto a bridge they know won't hold.

"It's not the only thing I have left of her," Ines said. He touched his own chest, over the hollow where the ember had slept all these years without him knowing. "She's here too. Just... quieter."

"That's not how memories work," Dara said. "You don't get to keep the song and lose the face. You'll hum it someday and not know whose voice taught you."

"I know." His throat was tight. He made himself smile anyway—the taster's smile, the one that hid poison. "But I also know what happens if I walk away. Those machines upstairs grind on. Armies march on the free cities wearing my soul as their engine. I'd rather forget her face than watch it burn cities."

Dara stepped forward and gripped his arm, hard, soldier-hard. "Then remember this instead: whatever you trade, we're what you keep. You hear me? Us. Don't come out of there empty."

He almost laughed. Almost cried. Did both, a little, in the space between heartbeats.

* * *

The vault-keeper did not look up when he approached. It was not quite a person—a shape of silvered shadow behind the black glass, wearing the suggestion of robes, holding the suggestion of scales. When Ines laid his palm flat on the counter, it spoke in a voice like coins settling.

*"Name your payment."*

"A memory," Ines said. "My mother's face. All of it. Every time I ever saw her."

The scales tipped. Somewhere in the deep of the vault, something vast turned over in its sleep.

*"Weight accepted. Speak it, and it is ours."*

And so Ines spoke his mother's face into the dark. The morning light through the shutters of their house in Thornfell, catching her cheekbone. The way her eyes crinkled before her mouth did when she laughed. The soot smudge she always had on her left temple from the stove. The last time he saw her, at the mouth of the mountain pass, pushing him toward the smuggler's road with tears freezing on her lashes—he spoke that too, gave away the tears, the lashes, the frozen morning, all of it.

It went out of him like water from a broken jar. He felt it leave. He felt the shape of the hole it left behind, smooth-edged and clean, and he understood suddenly why the rich below the mountain paid for memories the way the living paid for bread: because an absence that neat is its own kind of possession.

*"Paid,"* said the vault-keeper. *"Take what is yours."*

A drawer slid open in the black glass. Inside, on a bed of grey velvet, lay a small ember of light no bigger than a sparrow's egg—and it was warm. He could feel it through the velvet, feel it the way you feel a hand find yours under a blanket in winter. Thirty years it had burned down here while he walked around above ground missing something he couldn't name.

Ines picked it up.

It knew him.

That was the only word for it. The light pressed into his palm and then into his skin and then into everything, and the hollow in his chest filled—not with fire but with recognition, with the terrible simple feeling of a lock turning after decades of rattling. He gasped. His knees went. Someone caught him—Dara, her arm iron across his back—and he heard himself making a sound he didn't recognize, high and cracked, thirty years of namelessness pouring out of him all at once.

And in the flood, for one impossible instant, he remembered everything at once. The boy he had been. The lullaby. The pass through Thornfell, snow to his waist, his mother's hand letting go. The bargain struck in desperation beneath the roots of the Spire by a child who didn't understand that survival could be bought on credit. His own name—the first one, the real one, the one Wren had spoken in the safehouse like a key.

Then it settled. Not gone. Whole. He was whole.

"Ines?" Odile's voice, far away. "Ines, can you hear me?"

"He's crying," Wren said, wonder in it. "Look at him. He's crying and grinning like an idiot."

"I can hear you," Ines managed. "All of you. I—" He laughed, wet and disbelieving. "I remember my name."

"Tell us later," Dara said. "Move now."

Because the vault had begun to scream.

* * *

It was not a sound so much as a structural event. The black glass counters shivered; the grey velvet drawers slammed themselves; overhead, through miles of stone, came a groan like the mountain clearing its throat. The engines of the Spire ran on sold souls, and one of them had just walked out of the vault wearing a taster's body.

"The machines," Odile breathed, book clutched to her chest. "If the soul leaves the works—"

"The works stop working," Wren finished, already moving. "Which means the usurper's whole war stops working, which means every soldier and inquisitor topside is about to have a very bad day and take it out on us. Run first, celebrate never—that's the rule."

They ran.

The gilded underworld came apart around them as they fled through it. Chandeliers of captive light guttered and died. The beautiful, terrible market streets where souls were weighed and traded filled with panic—courtiers of the deep scattering like moths, vault-keepers sealing their counters, and everywhere the groan deepening into a roar as the Spire's engines failed floor by floor, level by level, stone remembering it was only stone.

Ines ran with the ember burning in his chest and grief riding shotgun in his skull. He was whole. He was more himself than he had been in thirty years. And he could not remember his mother's face.

He tried, even as he ran. Reached for it the way your tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth. There was the lullaby—still there, thank all the merciful dark, the melody intact. There was the smell of her kitchen, woodsmoke and barley. But the face was a smooth-edged hole, and he understood with horrible clarity that he had done exactly what Dara feared: he would hum the song someday and not know whose voice taught him.

"Hey." Dara fell into step beside him, reading him the way she always did, too easily. "Whatever you lost—it's not gone. It's just somewhere else for now. We'll get it back. That's a promise."

"You can't promise that."

"I'm a deserter, Ines. Promising impossible things is the only kind I make."

Despite everything, he laughed. The sound bounced off collapsing stone and sounded, for one moment, like hope.

* * *

The escape route was a service tunnel Wren had mapped on a previous theft, a crawl of rusted ladder-rungs and old air, and they were halfway up it when the breach came.

Not collapse—invasion. Boots below them, many boots, and the flat cold light of inquisitor-lanterns bleeding up the shaft. The usurper's forces had been beneath the mountain all along, garrisoned around their precious engine, and now the engine was dying and they wanted their property back.

"They've found the lower shaft," Wren called down, hanging off a rung, peering into the dark. "Six—no, eight. Lanterns. They'll reach the ladder in two minutes."

"Can we outrun them?" Odile asked.

"To the surface? With me on a bad leg and you carrying a library? No." Wren looked up the shaft, then down, then at Ines, and something settled in her face—the calm of a person doing arithmetic they've already finished. "Somebody holds the ladder. Somebody slow them down."

"No," Ines said immediately.

"Somebody fast and small and very good at tight spaces," Wren went on, as if he hadn't spoken, "which is me, obviously. I'll drop the rungs behind me—I've been unscrewing things since before you could walk, little ember, don't argue—and buy you the climb. You three go up. You go *up*, Ines. You carry that thing somewhere no army can follow."

"I'm not leaving you."

"Yes, you are." She smiled, and it was the safehouse smile, the thief's smile, but underneath it was the neighbor-girl from thirty years ago, the one who had survived the mountain beside him once already. "We made it out together once. Odds are good we make it twice. Now *climb*, before I start charging you for the delay."

He climbed. That was the truth of it, the thing he would turn over in the dark for years afterward: he climbed, because she told him to, because Dara hauled him upward by the collar, because Odile pushed from below, because four people cannot win an argument on a ladder and someone had to live to tell her story.

Below them, steel rang on stone. Wren's voice, bright and profane, buying seconds at a terrible exchange rate. A lantern shattered. Someone screamed. Then a sound Ines would hear in dreams for the rest of his life: the crack of a falling body, and silence where her voice had been.

"WREN!" Dara's shout echoed up the shaft.

Silence. Then—faint, breathless, unmistakably alive and unmistakably wrong: *"Still... charging you... for the delay..."*

They reached the surface as the mountain exhaled behind them, spilling dust and torchlight from a hundred cracks. Dawn was breaking over Thornfell's peaks, gold and indifferent, and Ines stood in it with a whole soul in his chest and half his heart still down a ladder shaft.

Dara was already turning back toward the entrance, sword drawn, face like flint. "I'm going back for her."

Ines caught her arm. Below, faint but steady, he could feel it now—his ember, his whole self, resonating like a plucked string with something else down in the dark. Something small and stubborn and still fighting.

"She's alive," he said. "I can feel her. But Dara—" He looked east, where the passes opened onto the free cities, and saw what dawn had revealed: roads filling with soldiers. Columns of them, rank on rank, marching on cities whose war-machines had just gone dark. "The usurper knows. And if a whole soul can be taken once—"

"—it can be taken again," Dara finished grimly.

Behind them, the Spire groaned. Ahead, the armies came.
