## 9. The Breaking

The world above the Spire was burning.

Ines stood on the ridge above Thornfell's southern pass, his soul a live coal behind his ribs, and watched the usurper's armies come down out of the mountains like a tide of iron. Ten thousand men, maybe more. The war-machines were dark—the engines had died with the Spire's collapse—but men with swords did not need engines. Men with swords only needed morning.

Behind him, the free cities' watchfires dotted the valley floor. Odile was somewhere down there with the scholars' council, arguing maps. Dara was organizing what soldiers remained. Wren was in a surgeon's tent with a wound in her side that should have killed her and hadn't, because Wren had never once done anything she was supposed to do.

And Ines Galloway—who had spent thirty years being no one, who had tasted poison for kings and smuggled contraband through passes that didn't officially exist—stood alone on the ridge holding the one thing in the world every army below wanted.

He could feel it now. That was the cruelest part of being whole. For three decades he hadn't known what was missing, and now that it was back, he felt it constantly: the ember's warmth under his sternum, pulsing like a second heart. His mother's lullaby lived there. Her face lived there—he'd traded it away to reach the vault, but when the Spire broke open, everything it held had poured back into him, debts repaid by catastrophe. He remembered her smile now. He remembered the smell of her hair. He remembered dying a little, at seven years old, in the cold beneath the mountain, and the bargain he'd made to keep breathing.

He also remembered, with terrible new clarity, exactly how it had felt to be without it. The hollow. The gray. Thirty years of tasting food for strangers and feeling nothing but the bitterness on his tongue.

*They can make you feel that again,* said the thing wearing an inquisitor's voice in his memory. *They can take it back.*

That was the problem. That was the whole rotten problem, laid out as plainly as a ledger. He had bled and burned and buried a friend's laughter in the dark to get his soul back, and all he'd done was repackage it. A soul in a body was just a soul in a smaller vault. The usurper's inquisitors had cracked the Spire's vault once; they could crack a man. Everyone could be cracked. Dara had told him enough about the interrogation tents to know that.

A whole soul could be stolen again.

Ines sat down on the cold rock and thought about it the way he used to think about locked doors: not whether they could be opened, but how many ways there were to open them, and which way cost least.

There was only one door here, and it opened inward.

---

He found them at dawn, or they found him—Dara came up the ridge path first, sword still belted over her nightclothes because she'd heard he'd left camp and hadn't waited for her boots. Odile followed, wrapped in two cloaks and clutching a satchel of books like a shield. Wren came last, gray-faced, moving like a woman made of hinges, and refused every offer of help with a look that ended the conversation before it started.

"You're going to do something stupid," Dara said.

"I'm going to do something permanent," Ines said. "Those are different."

"Those are *never* different."

"Odile." He turned to the scholar. "Tell them what you told me last night."

Odile's hands tightened on the satchel strap. She looked, for a moment, like the woman who had first shown him the prophecy book—guilty, careful, braced for blame. "The prophecy doesn't end where we thought. There are more lines. I translated them after the Spire fell." She swallowed. "*When the ember is whole, it will be taken whole. When it is broken, no crown shall hold it.*"

"That's not a prophecy," Dara said flatly. "That's a threat assessment."

"It's both," Ines said. "It's been both this entire time. The book wasn't predicting my future. It was describing the trap. Whole, I'm a weapon someone else can pick up. Broken—" he laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, "—broken, I'm worthless. To them. To everyone who wants what's inside me."

"Then we run," Dara said. "We're good at running. You know every pass through these mountains better than the people who named them. We go north, past the salt flats, across the—"

"Dara."

"—and we disappear, properly disappear, the way you taught me, and in ten years nobody remembers the name Galloway—"

"*Dara.*" He said it gently, and that stopped her more than shouting would have. "I spent thirty years disappearing. It didn't save anyone. It didn't even save me. And if I run, they burn the free cities looking for me, and they'll find me eventually, because everyone can be found, and everyone can be broken." He touched his chest, over the ember. "This has to stop being something they can take."

Wren had gone very quiet. She was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read—or maybe he could, and that was the problem. Like she was watching a door close that she'd spent her whole life trying to find.

"How?" she asked.

"I give it away," Ines said. "Freely. A soul that's taken can be taken back. A soul that's given—" he shook his head. "Odile?"

"Given things don't revert," Odile whispered. "The old law-craft is clear on that. A gift is a gift. Ownership transfers. If he shatters it and gives pieces freely to—to others—" She stopped. Looked at each of them. Understood, visibly, what he was asking, and went pale. "Ines. Do you know what you'd lose?"

"No," he said honestly. "That's rather the point. But I know what I'd lose if I don't. Everything. Again. And this time I'd know it was gone."

* * *

They argued until the sun cleared the peaks.

Dara argued longest, and loudest, and last. She paced the ridge like a caged animal, laying out alternatives—hiding, fighting, bargaining with the free cities' councils, forging a false soul-signature (which Odile shot down), faking his death (which Wren shot down, having once faked a death herself and found it "deeply overrated"). Every plan was good. Every plan bought time. None of them bought forever, and forever was the only currency the armies below accepted.

"It's your mother," Dara finally said, and her voice cracked on it. "Her face. Her voice. The lullaby. You got it back *yesterday*, Ines. You've had her for one day. One day, and you're going to hand her over to—"

"To the people who kept me alive long enough to want her back," Ines said. "Yes."

"That's not—that's not how grief works, you don't get to just—"

"I know how grief works." He stepped closer. "I grieved her for thirty years without knowing what I was grieving. That was the worst version. This—" he tapped his chest, "—this is the best version I'll ever have. One perfect day of her. And I would rather give her to you than leave her in a vault for some inquisitor to pry out of my corpse. Wouldn't you? If it were your mother?"

Dara stared at him. Her jaw worked. Then she turned away, shoulders rigid, and looked at the armies in the valley, and said nothing, which from Dara was a scream.

Wren spoke instead. "What happens to you? After."

"I don't know." He smiled at her. "Probably nothing dramatic. Probably I'm just... thinner. A man made of what's left."

"And what's left?"

Ines considered. "A taster's tongue. A smuggler's map-memory. A truly remarkable talent for being underestimated." He shrugged. "And whatever you all put back into me afterward. Apparently that's how it works. Odile says identity isn't stored, it's built. Built with—" he gestured at the three of them, standing battered and bleeding and stubborn on a freezing ridge at dawn, "—with whoever's beside you when the building happens."

"That's not what I said," Odile murmured. "But it's close enough that I won't correct you in front of an audience."

"There's no audience."

"There's an army."

* * *

He did it at sunrise, because his mother had loved sunrises, and if he was giving away the last day of her, he wanted it to end in light.

No ritual. No circle of candles, no incantation—Odile had offered the proper forms and he'd declined them. This wasn't law-craft. It was simpler and older than law-craft. It was a gift, and gifts only needed two things: a giver who meant it, and receivers who understood what they were taking.

He took Dara's hands first. They were calloused from a deserter's hard years, steady as bedrock.

"When they ask you who you are," he said, "and they will, eventually, someone will ask—you tell them. Not your rank, not your desertion, not your worst day. Tell them the true thing. That's yours now. Guard it."

"Ines—"

"That's yours now," he repeated, and pushed.

It didn't hurt. That surprised him most. It felt like exhaling after holding breath for thirty years—a piece of the ember sliding out through his palms, warm, and sinking into Dara like rain into drought-ground. She gasped. Her eyes went wide and gold-lit for one impossible heartbeat, and then she was just Dara again, except she was crying, except she was gripping his hands like she'd never let go.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, *Ines*. Your mother's voice. She's singing—I can hear—"

"I know." His own eyes were hot. "Keep it safe."

Wren next. She tried to refuse—of course she did, she actually stepped back, raised her hands, said "absolutely not, I steal things, I don't keep them"—but he caught her wrists, and she saw his face, and stopped.

"You knew me before the mountain," he said. "You kept my name for twenty-five years when I couldn't. Take this, and we're even for the name."

"What if I lose it?" she whispered. "I lose everything, Ines. It's kind of my whole thing."

"Then I'll remind you," he said. "Whatever's left of me will remind you. That's the deal. That's always been the deal with us, hasn't it? You remember me, I remember you, and between us we make one whole person."

She laughed wetly and nodded, and he gave, and the light went into her like a spark into tinder, and she pressed both hands over her heart and said his childhood name—his real name, the one he'd abandoned—and for the first time hearing it didn't feel like grief.

Odile last. The scholar was already crying, furious about it, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like tears were a translation error.

"This is academically irresponsible," she managed. "There's no peer review for this. There's no *precedent*—"

"Odile. You brought a dead man back to life with a book. Precedent is whatever you say it is."

She laughed, sobbed, held out her hands, and received her piece of him with her eyes open, watching the light the way she watched everything—like it was a text she was determined to read correctly. When it settled in her, she exhaled a word in Old Thorneish, something reverent, and then said, very quietly: "For the record, the prophecy continues past this point. I haven't translated those lines yet."

"Of course it does," Ines said.

* * *

The last piece stayed in his chest. Small. A coal-fragment, barely warm. Enough of the ember to be a man and not a lamp—but nothing worth an army's march. Nothing any crown could hold.

He felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth: constantly, at first, the tongue returning to the gap. His mother's face was gone again. Her voice, her lullaby—all of it, scattered into three people he loved. He stood on the ridge in the strengthening sun and searched himself and found, where the wholeness had been, a strange clean emptiness. Not the gray hollow of before. Something lighter. Room.

Below, horns were sounding. The vanguard of the usurper's army had reached the valley's mouth, and the free cities' bells were answering, and somewhere down there Odile's fellow scholars were unrolling maps of a defense that would need every hour he'd bought them.

Dara came to stand beside him. She was still crying, quietly, and she didn't try to hide it.

"I can hear her," she said. "All the time now. It's like—she's just *there*, humming, while I work. Is that—is that what it was like? Having her?"

Ines thought about it. Reached for the memory and found the gap where it lived, and found, filling the gap, something else: Dara's face when the light entered her. Wren saying his name. Odile's reverent whisper. Three people carrying his mother now, so he never had to carry her alone.

"No," he said. "It's better."

He turned toward the descent, toward the bells and the burning and the war that would decide everything, and Dara fell into step beside him, and Wren called down from the rocks that she'd found a smuggler's path around the army's flank, *obviously*, and Odile was already shouting something about the untranslated lines.

Ines Galloway—thinner now, lighter, mostly gone and entirely himself—started walking.

The prophecy, Odile would later swear, had one more line. And it wasn't about him at all.
