## 10. A Table Set for Five

The bread rose better on the third morning of every week. Ines had never worked out why—something in the way the mountain air cooled overnight, maybe, or some old habit of the yeast—but he'd built his schedule around it, and by the time the sun cleared Thornfell's eastern shoulder he had four loaves cooling on the sill and a pot of barley soup breathing steam into the kitchen's rafters.

The kitchen sat where the smuggler's pass met the valley road, a stone building that had been a tollhouse once, then a waystation for mule trains, then nothing at all until Ines had put a new roof on it and hung a sign he'd carved himself: *Open at Dawn. Bring Your Own Stories.*

He'd meant it as a joke. Nobody laughed. Everybody brought stories anyway.

He tasted everything before it went out. That was the one habit from his old life that had followed him down the mountain like a loyal dog. A spoonful of soup held to the tongue, counted to ten, swallowed slow. The hemlock years had left him with a tongue that could find bitterness in anything, and these days the only poison he was looking for was grief, which didn't hide in food but pooled in him anyway, especially at dawn, especially when the light came through the window at an angle that reminded him of a vault far below the earth where a small ember of light had waited thirty years for someone to come back.

He'd come back. That was the strange part. He'd gone down into the dark expecting to meet himself and instead he'd found a stranger—a boy's soul, small and frightened and *his*, unmistakably his—and when he'd shattered it into four pieces and pressed them into the hands of the people beside him, he'd thought he understood what he was giving away.

He hadn't. You couldn't understand a thing like that from inside it. He'd broken his soul the way you'd break bread, and now every morning Wren tore off a piece of him without knowing she was doing it, and Dara handed him another piece across the table, and Odile recited whole chapters of him from memory like scripture she'd never believed in until she'd watched it walk out of a book and start cooking breakfast.

* * *

Wren arrived first, as always, dropping through the kitchen's back window rather than using the door, because doors were for people who hadn't spent their childhood learning that walls had opinions about who belonged inside them.

"Smells like barley," she said, landing light as a cat on the floorboards. "And like you burned something."

"I did not burn—"

"The toast. Yesterday. You stood there talking to Dara about drainage ditches and the toast went black as midnight." She stole a heel of bread off the sill and bit into it. "I'm telling you this for your own good. A man should know his own history."

"My history," Ines said, "is whatever you three decide it is."

"Damn right," said Wren, and grinned at him with crumbs on her lip.

She'd been doing this for weeks now—the filling-in, the nightly accounting—and she'd developed rules for it. Never lie, even when the truth was ugly. Especially when the truth was ugly. She told him about the boy named Sef who'd lived two doors down from her in the shadow of the Spire, a thin quiet kid who could name every bird in Thornfell by its call and who'd once traded her half his dinner for a look at her stolen map collection. She told him how they'd both been sold within the same season, how the brokers came with their ledgers and their soft voices, and how she'd gotten out because a thief's skills turned out to be exactly what the court below needed, and he'd gotten out because his mother had run until her lungs gave out.

She told him the parts he couldn't remember and never flinched from them, and Ines had learned to receive it the way you'd receive rain in a dry country: gratefully, without asking where it came from.

But some mornings the gaps yawned wider than others. Some mornings he'd reach for his mother's face and find only the shape where it used to be—the trade he'd made at the vault door, the memory of equal weight—and the loss would open under him like a stair that wasn't there.

"You're doing it again," Wren said now, watching him from the windowsill. "That thing with your eyes."

"What thing?"

"The looking-for-something-behind-your-own-face thing." She hopped down. "Sit. Eat. She had brown skin and laugh lines before the running took them away, and she sang flat, Ines. Really flat. Like a door hinge with ambitions. And she loved you so hard it embarrassed the neighbors." Wren's voice gentled on the last part, the way it always did. "You don't have to see her face to know that. I'll carry it for both of us."

Ines looked at his hands. Flour to the wrists. A taster's steady fingers. "Some days I think I gave up too much."

"And some days you remember why." Wren shrugged. "The usurper's machines are dark. His armies are eating hardtack and grumbling in camps outside cities they can't crack, because the free cities hold, and they hold because a soul split four ways can't be stolen, and a tyrant with no engines is just a man with bad ideas and good uniforms." She tapped his chest with one finger. "You did that. Whatever it cost."

* * *

Dara came mid-morning, up from the valley with a crate of salt fish and news, because Dara always had news; deserters made excellent listeners and better correspondents, and half the garrison commanders in the lowlands still wrote to her thinking she was a man named Harrow who owed them money.

"The eastern passes are clear," she reported, setting the crate down with a thump. "Vane's scholars are cataloguing what's left of the Spire. Odile says the vault level collapsed clean—nothing recoverable, no souls left behind, no machines worth salvaging. The zealots are calling it a miracle. Odile is calling it *architecture*, loudly, at anyone who'll listen."

"And the armies?"

"Starving slower than they'd like. The usurper's pulled back to the capital. There's talk he's suing for terms." Dara leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and studied Ines with the particular attention she reserved for assessing threats and people she loved, which for her were the same category. "There's also talk of you. The prophecy's still circulating. The Galloway boy, the ember beneath the mountain. Some of the faithful think you died in the collapse. Some think you ascended."

"What do you think?"

"I think prophets make terrible cooks and you make excellent soup, and if anyone comes up this road looking for a messiah they're going to find a man who charges two coppers for bread and remembers everyone's order." She smiled, tired and real. "Let them talk. Names are just weather, Ines. It blows through."

But she stayed by the door a moment longer, and he knew that look too—the one that meant there was more, held back not from secrecy but from kindness.

"Say it," he said.

"There's a woman in the valley asking after you. Not a zealot. Older. Says she knew your mother, back before Thornfell." Dara's voice was careful. "Says she has letters. Things your mother wrote. She wants to give them to you herself."

The kitchen went very quiet. Steam rose off the soup pot. Somewhere outside a bird called—one of the ones he could still name, thank the gods, that memory had survived—and Ines stood with flour on his hands and a hole in his chest shaped like a woman singing flat, and felt the ground tilt the way it always did when the past reached up.

"Letters," he said.

"She hasn't come up yet. Said she'd wait until you sent for her. Said—" Dara hesitated. "Said she wanted to know if the boy who ran through Thornfell survived, or if only the man who broke his soul came out."

* * *

They talked about it all afternoon, the four of them, because that was how decisions got made now—not alone, never alone, that was the whole point, that had always been the point even when he'd been too blind to see it.

Odile arrived at dusk with ink-stained fingers and a satchel of books she pretended weren't for the kitchen's single shelf, and heard the news, and went very still in the way she did when scholarship collided with life.

"Your mother's letters would be primary sources," she said. "Irreplaceable. Historically—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started over, softer. "Ines. What do *you* want?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The one he'd been circling all day. Because the old Ines—the taster, the smuggler, the man built entirely out of useful silences—would have said no. Would have kept the past buried where it couldn't hurt anyone. And the boy in the vault, the ember, might have said yes desperately, grasping at any scrap of her.

But he was neither of those men anymore. He was something being built nightly at a scarred table by four people who refused to let him be less than the sum of all his stories.

"I want to hear her voice," Ines said finally. "Even secondhand. Even in ink." He looked around the table—at Wren spinning a stolen knife between her fingers, at Dara with her arms crossed and her heart on her sleeve where she pretended it wasn't, at Odile already reaching for paper to draft a reply. "Send for her. Tell her the boy survived. Tell her he's feeding people now."

* * *

That night, after the dishes were done and the fire was banked, they did what they always did. They told him who he was.

Wren started, because Wren always started—the boy Sef, the bird calls, the map traded for half a supper. Dara picked up the middle years, the capital, the tasting, the night he'd caught the hemlock and saved a court that never learned his name. Odile finished with the prophecy, reading passages aloud from memory now, the book itself locked away where zealots couldn't worship it, and when she reached the line about the soul sleeping below the Spire she closed her eyes and recited instead what actually happened: four friends in the dark, a choice made freely, a soul given away like bread.

"That's not what the book says," Ines murmured, half asleep against Dara's shoulder.

"No," Odile agreed. "The book ends with the breaking. But books end where the writing stops, not where the story does." She set down her cup. "Tomorrow we tell you the rest. The part we're still writing."

Outside, the mountain stood black against the stars, and somewhere down in the valley a woman waited with letters and a dead friend's words, and somewhere east a usurper drafted terms for wars he could no longer power, and none of it touched the kitchen at Thornfell's edge, where a man with an incomplete heart slept soundly among the people who held his missing pieces—not as a burden, never as a burden, but as a trust, returned to him nightly in stories, freely given, freely given back.

In the morning there would be bread. It would rise well. It was the third day of the week.
