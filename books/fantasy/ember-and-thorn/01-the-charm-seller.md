## 1. The Charm Seller

The pigeons of Thornhollow were terrible gossips, and Jude Amberdale loved them for it.

"Third stall from the fountain," said the speckled hen strutting along the awning above her cart. "Fat man. Green coat. He's been circling since dawn. Asking about you."

Jude didn't look up from her work. Her hands kept sorting charms—knots of braided thread, river stones wrapped in copper wire, little clay beads glazed with ash—while her eyes stayed fixed on nothing in particular, the way a person's eyes did when they weren't listening to anything at all.

"What kind of asking?" she murmured into her collar.

"The bad kind." The pigeon—she'd named him Aldous years ago, mostly out of spite because he was pompous even by pigeon standards—hopped down to the cart's rail and preened. "He asked the fishwife where the charm girl slept. She said she didn't know. She lied well. I gave her bread."

"You gave her my bread."

"I gave her *your* bread to buy *your* safety. This is called investment."

Jude exhaled through her nose and reached up to scratch the soft gray feathers under his beak, an old habit she had to keep herself from indulging too often in public. A woman haggling with a pigeon was just a woman haggling with a pigeon. A woman whose pigeon answered back was kindling.

She'd been doing this for eleven years now, ever since she was nine years old and learned that the world did not forgive certain kinds of listening. The gift wasn't dramatic. That was what made it dangerous. She didn't summon storms or turn milk to poison. She simply spoke, and beasts understood—and they answered, in voices like wind through reeds or gravel rolling downhill or, in Aldous's case, like a self-satisfied merchant who thought very highly of his own opinions.

The law called it abomination. The priests called it corruption. The crown, these days, called it treason, though Jude hadn't known that last part until recently, and the not-knowing was starting to feel like a luxury she could no longer afford.

"Green coat," Aldous repeated. "He's stopped at the pie stand. He's watching your cart in the reflection off the copper pot."

*Damn.* Jude lifted her head slowly, casually, and let her gaze drift across the market square as if browsing. There—the distortion in the polished copper, a smear of movement near the pies. A man standing too still amid the bustle. Broad shoulders. Gray cloak, hood back, hair cropped short. Not a soldier; soldiers wore the king's red. Gray meant something worse. Gray meant the Inquisition's quiet arm, the ones the dockworkers whispered about when the ale was cheap and the night was long.

She'd seen one before, three winters past, from across a burning field she still didn't let herself think about. One gray cloak was enough for a lifetime.

"Right," she said softly. "Aldous, go find Bram. Dockside, the tavern with the crooked anchor sign. Tell him—" How did you tell a knight-turned-dockworker, in bird speech, that trouble had found his name? "Tell him *the word is green*. He'll know."

"He won't know."

"He will. Go."

The pigeon launched off the cart in a clatter of wings, and Jude turned back to her charms with the pleasant, vacant smile of a woman thinking about absolutely nothing.

* * *

The morning crawled. She sold six charms—a luck-knot to a baker's boy, a fever-stone to a grandmother who swore by them, two love-beads to a girl who blushed and wouldn't meet her eyes. All harmless. All genuinely harmless, which was the point: every trinket on her cart was exactly what it appeared to be, thread and stone and clay, no magic in any of them. The magic was elsewhere. The magic was in the rats beneath the stalls who carried messages for coppers, in the sparrows who mapped the city's comings and goings, in the stray dogs who remembered faces better than any constable.

Hiding in plain sight meant being boring. Boring was survival. Jude had made herself the most boring charm seller in Thornhollow—modest prices, modest wares, a modest smile that never quite reached anything interesting behind her eyes.

The green-coated man bought a pie. He ate it slowly. He did not leave.

Pip arrived mid-morning, weaving through the crowd with the particular swagger of a fourteen-year-old who believed himself invisible. He was growing again—his sleeves were too short, his voice cracking in ranges that embarrassed them both—and he dropped onto the stool beside her cart with the sigh of a much put-upon nobleman, which technically he had been, once, in another life.

"You look terrible," he announced.

"Thank you, Pip."

"You do. You've got the face. The listening face."

"I don't have a listening face."

"You absolutely do. Your eyes go all—" he waved a hand, "—distant. Like you're hearing something nobody else can hear."

Jude's stomach tightened pleasantly and unpleasantly at once. Pip knew what she was. Had known for two years, since the night she'd pulled him half-drowned out of the canal and a rat had told her which way his pursuers had gone. He was the only person in Thornhollow who knew and was still alive, which made him either the safest secret she had or the most dangerous one. Some days she couldn't decide which.

"Well, stop making it," she said. "And stop sitting here. Go buy something and loiter somewhere else."

"I'm buying something." He picked up a fever-stone, examined it, put it down, picked up a luck-knot. "Bram says there are soldiers at the north gate. Mapping houses. Writing things down. He says they've got lists."

The words landed cold, despite the sun. "What kind of lists?"

"Names. Household names. Who lives where, how many sleep under each roof." Pip lowered his voice, and for a moment the swagger fell away and he was just a scared kid who'd run from a father who'd taught him that lists were never made for good reasons. "Jude. What are they hunting?"

"I don't know yet." Which was true, and also a lie, because some animal part of her had known for weeks—had felt it in the way the city's creatures had grown skittish, the way the rats had started avoiding the palace district entirely, the way even the gulls had stopped circling the keep. Animals always knew first. She just hadn't wanted to hear it.

Across the square, the green coat moved. He set down his pie crust, brushed off his hands, and began walking toward her cart—not hurrying, not hesitating, just walking the way a man walks when he has all day and all the authority in the world.

"Pip," Jude said quietly. "Go home."

"But—"

"*Now.* Take the canal road. Don't run."

He went, thank the saints, without arguing, slipping into the current of shoppers like a minnow into a school. Jude arranged her smile, straightened her shawl, and became boring with everything she had.

The man stopped at her cart. Up close he was unremarkable in the way that made her skin crawl—clean-shaven, mild-eyed, hands folded neatly at his belt. His cloak was fine wool, undyed gray, and pinned at the throat with a silver clasp shaped like an open eye.

"Charm seller," he said. It wasn't a question.

"That's me, m'lord. Charms against fever, against ill luck, against heartbreak. The heartbreak ones cost extra—they take longer to braid."

Something flickered across his face. Not amusement. Inventory. "You sell many?"

"Enough to eat. Not enough to get rich." She gestured at the cart, the honest poverty of it. "Thornhollow's got a charm seller on every corner, m'lord. We're like pigeons. Nobody counts us."

"No," he agreed mildly. "Nobody does."

His gaze drifted over her wares, slow and thorough, and Jude felt her heartbeat in her teeth. Because there—tucked at the back of the cart, half-hidden under a folded cloth she'd *meant* to move this morning, she'd *sworn* she'd moved it—sat the one thing on this cart that was not harmless.

It had come to her a month ago from a dying hedge-witch in the low quarter, pressed into her hands with shaking fingers and a whisper: *keep it buried, girl, or keep it running.* A small wooden whistle, carved in the shape of a sleeping fox, that hummed faintly when held near the ear. Old magic. Deep magic. The kind of thing that didn't call animals—it *woke* them, every beast within a mile, and bent their ears toward its bearer. A war-trumpet for anyone who could speak the way Jude spoke. A confession, worn openly, of everything she was.

She'd kept it because throwing away a gift felt like betrayal. She'd hidden it because selling it felt like murder. And now a gray cloak was standing three feet from it, and his eyes were drifting toward the folded cloth.

"Your stock seems modest," the inquisitor said. "Yet I'm told you've a reputation. That animals follow you through the market. That birds land on your cart unprompted."

"Birds land everywhere, m'lord. It's the crumbs." She laughed—a real laugh, easy, practiced until it had become almost true. "Half of Thornhollow feeds the pigeons. The other half curses them. I just sell to both halves."

"Mm." He reached toward the cart.

Not toward the cloth. Toward a luck-knot at the front, turning it over in his fingers, and Jude breathed again—but only halfway, because his other hand had come to rest on the cart's edge, and the folded cloth had shifted, and a sliver of carved wood showed pale beneath it. Fox-shaped. Humming, if you knew to listen. If you were trained to listen.

The inquisitor's fingers paused.

"Interesting piece," he said, without looking at it. "Under the cloth."

"That? Just a carving. Unfinished. My brother's work—he's got more enthusiasm than skill." The lie came smooth, and underneath it Jude's mind raced through the arithmetic of distance: three steps to the canal-side, the water below, the drain tunnel wide enough for a body if you didn't mind the smell. Bram had shown her the route years ago. *Always know two ways out,* he'd said. *The second one's the one that saves you.*

"May I see it?"

"There's nothing to see, m'lord."

"May I see it anyway?"

His voice hadn't changed. That was the worst part. It was still mild, still patient, the voice of a man asking to borrow a pen. But the market noise seemed to have thinned around them, the way it did when something predatory entered a space, and Jude realized that the pigeons had gone silent. Every bird on every roof within sight of her cart had stopped moving.

They knew. They always knew first.

Her hand moved before her fear could argue. She swept up the whistle, and as she did she let her thumb cover the air-hole and hummed—one note, low, deep in her chest, the deep speech's smallest cousin, a sound no human ear would catch but every enchantment would feel. The whistle flared warm against her palm. *Burn,* she told it. *Clean. Nothing left.*

Old magic fought. For half a heartbeat the whistle resisted, humming louder, wanting to live, wanting to wake the whole city's beasts to defend itself—and then it obeyed, because deep speech outranked everything, and it came apart in her hand like ash held too long. Warm dust sifted between her fingers. No smoke. No flame. Just wood becoming nothing, the way old magic did when it was unmade properly.

By the time the inquisitor looked up from the luck-knot, Jude Amberdale was holding a handful of ordinary sawdust, which she tipped casually into the gutter.

"Splinters," she said apologetically. "Told you. Enthusiasm, no skill."

The gray cloak studied her for a long moment. Mild eyes, patient hands, the open-eye clasp catching the light. Somewhere behind those mild eyes, Jude would have bet her life, a ledger was being written. Her name. Her street. Her face.

"Of course," he said at last. He set a copper coin on the cart. "For the luck-knot. I find myself suddenly in need of fortune."

He walked away. He did not hurry. At the mouth of the square he paused, spoke briefly to a man Jude hadn't noticed—a second gray cloak, posted at the corner like furniture—and then continued on, unhurried as morning.

Jude stood behind her cart with her heart slamming and her hands steady, because they had to be steady, because the whole square was watching the charm seller who was boring, just boring, nothing to see here.

The coin on the cart was warm. She hadn't touched it yet, but it was warm, and she knew—with the certainty of the beasts, with the certainty of the deep places in her that she spent her whole life pretending didn't exist—that the warmth wasn't from his pocket.

It was a marker. A claim. *We know where you sit.*

Above her, Aldous landed on the awning, breathless, feathers ruffled. "Bram's coming," the pigeon panted. "He says—Jude, he says the rats have been screaming since dawn. All of them. Every sewer, every wall. They say the soldiers aren't mapping houses."

Jude closed her hand around the warm coin.

"They're mapping *people*," Aldous said. "And the lists have a name for what goes on them. The gifted. Jude—they're hunting the gifted."

The market roared on around her, bright and loud and oblivious. Jude smiled her boring smile at no one, and began, very quietly, to plan how to burn her whole life down before the gray cloaks came back to collect it.
