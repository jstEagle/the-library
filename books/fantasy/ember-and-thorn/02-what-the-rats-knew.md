## 2. What the Rats Knew

The rats came to her at dawn, and they were afraid.

Jude knew it before she opened her eyes—knew it the way she knew rain coming, a pressure behind the teeth, a wrongness in the air. The walls of her tenement room were thin as parchment, and through them came the whisper-scratch of small claws, dozens of them, gathering in the gap between the floorboards and the ceiling of the flat below. Not the usual dawn traffic. Not the usual greedy, chattering scramble toward whatever crumbs the night had left.

This was a council.

She sat up in the gray light, hair sticking to her cheek, and put her palm flat against the floor. "All right," she murmured. "I'm listening."

The voice that answered was not one voice but many, layered like wet leaves—the way rat-speech always was, a chorus pretending to be an individual. *Small-mother. Small-mother with the open ears.* They called her that because she fed them, because she listened, because three winters ago she'd bargained a baker's whole sack of spoiled flour away from extermination by promising the rats would never touch his good stock. A stupid bargain. A kind one. The kind that kept her fed on gossip instead of coin most weeks.

*Soldiers,* the chorus said. *Men in the king's colors, walking with paper. Walking with measuring strings.*

Jude frowned. "Measuring what?"

*Houses. Doors. Windows. Counting the hearths. Writing down who sleeps where.* A pause, a ripple of whisker-twitching unease she felt more than heard. *They ask about dreams. About voices. About people who talk to things that cannot talk.*

The cold went through her all at once, from the floorboards up.

"They said *gifted?*" she asked quietly. "That word?"

*Gifted,* the rats confirmed, and the word came back to her wrapped in their fear like a blade wrapped in cloth. *A man in gray asked the sewer-kin under Copperlane. He had cheese soaked in something sweet. Two of ours took it. Only one came back.*

Jude sat very still for a long moment.

Poisoned bait and questions. Measuring strings and ledgers. In Thornhollow you learned to read trouble the way sailors read weather, and this—this was not a squall. This was the pressure drop before something enormous.

"Thank you," she said at last. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Stay out of the drains on the east side. Whatever's in the cheese, I'll bring you something clean tonight. Bread and drippings."

*Small-mother is kind,* they said, without conviction, and poured away through the walls like water finding its level.

She dressed in the dark, hands steady out of long practice even while her mind ran ahead of her. By the time she'd pinned her hair back and tucked her charm-box under her arm, she had already built the morning's face: the tired smile of a trinket-seller, nothing more. A woman who sold luck-knots and fever-charms that were just knotted thread and dried herbs and confidence. Harmless. Registered with the Guild of Small Trades, stamp and everything, bought with two months of saved coppers. If anyone ever looked—and no one ever had—there was paper to show.

Paper. She thought of soldiers walking with paper, and her stomach turned over.

* * *

The market at Wren's Cross was already roaring when she set out her tray.

Thornhollow woke hungry and loud: fish-hawkers crying the night's catch off the canal barges, the bell of the tallow-chandler tolling seven, cart wheels grinding through streets still slick with fog off the river. Jude loved it and hated it in equal measure, the way you love and hate a house that shelters you but knows too much about you. Ten thousand people, and every one of them a wall between her and the crown. Ten thousand people, and any one of them could be the crack in the wall.

"Pigeons," she said under her breath, scattering a handful of stale roll across the cobbles beside her stall. "Anything new?"

The pigeons were terrible gossips and worse liars, but they saw everything from above. The flock boss—a scarred old bird missing two toes, whom she privately called the Admiral—strutted through the crumbs and delivered his report in that flat, cooing arrogance only pigeons could manage.

*Tall men. Gray cloaks. On the bridges. Watching faces, not wares.*

"How many?"

*More than wings care to count. They stand where the crowds narrow.* The Admiral pecked a crumb with unnecessary violence. *One stood outside the Guildhall till dark yesterday. Asking after charm-sellers.*

Jude's hands did not shake. She'd trained them not to, years ago, when shaking hands meant questions and questions meant the register and the register meant—

Well. Nobody knew exactly what it meant, for gift-bearers. That was the horror of it. There had never been enough of them caught to find out.

She sold charms for four hours. Fever-knots, sleep-knots, a birthing charm for the cooper's wife, a luck-braid for a boy shipping out on the morning tide. Ordinary work, honest work, work that would keep her alive if her real trade ever got her killed. Between customers she watched the bridges.

There. Gray cloak, south end of Tanner's Bridge, standing just where the crowd funneled to three bodies wide. Not moving. Not selling anything, not begging, not doing any of the thousand idle things a man in a market city does. Just watching faces go by, and now and then writing something in a little book.

Another on the far side of the square, near the fountain. Another, maybe, half-glimpsed at the mouth of Rope Walk.

Her skin crawled. She smiled at a customer, took a copper for a fever-knot, made change, wished the woman's mother health. All the while some cold, quiet part of her mind counted gray cloaks and mapped exits: Rope Walk to the canal stairs, canal stairs to the water, or the alley behind the chandler's that cut through to Cooper's Lane, or—

"Jude."

Bram.

He came around the corner of the tallow-chandler's with a barrel of tar on one shoulder like it weighed nothing, which it didn't, and set it down by her stall with the deliberate casualness of a man delivering a message in public. Six feet of dockworker in a faded shirt, forearms like mooring rope, and eyes that had once been a knight's eyes and still missed nothing. His beard was going gray at the chin. He'd been going gray since the day they'd stripped his spurs and his name in the same breath, eight years ago, and he'd landed in Thornhollow like a stone dropped in deep water—no splash, just gone down and stayed down.

"You've heard," he said. Not a question.

"The rats told me at dawn. The pigeons agree." She kept her voice light, market-light, smiling at a passing customer. "Soldiers with measuring strings."

"Not just soldiers." Bram crouched to fuss with the tar barrel's hoop, putting his face close to hers, his voice dropping beneath the market noise. "I unload the grain barges, Jude. Grain comes down from the royal granaries. So do the escort riders. Last night there were six wagons through the east gate with no grain in them. Iron-barred. New. And the men riding escort weren't wearing livery. Gray cloaks."

"Inquisitors."

"Worse." His jaw worked. "I talked to a rider's horse."

Jude went very still. Bram couldn't speak to beasts—he was as deaf to them as any man—but he'd spent twenty years around her kind before the fall, and he knew how to get information out of a horse the honest way: sugar, patience, and a low voice. "And?"

"And the horse was terrified. Horses are always a bit afraid, but this—" He shook his head slowly. "It kept saying—well, you know horses, they say things simple. It kept saying *the gray house, the gray house, we take them to the gray house and they don't come back.*"

"The gray house," Jude repeated.

"There's a manor upriver past the toll bridge. Crown property, been shut for years. Looks like it's not shut anymore." Bram straightened, took up his barrel, became again just a dockworker passing time with a charm-seller. But his eyes stayed on hers. "Pip's been asking after you at the docks. Twice this week. Boy's got a nose for trouble."

"He's got a nose for worrying," Jude said. "It's different."

"It isn't." Bram hefted the barrel. "Whatever's coming, it's coming slow, the way bad weather does. Don't wait for the first thunder, girl. Get your affairs in order." He paused. "Get *out*, if it comes to it. You know I'd come with you."

"I know," she said, and meant it, and watched him merge into the dock crowd with his barrel and his careful nothing-to-see walk, and felt the dread settle into her bones like damp.

* * *

Pip was waiting at the tenement when she got home, which meant he'd climbed the drainpipe again, which meant she'd have to yell at him about the drainpipe again.

He was fifteen, all elbows and restless feet, sitting cross-legged on her doorstep with the particular studied boredom of a boy trying very hard to look like he hadn't been waiting for hours. A noble's son gone to seed: fine bones, quick dark eyes, clothes chosen carefully to look worse than they were. He'd turned up on her stall two years ago with a split lip and a story about a fall down stairs, and Jude—who knew what bruises from falling down stairs actually looked like—took one look and brought him home. He'd never told her the whole truth of whose son he was. She'd never asked. Some doors you leave closed because the thing behind them is heavier than both of you can lift.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm a businesswoman. Businesswomen are entitled to be late." She unlocked the door. "Off my step. You've been sitting on my step for hours, don't lie to me, I can see the imprint of your stubbornness in the wood."

"Three hours," Pip admitted, following her in. Then, before she could get the kettle on: "Jude. Something's happening."

"I know."

"Do you?" He was on his feet, pacing the little room, restless as a cat in a sack. "Because I don't think you do. I was at the docks—don't make the face, I wasn't stealing, I was *listening*—and there were men posting notices on the customs house. Big ones. Wax seals the size of my fist. The dockhands wouldn't go near them. Old Tammo read it out loud and then he went quiet, and Tammo doesn't go quiet, Tammo talks through executions—"

"Pip."

"—and he said it was a decree. A decree from the crown, sealed with the Regent's own mark, calling for the registration of all persons bearing unnatural gifts, whatever that means, and Jude, *whatever that means* means *you*, doesn't it, it means people like you, and they're going door to door with lists—"

"*Pip.*"

He stopped. Breathless, wild-eyed, fifteen years old and scared in the way that made him loud.

Jude set the kettle on the hook anyway, because her hands wanted something to do. "Sit down."

"Don't tell me to sit down—"

"Sit *down*, Pip." He sat. She took the stool across from him, knees almost touching, and made herself smile, the market smile, the everything-is-fine smile. "First: you were listening at the customs house, which is exactly the sort of thing that gets boys noticed by men in gray cloaks, so we're going to have a conversation later about your talent for being exactly where trouble is thickest. Second: a notice on a customs house is ink. Ink isn't soldiers yet. We don't run from ink."

"And when it's soldiers?"

She didn't answer fast enough, and he saw it.

"That's what I thought." Pip leaned forward, urgent, grabbing her wrist—not pleading, not quite; demanding, the way only a runaway noble's son could demand, as though fear were a thing you could command. "Jude, listen to me. My father taught me how power works. Power doesn't post notices unless it already knows what it wants. Registration means lists. Lists mean names. Names mean *doors*. You think they're going to knock politely and write things down and leave? They're building barred wagons, Bram says—I heard him telling the harbor master—they're building *wagons*, Jude, wagons with iron bars, and you want to wait for the knock?"

"Who told you about the wagons?"

"Bram. Who told *you*?"

"The rats," she said, "and the pigeons, and a horse, apparently. The whole city is talking except the people." She tried to make it a joke. It came out thin.

Pip wasn't laughing. "Run. Tonight. Now. Take the river road, take the marsh road, I don't care, take *any* road. I've got coin—"

"You've got stolen coin—"

"I've got *coin*," he insisted, "and you've got that face you make when you're deciding to be brave instead of smart, and I hate that face, Jude, I hate it—"

"Pip." She covered his hand with hers. His fingers were ice. Fifteen years old, and scared enough to freeze in a warm room. "Look at me."

He looked at her. Under all the sharpness he was just a boy who'd run from a house with too many locked rooms, and she was the only door he'd found that stayed open.

"We don't run tonight," she said quietly. "We watch. We prepare. I'll move the important things to Bram's. I'll sell nothing tomorrow, talk to no one, stay home. If the gray cloaks come to Wren's Cross, if they come to *this street*, then we go—Bram, you, me, the whole ragged family, out the marsh road before the gates wake up. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Pip said instantly, which told her he'd already decided they were leaving regardless, and would simply be patient about it.

"Pip."

"Agreed," he repeated, with the sincerity of a boy who had lied to better people than her and gotten away with it.

She let it go. Outside, the fog was coming off the canal in slow white sheets, and somewhere across the rooftops a dog began to bark—not the ordinary territorial barking of a Thornhollow evening, but frantic, rhythmic, the sound of a dog barking at something it didn't understand.

Then another dog joined it. Then another, streets away, until the whole city was speaking in the only language it had left.

Jude went to the window and listened past the dogs, past the fog, down into the walls and the foundations and the sewers where her small frightened subjects lived their short clever lives.

The rats were running. All of them, westward, away from the river, away from the gray house, in a tide of claw-click terror that had no words in it at all.

Dread arrived ahead of the danger, her mother used to say. Learn to trust it, because it's the only messenger that never lies.

Jude stood in the window with the kettle going cold behind her and the city barking itself hoarse, and trusted it completely.

Three days later, the proclamation came.
