## 3. The Proclamation

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Jude heard them from the loft above the charm stall—six strokes where there should have been none, a seventh that cracked halfway through like a man clearing his throat before bad news. Thornhollow's bells had opinions. Everyone knew that. The harbor bell rang for fires and drownings. The temple bell rang for weddings and funerals and the occasional tax. But the palace bell in the high square hadn't sounded in eleven years, not since the old king's funeral, and when it rang now it didn't ring so much as *announce*, each stroke landing on the rooftops like a boot on a stair.

She lay still under her blanket and listened to the pigeons.

The pigeons were wrong. That was the first thing she understood, before thought, before fear. The flock that roosted in the eaves of the dye-house across the lane had gone silent—not sleeping-silent but *held*-silent, two hundred small bodies pressed flat against the shingles, hearts hammering in a rhythm she could feel through the floorboards like rain.

*Bad thing coming,* said the oldest hen, the one with the crooked keel who had once traded Jude three crumbs of gossip about the harbormaster's mistress for a heel of bread. *Bad thing wearing red.*

Jude dressed in the dark. She did not light the lamp. She climbed to the roof ridge with the ease of nine years' practice and looked down into the high square, where the crowd was already gathering the way crowds gather everywhere—slowly at first, the curious and the idle, then all at once, because someone shouted *the Regent* and after that nobody wanted to be anywhere else.

The platform had been built overnight. That was the detail that made her stomach drop: overnight. Soldiers in gray-and-crimson had raised it while the city slept, which meant they'd brought the timbers with them, which meant this was planned, scheduled, *expected*. She counted forty spears along the square's edges. She counted the gray cloaks—three of them, standing apart, faces hidden, still as furniture—and her skin crawled the way it always crawled near inquisitors, some animal part of her insisting they could smell what she was.

A herald in white took the platform. Unrolled a scroll long enough to hang past his knees.

"By order of the crown," he read, and his voice carried on a wind that seemed conveniently arranged, "and by the grace of the Regent, keeper of the realm until the king's return—"

Nobody in the crowd murmured at that. Eleven years, and no one asked where the king had gone. You learned early in Vaelmoor which questions cost teeth.

"—a Great Hunt is declared throughout the kingdom. All persons bearing forbidden gifts—the speaking of beasts, the bending of sight, the whispering of things unseen—are commanded to present themselves for registration within thirty days."

Jude's hands went cold on the roof tiles.

"Registration is an act of mercy," the herald continued. "Those who register will be examined, catalogued, and *taken into the crown's care*, where their afflictions may be studied for the good of the realm." A pause, professionally weighted. "Those who do not register will be sought. Those who shelter them will be charged as accomplices. The reward for information leading to the capture of a gift-bearer is fifty crowns."

Fifty crowns. She heard the number move through the crowd below like a current through water—a low collective intake of breath, the sound of fifty crowns being weighed against neighbors, against cousins, against the odd quiet family down the lane whose dog never barked at strangers. Fifty crowns was four years of dock work. Fifty crowns was a new roof and a winter's coal and a daughter's dowry.

*They'll sell us,* said the crooked-keel hen from the eaves behind her, matter-of-fact as ever. *They sell everything. Last winter they sold the dead man's boots off his feet before he was cold.*

"I know," Jude whispered.

"The Hunt begins at dawn," said the herald, and rolled up his scroll, and the drums started.

She stayed on the roof until midmorning, watching. Watching was the whole of her trade lately, though she sold charms to pay for it. She watched the soldiers nail proclamations to every doorpost and bridge rail, the paper heavy with wax seals, the words printed twice—once in letters for those who could read, once in a crude picture beneath: a stick figure with a serpent's tongue, crossed out. She watched a fishwife squint at the picture, laugh, and say something to her neighbor that made them both laugh, and then stop laughing, and look around, and walk away fast.

She watched Bram push through the crowd with his shoulders doing what his shoulders always did—making room, ending arguments before they started—and stop in front of a proclamation nailed to the fountain rail. He stood there a long time. He was too far away for her to read his face, but she'd known him six years, since the day he'd fished her out of the harbor canal after a customs man got curious about her stall, and she didn't need to see his face. His back said enough. His back said *I told you so* without any satisfaction in it at all, which was somehow worse.

He looked up—straight at her rooftop, which meant he'd known exactly where she'd be—and jerked his chin toward the tenements. *Come down. Now.*

She came down.

* * *

Bram was waiting in the alley behind the stall, arms crossed, filling most of it. He'd been a knight once, before whatever happened at Redford Bridge, and he'd never entirely stopped being built like one—big through the chest, scarred across the knuckles, with the kind of face that made drunk men apologize. Only his eyes gave away the disgrace: they moved constantly, checking exits, checking hands, checking the sky.

"You saw it," he said. Not a question.

"I saw it."

"And?"

"And I'm thinking."

"Don't think too loud." He glanced up at the roofline out of habit, though there was nothing up there but gulls. "Pip's already packed."

"Pip packs when the weather changes."

"He packed your mother's knife, Jude. Your spare cloak. My old shield-boss from under the floorboards." Bram's jaw worked. "The boy's been waiting for this day since the rats started talking. Don't pretend you haven't."

She leaned against the doorframe and made herself breathe. Below them, the market was carrying on in that strained, over-loud way markets carry on when everyone is listening sideways—hawkers hawking harder than usual, laughter arriving a half-beat late. Somewhere a soldier's boot heels clicked past the alley mouth, unhurried, counting.

"Registration," she said. "Then taken. Those are two different words, Bram. They put them in the same sentence so you'd hear the second one late."

"I know how sentences work."

"Do you? Because half the city is going to hear *mercy* and line up." Her voice came out sharper than she intended. "An old woman came by yesterday asking if I had a charm for restful sleep. She's got a grandson who talks to moths. Sweet boy. Thinks the moths tell him secrets. She was asking whether it might—whether maybe registering would get him *help*." Jude pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Fifty crowns, Bram. And people who think the crown is kind."

Bram was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he used for hard truths, the one with no comfort in it and no cruelty either. "Your friend the hedge-witch. Old Sera. The one who kept you the winter your mother left."

Ice went down her spine. "What about her?"

"She lives outside the walls. Cottages by the leper road. No watch, no neighbors worth bribing." He met her eyes. "If I were planning a hunt, I'd start where the taking was easy. Practice on the ones nobody counts."

"Sera's careful. She hasn't worked a real working in years, she just sells teas and tells fortunes with the cards face-down—"

"Jude."

"—she wouldn't even show up on whatever they're using to find us, she's too old and too careful, she's been careful her whole life—"

"*Jude.*" He said it the way he said it when she was fourteen and about to do something stupid. "Go see her. Today. Before dark. Tell her to come inside the walls or go further out—it doesn't matter which, only that she moves. Can you do that much?"

"Yes," Jude said. "Yes. Fine."

But the day got away from her, the way days do when you're poor and watched. A customs clerk came by the stall at noon with new forms—vendor registration, updated fees, a space on the form for *household members and dependents* that hadn't been there last season. Pip materialized out of the crowd to stand at her elbow looking nobly bored while she filled it out in lies, and afterward he wouldn't leave, trailing her through the afternoon like a small anxious shadow, so that by the time she shook him loose with an errand and slipped out the Marsh Gate, the sun was already bleeding into the rooftops and the light on the leper road had gone the color of old honey.

Too late, she thought. Too late, but she went anyway, because Sera had fed her the winter her mother vanished, had taught her which herbs hid a scent and which lies hid a gift, had held her hair back the night she cried herself sick over a mother who'd walked north and never written. You didn't let debts like that stand. Not today of all days.

* * *

Sera's cottage sat alone where the leper road bent toward the fens, smoke curling thin from its chimney, herb rows gone leggy and autumnal in the yard. Everything looked ordinary. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the silence—no goat, no chickens, no muttering from the garden. Sera talked to her plants the way other old women talked to cats. The plants were silent too, and when Jude brushed her mind against the hedge sparrows roosting in the elder bush, they were packed tight and trembling, all facing the house.

*Men in gray,* said the eldest sparrow. *Came at dusk. Quiet men. They are still inside.*

Jude stopped moving. Twenty yards from the door, half-hidden by the hedge, heart going like a trapped bird.

There were three of them. Gray cloaks, faces shadowed, and between them in the doorway—no. Being brought *through* the doorway. Sera, in her nightdress with a shawl dragged over it, barefoot, her wrists bound in front of her with something that wasn't rope. It moved wrong. It flexed like muscle, like something alive, and where it touched her skin her skin went gray too, fading at the wrists like ash spreading.

She wasn't struggling. That was the worst of it. She was walking the way people walk when the fight has already been argued out of them, head high out of pure stubborn habit, and as the gray men turned her toward the wagon waiting on the road, her eyes swept the yard—the herb rows, the elder bush, the hedge—

And found Jude.

For one heartbeat the old woman's face did everything at once: fear, recognition, fury, and underneath it all, ferocious and unmistakable, *go*. Her mouth shaped the word without sound. *Go, girl.* Then one of the gray men shifted, and a hood came down over her hair, and she was only a shape being loaded into a wagon like a sack of meal.

Jude's body tried to move. She would swear afterward that it tried—that her legs gathered, that her weight tipped forward, that every instinct she owned screamed to run at those three calm shapes and do *something*, scream, bite, burn, anything rather than stand in a hedge like a rabbit while the woman who saved her life was folded into a wagon bed.

But her mind held her the way it had trained itself to hold her for nine years. Three of them. Gray cloaks. What are you going to do, Jude? What can you *do*? You're a girl with a voice and a stall full of tin trinkets, and if you step out of that hedge you die in this yard, and Pip waits at home, and Bram waits, and everyone who loves you learns what happens to people who love a gifted girl. The wagon wheels creaked. The gray men didn't speak. There was nothing to bargain with, no rat to bribe, no pigeon to send—only the arithmetic of survival, done and done again, always coming out the same: *stay hidden, stay hidden, stay hidden.*

The wagon rolled north. The sparrows didn't make a sound until it was out of sight.

Then Jude sat down in the dirt of the leper road, in the last gray light, and was sick into the ditch, and after that she just sat, because her legs had finally gotten their vote.

Inside the cottage a kettle had been left on the hearth. On the table, laid out neat as a Sunday, were two cups of tea—one large, one small. The small one was hers. Sera had known she was coming. Had set out the cup, put the kettle on, and waited, knowing exactly what dusk would bring, choosing to be taken with her door unlatched rather than run and lead the hunt to anyone else.

On the small cup's saucer, in flour dusted over the glaze, someone had written a single word in a shaky old hand:

*NORTH.*

Jude stared at it until the light failed, and the word did not change, and neither did the truth sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone: they were taking the gifted north. To feed the crown, the rats had said, weeks ago, and she had paid them in bread and filed it away as gossip.

Her mother had gone north three years ago.

Jude picked up the saucer, careful as handling an eggshell, and carried it into the dark.
