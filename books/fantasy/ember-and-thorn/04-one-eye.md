## 4. One-Eye

The knock came at midnight, and it was wrong.

Not the rattle of a fist on wood—Jude had heard every kind of knocking Thornhollow's tenements could produce, from drunk neighbors to debt collectors to Pip sneaking home past curfew with his boots in his hand. This was different. Three slow, deliberate thuds, spaced like a heartbeat deciding whether to keep going. And beneath them, so faint she might have imagined it, a smell that didn't belong on the fourth floor of a rotting boarding house: pine, cold river water, and something wild and musky that raised every hair on her arms.

She lay still in her cot and listened.

The rats would have told her if someone was climbing the stairs. She'd paid them their crusts that evening—a heel of black bread and half a dried plum, spread across the windowsill like an offering—and old Whisker, the scarred matriarch who ruled the walls of Amberdale Row, had given her the usual report in exchange: *soldiers on Cordwainer Street, chalk marks on doors, a cart of cages going north.* Nothing about footsteps. Nothing about anyone coming up.

But the knocking came again, and this time it was followed by a voice.

"Jude Amberdale."

Low. Rough as gravel dragged over stone. A voice that sounded like it had been buried for years and dug up again, still wearing its dirt. It spoke through the door without effort, without raising itself, the way you'd speak to someone across a table rather than through three inches of oak.

Jude sat up slowly, heart hammering against her ribs. Her gift was quiet—no beasts nearby except the rats in the walls, and they were huddled in their nests, silent as stones. Whatever stood on the other side of that door was not a rat. Not a pigeon. Not anything small enough to be safe.

"I know you're awake," the voice said. "Your breathing changed."

*So did mine,* Jude thought wildly, and then hated herself for thinking it.

She rose without a sound, bare feet finding the cold floorboards by memory. There was a poker by the hearth, its iron head still faintly warm from the fire she'd banked hours ago. She took it. The door had no lock worth the name—she'd wedged a chair under the knob when she moved in, and Pip had laughed at her until she explained, very patiently, exactly what happened to girls alone in boarding houses when the wrong person knocked. He hadn't laughed since.

"Who's there?" she called, keeping her voice flat. Unremarkable. The voice of a tired woman woken at an inconvenient hour, nothing more.

"A friend," said the voice, and then, after a pause that stretched long enough for Jude to count four of her own heartbeats: "No. That's a lie, and your mother taught me better than to lie to her daughter. I'm not a friend. I'm a debt."

The poker dipped in her grip. "My mother is dead," she said. "Or gone. Same thing."

"She's not dead." The words landed heavy, each one placed like a stone on a grave marker. "I'd know. I carried her secret for eleven years before she trusted me with yours."

Jude's mouth went dry. Eleven years ago she'd been nine years old, living above her mother's charm stall in the Shambles, learning which herbs soothed and which burned, learning—carefully, secretly, in whispers behind closed shutters—that the pigeons could tell her where the fish carts turned early and the rats knew which merchants watered their wine. Learning that her mother never once looked afraid when Jude told her these things. Only sad.

"Prove it," Jude whispered. "Tell me something only she would know."

A long silence. Then: "When you were six, you brought home a crow with a broken wing. You hid it in the flour bin. Your mother found it because the flour kept whispering—the bird was talking to it, or the flour was talking to the bird, she never worked out which. She didn't scold you. She sat down on the floor and cried, and when you asked why, she said, 'Because I hoped you'd be spared this, and now I know you won't.'"

The poker clattered to the floor.

Jude stood in the dark with her hand over her mouth, and the memory rose up whole and warm and terrible: the crow, the flour, her mother's tears soaking into her hair as she held her. She had forgotten that. She had *forgotten* it, packed it away somewhere deep behind the three empty years since her mother walked out the door and said, *I have business at the palace, little ember. Keep the stall. Keep your voice down.*

"Open the door," the voice said. Gently now. "Please. Standing in hallways is hard on old bones, even ones that aren't strictly my own."

Jude pulled the chair away and opened the door.

The hallway was dim, lit by one guttering tallow lamp at the far end. And filling most of it, standing on all fours with his head level with hers, was a wolf.

He was enormous—not the lean gray shadows that haunted the northern forests and featured in every cautionary tale her mother had ever told, but something older and heavier, built like a creature that had outlasted several centuries' worth of mistakes. His coat was the color of wet slate, shot through with silver at the muzzle and along his flanks. Scars crossed him everywhere: a torn ear, a seam of missing fur along his ribs, and worst of all, the left side of his face, where an old wound had taken his eye and left a puckered valley of flesh running from brow to jaw. One amber eye regarded her, patient and ancient and unbearably sad.

"You're—" Jude's voice cracked. She tried again. "You're a wolf."

"I'm a wolf," he agreed. "I'm also tired, hungry, and standing in a building that smells of mildew and despair. May I come in? I promise not to eat anything you'd miss."

She should have slammed the door. Every instinct honed by three years of hiding should have screamed at her to bar it, wedge the chair back under the knob, and press her ear to the wood while whatever this was lost interest and left. Instead she stepped aside, and the wolf padded past her into the room, moving with a strange, careful grace, avoiding the creaky board by the hearth without being told.

He settled onto the floor beside the dead fireplace with a sigh that seemed to come up from his toes. Up close, the smell of him was stronger—pine and river and underneath it something like struck flint, like the air before lightning.

"Pip," Jude managed. "Pip sleeps in the next room. If you—"

"The boy with the fine manners and the stolen boots?" The wolf's single eye glinted. "He's asleep. I passed his door. He snores like a man twice his size and dreams about falling off horses. He'll sleep till morning unless we're loud." A pause. "We shouldn't be loud."

"Who are you?" Jude crouched down across from him, keeping the low bed between them, though some part of her noted that the bed would stop precisely nothing. "What are you?"

"My mother called me Vael," the wolf said. "The men who took my eye called me *it*. You may call me One-Eye, if you like. Most do. It saves time." He lowered his great head onto his forepaws. "And what I am, girl, is the last of something, talking to the first of another something, in a city that will kill us both if we're heard. So let's be quick."

"First of what?"

"One-Eye," he said patiently, "you are speaking to a wolf. In your nightgown. At midnight. I think we can skip the part where you pretend you don't know what you are."

Heat flooded her face. Three years of hiding, three years of selling charms and trading crumbs for gossip and keeping her gift folded up small enough to fit inside a lie—and here was a stranger stripping it bare in a single sentence. "I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." One-Eye's ear twitched. "Your mother said the same thing, the first time. Word for word. 'I don't know what you mean,' she said, standing in her own doorway with flour on her hands, while a wolf told her she had a voice that could reach into things older than kingdoms." His eye softened. "She got better at lying less. Eventually."

Jude wrapped her arms around herself. "Where is she?"

"That," said One-Eye, "is the question, isn't it. And I've come a long way to answer it badly."

He told her then—or started to. He'd known her mother for eleven years, since before Jude was born, back when Sera Amberdale was a charm-seller with a dangerous voice and a talent for making herself unremarkable. He'd been her eyes in the wild places, her courier through country no rider could cross. When Jude was born, Sera had made him promise two things: watch the girl, and never let the girl know she was watched.

"So it was you," Jude breathed. "The winter the wolves circled our camp outside Thornhollow. The winter I was twelve. Everyone said it was a miracle we weren't attacked."

"It was me," One-Eye said. "And the winter the fever took half the Shambles, and the herbalist's cart arrived from the mountains two days early? Me again. I've been watching you fall down and get back up for nineteen years, Jude Amberdale. It's been the honor of my long, stupid life."

"Then where were you three years ago?" The question came out sharper than she intended, edged with three years of grief. "When she left. When she *went to the palace* and never came back. Where were you?"

One-Eye was quiet for a long moment. The lamp at the end of the hall guttered; somewhere below, a dog began to bark and was shushed.

"I was dying," he said finally. "In the Greybarrows, three hundred miles north. Poisoned by a hunter's arrow meant for my pack. My pack carried me toward the mountains and I told them to leave me, and I lay in a ravine for the better part of a season, too broken to move, listening to myself get closer to death. By the time I could walk again, she was gone. By the time I reached Thornhollow, the trail was three winters cold." His eye fixed on her. "But trails don't stay cold forever. Not for me. And six months ago, I picked up the end of it."

"Where?"

"Where it always ends," One-Eye said. "The palace."

The word dropped between them like a coin down a well. Jude thought of her mother's last morning—the good dress, the one with the blue embroidery she saved for fairs; the way she'd kissed the top of Jude's head and said *business at the palace*, so casually, as though the palace were a shop she visited weekly; the way she'd never once looked back through the window as Jude watched her go down Amberdale Row.

"She said it was business," Jude whispered. "Charm work. Court commissions. She used to sell to minor nobles sometimes."

"Sera Amberdale didn't go to the palace to sell charms." One-Eye rose, and the movement was slow, deliberate, an old soldier getting to his feet. "Sera Amberdale went to the palace to give herself up. There's a hunt on, girl. Has been for longer than the proclamation they nailed to the gates yesterday. The crown has been collecting people like you and your mother for years—quietly, one at a time, before the Great Hunt gave them permission to do it loudly. Your mother knew. She knew they were close to finding you. She'd seen the registers, seen the lists being drawn. So she went north and traded the one thing she had." His voice roughened. "Herself. For your name staying off those lists."

The room seemed to tilt. Jude gripped the bedframe. "That's not—she wouldn't—"

"She would," One-Eye said simply. "It's exactly what she'd do. I knew her eleven years. She was the bravest coward I ever met—afraid of everything, ran from nothing. And she loved you the way a banked fire loves the house it keeps warm: quietly, constantly, and willing to burn itself down to do it."

Jude's eyes were burning now. "Then we go get her. We go to the palace and—"

"No."

"We *go get her*—"

"*No.*" The word cracked like a whip, and the rats in the walls went utterly silent. One-Eye lowered his head, and when he spoke again his voice was gravel and grief ground together. "Girl. Listen to me. What the crown does with the taken, no one comes back from. Not whole. Not alive, maybe. Your mother knew the price when she paid it. She paid it so you could live a small, hidden life selling charms and gossiping with rats. And you want to march north and throw that away?"

"Yes," Jude said.

One-Eye stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a low, rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't opened in years. "Ah. There she is. There's Sera's girl." He shook his great head. "I argued with her mother too, you know. Same fire. It's a family curse."

"Then help me," Jude said. "You watched me my whole life. You owe her. You said it yourself—you're a debt. So pay it. Help me find her."

The wolf was silent for a long time. Outside, the city murmured its midnight murmur; a cart rattled over cobblestones three streets away; the tallow lamp down the hall finally drowned in its own wax and went dark, leaving only moonlight through the shutters, striping the floor in pale bars.

"If I help you," One-Eye said at last, "it's not a rescue. Understand that first. What's done to the taken happens in places under the palace, and I don't know what we'd find, or what's left to save. If I take you north, it's because the Hunt is coming here regardless—your rats told you about the chalk marks, yes? The soldiers mapping households? They're not mapping for taxes, girl. They're drawing a net. And this city is going to close around everyone in it who smells like us. Yours truly included." He exhaled through his nose, a plume of fog in the cold room. "So it's not a rescue. It's an escape that might become one. Escape first. Rescue later, if rescue's possible. Those are my terms."

Jude looked at him for a long moment—the scars, the single burning eye, the impossible fact of him sitting in her rented room like he belonged there. Three years of isolation pressed against the offer. Three years of doing everything alone, trusting no one, folding herself smaller every day. And here was a creature offering to crack that open, and terrified as she was, some starving part of her wanted to say yes just to stop being alone.

"What about Pip?" she asked.

"The boy stays or comes, his choice. But if he comes, he comes knowing what you are. No more secrets in the family you choose. Secrets are how the crown wins." One-Eye pushed himself upright, joints popping like green kindling. "I'll be at the marsh gate tomorrow, sundown. The old drainage arch where the beggars sleep. Come or don't. But decide fast—the net's drawing tighter every hour. Chalk marks today. Doors kicked tomorrow."

He moved to the window, and with impossible ease for something his size, slipped through the gap and onto the ledge beyond. Before he dropped into the dark, he paused, one amber eye catching the moonlight.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your mother asked me once, a long time ago, what I'd do if the crown ever came for someone I loved. I told her I'd run. She laughed at me and said, 'You'll fight. You always fight. Running's just fighting in the other direction.'" His eye gleamed. "She wasn't wrong. About either of us."

Then he was gone, silent as snowfall, and Jude stood alone in the moonlit room with her heart hammering and her mind racing and, for the first time in three years, a direction instead of a wall.

She looked at the door to Pip's room. Behind it, the boy snored on, dreaming about falling off horses, unaware that his life had just tilted on its axis.

Jude sat down on the edge of her cot and put her face in her hands.

Come morning, she'd have to tell him everything. Come sundown, she'd have to choose.

Outside, far off toward the river, a wolf began to howl—one long, low note that no city wolf had any right to sing, rising over the rooftops of Thornhollow like a flag being raised.

Jude listened until it faded.

Then she started packing.
