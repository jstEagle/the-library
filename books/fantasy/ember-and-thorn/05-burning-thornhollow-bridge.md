## 5. Burning Thornhollow Bridge

The rats woke her before the bells did.

Jude had learned to sleep with one ear tuned to the walls of the tenement, the way sailors sleep listening for weather. So when the whisper came skittering up through the floorboards—*boots, many boots, iron-shod, coming up Tanner's Row*—she was already moving before the first bell of dawn cracked the dark.

"Up," she hissed at Pip, shaking his shoulder. The boy came awake the way he always did now: instantly, silently, one hand already closing around the knife she'd given him. Three weeks ago he'd been a noble's son who flinched at loud voices. Now he was out of bed and dressed before her own boots were laced.

"The rats?" he asked.

"The rats." She'd told him about them on the marsh road's edge, in the safety of their little room above the dyer's shop—the way they spoke to her, the gossip they carried like coins in their cheeks. *Soldiers mapping houses. Lists being made.* Bram had said to wait, to be careful, to trust that careful people survived. Bram, who had once worn a knight's oath and now hauled fish crates on the docks because an inquisitor's report had stripped it from him.

The rats were saying something different this morning.

*Fire in the lantern room,* the oldest rat had said, the one missing half an ear who lived under the tannery. *They are not searching this time, girl-who-speaks. They are taking.*

She pressed her ear to the door and listened to the building breathe. Below, old Sera Venn was already stirring her dye vats; she could hear the wooden paddle knocking the vat sides, the shop's familiar heartbeat. Somewhere down the lane, a cart rattled. Normal sounds. Thornhollow waking up.

Then the normal sounds stopped.

Not faded—stopped, all at once, the way a forest goes quiet when something with teeth enters it. Even the gulls over the fish market cut off mid-cry. Jude felt the silence move up Tanner's Row like cold water rising.

"They're here," she breathed.

Pip was pale but steady. "Bram?"

"Bram's at the docks. He'll—" She stopped. He wouldn't know. He'd walk home from his shift straight into it.

A fist hit the door across the hall—the Marlow family's door—and a voice like a nail being pulled from wet wood announced itself to the whole floor: "By decree of the Crown of Vaelmoor. Open in the name of the Hunt."

The Hunt. Not registration anymore. Taking.

Jude grabbed Pip's wrist. "Back window. Now."

They went through the dyer's loft, past the racks of hanging cloth that smelled of mordant and ash, and out onto the sloping roof that overlooked the yard behind Sera Venn's shop. From there it was a drop to the neighbor's woodshed, then the alley, then the maze of lanes that every poor child in Thornhollow knew better than the soldiers ever would.

But as Jude dropped to the woodshed roof, she looked back toward the mouth of Tanner's Row—and saw them.

Gray cloaks. A dozen at least, moving house to house with the unhurried efficiency of men doing inventory. Behind them came wagons. Not prison carts—wagons with barred windows and iron rings bolted inside, the kind used for livestock. And walking beside the first wagon, holding a ledger against his chest like a holy book, was an inquisitor she recognized. The gray-cloaked man from the market, weeks ago, whose attention she had barely escaped. His face was forgettable in the way of all dangerous men, except for the eyes, which moved over everything and rested on nothing.

He stopped in front of Sera Venn's door.

"No," Pip whispered beside her. "No, no—she's not gifted, she's just old—"

"She sheltered me." Jude's voice came out flat and strange. "That's enough."

The inquisitor didn't knock. The door simply wasn't a door anymore—it was splinters, and two soldiers went through it into the dark of the shop, and there was a crash, and then Sera Venn's voice, high and furious and brave: "I've paid my taxes sixty years! I've buried a husband for the crown! What right—"

The voice cut off. Not screamed. Just—cut, like a hand over a mouth.

Jude's whole body wanted to go down there. Her hands were shaking with it. Every instinct she owned screamed that she could do something, say something, call the dogs in the yard or the rats in the walls or—

And One-Eye's voice rumbled up from memory, gravel and old grief: *If you speak, you die, and everyone you love dies after you, and nothing changes except the number of graves.*

She bit her lip until she tasted blood. She took Pip's hand. She ran.

* * *

They found Bram at the fish market, which meant Bram found them, because he was already running when they came around the corner of the salt sheds—big Bram, slow-seeming Bram, who moved like a landslide when he decided to move. He caught sight of them and changed direction without breaking stride, sweeping Pip up under one arm like a sack of meal.

"Docks," he growled. "There's a barge—"

"There's no barge," Jude said. "The gates."

Bram's face did something complicated. "How do you know?"

"The gulls left. All of them, at once. And the rats say the gates have been shut since before dawn." She swallowed. "Bram, they're not searching anymore. They're emptying the city."

For a moment the three of them stood in the shadow of the salt sheds while Thornhollow screamed quietly around them. Somewhere near the fountain square, a woman was crying a name over and over. Somewhere else, glass broke. The bells had begun ringing—not the hour, not alarm, just ringing, all of them at once, the way they did when the crown wanted the whole city to know that today was different from other days.

"We can't fight them," Bram said. It wasn't cowardice. It was arithmetic. He'd been a knight; he knew exactly what a dozen gray cloaks and a company of soldiers could do, and he knew what a dockworker, a boy, and a girl with a forbidden voice could do besides. "We can't fight them and we can't hide forever. So we run. Marsh road. Tonight if we can, now if we must."

"Now," Jude said. "While they're busy at the Row."

They went the back ways—through the tannery yard where the smell covered everything, along the drainage channel where the water came up to their knees, over a wall that Bram had to boost them both over and then couldn't quite manage himself until Pip hauled him up by the arm, laughing breathlessly, terrifiedly. For a few minutes it almost felt like a game, the kind of game children play in cities built on top of older cities, ducking watchmen and counting rooftops.

Then they reached the mouth of Cooper's Lane, and the game ended.

Between them and the marsh gate stood a checkpoint. Six soldiers, an inquisitor, a wagon, and a brazier burning something sweet and wrong that stank of the same magic-smell Jude had caught once in the market—a smell like hot copper and burnt hair. A sniffer-pyre. Burning harvested gift, drawing out anyone who carried it like smoke draws bees.

Jude felt it pull at her immediately, a faint tug behind her ribs, gentle and horrible, like fingers combing through her hair looking for lice.

"It can feel me," she whispered.

"Then we don't go through," Bram said. "We go over." He nodded upward—at the old bridge, Thornhollow Bridge, the great stone span that crossed the marsh canal and carried the north road out of the city. No checkpoint on the bridge itself; nobody crossed it on foot anymore since the tollgate below had been built. But its arches rose over the canal, and its parapet was chest-high, and beyond it lay the marshes and freedom.

"That's a forty-foot drop on the far side," Pip said.

"Into water," Bram said. "Probably."

"Probably," Pip repeated faintly.

They climbed. It was easier than it should have been, because the city was screaming and nobody looked up. Jude went first, then Pip, then Bram hauling himself up hand over hand with the grim competence of a man who had once scaled siege walls for a living. From the top of the arch, the whole city spread out below them: the smoking tenements of Tanner's Row, the gray cloaks moving through the streets like ink through water, the wagons already half full.

And on the far side of the bridge, at the marsh gate, soldiers were swinging the great doors closed.

"Not yet," Jude breathed. "They're not shut yet—"

"They will be by the time we're down," Bram said. "So we jump, and we run, and we don't stop."

It was Pip who saw them first. "Jude." His voice had gone very small. "Jude, look."

At the foot of the bridge, dismounting from a horse too fine for any soldier, stood the gray-cloaked inquisitor from the market. From Tanner's Row. He hadn't been leading the raid. He'd been waiting here, at the only road out, the way a spider waits at the throat of its web.

As they watched, he raised one hand, and the soldiers at the gate began to close the doors.

"He knows," Jude said. The knowledge settled into her like a stone dropping into deep water. "Somehow he knows someone's leaving. Maybe he doesn't know who. Maybe he doesn't care."

"Jump now, argue later," Bram said—and then he did something Jude would spend the rest of her life trying to repay.

He stepped back off the arch.

Down, onto the bridge itself, into plain view, landing with a crack of stone and standing up out of a crouch with his arms wide, filling the whole width of the span, a big man making himself bigger. "OI!" he bellowed, in the parade-ground voice that had once commanded a hundred men. "You want the gifted? Here's a disgraced knight of Vaelmoor, come to give you lot a lesson in manners!"

Every head turned. Every crossbow swung. The inquisitor's forgettable face tilted up, interested.

"Bram—" Jude started.

"Go." He didn't look back at her. His voice was steady and warm and absolutely final. "Marsh road, the drowned church, wait three days. If I'm not there, go on without me and don't you dare come back." He rolled his shoulders, and grinned his terrible grin, and drew the short dock-worker's knife he'd carried since the day they'd taken his sword. "Now, gentlemen. Who's first?"

Jude grabbed Pip and jumped.

The fall lasted forever. The canal water hit like a slap, cold enough to stop her heart, and then she was surfacing, dragging Pip up beside her, gasping, and the world above the bank was noise and torchlight and Bram's voice roaring defiance, and the sound of the first crossbow bolt slamming into stone.

"Swim!" she choked. "Pip, swim, we have to—"

They swam. The canal was thirty feet wide and it might as well have been thirty miles. Behind them, Bram's voice never wavered—he kept talking, kept taunting, kept every eye and every bolt on himself, and Jude understood with a clarity that felt like dying that he was spending himself, coin by coin, minute by minute, to buy them the width of a canal.

Her feet found mud. She hauled Pip up the reedy bank, and they ran, dripping, into the marsh grass, and behind them the gates of Thornhollow boomed shut like a coffin lid.

* * *

They ran until the city was a smudge of torchlight behind them and the marsh had swallowed the sound. Then Pip collapsed, coughing canal water, and Jude knelt beside him in the black reeds with her heart still hammering Bram's name.

A shape loomed out of the dark. Huge. Gray. Eyes catching the starlight—one of them dead white.

One-Eye had found them. Of course he had. He'd probably been watching the bridge all night.

"You saw," Jude said. It wasn't a question.

"I saw." The wolf's voice was gravel over old grief. "The knight bought you a mile. Perhaps two. They will take him to the harvesting cells, little speaker. That is what they do with strong ones."

"Then we go back."

"No."

"Then I go back—"

"*No.*" The word came out of the wolf like a landslide. In the dark his single living eye fixed on her, ancient and pitiless and kind. "You go back, and you are taken, and the knight's sacrifice buys nothing. You stay free, and it buys everything. That is the arithmetic of sacrifice, Jude Amberdale. Learn it, or make his spending worthless."

Jude knelt in the freezing mud and shook. Not from cold—or not only from cold. Rage moved through her like floodwater, rage at the gray cloaks, at the wagons with their iron rings, at Sera Venn's voice cut off mid-sentence, at the gates booming shut on everything she had ever loved. And underneath the rage, helplessness, vast and oily, the feeling of being small in a world run by men with ledgers.

"I couldn't save her," she said. "Sera Venn. I stood on a roof and watched."

"Yes," One-Eye said simply. "You did. And tomorrow you will stand somewhere else and watch something else, unless you become something that cannot be watched. Grieve later. Walk now."

Pip got up. He didn't say anything—just put his small cold hand in hers, and the three of them moved off into the marshes, away from the burning bridge-light of Thornhollow, toward the drowned country where no ledgers followed.

Behind them, the city bells rang on and on, and Jude made herself a promise in the language she was forbidden to speak aloud: that she would come back. That she would find Bram in whatever cell they'd put him in. That the men with the wagons would learn what happens when you hunt a girl who talks to wolves.

Somewhere far behind, faint on the wind, she thought she heard a wolf howl answering her anger—and it sounded almost like laughter.

Or maybe it sounded like a warning.

Either way, she walked north.
