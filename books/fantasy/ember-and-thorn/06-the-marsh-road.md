## 6. The Marsh Road

The marshes began where the road ended, which Pip decided was the most honest thing the world had done in days.

One moment there was packed dirt and hedgerows and the last gray smudge of Thornhollow behind them; the next, the land simply gave up on being land. Water lay everywhere—shallow and brown-gold, threaded with reeds, stitched over with a skin of green scum that looked solid until you stepped on it. The sky sat so low it seemed to be leaking. Somewhere out in all that drowned country, frogs were singing to each other in long croaking lines, like a choir that had lost its conductor.

Pip hated it immediately.

He hated it because it was flat. There was nowhere to hide up high, no rooftops, no bell towers, no convenient chimney to wedge himself into while soldiers passed below. In Thornhollow he had survived three years by knowing every angle of the city, every shadow that fell at the right hour. Out here the only angles were the horizon and his own knees, and neither one was going to save him.

"You're walking like the ground insulted you," Jude said.

She was ahead of him, skirts hitched up and tied around her thighs, boots black to the knee with marsh water. She moved through the reeds the way she moved through everything—like she had somewhere better to be but wasn't going to rush about proving it.

"It did," Pip said. "It's soft and it lies."

"Everything soft lies." She glanced back at him, and for a second her mouth almost did something that wasn't a grimace. "Step where I step. The green scum is a liar. The brown water is honest. The places where the reeds grow thickest are usually firm underneath—the roots hold the mud together."

He filed that away. That was the thing about Jude Amberdale: half her sentences sounded like insults and the other half were survival instructions, and you learned fast to catch the second kind before they sank.

Behind them both, One-Eye padded through the shallows without making a sound. A wolf the size of a pony should have splashed. This one didn't. The water seemed to part around his legs rather than swallow them, and when he shook himself, the droplets flew off in a perfect ring, not one landing on Pip's collar. He'd been doing that for two days now. Pip had stopped commenting on it, mostly because every time he opened his mouth near the wolf, the animal looked at him with its one amber eye like it was reading a letter Pip hadn't finished writing yet.

"How far is this sanctuary?" Pip asked.

"As far as it needs to be."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Jude pushed through a curtain of reeds and held it aside for him—an oddly courteous gesture from someone who'd threatened to leave him behind twice since the bridge. "Bram knew the direction. Bram also got himself captured so we could walk out of that city, so I'm not inclined to trust anything he told us except that it was true."

Pip went quiet. They were all quiet about Bram. It had been two days and none of them had said his name more than twice, as if saying it too often might spend it.

They walked. The frogs sang. The sun made a brief, watery attempt at existing and then gave up, sinking into cloud the color of old dishwater.

* * *

They camped that night on a hummock—a mound of firm ground no bigger than a kitchen, rising out of the water like the back of some sleeping animal. Jude built a fire the size of a cupped hand, using peat she cut from the bank with her knife, and cooked eels she'd caught by standing hip-deep in freezing water with the patience of a statue. Pip had watched her do it and still didn't understand how she'd known where to put her hands.

"Eat," she said, handing him a skewer. "You've been chewing your lip instead of food since noon."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You eat like a nobleman's son who discovered hunger last month and can't get enough of it."

"That's exactly what I am," Pip said, before he could stop himself.

Jude looked at him across the tiny fire. The light caught the edge of her face, the sharp cheekbone, the soot-colored hair escaping its braid. She didn't smile. But she waited, in that way she had, like a door left open a crack.

Pip turned the eel skewer in his fingers. He'd been carrying the story for three years, wrapped up tight, and it had gotten heavier every mile since the bridge. Maybe it was the marsh. Maybe it was the fact that they'd watched Bram get taken and Jude had screamed his name until her voice broke, and afterward she hadn't pretended it hadn't happened. She just kept walking, and somehow that made it possible to keep walking too.

"My father sold my sister," Pip said.

The frogs sang. One-Eye lifted his head from where he lay at the edge of the firelight, and his single eye reflected the flame.

"Not—" Pip stopped. Started again. "Not sold, exactly. Not for coin. That would almost be better. He *registered* her. When the first notices went up—before the Great Hunt, there were early lists, quiet ones, in Aurelmark, in the capital—he wrote her name down himself. Her own name, written in my father's handwriting, delivered to the crown with our family seal on it."

"Why?" Jude asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking.

"Because Lissandra talked to birds." Pip heard his own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when he was reciting something he'd rehearsed in the dark too many times. "Songbirds, mostly. She'd sit in the garden and sparrows would land on her fingers, and she thought—I think she genuinely thought—that if she never did it in front of anyone important, it wouldn't matter. My father found out. And my father is a man who believes that a family survives by feeding itself to whatever's largest in the room. So when the crown came looking, he handed them Lissandra and kept the rest of us clean. Got a commendation. There's a letter. It has a wax seal on it. I burned it in the garden the night I left."

Jude said nothing. The fire popped.

"He didn't even do it angrily," Pip went on, and now the words were coming faster, three years of them, tumbling. "That's the part I can't—that's what keeps me up. He wasn't cruel about it. He was *reasonable*. He sat me down at dinner and explained it like an investment. 'One child, unfortunately afflicted, versus the house entire.' He used the word *afflicted*. She was fourteen. She laughed at his jokes. She taught the kitchen cat to sing—badly—and he stood in the doorway of the garden and watched her do it and then he went and wrote her name down."

His hands were shaking. He noticed distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

"And I ran," he said. "I didn't fight. I didn't steal her back. I was fifteen and I ran to Thornhollow and I told myself I'd figure out how to help her later, and then later became three years, and now the Hunt has her and probably has had her for—" His voice cracked. He pressed his mouth shut.

The marsh breathed around them. Water lapping. Reeds knocking together overhead.

"She could be dead," Pip said finally, quieter. "Or worse. Whatever 'worse' means, out here, with what the Hunt does. And I ate dinner with the man who made it happen, and I slept under his roof for another year after, and I never once—" He stopped again. Swallowed. "I never once told her I knew."

When he finally looked up, Jude was watching him with an expression he couldn't read. Not pity. He'd have hated pity. Something harder and kinder than pity.

"You were fifteen," she said.

"So?"

"So nothing. You were fifteen, and the man whose roof you lived under held all the power, and you did the only thing available to you, which was survive." She poked the fire with a stick. "I know a little about surviving when fighting isn't on the table. It doesn't feel like courage. It feels like cowardice wearing courage's coat. But it's the same coat."

"You don't know what he—"

"I know my mother went to the palace three years ago and never came back," Jude said, "and I know I stayed in Thornhollow selling charms and talking to rats because leaving meant admitting she wasn't coming home. So yes. I know the coat."

Pip stared at her. In two days of walking, she had never once mentioned her mother. Now she'd said it the way you'd say the sky was gray—flat, factual, and completely devastating.

"The rats talk?" he managed.

"The rats won't shut up." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "Worst gossips in Vaelmoor. You want to know which baker waters his bread or which councilman owes money to whom, ask a rat. They see everything. Nobody looks down."

Pip laughed. It surprised him—it came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh, his first since the bridge. One-Eye's ear twitched toward the sound.

"Lissandra's birds," he said. "Do you think—could you ever learn? To hear them, I mean. Or is it—you're born—"

"You're born with it, mostly. Like a tongue. Some people are born mute to it." Jude tilted her head, studying him. "Why? You're not gifted. Are you?"

"No. I checked. Believe me, I spent a year hoping I was, because then at least I'd understand her." He hesitated. "But you could teach me... things. Around it. How to listen. What to watch for. If I'm going to be walking through drowned country with a wanted woman and a wolf that doesn't splash, I'd rather not be the useless one."

Something changed in Jude's face then. Pip couldn't have named it—some small loosening around her eyes, like a knot worked halfway free.

"All right," she said. "Lesson one. Tomorrow. Sleep."

* * *

Lesson one happened at dawn, waist-deep in cold water, and consisted entirely of Jude telling him to shut up.

"Quiet," she said. "No. Quieter than that. You're breathing like a bellows. Frogs stop singing when danger passes—they go silent in a wave, from wherever the threat is outward. So when the song dies in one direction, that direction is bad. When it comes back, the bad thing has passed. Right now the song is fine, which means right now nothing is hunting us, so stop breathing like you're about to be arrested."

Pip clamped his mouth shut and listened. Really listened, maybe for the first time in his life. Under the frog-song there were other layers: the whirr of insects, thin and constant; the far-off complaint of some marsh bird; the tiny ticking sounds of the reeds growing, which he'd honestly never known existed.

"Hear the insects?"

"Yes."

"If they stop—all of them, at once—that's a hawk overhead, or something bigger. Birds go quiet for hawks. Insects go quiet for anything with wings wide enough to block the sun." She nodded at the water around their legs. "Fish. Feel for fish against your calves. Little fish scatter from big shadows. If minnows brush past you heading one direction, all of them, fast, something large is moving that way. The water tells you before your eyes do."

"It's beast-speech," Pip said slowly. "This is just... listening."

"This is the cheap version. Beast-speech is when they answer back." She crouched, scooped something from the water, and held up a fat brown snail. She held it near her lips and made a sound—not words, exactly, more a shape of sound, a low burring pulse like wind through a bottle. The snail's stalked eyes swiveled toward her. Then it extended itself, deliberately, toward the east.

"That way," Jude translated, "there's water chestnuts. Or it wants me to put it down. Snails are not deep thinkers."

"What did you *say*?"

"I asked who's been through here lately. Big feet make animals nervous for hours after. Nothing big has come through recently, or the snail wouldn't be so relaxed." She set it back in the water. "Now you try."

"I can't—"

"You can't speak it. But you can *ask* with your body. Slow movements. Low posture. No staring—staring is a challenge. Crouch, breathe slow, and put your hand flat in the water and wait. Patience is half the language. The other half is meaning what you say, because they can tell when you don't."

Pip crouched. He put his hand flat in the brown water and waited, feeling ridiculous, feeling fifteen again and useless, feeling the cold seep through his sleeves. A minute passed. Two. Then something brushed his palm—small, quick, silver. Then another. Minnows, swirling once around his fingers, unbothered, and darting off west.

"West," he said.

"West what?"

"Minnows went west. Fast. All of them." His heart picked up. "Something big is moving west of us."

Jude was already turning, scanning the reed line, and One-Eye rose from his doze with his head low and his hackles making a slow wave from shoulders to tail.

For a long moment nothing happened. The frogs sang. The insects whirred.

Then, far off to the west, the frog-song died. Not gradually—in a wave, a silence rolling toward them across the water like a door closing room by room.

Jude's hand closed on Pip's shoulder. "Down," she breathed. "Reeds. Now."

They folded themselves into the reeds, cold water to their chins, One-Eye a dark shape beside them barely breaking the surface. Pip's heart hammered so loud he was sure it would give them away. Through the screen of stalks he could see it: a boat, long and low, sliding through a cut in the marsh perhaps two hundred yards off. Figures in it. Gray cloaks.

Inquisitors.

There were four of them, poling the boat with long staffs, sweeping the water with a lantern even though it was nearly noon. At the bow, a man held something that glinted—a cage, small and brass-barred, and inside the cage something moved. A bird. A songbird, thrashing against the bars.

Pip felt Jude go rigid beside him. He understood a heartbeat later, and the understanding was worse than fear.

They weren't tracking footprints. They weren't following rumors.

They were using a gift-bearer as a compass. Some poor captured soul, forced to point toward their own kind—or toward anyone the Regent's instruments deemed useful—and carried through the wilds in a cage like a lamp.

The boat slid past. The bird thrashed. The frog-silence rolled over them and away, and the gray cloaks dwindled into the reed-haze to the north, and still none of them moved for a long time.

When Jude finally spoke, her voice came out low and scraped raw.

"They're using her ears to find us."

"Her?" Pip whispered.

"A songbird-speaker." Jude's jaw was tight. "Like your sister would have been. Like—" She stopped. Looked north, where the boat had gone. Then south, toward the sanctuary they hadn't reached. "We need to move. And Pip—"

"Yeah."

"When we get where we're going, remind me to teach you lesson two." She was already wading for the hummock, wringing out her braid, her voice hardening into something that was almost—but not quite—steady. "How to run faster."

Behind them, far to the north, faint and terrible, the songbird in the cage began to sing.
