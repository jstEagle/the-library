## 7. The Hollow Village

The silence found them before the village did.

Jude noticed it first as an absence in her feet—a wrongness in the ground itself. For three days they had walked the marsh road with the world murmuring around them: frogs arguing over territory, herons grumbling about the weather, worms complaining about the drainage like tiny drowned philosophers. The marsh was never quiet. It was a city of small voices, and Jude had learned to read it the way other people read weather.

Now the voices were thinning. Not fading—*thinning*, as if someone were plucking threads out of a tapestry one by one.

She stopped on the raised causeway and held up a hand. Behind her, Pip shifted the bundle of foraged cattail roots higher on his shoulder. One-Eye paused at her left flank, ears forward, his one amber eye fixed on the same horizon hers was.

"You feel it too," she said quietly.

"I hear it," One-Eye rumbled. His voice always sounded like stones rolling downhill in some distant cave—gravel and old grief, exactly as it had the night he'd appeared at her door. "Or rather, I don't."

"What is it?" Pip asked. He couldn't hear the beasts the way she could; he only heard One-Eye when the wolf chose to speak plainly, which was rare enough that Pip still flinched sometimes at the sound of a talking wolf. Three days hadn't cured that. Jude doubted three years would.

"Something's missing," she said. She turned slowly, scanning the flat green country. Reeds. Water. A line of stunted willows marking where the marsh gave way to firmer land to the north. A thread of chimney smoke rising far off, thin and gray against the pale sky. "There should be birds here. Marsh country like this should be *loud*. Where are the birds?"

Pip listened. He was a noble's son, runaway, sixteen, all sharp cheekbones and sharper tongue, but he had learned in three days of walking to listen the way fugitives listen—with his whole body. "I don't hear any," he admitted. "I thought maybe they were just... quiet today."

"Birds aren't quiet," Jude said. "Birds are never quiet. That's how you know something's wrong with the world—when the birds shut up." Her mother had taught her that, back before the palace took her. Back when the lessons had been about charm-songs and pigeon gossip instead of survival. The thought landed in her chest like a swallowed stone, the way thoughts of her mother always did now, since One-Eye had told her what he knew: that Maren Amberdale had walked into Aurelmark willingly, trading herself so her daughter's name would stay off the registers.

She pushed the thought down where she kept all the others. Later. Grieve later. Walk now.

They walked.

* * *

The village was called Merrow's End, according to the leaning signpost at its edge, though the paint had weathered to ghosts of letters. It sat on a shelf of dry ground above the reed beds: perhaps forty houses, a chapel with a sagging roof, a well, a smithy cold and doorless. Smoke rose from a handful of chimneys. Laundry hung on lines between two cottages, shirts and shifts stirring limply in the wind.

It looked, at first glance, like any poor village anywhere in Vaelmoor. Jude had grown up in Thornhollow's tenements, and poverty had a look she knew the way she knew her own hands.

But the silence—

No dog barked. Every village had dogs. Even the poorest kept a cur or two, chained or loose, and every dog in Vaelmoor would have announced three strangers and a wolf long before they reached the first house. Jude strained with her gift, reaching out the way she'd done ten thousand times in the market city—casting her voice ahead like a lantern, asking the empty air *anyone home? anyone?*

Nothing answered.

Not even insects. She realized it as they passed the first cottage: no flies buzzing around the muck heap, no crickets in the grass, no bees working the straggling flowers by the door. The world had been scooped hollow, and what remained was a painted set, a stage where the play had ended and everyone had gone home.

Except someone was still here. An old woman watched them from a doorway, arms folded, face like a closed shutter. She didn't call a warning. She didn't run. She just watched, and as they passed, she stepped inside and pulled the door shut with a sound that seemed obscenely loud.

"They know we're coming," Pip whispered. "Word travels."

"Word travels," Jude agreed. "That's not what this is." She glanced at One-Eye. "You smell anything?"

The wolf lifted his muzzle. Wind came off the marsh, carrying reek of mud and rotting weed—and under it, faintly, something else. Jude saw his nostrils flare, saw the hackles rise along his spine before he smoothed them down again with visible effort.

"Iron," he said. "Old iron. And fear, soaked deep into the wood of these houses. This place has been afraid for a long time."

"Fear I understand," Jude said. "Fear I've lived in my whole life. But the quiet—" She shook her head. "The quiet frightens me more."

They made their way up the single street. Doors closed as they passed—not slammed, just *closed*, one after another, soft and deliberate, like eyes shutting. A child peered from a window, saw One-Eye, and vanished. Somewhere inside a house, a voice murmured a prayer to the Nine, low and urgent.

At the well in the center of the square, an old man sat on an overturned bucket, mending a fishing net with slow, precise hands. He was the only person in Merrow's End who didn't hide. He looked up as they approached, took in Jude's travel-worn clothes, Pip's fine boots gone muddy, and the enormous scarred wolf sitting calm as a statue at Jude's side—and none of it seemed to surprise him.

"You'll be wanting water," he said. His voice was dry as the net in his hands. "Well's good. Draw what you need."

"Thank you." Jude hesitated, then crossed to the well and worked the crank. The bucket came up dripping. She drank, then filled their skins, then offered the dipper to the old man, who shook his head.

"We don't drink much this time of day," he said, which explained nothing.

Pip crouched near him, careful, unthreatening—the posture he'd been learning, the one that said *we're not soldiers, we're not inquisitors.* "We're headed north," he said. "Toward the king's road. We heard there might be work in the river towns."

The old man's hands did not stop moving. Over, under, through. The net grew in his lap, knot by knot. "Work," he repeated.

"And passage, maybe. If the ferries still run."

"The ferries run." Over, under, through. "The carts run too."

Jude went still. "What carts?"

The old man looked at her then, really looked, and she saw his eyes properly for the first time: pale gray, washed-out, the eyes of a man who had seen something once and spent every day since trying to unsee it. "You're from the city," he said. "Thornhollow, by your talk. They say the Hunt started there first. Big cities first, then the roads, then us." He returned to his net. "Out here we're last. Small mercy. Means we got to watch it happen somewhere else before it happened here."

"Happened here," Jude said. "What happened here?"

The old man tied off a knot. Pulled it tight. Started another.

"Where are your dogs?" she asked, because she couldn't help herself, because the question had been clawing at her since the signpost. "Every village has dogs. Where are yours?"

"Gone north," the old man said.

"With the carts?"

"With the carts."

* * *

He wouldn't talk in the open square. He finished his row of knots, coiled the net over his shoulder, and jerked his chin toward the chapel. Inside, it smelled of dust and cold ash; the altar was bare, the offering candles long burned down to stubs nobody replaced. He settled onto a bench near the door where he could see the street, and only then, in the gloom, did his shoulders come down from wherever they'd been living.

"Names," he said. "I'm Aldous. I was the ferryman, before."

"Before what?"

"Before there was nothing left to ferry worth the fare." He studied her face. "You asked about dogs. You noticed the quiet. You're one of them, aren't you. Gifted."

Jude's whole body went to ice. Beside her, she felt One-Eye shift his weight, felt Pip stop breathing.

Aldous waved a knotted hand. "Don't trouble yourself. Half this village could talk to something or other, if you count the small gifts. Tam could calm a spooked ox with a hum. The miller's wife knew when rain was coming three days out, and not from her knees neither. Old Sefa down the lane—she could make bread rise just by singing to it. Little things. Harmless things." His voice caught on harmless, snagged like the net. "The gray-cloaks didn't care about little. They had a list. Names written down by somebody who'd come through two winters back, taking a census. Asking who kept odd animals. Who had luck with crops. Whose children said strange things."

"A census," Jude whispered. She thought of the charm stall, the pigeons, the rats, the years of hiding in plain sight, the exhausting arithmetic of being small enough to miss. In Thornhollow she had been a needle in a haystack. Here, in a village of forty houses, there was nowhere to be small.

"They came at harvest," Aldous said. "Six wagons and thirty soldiers and two of the gray-cloaks, riding up the king's road with the sun behind them so we couldn't see their faces. They had the list. They went door to door. Polite as tax collectors." His hands had stopped moving on nothing; he was gripping the edge of the bench. "Tam argued. Tam's dead. The miller's wife ran for the fens. They brought hounds—big black ones, not right somehow, too quiet themselves—and the hounds found her in the reeds in an hour. The rest went quiet. Went into the wagons. My own granddaughter among them. Eleven years old. Could make flowers open out of season. That was all. Flowers."

The silence in the chapel was worse than the silence outside. Outside, at least, the silence was the point. In here it was just grief with nowhere to go.

"They took the animals too," Aldous went on, flat now, reciting. "Dogs, cats, the oxen that Tam had calmed—they said touched things carried the taint. Everything that had ever been spoken to kindly, off to the wagons. And then the gray-cloak stood in the square where you're standing, boy, and he told us it was a great honor. Told us our people were going north to serve the crown. To *feed* the crown." He laughed, one dry bark with no humor in it anywhere. "Feed the crown. Like we were sacks of barley."

"To feed the crown," Jude repeated. Her mouth was dry. One-Eye had told her fragments—of the Regent, of stolen magic, of harvested gifts—but hearing it from a ferryman in a bare chapel made it real in a way the wolf's gravel voice had not. This wasn't a story. This was a place. These were laundry lines and cold smithies and a grandmother's empty chair.

"That's what he said," Aldous confirmed. "Word for word. Feed the crown. And then they rolled north with forty-one of our neighbors in six wagons, and the hounds trotting alongside happy as lambs, and the whole village stood in the road and watched until the dust swallowed them." He finally looked away from the street, down at his hands. "Nobody spoke for three days after. Couldn't. Wasn't a rule or nothing. Just—what do you say?"

Pip sat down heavily on the bench across from him. He was sixteen and had run from a father who'd sold his own sister to the Hunt, and Jude watched him take Aldous's words in and file them next to his own wound, and she saw the boy age a year in the space of a breath.

"Your granddaughter," Pip said. "What was her name?"

"Liss." The old man's jaw worked. "Liss. She liked the ones that opened at night. Moonflowers. Said they were shy." He cleared his throat roughly. "Why d'you want to know?"

"So she's not just a number," Pip said simply. "Somebody should remember her name besides you. Somebody should carry it north."

Aldous stared at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and looked away, and blinked hard at the bare altar, and pretended very hard that he wasn't crying, and everyone in the chapel let him pretend.

* * *

They drew water and bought—paid for, over the old man's objections—smoked eel and a heel of dark bread from a woman who opened her door only wide enough to pass the food through and take the coins. The village watched them go the way it must have watched the wagons go: silent, from behind glass.

At the edge of Merrow's End, where the causeway climbed toward the king's road, Aldous caught up with them, breathing hard, the coiled net still on his shoulder.

"You're going north," he said. It wasn't a question. "Into it. Toward it."

"We have to," Jude said.

"Don't." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "Don't be heroes. Heroes end up in wagons. I buried enough of this village to know the shape of it." He fumbled at his neck and pulled free a cord, and on the cord, a small wooden flower, carved crude and lovely, moon-pale even unpainted. "Liss made it. I can't carry it north—I'm not going anywhere, I'm the last ferryman of a river with no passengers. But if you're going anyway." He pressed it into Jude's hand and closed her fingers over it. "Take her name with you. You said somebody should."

"I said that," Jude managed.

"Then do it." He stepped back. His eyes went to One-Eye, and for the first time something moved behind the washed-out gray—not fear, not quite. Recognition. "Wolf. You knew, didn't you. Coming up the road. You knew what this place was."

One-Eye inclined his great head. "I knew."

"Then you know what's waiting up that road." Aldous looked north, where the king's road ran straight as a scar toward a horizon that held, somewhere beyond the fens and the hills, a capital full of bells and a crown that ate people. "Forty-one from here. Hundreds from the villages past. Thousands from the cities. All of them carted north to feed the crown." He spat, an old man's gesture, ancient and helpless. "And the crown's still hungry."

He turned and walked back down the causeway, a bent figure with a fishing net, returning to a village of closed doors, and Jude stood watching him until the marsh haze took him.

She opened her hand. The wooden moonflower lay in her palm, smooth from a child's carving knife.

"North," Pip said quietly. Not a question either. Not anymore.

"North," Jude agreed, and closed her fist around Liss's flower, and they took the king's road together—one girl who could speak to beasts, one boy carrying his sister's ghost, and a one-eyed wolf walking point into the hungry dark.
