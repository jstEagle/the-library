## 8. Wolves at the Ford

The river found them before they found it.

Pip heard it first as a change in the birdsong—or rather, the absence of it, a silence spreading through the reeds like spilled ink. Then the ground went soft under his boots, then softer, and then there it was: the Sallow, running wide and brown and fast with three days of rain, too deep to wade, too cold to swim in autumn.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then."

Jude stood at the bank with her hood back and her jaw set in the way he'd learned to dread, because it meant she was about to do something stupid and brave and she hadn't decided which yet. One-Eye sat beside her, ears forward, nose working the wind.

"Ford's downstream," the wolf said. His voice always made Pip's skin prickle—gravel poured over old grief. "Half a mile. Water's chest-deep but it'll hold."

"Chest-deep," Pip repeated. "In this current. With our packs."

"You can float," One-Eye said. "You're mostly noise."

Pip decided not to dignify that. Three days in the marshes had taught him that arguing with a wolf was like arguing with weather. You endured it.

They moved downstream along the bank, single file, Jude in front. She'd barely slept since Thornhollow. None of them had, really, but Pip watched her carry it differently than he did. He wore his grief loud—complained about the mud, the cold rations, the way Bram's absence sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Jude wore hers quiet, folded up small, and that scared him more. People who folded things small were people who eventually snapped.

He thought about Bram every time the wind came off the water. The last image he had of him: broad shoulders squared in the tenement doorway, sword drawn for the first time in six years, saying *go* in a voice that brooked no argument. Go, and don't look back, boy. Pip had looked back. Of course he'd looked back. He was still looking back, would probably be looking back when the inquisitors finally caught them, which felt less like an if every day.

"They're behind us," One-Eye said suddenly.

Pip's stomach dropped. "How far?"

"Far enough that you have time to stop asking questions."

* * *

The ford was exactly as bad as promised—a gravel bar splitting the current, water tearing across it at thigh height, the far bank a wall of reeds. Jude tested it with a stick and the stick vanished downstream like it had been yanked by the hand of God.

"We cross now or we don't cross," she said.

"I vote don't," Pip said. "I vote we go around. Rivers bend. It's their whole thing."

"And the hounds?" One-Eye asked mildly.

Pip shut up.

They could hear them now, faintly—the belling of the pack, that deep musical cry that wasn't quite dog. Inquisitor hounds. He'd seen them once from a distance in Thornhollow, before everything: huge black-and-tan beasts on silver chains, bred for one purpose. Not tracking scent, the rumors said. Tracking *gift*. They could smell the wrongness in a person, the thing the crown had outlawed, the thing that lived in Jude like a second heartbeat.

Jude waded in first. The water hit her at mid-thigh and she staggered, caught herself, kept going. Pip followed with his pack held over his head, gasping—the cold went through him like a blade, found every bruise the marshes had given him. Halfway across, feet slipping on the gravel bar, he heard One-Eye enter behind him and felt absurdly comforted by it, which was a sentence he never expected to think about a wolf.

Then the belling stopped.

Silence on the water. Pip turned, still knee-deep in the current, and saw them come out of the reeds on the south bank.

Six hounds. They moved without hurry, low to the ground, coats slick and dark as oil. And behind them, two figures in gray cloaks with the sunburst-and-chain of the Inquisition stitched at the shoulder—one tall, one short, both with the particular stillness of men who had done terrible things so many times it had become boring.

"Pip." Jude's voice, very calm. "Keep walking."

"I'm walking. I'm walking."

The tall inquisitor raised a hand and the hounds fanned out along the bank, cutting off the ford's downstream edge. One of them—the leader, a massive animal with a white scar bisecting its muzzle—waded into the shallows until the water pulled at its chest, and stood there, staring at Jude with eyes like chips of amber.

"Hold!" the tall inquisitor called across the water. His voice carried strangely, flattened by some charm woven into his collar. "By decree of the Regent, you are named gift-bearers and are commanded to submit to registration. Resistance is heresy against the crown. Heresy is death."

Jude didn't answer. She stood in the middle of the river with the current shoving at her, and Pip saw her hand drift down—not to a weapon, because she didn't carry one, but to the water itself, fingers half-curled like she was about to play a harp made of river.

"Pip," she said again, quieter. "Whatever happens. Don't run. Running makes it worse."

"That is not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be."

The scarred hound growled, a sound like a millstone turning, and started forward through the water toward them.

And Jude spoke.

Not shouted. Spoke. Low, in a language Pip had never heard and recognized instantly anyway, because it wasn't a language at all—it was older than language, all growl and breath and cadence, the shape of meaning without words. It came out of her throat sounding nothing like her voice, and the river seemed to lean in to listen.

The hound stopped.

For a long moment nothing moved except the water. Then the great beast tilted its head, and something in its amber eyes shifted—recognition, maybe, or memory, or just the simple animal arithmetic of a creature realizing that the thing in front of it spoke its own tongue better than its handlers ever had.

Jude said something else. Shorter. Pip couldn't follow any of it, but he felt it land, felt it the way you feel thunder in your ribs.

The hound turned around.

It walked back to the bank, shook the water from its coat, and sat down in front of the tall inquisitor with the deliberate insolence of a cat ignoring its name. Then it began to howl—and the other five joined it, and the sound wasn't the hunting belling anymore. It was a wall of noise, a shrieking chorus, and it wasn't aimed at the river.

It was aimed at the handlers.

"What—" the short inquisitor started, and then the pack hit them.

Pip had seen dogs fight. This wasn't that. Six hounds bred and broken their whole lives to serve the Hunt turned on their masters with a coordination that was almost worse than savagery—no frenzy, no chaos, just terrible efficiency. The tall man got his charm-collar up in time to turn the first lunge; the second tore it off his throat like paper. The short one ran. Two hounds ran him down in the reeds and Pip looked away, because some things you didn't need to keep.

"Jude," he said. His voice came out thin. "Jude, what did you *do*?"

She was still standing in the river. Her eyes were wrong—not glowing, nothing so kind as glowing, but distant, unfocused, like someone listening to a room far away. The scarred hound had come back to the water's edge and stood before her, and she rested her hand on its scarred muzzle, and murmured something gentle, and the beast pressed its head into her palm like a dog greeting its master after a long war.

"Pip," she said, without turning. "Get to the far bank."

"Jude—"

"*Now.*"

He went. He didn't remember deciding to; his legs simply obeyed, hauling him through the current while his mind spun uselessly behind him. On the north bank he turned and watched her walk out of the river, unhurried, the wolf falling in beside her, the hounds melting back into the reeds like a bad dream dissolving at dawn. Behind them, on the south shore, the tall inquisitor lay very still with his collar torn open, and the marsh grass bent slowly in the wind over what was left of the other one.

Jude climbed the bank. Water streamed off her cloak. She looked at Pip, and her eyes were her own again, and she smiled, and it was the smile that frightened him more than anything the hounds had done.

"See?" she said. "No problem."

* * *

They put four miles between themselves and the ford before anyone spoke. The light was failing, gold going to gray, and they made camp in a stand of alders on a hummock above the wet ground. One-Eye ranged out and came back reporting no pursuit—which, he noted dryly, was either good news or very bad news, because inquisitors who lost their hounds didn't report failure. They reported miracles. And miracles drew bigger audiences.

Pip sat with his knees drawn up, working a piece of dried fish he had no appetite for, watching Jude across the small fire.

She was feeding strips of meat to the scarred hound.

It had followed them. Of course it had. It lay at the edge of the firelight now, enormous and calm, taking food from the hand of the woman whose death it had been bred to deliver, and nobody found this strange except Pip, who was beginning to suspect that strangeness had simply relocated—that it used to live in the world and now it lived entirely inside Jude Amberdale.

"How long," he said.

She glanced up. "How long what?"

"Don't do that. Don't do the thing where you pretend you don't know what I'm asking." He set the fish down. "How long have you been able to do that? Talk to them. Command them."

"It's not commanding."

"It looked like commanding."

"It's *talking*, Pip. There's a difference." She fed the hound another strip. "I've been able to talk to animals since I was six. My mother taught me to hide it before she taught me my letters. That's the whole story."

"That is not the whole story." His voice cracked and he hated it, hated being fifteen and small-voiced in front of a wolf. "Six years of charms and rats and pigeons, fine. I've known that since the night we ran. But that thing at the river—you didn't talk to those hounds. You *turned* them. You turned six trained killers against the men who own them, and they went like—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Like they'd been waiting their whole lives for someone to ask."

Jude was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a marsh bird called and was answered.

"When I speak to an animal," she said finally, "it's a conversation. Two voices. They can say no. Most of them say no, actually—cats especially, cats are bastards." A ghost of a smile. "But those hounds..." She looked at the scarred beast, and the beast looked back with its amber eyes, and something passed between them that Pip couldn't read. "Those hounds have been screaming their whole lives and no one could hear it. All I did was open the door. What they did on the other side of it was theirs."

"And if they'd chosen differently?"

"Then we'd be dead."

She said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse.

One-Eye lifted his head from his paws. "You're afraid of her now," the wolf said to Pip. Not unkindly. Just factually, the way he said everything.

"I'm not—" Pip started, and then stopped, because lying to a wolf was even stupider than arguing with one. "Yes. A little. Is that—is that wrong? She talked a pack of hellhounds into murdering their handlers and then smiled about it. Everyone acts like this is fine. Like it's just Jude being Jude. But that's not a trick, that's not a charm, that's—" He gestured helplessly. "That's power. Real power. The kind the crown kills people for. The kind that *changes* people. I've read enough history to know what happens to people who hold that kind of thing. It doesn't stay in their hands. It gets in their blood."

The wolf regarded him for a long moment with his one pale eye.

"Good," One-Eye said.

"...What?"

"Afraid is correct. Fear means you're paying attention." The wolf settled his head back down. "But hear me, boy, and hear me once. I've walked beside many gifted in a long life, and I will tell you the truth of it: the gift doesn't make monsters. It only shows you what was already there. Some people look at a voice that can turn armies and see a weapon. Your Jude looks at it and sees a door to open for things that are screaming." His eye slid toward the fire, toward her. "Whether that saves the world or breaks it depends entirely on what the world does to her next. So watch that. Worry about that. Not about her."

Pip didn't answer. Across the fire, Jude had gone still, listening to all of it with her chin on her knees, and when their eyes met she didn't look away and neither did he.

"I'm sorry," she said quietly. "That you had to see it like that. I wanted—I kept hoping I'd never have to show you. Hoping we'd get somewhere safe and I could just... sell charms. Be boring."

"You saved our lives."

"I know what I did." Something flickered across her face, fast, gone. "I'm telling you it cost something. When I talk that deep, when I push like that—it takes a piece out of me. I don't know what yet. I'll find out later, probably. That's how it works, my mother said. The deep speech always collects." She shrugged, too casual by half. "Anyway. Ask me again when we're not fugitives."

Pip wanted to say a hundred things. Instead he picked his fish back up and ate it, because grief and fear and awe all apparently ran on dried fish, and because she'd asked him, in her sideways way, to let it rest.

The scarred hound sighed hugely and laid its head on its paws. One-Eye closed his eye. The fire burned down to coals.

Pip was nearly asleep when Jude spoke again, so softly he almost missed it.

"One-Eye. The way those hounds fought—the handlers' collars, the way the big one knew to go for the throat-charm first. That wasn't instinct."

"No," the wolf said. "It wasn't."

"Where did they learn it?"

A pause. Long enough that Pip opened his eyes.

"From others like them," One-Eye said. "Hounds who broke free before. There are more of us in the wild places than the crown knows, girl. Dogs and wolves and worse, all of us listening, all of us passing word. And lately the word has been the same everywhere." He rose, shook himself, and looked north, past the dark line of the reeds, toward whatever waited beyond the marshes.

"The taken aren't all dead. Some of the gifted the Hunt carries north—they get away. And they've founded something. A sanctuary, under the hills, where the Regent's writ doesn't reach." His eye came back to Jude, and to Pip, and it was impossible to tell what moved behind it. "I know the road. And I think it's time you two met the others who can do what you do."

Jude stared at him. "There are *others*?"

"There are always others," One-Eye said. "That's rather the point of hunting us."

He turned and trotted to the edge of the firelight, and sat facing north, and did not explain anything further, because he was a wolf, and wolves were bastards about exposition.

Pip lay back and stared up through the alder branches at the first stars, his heart doing something complicated between hope and terror. Others. A sanctuary. People like Jude, gathered in one place—which meant safety, and also meant a target, and also meant that the life he'd stumbled into three weeks ago was becoming something much larger and much more dangerous than running.

Beside him, Jude whispered something to the scarred hound in that old rolling language, and the beast answered, and Pip understood not one word of it.

But he caught the tone. And the tone sounded, for the first time since Thornhollow Bridge, like hope.
