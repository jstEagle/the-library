## 9. The Wolf's Memory

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time One-Eye spoke.

Not with his mouth. Wolves didn't have the architecture for human words, and even if they had, he'd have told her it was a waste of good teeth. No—his voice came the other way, rising up through that strange channel between them that had opened the night he'd appeared at her door in Thornhollow. It arrived inside her skull like water finding a crack in stone: low, gravelled, patient as erosion.

*You asked me once how I knew your mother.*

Jude sat up from her bedroll. Across the dead campfire, Pip slept curled like a comma, one hand still gripping the foraging knife she'd given him. The marshes lay black and breathing around them, full of small sounds—the plip of fish, the complaint of frogs, the whisper of reeds rehearsing their one word over and over. *Marsh. Marsh. Marsh.*

*I did,* she said. Her own voice, in this place between them, wasn't sound at all. It was intention shaped into meaning. It felt like holding her breath underwater. *Every day since you showed up. You just never answered.*

*I was deciding how much truth you could carry.* A pause, dry as old bone. *You've been carrying more than most since the bridge. I misjudged you. That happens to me less than it used to.*

"Charming," she whispered aloud, and the sound broke strangely against the silence of the marsh, too loud, too human.

*Words are heavy,* One-Eye said. *What I have to show you is heavier. So I'll ask first. Do you want it?*

She thought about lying. About saying no and rolling back into her bedroll and pretending the question hadn't been asked. But she'd spent three years not knowing where her mother had gone, three years building stories out of absence—a fever, a new life in the southern provinces, an accident on the river road—and every story had rotted her from the inside like fruit left too long in its skin.

*Show me,* she said.

*Then come here. Closer. This works better skin to skin.*

---

She crossed the cold ground and knelt beside him. Up close, the wolf was enormous—not the lean gray hunters she'd spoken to all her life, but something older, built on a different scale entirely. His fur was the color of wet slate, shot through with threads of white at the throat and chest. The ruin of his missing eye had healed long ago into smooth, puckered scar tissue, but somehow that eye watched her more intently than the living one.

*This will feel like falling,* he warned. *Don't fight it. Fighting it is how people break.*

*People? How many people have you done this to?*

*One.* His living eye caught the ember-light. *Your mother. Once. To say goodbye.*

Before Jude could form a question, he pressed his broad head against her sternum, and the world dropped out from under her.

---

*Falling.*

But not down. Sideways. Through years.

She landed in autumn. She knew it was autumn because the light came slantwise and gold through the shutters of a house she recognized with a jolt that hurt worse than any fall—the little crooked house on Tanner's Lane, before the rent went up, before everything. Woodsmoke and tallow and the particular smell of her mother's hands: lye soap and crushed mint.

Liora Amberdale stood at the hearth with her back turned, and she was *alive*, so alive it made Jude's throat close, her dark hair escaping its braid, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, humming something tuneless while a pot of barley soup muttered to itself.

A wolf lay by the door.

Younger. Both eyes whole and bright and amber. But the same wolf—Jude knew it the way you know your own reflection in a warped mirror, the proportions slightly wrong but the essence unmistakable.

*This was three autumns ago,* One-Eye said, and his voice now came from everywhere and nowhere, the memory's narrator and its ghost. *I had known Liora Amberdale for eleven years by then. She found me caught in a poacher's snare on the moor above Thornhollow, half-dead, and instead of taking my pelt she took me home and pulled the iron out of my leg with her bare hands and never once flinched. Gift-bearers recognize each other. It's in the voice. Hers was deep-kind, like yours. Deeper, maybe. She could talk to enchantments the way you talk to rats.*

*She could what?*

*Watch. And listen. And understand why I'm showing you this instead of telling you. Telling is cheap. Memory has weight. Weight is what makes it true.*

Outside the memory's window, the streets of Thornhollow were wrong. Quiet in the daytime. Fewer carts. And on the corner of Tanner's Lane, where a pie-seller used to stand, two men in gray cloaks stood with a ledger open, speaking to neighbors who wouldn't meet their eyes.

*The first registers,* One-Eye said. *Small then. Voluntary, they claimed. Names taken "for the crown's protection." Your mother knew better. Deep-kind voices can taste a lie in written words, same as spoken ones. She read the proclamation nailed to the chapel door and came home shaking.*

In the memory, Liora turned from the hearth. She looked directly at the wolf by the door—at *him*, eleven years younger, both eyes burning—and her face was the face Jude saw every night behind her eyelids, and it was afraid.

"I won't give them Jude," she said aloud, to the wolf, to the room, to whatever gods still listened. "Do you hear me? They can take anything else. Not her name. Not her into their books."

*She'd already decided,* One-Eye said. *That's the part you need to see. She wasn't weighing options that night. She was saying goodbye.*

The memory folded like paper.

---

Autumn became winter.

The second scene opened in the same house, but colder, the fire smaller, the soup thinner. Snow ticked against the shutters. Liora sat at the table writing something in a careful, disguised hand—letters that looked like a merchant's inventory, nothing more. On the table beside her lay a leather cord with a charm knotted into it. Jude knew that charm. She'd worn its twin against her heart for three years, the one her mother had pressed into her palm at the ferry landing with the words *wear this and don't ask questions.*

"She'll be safe in Lowmarsh," Liora murmured, writing. "Old Tansy owes me. And the girl thinks she's going to help with the lambing. By the time she understands—" Her pen stopped. "By the time she understands, it'll be done, and she'll hate me, and she'll be alive."

The young wolf raised his head from the floor.

"You could stop me," Liora said to him. Her voice cracked down the middle. "You're the only one who knows what I am. What I can do. You could bite me, keep me here, tell them where I've gone."

The wolf crossed the room and laid his great head in her lap, and she buried her hands in his ruff and wept without sound, the way people cry when they've taught themselves that noise costs too much.

"They're asking about children now," she whispered into his fur. "Did you see? The gray cloaks. They've started asking about *children*. If they put Jude's name in a book, that book outlives us all. Books get found. Books get *read*." She lifted her face, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "But if they have me—if they have a deep-kind voice who walks in willingly—they stop looking. The Hunt fills its quota. The registers close. One name kept off the page in exchange for mine." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Jude had ever heard, because it was her mother's laugh, hollowed out and repurposed. "I always told her our gift was a blessing. Turns out it's currency. And I'm rich enough to pay for her."

*She went to the palace gates on the last morning of winter,* One-Eye said, and the memory shifted again—

---

—And Jude stood on the processional road outside Aurelmark itself, the capital rising white and gold against a pewter sky, bells tolling from a hundred towers in patterns that sounded almost like language. Almost like a warning.

Her mother walked toward the gates in her best dress, the blue one, the one with the mended hem. She walked like a woman going to market. She had scrubbed her hands clean of lye soap and mint. Around her neck, on the leather cord, hung a different charm—one Jude had never seen, a knot of silver wire around a sliver of something dark.

At the gate, a gray-cloaked inquisitor met her. He didn't bind her. He didn't even touch her. He simply held out the register book, open, and Liora Amberdale wrote her own name in it in the same careful hand she'd used for the fake inventory letters, and beneath it, in smaller script, added a line that made the inquisitor's eyebrows rise:

*Beneath the throne, the king sleeps. I know the song that binds him. Take me to him or lose me.*

*She bluffed,* Jude breathed. *She bluffed her way into the palace—*

*No,* One-Eye said, and there was grief in it, old and worn smooth like a stone in a river. *She didn't bluff. That's what I have to show you last, Jude. That's the part I've carried alone for three winters because carrying it alone was killing me and wolves aren't meant to carry things alone.*

The memory tightened. Drew closer. Focused on the charm at her mother's throat—the silver wire, the dark sliver within.

*Deep-kind voices can speak to enchantments. Did Maren tell you that much? Then she may also have told you the cost. Every unbinding takes something from the speaker. A memory. A season of your life. A piece of your name.* The wolf's voice dropped lower. *Your mother spent three years before she walked to that gate doing something no deep-kind speaker had ever dared. She didn't go to the palace to surrender herself, Jude. She went to finish a job.*

*What job?*

*She'd been unbinding the Regent's wards for years. Slowly. One thread at a time, paid for with pieces of herself—that's why she aged fast those last years, why her hair went gray at thirty-six, why she forgot small things, the neighbor's name, which drawer held the knives. She was hollowing herself out on purpose, buying passage inward, learning the shape of the binding beneath the throne. And when she ran out of self to spend—*

The memory flared white.

*—she traded the only coin she had left. Herself. Because a captured deep-kind voice, kept alive in the harvesting cells, is worth more to the Regent than a hundred ordinary gifts. Because while they feed on her, she is *inside*, close to the binding, close to the sleeping king, and as long as she lives, the work isn't finished. It's paused.*

The whiteness resolved into one final image: Liora Amberdale passing under the gate, small and straight-backed in her mended blue dress, and turning once—not to wave, not to look back at the city—but pressing her hand flat over the charm at her throat, over her heart, where a twin charm hung invisible against another heart in another city.

*Wear this and don't ask questions.*

*It's not a ward,* Jude said, and her voice in the between-place was shaking. *The charm she gave me. It's not protection, is it.*

*No.* One-Eye's voice was very quiet now. *It's a key. Half of a key. She split her master-work between two vessels so the Hunt could never take both. The knot-work is her own design—I helped her braid it, the night before she left. Wolf-guard hair and rowan and a thread of her own voice, sung into the silver until it remembered the shape of the binding beneath the throne.*

*And the other half?*

*Was on her neck when they took her.* A pause like a held breath. *Which means the Regent has been wearing the other half of your mother's key around its beautiful stolen neck for three years, Jude. And it doesn't know what it has.*

The memory released her.

---

She came back to her body gasping, on her knees in frost-stiffened grass, with the taste of woodsmoke and tallow in her mouth and her mother's fear soaked so deep into her bones she doubted she'd ever be warm again.

Pip was awake. Sitting bolt upright, staring at her with wide eyes, one hand still on his knife. "You were—you were *glowing*, Jude. Just a little. Around the edges. Like a lamp behind cloth." His voice wobbled. "Is that normal?"

"No," she managed.

One-Eye rose, shook the frost from his coat, and looked at her with his single amber eye, and in it she saw reflected everything he'd shown her: the crooked house, the mended blue dress, the woman walking willingly through the gate.

*Three years I've waited to give that to you,* he said. *Because she made me promise. "When she's ready," she said. "When she's strong enough to carry it. Not before."* He stepped closer, close enough that his breath fogged her cheek. *I think you're ready now. I think you have to be.*

Jude's hand had already risen to her collarbone, to the leather cord hidden beneath her shirt, to the charm that had warmed against her skin every night since the bridge and she'd never understood why.

"It's a key," she said aloud. Her voice came out raw. "Half of a key. And my mother is alive, and she's been *inside* the palace this whole time, three years, feeding that thing, waiting—"

*Waiting for someone with her blood and her voice to finish the work,* One-Eye finished. *The debt passes down, Jude. That's what inheritance means, for people like us. Not just the gift. The bill that comes with it.*

Pip looked between them, the boy who'd run from a father who sold his sister, and something hardened in his thin face. "Then we go north," he said simply. "To the capital. We find her."

Jude closed her fist around the charm, and it pulsed against her palm like a second heartbeat, like a voice calling from very far away, from beneath a throne, from a cell of woven song—

—and far to the north, faint as a dying star, she felt it answer.
