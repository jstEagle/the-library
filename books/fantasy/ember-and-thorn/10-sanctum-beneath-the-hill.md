## 10. Sanctum Beneath the Hill

The hill didn't want to be found.

Jude had felt that from the moment they turned off the marsh road—a pressure against her thoughts like a hand pressed flat over a door, gentle and absolute. *Not here. Nothing here. Keep walking.* She'd been walking for three days on One-Eye's word alone, through reed flats and birch scrub and a stretch of drowned orchard where the trees stood to their knees in black water, still bearing fruit no one would ever pick.

Now the wolf stopped at the base of a slope so ordinary it hurt—grass, gorse, a few wind-bent hawthorns—and looked back at her with his one amber eye.

"This is it?" Pip said. He had a blister the size of a coin on his heel and had complained about it in eleven distinct ways since dawn. "It's a hill."

"It's more than a hill." Jude crouched, pressed her palm to the grass. The pressure was stronger up close, a hum she felt in her teeth rather than heard. "There's something woven over it. A ward. A big one." She frowned. "Older than anything I've felt. It doesn't push like the wards in Thornhollow do. It just... suggests."

"Suggests," Pip repeated.

"That you were never here. That you never wanted to come." She stood, brushing dirt from her knees. "It's good work. It's the kind of work that keeps people alive."

One-Eye's growl was low, almost amused. *Can you open it?*

"I don't know. I've never talked to a ward before. Only around one." She flexed her fingers. Since the ford—since she'd put her voice into a pack of inquisitor hounds and turned them inside out—she'd been aware of a new layer in the world, faint and constant, like a sound just below hearing. Beasts spoke in scent and posture and hunger. But this, under everything, was different. This was the voice things were made with.

*You opened the hounds,* One-Eye said.

"The hounds were alive. They had ears." She looked up the blank green face of the hill. "This is a sentence someone wrote into the world. I'd be talking to the writing."

*Your mother could.*

That landed the way he meant it to. Jude breathed out slowly. "Fine. Everyone stand behind me. If I fall over, don't laugh."

She closed her eyes and reached.

It was like pressing her ear to a wall and finding the wall listening back. The ward wasn't words, exactly—it was intention given grammar, a long patient sentence that said *unwanted, unwanted, unwanted* in a hundred braided strands. She found the edge of it, the place where the weaver's will met the world, and did what she had done with rats and pigeons and one very confused mule outside Thornhollow: she introduced herself.

*I'm Jude Amberdale. I'm tired, and I'm being hunted, and I was invited.*

The ward considered her. She felt it the way you feel a stranger studying your face at market—not hostile, but weighing. Then, deep in the weave, something shifted. A strand loosened. A door, somewhere in the sentence, unlatched.

*Jude.* The voice came from everywhere—the grass, the gorse, the ground itself—a woman's voice, dry as paper and warm as bread. *You speak the old way. Come up before you bleed all over my threshold.*

Her eyes snapped open. Pip was staring at her. "What? What happened?"

"It talked back." Her own voice came out strange. "The ward talked back."

"Is that bad?"

"I genuinely don't know."

They climbed. Halfway up, the hill stopped pretending. The gorse thinned, the grass gave way to packed earth, and the air changed—woodsmoke and tallow and something green growing, the smell of a place where people actually lived. The ward peeled back around them like fog retreating from a window, and there it was: a village folded into the hill's shoulder, half-dug into the earth itself, turf roofs and round stone walls, chimneys leaking thin smoke into the gray morning. Maybe forty structures. Maybe a hundred people, moving quiet between them, pausing to watch the strangers come.

No dogs barked. But Jude heard, beneath the human silence, the small busy voices of goats and hens and bees, and one enormous presence underground that she decided not to think about yet.

A woman came down the path to meet them. She was perhaps sixty, gray-haired, dressed in undyed wool, and she walked without a stick, without hesitation, without eyes—her lids sunken smooth over empty sockets, her face turned toward them with unnerving precision.

"You're the wolf," she said to One-Eye. "You took your time." Then her head tilted toward Jude, and something in her expression shifted—something like hunger, or grief, or both. "And you. Say something else."

"Say something else?" Jude said.

The woman made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor girl. You have no idea what you are."

* * *

They fed them first. That seemed to be the rule of the place—questions after bread. Jude sat on a bench in a long low hall dug into the hillside, watching Pip eat like a boy who'd learned not to trust the next meal, while One-Eye lay across the doorway like a dropped portcullis and pretended not to listen to every conversation in the room.

The woman was called Maren. She was, as far as Jude could tell, in charge, though she never said so; people simply arranged themselves around her the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone. There were others: a broad-shouldered man named Col who could calm any animal to sleep with a hum; twin sisters who finished each other's sentences because they literally shared one mind between them; a nervous young man with burn-scarred hands who could light fires by asking them politely. Gift-bearers. Dozens of them. Alive, unregistered, hidden under a hill that told the world *nothing here.*

"How many know about this place?" Jude asked.

"Everyone who needs to, which is fewer than you'd hope." Maren sat across from her, hands wrapped around a clay cup she never drank from. "The Hunt has taken four hundred souls since the Proclamation. We know because some of them passed through here first. Some of them we couldn't convince to stay." Her ruined eyelids didn't move, but Jude felt her attention sharpen. "Fear makes people stupid. They go home to fetch a keepsake. They trust a neighbor. They register, because the criers promise registration is protection."

"It isn't."

"No. Registration is inventory." Maren set down the cup. "Now. Tell me about the ford."

So Jude told her—the hounds, the handler with the hooked pole, the moment she'd stopped running and spoken to the pack leader. She told it plainly, but even so, the hall went quiet around her. When she described turning the hounds back on their handlers, a woman near the fire made a small involuntary sound.

"And before that," Maren said. "In Thornhollow. What did you do there?"

"Bought gossip from pigeons. Sold charms that were mostly theater. Kept my head down."

"What kind of charms?"

"Luck knots. Fever strings. Nonsense, mostly. I stitched a little intent into them—enough to make a farmer feel luckier, which is half the cure anyway—but nothing that would show. Nothing deep."

Maren smiled. It transformed her face, made her look briefly like someone who had once laughed often. "Child, I have listened to gift-bearers for fifty years. Beast-speakers, weather-callers, finders, healers. Every gift has a texture. A grain, like wood." She leaned forward. "Yours has no grain. Yours goes all the way down."

"I don't understand."

"When you spoke to my ward at the boundary—what did it cost you?"

Jude hesitated. "A headache. A nosebleed, after the hounds. Why?"

"Because when Col speaks to an ox, the ox speaks back, and nothing is spent. Because when Sera calls rain, she pays in sleep. Every gift is a trade." Maren's voice dropped, and the whole hall leaned in without meaning to. "But yours opens doors that were locked from the other side. You spoke to a weaving. Do you understand how impossible that is? A ward is not alive. It does not have ears. Unless the speaker can address the magic itself—not the beast wearing the charm, but the charm. Not the bird, but the wing. Not the hound—" her head turned, precise, "—but the leash."

The word went through Jude like cold water. *The leash.* On the ford bank, when she'd spoken to the pack leader, she hadn't just asked. She had *unhooked* something—she'd felt the inquisitors' binding on the animals, the compulsion knotted into their training, and she had spoken to the knot, and the knot had answered.

"I heard it," she said slowly. "Under their training. There was a... a shape holding them. I talked to the shape."

Maren sat back. Around the hall, people were looking at each other. The twins whispered in unison. The fire-tender had gone pale.

"In fifty years," Maren said quietly, "I have met two of the deep kind. The first taught me everything I know and died in Aurelmark. The second was a woman named Lira Amberdale."

The hall's hearth cracked. Somewhere a goat complained. Jude heard none of it.

"My mother's name," she said, "was Elira."

"Elira," Maren repeated, tasting it. "Yes. That would be right. She shortened it even here, even among friends. Careful habits." Her blind face turned fully toward Jude, and though there were no eyes to meet, Jude felt seen more thoroughly than she had ever been seen in her life. "Your mother sat where you're sitting, nine years ago, with a baby's blanket in her lap and fear coming off her like heat off a road. She asked me to hide her daughter's name from every register in Vaelmoor. I did. And then she went north, to the palace, and I never saw her again."

"She went to find out what happened to the king," Jude said. "That's what everyone says. That's what the rats said. She went to the palace and didn't come back."

"Is that what you believe?"

"It's what I have."

Maren was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gentled into something almost apologetic. "Then let me give you one more thing to carry, and you may do with it what you like. Your mother had your same voice. Deep speech. And before she left, she told me why she was going, in words I have repeated to myself every night since." She paused. "She said: *The Regent eats voices. Someone has to teach the kingdom to sing louder.*"

* * *

Pip found her afterward, sitting on the turf roof of the meeting hall with her knees drawn up, watching the sanctuary move through its evening. Lamplight in round windows. Bread smoke. A child laughing by the goat pen, and the goat answering in its flat, contented way. It looked like safety. It smelled like safety. Jude had spent twenty-two years learning that both could lie.

"You've been up here an hour," Pip said, hauling himself onto the turf beside her. His heel was bandaged now—one of the healers had looked at it, actually looked, without checking over her shoulder first. He'd cried a little about it and pretended he hadn't. "One-Eye says you're doing the thing where you think instead of talk."

"One-Eye should mind his own business."

"He says you'll say that."

Despite herself, Jude snorted. Below, Maren moved between buildings on her silent sure feet, and people bent toward her and straightened lighter. Belonging, Jude thought. It was being offered down there. It had been offered the moment the ward opened. All she had to do was climb down and take it, and stay, and dig herself into the hill beside the rest of them, and wait out the Hunt like a seed waits out winter.

"They want us to stay," Pip said.

"I know."

"We could. Bram—" His voice caught on the name. Three days now, and he still said it like a question. "When Bram gets out—if he gets out—this is where he'd come. This is exactly where he'd tell us to wait."

"Yes."

"So what's wrong?"

Jude watched the lamplight. "Do you know what Maren called me? The deep kind. Like it's a lineage. Like there's a shelf I belong on." She pulled a thread of loose turf and rolled it between her fingers. "Every gift costs, she said. Fine. Rain-callers pay in sleep. Finders pay in headaches. So what does the deep kind pay? I talked to a ward today, Pip. A ward is a lock. And locks don't open for free."

"You got a nosebleed."

"After the hounds, I slept fourteen hours and dreamed about nothing. Not peaceful-nothing. Empty-nothing. Like something had been scooped out and hadn't grown back yet." She looked at him. "And if that's the price for opening a lock, what's the price for opening whatever's under the palace? What's the toll for waking a king?"

Pip was quiet. He was fifteen and had run from a house of silk and cruelty, and he was better at silence than most adults Jude knew. Finally he said, "You're going anyway. Aren't you."

"I haven't decided."

"You have. You've had it decided since the ford. You just keep hoping it'll change its mind." He hugged his knees, mirroring her. "My father sold my sister to the Hunt for a title and a tax exemption. Everyone in that house knew and nobody said a word, because saying a word was dangerous and staying comfortable was easy. I swore I'd never be that." He shrugged. "So. Whatever you decide. I'm not letting you walk north alone."

"You're fifteen."

"I'm a baron's son with a forged travel seal and a talent for being underestimated. You need me."

Below them, a bell began to ring—not alarm, something softer, calling the sanctuary to evening meal. Doors opened. People emerged. And among them, moving with terrible certainty straight toward the turf roof, came Maren.

"She wants you," Pip said.

"She always wants me. It's very flattering and very alarming."

Maren stopped below them, face tipped up. "Jude Amberdale. Come down. There's a rider on the south road, and he's carrying a crown warrant, and he's stopping at every farm between here and the river asking after a knight, a boy, and a wolf." A pause. "He's asking after you by name."

The turf thread slipped from Jude's fingers. Below, the sanctuary lights burned warm and gold, and for the first time since Thornhollow Bridge, since Bram's hands shoving her toward the marshes, since the gates closing like a mouth—she understood exactly what she stood to lose.

"Get everyone below," she said, already sliding down the roof. "Wake the ward. Whatever it costs—I'll pay it."
