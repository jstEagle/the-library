## 11. Lessons in Deep Speech

Pip learned the sanctuary's rhythms by sound before he learned them by sight.

The hill-hollow woke in layers: first the goats, complaining in their pen like old men at a funeral; then the smell of barley porridge drifting up from the hearth-room; then Maren's staff tapping stone, tap-tap-tap, a metronome that meant *come, we're beginning*. By the third morning Pip was awake before the tapping, lying on his straw pallet with his eyes fixed on the low ceiling of woven branches, listening to Jude breathe in the next alcove over.

She'd started talking in her sleep. Not words—more like the *shape* of words, a low murmur that made the mice in the walls go still and the candle-flame lean toward her as if it were listening too.

That was the thing about living with gift-bearers, Pip decided. You never got to stop being amazed, and you never got to stop being afraid. Sometimes both at once.

* * *

The training ground was a shelf of flat rock behind the sanctuary, half-hidden by a curtain of ivy and warded so thoroughly that Pip felt it like a change in air pressure when he stepped through—a faint hum behind his teeth, gone as soon as he noticed it. Maren was already there, seated on a stump with her blind eyes turned toward the morning sun, her face tilted up like a plant.

"You're late," she said, though she couldn't have known that. Or maybe she could. Pip had stopped trying to figure out which things Maren knew by magic and which she knew by being Maren.

"He's not," Jude said, dropping down beside him on the mossy edge of the rock. "I'm early. He's exactly on time."

"Then you're both late to my lesson." Maren patted the stump across from her. "Sit, boy. You're not here to watch. You're here because watching is how people convince themselves they understand something."

Pip sat. He'd been at the sanctuary six days and he still hadn't figured out how to argue with her. Nobody had. Even Tam—the big quiet man who'd once tried to steal from the stores and been caught by Maren before he touched the latch—just went red and apologized.

Maren held out her hand. In her palm lay a charm: a knuckle of river-stone wrapped in copper wire, worn smooth, the kind of trinket Jude used to sell in Thornhollow markets for two coppers apiece. Harmless. A warmth-charm, maybe, or a luck-turner.

"This belonged to a girl named Sela," Maren said. "She was taken in the first spring of the Hunt. She left this behind in a wall-cavity where she hid her real work." Her thumb moved over the stone, slow, almost tender. "It's just a charm. Small magic. A warming stone, nothing more—it would keep your hands comfortable through a cold night. But it's *bound*, boy. Everything made with the gift is bound. Do you know what that means?"

"That someone tied it together?"

"It means there's a knot in the world where there wasn't one before." Maren turned her face toward him, and even blind, the look went straight through him. "Sela tied this knot when she was eleven years old. It will hold until the stone crumbles or the wire rusts through, unless someone speaks to it. Unless someone asks it, politely, in the deep speech, to let go."

Jude shifted beside him. "You want me to unbind it."

"I want you to *listen* to it first. Unbinding without listening is not unbinding. It's tearing." Maren set the stone on the flat rock between them. "And tearing has a price. The deep speech always takes something from the speaker. Always. The only question is whether you pay it knowingly or stupidly."

"What does it take?" Pip asked, before he could stop himself.

Maren smiled, and it wasn't a comforting smile. "That depends on what you have. Sit still, both of you. We begin."

* * *

The first hour was listening, and Pip discovered that listening—at least the way Maren meant it—was harder than any fencing drill his father's master-at-arms had ever put him through.

"Close your eyes," Maren said. "Not all the way. A sliver. Enough that the world goes soft at the edges. Now breathe. Slow. Slower. When your breath is even, reach—not with your hands, not with your mind, whatever you think those are. Reach with your *voice*. The voice you have before you speak."

"That makes no sense," Pip said.

"Good," said Maren. "Sense is what stands between you and hearing."

He tried anyway, mostly because Jude was doing it and he refused to be worse at something than her. He closed his eyes to slits. He breathed. The morning sounds came in layers—the goats, the wind in the ivy, Tam splitting firewood somewhere below, the far-off complaint of a jay—and slowly, as his breath evened out, the layers began to separate. Not louder. Clearer. Like mud settling out of water.

And underneath everything, so faint he thought at first he'd imagined it, there was a hum.

It came from the stone on the rock between them. A tiny, patient vibration, like a fly asleep inside a wall. Warmth, he realized. The charm wasn't warm to the touch—he'd picked it up last night, snooping, and it had been cold as any stone—but somewhere inside its binding, the idea of warmth was still burning, still holding, eleven years after the girl who made it had been carted north.

"Sela," he whispered, without meaning to.

Beside him, Jude's breath caught. "You hear it," she said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes were open now, wide, fixed on him. "Pip. You actually hear it."

"I don't—" He did. He absolutely did. "I don't have the gift. I can't talk to beasts. I've never done anything. I ran away from home, that's my whole story."

"Mm." Maren's voice was dry as old paper. "The gift wears many coats, boy. Most people who carry it never find out—they die never having heard a single song in a stone. Some hear it and call themselves mad. And some," and here her face turned toward Jude, "some are born with the deep kind, and make everyone around them louder without knowing it. You've been sitting next to her for weeks. She hums, whether she means to or not. Anyone with ears for it would start to grow ears."

Pip looked at Jude. Jude looked at Pip. Something passed between them that he didn't have words for—that she'd been making him *more* without either of them knowing it, that the thing he'd always counted as his defining emptiness might not be empty after all.

"Does that mean I could—"

"No," Maren said. "Not today. Today you listen and you keep your mouth shut, because an untrained voice poking at a binding is a child with a knife in a rope shop. You'll learn patience if it kills us both." She settled back against her staff. "Again. Both of you. Breathe. This time, don't just hear the hum. Hear the *shape* of it. Where does the knot sit? How many strands? What holds it tight?"

They breathed. The morning wore on. Pip sank deeper into the hum than he'd ever sunk into anything, and found that the charm's binding was like a woven basket seen from inside—strands crossing strands, each one humming a slightly different note, and at the center, small and stubborn and heartbreakingly clear, a child's intent: *warm hands, cold nights, for Mama.*

His eyes stung. He kept breathing.

* * *

By the fourth day, Jude stopped listening and started speaking.

Pip heard it happen because he was three feet away. One moment she was silent, eyes narrowed to slivers, breath even; the next, a sound came out of her that wasn't quite sound—a low resonance more felt than heard, moving through the rock under them, through the ivy, through Pip's own ribs. The jay in the oak above went silent. The firewood-splitting below stopped mid-swing.

The charm's hum changed pitch. Rose. Wavered. Answered.

"There," Maren murmured. "There it is. Ask it, girl. Don't command. Ask."

"*Let go,*" Jude said—or sang, or *spoke*, the word sliding between language and music—and the copper wire on the stone shivered, unwound a quarter turn, and went still.

Jude opened her eyes. She was gray-faced and shaking, and a thin line of blood had run from her nose to her lip without either of them noticing.

"Pip," she said, and her voice sounded scraped. "What day is it?"

"Fourth day of lessons. Why?"

"What season?"

He laughed, because it had to be a joke. Then he saw her face. "Jude. It's autumn. Harvest's coming in. You helped carry the barley two days ago."

"Barley," she repeated, like the word was foreign coin. Then her eyes focused, and the panic drained out of them, replaced by something worse: recognition. "No. No, I remember the barley. I remember—I remember carrying it, and Tam dropping a bundle, and—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "But it's *farther away* than it was yesterday. Like a room I walked through instead of lived in."

"The toll," Maren said quietly.

Pip's stomach dropped. "The what?"

"The price." Maren reached out and took Jude's wrist, steadying her with surprising strength. "I told you the speech takes something. For a small binding, a small thing. A memory doesn't vanish, girl—it recedes. It becomes a story instead of a life. Each time you speak deeply, you spend a little of yourself to do it. That is why the Regent's harvest works, if you're wondering. That is why stolen voices are worth feeding to a machine." Her blind gaze settled on Jude with terrible gentleness. "And that is why your mother, if she attempted the great unbinding beneath the throne, paid a price no one should ever have to pay."

Jude lowered her hands. Her nose had stopped bleeding. She looked, Pip thought, like someone standing at the edge of a very tall cliff in fog, unable to see the bottom.

"How much?" Jude asked. "For the king. For something that big. How much would it cost?"

"Everything you can spare," Maren said, "and then everything you can't."

* * *

That evening Pip couldn't sleep. He got up, careful of the creaking floorboard, and found Jude already outside, sitting on the sanctuary steps with her knees pulled up, watching the moon come over the ridge. One-Eye lay beside her, a shadow with one silver eye, and the wolf lifted his head as Pip approached but didn't growl. He'd stopped growling at Pip around day three, which Pip chose to interpret as acceptance rather than tolerance.

"She's going to kill herself," Pip said, sitting down on Jude's other side. "Doing this. You saw her today. One little charm and she lost a season of her own life. And she's still going to do it. She's going to walk into that palace and pay and pay until there's nothing left of her to pay *with*."

One-Eye made a sound low in his chest—not quite agreement, not quite warning.

"I know," Jude said.

"You *know*? That's it? You know, and you're going to do it anyway?"

"What would you have me do, Pip?" She turned to look at him, and in the moonlight her face was young and tired and absolutely certain, which was the most frightening combination he knew. "Stay here? Let the Hunt widen? Let them take more Selas, more mothers, more sisters?" Her jaw tightened. "My mother is under that palace. Maybe alive. Maybe hollowed out like the rats said the taken ones get. And the only voice in the world that can wake the true king is mine, and every day I wait, the price gets paid by somebody else."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He stared out at the dark valley. Below, the sanctuary's few lamps glowed like embers banked for the night. Somewhere in there were twenty-odd people who'd trusted a blind seer to keep them safe, and a blind seer who'd looked at a market-charmer from Thornhollow and called her *the deep kind*, and Pip—who had spent his whole life being nobody's son worth keeping, whose own father had traded his sister to the Hunt for favor and silence—was sitting on a step in the cold realizing that he'd finally found people worth losing, and that losing them was exactly what was coming.

"If it takes memories," he said slowly, "then practice on things you won't miss. Bad ones. Sell the bad ones first."

Jude blinked at him. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, cracked, real laugh. "Sell my bad memories."

"Why not? You've got years of them. Bram getting captured. The hedge-witch on the roof. Your father, if he was anything like mine." He shrugged, aiming for casual and probably missing. "Seems wasteful to spend the good ones."

The wolf rumbled again, and this time it sounded almost like laughter too.

"Maybe," Jude said softly. "Maybe that's the first sensible thing anyone's said since I got here." She leaned her head back against the doorpost. "Ask Maren tomorrow. If she says yes—"

The wards screamed.

There was no other word for it. The hum Pip had learned to feel behind his teeth tore upward into a shriek that rattled the shutters, and every lamp in the sanctuary flared blue-white, and Maren's voice cut through the night from the hearth-room, sharp as a blade:

"*Someone's testing the outer weave.*"

One-Eye was on his feet, hackles up, a growl rolling out of him like distant thunder. Down in the valley, far off but unmistakable, a horn blew—one long note, then another, then a third, in the pattern Pip had heard described by every refugee who'd staggered up the hill.

Three notes. Search-party signal. The Hunt had found the marsh road.

And they weren't leaving.

Jude stood, and the moonlight caught her face, and Pip saw that the fear in it had already burned down to something harder.

"Wake Tam," she said. "Get everyone to the lower vault. Now."

The wards screamed again—closer this time—and somewhere out in the dark, hounds began to sing.
