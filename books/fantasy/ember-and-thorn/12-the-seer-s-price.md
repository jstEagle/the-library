## 12. The Seer's Price

Maren did not sleep in beds. She slept sitting up, in a chair by the sanctuary's eastern window, her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a coach that had been delayed thirty years. Jude found her there before dawn, the sky still the color of wet slate, and stood in the doorway long enough for the blind seer to know she'd come.

"You dreamt of chains," Maren said. Not a question.

"I dreamt of singing. Chains made of it." Jude crossed the room and sat on the stool across from her, knees nearly touching. "I've been dreaming it since the ford. Since I turned the hounds. It gets louder every night."

"Good." Maren's clouded eyes found her with unsettling precision. "That means it wants to be heard."

"Nothing *wants*, Maren. Chains don't want."

"The ones under Aurelmark do." The old woman leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep seams of her face. "Sit properly, girl. What I'm about to tell you, you'll carry for the rest of your life. Best not to hear it slouched."

Jude straightened. She'd been braced for bad news since the wolves at the ford—braced since Thornhollow, if she was honest—but something in Maren's voice made the bracing useless. You couldn't prepare for a thing until you knew its shape.

"Tell me about the king," Maren said. "What do they teach in Thornhollow?"

"That he rules from Aurelmark. That he's old and wise and rarely seen. That the Regent speaks with his voice when he's... indisposed."

"And you believed that?"

"I believed it the way you believe stone is hard. I never thought to test it."

Maren smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "No one does. That's the genius of it. A lie worn so long it becomes furniture." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and shuffled to the hearth where a kettle hung. She poured two cups of nettle tea without looking, without spilling, and pressed one into Jude's hands. Her fingers were cold as river stones.

"Thirty years ago," Maren said, "there was a king named Aldric Vaelmoor. A good man—not a great one, but good, which is rarer. He had a wife who died young, a son he adored, and a kingdom that mostly ran itself because he let clever people be clever. And he had one flaw that mattered." She sipped her own tea. "He was lonely. Lonely men in high towers will talk to anything. Even things that aren't alive."

"What isn't alive?"

"A construct. A made-thing. The court mages of that era were brilliant and bored, and boredom breeds ambition. They wove a servant out of song and shadow—a regent-in-waiting, an advisor that could never die, never tire, never betray. A perfect counselor." Maren's mouth twisted. "They forgot that a thing built to advise will eventually want to decide. And a thing that cannot die learns patience the way rivers learn stone."

"It killed him?"

"Worse. It put him to sleep." Maren set down her cup. "Beneath the throne room there is a vault. Old even then—older than the palace, older than the city. The founders of Vaelmoor built it for reasons that were already forgotten. The Regent moved Aldric into that vault and bound him in chains of woven song. Enchantment, girl. Living enchantment, self-renewing, fed on—" she paused, choosing the word with visible care— "*voice*. On gift. Every gift-bearer the Hunt takes, every hedge-witch and beast-speaker and storm-caller dragged north in those carts—they're not being punished. They're being *harvested*. Their magic is drained into the Regent, and the Regent feeds the chains, and the chains keep the king asleep, and the sleeping king keeps the lie alive."

The tea had gone cold in Jude's hands. She hadn't noticed drinking any of it.

"The Hollow Village," she said quietly. "The silence. No dogs barking. No birds."

"Harvested clean. When they take a gifted one, everything nearby goes quiet for a while. The land itself misses their voice." Maren turned her blind eyes toward the window, toward the gray light coming up over the hill. "The taken are kept in cells beneath the palace. Drained a little each day. Enough to stay alive. Enough to keep feeding it forever."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I was there." Maren's voice didn't waver, but her hands, wrapped around her cup, went very still. "Thirty years ago I was a girl at court. A seamstress with a small gift—I could feel the truth of a woven thing, thread by thread. I felt what the Regent was the day it was finished. I ran before they could register me. I've been running, and gathering, and waiting, ever since."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone who could unbind him." Maren faced her fully now. "There is exactly one kind of voice that can unravel woven song, Jude. Not storm-callers. Not fire-hands. Not beast-speakers, even. Deep speech—the kind that doesn't talk *to* enchantment but talks *as* it, thread to thread, sister to sister. I've listened to hundreds of voices in forty years of hiding. Yours is the first deep kind I've heard since—" She stopped.

"Since when?"

"Since your mother."

* * *

The word landed like a dropped stone. Jude sat with it, and around it, and tried to breathe through the ringing in her ears.

"My mother," she said finally, "was a charm-seller. A good one. She taught me everything about hiding—how to pass as ordinary, how to make trinkets that were just trinkets. She hated the gift. She told me it was a curse we carried and the only mercy was silence."

"She told you the truth as she needed you to believe it." Maren refilled Jude's cup though Jude hadn't asked. "Did she ever go to the capital? Three years ago—did she leave suddenly? Say she had business in Aurelmark?"

Jude's throat worked. "She said she'd be back before the frost. She kissed my forehead and told me to mind the shop and she—" The memory came whole and terrible: the way her mother had held on a half-second too long. The way she'd looked back from the gate. "She never came back. I got one letter. It said she'd found work and not to look for her. The handwriting was hers. Mostly."

"Mostly," Maren repeated softly. "Now hear the rest, child, because this is the part that will hurt." She took Jude's hands. Her grip was stronger than it should have been. "Your mother wasn't a charm-seller who happened to have the deep kind of voice. She was a listener. One of us—one of the scattered network Maren had spent decades building. We called ourselves the Unbound. Your mother was our finest ear. And three years ago, when the Regent's harvest began to fail—when the taken started dying faster than the Hunt could replace them—she made a decision."

"No." Jude pulled her hands back. "No, she wouldn't—"

"She walked into Aurelmark through the front gate. Registered herself. Let them take her." Maren's voice was gentle and relentless, a tide that would not stop. "Not because she was caught. Because she'd learned what was under the throne, and she had the deep speech, and she thought—she *believed*—that one voice, offered freely, could get close enough to unbind the king from inside. She went to save the kingdom, Jude. And she went to save you. Because your name was already in the registers. One more harvest season and the Hunt would have come for a girl who sold charms in Thornhollow. She traded herself for your name being struck from the lists."

The room tilted. Jude gripped the stool's edge. Somewhere far away, Pip was splitting firewood behind the sanctuary—she could hear the rhythmic thunk, thunk,Thunk—and One-Eye was somewhere on the ridge, and the world was continuing its ordinary business while her entire life rearranged itself around a single terrible fact.

"She went to free the king," Jude whispered. "And she failed."

"No." Maren leaned in close, and her breath was cold and smelled of nettles and old stone. "That's what I need you to understand. She did *not* fail."

"I don't understand."

"The unbinding requires two things. A deep voice, yes. But also proximity sustained over time—the chains are woven through the vault itself, through the bedrock, and you cannot sing them loose in an hour or a day. It takes weeks of work, thread by thread, and the toll—" Maren hesitated. "You've felt the toll already. In your lessons. What the deep speech takes from you when you use it."

Memories. Pieces of herself. The taste of her seventh summer, gone. The color of a door she'd loved. Small things, so far. Small payments on an enormous debt.

"Your mother began the unbinding," Maren said. "In the dark, in the vault, cell after cell, night after night, while they drained her. She loosened threads, Jude. Real ones. The chains are weaker today than they were three years ago because of her. But the Regent found her before she finished. And here is the horror, the thing I've carried alone for three years: the Regent did not kill her. Could not afford to. A deep voice is the rarest treasure it knows. So it *kept* her. Hollowed her just enough to stop the work, left her alive in the harvesting cells, and has been feeding on her ever since—the richest vein it owns."

The fire popped. Outside, Pip's axe had stopped.

"So she's alive," Jude said. The words came out flat, strange, like coins from a dead man's pocket. "My mother is alive. Under the palace. Right now. Tonight."

"Yes."

"And she's been there three years while I sold charms and hid and survived and—" Her voice cracked open. "While I thought she'd abandoned me. While I hated her a little, Maren. I hated her a little for leaving."

"I know." Maren reached out and, for the first time since Jude had arrived at the sanctuary, touched her face—found the tear tracks before they fell. "And she has spent those same three years in the dark, listening to them drain her, holding on to one thought. Do you know what that thought was?"

Jude shook her head, unable to speak.

"That her daughter's name stayed off the lists. That you were safe. That the price was worth it." Maren withdrew her hand. "Grief is a luxury, Jude Amberdale. Yours can wait until she's free. Hers has waited three years."

* * *

Jude walked out of the sanctuary and up the hill without deciding to. Her feet chose the path; she simply followed. The morning was coming up pale and cold, mist burning off the hollows, and One-Eye materialized beside her the way he always did—silent, sudden, a gray shape at the edge of vision.

"You know," she said to him. "You've known all along. That's why you came to my door. That's why you led me here."

The wolf's single amber eye regarded her. "I knew your mother," he said, in that voice like gravel and old grief. "I promised her I'd watch for you. I didn't promise to tell you everything at once. Some truths break a person if they arrive too fast."

"Would it have broken me? Knowing she was alive?"

"Knowing she was alive?" One-Eye's lip lifted slightly, not quite a snarl. "No. Knowing *why* she was alive—that she sold herself for you, that she's been hollowed and harvested while you learned to hide—that would have broken you before you were strong enough to hold it. Now you're strong enough. Barely."

Jue sank down onto a flat stone, and the wolf lay at her feet, warm against her boots despite the cold coming off the ground. Below them, smoke rose from the sanctuary chimney. Pip's axe had resumed—thunk, thunk, thunk—steady as a heartbeat.

"Maren wants me to finish what my mother started," Jude said. "Go to Aurelmark. Get taken. Go down into the vault and unbind the king."

"Yes."

"It'll cost memories. Maybe all of them. Maybe everything I am."

"Yes."

"And if I don't go, my mother stays in the dark forever, and the Regent keeps eating this kingdom one voice at a time, and eventually the Hunt finds this sanctuary too, and everyone I love burns with it." She stared at the horizon, where the road north disappeared into haze. "Those are the choices."

"One more," One-Eye said. "You could refuse. Live. Hide. Grow old. There's no shame in it. Your mother wouldn't blame you—she paid so you'd have the right to choose."

Jude was quiet a long moment. Then: "That's the worst thing you could have said. Because now I actually have to choose."

The wolf huffed—almost a laugh. "Yes. That's how freedom works. It's terrible."

She looked down at him. "How do I get into the harvesting cells?"

"You don't sneak in. You get *taken*. Same as your mother. The Hunt is always hungry for new voices, and a deep voice walking willingly into a registration line—" His eye gleamed. "They won't ask questions. They'll celebrate."

"They'll drain me."

"A little at a time. Which buys you time. Weeks, maybe. Long enough to find her cell. Long enough to start the work she began." He paused. "It's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan."

"It usually is."

Pip crested the hill then, axe over his shoulder, wood dust on his sleeves, and stopped when he saw them sitting there. He was sixteen and sharp-eyed and missed nothing, and whatever he saw in her face made him drop the axe and cross the grass at a run.

"What happened?" he demanded. "What did she tell you?"

Jude looked at him—at this boy who'd run from a father who sold his own daughter to the Hunt, who'd followed her across marshes and burning bridges, who called her family without flinching—and she made the choice right there, on the cold stone, with the mist still burning off the hills.

"Pip," she said. "My mother is alive. She's under the palace in Aurelmark. And I'm going to go get her."

His face went through a dozen expressions in three seconds—shock, fear, grief, and then something harder settling underneath, something that looked a lot like the same resolve she'd just found. He sat down heavily on the stone beside her.

"Then I'm coming," he said. "Don't argue. You're terrible at arguing."

One-Eye snorted. From below, faintly, Maren's voice drifted up the hillside—not words, just tone, the seer singing softly to her wards the way she did every morning. But this morning the melody was different. Older. Slower. A lament, maybe. Or a warning.

Or a farewell.

Jude stood, and the wind coming down from the north smelled faintly of bells—Aurelmark's bells, a hundred miles away, ringing the hour in a city that didn't know yet what was walking toward it.

"We leave at first light tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I learn everything Maren knows about the vault. Every thread she remembers. Every note of the binding song."

"And after that?" Pip asked.

"After that," Jude said, "we let them take me."

The hook is set. The next chapter belongs to Pip, and to the road north.
