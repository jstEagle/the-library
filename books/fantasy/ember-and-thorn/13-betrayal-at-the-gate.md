## 13. Betrayal at the Gate

Pip had never trusted Aldous Fen.

It wasn't anything the man said. It was the way he said nothing. While the rest of the sanctuary's survivors argued around the fire about roads and risks, Aldous sat apart with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a verdict. He had two children—Wren and little Tam, seven years old and always cold—and Pip had watched him watch them with an expression he'd never once turned on another living soul.

Fear, Pip knew by now, looked different on everyone. On Bram it had looked like a jaw set against shouting. On Jude it looked like stillness, a held breath that could last hours. On Maren it looked like nothing at all, because Maren had spent her whole life walking toward the things other people ran from.

On Aldous Fen it looked like arithmetic.

Pip saw him doing it three nights before the fire. Saw him counting heads at supper. Counting the inquisitor patrols from the ridge. Counting, Pip understood much too late, the price of his children's lives against the price of everyone else's.

But that was later. First there was the night of lessons, and the last good night any of them would have for a long time.

* * *

Maren taught deep speech the way she did everything: by touch. She couldn't see Jude's face, so she read her voice instead, and she said it worked better anyway. "The gift lives in the voice," she told Jude, her blind eyes aimed somewhere past the firelight. "Not the throat. The *voice*. The part of you that was speaking before you had words."

Pip sat outside the circle, ostensibly keeping watch, actually listening. He'd stopped pretending he wasn't listening days ago. Jude hadn't stopped pretending not to notice, which was its own kind of kindness.

"Say it again," Maren said. "The warding charm on the eastern gate. What does it sound like?"

Jude closed her eyes. When she spoke, her voice came out strange—layered, almost doubled, as if two people were saying the same word a half-breath apart. "Like a rope," she said. "Braided. Three strands. One of them is fraying."

"Because I wove it tired." Maren smiled faintly. "Good. Now unbind it."

"No," said One-Eye, from the shadows where wolves sat when they wanted to be ignored and never were.

"It's my own work," Maren said. "And she has to learn what it costs while the stakes are a gate and not a throne."

Pip watched Jude reach for the ward the way you'd reach into a snare you already knew was sprung. Her lips moved without sound. The fire guttered. And then Jude made a small noise—not a scream, more like the sound of someone setting down something heavy they'd forgotten they were carrying—and pressed both hands flat against the ground.

"What did it take?" Maren asked quietly.

"I don't—" Jude frowned. "The name of my first pony. I know I had one. I can't—"

"Bram will remember it," Maren said. "That's what we're for. That's why no one carries this alone." She turned her face toward Pip, though she couldn't see him, and somehow he felt seen anyway. "You're listening again, boy."

"I'm keeping watch."

"You're keeping company. There's a difference, and it matters." She patted the ground beside her. "Come here. If you're going to follow her into Aurelmark, you should know what the deep speech sounds like when it's dying."

Pip went. He didn't understand half of what Maren said that night—the tolls and threads, the way every enchantment was a song someone had paid to sing—but he remembered one thing exactly, because Maren gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks and said it twice:

"When the wards fail—and someday they'll fail—you won't hear it happen. You'll feel it. Like a door opening behind you. Don't look back to check. Look forward. Run toward whoever you love fastest."

He laughed, because it sounded like nonsense, and because laughing was easier than asking what she meant.

She meant all of it.

* * *

Aldous left the sanctuary two nights later, telling everyone he was going down to the lowland farms to trade for salt and news.

Bram offered to go with him. Aldous refused, too quickly, with a smile Pip would spend the rest of his life taking apart.

* * *

He came back at dusk on the third day, alone, smelling of woodsmoke that wasn't sanctuary woodsmoke, and he wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. He brought salt. He brought bread. He brought his children honey cakes, and Wren squealed and Tam dropped his stick and Pip stood in the doorway of the longhouse watching Aldous hug them and thought, with a coldness that surprised him:

*What did it cost?*

He didn't ask. That was his sin, and he'd carry it. He didn't ask, because the sanctuary was warm and the bread was real and asking questions felt like inviting the answer inside.

One-Eye asked instead. The wolf appeared at Aldous's shoulder as he set the honey cakes down, close enough that his breath stirred the man's hair, and said in that gravel-and-grief voice: "You smell of the king's road. And of dogs."

"Just farm dogs," Aldous said. His hands were very steady. Steadier than his voice. "Nothing else."

One-Eye held him in that single yellow stare for a long moment. Then he walked away, and found Pip splitting kindling, and said only: "Watch him."

"I have been."

"Watch harder."

* * *

They came in the dark, three nights later, and Pip felt it before he heard it—just as Maren had promised. A door opening behind him. A coldness sliding up between his shoulder blades like a knife laid flat against skin.

He woke in the loft of the longhouse with his heart hammering and lay still, listening. Crickets. Wind. Then—faint, miles off but closing—the belling of hounds. Not hunting cry. *Cast.* Dogs being given a scent they already had.

He was down the ladder and across the yard before he'd decided to move.

"Maren." He shook her awake. "Maren, the wards—"

Her eyes opened, white and calm in the dark. "I know, child. How many?"

"How would I—"

"The hounds. How many voices?"

He listened. God help him, he listened, and the night was full of them. "Twenty. Thirty. More behind them. And horses."

Maren rose without hurry, the way she did everything, and began dressing as if for market. "Wake the others. Everyone to the north tunnel. You have perhaps ten minutes."

"And you?"

"I'm going to hold the door."

"There's another way—"

"Pip." She took his face in her hands, and her palms were dry and warm and steady, and Pip hated her a little for how steady they were. "I am seventy-one years old, blind as a stone, and I have been running since before your father was born. This is the first night in forty years I've known exactly what I'm for. Don't you dare take it from me."

He didn't take it from her. That was the second sin he carried.

* * *

Afterward—after the marshes, after the ash, after everything—he tried to reconstruct those ten minutes and could never make them fit together right. Memory broke them into pieces, the way a wave breaks glass.

Jude in the yard, screaming Aldous's name and getting silence, then getting truth: Aldous on his knees by the south fence with Wren and Tam clutched to his chest, sobbing, "They have my sister, they had her letters, they said they'd only want me, they *promised*—" and Jude looking at him for one long terrible moment before saying, in a voice Pip had never heard her use, "Get your children and run north with the others, and pray I never stop being the person who lets you."

Bram hauling the tunnel bar aside with his bare hands, splintering his palms, roaring for everyone to move, MOVE—

Tam crying because he'd left his stick.

One-Eye carrying Wren when her legs gave out, the girl buried in the wolf's fur like she'd been born there.

And Maren standing alone in the center of the sanctuary green, facing the darkening treeline, raising her arms as the first hounds broke from it, and beginning to sing.

Not beast-speech. Not deep speech, even. Just an old woman's voice, cracked and true, singing a lullaby Pip's mother used to sing him, and the wards rising around her like walls of light made out of every thread she'd ever woven, and the hounds breaking against them like water on rock.

"GO!" she called back over her shoulder, not turning, not ever turning. "Don't look back! Run toward whoever you love fastest!"

Pip ran. He looked back anyway. Once.

He saw the first inquisitor arrow take her through the chest, and he saw her keep singing, and he saw the wards hold, hold, hold—and then the fire came, green and wrong, alchemist's fire that ate magic along with wood, and the last thing he saw of Maren was her silhouette inside the burning light, arms still raised, still singing, a lullaby going up with the smoke.

Then Bram's hand closed on his collar and dragged him into the dark, and the night behind them filled with orange, and the sanctuary hill burned like a second dawn.

* * *

They walked until the sun came up. Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the ashes weren't already saying.

They were nineteen now. Nineteen, out of sixty-three who had slept safe under Maren's wards a week ago. Some had scattered on their own roads. Some had been taken. Aldous and his children had vanished into the marsh paths during the night—gone north, someone said, gone to ground—and no one went after them, and no one said Jude's name in connection with the empty space where her rage had been, and that silence was worse than shouting.

At gray morning they stopped in a hollow of birches, and Jude finally sat down, and Pip watched her put her face in her hands and stay that way for a long time.

He sat beside her. He didn't touch her. He'd learned that much about grief—it bites the hand that reaches too soon.

"They sang her name," Jude said finally, muffled. "The hounds' handlers. When the wards fell. They sang it back at her. Mocking her. Using her own song." Her hands lowered. Her eyes were dry, which frightened Pip more than tears would have. "They harvest voices, Pip. They *keep* them. Somewhere in that palace is a room full of stolen songs, and one of them is hers now."

"Then we get it back."

"With what? I've got a gift that eats my memories every time I use it, a wolf who's older than the kingdom, a disgraced knight, and a runaway boy who can't even—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "I'm sorry. That's not fair."

"No," Pip said. "It's completely fair. Keep going."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, come to pay respects.

One-Eye limped out of the trees—truly limping, favoring a foreleg scorched by the alchemist's fire—and lay down heavily across their feet like a wall built out of wolf.

"The Regent keeps the voices in the vault," he said. "Below the throne. I told you this."

"You told us the vault exists. You didn't tell us how to get into a palace that's expecting us."

"It isn't expecting *you*." The yellow eye turned to Jude. "It's expecting gift-bearers. It's hungry for them. Every cage it builds, it builds with an open door."

Pip understood one heartbeat before Jude did, and the understanding hit him like cold water.

"No," he said.

"It's the only door that opens," One-Eye said.

"I said *no*. You are not walking into a harvesting cell. You are not letting them put you in chains and—"

"Pip." Jude reached over and took his hand. Her grip was steady. Her hands were cold. "Maren died holding a door so we could choose our road. I'm choosing it. But I'm not choosing it alone, and I'm not choosing it tonight." She looked around the hollow—at Bram, silent and splinter-handed; at the ragged remnant of the nineteen; at the smoke still smudging the northern sky. "We bury our dead today. We plan tomorrow. And in three days, we walk to Aurelmark."

"To do what?" Bram asked roughly. "Walk up to the gates?"

"No." Jude's gaze settled on Pip, and there was something in it he didn't like at all—a plan forming, and himself inside it. "His father sold his sister to the Hunt. Which means his father knows how the taken are moved, catalogued, delivered. Which means Pip's name might still open doors in the capital—doors that don't know he's supposed to be dead."

"That name disowned me," Pip said. "That name is the reason my sister is in a cell."

"That name is also the only key we have." Jude squeezed his hand once and let go. "I'm not asking tonight. Tonight we grieve. But think about it. Because I meant what I said on the ridge, and I mean it double now."

She looked north, toward the bells neither of them could hear yet.

"We let them take me. And you're going to be the one who opens the door from the inside."

Above the birches, the smoke from the sanctuary hill had begun to thin, unraveling into the gray morning like a song fading out. Pip watched it go and thought of Maren's hands on his face, steady and warm, and her voice saying *don't look back*—and understood, finally, that she had never meant the burning hill.

She had meant everything behind them.

He turned north, and did not look back again.
