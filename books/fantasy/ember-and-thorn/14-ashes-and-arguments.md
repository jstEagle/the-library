## 14. Ashes and Arguments

The sanctuary hill was still smoking when the sun came up.

Jude stood at the edge of what had been Maren's garden and watched the ash drift like gray snow. It settled on her shoulders, in her hair, on the scorched frame of the gate where the wards had finally failed. Everything smelled of char and wet stone and something sweeter underneath—the burned remains of Maren's herb beds, rosemary and sage cooked into the earth.

Three days since the fire. Three days since she'd run down this hill with Pip's hand crushing hers and One-Eye's teeth in her sleeve, hauling her away while the wards behind them went up like a second dawn. Three days since Maren had turned to face the Hunt alone, blind eyes lifted toward the sound of their boots, and said, in that calm voice that had never once wavered: *Go. I'll hold them as long as I can.*

She'd held them for eleven minutes. One-Eye had counted the bells from the valley below.

Eleven minutes. That was what a life weighed, when you set it against other lives.

"Jude."

Pip's voice behind her. She didn't turn. He came and stood beside her anyway, because he always did, because he was a boy who had learned somewhere—maybe from her—that you didn't let people grieve alone even when they wanted to.

"You haven't slept," he said.

"Neither have you."

"I slept." A pause. "An hour. Maybe."

"That's not sleeping, that's blinking slowly."

Something almost like a laugh escaped him, thin and cracked at the edges. Below them, in the hollowed-out root cellar that had become their camp, the survivors were waking. There were nine of them now. Nine, out of the forty-odd who had lived on this hill under Maren's protection. The rest had scattered in the night—some north toward the coast, some back to cities they'd fled years ago, gambling that the registers hadn't reached them yet. Gambling that hiding would hold.

Jude didn't blame them. She envied them, a little. They still believed running was a plan.

* * *

The argument started at midday, around the cook fire, over a pot of nettle soup that tasted like boiled regret.

It began, as these things did, with Tam.

Tam was a hedge-charmer from the eastern valleys, sixty if he was a day, with hands that shook unless they were working a spell and a granddaughter named Liss who was seven and could make flowers bloom by humming at them. He'd carried the girl three miles through the burning woods on his back. Now he sat across the fire from Jude with his soup untouched and his jaw set, and he said the thing everyone else had been thinking:

"We should go north. To the coast. There are boats."

"There are boats," Jude agreed.

"Fishing villages that don't ask questions. Islands. The Regent's reach thins at the water—we all know it does. We take the children and we go, and we live." His voice cracked on the last word. *Live.* As if it were a spell he was trying to cast and couldn't remember all the words for.

"And then what?" This came from Sera, one of the younger ones—a storm-speaker, nineteen, who'd been at the sanctuary only a season. "We live on an island until Liss is grown, and then what? Until her children are grown? The Regent doesn't age, Tam. It doesn't tire. It doesn't stop. We'd be running forever, and forever is a long time to be afraid."

"Forever alive," Tam snapped, "is better than dead on some palace floor!"

"No one has to be dead on any floor—"

"Tell that to Maren!"

The words dropped into the fire like a stone into still water. Everyone flinched. Sera looked down at her soup. Tam's face crumpled, and he pressed both shaking hands over his mouth, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind moving through the burned trees above them, clicking the black branches together like dry bones.

Jude set down her bowl.

"I'm going to Aurelmark," she said.

Every head came up.

"The capital," Sera said flatly. "The city of bells. The place where they harvest people in public squares and call it registration."

"Yes."

"You're going to walk into it."

"I'm going to walk under it." Jude kept her voice level, though her heart was hammering the way it always did now when she spoke of this—as if her body knew before her mind did what the price would be. "Maren told me what's beneath the throne room. The true king, asleep, bound in chains of woven song. The Regent is nothing but a puppet without its strings cut—it can't be fought, it can't be starved, it can't be outlasted. But it can be unmade. And there's exactly one thing in this world that can unmake it."

"Deep speech," Pip said quietly.

"One person who speaks it." She looked around the fire, at each of their faces. "Me."

Tam laughed, but it was a horrible sound, more grief than humor. "Child. You heard what Maren said about the toll. Every word of deep speech takes something from you. A memory. A piece of yourself. You'd be unraveling your own mind thread by thread to unbind a man most of us have never seen. What kind of trade is that?"

"The only one on offer."

"And what happens when you run out of self halfway through?" Sera asked—not cruelly, just honestly, which was worse. "What if you forget why you're doing it? What if you forget how to finish?"

"Then I'll have paid everything for nothing," Jude said. "But I'd rather spend myself trying than sit on an island spending nothing, waiting for the Hunt to find better boats than ours."

"It's suicide dressed up as a quest," Tam said.

"Maybe." She thought of her mother's face in the harvesting cell—hollowed, gray-lipped, eyes that had looked straight through her. *Alive,* One-Eye insisted. *Kept.* "Maybe. But my mother tried this first, and she didn't fail. She was stopped. Which means the binding can be reached. Which means it can be broken. And every day we don't break it, more people go into those cells. More voices get poured into that thing wearing the crown." Her hand found the wolf's ruff beside her; One-Eye leaned into her palm, warm and heavy as an anchor. "I'm done being quiet. I've been quiet my whole life, and look what it bought us."

Silence again. The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a crow—one of the few birds that had come back after the fire—made a raw comment on the whole proceedings.

Then Tam stood, and gathered his sleeping granddaughter onto his hip, and said, "We go north at first light. I'm sorry, girl. I buried a wife already. I won't bury Liss for someone else's war."

"I know," Jude said. "Go safely, Tam."

He hesitated. Then he crossed the space between them and gripped her shoulder hard, once, with his trembling spell-worker's hand. "Your mother would be proud of you," he said. "And furious. Both at once. That's how you know it's love."

He walked down into the trees, and did not look back.

Sera stayed. So did two others—young Ferren, who could mend broken things with a touch, and old Widow Cass, whose gift was so small she'd never been registered at all: she could tell when milk was about to turn. Four, plus Jude and Pip and One-Eye. Seven souls to march on a kingdom.

"Well," said Widow Cass, ladling herself more soup. "I've survived worse odds. Though admittedly not many."

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold. Jude sat on the ridge above the camp, working a whetstone along a knife she barely knew how to use, and listened to the hill.

That was the thing no one understood about her gift, or maybe about her: she was never really alone. The rats in the cellar walls murmured their small greedy dreams. The crows traded insults overhead. The worms in the burned garden soil turned patiently through the ash, and the oak roots beneath the ridge groaned as they grew, slow as centuries. The whole world talked, if you had ears for it. Even now—even with the wards dead and the hill stripped bare—the land itself whispered. Grief, mostly. The ground remembered Maren too.

One-Eye materialized out of the dusk and lay down against her side, a furnace of fur and scar tissue.

"Say it," she said. "You've been circling me for hours like I'm a wounded deer. Say whatever it is."

The wolf's amber eye caught the last light. When he spoke, it was that voice like gravel and old grief, the one only she could hear. *I knew your mother. I watched her decide to walk into that palace. I am not going to watch you do the same thing badly prepared.*

"So prepare me."

*I intend to.* He shifted, and his shoulder pressed harder against hers. *But hear me first. The deep speech takes memory. Your mother knew that better than anyone, and she went anyway—and do you know what she made me swear?*

Jude's throat tightened. "No."

*She made me swear that if it came to it—if you ever stood where she stood—I would remind you what you're paying with. Not stop you. She was very clear about that part. Stubborn woman. But remind you.* The great head turned, and the eye fixed on her, ancient and unblinking. *So I'm reminding you. Every memory you pour into that binding is gone. Not sleeping. Gone. You could come back from Aurelmark victorious and not know why you went. Not know my name. Not know his.* A flick of his ear toward the camp, toward Pip. *Is the king worth that? Is any king?*

Jude looked out at the darkening valley. Down there, somewhere south, Thornhollow sat behind its closed gates with Bram rotting in a cell—or worse. Somewhere east, Tam and Liss were walking toward the sea. Somewhere beneath the capital, her mother breathed in a cell, hollowed out, kept alive the way you keep a tool.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But I know the Regent isn't going to stop. I know that hiding is just dying slowly with extra steps. And I know—" her voice caught, and she let it, because One-Eye deserved honesty, "—I know that Maren died holding a door so the rest of us could get through it. I'm not going to waste her eleven minutes standing on this hill forever."

The wolf was silent a long time. Wind moved through the burned trees.

*You sound like her,* he said at last. *It terrifies me. Come on. If we're doing this, we do it properly. Sleep tonight. Tomorrow I teach you the servant tunnels—I memorized them thirty years ago, and stone remembers longer than flesh.*

* * *

She was almost asleep when Pip sat down across the embers from her.

He'd been quiet all evening—quieter than usual, which for Pip was saying something. He hugged his knees and stared into the coals, and the firelight moved on his face, and Jude waited, because she'd learned that Pip opened like a lock: you didn't force him, you just held the right key near enough.

"You're really going," he said finally. Not a question.

"Really going."

"To the palace. Where they keep the harvesting cells. Where your mother is. Where the Regent smiles at people with its wrong face." He picked up a twig and broke it. Broke it again. "Alone."

"I'll have One-Eye."

"A wolf." He gestured at the darkness, at the ruined hill, at the whole unfair world. "Against a kingdom."

"Pip—"

"No. No, you don't get to do the noble speech." His voice shook, and he hated that, she could see him hating it, fourteen years old and trying so hard to be steel. "You don't get to decide your life is worth less than everyone else's. That's not bravery, that's just—another way of running. You taught me that. On the marsh road, remember? You said running isn't always feet. Sometimes it's deciding you're the one who doesn't matter."

Jude opened her mouth. Closed it. Because he was right, and they both knew it, and the truth of it sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

"I'm coming with you," Pip said.

"You can't—"

"I'm coming *with you*." Louder now, cracking across the embers. "My father sold my sister to the Hunt. My name is the only reason we got inside Aurelmark's walls last time, and it'll work again—better this time, because I know the streets now, I know the bell schedule, I know which gates the carts use. You need a face they don't suspect. You need someone small enough to fit in the tunnels and quick enough to talk his way past a guard. You need me." He ran out of breath and sat there panting slightly, glaring at her with wet eyes, daring her to argue.

"Pip." She kept her voice gentle. "If I lose pieces of myself down there—if the deep speech takes my memories—someone has to remember. Someone has to know who I was, and tell me, if I come back and don't. Bram's in a cell. One-Eye's a wolf. Maren's dead." She swallowed. "You're the only one left who knows all of me."

"Exactly." He wiped his nose fiercely on his sleeve. "So I'm not letting you go where I can't see you. I'm not losing another family. I lost my sister because I hid. I'm not making that trade twice." He jabbed the broken twig at her like a tiny sword. "Besides. You'll need someone to carry the soup."

Despite everything—despite the ash and the grief and the eleven minutes—a laugh broke out of her, ragged and real. "The soup."

"Palaces have terrible food. Everyone knows this."

She reached across the embers and took his hand, and he gripped back hard enough to hurt, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because some things don't need saying twice.

Above them the stars came out over the burned hill, indifferent and bright. In the morning, Tam and Liss would walk north, and Sera and Ferren and Widow Cass would decide their own roads, and the sanctuary would be empty for good. But tonight the embers held, and the wolf watched the dark, and a boy refused to let go of her hand.

South, then. South to the city of bells, where the Regent wore a stolen crown and smiled its beautiful, wrong smile.

"Get some sleep," Jude told Pip. "Tomorrow we start walking."

And far off, at the edge of hearing, the wind through the dead trees sounded almost like applause.
