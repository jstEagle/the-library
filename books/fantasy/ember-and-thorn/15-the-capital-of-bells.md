## 15. The Capital of Bells

The bells woke Pip before the sun did.

Aurelmark rang them at dawn, noon, dusk, and midnight—fourteen towers, each with its own voice, so that the capital never fell silent. He'd heard about it as a boy, in the nursery at Vessing Hall, when his nurse had described the city as a place where even the poor could tell time by listening. She'd made it sound like music.

It sounded like a cage closing.

He lay on the narrow cot in the room above the Gilded Sparrow and counted the bells until they stopped. Nine. Then he listened to the other sounds: Jude breathing slow and even in the next bed, One-Eye's claws clicking once across the floorboards before settling, and below, the ordinary noise of a city that didn't know what it was yet—the clatter of carts, a fishwife shouting prices, somewhere a child laughing.

*They don't know,* Pip thought. *Or they know and they've decided not to.*

Three days in Aurelmark, and that was the thing that frightened him most. Not the gray-cloaked inquisitors on every corner. Not the registration notices pasted over every wall, their black ink crisp and official, promising safety to all who came forward. It was how *normal* everything looked. How people stepped around the notices without reading them, the way you step around a beggar. How the baker had chatted cheerfully about the weather while a family was escorted from the square by four soldiers, walking calmly, unresisting, as if they were going to see a sight.

"Up." Jude's voice, rough with sleep. "We're moving."

Pip sat up. In the gray light she looked older than her years, which wasn't new, but there was something else now—a stillness in her that hadn't been there on the marsh road. Since Maren died, since the sanctuary burned, she'd gone quiet in a way that reminded him of deep water. Calm on top. Things moving underneath.

"We're doing it today," he said. Not a question.

"Today." She pulled her boots on. "Bram's in the cells under the palace. Every day we wait is a day they—" She stopped. Started again. "Today."

One-Eye rose from the corner, all shadow and scar tissue, his single eye catching the light like a coin at the bottom of a well. *You look terrible,* he said, in that voice like gravel poured over old grief. *Both of you.*

"You look worse," Pip said.

*I'm a wolf. I'm supposed to look like this.* The wolf's lip lifted, which was his version of a smile. *Remember the name?*

Pip swallowed. "Aldous Vessing."

*Aldous Vessing,* One-Eye repeated, tasting it. *Dead three years. Son of Lord Edran Vessing, who sold his own daughter to the Hunt to buy his family's name off the registers. And nobody in this city remembers your face.*

"I was twelve when I ran. I've changed."

*Then let's hope you changed enough.*

---

The plan was simple, which meant it would probably kill them.

Pip walked into the Ministry of Registration at midmorning wearing his dead grandfather's coat, brushed and mended by Jude's careful hands, and carrying a letter sealed with wax he'd stamped himself using a signet ring he'd stolen from his father's desk the night he ran away. Three years ago. A lifetime ago.

The letter said what he and Jude had spent two nights composing: that Aldous Vessing, having learned of his sister Seraphine's enrollment in the crown's service, wished to make formal inquiry after her welfare and present himself for voluntary assessment, being a loyal subject of the crown.

It was a lie built out of true things. His sister *had* been taken—he'd learned that much from a merchant caravan on the marsh road, a gossiping driver who'd mentioned the Vessing girl, pretty one, went north with the second harvest cohort. And Lord Edran Vessing *was* loyal, in the way of men who fed smaller dogs to bigger ones. Pip was simply borrowing his father's most useful quality.

The ministry clerk was a thin man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that never quite settled on anything. He read the letter twice. He looked at Pip. He looked at the seal.

"Vessing," he said. "Of the eastern Vessings?"

"The same."

"Hm." The clerk made a note. "Your sister's cohort went to the palace service. Domestic and ceremonial. Very sought-after placement." Something in his tone suggested he'd said this many times, to many families, and knew exactly what it was worth. "You may submit an inquiry. Response time varies."

"And my assessment?"

The clerk finally looked at him directly. "Assessment is conducted at the palace, upon invitation. Presently." He stamped the letter with a thump. "Inquiry received. You'll be summoned within the week."

Pip walked out into the sunlight feeling like he'd handed someone a knife handle-first.

*Well?* One-Eye's voice found him in the alley behind the ministry, where the wolf waited beneath a pile of sacking that fooled no one.

"They took it. We're inside the machine now—we just have to wait for it to swallow us."

*That's not comforting.*

"It wasn't meant to be."

---

They saw the registration on the fourth day.

Jude heard about it first—from the pigeons, naturally, who had opinions about everything in Aurelmark and no loyalty to anyone. A public registration in Coronet Square, they told her. A big one. Dozens coming forward. There would be a procession.

"Processions mean the Regent," Jude said. They stood at the window of their rented room, looking down at the street where a flower-seller arranged her stall with the unconcerned hands of someone who had done it ten thousand times. "Maren said it never leaves the palace. If it comes out—if it *shows itself*—"

"Then we watch," Pip said. "That's why we're here."

Coronet Square at midday was a held breath. Pip had expected crowds; he hadn't expected the *quality* of the crowd. These weren't the desperate and the frightened, though there were plenty of those, pressed against the barriers with their caps in their hands. These were citizens come out for a spectacle. Families with children on their shoulders. A man selling roasted chestnuts. Somewhere, absurdly, a fiddler.

In the center of the square stood a platform draped in white cloth, and on the platform, a line of chairs. And beside the platform, a cage on wheels—not a cage, Pip corrected himself, a *carriage*, barred and gilded, drawn by six white horses whose eyes rolled white-rimmed with fear. Something was in it. Something the crowd didn't look at directly.

"They're excited," Pip whispered. "Look at them. They're *excited*."

"People are always excited," Jude said quietly. "Right up until it's their turn."

The bells began.

All fourteen towers at once, and the sound was enormous, a wall of bronze that Pip felt in his teeth, in his chest, behind his eyes. Under its cover, the procession entered the square: ranks of soldiers in white-and-gold, then the inquisitors in their gray, then a column of twenty-odd men and women walking freely, unbound, smiling nervously—volunteers, come forward in answer to the notices, come to be registered and protected and kept safe.

Pip watched them climb the platform. A farmwife with dirt still on her knuckles. A boy of maybe fifteen who couldn't stop smoothing his hair. An old man leaning on a stick, helped up the steps by a soldier's courteous hand. They sat in the white chairs. They waited, blinking in the sun, like children at a recital.

And then the carriage doors opened, and the Regent came out.

Pip had prepared himself for a monster. He'd imagined the thing Maren had described: a hollow shape, a puppet, something stitched together out of stolen magic and crowned with a dead king's circlet. He'd braced himself to see wrongness, horror, a mask slipping.

What he saw was beautiful.

The Regent was tall and slender and moved like water finding its level. Its face was the kind of face painters wept over—symmetrical, serene, ageless, framed by dark hair that moved in a wind Pip couldn't feel. It wore white and gold, and the crown sat on its brow as if it had grown there. When it smiled at the crowd, the crowd sighed as one body.

*Don't look at its eyes,* One-Eye breathed. He was pressed flat against Pip's leg beneath a vendor's awning, invisible to everyone but those who knew to look. *Don't look at its eyes.*

Pip looked at its eyes.

They were perfect. That was the wrongness. Not red, not black, not burning—just *perfect*, irises of a brown so warm and deep and evenly colored that no living eye could hold it. Eyes painted by someone who had been told about eyes but had never owned one. As Pip stared, the Regent turned its head—slowly, gracefully—and for one impossible moment its gaze swept the crowd and seemed to catch on the space where a wolf crouched, and pause there, and smile a fraction wider.

Then it turned back to the platform, and raised its hands, and spoke.

Its voice carried to every corner of the square without effort, warm as honey, kind as a grandmother. It welcomed the volunteers. It praised their courage. It promised them honor, purpose, protection. "The gift you carry is not yours alone," it said, and the words fell over the crowd like a blessing. "It belongs to Vaelmoor. And Vaelmoor keeps what belongs to her."

The soldiers brought out a basin of water, silver, wide as a cartwheel. Each volunteer was led to it in turn. Each was asked to place both hands in the water.

The farmwife went first. She dipped her hands, and smiled, and laughed—a small surprised laugh—and then she stopped laughing. Her face went slack. Her knees buckled, and the soldiers caught her, gently, courteously, and laid her on a litter, and carried her away. Her mouth kept smiling. Her eyes had gone somewhere else.

The boy with the fine hair lasted longer. He fought, at the end—two seconds of thrashing, a sound halfway between a word and a scream—before the water took him too.

The old man with the stick didn't fight at all. He looked out at the crowd as they guided his hands down, and he said, clearly, in a voice that carried: "I only wanted to be safe."

Then the water took him.

Nobody in the crowd screamed. Nobody threw anything. A woman near Pip dabbed her eyes and murmured, "Brave souls," to her neighbor, who nodded. The chestnut seller did brisk business. The fiddler struck up something cheerful.

Pip discovered he was gripping the awning post hard enough to splinter the wood. He made himself let go, finger by finger.

*Now you've seen it,* One-Eye said. His voice was very quiet. *Now you know what we're walking into.*

"It eats them," Pip said. His own voice sounded far away. "It doesn't even—it just *pours them out*. Like emptying a cup."

*Yes.*

"And they clap."

*They always clap.* The wolf's eye reflected the square, the platform, the white-clad figure raising its graceful hands to acknowledge the applause. *I have watched this kingdom for a long time, little noble. Men do not need monsters to do evil. They only need to be told it is kindness.*

On the platform, the last volunteer was carried away. The Regent descended the steps, pausing to touch a child's cheek—the child giggled—and crossed to the gilded carriage. At the door it stopped. Turned. Looked out across the square one final time, that painted gaze drifting slow as a searchlight.

It passed over Pip. Passed over the space where Jude stood rigid as a fencepost. Passed over the wolf.

Stopped.

Came back.

For three heartbeats—Pip counted them, because counting was the only thing keeping him upright—the Regent looked directly at the three of them, across forty feet of cheering crowd, and smiled its perfect smile. And in Pip's skull, soft as a lover's whisper, he heard a voice that was not a voice:

*How interesting. New faces.*

Then the carriage doors closed, the horses screamed—actually screamed, six white throats at once—and the procession rolled away toward the palace, taking the harvested volunteers with it, and the crowd began cheerfully to disperse, and the fiddler played on, and somewhere a child asked her mother if they could come again next time.

Jude's hand closed around Pip's wrist. Her grip was shaking.

"It saw us," she said.

"No," Pip said. "No, it—maybe it didn't—"

"*It saw us.*" Her eyes were fixed on the empty platform, the drained silver basin being wheeled away by unconcerned soldiers. "Pip. It looked right at me. And I heard it. Inside my head, I *heard* it."

One-Eye rose slowly from beneath the sacking, and for the first time since the sanctuary burned, the old wolf looked afraid.

*Then we're out of time,* he said. *Because if it marked you today, tomorrow it won't send an invitation.*

*Tomorrow it will send hunters.*

Behind them, the fourteen towers began to ring the noon bells, and the sound rolled over Coronet Square like the lid of a box coming down.
