## 16. What Wears the Crown

The cage-cart smelled of sick and straw and old fear.

Jude sat with her wrists bound in iron-cored rope—real bindings, not the theater she'd planned—and told herself the shaking in her hands was an act. Around her, eleven other prisoners huddled in the dark of the cart as it rattled through Aurelmark's bell-hung streets. A boy no older than Pip who wouldn't stop crying. An old woman with moss-green eyes who hummed to plants that weren't there. A fisherman whose gift, he'd whispered to her in the first hour, was knowing where the storms would turn.

None of them knew her name was a lie. None of them knew that when the inquisitors had swept through the tenement district three nights ago, Jude Amberdale had walked out of her hiding place on purpose, hands raised, voice pitched to the dull flatness of a frightened woman with a small gift and no fight left in her.

*Let them take you,* Maren had said, blind eyes turned toward some middle distance only seers could see. *The palace opens its mouth for the gifted. You have to be swallowed to reach the throat.*

One-Eye had argued until his voice went raw. Pip had cried, which was worse. In the end they'd agreed on the plan because there had never been another one: get taken, get inside, find the vault beneath the throne. One-Eye knew the servant tunnels from another life; Pip's forgotten noble name would open doors in the capital that no smuggler's coin could. They would come for her.

They had to come for her.

The cart stopped. Bells overhead rang the ninth hour, and the sound came down strange and heavy, like the city itself was being struck.

"Out," said a guard. "Eyes down. Speak to no one."

Jude looked down at her boots and obeyed, and hated how easily obedience sat on her shoulders.

* * *

The palace of Aurelmark was beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful.

She saw it in pieces as they marched her through it—the great hall with its ribbed ceiling painted like dawn, the colonnade where fountains sang over pools of water so clear it looked like glass, the staircases of white marble worn soft by centuries of royal feet. Servants moved through it all with their heads bowed and their faces empty, and Jude realized after the third corridor that none of them were looking at anything. Not the art. Not each other. Not the prisoners being marched past in chains.

They're harvested too, she thought. Or afraid to be. There wasn't much difference, here.

The other prisoners stared anyway, drinking in the impossible wealth of the place, and Jude understood that too. If you were going to die in a building, better a beautiful one.

Then the corridors changed. The marble gave way to gray stone. The fountains stopped. Torches replaced windows, and the air took on a taste she recognized from her worst dreams: copper and ozone, the smell of magic being worked hard against its nature.

Down. Always down.

The harvesting cells were cut into the bedrock beneath the east wing, and they were nothing like she'd imagined. She'd imagined torture chambers—irons, racks, screaming. What she found was worse. She found quiet.

Rows of small rooms behind glass, lit by a soft gold light that came from nowhere. Each room held a chair, and each chair held a person, and thin silver threads ran from the base of each person's skull up into the walls, into conduits of pale metal that ran along the ceiling and away into the dark, converging somewhere deeper, somewhere below even this.

The people in the chairs were awake. That was the thing that made her stomach turn over. Their eyes were open. Their chests rose and fell. But no one in those chairs was home.

"The Regent thanks you for your service," said the warden, a broad man with a bored voice, reading from a slate as they were processed in a line. "Gift-bearers of Vaelmoor contribute to the stability and prosperity of the realm. Registration is an honor."

An old man ahead of Jude began to weep, quietly, the way you cry when you've already accepted it. The guards didn't even look at him.

Jude kept her eyes down and did what she'd sworn she wouldn't do: she listened.

Not with her ears. With the other thing, the deep kind, the voice Maren had spent weeks teaching her to hear without drowning in. The cells were full of speech—not human speech, but the low continuous murmur of enchantment, the silver threads singing their work-song as they drew something out of the seated figures and carried it away. It sounded like bees. It sounded like a river taking silt. It sounded, God help her, like contentment, because the spell had been built to feel like rest, to make the giving feel like sleep.

That was the cruelty of it. They hadn't built machines. They'd built lullabies.

*Steady,* she told herself. *Find her first.*

Because that was the other reason she'd let herself be taken. Three years of not knowing. One-Eye's memory of her mother walking through the palace gates with her chin up. *She traded herself,* the wolf had said. *For your name. To keep you off the registers.* And if Lira Amberdale had been brought here—if she'd been sitting in one of these chairs for three years while her daughter learned to speak to wolves—

Jude followed the warden's slate down the row, past cell after cell, letting her deep hearing brush each seated figure the way you'd check a sleeping child for breath. Empty. Empty. A woman who spoke to moths. Empty. A man whose gift was mending broken things, still faintly trying, even now, to mend himself.

Empty. Empty.

And then, in the last cell on the left, a voice answered her before she touched it.

*Who's there?*

Jude stopped walking.

The voice was threadbare. It was frayed down to almost nothing, worn like a coat worn every day for years, but under the wear there was a shape she knew. She had heard that shape across kitchen tables and market stalls and sickbeds. She had heard it scold her and sing to her and say her name in the dark when she was small and afraid of the dark.

She looked up.

The woman in the chair was gray. That was the first thing. Her hair had gone the color of ash, though Jude's mother had been thirty-six when she vanished, and her face had thinned until the bones stood out like branches under skin. Her hands lay open in her lap, palms up, the way you hold them when you've given up holding anything. Silver threads ran from the base of her skull into the wall, humming their lullaby.

But her eyes were open. Gray-green. Watching the door of her cell with an expression that wasn't hope—hope had clearly been starved out of her long ago—but was maybe the scar tissue hope leaves behind.

Jude's legs nearly went out from under her.

"Mama," she said. Or started to say. What came out was barely air.

The warden glanced at her. "You. Keep the line."

"I—" Jude swallowed the name back down her throat, where it burned like a coal. Think. Think like Maren taught you. The deep speech could do more than listen; it could touch, gently, the way you'd tap a stranger's shoulder. She reached out along the floor, along the stone, careful as a thief, and brushed the edge of the silver threads' song.

And her mother's head turned.

Not much. An inch. But it turned toward the door, toward the girl standing in the processing line with her eyes fixed on the floor, and something moved behind the hollowed face—a flicker, a fish under ice.

"You," her mother whispered. Her voice was rust and dust. "I know your voice."

The warden stepped between them. "Next," he said, and took Jude's arm, and pulled her on down the row, and Jude let him, because fighting here meant dying here, and dying here meant her mother stayed in that chair forever.

But as they drew her away, she did the only thing she could. She opened the deep speech wide—all of it, everything Maren had wrung out of her, every lesson paid for in blood and memory—and she poured one word into the stone of the cell, into the walls, into the humming threads themselves:

*Hold on.*

And beneath the lullaby, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, she heard her mother answer.

*Jude?*

* * *

They put her in a holding cell to wait for assessment. Small, dry, doorless in the sense that the doorway simply opened onto a guarded corridor—there was nowhere to run and everyone knew it. Jude sat against the wall and shook, properly now, the act finally falling off her like a wet cloak.

Alive. Her mother was alive.

Three years she'd mourned a body never found. Three years she'd built a shrine out of absence, laid flowers at the river gate on the anniversary, told herself stories—that bandits took her, that she'd run from debts, that she'd died quick. And the truth was that she'd been forty feet underground the whole time, propped in a chair, being drunk through a straw by the thing that wore the crown.

Rage came in slow and cold, which was new. Usually her anger burned fast and stupid. This was different. This was the anger of someone with a plan.

*Assessment* came at dusk—or what she guessed was dusk, measured by the shift change in the corridor. Two wardens and a thin figure in gray robes with a chain of little silver bells, who crouched in front of her and peered into her eyes like a horse trader checking teeth.

"Name?"

"Jessal Vane." The false name sat easy; she'd worn it for two days already.

"Gift?"

"Small." She let her shoulders curl inward. "Animals come calm around me. Dogs mostly. It's nothing."

The assessor smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "It's never nothing. That's rather the point." He pressed two fingers to her temple, and cold flooded in—not painful, just invasive, like fingers rifling through a drawer. Jude thought about Maren's lessons. Thought about walls. She built the wall the old seer had drilled into her, the one that made her gift look shallow, a puddle instead of a well, and felt the assessor's cold probe slide over it and find what she wanted him to find.

"Hm. Minor empathic resonance. Fauna-directed." He withdrew his fingers and made a note. "Low yield. She'll go to the general rows."

"And then?" Jude asked, keeping her voice small.

The assessor looked at her with mild surprise, as if a chair had asked him a question. "And then you'll rest," he said. "Everyone rests here. It's very peaceful. You'll see."

He said it kindly. That was the worst part. He believed it.

They left, and the torchlight in the corridor guttered, and Jude put her forehead against her knees and breathed until the shaking stopped.

*General rows.* Her mother's row. Close enough to reach, once—once—she found the way down to the vault. The plan had been simple in the telling: unbind the king, wake him, and let the true crown break the machine that fed the false one. Wake the king, free the harvesters.

But plans made on hillsides in the clean night air didn't account for the sound her mother's voice made saying *Jude?* like a question asked of a ghost.

She couldn't leave her in that chair. Whatever the order of operations was supposed to be, whatever Maren or One-Eye or anyone else would tell her about priorities and timing and not splitting your strength—she could not walk out of this mountain and leave Lira Amberdale humming in a lullaby while the kingdom got saved around her.

So it would have to be both. The king below, and her mother above. Both, or nothing.

Footsteps again. She looked up, ready to perform smallness.

But the figure in the corridor doorway wasn't a warden. It was tall and gray-cloaked, hood back, and its face was the most beautiful face Jude had ever seen, and the most wrong—too symmetrical, too smooth, like a portrait of a person painted by someone who had only had a person described to them. It smiled. The smile was perfect. Nothing behind it moved.

"There you are," the Regent said, and its voice was warm as honey and empty as a drum. "The little beast-speaker from Thornhollow. Did you really think I wouldn't know my own harvest coming?"

Every lesson, every wall, every trick Maren had ever taught her went white and silent in Jude's head. Because the Regent tilted its head, and its perfect smile widened by exactly one degree, and it said:

"Don't worry. Your mother and I have become such good friends these three years. I'll take you to her myself."

It stepped aside, gesturing graciously down the corridor, toward the cells, toward the rows, toward the dark.

"After you."
