## 17. The Voice Beneath the Throne

The servant tunnels smelled of dust and old tallow, of stone that had not been touched by sunlight in a hundred years. Jude kept one hand on the wall and the other on the hilt of her knife, though she knew—she *knew*—that neither would matter if they were found.

One-Eye moved ahead of her, a gray shape in the dark, his single amber eye catching what little light there was and throwing it back. He did not need light. He had told her so in his voice like gravel dragged over old grief: *The tunnels remember me. I walked them when your mother walked them. They will let us pass.*

She hoped he was right. She hoped it with the whole of her chest, because behind them Pip was holding the door at the top of the stair, and somewhere above them the Regent's palace hummed like a struck bell, full of stolen voices.

*Left here,* One-Eye said. *Mind the third step. It lies.*

"The step lies," Jude muttered. "Of course it does."

*Everything in this place lies,* he said. *That is how it was built.*

They went left. The third step dipped under her boot like something breathing, and she snatched her foot back and stepped over it, heart hammering. The tunnel narrowed, then opened into a low chamber where the ceiling dripped and the walls were carved with figures she didn't want to look at too long—robed shapes with their mouths open, singing, their faces worn smooth by time or by hands.

"Whose faces are those?"

*Singers,* One-Eye said. *From before. When the crown was made, someone had to sing it into being. And when the king was bound beneath it, someone had to sing that too.* His eye turned to her. *It will be you now.*

"I know."

*You do not.* He stopped, turned, and stood before her, close enough that she could feel the heat of him, the wolf-smell of pine and blood and winter. *Jude. Listen to me as you have never listened to any beast. The deep speech takes a toll. Maren told you. You felt it with the charm, with the wards. But this—" he nodded down the tunnel, toward whatever waited below, "—this is not untying a knot. This is unwinding a life. The chains holding the king are woven song, and song can only be answered by song, and the answer must be paid for out of what you are.*

"Maren said the last person who tried was my mother." Her voice came out flat. "And she didn't fail. She was *kept*. So either way, I lose something. At least this way I choose what."

One-Eye held her gaze for a long moment. Then he pressed his scarred forehead against hers, briefly, the way wolves greet their own.

*Then let us go and pay the bill,* he said.

* * *

The tunnel ended at a door that was not a door.

It was a slab of black stone set into the wall with no handle, no hinge, no seam—except that when Jude raised her hand, she heard it. Not with her ears. With the deep part of her, the part that spoke to rats and rivers and the small hot minds of sparrows. The door was singing. A single note, low and patient, held for three hundred years.

"It's a ward," she whispered.

*A listening ward,* One-Eye corrected. *It hears footsteps, heartbeats, lies. It does not hear the deep speech. Nothing the Regent built hears the deep speech, because the Regent cannot bear to be spoken to. It only knows how to take.* His lip lifted from his teeth. *Sing to it.*

Jude closed her eyes. She found the note—not with her throat but somewhere deeper, in the place where her gift lived, the place that had been outlawed since before she was born. She sang back the door's own note, matched it, held it, and then—gently, the way you'd take a bird's egg from a nest—she asked it to open.

The door sighed. Stone ground against stone, and a breath of air came out, cold and clean and impossibly old, smelling of snowmelt and iron.

Beyond it, stairs wound down into blue darkness.

*Three hundred years,* One-Eye said quietly. *He has been down there three hundred years, and no one has come to wake him.*

"Then we're late," Jude said. "Let's not be later."

* * *

The vault was round, and vast, and wrong.

Not wrong the way the palace above was wrong—all gilt and grinning beauty stretched over rot. Wrong the way a wound is wrong. The walls were living rock veined with threads of pale gold, and those threads all ran downward, converging, gathering toward the center of the chamber like roots growing toward a buried heart.

And at the center, on a plinth of the same black stone as the door, lay the king.

He was younger than she'd expected. That was the first thing—the shock of it. In the chapels they showed a gray-bearded effigy, stern and ancient, but the man on the plinth could not have been past forty. Dark hair, shot with early silver. A face that even in sleep looked like it was listening. He wore no crown. The crown hung above him, suspended in the air by nothing, turning slowly, and from its underside ran the threads of gold—hundreds of them, thousands, fine as spider silk, and every thread pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

*Chains of woven song,* One-Eye breathed. *I had forgotten how beautiful they made it look. That is the cruelty of it. It is beautiful.*

"What are the threads?"

*Voices.* His voice dropped to almost nothing. *Every gift-bearer ever taken. Every stolen voice became a thread, and every thread holds the crown, and the crown holds the binding, and the binding holds him under. Your mother's voice is in there, Jude. Somewhere among those threads is the sound of her singing.*

Jude's throat closed. She thought of the harvesting cells above them, the rows of hollowed people with their empty eyes, and understood, suddenly and completely, why the Regent had built it this way. It wasn't just cruelty. It was architecture. Every stolen voice was a brick in the prison. To free the king, she would have to unravel voices—her mother's voice among them—and each one would cost her.

"How do I start?"

*You already know,* One-Eye said. *You opened the door. Now open him.*

She knelt beside the plinth. Up close she could see the king's chest rising and falling—so slowly. One breath a minute, maybe less. Three hundred years of almost-breathing. She reached out and laid her palm flat over his heart, and through her skin she felt it: the deep speech rising in her unbidden, reaching toward him like water finding a crack.

*Careful,* One-Eye murmured. *Ask first. Even sleeping kings deserve to be asked.*

So instead of pushing, she listened. And beneath the weight of the binding, beneath three hundred years of held breath, she heard him. Faint. Frightened. A voice asking, over and over, the same question it had been asking since the day the Regent sealed it over his mouth:

*Is anyone there?*

"Yes," Jude said aloud, and her voice cracked. "Yes. I'm here. My name is Jude Amberdale, and I've come to take the chains off."

The heartbeat under her palm stuttered. Quickened. And somewhere far above them, in the throne room directly overhead, Jude felt the palace notice—a shiver running through its stones, a dissonant chord sounding in every ward from the gates to the gutters.

One-Eye's ears flattened. *It knows.*

"Good," Jude said, and began to sing.

* * *

The first thread was easy.

She found it near the edge of the weave—a thin, frayed thing, an old voice, taken generations ago, worn nearly to silence. She wrapped her own voice around it the way Maren had taught her, felt for the knot, and pulled.

The toll came immediately.

It wasn't pain. That was the horror of it—it wasn't pain at all. It was subtraction. One moment she knew the name of the street she'd grown up on in Thornhollow. The next moment there was a smooth blank space where that knowledge had been, and she couldn't even grieve it properly, because she couldn't remember what she'd lost. She only knew that something was gone, because One-Eye made a low sound in his chest, and because the thread in her hands came loose, drifting upward, dissolving into light.

The crown above the king shuddered. Turned a fraction slower.

"One," she gasped. "One thread."

*There are thousands,* One-Eye said. *But see—they help each other. Look.*

She looked. Where the first thread had come loose, the neighboring threads had slackened, just slightly, just enough. The weave remembered being a weave, and it missed its missing piece. Each thread freed would loosen the next. It would not be thousands of separate battles. It would be one battle, fought thousands of times, each one costing more than the last.

She reached for the second thread.

This one was stronger—younger, taken within the last decade. She recognized the voice inside it somehow, some quality of stubbornness, and she hated that she recognized it, hated that she was about to spend a piece of herself to free a stranger's stolen song. She sang the knot loose. The toll took her grandmother's laugh. Just like that. Gone. She knew she'd had a grandmother; she knew the woman had laughed; the sound itself was a hole shaped exactly like laughter.

She was crying without noticing she was crying. She kept singing.

The third thread cost her the smell of Bram's cooking—the dockside stew he'd made the night before everything fell apart, the night he'd stayed behind at the bridge so she and Pip could run. She clung to that one, fought the toll, tried to pay with something smaller, and the deep speech simply ignored her. It took what it took. The magic was not cruel. It was honest. It weighed her memories against the knots and paid itself from the closest coin.

The fourth. The fifth. The tenth.

By the twentieth thread, sweat was pouring down her face and her knees ached against the stone, and she had begun keeping a list in her head, whispering it between songs like a prayer: *Thornhollow market on a rainy morning. The name of my first teacher. What the sea smells like. Pip's real birthday.* Some things the toll took cleanly. Some things it took and left the shape behind, so she knew there had been a birthday but couldn't find it anywhere in herself, and that was worse than the clean losses.

Somewhere past the thirtieth thread, she realized she was no longer sure what her mother's face looked like.

She stopped. Her hands shook against the king's chest.

"No," she whispered. "No, not that one. Take anything else. Take—"

*Jude.* One-Eye's muzzle pressed against her shoulder, warm and heavy. *Look at the thread you're working. Look whose voice is in it.*

She looked. The thread in her hands was thick and strong and recent, and when she listened to it—really listened, with the deep part of her—she heard it. A woman's voice, worn thin but unmistakably alive, singing inside the chain like a bird in a cage. Singing, all these years, to keep from going mad. And the song it was singing, over and over, soft and stubborn—

It was a lullaby. The lullaby from the edge of her memory, the one she'd been humming her whole life without knowing where it came from.

Her mother's voice. Bound into the crown. Holding the king down.

Jude understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling: the Regent hadn't just harvested her mother. It had made her mother a load-bearing wall. Every thread Jude cut brought the whole weave closer to collapse—and her mother's thread was near the center, near the keystone, and cutting it might bring everything down at once, might free the king in a rush of unbound magic that could crush everyone in the vault, might—

Might also be the only way.

Above them, the palace was screaming now. She could feel it through the stone—boots, bells, wards flaring, the Regent's attention bending downward like a searchlight. They had minutes. Maybe less.

*Choose,* One-Eye said gently. *That is what the deep speech asks. Not strength. Choice.*

Jude wiped her face with the back of her wrist. She looked at the sleeping king—at the question still written on his listening face, *is anyone there*—and she thought of her mother in the harvesting cell upstairs, hollowed and breathing, waiting for a daughter who was about to forget her face in order to save her.

"Yes," she said. "Someone's here."

She gathered her voice, wrapped it around her mother's thread, and pulled.

* * *

The toll came for her childhood.

Not all of it. Not at once. It started at the edges—the name of the lane she'd lived on, gone. The color of her bedroom wall, gone. Her father's voice reading aloud on a winter night, gone, gone, gone—and she screamed as it went, screamed and kept singing, because stopping meant the toll had been for nothing, meant her mother's thread stayed bound, meant the king slept another three hundred years while the Regent wore his face above.

Thread after thread after thread. The weave was collapsing now, threads sloughing off in cascades, the crown spinning wild above the king, and the vault filled with light and with sound—with *voices*, hundreds of them, thousands, freed and rising, and among them, suddenly clear, suddenly close, her mother's lullaby soaring up out of the ruin of the chains like a bird out of a burning house.

The last knot held. The keystone. The oldest thread of all, spun from the first voice the Regent ever stole—three hundred years old, dense as iron, and it would not move for anything she offered. She offered and offered and the toll drank and drank and she felt herself thinning, felt whole years going soft and gray at the borders of her mind, and the knot did not care.

*Jude.* One-Eye's voice, very quiet. *You don't have enough left. Not alone.*

"Then help me!"

*I am a beast, little singer. My voice is not woven into that chain. Only human voices bind human kings.* A pause. *But there is another voice in this room. Sleeping. Listening. Asking if anyone is there.*

She understood.

The king himself. His own voice, held beneath the binding, could answer the last knot—if she could reach it, if she could carry her song down past three hundred years of held breath and give him back one word, one note, one thread of his own self to pull with.

She bent until her lips nearly touched his ear. She poured everything she had left—not skill anymore, not technique, just raw self, the last unspent coin of who she was—into a single question, sung in the deep speech:

*Who are you?*

And the king, three hundred years asleep, answered.

His eyes opened.

They were gray, and furious, and awake, and the sound that came out of him was not a word but a chord—the true royal voice, the voice the crown had been made to amplify, and it struck the final knot like a hammer and the keystone thread *shattered*, and the crown fell.

It did not hit the floor. It came apart in the air, thread by thread, voice by voice, a storm of liberated song filling the vault, and through it Jude heard—faintly, fading, as the last of her childhood drained away to pay the debt—her mother's lullaby, complete at last, unbound, rising.

Then the light died, and the vault went dark, and the king sat up.

* * *

For a moment, nobody moved.

The king swung his legs off the plinth like a man waking from a nap he resents, which would have been funny in any other circumstance, and looked at his own hands, flexing them, as if checking whether they still belonged to him. Then he looked at Jude.

"You're bleeding," he said. His voice was rough with disuse. "From the nose."

Jude touched her upper lip. Blood. She stared at it without comprehension, the way you'd stare at rain falling upward.

"I don't remember my mother's face," she said. It seemed important to say it out loud. "I remember that I have one. I remember that I love her. I don't remember her face."

The king was quiet for a moment. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Get up. We have to—"

The vault shook.

Dust sifted from the ceiling. From the tunnel behind them came a sound Jude had heard once before, at the sanctuary, the night it burned: the sound of wards dying in sequence, each one a small note cut short, moving closer.

*He is coming,* One-Eye said. His hackles were up, his teeth bared at the dark tunnel. *The Regent is coming down. It felt every thread break. It felt *him* wake. It is afraid, Jude. For the first time in three hundred years, the thing wearing the crown is afraid.*

The king rose to his feet, unsteady, one hand braced on the plinth. Three hundred years of sleep, and he stood anyway, swaying like a ship in a storm, and looked toward the tunnel with an expression Jude would remember for the rest of her life—which, given the state of her memory, might not be long, but would certainly be vivid.

"Then it's finally come to collect," he said. "How many years has it worn my face?"

"Twenty," Jude said. "Maybe more. It calls itself the Regent."

"The Regent." He tested the word, spat it out. "It never had the imagination to be anything else." He straightened. Whatever frailty had been in him a moment ago was gone, burned away by something older and harder. "Girl. Singer. What's your name?"

"Jude Amberdale."

"Amberdale." Something flickered across his face—recognition, grief, guilt, all at once. "Your mother sang to me. All these years, through the chains. Did you know that? Hers was the only voice that ever sounded kind."

Jude's chest seized. "Is she—"

"She's alive. The binding fed on her, but it couldn't kill her. Killing the voice kills the thread." He turned toward the tunnel, toward the approaching dark. "Which means the Regent will want her dead the moment it realizes the binding is broken. Along with everyone in those cells."

The shaking grew worse. Somewhere above, a section of ceiling groaned.

"We have to get to her," Jude said. "We have to get to all of them. Pip's up there. Bram—"

The tunnel mouth lit up.

Not torchlight. Not lamplight. A cold, sourceless radiance, and standing in it, framed by the black stone doorway, beautiful and wrong and smiling, was the Regent.

It looked at the empty plinth. It looked at the ruined crown, scattered across the floor in threads of dying gold. It looked at the king—its king, its prisoner, its mask—and its smile did not waver, but something behind its eyes flickered, quick and reptilian, like a calculation arriving at a number it did not like.

"Well," the Regent said pleasantly. "Look who's awake."
