## 18. The Regent's Offer

Pip had never run so fast in his life, and he had once fled his father's house with nothing but a stolen signet ring and the clothes he stood up in.

The servant tunnels beneath the palace were a maze of brick and dripping dark, and One-Eye had led them through them at a dead sprint, the wolf's voice rumbling out of him like a landslide—*left, left, down, the old way, the way she came*—and Pip had not asked who *she* was because there was no time to ask anything. Behind them, above them, all through the bones of Aurelmark, the palace was screaming.

Not the people. The building itself.

He felt it in his teeth first, then in his chest: a grinding, harmonic wrongness, like a thousand bells being struck at once and none of them agreeing on a note. The harvested voices. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, ripped from gift-bearers across Vaelmoor and stored somewhere in this glittering corpse of a kingdom—and now they were waking, because Jude was waking something else.

*Hold the line,* One-Eye had said, which was a strange thing for a wolf to say, and stranger still that Pip understood him. But three months on the marsh road will teach you a language faster than any tutor, and Pip had learned to hear the wolf's speech the way you learn a fever: suddenly, and all at once, and never quite the same afterward.

Now they burst through a rusted door into a gallery above the throne room, and Pip looked down, and forgot how to breathe.

The vault beneath the throne had opened like a wound. Chains of woven song—Maren had described them once, her blind eyes turned toward some middle distance, her voice soft with old horror—rose from the dark in luminous strands, silver-gold and thrumming, and they were breaking. Snapping one by one, each snap a sound like a name being spoken for the last time. And in the center of it, kneeling in a circle of guttering candlelight with her palms flat against the sleeping king's chest, was Jude.

She was singing.

Not with her mouth. With whatever she had that was deeper than a mouth. Pip could feel it pressing against the inside of his skull, patient and vast, a voice made of roots and rivers and the long memory of beasts. The chains were listening to her. The chains were *answering* her.

And standing at the edge of the vault, beautiful and wrong and smiling, was the Regent.

Pip had seen it before, from a balcony in the capital of bells—a glimpse of gold and grace amid the harvesting ceremony, a face that made crowds weep with love. Up close it was worse. It wore the shape of a man the way a glove wears a hand; there was nothing inside the silhouette but light bent into the suggestion of one. Its smile did not move its eyes, because its eyes were not eyes. They were apertures. And through them Pip could see something turning, slow and cold, like weather seen from very far away.

"Jude Amberdale," the Regent said, and its voice was every voice at once—the harvested ones, layered and harmonized into something almost lovely. "You are spending yourself. Do you feel it? Piece by piece. Memory by memory. You are paying for a man you have never met with the only currency that is truly yours."

Jude didn't stop singing. Sweat ran down her temple. Her lips were moving around words that weren't words.

"I can give it back," the Regent said. It drifted closer, robes whispering against stone though its feet did not seem to touch the floor. "All of it. Every memory you have already fed to the unbinding. And more." It raised one perfect hand, and the air above the vault bloomed.

Visions. That was the only word Pip had for what unfolded in the air like smoke given color. He saw a kingdom at peace—Vaelmoor as it might be, green and golden, roads full of merchants and children, no hunts, no gray cloaks, no registers. He saw Thornhollow's market bright with bunting. He saw Bram—alive, whole, laughing over ale in some sunlit yard. He saw a cottage with smoke rising from its chimney and a woman in the doorway with Jude's same dark hair, whole and warm and *hollowed no longer*, calling someone home to supper.

He saw himself, too. Pip of House Vessant, restored, his sister beside him, his father's estate green behind them, everything forgiven, everything mended.

"There is no need for any of this," the Regent said gently. "The king is dust that dreams it is a man. I have kept this kingdom breathing for nine years while he slept. No famines. No wars. Order. You want your mother back—I am the only thing in this world holding the thread of her. Stop the unbinding, and I will unmake her hollowing with a thought. Stop, and the boy goes free, the knight goes free, the wolf goes free. Stop, Jude Amberdale, and you may simply *live*."

And Pip saw it happen. Saw the vision take root in Jude's face. Her song faltered—one thread of it, just one—and the nearest chain stopped unraveling and began, horribly, to *reweave*.

"No," Pip whispered.

Then louder. Then with his whole chest, his whole stupid noble-born lungs, his whole self:

"*JUDE!*"

Her head snapped up. Their eyes met across the vault, across the drifting visions, across the Regent's smile.

"Don't listen to it!" He was climbing down before he knew he'd decided to, hands and boots finding holds in the ancient stonework, the wolf snarling somewhere above him. "It's lying! It always lies! You told me—it showed me my sister too, on the road, remember? It shows everyone what they want—"

"It does not lie," the Regent said, and its many-voiced calm did not waver. It turned those aperture eyes on Pip, and Pip felt them go through him like winter through a shuttered window. "Ah. The runaway. The boy who was sold." Something in the visions shifted; his sister's face grew sharper, closer, her voice almost audible. "I could return her to you as well, child. Both of you. A family reassembled. All I ask—"

"No." Pip's voice cracked and he hated it and he kept going anyway. "No. You don't get to bargain with me. You don't get to offer *me* like I'm yours to give."

He dropped the last few feet and landed hard, stumbling, and put himself between Jude and the Regent—small, shaking, absurd, a boy with a stolen knife against a thing made of stolen voices.

"You want to know why your visions don't work?" he said. His hands were trembling. He let them. "Because I've been bought before. My own father priced me and sold me to save his own skin, and I swore on the marsh road that nobody would ever do that again—not to me, and not *for* me. So take your peace and your green fields and shove them. Jude doesn't stop. Not for me. *Especially* not for me."

"Pip—" Jude's voice was ragged, half-dissolved in the deep speech.

"If you stop," he said, "if you trade us for her, then she wakes up in a world where her daughter is still hiding, where people are still being harvested, where the only reason she's whole is because a monster decided to be generous. That's not saving her. That's just a prettier cage." His throat closed. He pushed through it. "You taught me that. On the marsh road. You said—we don't leave people in cages. Even gilded ones. *Even ours.*"

The Regent watched him with those faraway weather-eyes, and for the first time, something in its smile flickered.

"Sentiment," it said. "Fragile. Finite. It will not hold a kingdom together."

"It held *her* together," Pip said, jerking his chin at Jude. "Through a hunt, and a burned sanctuary, and a dead seer, and a mother in a cell. It'll hold."

And then One-Eye came down the wall like a falling star.

The wolf hit the vault floor between the Regent and Jude, hackles up, one eye blazing, and his voice when it came out was not gravel anymore but bedrock, ancient and furious: "*She does not bargain. She pays. There is a difference, hollow thing, and you are about to learn it.*"

The Regent's head tilted. And Pip would swear, for one heartbeat, that behind the beauty, behind the borrowed harmony of a thousand stolen voices, something flinched.

"You," it said softly. "The last of the old pack. I thought I had unmade all of you."

"You unmade the pack," One-Eye growled. "Not the promise."

Above them, the next chain snapped. The sound rang through the vault like a struck bell, and the Regent's composure cracked along with it—its form rippling, the beautiful face sliding for an instant into something raw and threaded and *hungry*, a lattice of light stretched over an absence.

"So be it," it said, and the harmony in its voice tore open into discord. "Then I will spend what I have hoarded. Every voice. Every gift. Everything this kingdom gave me to keep it safe from itself."

The air went white.

Pip threw himself down beside Jude, grabbed her shoulder. "Tell me what to do!"

"Hold onto me," she gasped, and her eyes were streaming, and she was smiling, actually smiling, blood at the corner of her mouth. "Whatever happens—don't let go."

The Regent raised both arms, and the walls of the vault filled with faces—hundreds of them, flickering in the stone like fish under ice, the harvested, the taken, the kept. The true king stirred beneath Jude's hands. The chains sang their final verse.

And the Regent spoke the first word of the end of the world.

* * *

It caught Pip in the chest like a wave.

Not sound—pressure. The Regent's hoarded voices came off it in a tide, a flood of stolen magic with nowhere left to go, and the vault became a storm of light and screaming harmony. Pip pressed his forehead to Jude's shoulder and held on, and through the roar he heard her answer.

Not louder than the storm. *Deeper* under it. Her deep speech rose through the floor, through the mountain itself, out into the night beyond the palace walls—and the night answered.

He felt it come. A tremor in the stones, then a gallop, then a thunder. Through the high vents of the vault poured the city: rats in rivers, dogs in packs, hawks off the bell towers, the great carthorses breaking their traces in the streets above, pigeons in clouds, foxes down from the hills, every creature within reach of her voice rising and pouring toward the palace like a tide answering the moon. The hounds of the Hunt arrived first and did not attack—they *turned*, wheeling on their gray-cloaked handlers with a new light in their eyes.

The Regent staggered. For the first time, it looked afraid.

"This is impossible," it said, and its many voices were fraying, coming apart into their separate griefs. "They are only animals. They are only *animals*—"

"They're *mine*," Jude said, and stood.

She rose out of the circle of candles with the king's hand in hers, and her eyes were not her own eyes—they were older, amber-shot, lit from underneath by everything wild that had ever answered a call. The last chain shattered. From the opened vault below, wrapped in the tatters of nine years of enchanted sleep, the true king of Vaelmoor drew his first free breath.

And the Regent screamed with a thousand throats, and lunged—

* * *

What happened next, Pip would spend the rest of his life trying to describe, and never quite manage.

The king met it standing. He was gaunt and gray-bearded and half-blind with sleep, and he moved like a man made of iron remembering it was iron, and when the Regent's tide of stolen voices broke against him, he *sang back*—a low, worn, human note, the plain strong voice of a man who had ruled by listening, and the storm faltered against it.

But the storm was too much even for him. Pip saw it—the king driven back a step, two, the Regent's light crawling over him like frost claiming glass. Jude threw herself into the gap, deep speech pouring out of her, and One-Eye hit the Regent's flank like a battering ram, and still it was not enough, still the hoarded magic of hundreds of stolen lives pressed them down, down toward the stone—

Until Jude stopped fighting the tide, and started *unbinding* it.

Pip felt the cost of it like a blade going past his ribs. She reached into the Regent's hoard with that root-and-river voice and began, thread by thread, to speak each stolen voice *free*—not destroying the harvest but undoing it, releasing gift after gift after gift back into the dark, and each freed voice left the Regent smaller. Dimmer. Thinner. The beautiful face collapsing inward like a mask losing the face beneath it.

"No," the Regent said—fewer voices now, dozens, then a handful. "No—you cannot—you will *unravel*—"

"That's the idea," Jude said.

The last of the hoard tore loose in a single blinding rush, and the Regent stood revealed: a lattice of pale threads stretched over nothing at all, a shape of woven light around an absence, thrashing, whistling, thin as smoke.

The king raised his hand. Jude raised hers. And together—old worn iron and wild amber fire—they took hold of the final thread.

Pip closed his eyes. He heard it part with a sound like a sigh, like a register closing, like a name spoken gently for the last time.

When he opened them, the vault was quiet. Candles guttering. Dust settling. The Regent was gone—unraveled to nothing, not even ash. In its place, drifting down through the ruined air like snow, fell small lights: freed gifts returning to the world, seeking, perhaps, the people they'd been taken from. Or new bearers. Or no one. Pip would wonder about that for years.

Jude swayed. Pip caught her.

"Hey," he said. "Hey. You're okay. We won. You're okay."

She looked at him and smiled—and for a moment, just a moment, her eyes searched his face like she was trying to place him. Like he was a kind stranger.

"Pip," she said slowly, tasting the name. "Your name is Pip."

Something cold slid down his spine. "Jude. It's me. It's Pip. We—"

"The toll," One-Eye said quietly. The old wolf limped to her side, pressed his scarred head against her hip, and his voice was gentle now, gentler than Pip had ever heard it. "The unbinding takes what the speaker feeds it. She paid more than she meant to, little lord. She paid deep."

Jude blinked at them both, and laughed—a little lost, a little wondering. "Why does everyone look so sad? We *won*, didn't we?" She frowned, pressing fingers to her temple. "There's something... there was something I was supposed to remember. Someone. Someone with my face. Down here, or—no. Below. Below the throne, there's—"

She stopped. Went white.

"Cells," she whispered. "There were cells. I came down here for someone. Pip—who did I come here for?"

And above them, through the broken ceiling of the vault, boots were thundering down the stair—gray cloaks, drawn steel, the Hunt arriving late to a war already lost—and Pip gripped her hand hard and said the only true thing he had left:

"Come with me. I'll remind you who you are."
