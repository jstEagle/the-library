## 19. Ember and Thorn

The last chain of song snapped like a harp string breaking, and the sound went through Jude's chest and out the other side.

She was kneeling in the dark beneath the throne room, both hands pressed flat against the sleeping king's breastbone, and she could no longer remember the name of the street she'd grown up on. She could not remember the smell of Bram's stew. She had paid those already—fed them into the unbinding like coins into a merchant's palm—and the vault had swallowed them without comment. What remained of her felt thin as paper, but the king beneath her hands was *waking*, and that was worth every memory she'd poured away.

His eyes opened. They were gray, the gray of river stones, and they found her face with an expression she couldn't read—gratitude or grief or something older than either.

"You paid," he said. His voice was rust and honey. "Child, you paid too much."

"Not enough," Jude said. "Not yet."

Above them, through fathoms of stone, the bells of Aurelmark began to scream.

* * *

They came up through the servant tunnels with One-Eye loping ahead, and by the time they reached the antechamber behind the throne room, the screaming had resolved into something worse: silence. The Regent had stopped the bells. In a city of bells, that silence was a shout.

The throne room doors stood open. Of course they did. The Regent wanted them open.

It stood at the far end of the hall, before the empty throne, and it was beautiful in the way a blade is beautiful—flawless, symmetrical, lit from within by a soft gold light that did not flicker. Its face wore the smile Jude had seen from the crowd in the registration square, the smile that never reached anywhere because there was nowhere for it to reach. Around its feet, the harvested magic of hundreds of stolen gift-bearers coiled like smoke made solid, threads of stolen voice and sight and song woven into a cloak that moved when there was no wind.

"Jude Amberdale," the Regent said, and its voice was every voice at once—a market crier, a child, her mother, a stranger, all layered and wrong. "You woke him. How industrious."

Behind her, the true king of Vaelmoor drew himself up. He looked smaller than a king should look—thin from three years of enchanted sleep, dressed in the rotted remnants of court finery—but when he spoke, the stones themselves seemed to lean in to listen.

"You are not crown," he said. "You are a *leech* wearing one."

"I am continuity," the Regent said pleasantly. "I am peace. Ask them." It gestured, and the smoke-cloak around its feet shivered, and from within it came sounds—faint, threadlike, human. Voices. Hundreds of them, murmuring, weeping, singing fragments of songs they'd loved. The harvested gifts, still alive inside the thing, still aware.

Jude's stomach turned over. She knew some of those voices. Not names—but *textures*. The deep speech had taught her to hear magic the way you hear a friend's footsteps in another room, and these were footsteps she recognized. Gift-bearers. Taken. Fed into this thing for years.

"There is a place for your mother among my threads," the Regent said to her, gentle as a nurse. "There is a place for the boy. For the wolf. Stop this, and I will weave them somewhere soft. A kingdom at peace, Jude. No more hiding. No more burning sanctuaries. No more fear. Only order, and quiet, and everyone in their proper place in the pattern."

"It's lying," the king said quietly.

"Yes," Jude said. But her voice cracked on it, because part of her—the part that had watched Maren die holding wards alone, the part that had left Bram in inquisitor hands—wanted so badly to believe there was a version of this where nobody else got hurt.

The Regent smiled wider. "Then let me show you what refusal costs."

It raised one hand, and the cloak of voices rose with it.

* * *

What came at them was not an army. It was worse. It was *everyone*.

The stolen magic unfolded across the throne room like a wave of light and sound, and each thread became something—a shape of force, a construct of pure harvested power. A woman's gift of flame became a serpent of fire that filled half the hall. A man's gift of stone became fists the size of carthorses punching up through the marble floor. A child's gift—the deep speech itself, taken from some small terrified kid in a cellar somewhere—became a shrieking wind that hit Jude like a wall and threw her across the dais steps.

She rolled, tasted blood, heard the king shouting words in a language that made the air go taut and bright. He was fighting. Three years asleep and he was fighting, unraveling constructs with gestures that bent reality like wet clay—but there were too many. There were *hundreds*. Every gift-bearer taken since the Regent had replaced the king, all of it pouring out of that beautiful hollow thing at once.

One-Eye hit the fire-serpent's flank and was thrown twenty feet into a pillar. He rose limping, growling low, and his one eye found hers across the chaos.

*Little speaker,* he said, gravel and old grief. *You cannot fight a hundred voices with two.*

*I know,* Jude said.

*Then stop trying.*

She understood.

She stopped fighting the threads. She closed her eyes—which was insane, mid-battle, with stone fists falling—and she opened herself the way Maren had taught her, the way that had nearly killed her in the sanctuary, and she *listened*.

The throne room was full of voices. Stolen ones, screaming inside their constructs. And beneath them, fainter, the other voices—the real ones, the free ones. The rats in the palace walls who had crept close to hear her speak their language. The pigeons roosting in the rafters of the great hall. The palace cats, aloof and curious. The horses in the royal stables three wings away. The dogs of the city beyond the walls, the gulls on the river, the mice in the granaries, the crows on every rooftop in Aurelmark.

All of them listening. All of them had been listening since she entered the city, because word travels fast in the beast-world when a speaker walks through it, and what travels faster is *hope*.

Jude Amberdale opened her mouth and used the forbidden gift—not to bargain, not to whisper, but to *call*.

**"RISE."**

It wasn't a word so much as a bell struck inside every animal skull in the capital. It went out from her like a shockwave, through stone and wall and street, and the answer came back like a flood.

The rats came first, because rats always come first—pouring from the walls in a gray tide, swarming the Regent's constructs, finding the seams where harvested magic met physical form and *chewing*. Magic resists magic; teeth do not care. The pigeons fell from the rafters in a storm of wings, blinding the stone-fists, fouling the fire-serpent's burning eyes. The cats came down the stairwells yowling like demons. From the stables, horses broke their stalls and thundered into the hall through shattering glass, and the guards in the courtyards fled screaming from a river of dogs flowing over the palace gates—mastiffs and mongrels and sheepdogs, hundreds of them, answering a call no human could hear.

The throne room became a storm of fur and feather and fury.

And through it all, Jude walked toward the Regent, speaking constantly now, her voice layered over itself in the deep speech—commanding, pleading, *unbinding*. Because she could hear it now, clearly, under the chaos: the threads. Each stolen voice was knotted into the Regent's core, and knots could be spoken loose. Maren had taught her that. Maren had died teaching her that.

She put her hand into the smoke-cloak and spoke to the first knot she found.

*You are not his,* she told it. *You were never his. Come home.*

The thread shuddered—and unraveled. Somewhere in the hall, a construct collapsed into ordinary light, and a sound like a sigh went through the room.

The Regent screamed with a hundred mouths.

*Come home,* Jude said to the next knot, and the next, her voice raw, her head splitting, memories flickering at the edges of her mind like paper near flame—the deep speech taking its toll even now, taking and taking—*come home, come home, come home—*

Threads unraveled. Constructs fell. The king fought beside her, tearing the great ones apart, and One-Eye guarded her back with his broken body, and Pip—she caught one glimpse of Pip in the doorway, white-faced, hurling a candelabra at a stone fist with the courage of pure desperation—

And the Regent, losing threads by the dozen, losing *itself*, did the only thing left to it.

It grabbed the deepest knot in its cloak—the oldest one, the strongest, the voice Jude had known the texture of since childhood—and held it up between them like a shield.

Her mother's voice.

"Unravel this," the Regent said, gasping now, fraying, its beauty cracking like porcelain, "and you unravel *her*."

The battle seemed to hold its breath. Even the animals stilled.

Jude stood with her hand inches from her mother's thread, and the whole war narrowed to that single point of light.

* * *

She thought of Maren saying, *the deep speech takes something from the speaker each time.* She thought of the toll she'd already paid in the vault, the missing rooms in the house of her memory. She thought of her mother's face, hollowed in the harvesting cell, and how she had sworn—sworn on Bram's capture, on Maren's ashes, on everything—that she would get her back.

The Regent smiled its cracked smile. "Choose, Jude Amberdale."

"No," Jude said.

And she did the thing the Regent could not predict, because the Regent had never had a family, had never been anything but theft wearing a smile: she chose *both*.

She didn't cut the knot. She didn't spare it. She *spoke* to it—to her mother's stolen voice, directly, past the Regent's grip, in the deep speech at its very deepest, spending memories she could feel leaving her like breath in winter, her childhood going, her mother's face going, *going*—

*Mama. It's Jude. Come home.*

The thread went still.

Then it *moved*—not unraveling but *pulling*, hauling itself free of the Regent's core with the strength of three years of stolen love, and the Regent shrieked as its anchor-thread tore loose, and every remaining thread in the cloak felt that tear and began, all at once, to pull toward the sound of Jude's calling voice—

The king raised his hand. "Now, child!"

"Together!" she cried, and the king's ancient unbinding-magic met her deep speech in the space above the dais, and the Regent of Vaelmoor—hollow, beautiful, wrong—came apart.

Thread by thread by thread.

Each freed voice rose like a spark, hung a moment in the smoky air of the ruined throne room, and then flew—out the shattered windows, into the city, home to bodies waiting in cells and hospitals and harvesting halls, and whether they would wake, whether they would know themselves again, Jude could not know. She only knew she had called them home, and they had come.

The last spark to fly was warm gold, and it brushed her cheek like a hand before it went.

When it was over, Jude Amberdale sat down hard on the steps of the throne she had never wanted, in a hall full of feathers and fur and silence, and discovered she could not remember her mother's face.

But she could remember the voice. The thread had passed close enough to leave that.

"Mama," she whispered, to no one, to everyone. "I'm coming to find you."

Beside her, One-Eye leaned his ruined body against hers, and somewhere in the wreckage of the doorway, Pip started to cry and laugh at once.

Above the palace, through the broken windows, the bells of Aurelmark began—hesitantly, then all together—to ring.
