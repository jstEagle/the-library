## 20. The Unmaking

The king woke screaming.

Not a man's scream—a sound like a bell cracked open, like a cathedral of song collapsing in on itself. The chains of woven melody that had bound him for years snapped one by one, each note shrieking free, and Aldric Vaelmoor, true king of Vaelmoor, came up off the cold stone of the vault with his eyes blazing gold and his hands already shaped for war.

Jude staggered back. Her head was full of holes.

She could feel them—gaps where memories had been, smooth-edged absences like missing teeth. She'd paid three already to get this far: the smell of Bram's stew, the name of the street she'd grown up on, the color of the sky the day her father died. The toll of deep speech, and she had barely begun.

Above them, through miles of stone and marble, the Regent screamed too.

* * *

They came up out of the vault into chaos.

The throne room was burning—not with fire but with light, harvested magic bleeding from the Regent's opened veins of power, pouring down through the palace in rivers of stolen voice. Servants fled. Guards dropped their weapons and ran, or knelt, or simply stood weeping, feeling for the first time in years the weight lift off minds they hadn't known were weighted.

And at the center of it all, on the steps before the throne, stood the Regent.

Beautiful. Wrong. Smiling.

Its face was perfect the way a mask is perfect, and its body shimmered faintly, as if seen through heat-haze, because there was nothing under the shimmer. No flesh. No bones. Just thread—Jude could see it now, with Maren's lessons singing in her blood and the deep speech loose in her throat. The Regent was woven. Thousands upon thousands of threads, silver and black, each one a stolen gift, a harvested voice, a person fed into the crown's hunger.

"Little speaker," the Regent said. Its voice was every voice at once, a choir flattened into one note. "You woke him. Do you know what you've done?"

"I know exactly what I've done." Jude's voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm going to finish it."

The Regent sighed, almost fond. "You are one girl with a borrowed language and half your mind already spent."

"I'm one girl," Jude said, "with every creature in this city behind me."

She opened her mouth and spoke to the capital of Aurelmark.

It was not words. It was deeper than words—the deep speech poured out of her like floodwater, and the city answered. Rats poured from the sewers in silver rivers. Pigeons fell off rooftops in clouds. Dogs slipped their chains, horses shattered their stalls, crows came down like a black storm, and beneath it all, older things stirred—things in the river, things under the hill, things that had waited generations for someone to give them permission.

The Regent's smile flickered.

The king moved.

Aldric Vaelmoor fought the way mountains would fight if mountains could hate. He had no gift—or rather, his gift had been eaten first, decades ago, which was how the Regent had taken him—but he had twenty years of rage banked in his body, and he went through the Regent's summoned guards like a landslide. Jude saw him take a halberd meant for his neck and break it, and the man holding it, and keep moving.

"Together!" he roared at her. "Speaker! It cannot be killed—it must be *unmade*, thread by thread, and I cannot hear the threads!"

That was her part, then.

She closed her eyes and listened.

The Regent was not silent. Nothing woven is silent. Every thread sang its own small grief—a milk-seller's charm for rain, a child's knack for finding lost things, a hedge-witch's warming cantrip, hundreds of voices, thousands, all of them crying in keys only she could hear. And threaded through them, binding them, the Regent's own spine: a single black cord of pure command, old and cold, older than the false king it wore.

*There,* said One-Eye's voice, gravel and grief, somewhere at the edge of the battle where the wolf fought like ten wolves. *The black cord. Pull it and the weave dies.*

"It will take everything," Jude said aloud, without opening her eyes. She knew it the way you know the weight of a wave before it breaks. "Every memory I have left. Maybe more."

*Then spend it well,* One-Eye said. *Your mother spent less for more.*

Jude began to unmake the Regent.

* * *

It was like unpicking a knot with her bare teeth.

Each thread she seized in the deep speech had to be *unbound*—not cut, cutting would shatter the gift inside and kill whatever fragment of its owner still lived within, but gently, patiently unwoven, the way Maren had taught her, the way you calm a trapped animal. And each unbinding took its toll. Not coin this time. Not strength.

Memories.

She paid the taste of Pip's terrible cooking. She paid the sound of Bram laughing so hard he snorted. She paid the name of the inn where they'd hidden after the bridge burned, the feel of One-Eye's fur under her hand the first night, the words of the lullaby her mother used to hum while mending.

The Regent fought her the whole way. Threads whipped and struck; harvested magic slammed against her mind in waves of static and screaming. Blood ran from her nose. From her ears. She kept pulling threads.

Around her, the battle turned. The king carved a circle of clear stone around her, guarding the small blind girl with the bleeding face who stood unmoving in the center of a hurricane. Crows harried the Regent's summoned soldiers. Rats chewed through the harnesses of the inquisitor hounds until the hounds, confused and freed, sat down and refused to bite anyone. Pip appeared out of nowhere—of course he did, of course he came—and threw himself into shielding her left side, shouting at her, begging her to stop, and she loved him so much it hurt, and she paid that ache too, spent it like a coin, because the deep speech took everything and she was nearly empty now.

Thread by thread by thread.

The Regent was unraveling. Its beautiful face sagged on one side. Its choir-voice fractured into individual cries—dozens of voices now, then hundreds, all the stolen gifts crying out as they came free, little ghosts of magic dispersing into the air like sparks off a bonfire.

"You do not understand what you unmake," the Regent said, and now its voice was thin, fraying, almost human. Almost pitiful. "I held the kingdom together. Without me there is famine, there is war, there is—"

"There is *truth*," Jude said, and pulled the last great handful of threads.

And reached for the black cord.

She had nothing left to pay with. She knew that. She reached anyway, and the deep speech took without asking—took her childhood entire, the whole sunlit country of being small, the market stalls and the rooftop nights and her mother's face—

Her mother's face.

She felt it go. Felt it lift out of her like a bird off her open palm, and she grasped at it, *no, not that, anything but that*, but the deep speech does not bargain, and the black cord slid free of the Regent's spine with a sound like the world exhaling.

The Regent came apart.

Not violently. That was the terrible thing. It simply loosened, all at once, thousands of gray threads drifting apart in the air like ash from a burnt page, and the light that had filled the throne room drained away, and the silence afterward was the deepest silence Jude had ever heard.

She was still standing when her knees gave out.

* * *

Someone caught her. Strong arms, chain-mail biting cold through her sleeve—the king himself, lowering her to the marble like she was made of glass.

"Speaker," he said. "Look at me. What did it cost? What did it cost *you*?"

Jude tried to answer and found she didn't know. She looked up at his face—she knew him, didn't she? The sleeping king, the quest, the vault. She knew the shape of the story. But when she searched for the warm particulars, the people who should be standing around her, she found names with no faces attached. *Pip.* Someone called Pip. *Bram.* *One-Eye.* A mother.

*A mother.*

"She's here," Jude heard herself say. "My mother's here. In the cells below. We have to—someone has to bring her up. I don't—" Her voice cracked. "I don't remember her face."

The king's expression broke into something ancient and kind. "Then let her come to you," he said, "and be known again."

They brought her mother up from the harvesting cells on a litter, hollow-eyed and gray-skinned, barely breathing, a woman emptied so thoroughly that even her own gift had gone quiet in her. They laid her beside Jude on the marble floor of the ruined throne room, among drifting threads and crow feathers and the smell of scorched song.

And Lira Amberdale opened her eyes.

She looked at her daughter. Jude looked back—at a stranger's face, worn and lovely and utterly unknown, a woman she had crossed a kingdom and unmade a monster to reach, and could not recognize.

But her mother knew *her*. Something moved behind the hollow eyes—recognition, wonder, grief, all arriving at once like birds coming home to roost.

"Jude," her mother whispered. "Oh—oh, my *bird*. Look how you've grown."

And Jude, who had forgotten her mother's face even as her mother woke beside her, found that some things run deeper than memory. She did not know this woman. She did not need to. She reached out and took her hand anyway.

"I don't remember you," Jude said honestly, tears cutting clean lines through the blood on her cheeks. "But I'm told I never stopped looking for you. So—we'll start there."

Somewhere behind her, a boy with noble manners and dockside swagger was crying openly. Somewhere above, bells began to ring—all the bells of Aurelmark at once, wild and unsanctioned and joyful, because word was spreading through the palace like fire through dry grass: *the king lives, the Regent is dead, the Hunt is over.*

One-Eye limped to her side and pressed his scarred head against her shoulder, and though she had lost the memory of the first time she'd touched him, the wolf's warmth was new every time, and new was enough.

"The registers," the king said quietly, rising, turning to face the hall of weeping courtiers and freed servants. "Bring me the registers. Every name. Tonight we burn them."

Jude leaned against her wolf, held her stranger-mother's hand, and watched the first morning of a kingdom without the Hunt begin to dawn through the shattered windows.

She just couldn't remember why she'd ever been afraid of bells.
