## 21. What Remains of Jude Amberdale

Pip counted the bells.

He'd learned to count them in Aurelmark, when the sound of the city had been a threat—every toll a reminder that the Regent was listening, that the Hunt was moving, that the world was made of cages. Now, three days after the unmaking, the bells of the capital rang for a different reason. They rang for joy. They rang because the true king of Vaelmoor had walked out onto his own balcony, gaunt and gray and blinking at the sun like a man surfacing from deep water, and the first thing he had said was: *Stop.*

Stop the Hunt. Stop the registers. Stop the carts that rolled north with their cargo of stolen voices.

The word had gone out on fast horses and faster pigeons, and the kingdom had obeyed, because it turned out that all those years of fear had been built on nothing but a hollow thing wearing a crown, and hollow things shatter easily once you know where to strike.

Pip sat on the steps of the palace's eastern colonnade and counted the bells and tried to feel the joy everyone else seemed to be finding so easily.

It wasn't working.

* * *

They'd put Jude in a room with tall windows and a bed too soft for her, because that was what palaces did—they wrapped everything in velvet until you couldn't tell comfort from captivity. Pip had argued for the barracks instead. He'd lost. Kings, even newly woken ones, were stubborn about gratitude.

So now he climbed the stairs for the fourth time that day, carrying bread and honey and a cup of the bitter tea Bram swore by, and he paused outside her door the way he always paused now, gathering himself, rehearsing a smile.

Inside, she was sitting by the window. She was always by the window. She'd been a girl who couldn't sit still—she'd fidgeted through every lesson Maren ever gave, she'd talked with her hands, she'd once climbed a sanctuary roof just to argue with a crow about the weather. This Jude sat like folded laundry. Still. Quiet. Watching the city below with eyes that belonged to someone older.

"Rats again?" she asked without turning.

"How'd you know?"

"You walk different when you've been talking to them. Looser. Like you're embarrassed."

Pip set down the tray. "I'm never embarrassed. I'm a nobleman's son. Embarrassment is beneath me."

"That's what I keep hearing." She turned then, and smiled, and it was almost the right smile—the crooked one, the one that pulled harder on the left side. Almost. He'd spent three days learning the difference between *almost* and *right*, and it was a education he would have given anything to skip.

"The king wants to see you," Pip said. "Afternoon. He says there are things only you can decide."

"Like what?"

"Like what happens to the gifted. The registers are burned, but the names in people's heads aren't. Half the kingdom still flinches at their neighbors. He says—" Pip hesitated. "He says you paid more than anyone for this, so you should get a say in what it becomes."

Jude looked at her hands. There was a mark on her palm now, faint as an old scar, where the deep speech had finally gone quiet—a spiral, like a snail shell, like the rings inside a tree. When she slept, sometimes, her lips moved around words no one could hear.

"I don't remember deciding anything," she said. "That's the joke of it. Everyone keeps telling me what I did. What I sacrificed. Like reading a letter from a stranger." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I know I had a mother. I know it the way you know a fact in a book. I can't—I look for her face and there's just a door where it should be."

Pip sat down across from her. Close enough to touch. Not touching, because they'd learned that lesson too—when she startled, she startled hard, some animal part of her still fighting a battle that was already over.

"Bram's coming up," he said. "And One-Eye. We thought—we thought maybe we'd do it together. Telling you things. About before. In case you wanted to know."

"In case I want to know who I am," Jude said. "Yes. Imagine that."

There it was. The edge under the calm. Pip clung to it like a handhold. A Jude with edges was still, somewhere, a Jude.

* * *

Bram filled the doorway the way he always had—all shoulders and old scars and a beard he'd been growing since Thornhollow, gray coming in at the edges now. Three days of freedom hadn't softened him; if anything, the cells had carved him sharper. He moved like a man relearning how much space he was allowed to take up, and he'd decided the answer was *all of it*.

One-Eye came in low behind him, gray muzzle first, and settled at the foot of Jude's bed with the particular gravity of a wolf who has decided a vigil is not yet over.

"There she is," Bram said, gruff, and then visibly wrestled with what came next, because Bram had never once in his life been good at tenderness. "You look terrible."

"Thank you," Jude said dryly. "I've been told I have my mother's looks."

The silence that followed had teeth. Bram winced. One-Eye's ear flicked.

"Too soon?" Bram asked.

"It's been three days," Pip said. "Everything is too soon. Everything is also too late. It's very confusing."

But Jude laughed—a short, surprised bark of a laugh—and Pip felt something in his chest unclench for the first time since the throne room. Since the light. Since the moment the last thread of the Regent came apart and Jude Amberdale swayed on her feet and said, in a voice scraped down to bare wood: *I can't remember her name. Why can't I remember her name?*

"Sit," she said now, gesturing at the floor, because the chairs in palace rooms were decorative and everyone knew it. Bram sat. Pip sat. One-Eye didn't bother pretending—he simply flowed closer until his shoulder pressed warm against her knee, and Jude's hand came down into his fur, automatic, and stopped there, and her brow creased.

"I know your fur," she said slowly. "I don't know why I know your fur."

"You knew me before you could talk," One-Eye rumbled. His voice was gravel and old grief, same as the day he'd appeared at her door in Thornhollow, except now Pip understood the grief in it, having seen what lived underneath the palace. "Your mother carried you to the northern woods when you were small. You fell asleep against my flank and drooled on me. I have never forgiven you."

"That sounds like me," Jude said, and there was hunger in it, plain and awful. "Tell me more. Tell me everything. Tell me the stupid parts especially."

So they did.

Bram told her about the docks, how she'd bargained with gulls to find the best fishing spots and split the profits with a boy who owed money to the wrong people. How she'd once talked a dockmaster's mastiff out of biting him by explaining, reasonably, that Bram tasted terrible. How she'd taken in a runaway brat off the streets because he looked hungry and lied badly about where he came from.

"That was you," Jude said, looking at Pip.

"That was me."

"What did I say? Exactly. Word for word. I want the embarrassing version."

"You said, 'You eat like a squirrel preparing for a war.'" Pip grinned despite everything. "And then you fed me anyway. And then you wouldn't stop feeding me. For two years. I gained half a stone in the first month and you acted like you'd personally won a battle."

"I like her," Jude said quietly. "This person you're describing. I wish I'd met her."

The room went still. One-Eye pressed harder against her knee.

"You'll meet her," Bram said. Rough voice, gentle meaning. "She's in there. Memory's not the only thing a person's made of. You still bargain before breakfast—you did it with the kitchen cat yesterday, I watched. You still stand between people and trouble like it's a job you applied for. You still lie about being fine." He ticked them off on scarred fingers. "The girl's still here, Jude. She's just missing some furniture."

"Furniture," Jude repeated.

"Rooms look strange empty," Bram said with a shrug. "Doesn't mean the house is gone."

* * *

In the afternoon, they took her to the king.

Aldric Vaelmoor had been asleep beneath his own throne for eleven years, and it showed. He was thin as winter. His hands shook unless he gripped something. But his eyes were awake in a way that the Regent's had never been—they were tired eyes, kind eyes, eyes that had watched the whole horror through some thin membrane of dreaming and could not forgive themselves for sleeping through it.

He received them in a small room, not a grand one. Pip suspected that was deliberate. The king stood when Jude entered, which made the courtiers gasp, and bowed his head, which made them gasp louder.

"Jude Amberdale," he said. "I owe you a debt the treasury cannot pay."

"Don't bow to me," Jude said, uncomfortable. "I'm a charm seller from Thornhollow."

"You're the woman who unmade the thing that ate my kingdom while I dreamed." The king's voice roughened. "I heard you. Down there, in the dark. All those years, I heard pieces—songs woven into my chains, voices passing above—and near the end, I heard yours. Clearer than any of them. Like someone calling down a well." He looked at her marked palm. "What did it cost you?"

"Memories," she said flatly. "Paid by the handful. I don't remember my childhood. My mother. The years in Thornhollow before Bram. It's—" Her jaw worked. "It's a fair price. I keep telling myself it's a fair price. I got a kingdom. She got eleven years in a cell."

"No," the king said quietly. "It is not a fair price. Nothing about any of this was fair. That is rather the point of what comes next." He straightened, and though his body was ruined, something in him stood up tall. "The Hunt is ended. I signed the order this morning. Every register burned, every cell opened, every gift-bearer freed and named innocent. But laws are paper, Lady Amberdale. Fear is older than paper. The gifted will need a voice at my side—not a prisoner's advocate. An advisor. Someone who knows what the gift costs, and what it's worth, and what it means to hide it." He paused. "Someone the beasts already trust, if the reports from the city are true. The pigeons of Aurelmark have apparently declared a holiday in your honor."

Despite herself, Jude laughed. "They would."

"Think on it. No hurry. I have waited eleven years; I can wait a week." The king's gaze went briefly to the window, northward, toward the hills. "And there is another matter. The woman they kept in the harvesting cells. Your mother. The healers say her body will mend. The rest—" He spread his shaking hands. "The Regent drank deep from her for a long time. What remains may be little. But she is asking for you. Has been asking since she woke. She doesn't understand why you haven't come."

Pip saw Jude go white.

"She doesn't understand," Jude whispered. "She's lying in a bed waiting for her daughter, and her daughter walks in and doesn't know her face." Her breath caught. "I can't. If I go in there and she looks at me like I'm supposed to be someone—if she says my name and I just stare—"

"Then you stare," Bram said simply. "And then you stay anyway. That's what family does. Trust me. I'm an expert on families that start broken."

Jude looked at him. At Pip. At the great gray wolf who had risen to stand at her side like a shadow given weight.

"When?" she asked the king.

"She's awake now."

* * *

The healing wing smelled of clean linen and yarrow smoke. Pip walked beside Jude and felt her trembling through the air between them, and did the only thing he could think of: he talked.

"The first time I met your mother—no, wait, I never met her, that's the thing, isn't it? I only know her from your stories. From the way you used to talk about her when you thought I was asleep." He kept his voice low and steady. "You said she sang to the bees. You said she could make a frightened horse calm just by humming. You said she went to the palace to protect you, and you hated her for it and loved her for it, both at once, and you carried that both-at-once thing around like a stone in your boot for three years."

"Stop," Jude said.

He stopped.

They stood outside a door. Through it, faintly, came a sound. Singing—thin, cracked, barely a thread of a voice, but unmistakably a lullaby. A melody shaped for bees and frightened horses.

Jude's hand found the doorframe and gripped it.

"I don't know her," she said. "Pip. I want you to hear me say it plainly, so nobody pretends later. I don't know her. I will walk through that door and see a stranger."

"I know," Pip said.

"And it might break me."

"It might," Pip agreed. "But you broke worse things recently. You've got practice."

That startled the laugh out of her again, wet and ragged, and maybe that was the point. She pushed the door open.

The woman in the bed was small. That was Pip's first thought—that the mother of the woman who had unmade the Regent was small and gray and worn nearly smooth, hands folded on the blanket, hair loose and lank, cheeks hollowed by years of harvested magic. Her eyes found the doorway immediately. Found Jude immediately. And lit up—gods, they *lit up*, the whole ruined face rearranging itself around a joy so fierce it hurt to witness.

"There you are," the woman whispered. "There you are. I knew you'd come. I told them. I told the healers, my daughter comes for me, she always comes, she has her father's stubbornness and my—"

Her voice faltered. Because Jude had stopped in the middle of the room, and her face was doing something complicated—searching, reaching, grasping at shapes in fog—and whatever she found or didn't find showed plainly enough that the woman in the bed understood.

The joy didn't die. Pip watched it happen and the joy did not die. It changed. It bent, the way a tree bends in wind that should snap it, and held.

"Oh," her mother said softly. "Oh, sweetheart. What did it cost?"

"Everything," Jude said, and her voice cracked clean through. "I paid everything. I don't remember you. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, I don't—there's a door where you should be and I can't open it—"

"Hush." The woman held out her hands. "Hush, come here. Come here, child."

And Jude crossed the room like a drowning woman, and her mother gathered her in, small arms around the woman who had saved a kingdom, and over Jude's bent head her eyes met Pip's—wet, fierce, unbroken.

"You'll do," she murmured, to Jude, to the room, to fate itself. "We'll do. I spent three years in the dark telling myself stories about you. I can spend a few more teaching you the true ones." Her hand moved in Jude's hair, slow, certain. "Besides. You drooled on a wolf when you were four. Some things a mother never forgets, even when everything else is taken. So we'll trade. Piece by piece. I'll give you back what I can carry."

One-Eye padded forward and laid his head on the edge of the bed, and the lullaby started again, cracked and thin and alive.

Pip stood in the doorway with Bram's heavy hand landing on his shoulder, and let himself cry, quietly, the way you cry when the worst is over and the hardest part is just beginning.

Outside, the bells rang on. And far to the north, on a burned hill where wards had died and children needed somewhere safe to learn, the first stones of something new were waiting to be laid.
