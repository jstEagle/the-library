## 22. A Family, Chosen

The bell over the schoolhouse door was a gift from the crows.

Jude had asked them for a chime that would carry across the whole sanctuary hill, and three days later a crow named Sorrow—because that's what he'd called himself when she finally got him to speak properly—dropped a cracked bronze bell at her feet and looked insufferably pleased with himself. She'd hung it above the door with braided rope, and now, every morning when the sun cleared the eastern ridge, she pulled the cord twice and listened to it ring out over the terraces where the sanctuary was slowly becoming a village again.

Two pulls meant class. One pull meant danger. The children had learned the difference fast. Children always did.

She stood in the doorway now, watching them come up the hill path through the late-summer grass. There were eleven of them this season—the first class of openly gifted children in Vaelmoor since before the Hunt. Eleven small people who no longer had to flinch when their gifts stirred. A girl who spoke to moths and could tell you what the weather would do three days out. Twin boys who could put any animal to sleep with a hummed note, which made them either the most useful or most dangerous children in the kingdom depending on who you asked. A solemn little boy named Wren who heard what the stones remembered, and who had told Jude, on his first day, that the foundation stone of the old schoolhouse still held the echo of Maren's voice, singing wards into it forty years ago.

Jude had gone out that night and laid her palm against the stone until dawn. She hadn't told anyone why.

"Teacher." Wren arrived first, as he always did, because he liked to be early enough to hear the building settle. "The floor's happy today."

"The floor's happy," Jude agreed gravely. "Tell it we appreciate that."

Behind him came the others, a ragged parade of patched coats and mismatched boots, carrying slates and apples and, in one case, a very indignant hen that the moth-girl, Tansy, insisted was her study partner. The hen had opinions about arithmetic. Most of them were wrong. Class proceeded anyway.

This was the work now. This was what remained after everything: chalk dust and small voices and the slow, patient business of teaching children that the thing inside them was not a curse to be hidden or a resource to be harvested but simply *theirs*—a language, a way of listening, a door that opened both ways if you weren't careful.

She taught them the rules first. She taught them the cost second. She never let them forget either.

*Deep speech takes something from the speaker.* Maren's voice, in memory, calm as still water. *Every time you open the deep door, you pay a toll. So you ask yourself, before you speak: is this worth a piece of me?*

Most of the time, for most of them, the answer was no. That was the lesson. The gift was not a sword. It was a voice, and voices were for saying true things, not loud ones.

But once—only once—Jude took the oldest of them, a scarred girl named Bree whose gift was so quiet even she had doubted she had one, out past the tree line where the hill met the wild country. Bree could speak to growing things. Not command them. Just... greet them.

"I want to show you something," Jude said. "And then I want you to decide if you ever want to do it. There's no shame in deciding no."

Bree nodded, chin up, the way Pip did when he was scared and refusing to admit it.

Jude knelt in the moss and pressed both hands flat against the earth and opened the deep door.

The toll came, as it always did. Something small slipped loose from her—a memory this time, she realized afterward: the smell of the particular bread a baker on Candlemaker Row used to make, gone now, just gone, like a coin dropped down a well. She paid it without flinching. She was practiced at paying.

And then she said, in the speech beneath all speeches, *Grow.*

The clearing answered. Grass surged green around her knees. The birch saplings at the edge of the trees straightened and stretched, leaves unfurling like hands opening. Wildflowers she had never planted broke through the soil in a ring around them both, blooming out of season, out of order, a crown of color laid down at her feet by something that had been waiting years for someone to say hello.

Bree made a sound Jude had never heard from her—not a word, not a cry, just a small raw noise of recognition, like a lock turning in a door the girl had thought was painted shut.

"That's yours," Jude said quietly, as the bloom settled and the world went back to being merely beautiful instead of miraculous. "Not mine. I just knocked. You're the one who lives here."

Bree cried. Jude let her. Then they walked back down the hill side by side, and neither of them mentioned it again, because some things are too large for talking and too good for hiding, and you just carry them.

* * *

Her mother came to the fence on Thursdays.

That was the arrangement, unspoken and then spoken and then simply understood. Lira Amberdale—though she didn't remember being Lira Amberdale, not really, not yet—would walk up from the healer's cottage where she lived with two other hollowed survivors of the harvesting cells, women the king's physicians said might mend in a year or might mend never, and she would stand at the low fence around the schoolyard and watch the class through the afternoon.

She never came closer. She never needed to. The children knew who she was. Children always did.

"She's your mother," Tansy had said once, matter-of-fact, while the hen glared at fractions. "Why doesn't she remember you?"

"Because she paid too much," Jude had said. "For me. For everyone. And what she paid hasn't come back yet."

"But it will?"

Jude had thought about the vault beneath the throne room, about chains of woven song, about the price of unbinding. She thought about the hole in herself where her childhood used to be—the missing rooms, the missing years, the mother-shaped absence that she now navigated by faith instead of memory. She thought about how her mother sometimes looked at her across the fence with a faint crease between her brows, like a person trying to recall a word that sat just behind the tongue.

"It will," Jude said. "Or we'll build something new next to the empty space. Either way, we won't leave it empty."

So on Thursdays, her mother stood at the fence, and Jude taught, and when class ended she walked over and leaned on the wood beside her, and they talked the way strangers talk when they suspect they might have been friends in another life.

Today her mother was watching Bree practice greeting the schoolyard oak, her palm flat on the bark, her lips moving in the small careful words Jude had taught her. The oak shivered though there was no wind.

"She's good," her mother said. "Better than you were."

Jude went very still. "What?"

Her mother frowned, the crease deepening, chasing something. "I don't know why I said that. I don't—" Her hand rose to her temple. "There was a garden. You were small. You talked to the beans in the garden and the beans grew crooked because you kept making them laugh."

The world tilted gently, like a boat catching a swell.

"You remember that?"

"No." Her mother's voice was steady, puzzled, honest. "But my mouth does. It came out before I knew I was going to say it." She turned and looked at Jude then, really looked, and for a moment the crease smoothed and something older surfaced in her eyes—something that recognized without remembering. "You have her face. I keep thinking that. Whoever I was, I must have loved looking at you."

Jude's throat closed. She gripped the fence rail and breathed through it, the way Bram had taught her—*in for four, hold for four, out for four, soldier*—because Bram was alive, Bram was free, Bram had walked out of the palace cells thin as a winter branch and furious about it, and every day he was alive was a day she refused to waste on crying.

"Her name was Lira," Jude managed. "Yours, I mean. When you find it again."

"Lira," her mother repeated, tasting it. Then, softer, testing a door: "And yours? I know the children say it. But I want to say it myself."

"Jude."

"Jude." A pause. The wind moved the grass. Somewhere down the slope, One-Eye's voice rumbled through the earth, low and constant, the old wolf teaching the twins' hound patience, or complaining about the weather, or simply being present in the way only a creature who had carried grief for thirty years could be present. "Jude Amberdale."

"Yes," Jude said. "That's me."

"Almost," her mother said. And smiled—the first real smile, unguarded, unconfused—and for half a heartbeat Jude saw her, *saw her*, the woman from the missing rooms, the woman from the garden with the laughing beans. "Almost. Give me time. I'm relearning my daughter's name."

Then the crease returned, and the moment folded itself away like a letter, and her mother said, "Is it Thursday already?" and they began again, patiently, the way you begin anything worth doing.

* * *

Pip found her at dusk on the western terrace, where the sanctuary's new wall was going up—stone by stone, carried by volunteers and, on alternating days, by a very smug team of mules whom Jude had negotiated a generous oat contract with.

He came up the path with his coat collar turned up against the evening chill, nineteen now and taller than her, still carrying himself like a boy who expected doors to be locked and had plans for getting through them anyway. His sister's letters came monthly now, from the estate where she'd been reclaimed and restored; he read each one three times and pretended he read them once.

"You missed supper," he said.

"I was talking to the wall."

"How's the wall?"

"Grateful. Overworked. Thinks we should move the gate twenty feet east."

"See, this is why the kingdom fears you." He sat down beside her on the half-built course of stone, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was its own kind of language—one they'd been speaking since a burning bridge and a marsh road and a cell block where he'd stood in front of her and told the Regent, in a voice that shook and did not break, *you don't get to trade in people I love.*

Below them the sanctuary spread out in the blue hour: lamplight kindling window by window, the healers' cottages, the long hall where Bram was loudly losing an argument about crop rotation to a farmer who had once been an inquisitor and now grew turnips with a vengeance. On the ridge, a gray shape sat silhouetted against the last light, keeping the watch he kept every night, though there was nothing left to watch for. Old habits. Old griefs. Old wolves.

"Pip," Jude said. "Thank you. For finding me up here. You always know."

"You always come here when you're counting what you lost." He shrugged. "I count with you. That's the job."

They sat awhile. Down in the village, someone was singing—an actual song, not a ward, not a working, just a person singing because the evening was fine and no one would take them for it.

"Do you ever think about what it cost?" Pip asked. "All of it. What you paid under the throne. Do you sit up here and add it up?"

"Sometimes."

"And?"

"And I remember everything except being a child." She said it plainly now; the wound had calloused into fact. "I don't remember her face from before. I don't remember Thornhollow's streets from a kid's height. I don't remember learning to speak to rats—I just know that I always could. It's like a house where someone took the ground floor and left the rest standing." She turned her hand over, looked at it. "Maren told me the toll would be fair. She never said fair wouldn't hurt."

"And was it worth it?"

Jude watched the lights below. The schoolhouse. The fence where her mother stood on Thursdays. The hall where Bram argued about turnips. The ridge where a one-eyed wolf guarded a family he'd once tried not to love.

"A king woke up," she said. "A hunt ended. My mother breathes. You're here. Ask me again in fifty years, but yes. Yes, it was worth it."

Pip nodded slowly. Then he did the thing he did, the thing he'd been doing since the marshes, the thing that had started as a joke and hardened into truth somewhere along the drowned road: he counted them off on his fingers.

"Bram's yelling about turnips. One-Eye's brooding on the ridge. Your mother's relearning your name one Thursday at a time. Tansy's hen has escaped again, probably into the granary. And you're up here talking to walls." He looked at her sideways, that crooked grin that had survived a noble house that sold its own daughter, a war, and a hollow god wearing a crown. "Family's all accounted for."

No one argued. No one ever argued anymore. It had stopped being a claim and become simply a fact, the way the hill was a fact, the way the bell was a fact—something built by hand and choice and stubbornness until it rang true.

"Family," Jude agreed.

From the ridge, One-Eye's voice rolled down through the dark earth, gravel and old grief worn smooth into something almost like contentment: *Heard that. About time.*

From the hall, Bram's bellow: *If anyone's family, I demand a recount!*

From the granary, the hen, inexplicably, screamed.

Jude laughed—really laughed, the kind that cost nothing and gave everything back—and leaned her head on Pip's shoulder, and the first stars came out over the sanctuary hill, over the schoolhouse where tomorrow eleven children would learn that their voices were theirs, over the fence where a mother was slowly, patiently, word by word, relearning her daughter's name.

The bell hung silent above the door, waiting for morning.

Two pulls meant class.

And class, like everything else they had chosen and rebuilt and refused to surrender, would come.
