## 1. Ink That Should Not Glow

The ink was the first thing Gideon Amberdale had ever loved, and it would very likely be the thing that killed her.

She thought this not with despair but with the mild, detached amusement of a woman who had made her peace with the arithmetic of her life. It was a quarter past the seventh bell, and the Scriptorium of Ashen Vale smelled the way it always did on market mornings: tallow smoke, cold stone, and the iron-bitter tang of oak gall ink. Outside the tall, slotted windows, the city went about its business under the banner of a king who had no right to it—Vessic's black boar on a field of ash-grey, hanging limp in windless air from every public post. Inside, Gideon sat at her desk in the third row of the copying hall, bent over a ledger she was forging.

Not forging, precisely. *Correcting.* The distinction mattered to her, though she kept it folded away where no one could find it, the way she kept everything else. The tithe-rolls for the Weavers' Quarter had come in wrong—wrong by design, since Vessic's assessors had doubled the grain levy and then recorded only half of what they'd taken, pocketing the difference. Brannoc, the master of the Scriptorium, had looked at the rolls that morning with his one good eye and said nothing at all, which was his way of saying everything. So Gideon corrected them. She had a gift for numbers that matched hands to pages, for making a lie look like it had been written by an honest man with tired wrists. It was, she sometimes reflected, the most honest dishonesty in Ashen Vale: she stole from thieves and gave back to bakers.

Her official work sat to her left—a fair copy of a psalm-cycle for the shrine of Saint Ellowen, each letter drawn with the patient reverence the temple paid for. Her other work sat to her right, beneath a false bottom in the desk's drawer: forty-one small charms, wrapped in grey linen, waiting for the evening market.

Each one was a blessing. That was what she told herself, and mostly it was even true. A knot-charm for a fisherman's wife whose husband worked the weirs above the mill-race. A fever-line drawn on birch bark for the cooper's daughter. Small magics, quiet magics, the kind that warmed a bed or steadied a hand or turned a fever's edge. Nothing that flared. Nothing that sang. Gideon had spent eleven years learning how to make magic so modest that even magic itself might overlook it.

Because magic, in Ashen Vale, was death. Not immediately—Vessic's regime preferred its cruelties administrative at first. A name entered into the Roll of the Gifted. A summons. An interview at the Bastion House from which no one returned looking the same, if they returned at all. Then the cart, or the pyre, depending on the season and the mood of the witch-hunters. The silver paid for denunciation was generous: two crowns per name, four if the accused was convicted. In a city where a loaf cost a copper and winter was coming, four crowns bought a great deal of silence from neighbors who had once been friends.

Gideon blew gently across the wet ink of the tithe-roll and let her mind do what it always did in the quiet hours: count exits. The Scriptorium had three. The river gate was watched since midsummer. The coal stair behind the scriptorium was narrow and unwatched but ended in the tanners' yard, which meant emerging smelling like a corpse, which meant being memorable, and memorable was fatal. The third exit was through the archive stacks, past the reliquary, down into the old crypt where the founders were buried—and beyond that, supposedly, nowhere. But Gideon had found the nowhere. There was a door in the crypt wall, older than the temple built around it, and it opened onto the storm-drains, and the storm-drains opened onto the river below the weir. She had never used it. She checked it monthly anyway, the way a sailor checks a lifeboat he prays never to need.

"Gideon." Brannoc's voice, low, from the end of the row. He didn't look up from his own desk. He never looked up; it was part of their grammar. "Customer for you. Shrine-side."

Her stomach performed a small, controlled drop. Shrine-side customers were the risky ones—the ones who'd heard something, half-heard something, from someone whose cousin had been helped once. She rose, smoothed her scribe's smock, and became, with the ease of long practice, exactly what she appeared to be: a temple functionary, pale from indoor work, competent, forgettable.

* * *

The customer was waiting in the side chapel off the cloister, where petitioners were received among donated candles and the painted stare of Saint Ellowen. He was a carter by his hands and his smell—horse, road dust, axle grease—and he held his cap in both fists like a man in church who wasn't sure God was home.

"Mistress scribe." He bobbed his head. "They said—you're the one does the blessings. Proper ones."

"I copy prayers," Gideon said mildly. "The shrine sells votive ribbons. Two coppers."

He shifted. His eyes went to hers and away again, quick as a fish. "It's my boy. Eleven. Fits. The physicians bled him twice and it's worse after. My wife got a ribbon off you at Lammas—" He stopped, hearing himself, and went grey. "I mean. Off the shrine."

Lammas. Gideon remembered it: a hot day, a crowd, a woman pressing a copper into her palm at the candle-stall while Gideon palmed her a folded slip of birch bark with a fever-line drawn so fine it looked like decoration. She remembered thinking, even then, *too many eyes, too fast, why did I—* Because the woman had looked at her the way this man looked at her now, with that terrible open hope, and hope was the one currency Gideon had never learned to refuse.

"The shrine takes petitions," she said carefully. "And I take dictation. If you wish a prayer copied for your son's bedside, I can do that. Three coppers, and I'll write it well."

"A prayer," he repeated. His face did something complicated—disappointment folding itself around understanding, because men like him knew exactly what was being offered and what was being withheld, and why. "Aye. A prayer. Written well."

She wrote it there, on good vellum, a proper intercession to Saint Ellowen with all the correct flourishes, and beneath the final amen, in ink mixed with a single drop of something from a vial in her sleeve, she drew a line so faint it read as a pen-flourish, a tail of calligraphy curling off the page. A fever-line. Damped, muted, folded seven times into the letterforms themselves. Even if a hunter held it to a revelation-glass, it would show as nothing but ornament.

"Keep it by his bed," she said. "Under his pillow. And—" she hesitated, hating herself for the caution and knowing the caution was the only reason either of them was still alive, "—don't tell anyone it came from anywhere but the shrine stall. Don't say my name with it. Prayers don't have authors."

"No," he agreed, too quickly, taking the vellum like it was made of eggshell. "No authors. Thank you, mistress. Bless you." And he laughed a little, wretchedly, at the word *bless*, and pressed five coppers into her hand instead of three, and left.

Gideon stood alone in the chapel a moment longer than she should have. The candles guttered. Saint Ellowen gazed down with her painted, patient eyes, and Gideon had the sudden, irrational conviction that the saint was disappointed in her—not for the magic, but for the cowardice dressed up as craft. *Prayers don't have authors.* Except they did. Every charm she sold had her hand in it, literally, her handwriting, and handwriting was a signature, and signatures were names, and names—

Names were what they wrote on the Roll of the Gifted before the carts came.

She shook it off and went back to work.

* * *

The trouble came at dusk, in the market square, and it came wearing someone else's mistake.

Evening markets in occupied Ashen Vale had a particular quality of noise: the ordinary commerce of a city laid over a lower, watchful hum, like water over something submerged. Gideon moved through it in her second self—a plain grey cloak over the smock, hood up, carrying her basket of votive ribbons and blessed candles from the shrine stall, which was true, and the twelve charms sewn into the basket's false lining, which was also, technically, true, since she had blessed them. She made her rounds the way she always did: the fishmonger's widow, the cooper's wife, the old ferryman with the bad shoulder, each exchange lasting no more than ten heartbeats, coin passing one way and folded linen the other, conversation never rising above the pitch of ordinary neighborliness.

The cooper's wife took her fever-charm for the daughter and pressed Gideon's hand and said, "You're a saint, is what you are," too loudly, and Gideon smiled and said, "I'm a scribe," too quietly, and moved on, and did not let herself think about how loudly people had begun to say things lately. Gratitude, unguarded gratitude, was becoming common in the Weavers' Quarter, and common gratitude was a kind of smoke, and smoke drew hunters.

She was at the ferryman's stall, trading him a shoulder-charm for smoked eel she didn't need, when the light happened.

It began at the far end of the square, near the fountain—a soft gold bloom, like a candle seen through horn, then brighter, then unmistakably, catastrophically bright: a flare of warm light rising out of a woman's cupped hands, throwing her face into relief, throwing shadows long across the cobbles. The market noise stuttered, thinned, stopped. Somewhere a pot dropped. The light pulsed twice, gentle as breathing, beautiful in the way that forbidden things are beautiful, and Gideon knew it before her mind finished naming it.

She knew the line of it. She had drawn that line. A hearth-warming charm, one of her stronger stock, sold three days ago to the laundress Marta, whose lodgings were cold and whose hands were cracked raw from lye-water. A charm meant to be cupped indoors, in the dark, in secret. A charm that should have given off no more glow than a banked coal.

Marta stood in the middle of the square holding it like a miracle, because to her it was one, and she was saying something—Gideon couldn't hear the words over the roaring in her own ears, but she could see the shape of them: joy, wonder, *look, look what I have, look what was done for me.*

And around the square, in the doorways and the upper windows, in the shadow of the fountain and behind the fish-stalls, people were watching. Most with fear. Some with hunger. Gideon had lived in occupied cities long enough to know the difference between those two kinds of watching, and she counted the hungry ones the way she counted exits: two by the chandlery, one at the tavern steps, a boy on the fountain rim whose eyes had gone bright and calculating in the charm-light. Four. Maybe five. Two crowns a name. Four if there was a conviction.

*Marta,* Gideon thought, with a grief that had no safe outlet, *oh, Marta, put it away, put it away, love.*

But Marta was cold and poor and holding summer in her hands, and she did not put it away.

The charm flared once more and died to embers, and the square exhaled all at once, noise flooding back in, and everyone pretending very hard that nothing had happened, which was its own kind of confession. Gideon finished her transaction with the ferryman—her hands steady, her voice level, some deep-buried discipline holding both of them together like stitching—and walked, at exactly the pace of a woman with nothing to hide, out of the square by the chandlery lane. Away from the two hungry watchers, not toward them. Never toward.

Only when she reached the mouth of Cooper's Alley did she allow herself to stop, one hand flat against the cold brick, and breathe.

Think, she told herself. Like a ledger. Debits and credits.

The charm had flared. Charms didn't flare unless they were fed too much at the drawing—or unless something had *woken* them. She had drawn that hearth-line eleven years damped and dim; it could not have flared on its own. Which meant either her craft had slipped without her knowing—which she refused to believe—or something else had touched it. Something in the ink, or the bark, or—

Or someone had wanted it to flare. Someone who had known which charm to buy, and when, and how to make it sing in the most crowded square in the Weavers' Quarter at the hour of fullest traffic.

A demonstration. A test. Someone had reported her already, and tonight's little blaze had been arranged to confirm the report—to see whether the rumor of a gifted scribe in the shrine quarter would produce visible proof. And it had. Oh, it had. Somewhere in the Bastion House, a clerk was probably writing it down right now: *unlicensed light, market square, dusk, source traced to shrine goods.* Two crowns changing hands. Four, if they caught the author.

Gideon pushed off the wall and made herself walk, normally, home-ward, past the shuttered dyeshop, past the watch-post where a bored militiaman glanced at her and dismissed her in the same heartbeat—forgettable, forgettable, bless the dullness of her face. Her mind was already moving faster than her feet, laying out the night's work: burn the stock. All of it. Forty-one charms plus the twelve remaining in the basket lining, every scrap of treated bark and dosed ink, down to the last drop, and scatter the ash in the river. Move the vials. Take nothing across the threshold of the Scriptorium that could hang her. And the stone—

Her hand went, without permission, to the locket at her throat, where a small rough stone rode against her breastbone, warm as always, warmer than body-heat, warmer than it had any right to be. Her dead mother's token. The one thing she owned that she could not explain and had never dared to examine too closely, because examining it felt like opening a door she couldn't close.

*Tomorrow,* she promised it silently. *Tonight we burn. Tomorrow we think about you.*

She climbed the Scriptorium stairs in the dark, nodded to old Pell the night-porter, and let herself into the copying hall, where the moon through the slotted windows laid pale bars across the desks like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. Everything was exactly as she had left it. Everything was peaceful. Everything was fine.

That was what frightened her. Eleven years in occupied Ashen Vale had taught Gideon that the worst nights never announced themselves. They arrived looking like any other night, quiet and orderly and fine, and it was only afterward—after the doors came down—that you understood the silence had been the sound of something circling.

She knelt at the cold hearth in the scribes' common room, fed in the first armful of charms, and struck flint to birchbark, and watched her eleven years of careful, hidden, merciful work go up in a column of sparks.

The smoke smelled almost sweet. She hated that.

Outside, far off toward the Bastion House, a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, not yet, just the ninth bell, ordinary, measured, marking the hour the way it marked every hour. But Gideon stood in the firelight with ash settling on her sleeves and found she could not stop listening to it, the way you listen to footsteps behind you on a dark street, the way you listen for the second ring that doesn't come and the third that does.

By morning, she knew, the hunters would have the square's testimony. By tomorrow evening, they would have the shrine stall's accounts. And somewhere between those two facts lay whatever thin margin of time she had left to become, once and for all, no one at all.

She banked the fire, touched the warm stone at her throat—it pulsed, faintly, like a second heartbeat answering—and went up to her cell to pack.

She did not yet know that she was already too late.
