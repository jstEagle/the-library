## 2. The Proclamation

The bells of Ashen Vale rang wrong that morning.

Gideon Amberdale noticed it before she was fully awake, lying in her narrow room above the Scriptorium with the grey light seeping through shutters that had warped in their frames. The bells of the Dawn Temple rang in sevens on a feast day, in threes on a workday, and in slow, mournful pairs for a death. This morning they rang once, then stopped, then rang again—a stuttering, broken rhythm that had no place in any rite she knew.

She lay still and listened to it the way she had learned to listen for everything in the last three years: with her whole body, cataloguing, measuring, afraid.

The city had a new sound beneath the bells, too. Boots. Not the loose shuffle of the morning markets or the drum of children running to the fish-quay, but the coordinated, deliberate tread of soldiers moving in formation through streets that had not asked for them. Gideon had grown up in Ashen Vale when the garrison numbered forty men who mostly drank and chased the tanner's daughters. She knew what a city sounded like when it was merely inhabited. She was learning, day by day, what it sounded like when it was held.

She rose, dressed in her scribe's grey, and bound her hair back with the plain cord that made her look like what she appeared to be: a minor functionary of the Temple scriptoriums, a woman who copied deeds and death-records and the endless inventories of a realm that had stopped trusting its own paperwork. In the little looking-glass by the door, her face looked back at her—ordinary, watchful, twenty-six years old and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

*You are no one,* she told the reflection, as she did every morning. *You copy letters. Your hands are ink-stained and unremarkable. You have no gift worth naming.*

The reflection, as always, did not quite believe her.

* * *

The criers came at mid-morning.

Gideon was at her desk in the long scriptorium hall, ruling lines onto a ledger of grain-tithes, when the first voice rose above the noise of the market square outside the open windows. She knew the criers by their cadence—old Master Ellery with his wheedling lilt, the pair of twins who traded lines like a song. This was none of those. This voice was trained in a different school, hard and clear as a hammer on an anvil, and it carried the way only paid voices carry.

"Hear now the will of Vessic, rightful King of Ash and Ember, by grace of conquest and the consent of the crown!"

The scriptorium did not stop working. That was the first horror of it. Forty scribes bent over their desks, and not one of them lifted his head. Quills scratched. A boy in the corner turned the great wheel that fanned the drying racks. Only Gideon's hand faltered, and a single drop of ink fell from her nib and bloomed on the ruled line like a small black wound.

"Let it be known throughout the Vale, the Marches, the river-countries and the coast: that the realm harbors a corruption older than the crown and darker than the deep mines. There are those among you who carry the *gift*—the unlicensed working of forces beyond the temple's sanction. They walk among you. They sell you charms in the guise of blessings. They warm your hearths with fire that is not fire, and they mean, in time, to warm themselves with what is yours."

Gideon's mouth had gone dry. She kept her eyes on the ledger. She ruled another line, perfectly straight, and her hand did not shake, because she had trained it not to shake the way other girls had trained their hands to embroider.

"Therefore the King proclaims: any soul who gives information leading to the seizure of a gifted one shall receive ten crowns of silver. Ten crowns—enough to feed a family through the winter. Enough to buy back a debt. Enough to bury a parent with honor. The King does not ask you to love him. He asks only that you see clearly what stands beside you."

The voice paused, and when it resumed it had dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse.

"Denounce no innocent. The King's hunters are trained to know truth from spite. But know this also: those who *shelter* the gifted share their crime. Those who keep silent share their silence. There is no neutrality in the war against the corruption."

Outside, the market square had gone quiet in the way markets go quiet—not silent, but hushed, the noise folding in on itself like a crowd drawing its coat tighter. Gideon could hear the criers moving on, their voices overlapping down the street, the proclamation repeating itself in staggered pieces so that the whole city would be marinated in it by noon.

Ten crowns of silver.

She thought of the tallow-chandler's widow on the ground floor, who owed back-rent since spring. She thought of the fish-porter with the sick daughter, who paid Gideon two coppers a month for a charm against fever that was, in truth, a blessing she had written and folded into the shape of an ordinary prayer-slip. She thought of Master Ellery, who had a gambling debt and a weak character and had looked at her a little too long last week, when she had handed him a copy of the tithe rolls and her fingers had been warmer than they should have been, because the ink had been fresh and her gift had been restless and she had not been careful enough.

*Someone reported me,* she had thought yesterday, watching a charm flare gold in a market square where no charm had any business glowing. *Someone always reports you, eventually. The only question is whether they do it out of fear or greed.*

Now she had her answer. Fear paid nothing. Greed paid ten crowns.

"Master Amberdale." The senior scribe, old Corvess, stood at her elbow with a stack of fresh vellum. He was seventy, half-deaf, and the most careful man in the building. His eyes flicked, just once, toward the window. "The provost wants the proclamation copied. Fifty copies, for posting in the towns downstream. You have the clearest hand."

"Of course," Gideon said.

Her voice was level. Her hand, when she took the master copy from him, was level. Only her heartbeat betrayed her, hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out.

She copied the proclamation of her own hunting in a beautiful, flawless hand, and not one letter of it trembled.

* * *

She burned her stock that night.

Not in her own hearth—a hearth leaves ash, and ash can be read, and Gideon had learned from her mother, in the one year they had together before the fever took her, that everything can be read if you know how to look. She carried her work down through the Scriptorium's back passages, past the drying racks and the binding-room with its smell of glue and leather, out to the old kiln-yard behind the temple annex, where the incense was rendered in autumn and no one came after dark.

The kiln was cold. She lit it with flint and steel, the ordinary way, because using the other way—even here, even alone—felt like striking a match inside a powder-store.

Then she opened the satchel.

There were forty-one pieces in it. She had counted them that morning, and counting them had been like counting the beats of her own heart. Forty-one small works of a craft that had no name in any ledger: prayer-slips folded around threads of warmth, little stones that soothed a fever or stilled a nightmare, a copper ring she had made for the fish-porter's daughter that would keep the lung-rot from settling deeper. Each one carried a thread of her. That was the thing no proclamation said plainly, the thing that made the whole enterprise obscene: a charm was not an object. It was a small piece of the maker, given away. Every one of those forty-one pieces was a piece of Gideon Amberdale, and every one of them, in the hands of a trained hunter, could be read back to her like a signature.

She fed them into the kiln in handfuls.

The prayer-slips went first, curling and flashing as they burned—flashing, because that was the treachery of her work, it *glowed* when it died, little bursts of gold light that she had to smother with the kiln-door and a wet sack. The stones went harder, resisting, humming against her palms as if pleading, and she had to close her eyes and drop them in and listen to them crack. The copper ring went last. She had made it in March. She had held the girl's small hot hand in both of hers and told her to breathe slowly, and the charm had drunk her warmth and her will and a little of her hope, and now it softened and slumped in the fire like something fainting.

Forty-one pieces. Forty-one small treasons, gone to smoke.

She stood in the kiln-yard with the satchel empty in her hands and felt the fear move through her, cold and thorough, the way floodwater moves through a cellar—finding every low place, filling every hollow she had not known was hollow. They would come. Perhaps not tomorrow, perhaps not this week, but the criers had said *hunters*, and hunters did not mean the garrison's drunk lieutenants. Hunters meant the King's new order, the ones in grey and iron who had arrived with Vessic's coronation and who asked questions in a soft voice and wrote everything down. She had seen two of them at the market yesterday, standing too still among the stalls, and one of them had been a woman with a scar through her eyebrow, and something about the set of her shoulders had snagged at Gideon's memory like a fishhook—

She put that thought down. She would not look at it. There were thoughts that could unmake you if you looked at them too soon.

She was turning to go when she felt the weight.

It was small. It sat at the bottom of the satchel, and it had sat there for three years, and she had carried it through every search and every move and every quiet night of dread without ever once deciding to carry it. It was a stone the size of a plum, dark as cooled slag, shot through with a single thread of red that caught the kiln's dying light like a vein of ember.

Her mother's token.

Gideon sat down on the cold kiln-stones and turned it in her hands. She knew everything a daughter could know about it and nothing that mattered. Her mother had worn it on a cord at her throat, always, and had told her only this much: *It was given to me by someone who owed me a debt, little wren, and one day it will call that debt due, and when it does, you must not be afraid.* Then the fever had come, in the space of a week, and the debt had never been named, and the stone had never done anything at all—except once, the night of the funeral, when Gideon had held it and wept, and she could have sworn it had been warm.

It had never been warm since.

She should burn it. That was the whole logic of the night; that was the discipline she had built her survival on, brick by brick, for three years. Everything that glows must go into the fire. Everything that can be read must be unread. The stone had a thread of ember in it that was not ordinary mineral—she had known that since she was a child, had felt it hum against her palm on bad nights like a second heartbeat—and a hunter with a reading-glass would see it in a moment, would see it and trace it, and ten crowns would change hands, and Gideon Amberdale would end her life in a grey-and-iron cart.

She stood up. She walked to the kiln. She held the stone over the coals.

The coals had gone down to a dull red breathing, and the stone in her hand was cold, and the thread of red in its heart was dark, and there was no reason at all—no reason in the world—for what happened then, except that it happened.

The stone warmed.

Not much. Not the dramatic blaze of a charm flaring in a market square. Just a slow seep of heat, gentle as a hand closing over hers, as if the stone had felt her intent and had said, in a language older than words: *no.*

Gideon stood there a long time, her arm out over the coals, and the stone sat in her palm and stayed warm, and the kiln-yard was silent except for the settling of ash.

*You must not be afraid,* her mother had said. *When it calls the debt due.*

She thought about the proclamation, and the ten crowns, and the hunters in grey and iron. She thought about the widow who owed back-rent, and the fish-porter, and old Corvess with his careful eyes, and the whole city of Ashen Vale drawing its coat tighter around itself, neighbor turned toward neighbor, everyone learning the price of everyone else. She thought about how fear worked: not like a fire that burns you at once, but like water, seeping, filling the low places, until one morning you could not remember what it felt like to stand upright.

Her mother had stood upright. She knew that the way she knew almost nothing else about the woman—knew it from the token itself, from the way it had been worn smooth by years against a sternum, from the single red thread that had never gone dark even in a dead woman's keeping.

Gideon closed her hand around the stone. It was warm. It was warm, and it was hers, and whatever debt it carried, it had chosen, tonight of all nights, to make itself felt.

"Then I won't be afraid," she said aloud, to the kiln, to the ash, to the dead.

Her voice sounded small in the yard. But the stone pulsed once against her palm—soft, brief, unmistakable—like a heartbeat answering, or a promise being kept, and Gideon Amberdale slid it into the secret pocket inside her sash where it had always lived, and picked up her empty satchel, and walked back through the dark passages of the Scriptorium with her spine straight and her fear, for the first time in three years, walking behind her instead of in front.

She did not see the figure at the end of the annex corridor, standing in the shadow of the drying racks. She did not see the grey-and-iron coat, or the reading-glass catching the lamplight, or the woman with the scar through her eyebrow go very still as the scribe passed, and mouth, half to herself, a name she had not spoken in fifteen years:

*Gideon?*
