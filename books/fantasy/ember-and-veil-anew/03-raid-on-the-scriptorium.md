## 3. Raid on the Scriptorium

The knock came at the hour when ink dries slowest.

Gideon knew this because she was hunched over a ledger in the Scriptorium's lower scriptorium—*lower* being a joke among the scribes, since everything in Ashen Vale was built downward into the hill to escape the wind—copying tithe records for the granary wardens. The lamp beside her elbow burned low and yellow, and the iron-gall ink had gone tacky in the cold, dragging at her nib like something reluctant to be written. She had been thinking about supper. She had been thinking about the way the market square light had flared two days past, and how no one had come for her yet, and how that silence felt less like safety than like a held breath.

Then: three knocks, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned discipline. Not the knock of a supplicant. Not the knock of a delivery boy with a bundle of vellum.

The knock of someone who expected doors to open.

Brannoc looked up from his desk across the room. He was an old man shaped like a question mark, all spine and stoop, with ink stains so deep in his knuckles they might have been tattoos from some vanished tribe of scriveners. His eyes found hers across the lamplight, and Gideon watched him do the same arithmetic she had just done—the hour, the knock, the quality of silence after it—and arrive at the same sum.

"Back door," he said quietly. "Now."

"I haven't finished the—"

"Gideon." Her name, and beneath it, unmistakable now even through stone and timber: boots. Many boots. The measured cadence of soldiers who did not need to hurry because hurry was for people who could still run.

She closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; somewhere inside, a small hysterical creature was already screaming. She slid the ledger back into its shelf-slot with the others, forty years of forged tithes and laundered ledgers, and reached beneath her workbench where a loose floorboard hid her charm-stock—two dozen small blessings stitched into copper wire and riverglass, each one a hanging offense, each one sold last week to buyers whose names she had been careful never to learn twice.

Empty. Of course empty. She'd cleared them out the night the criers came, fed them to the hearth in her rented room upstairs, watched twenty years of careful work curl and blacken like moths. All but one thing, which lived not under the floorboard but against her sternum, on a cord, warm as a swallowed coal: her mother's covenant-stone, the one object she had never been able to explain or destroy or sell.

The boots reached the outer door.

"Through the archive," Brannoc said, already moving, astonishingly fast for a man his shape, sweeping his own workbench clear with one arm. "The stacks go back into the hill. There's a drain at the far end, old, older than the temple—it comes out above the millrace. Can you swim?"

"In theory."

"Wonderful. In theory we're both innocent men." He pressed something into her hands as he shoved her toward the archive arch—a slim roll of vellum, sealed with plain wax. "If I don't—when I don't follow, you take this to Duskharbor. To the Council. You ask for Ondrell. Tell him the Vale is ash and the ledger is open."

"What ledger? Brannoc, what are you—"

The outer door came down.

Not opened—*came down*, hinges shrieking, wood splintering at the frame because whoever stood beyond it had decided the frame was optional. Lamplight from the entry hall spilled in sideways, and in it, silhouettes: six, eight, ten figures in the grey-lacquered half-armor of Vessic's witch-hunters, visors down, irons in hand. And at their front, stepping over the ruined door without breaking stride, a woman in a captain's coat with her dark hair bound back tight and a hunter's badge gleaming at her throat.

Serah Vole.

Gideon's whole body went cold and stupid at once. Because she knew that walk. She had known it since they were nine years old, racing along the top of the aqueduct wall, since Serah had shared her stolen plums and her father's forbidden maps and her fierce, ridiculous dream of joining the city watch *to protect people*. That walk had always been certain. It had simply never before been pointed at anything Gideon loved.

"Captain," Brannoc said, and his voice was magnificent, bored, the voice of a man interrupted over paperwork. "The Scriptorium of Ashen Vale is consecrated ground. Whatever warrant you imagine you carry—"

"We carry the king's word," Serah said. "That's better than paper." Her voice too had changed—or maybe it hadn't changed at all, maybe Gideon had simply spent fifteen years remembering it softer than it was. "Master Brannoc. By order of King Vessic, this house is seized for the crime of harboring the gifted. Everyone kneels. Hands flat on the floor."

The junior scribes—there were four of them tonight, poor Maren and the twins and old Hesk who mostly slept—knelt with the terrible speed of people who had grown up learning which orders were survivable. Gideon did not kneel. She stood at the edge of the archive arch, half in shadow, and Brannoc stepped sideways, casually, catastrophically, until his stooped body filled the gap between her and the hunters' sightline.

"There is no one gifted here," Brannoc said. "There is only ink. Ink, and old men, and children who copy out grain sums until their eyes bleed. If your king needs witches, Captain, I suggest the theater. Very convincing lot."

"Search the stacks," Serah said. "All of them."

And here was the thing Gideon would carry afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest for years like a swallowed stone of its own: Serah's eyes flicked to the archive arch. Flicked, and held, for exactly one breath too long. Long enough for Gideon to see recognition move behind the visor's shadow—not the visor; Serah wore no visor, only the badge and the certainty—recognition, and something underneath it that might have been pain, quickly buried.

She knows me, Gideon thought. She knows I'm here. And she is searching anyway.

Two hunters brushed past Brannoc—he didn't quite resist, or perhaps he resisted precisely enough to make them shove him, precisely enough to make it a crime—and their lanterns swung into the archive dark, and Gideon ran.

* * *

The archive stacks were the oldest part of the Scriptorium, older than the temple that had grown around them like a barnacle, and they went back into the hill in long descending galleries lined floor-to-ceiling with pigeonholes and scroll-jars and the dry smell of ten centuries of other people's business. Gideon knew them the way she knew her own hands. She had catalogued half of these shelves herself, by lamplight, in the years when cataloguing had seemed like a life.

Now she fled down them, barefoot-silent out of habit though her boots made noise enough, past *Wardens' Rolls 311–360* and *Charters, Damaged* and the jar of extinct inks that Brannoc refused to throw away because "they might come back into fashion." Behind her, lanternlight bloomed and multiplied. Voices called to one another in the clipped cadence of a search pattern. And beneath it all, rising, the first thin thread of smoke-smell.

They've fired the entry hall, she realized. Standard practice, some part of her mind supplied coolly, the part that had memorized the witch-hunter protocols Serah used to recite bitterly, back when reciting them was a joke between friends. Burn what you can't carry. Burn what might hide a charm in its binding-glue. Burn the past, because the past is where the gifted keep their evidence.

Her lungs hurt. She told herself it was the running.

At the third gallery junction there was a choice: left, down the drain-passage Brannoc had named, toward the millrace and the river and whatever came next; or right, up the narrow stair to the reading gallery that overlooked the entry hall, where she could see—

She went right. She hated herself for it even as her feet chose it, and she went right anyway.

From the reading gallery's iron rail, through the heat-shimmer and the growing grey bloom of smoke, Gideon looked down into the entry hall of the place she had worked for eleven years, and saw it ending.

Hunters moved through it like a tide, overturning desks, splitting scroll-cabinets with hatchet-blows, feeding the hearth-fire—which had been banked for the night, which was now roaring—with armloads of ledgers. The junior scribes knelt in a row, wrists already bound, Maren crying without any sound at all, just the shaking. And in the center of the floor, forced down onto his knees with two hunters gripping his shoulders, knelt Brannoc.

He was not struggling. He was talking. Even from above, even through smoke, Gideon could see the set of his jaw, the tilt of his head, the lecturing finger he somehow managed to raise despite the irons—old Brannoc, correcting them, *correcting them*, probably citing statutes, probably informing Captain Vole that the seizure of consecrated texts violated three separate accords and that her penmanship in the seizure order was, frankly, sloppy.

And Serah stood before him, arms folded, listening.

That was the worst of it. She listened. Gideon saw her hold up a hand and stop her own hunters mid-shove, saw her lean in and actually *hear* the old man out, saw something in her stance that was not cruelty—was never cruelty, Serah had never once in her life kicked a dog—and was instead something far more frightening: conviction wearing patience like a coat.

Then Serah straightened, said something short, and nodded, and the hunters hauled Brannoc upright, and the captain herself took the coil of binding-rope from her belt and bound his wrists. Carefully. Gently, even. Checking the knots the way you check knots when you were taught rigging by a fisherman father who loved you.

You're doing it right, Gideon thought, pressing her forehead against the cold iron rail. You're doing every bit of it right, and it's all wrong, and you know, you have to know—

A hunter below turned his head. Followed the smoke upward. Shouted.

Gideon ran.

* * *

The drain-passage was exactly as Brannoc had promised: old, older than the temple, a brick throat that had once carried the hill's rain down to the millrace and now carried mostly rats and regret. Gideon went into it crouched, then crawling, then wriggling on her belly through a stretch so narrow she had to exhale fully and drag herself forward by her fingertips while the covenant-stone swung on its cord and tapped her chin with every pull, tap, tap, tap, patient as a metronome.

Behind her, the Scriptorium burned.

She could hear it even here—could hear the archive galleries going, the groan of shelving timber, the soft catastrophic rush of a thousand scrolls catching at once, a sound like the hill itself inhaling. Ten centuries of record. Charters and rolls and damaged things and extinct inks. Every forged ledger she had ever hidden inside honest ones, every charm-sale she had ever laundered through the tithe books—all of it, the entire architecture of her double life, dissolving into smoke that would rise through the hill's throat and out into the night sky over Ashen Vale, where anyone could read it.

Let them read it, she thought, and then, immediately, horribly: *Maren is still in there. The twins. Hesk.*

They'll be questioned and released, she told herself. They know nothing. That was the point of knowing nothing; she had built their ignorance as carefully as she'd built her ledgers. But the thought rang false and tinny, because she had seen witch-hunter protocol recited as a joke once, and jokes have a way of becoming manuals. Questioned hard. Released, if they were lucky, if they could prove the negative, if someone with silver didn't decide denouncing a scribe was easier than defending one.

Silver for denunciations. The criers had said it plainly enough. Gideon had stood in the crowd with her hood up and heard the price of a neighbor's name announced like the price of bread, and she had thought: *not my neighbors, not mine.* Two days later, a charm had flared in a market square.

She crawled, and the smoke followed her into the passage like something with intent, and she crawled faster.

The drain ended in a grate crusted with a century of lime, and Gideon got her fingers through it and pulled until her nails tore, and it gave all at once, dumping her out onto wet stones above the millrace with the river roaring black and star-scattered below. Cold hit her like a slap. She lay there a moment, gasping, scraped, one boot soaked, hair full of lime dust, alive.

Above and behind her, orange light pulsed in the drain-mouth like a heartbeat. The Scriptorium's roof was visible from here, or had been; now it was a glow, a column of sparks climbing into the dark, and the smoke rolled out over the valley in a long grey shroud, and somewhere inside it were four kneeling scribes and one stooped old man being walked away in gentle, meticulous ropes.

"You should have run left," Gideon whispered to herself. "You should have run left, you stupid, selfish—"

But she hadn't. She had looked. She had needed to see him taken, needed to see Serah's face doing what it did, because some part of her had understood, even in that moment, that this was the last time she would ever see either of them—that Brannoc was walking out of her life through fire, and Serah was standing on the far side of it, and the girl who had shared plums and maps and dreams on the aqueduct wall had chosen her side of the burning, and it was not Gideon's.

She got up. She made herself get up, knees shaking, and stand on the wet stones, and look at the fire one last time—not because she wanted to, but because she owed it. Because forgetting would be easy, and easy was how people like Serah were made.

"I'll take it to Duskharbor," she said aloud, to the smoke, to the old man in the ropes. "The ledger. I don't even know what it is, but I'll take it. I swear it on—" On what? On the only thing she had left that wasn't forged. Her hand found the cord at her throat, the covenant-stone warm against her palm, warmer than it should be, warmer than body-heat, warm the way a hearth is warm, the way a promise is warm. "On this," she finished, not knowing what she promised to.

The stone pulsed once under her fingers. A single beat, distinct, deliberate, like a heart answering.

Gideon stood very still on the millrace stones with the fire painting her shadow long and trembling on the water, and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives only at the exact center of catastrophe, that the stone had heard her. That it had perhaps been waiting a long time for someone to say those words. That the warmth in her palm was not memory, not sentiment, not grief playing tricks.

It was attention.

Downriver, faint on the wind, came the creak of rigging and the lap of water against hulls—the smugglers' quarter, where coin bought silence and boats left before dawn and no one asked a passenger's name twice. Gideon pulled her cloak around her scorched dress, touched the vellum roll Brannoc had pressed into her hands, and started walking along the black water toward the lights, while behind her the hill that had raised her glowed like a forge, and ahead of her the river ran dark and patient toward the sea.
