## 4. The Smuggler's Price

Tamsin Roe had been cheated by better liars than this, and she'd cheated a few of them herself, so when the scribe in the grey cloak sat down across from her at the Drowned Bell and said, "I need passage downstream," Tamsin already knew three things: the woman was running from something, the woman was lying about how much she was running from, and the woman was going to pay anyway.

The tavern earned its name honestly. The Drowned Bell sat at the crook of Ashen Vale's river quarter where the water went brown and slow and forgetful, and its ceiling beams were black with forty years of pipe-smoke and river damp. A bell hung above the door that no one had ever heard ring — it had been dredged up from a sunken chapel two generations back, green with rot, mute as a stone. The regulars liked to say it would ring only for the dead. Tamsin thought that was sentimental nonsense. She also never sat with her back to it.

She kept her back to it now, out of habit, and studied the scribe over the rim of a cup of wine that cost her nothing because she'd won it at cards an hour before the scribe walked in.

Grey traveling cloak, good wool but travel-stained, hem darkened with mud from the archive district — which was interesting, because the archive district had been burning since sundown. Ink stains on the right hand, the particular callus of a quill held too many years. Eyes that kept doing arithmetic on the room: exits, faces, the two off-duty watchmen drinking near the hearth. Hands steady, though. Steadier than most soldiers' hands, and steadier than they had any right to be for someone whose hem said *fire*.

"You've got the wrong table," Tamsin said. "I'm not a ferrywoman."

"I know what you are." The scribe's voice was low and even, pitched under the tavern noise without effort. Someone who'd spent years being quiet in rooms full of people who punished noise. "You're Tamsin Roe. You run cargo downriver to Duskharbor twice a month. You don't ask what's in the crates."

"I ask what's in the payment," Tamsin said. "That's my one question, and you haven't answered it yet."

"My name is Gideon." A pause, deliberate, like a card laid face-up to distract from the ones still hidden. "Gideon Marsh. I'm a copyist. My employer's business has become... complicated, and I need to be out of Ashen Vale before the gates open tomorrow morning."

*Gideon Marsh.* Tamsin turned the name over and found nothing sticking to it, which was itself informative. Nobody came to the Drowned Bell at midnight with clean names. Clean names were bought, and bought names meant money, and money meant the kind of trouble worth fleeing.

"And your employer's complication," Tamsin said, "is the reason half the archive district is ash?"

Something flickered behind the scribe's eyes — quick, quickly buried. Grief, maybe. Or guilt. Both wore the same face at this hour.

"The fire wasn't mine," Gideon said.

"No," Tamsin agreed pleasantly. "Fires rarely are. That's what makes them useful." She let that sit, then leaned back and hooked one boot heel on the bench opposite, making a little fence of herself between the scribe and the door. Not threatening. Just present. "Here's how this works, copyist. I don't carry people. People scream, people bleed, people get ideas about giving orders on my deck. I carry crates, because crates keep their opinions to themselves. If I were to make an exception — and I'm not saying I would — it would cost more than your employer's whole complicated business is probably worth."

"How much?"

"Two hundred crowns."

It was an obscene number and Tamsin knew it. Passage to Duskharbor for a single passenger ran thirty crowns at the greedy end, fifty if the passenger was stupid. Two hundred was the number you quoted to make someone stand up and leave.

Gideon didn't stand up. She reached into her cloak, and Tamsin's hand drifted — casually, professionally — toward the knife at her hip, but what came out was a purse. Heavy. The sound it made on the table was the sound of real silver, the deep dull clank that clipped coin makes against clipped coin, and every ear within ten feet heard it whether they pretended otherwise or not.

"One hundred now," Gideon said. "One hundred in Duskharbor. And I board tonight."

Tamsin looked at the purse. Then at the scribe. Then, briefly, at the mute bell above the door, which did not ring, because bells never do anything helpful.

*One hundred crowns in clipped silver.* That was guild money, or temple money, or stolen money, and all three flavors came with teeth. But it was also, undeniably, one hundred crowns, and the *Kestrel's Debt* had been sitting high and dry in the mud since the spring floods dropped the river, waiting on careening and caulking that Tamsin could finally afford. Her crew hadn't seen proper wages in two months. Old Wren's daughter needed a physician in Duskharbor. Tamsin's own debts had their own teeth, and they bit on schedule.

And beneath all that — beneath the arithmetic, which was honest work and she respected it — there was the other thing. The thing she'd learned to listen for in twenty years of smuggling, the small cold voice that noticed what the mouth was doing while the words were busy elsewhere.

Because the scribe had set the purse down with her left hand.

Her right hand stayed inside the cloak the entire time, curled around something at her breast, and she guarded it the way a miser guards a ledger and a mother guards a child and nobody guards a purse. Whatever was under that cloak was worth more than the silver. Worth enough that a woman whose district was burning, whose mentor was presumably in chains or worse, whose name was almost certainly not Gideon Marsh — worth enough that *she* was guarding *it*, instead of the reverse.

Tamsin had carried relics once, for a collector in Duskharbor. She'd carried contraband salt, smuggled letters, a fugitive countess, and one very illegal shipment of prayer-flags during the last purge. In twenty years she had developed a nose for value the way a fishmonger develops a nose for fish, and her nose was telling her that the thing under the grey cloak smelled like all of them at once.

"Boarding tonight costs extra," Tamsin said.

"That's extortion."

"That's *pricing*." Tamsin smiled, showing teeth. "Extortion is when I threaten you. Pricing is when I tell you the truth slowly. Boarding tonight means my crew works through the night loading in the dark, which means hazard pay, which means—" she tapped the purse, "—one hundred and twenty now, eighty after. Final offer, and I'll thank you to remember I'm offering it to someone whose name I don't believe."

For a long moment the scribe just looked at her. It was a level look, unafraid, and Tamsin found it more unsettling than fear would have been. Most fugitives either groveled or blustered. This one did sums.

"You're wondering what I'm carrying," Gideon said quietly.

"I'm wondering what you're carrying that you didn't put in the purse."

Something moved at the corner of the scribe's mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe — the look of someone finding a fellow professional in an unexpected trade.

"If I told you," Gideon said, "you'd charge me more."

"Oh, absolutely."

"Then we understand each other." She pushed the second purse across — twenty crowns heavier than agreed, counted out fast, like she wanted the transaction done before her nerve went. "One hundred and twenty. Eighty in Duskharbor, on delivery, at whatever dock you choose. I'll be cargo. I'll keep my opinions to myself. I won't give orders on your deck."

"See, you say that now," Tamsin said, sweeping both purses into her coat, "but everyone says that now. I had a crate once that gave orders. Terrible voyage."

"What happened to it?"

"Sold it in Duskharbor. Last I heard it was giving orders to a merchant prince." She stood, draining the won wine, and jerked her chin toward the door. "Come on, then, Gideon Marsh. My boat's at the Tanner's Stair. You'll ride in the hold with the dye-barrels, and I'll warn you once: the blue one leaks, and if it stains your cloak that's between you and the barrel."

They went out into the river fog together, and above the door the drowned bell hung silent as ever, and Tamsin, who did not believe in omens, nevertheless glanced at it as she passed, the way you glance at a dog that hasn't barked.

* * *

The Tanner's Stair stank of lime and old hides, and the *Kestrel's Debt* sat at the bottom of it looking exactly like what she was: a flat-bottomed river barge-rig with a patched lateen and a hull that had been repaired with more argument than timber. Forty feet of stubbornness. Tamsin loved her the way you love a thing that has never once done what you asked but has also never once sunk.

Her crew was already moving. She'd sent word ahead by urchin — *night loading, double pay, tell no one why* — and by the time she and the scribe came down the stair, Brackish Pete and young Fen were rolling barrels down the planks in the fog, and old Wren stood at the rail with a lantern hooded to a coal, watching the water the way he always watched the water, like it owed him an apology.

"Tamsin." Wren's voice was gravel soaked in river. His eyes went past her shoulder to the grey-cloaked figure and narrowed. "That's not cargo."

"Cargo doesn't walk, Wren, correct. Well spotted."

"That's a person."

"People are cargo that complains. Get her aboard and below." She lowered her voice as the scribe passed her on the gangplank. Close up, in the lantern-coal light, Tamsin saw the ink stains properly for the first time, and the fine tremor in the left hand that hadn't been there in the tavern — the tremor of someone who had been calm for hours on purpose and was now, in the dark, letting the cost show. "Hold," Tamsin murmured. "Rules of my deck. One: I'm captain, always, even underwater. Two: if hunters come alongside, you're my cousin's clerk and you're deaf and boring. Three—" she paused, and made herself say it, because it was true and true things were the only currency she dealt in, "—if it comes to swimming, I save my crew first. You'll be third or fourth. Sign on anyway?"

The scribe looked at her for a moment. Fog beaded on the grey hood.

"You counted wrong," Gideon said. "There's no one else aboard. I'm second."

"Confident, aren't you."

"No," said the scribe, and the word came out worn smooth, like a stone carried a long way. "Just accurate."

She went below, and Tamsin stood on the deck a moment longer, listening to the river slap the hull, doing the sum she'd been avoiding since the tavern. One hundred and twenty crowns against the risk of carrying whatever-that-was past Vessic's new chain-tolls. Against the witch-hunters' barges with their white banners and their iron cages, which had started appearing on the river this season like a sickness. Against the simple fact that the archive district was still glowing on the horizon like a bad memory, and fires that big always sent out sparks, and sparks always landed somewhere inconvenient.

Against the other sum, the one without numbers. The way the scribe had said *just accurate*. The way she'd guarded the thing under her cloak. The way she'd paid too much, fast, like a woman buying her own life at a price she considered fair — and Tamsin had haggled with enough dying men to know what that looked like.

"Fen," she called. "Cast off when the last barrel's down. Quiet lines."

"Aye, Captain."

"And Fen." He paused, one hand on the mooring line, all elbows and seventeen years. "If anyone asks at the tolls, we're carrying dye and salt-fish and my cousin's clerk, who is deaf and boring."

Fen grinned. "Who's your cousin, Captain?"

"Anyone I say is. Cast off."

* * *

The river took them the way it took everything, without comment. Ashen Vale slid past on the left bank, its towers stitched with orange light where the archive districts still smoldered, and the smoke lay along the water in a long grey wound. Tamsin stood at the stern with the sweep-oar in her hands, steering by feel and by Wren's low calls from the bow — *shoal left, deadfall right, current's lazy tonight* — and let the city shrink behind her.

Below decks, the scribe was silent. Tamsin checked twice, on pretense of inspecting the dye-barrels, and both times found Gideon Marsh sitting cross-legged in the narrow space between cargo, hood back, face lit by the tiny hooded lamp she'd brought, reading. Of course reading. A book from her cloak, some battered volume with a cracked spine, and her lips moving faintly around the words like a woman praying in a language she no longer fully believed in.

The second time, Tamsin lingered.

"That book's worth more than my boat," she said.

"It isn't."

"Everything's worth more than my boat. That's not the point. The point is you packed a book." She crouched, forearms on knees, and studied the scribe the way she'd study a chart with a smudged coastline. "When your district was burning, when you were running for your life with hunters at your back, you stopped to pack a book. Either you're the bravest fool I've ever carried, or that book is worth dying for, or—" and here she tilted her head, "—it's not the book at all, is it. It's what you keep with the book."

Gideon closed it. Slowly. Her right hand had gone, again, to her breast, to the shape beneath the cloak, and Tamsin watched her do it — watched her catch herself doing it, and stop, and fold both hands flat on the book instead, deliberately, like a gambler showing empty palms.

"You ask a lot of questions for a woman who promised me silence," Gideon said.

"I promised you nothing of the sort. You promised *me*. I never sign contracts I intend to keep myself — bad business." Tamsin smiled, and got nothing back, and pressed on anyway, gently, the way you press on a bruise to learn how deep it goes. "Here's what I know, copyist. I know your silver's clipped, which means temple mint or guild mint, and neither pays scribes in clipped coin. I know your hands are ink-stained but your boots are walking boots, broken in hard, and scribes sit. I know the archive district burned the same night you came looking for me, and I know you flinched when I mentioned it — not at the fire. At the *word*. Which means someone you loved is in it, or of it, or under it." She let that breathe. "And I know that whatever you're carrying against your heart, you touch it when you're frightened, and you've been frightened since the moment you sat down, and you hid it so well that only a smuggler would notice."

The hold was very quiet. The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere overhead, Wren's boots crossed the deck.

"You could throw me off at the next bend," Gideon said at last. "Take the hundred and twenty and be rid of the risk."

"I could."

"Why don't you?"

And there it was — the question Tamsin had been circling all night, the one she'd been avoiding with haggling and rules and jokes about crates that give orders. Why *didn't* she? Twenty years of survival said throw the mystery overboard, take the coin, keep the hull clean. Twenty years of survival had also taught her that mysteries thrown overboard had a way of swimming after boats.

"Because I've carried a lot of things down this river," Tamsin said, standing, brushing off her knees. "Salt, silk, secrets, sinners. And every single thing worth carrying had the same look about it — heavy in the wrong place, like a crate marked *feathers* that takes four men to lift. You've got that look, copyist. Your whole self has got that look." She moved to the ladder, then paused, one hand on the rail, and didn't look back, because some conversations go easier sideways. "So here's the last of it, and then I'm done asking. Whatever that stone is — and it's a stone, don't bother denying it, I can see the shape of it through wool — you keep it safe until Duskharbor. Not because I care what it is. Because if hunters board my boat and find it, they don't arrest you and leave me my hull. They burn the hull, the crew, and the river's reputation into the bargain. So your secret and my ship are the same cargo now. Mind it accordingly."

She climbed toward the deck and the night air, and was halfway up when the voice came from below, quiet and clear through the hold:

"It was my mother's."

Tamsin stopped.

"She died owing debts," Gideon said, "the same as everyone. This is the only thing she left that wasn't owed to somebody else. That's all you get. That's everything I have."

Tamsin stood on the ladder a moment, fog drifting past the hatch above, and found, to her considerable irritation, that she believed it. Every word. Which was worse than a lie, because lies you could price.

"Everything you have," she repeated, "except eighty crowns, which you still owe me, and a berth, which you're still paying for. Sleep if you can. We run the first chain-toll at dawn, and you'd best be deaf and boring by then."

She came up onto the deck and shut the hatch behind her, and stood a while at the stern with the sweep in her hands, watching the burning city fall away astern and the black water open ahead.

Wren drifted back along the rail, unhurried, and settled beside her without being asked. For a while neither spoke. The current pulled. Downstream, far off, the first pale suggestion of the toll-station's lanterns pricked the dark — Vessic's new iron on the old river, white banners limp in the windless night.

"Well?" Wren said finally.

"Well what?"

"You know what." He nodded at the hatch. "What's she carrying?"

Tamsin rolled the sweep, easing the bow a hair to starboard, and thought about stones guarded like children, and books packed while cities burned, and the way the scribe had said *just accurate* in a voice like something worn smooth by a very long road.

"Worth more than coin," she said at last. "That's all I know, and it's plenty. Coin spends. That kind of cargo *decides* things." She squinted at the distant lanterns, and felt the small cold voice at the back of her skull — the one that had kept her alive through two purges and one shipwreck — begin, very softly, to pay attention. "Get Fen. Wake the rest. We'll want everyone on deck and every lie we own ready before we reach those lights."

Above the stern, high and thin, a bird cried once in the dark — some night-heron startled off the water — and Tamsin, who did not believe in omens, told herself firmly that it sounded nothing at all like a bell.
