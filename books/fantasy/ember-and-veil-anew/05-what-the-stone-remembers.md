## 5. What the Stone Remembers

The river had a smell at dawn that Gideon had never learned in the Scriptorium: mud and fish-rot and something green underneath it all, the green of things growing without permission. She sat on the deck of Tamsin Roe's flat-bottomed barge with her back against the cargo rail, watching mist peel off the water in long gray ribbons, and tried to remember what it felt like to be a person whose worst problem was a smudged ledger.

Three days since Ashen Vale. Three days since Brannoc's voice, hoarse through the smoke—*run, girl, don't you dare look back*—and she had looked back anyway. She was still looking back. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the archive stacks burning, forty years of her mentor's careful cataloguing curling into ash, and Serah Vole standing in the doorway of the Scriptorium with a hunter's black coat buttoned to her throat and Gideon's own name on her lips like an accusation.

*Gideon.* Just her name. Not even a curse. That had been the worst of it.

"You're doing the thing again," Tamsin said from the tiller.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at nothing like it owes you money." The smuggler was a lean woman with salt-and-pepper hair cropped short and hands that never seemed entirely still, one always resting near the tiller, one always turning some small object over between her fingers—a river-stone, a bent nail, today a strip of dried eel she was chewing with the grim dedication of a woman who had made peace with her breakfast long ago. "I've hauled fugitives before. Priests, mostly. They do the same thing. Stare at the water like it's going to give them absolution."

"It isn't?"

"Not this stretch. This stretch only gives you leeches." Tamsin spat the eel gristle over the side. "Eat something."

"I'm not hungry."

"Nobody's hungry on day three. That's how you end up fainting into the bilge on day five, and I've got enough bilge problems." She fished a wrapped bundle from under the tiller bench and tossed it; Gideon caught it by reflex, which she suspected was the point. Dried plums, hard bread, a wedge of pale cheese sweating in its cloth. "You paid me enough for three fares and a funeral. Eat like it."

Gideon ate because it was easier than arguing, and because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. The bread tasted like nothing. The plum tasted almost violently of itself, sweet and dark, and she had to stop after the second bite and look at the river until her eyes stopped stinging.

The stone hung against her sternum under three layers of clothing, on a cord she'd braided herself from thread meant for binding manuscripts. Her mother's covenant-token. She'd told Tamsin it was a family heirloom worth nothing but sentiment, and Tamsin had looked at her with those flat gray eyes and said, "Sure," in a tone that meant *I have carried contraband for twenty years and you are the worst liar I've ever shipped*, and had not raised the fee.

That frightened Gideon more than the hunters did, somehow. Hunters wanted simple things. Tamsin Roe wanted to know.

They passed the shrine just past midmorning.

She wouldn't have known it for a shrine at all if the stone hadn't told her. It was a hump of old masonry on the left bank, half-swallowed by willow roots, moss-eaten, its carved faces worn down to suggestions—she might have taken it for a boundary marker or a drowned chapel's last tooth. But as the barge slid abreast of it, the stone against Gideon's chest went hot.

Not warm. *Hot.* A coal laid bare against her skin, and she gasped and clapped a hand over her breastbone and felt it through the cloth, felt it *pulsing*, a slow rhythm like a heartbeat that wasn't hers, and the cord she'd braided began to smoke.

"Tamsin—"

"I see it." The smuggler's voice had gone very quiet and very level, the voice of a woman calculating distances to the far bank. "What is that? Because if my boat's about to be a bonfire, I want to renegotiate the fee."

"I don't—" Gideon's hands were moving without her permission, tugging the cord over her head, and the stone came free into her palm and it was *blazing*, light leaking out between her fingers in seams of deep ember-orange, and every instinct bred into her by twenty years of hiding screamed *put it away, hide it, bury it*—but her fingers would not close. The stone would not be hidden. It rose, actually rose, a hair's breadth off her palm, straining toward the bank like a compass needle finding north, and where it pointed, the air above the ruined shrine had begun to shimmer.

The shimmer resolved into a seam. A vertical line of paler air, thin as a knife cut, hanging over the masonry, and through it Gideon could see—not the far bank, not the sky—something else. Warmth. A glow like a hearth seen through a cracked door.

"Oh," Tamsin said softly. All the calculation had gone out of her voice. "Oh, that's not contraband. That's a door."

The stone pulled. Not hard—it pulled the way sleep pulls, the way memory pulls—and Gideon found she had crossed the deck without deciding to, that Tamsin had thrown a rope around a mooring stump without being asked, that her boots were on the muddy bank and the mist was closing behind her and the seam of light stood open an arm's length away, breathing warmth onto her face.

"Don't," Tamsin said, and for the first time there was something other than transaction in it. "Hey. Scribe. Whatever your mother gave you, it doesn't get to walk you into a hole in the world without asking."

"She didn't give it to me." Gideon heard her own voice as if from a distance. "She died when I was nine. I found this in her things when I was fourteen. I thought—I thought it was just a keepsake. I've carried it for eleven years and it has never once done *anything*."

"And now?"

"Now it remembers where it is." Gideon stepped closer to the seam. The warmth was extraordinary—not heat exactly, but the feeling of heat, the way a cold room remembers a fire. "It's a covenant-stone. I read about them once, in a folio Brannoc kept locked—the Ember-Veil accords, the signing that ended the Sundering War. Seven signatories. Seven stones cut from the first hearth of both realms, so each realm could always find the door to the other." Her throat tightened. "Everyone says the Veil's been quiet for two hundred years. Sealed. Dormant. Everyone says the doors don't open anymore."

"And yet." Tamsin gestured at the hole in the world with the weary air of a woman whose cargo manifests had just become useless.

"And yet." Gideon lifted the stone. It strained toward the seam, and the seam *answered*, widening by a finger's width, and inside she saw movement now—shapes, slow and flickering, like figures seen through flame. "It's not opening the door. It's... knocking. And something on the other side is answering because it recognizes who's holding it."

"Do you know who's holding it?"

"No," Gideon said honestly. "But I think it does."

She touched the seam.

There was no falling. There was no crossing, either, not really—one moment the mist and the mud and Tamsin's alarmed shout, and the next she was standing in a great hall built of black stone veined with firelight, and the hall was full of people, and none of them could see her.

They were signing something.

Seven figures stood around a table of fused glass—actual fused glass, sand melted to mirror by some heat beyond any furnace—and upon the table lay a document unlike anything in Gideon's training. It was not written. It was *growing*. Letters of living ember crawled across its surface like glowing insects arranging themselves, and each time one settled, the whole page brightened, and Gideon understood with a scribe's sick delight and horror that she was looking at a text that wrote itself, that had perhaps been writing itself since the beginning of both worlds, and that these seven people were about to add their names to it.

Six she did not know. A broad man with a smith's forearms and burn-scarred knuckles. A woman in traveling robes stiff with frost, breath steaming though the hall was warm. An old soldier missing an arm, his empty sleeve pinned with a medal. A pair of twins, ember-realm folk by their look, their skin shot through with faint veins of light like cooling lava. A young man who kept glancing at the document as if afraid it would bite him.

And the seventh—

The seventh was her mother.

Not older. Not idealized. Exactly as Gideon remembered her from the ragged edge of memory: dark hair going gray at the temples too early, ink stains on her fingers that no scrubbing ever fully lifted, a small scar through one eyebrow from a childhood fall she used to make up stories about. Maera Amberdale, scribe of Ashen Vale, who had died of a winter fever when Gideon was nine, who had baked terrible bread and corrected Gideon's letterforms with a patience that never frayed, who had told her bedtime stories about a land of hearths and gentle fire and had always, always stopped at the same point in the tale and said *the rest is grown-up business, little ember*.

She stood at the glass table with a stylus of white metal, and she was afraid. Gideon could see it—the set of her shoulders, the careful steadiness of her breathing, the way her eyes kept going to the others as if seeking permission to be terrified. She was maybe thirty. Which meant this vision was no memory of Gideon's childhood. Which meant either the stone was lying, or her mother had stood in this hall two hundred years ago, and Gideon's entire understanding of her own life was kindling.

The smith spoke first, his voice like a hammer on iron. "Once we sign, it cannot be unsigned. My fires will dim to embers and stay embers, and yours will learn to live beside ours without burning. Both realms breathe, neither devours. Is that the accord?"

"That is the accord," said the frost-woman.

"Then let the record show," said the old soldier, "that we did not come here to win. We came here to stop."

Her mother laughed then—a small, cracked, utterly familiar laugh, the laugh Gideon had spent fifteen years failing to forget—and said, "A scribe's dream. A document no one can forge and no one can erase." She looked down at the self-writing page. "Will it take our names truly? Not ink. Not imitation?"

"It takes what you give it," said one of the lava-veined twins, and her voice was kind and sad. "That is its mercy and its price. Give it a name, and it holds the name safe between the worlds forever. Nothing can steal it. Nothing can change it." A pause, delicate as ash settling. "Nothing can return it, either. What the covenant holds, it holds."

Silence in the black hall. Gideon watched her mother's hand tighten on the stylus until the knuckles went pale.

"Maera," said the smith gently. "You can refuse. We only need six. The seventh is courtesy, not necessity."

"The seventh is a scribe," her mother said. "Every covenant needs someone whose whole craft is keeping words intact. If I sign, then as long as I hold my stone, the accord can always be *read*. Found. Renewed, if it must be." She smiled, and it wobbled, and Gideon's heart broke along the same fault line it had broken at nine years old. "Besides. Someone should leave a child something better than a war."

She signed.

The stylus touched the page and the ember-letters surged up to meet it, and light poured out of the document the way water pours from a breached dam, and the seven stones on the table—seven rough-cut hearts of hearthstone—flared in answer, and the Veil itself seemed to inhale. Gideon felt it even outside the vision, felt it in the stone against her palm: a vast, slow settling, two worlds leaning together and agreeing, for once, to carry each other.

Then the hall blurred. Years poured past like water through fingers—she saw the shrines built, dozens of them, the thin places tended like gardens; she saw her mother older, gray to the roots now, teaching a hooded figure in a scriptorium (Brannoc—young Brannoc, with both eyes unlined) how to tend a stone that was not hers; she saw the visits dwindle, the shrines moss over, the ember-light in the seams grow dimmer year by year, decade by decade,
