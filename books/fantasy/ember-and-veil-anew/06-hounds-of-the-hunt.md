## 6. Hounds of the Hunt

The hounds lost the scent at the river's edge, and Serah Vole was almost grateful.

She stood in the reeds where the Ashen Vale's last tributary slid brown and slow toward the lowlands, watching her two tracker-hounds—Briar and the younger one, Cinder—snuffle along the mud with their ears flat. The morning fog had not yet burned off, and it lay over the water like a sheet drawn up to the chin of something sleeping. Somewhere downstream, a barge horn sounded, long and mournful, and Serah felt the sound settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well.

"She went by water," said Dree, her second, crouching at the bank. He turned over a scrap of oilcloth snagged on a willow root, sniffed it, grimaced. "Smuggler's wrapping. Cheap stuff, river-grade. There'll be a crew that took coin for her."

"Then we find the crew." Serah heard her own voice and disliked it: flat, clipped, the voice of a hunt-captain of Vessic's new order. It did not sound like hers. It sounded like the uniform she wore—the grey-and-black livery with the iron sunburst at the collar, the badge that had been pinned on her six weeks ago in a courtyard smelling of wet stone and fear.

Cinder looked back at her from the waterline, tail low, whining once. The dog had liked Gideon Amberdale. Dogs always had. That was one of the many small betrayals Serah had never learned how to carry—that even the animals remembered the girl more kindly than the regime allowed her to be remembered.

"Captain?" Dree was watching her. He was a good soldier, Dree—thirty years old, patient, a farmer's son from the barley country who had joined Vessic's hunt because the hunt paid in grain as well as silver, and his mother's village had eaten through its winter stores by mid-autumn. Everyone in the hunt had a story like that. That was how Vessic built an army out of a usurpation: he fed people.

"Mount up," Serah said. "We follow the towpath south. Every landing, every ferry, every fish-weir. Someone moved a fugitive down this river, and someone took money to do it."

* * *

They rode south through country that had once been beautiful and was now merely obedient.

Serah had grown up in these hills, in the shadow of the Scriptorium's bell tower, in a house with a slate roof and a garden where her father grew beans up strings of twine. She had known Gideon Amberdale since they were nine years old, when Gideon's mother had brought her to the temple school and the two girls had been seated alphabetically—Amberdale before Vole—and Gideon had immediately, solemnly, slid her inkwell across the desk because Serah's had cracked in her satchel.

That was the whole of their beginning: an inkwell passed across a desk. Serah had thought about that inkwell more times in the past six weeks than she had thought about it in the previous fifteen years.

They had shared books after that. That was the true architecture of their friendship—not adventures, not secrets whispered under blankets, but books. Gideon read fast and carelessly, skipping ahead, spoiling endings; Serah read slowly and completely, and so they made a bargain: Gideon would read aloud only what Serah had already read, and Serah would let Gideon guess at what came next until she begged. They read the old romances, the dry chronicles, a forbidden translation of ember-realm hearth-tales that Gideon's mother kept wrapped in wax cloth beneath a floorboard. Serah could still recite three of those tales from memory. She had not dared to speak them aloud since the Proclamation, since hearing them now would be like carrying lit coals in her mouth.

Her father had died two winters ago. Her mother followed him within the year, and the house with the bean-garden had gone to a cousin who sold it within the month. So when Vessic's recruiters came to the garrison town offering bread rations for a family's worth of loyalty, Serah had signed her name on a line, and the line had turned out to be longer than any page she'd ever copied.

That was the arithmetic she carried now, riding south with forty riders behind her: on one side of the ledger, the memory of a girl sliding an inkwell across a desk; on the other side, four hundred soldiers' families eating bread stamped with the iron sunburst. She knew which side the world would call heavier. She was no longer certain which side *she* did.

* * *

The first landing was a mud-flat called Ferrier's Stair, where a rope-ferry crossed to the orchard country. The ferryman, a stooped man named Osk, met them at the plank-walk with his hat in his hands and his eyes doing the careful arithmetic of a man calculating how much trouble honesty was worth.

Serah dismounted and let him look at the sunburst on her collar. She had learned that the badge did half her work; men saw it and began confessing to things she hadn't asked about.

"When did you last carry passengers south?" she asked.

"Passengers, Captain? I carry apples. Apples and wool." Osk's gaze flicked—just once, just slightly—downstream.

Dree stepped forward and set a silver mark on the ferry rail. Then another. Then a third. "For the truth," Dree said mildly. "And there's more where that came from. And less where your ferry comes from, if the truth comes late."

Osk looked at the silver, then at Serah, and something in his face shifted—the particular resignation of a small man caught between large powers. "Two nights ago," he said. "A woman. Young. Scribe's hands, ink-stained. Hooded. Paid in old coin—temple coin, if you follow me. A smuggler crew took her aboard a flat-bottomed barge, black hull, green trim. They were running wool, they said." He swallowed. "I didn't know she was wanted, Captain. On my mother's grave."

"What was the crew's name?"

"They fly no name, Captain. But the woman at the tiller—" Osk hesitated, and Serah watched him decide, in real time, whether the silver outweighed whatever fear held his tongue. Silver won. "Red hair. Scar through the eyebrow. They call her Tamsin Roe, down-river. Runs the Salt Road water. Fast boat, mean crew, pays her debts." He paused. "Pays most of them."

Serah wrote the name in her field book, and her hand was steady, and she hated how steady it was. *Tamsin Roe.* A name to hunt. It occurred to her, distantly, that eighteen months ago she had been a person who lent books to children at the temple school, and now she was a person who paid silver to peel open strangers' lives like fruit. She did not know when the change had happened. Changes like that never announced themselves. They simply arrived, fully grown, like weather.

"Thank you, Osk." She left the silver on the rail. "If anyone else asks you about that barge, you told them nothing. If I learn you told them otherwise, the sunburst will remember."

He nodded quickly, hat crushed against his chest, and she mounted and rode on, and did not look back at him, because looking back meant seeing what she had become reflected in his face.

* * *

They made the toll-bridge at Marrow Ford by late afternoon, and there Serah found the thing that bent her ledger harder than anything before it.

Vessic's proclamations had been nailed to the bridge posts in fresh paste—a realm-wide hunt for the gifted, silver for denunciations, the usual litany. But beneath the official notice, someone had posted a bill of a different kind. Hand-lettered, careful, in a scribe's neat hand:

**MISSING — BRANNOC WICK, SCRIBE OF THE ASHEN VALE SCRIPTORIUM. LAST SEEN IN CUSTODY OF THE HUNT. ANY WHO KNOW HIS WHEREFORE, SPEAK TO THE TEMPLE REMNANT AT DUSKHARBOR. HE TAUGHT US ALL OUR LETTERS.**

Below it, smaller, almost shyly added: **He is seventy-one years old and walks with a cane. Whatever he has done, the cane has done worse to him.**

Serah stood before the notice for a long time. Behind her, her riders waited in disciplined silence, because discipline was all they had that was theirs.

Brannoc Wick. She knew the name the way she knew rain. Every child in the Vale had learned letters under Brannoc's ferocious kindness—he had caned knuckles for sloppy serifs and then wept, actually wept, when a poor student finally mastered a capital B. He had taught Serah. He had taught Gideon. He had once told fourteen-year-old Serah, after she'd copied out a psalter flawlessly, that she had "the steadiest hand and the most stubborn heart in the class, and God help whichever one wins."

She had ordered the raid on the Scriptorium. Her signature was on the warrant. She had stood in the archive stacks while her soldiers bound old men and carried out ledgers to burn, and she had told herself the words that all of Vessic's officers told themselves: *orders are orders; mercy is a luxury of peacetime; the families eat.*

But she had not known Brannoc was among the taken. Or rather—she had known the Scriptorium housed him, and she had not let herself know it, which is a different sin wearing the same coat.

"Captain?" Dree again, quiet. "Orders?"

Serah looked at the notice a moment longer. Then she reached up and tore it down.

Dree's breath caught audibly. Around her, forty riders shifted.

"I didn't do that," Serah said, folding the paper into her breast pocket, over her heart, where it lay like a coal. "No one did. Paper rots in the rain. Write it in your report however you like." She swung into the saddle. "We ride for the Salt Road crossing. The Roe woman's barge will put in at the saltmarsh shrines—smugglers always do, for the tide-chapels' blessing fees. We'll be there first."

"And the fugitive, Captain?"

Serah looked south, where the fog was lifting off the hills and the road ran straight as a sentence toward the sea.

"The fugitive is a scribe with stolen property and a dead woman's trinket," she said. "Nothing more. Move."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow above the river, and Serah could not sleep.

She sat apart from the picket fires, on a fallen log slick with moss, and took out the things she should have burned months ago: a bundle of letters tied in twine, and beneath them, wrapped in the same wax cloth Gideon's mother had once used for the hearth-tales, a single book. *The Lamps of the Ember Court,* a child's edition, woodcut illustrations, the spine broken from a hundred readings. Gideon had given it to her when they were eleven. *So you'll stop being frightened of the dark,* Gideon had said, deadly serious. *It's not empty, you know. It's full of lamps. You just have to be the kind of person a lamp will show itself to.*

Serah turned the pages by moonlight. The woodcuts showed the ember-realm as children imagined it: little hearths with faces, spirits shaped like sparks, a great Veil rendered as a curtain of woven light with knots of gold at every seam. In the margin beside the picture of the Veil, in a child's careful pencil, Gideon had written: *this is my favorite part. imagine holding it together.*

Imagine holding it together. Serah closed the book and pressed it to her sternum and let herself, for exactly sixty heartbeats, feel everything she had refused to feel since the raid: the grief for Brannoc in whatever cell they kept him; the shame of the warrant with her name on it; the cold knowledge that Gideon Amberdale—book-sharer, inkwell-lender, girl who believed lamps chose their people—was alive somewhere downriver, hunted by *her*, specifically her, because Vessic's spymasters had decided that the childhood friend made the perfect hound. They had chosen her for this assignment deliberately. She understood that now. Who better to track a fox than another fox raised in the same earth?

And the worst of it—the part that bent her loyalty until it groaned—was that she was good at it. She had found the ferryman in half a day. She would find the barge tomorrow. She would find Gideon, because finding people was what the iron sunburst had made her for.

Unless.

She took out the torn proclamation and smoothed it on her knee. Beneath the official seal, in the fine print she had helped draft and had never truly read, was the clause: *property of the condemned to revert to the Crown, including covenant-tokens, relics, and stones of binding.* Stones of binding. She had signed that phrase without asking what it meant. Now she turned it over in her mind like a coin of unknown minting, and the more she turned it, the more it seemed to catch light that wasn't there.

Why would a temple scribe—a forger of charms, a seller of blessings, small fry, minnows—why would *she* matter enough to warrant a hunt-captain, forty riders, and a standing order from the palace itself that the fugitive be taken *alive and unharmed*?

You did not order a living capture for a charm-seller. You ordered it for something a crown wanted whole.

Serah looked at the fire, at her sleeping riders, at the dark river running south with its cargo of fog. The ledger in her chest tipped, quietly, without ceremony—one weight sliding to the other side the way a scale tips when no one is watching.

Tomorrow she would ride for the saltmarsh shrines. What she would do when she got there, she genuinely did not know. Perhaps she would take the fugitive as ordered. Perhaps she would ask a question first—the question she should have asked in the Scriptorium stacks, with the smoke rising: *Gideon, what did your mother leave you, and why does the king want it?*

Perhaps asking the question was already treason. She suspected it was. She suspected she had committed it the moment she tore down the notice, or the moment she pocketed the child's book instead of burning it, or—truthfully, if she was honest, if she was the kind of honest Brannoc had caned knuckles for—she suspected the treason had begun years ago, at a desk, with an inkwell passed across the wood by a girl who believed the dark was full of lamps.

Serah wrapped the book away, banked the fire, and lay down in her cloak with her sword unbuckled for the first time since her commissioning.

Downriver, unseen, the fog rolled on toward the sea, carrying whatever it carried.
