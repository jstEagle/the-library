## 7. The Free City of Duskharbor

The fog came first.

Gideon had heard the sailors on Tamsin's ketch speak of it for two days as they beat down the coast—*Duskharbor's breath*, they called it, a grey shroud that rolled off the sea each evening and swallowed the city whole, so that a stranger arriving at night might walk its streets and never see a single face, only hear them: the slap of water against pilings, the clink of rigging, a hundred languages braided together in market-cry and curse. She had imagined it would feel like freedom, that fog. A place where no one knew her name, where witch-hunters' writ ran thin, where a scribe with dangerous hands could simply be another body in the crowd.

She had not imagined it would feel like drowning.

She stood at the rail as the ketch slid through the harbor mouth, and the fog closed over the deck like a lid. Lamps appeared out of nothing—hanging from unseen poles, swaying on unseen prows of anchored ships—and then vanished again behind the grey. Somewhere close, a bell tolled three slow strokes, and somewhere farther, something answered it, and Gideon understood that the bells were how this city saw: a blind creature feeling its own edges in the dark.

"You'll want to keep your hand on your purse," Tamsin Roe said beside her, not looking at her. The smuggler's eyes were on the water, on the shapes of other vessels sliding past close enough to touch, their crews visible only as voices and lantern-glow. "Half these boats are honest trade. The other half will board you for your boots if you drift too near."

"And which are you?"

Tamsin's mouth curved. "I'm the third kind. The kind that gets paid." She nodded toward the looming dark ahead. "There. Saltquay. Best landing for someone who doesn't want to be seen arriving."

Gideon's hand went to the satchel at her hip, to the shape within it that had grown heavier with every mile downstream—the covenant-stone, her mother's stone, warm even through oiled leather, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. Since the shrine on the river, since the visions, she had hardly slept. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the long table of the first signing, the nine figures robed in ember-light, and among them a woman whose face was her own face worn by twenty extra years, whose hands moved in the same small way hers did when they were nervous, tucking a strand of hair back, pressing fingertips briefly together.

*You are the last,* the vision had said, or the stone had said through the vision, and Gideon still did not know what she was last *of*, exactly, only that the word sat on her chest like a stone of its own.

The keel ground against sand. Hands she hadn't seen helped her over the side onto a quay slick with fish-scale and brine, and then Tamsin was beside her, coin-purse already counting itself in her palm.

"This is where I leave you," the smuggler said. "Contract was river passage and one night's berth. We're past both."

Gideon turned. In the lamplight leaking through the fog, Tamsin Roe looked younger than she pretended to be and older than she probably was, all at once—a woman built out of transactions, every softness mortgaged. "You said you'd help me find the Council."

"I said I'd get you here. Finding the Council is your own fool errand." But Tamsin hesitated, and Gideon watched the hesitation like a crack in a wall. "Saltquay Steps, up past the chandlery. There's a tavern called the Drowned Lantern. Ask for the room above it. If anyone asks why you're asking, say you've come about a debt. Everyone in this city has come about a debt; no one will look twice."

"Why are you telling me this? You said you were leaving."

"I am leaving." Tamsin flipped a coin, caught it, and pressed it into Gideon's palm—warm from her hand, stamped with a face Gideon didn't recognize, some dead king of some drowned nation. "Consider it an investment. If your stone is what I think it is, I'd rather be owed a favor by you than have sold you short." She stepped back into the fog, and her voice came drifting after, wry and fading: "Don't die before I can collect!"

And then there was only the fog, and the coin in Gideon's hand, and the covenant-stone breathing warmth against her ribs.

* * *

The Drowned Lantern was exactly the sort of place that existed everywhere and nowhere: low-beamed, smoke-dark, smelling of tar and stewed eels, full of people who did not look up when strangers entered because looking up was a habit that got people killed. Gideon climbed the stairs at the back, found the door marked with a chalked lantern-sign, and knocked the pattern the barkeep had grunted at her—two, pause, three.

"Come about a debt?" said a voice inside.

"So I'm told."

The door opened on a small round room and a small round woman with iron-grey hair cropped short and hands stained to the wrist with ink of a color Gideon had never seen—a deep violet-black that seemed to move slightly when she wasn't looking directly at it. The woman looked Gideon over once, head to boots, the way a horse-trader looks at a yearling.

"Scribe," the woman said. It wasn't a question. "Ashen Vale scriptorium, by the callus on your middle finger. Left hand, so you were trained old-style. And you're carrying something under that satchel-flap that my ink can smell from across the room." Her eyes flicked down. "Covenant-work. Real covenant-work. Saints' teeth, girl, do you know how long it's been since real covenant-work walked into this city?"

"My mother's name was Maren Amberdale," Gideon said. "I'm told the Council keeps a registry of signatories. I want to see it."

Something changed in the woman's face—not warmth exactly, but a settling, like a scale finding its weight. "Maren Amberdale." She said the name slowly, tasting it. "Yes. Yes, that name is in the registry. Come in, child, and shut the door. Name's Odile Vess. I keep the Council's records, which means I keep the Council's secrets, which lately means I keep the Council's lies." She gestured to a chair. "Sit. And then tell me everything, and leave nothing out, because the Council is going to ask you questions far less polite than mine."

So Gideon told her. The Scriptorium. The raid. Brannoc's face as they took him, the archive stacks burning, Serah Vole's voice calling her name through the smoke. The river. The shrine. The visions. She left out nothing, and when she finished, Odile Vess sat very still for a long moment, her moving-ink hands folded on the table.

"They took Brannoc," she said finally. "Old Brannoc, who taught half the free cities' archivists their letters. Vessic's hunters took him alive." Her jaw tightened. "That means they're questioning him. That means they know more than we hoped and less than they want. And now his last student walks into Duskharbor wearing a signatory's stone." She looked up. "Child, I must ask you plainly. Do you understand what you're carrying?"

"No," Gideon said. "Not entirely. I understand it matters. I understand people have died for it. I understand that I dreamed of it before I ever touched it, which frightens me more than the hunters do."

Odile almost smiled. "Honest. Good. The Council gets enough liars without importing them." She rose, snuffed the lamp, and opened a panel in the wall that Gideon would have sworn was solid plaster. Beyond it, stairs descended into darkness that smelled of cold stone and old paper. "Council convenes tonight, below the Tidehall. They'll want to see you. Some of them will want to *use* you. Remember the difference."

"What difference?" Gideon asked, following her down.

"The ones who want to use you will smile while they do it."

* * *

The chamber beneath the Tidehall had been a cistern once, Gideon thought—vaulted, dripping, ringed with channels where seawater still crept and retreated with some tide deeper than the harbor's. Nine chairs stood in a broken crescent around a central table, and only five of the chairs were filled. Three stood empty with their seats draped in black cloth. One had been smashed, its fragments left lying where they'd fallen, a message in wreckage that everyone present politely ignored.

The five who remained did not look like saviors. They looked like tired, clever, frightened people dressed well enough to hide it, and Gideon felt her heart sink before a single word was spoken, because she had spent seven years in a scriptorium and she knew what councils looked like from the inside: they looked like this.

"Gideon Amberdale." The speaker sat at the crescent's center—a broad man with a magistrate's voice and a merchant's rings, silver at every knuckle. "Lord Aldous Crane. First Chair of the Covenant Council. You honor us."

*He rehearsed that,* Gideon thought. *The phrasing, the pause. He has been practicing saying my name.*

"Lord Crane." She kept her voice level. "I've come to—"

"Yes, yes, Odile's report reached us an hour ago." Crane waved a ringed hand, and Gideon noticed the others exchange glances at the gesture—at the ease of it, the ownership. "The stone, the visions, the lineage. Remarkable. Remarkable. The Council is, of course, deeply moved by your arrival, and eager to discuss how best to proceed." He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes and stopped there, like a guest at a border. "But first, perhaps, some context. Do you know, Mistress Amberdale, what became of the original nine signatories?"

"Some died at the Breaking," Gideon said. "My mother among them, I believe. The rest scattered."

"Four died at the Breaking. Two died after, hunted. Your mother was the seventh." Crane's voice softened into something almost gentle. "One renounced and lives under a false name—we know where; we do not press. And the ninth—" he gestured at the empty chairs, the black cloth, the wreckage "—was Councillor Ede Vayle, who sat in this chamber eleven years ago and argued, as several of us argue now, that the Veil should never be reforged at all. She was murdered for that position. By persons unknown. Possibly by persons in this room." He let that hang in the dripping air. "So you see, we are a council with ghosts. I want you to understand that before anyone makes you promises."

"Then let me understand the positions," Gideon said. "Plainly. What does the Council want?"

Silence, of the particular quality that meant everyone had been waiting for someone else to answer first.

It came from the chair at Crane's right hand: a lean woman with a burn-scarred jaw and the bearing of a soldier, though she wore scholar's robes. "Seal it," the woman said flatly. "Reforging is impossible—we've known that since Ede Vayle's research burned with her. Sealing is possible. Close the Veil forever, cut the ember-realm loose, and our world survives on its own strength. Painful. Necessary. I am Magister Corrin Hale, and I have buried forty gifted folk in six years, Mistress Amberdale, because Vessic blames our kind for the Veil's weakness. Seal it, and give the usurper no cause, and maybe the killing stops."

"Or," said the fourth voice, dry as old vellum, from the crescent's far end—a tiny man, ancient, wrapped in so many shawls he seemed mostly textile, "we stop pretending the ember-realm is a wound to be cauterized. It is a *world*. It is dying, yes, but dying things can be fed. The Veil was never a wall, girl—it was a hearth shared between two rooms. Break it open, channel the ember-fire deliberately, and both realms draw from it. Our world gets power beyond anything Vessic dreams of. Theirs gets warmth. Everyone eats." He smiled with too few teeth. "I am Quill-master Fenwick. I represent those of us who think fear is a poor architect."

"Fear built every wall that ever saved a life," Hale snapped.

"And starved everyone behind it," Fenwick returned pleasantly.

"And the fifth chair?" Gideon asked quietly.

Everyone looked at the fifth occupied seat, which until now had been silent: a young man, barely older than Gideon herself, elegant, watchful, with the pale polished look of someone raised in rooms where being overlooked was survival. He met her gaze without hurry.

"Dorian Sel," he said. "I hold the swing vote, as it happens. Which means everyone in this room has been very charming to me for eleven years." He glanced at Crane, at Hale, at Fenwick, and something flickered across his face that Gideon recognized, because she had worn it herself for seven years behind a scribe's desk: the look of a person doing arithmetic with other people's lives. "Magister Hale wants to seal the Veil because sealing requires nothing of us but courage at the end. Quill-master Fenwick wants to break it open because breaking it offers power, and power is honest about what it is. Lord Crane—" a thin pause "—has not yet told you what *he* wants. Ask him. He's been waiting for you to ask."

Crane's smile did not waver, but his rings clicked softly against the arm of his chair. "Master Sel is being dramatic."

"Ask him," Sel repeated, to Gideon, gently.

Gideon looked at Lord Aldous Crane, First Chair, silver-ringed, rehearsed. "What do you want?"

"To save this realm," Crane said smoothly. "By whatever means prove most durable. Sealing is clean but leaves us weak before Vessic's armies. Opening is powerful but unstable—I've read Ede Vayle's surviving notes, Mistress Amberdale, and uncontrolled ember-fire burns cities. But a *third* path exists. A reforging, done properly, under Council authority, with the covenant bound not to a person but to an institution. To us. Perpetual, governed, accountable." He spread his ringed hands. "Your mother and eight others made a covenant and then died and left the world to fend with the consequences. We propose simply to finish their work—with safeguards."

"With control," Gideon said.

"With stewardship."

"With yourselves holding the key." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and she felt the stone at her hip pulse warm, once, like a heartbeat answering anger. "Forgive me, Lord Crane, but I've spent seven years forging ledgers. I know what 'stewardship' looks like when the stewards write their own accounts."

The silence that followed had teeth. Magister Hale's scarred jaw twitched—toward approval, Gideon thought, or something adjacent to it. Fenwick chuckled like a dry leaf. And Crane's smile finally, finally faltered, just at one corner, just enough.

"Child," he said, "you misunderstand your position. You are not our guest. You are our *instrument*. The last signatory's heir walks into our chamber carrying the only covenant-stone left in existence, and you imagine you have standing to lecture this Council on—"

"On what the stone is for?" Gideon stood. Her voice shook, and she let it shake; hiding it had gotten people killed. "I stood in a burning archive two weeks ago because your Council's secrecy taught me nothing—because no one told a temple scribe in Ashen Vale that the Veil mattered, that the gifts mattered, that any of it was real. My mentor is in Vessic's chains. My friend hunts me. And the great Covenant Council of the free cities sits in a flooded cellar arguing about who gets to hold the key while the fire on the other side of the sky goes out." She lifted the satchel, and drew out the stone, and set it on the wet table between them, where it lay glowing faintly, stubbornly, like a coal refusing the dark. "I didn't come here to be used. I came here because this was the only door left. But hear me—all of you—if the choice comes to handing this to a council that smiles like Lord Crane, or a magister who wants to bolt the door on a dying world, or a quill-master who smells profit in the smoke, or whatever Master Sel is waiting to become—then I will take this stone back out into the fog and find the door myself."

Nobody spoke. Water dripped. The tide-channels breathed.

It was Dorian Sel who broke it, rising unhurried, adjusting his cuffs. "Well," he said lightly, to the room, "now we know she's genuine. No heirloom would survive that speech intact unless the blood was true." He inclined his head to Gideon, courteous as a duelist. "Mistress Amberdale. The Council will deliberate. You'll be lodged in the Tidehall under Odile's care—for your protection, naturally." His eyes held hers a moment longer than courtesy required, and in them Gideon saw the arithmetic again, turning, turning. "Do try to sleep. I suspect you'll need your strength sooner than any of us would like."

* * *

They gave her a cell that had been a monk's cell once, high in the Tidehall's seaward tower, with a narrow bed, a shelf of salt-swollen books, and a window that looked out over the fog-drowned harbor where lamps bloomed and vanished like thoughts. Odile brought her bread and stewed eels and a flask of something that burned pleasantly, and stood a while at the window with her, saying nothing.

"Sanctuary," Gideon said at last. "Is that what this is?"

"It's what they'll call it." Odile's violet-stained fingers drummed the sill. "In my experience, child, sanctuary is just a cage with better acoustics. Crane will move against you within the month—he needs that stone bound to Council authority before anyone asks him hard questions about where the Council's funds have gone. Hale believes every word she says, which makes her the most dangerous person in the building. Fenwick would sell the Veil's hinges for a seat at Vessic's table if the price were right. And Sel—" she paused "—Sel I've watched for eleven years, and I still couldn't tell you what he wants, which frightens me more than all the rest combined."

"And you? What do you want, Odile?"

The old record-keeper was quiet for a long moment. Out in the fog, a bell tolled the hour, and something answered it across the water.

"I want what your mother wanted," she said finally. "Which was never a sealed door nor an open one. It was a *kept* fire. Someone tending it. Someone paying the cost honestly, in the open, where the world could see the price and choose." She turned from the window, and her eyes were very old and very tired. "Sleep, Gideon Amberdale. Tomorrow the Council deliberates, and the day after, likely, someone will try to steal that stone. And sooner or later—my ink says soon—you'll have word from Ashen Vale. Hunters don't lose a scent like yours. Not with Captain Vole on it."

At the name, the stone pulsed against Gideon's ribs—warm, insistent, almost like warning—and outside, far out in the fog where the harbor met the open sea, a lamp that had been steady for hours suddenly went dark.

Gideon stood at the window a long time, watching the space where it had been, and could not make herself believe it was nothing.
