## 8. Lessons in Broken Magic

The tower Ondrell had claimed in Duskharbor's drowned quarter leaned like a drunkard against its neighbors, three stories of salt-eaten stone that the tide still visited twice a day, filling the ground floor with cold green water and the smell of dying kelp. Gideon climbed the exterior stair each morning before dawn, her boots slick with spray, and each morning she half expected to find the old mage gone—dissolved into rumor like everything else the Council had discarded.

He was always there. That was the first unsettling thing about Ondrell: his permanence. Everything else in her life had proven perishable—the Scriptorium a shell of scorched shelves, Brannoc somewhere in Vessic's dungeons or worse, her mother's face fading even as the covenant-stone grew heavier in her satchel. But Ondrell endured, hunched over his worktable among jars of preserved moths and bottled tides, as if exile were simply another room he'd grown comfortable in.

"You're late," he said this morning without looking up. His voice was dry as old vellum. "The tide turned a quarter hour ago. A binding made on a turning tide sets crooked."

"I was watching the harbor." She set down her satchel and unwrapped the stone from its cloth. It sat on his scarred table like a sleeping animal, dark and faintly warm. "There are soldiers at the fish-gate now. Vessic's colors. Someone in the Council is selling information."

"Someone in the Council has been selling information since before you were born, girl. The difference is that now they're selling it to a man who intends to use it." He finally looked up, and his eyes—milk-pale, veined like river ice—found hers with uncomfortable precision. "Sit. Hands flat on the table. We begin where all bindings begin."

"Which is?"

"With what you're willing to lose."

* * *

She had thought, in the weeks since Ashen Vale, that she understood sacrifice. She had burned her charm-stock rather than let it be found. She had paid Tamsin Roe nearly every coin she'd hoarded across six years of forged ledgers and sold blessings. She had watched Brannoc dragged out of the archive stacks with his hands bound behind him, calling her name—or not calling it, which was worse, because his silence had been a gift, an attempt to keep her unmentioned, and she had run anyway, down through the burning stacks while the past went up in smoke behind her.

Ondrell disabused her of these notions within the hour.

"A covenant," he said, arranging seven small stones in a circle on the table between them, "is not a spell. Spells spend power. Covenants spend *self*. Do you understand the distinction?"

"I think so."

"You don't. No one does, until they've felt it." He tapped the first stone. "When you cast one of your little charms—and don't flinch, girl, I know exactly what you sold in that market square; light like that doesn't come from blessing—you draw from your gift, which replenishes, like a well refilled by rain. A covenant draws from something that does not refill. A memory. A name. A love. A year of your life. Something given is something gone, permanently, and the binding lives on what you no longer have."

Gideon looked at the seven stones. River-smoothed, ordinary. "And the Ember-Veil? What did the original signatories give?"

His pale eyes didn't waver. "That," he said, "we will come to when you're ready to hear it. Today we practice with pebbles."

* * *

The first binding was simple: make the stone remember warmth.

Ondrell heated it over his lamp until it was almost too hot to touch, then set it before her. "Hold it. Not with your hands—with your gift. Tell it what warmth is. Then take the warmth away and ask it to keep the memory."

Gideon closed her eyes. Her magic rose easily, familiar as ink under a nib—a warm pressure behind her sternum, flowing down her arms. She wrapped the stone in it, felt its heat, its grain, the way it held the lamplight's ghost. Then she drew back, pulling her gift away like a hand withdrawing from water, and spoke the small words Ondrell had taught her. *Keep. Hold. Remember.*

The stone went cold in her palm.

Not gradually—all at once, as if the warmth had never touched it. She frowned and tried again, slower, more careful, tracing the binding syllable by syllable the way she'd once learned difficult scripts, letter by letter until the whole word lay clean on the page.

The second stone forgot faster than the first.

"The problem," Ondrell said, watching her with maddening calm, "is that you're giving instructions. You're a scribe; you want to write the binding clearly, legibly, so any reader could follow it. But a stone is not a reader. A covenant is not a document. It is a wound that remembers being a promise."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. "Say it differently, then."

"No." He folded his hands. "I will say it once more, and then you will fail for the rest of the morning, and the failing will teach you what my words cannot. A binding holds only what is truly given. If you give the stone a copy of warmth, it keeps nothing. If you give it the warmth itself—your warmth, taken from yourself and never returned—it will hold heat for a hundred years."

Gideon stared at him. "You're saying I have to hurt myself to teach a pebble a lesson."

"I'm saying that everything worth binding costs. Yes." For a moment something moved behind the milk-pale eyes—not cruelty, but an old, tired grief, the look of a man who had paid many prices and kept the receipts. "You asked to learn this. The Council wants a weapon or a symbol; I don't know which. But you came to me asking how covenants are reforged, and this is the first lesson, and there is no gentler version."

* * *

She failed eleven more times that morning.

The twelfth time, she did what Ondrell had actually asked. She held the stone and let her gift sink into it, and instead of describing warmth to it, she opened the place inside herself where warmth lived—the memory of the Scriptorium's hearth in winter, Brannoc's spiced tea pressed into her hands, the specific golden weight of a fire built against the dark. And she gave it. Not the memory of it, not a description, but the warmth itself, drawn up out of that remembered hearth and poured into the stone, feeling something go out of her as it left—a dimming, small but real, like a candle carried from one room to another.

*Keep,* she said. *Hold. Remember.*

The stone stayed warm.

It wasn't blazing—barely more than skin-heat—but it held, steady, while the tower's damp cold crept around them and the tide sighed below. Gideon laughed, a startled, cracked sound, and Ondrell allowed himself the ghost of a nod.

"Now break it," he said.

Her laugh died. "What?"

"Break it. Unmake the binding. Give the warmth back to yourself."

"But you said—"

"I said what was given cannot be returned. Now prove me right, or prove yourself a fool who thinks rules are for other people. Break it."

She tried. She reached into the stone with her gift and pulled, and the binding held like knotted rope, like roots in clay, like a word carved into granite. She pulled harder, sweat beading at her temples, and the stone simply *kept*, placid and immovable, holding her hearth-warmth in its gray heart while she strained against it.

"It won't—" she gasped.

"No. It won't." Ondrell pried the stone gently from her fingers and set it apart from the others. It continued to radiate its small borrowed summer. "That warmth is spent. Yours. Gone from you forever. Somewhere in your memory, the Scriptorium's hearth is a little colder now, and it will always be so. That is the price of a successful binding. Most people need to fail a dozen times before they believe it."

Gideon flexed her aching fingers. "How many times did you fail?"

A long silence. The tide turned below; water chuckled in the flooded ground floor.

"Thirteen," Ondrell said at last. "And the fourteenth cost me something I have regretted every day since, though not for the reason you'd think." He gathered the failed stones and dropped them back in their jar. "Enough for today. Your gift is bleeding; I can see it fraying at the edges. Rest. Eat. Come back tomorrow."

"Tomorrow we do a bigger binding?"

"Tomorrow," he said, "we discuss names."

* * *

She didn't go straight back to the safehouse. She walked the seawall instead, the warm stone in her pocket like a small guilty heart, and watched Duskharbor wake around her.

The free city was nothing like Ashen Vale. Where home had been ordered, cobbled, candle-lit, Duskharbor was a riot—rooftops stitched together from five nations' wrecks, streets that smelled of tar and cinnamon and brine, a hundred tongues arguing over fish prices in the morning market. Refugees camped under the aqueduct arches. A temple to some drowned god rang bells that no one seemed to obey. Children ran past her legs chasing a gull with a stolen roll, and for one absurd moment she thought of Serah, twenty years ago, running these same kinds of streets before the war took their city and set them on opposite sides of a hunt.

Serah would be hunting her now. The thought arrived on schedule, daily, like the tide. Somewhere behind her, Captain Vole was reading reports, following rumors of a scribe with a glowing charm, working her way downstream along the same river Gideon had fled down. It was only a matter of time. Every lesson Gideon learned here was a lesson bought with days she might not have.

She climbed to the top of the seawall and sat, and took out the covenant-stone.

It warmed at her touch—that deep, slow warmth it always had, different from the pebble's borrowed heat. Older. She thought about what Ondrell had said, and for the first time let herself really wonder: what had her mother given to make this? What piece of herself lived in here, still keeping, still holding?

*I'm sorry,* she thought, not for the first time, to the woman she barely remembered. *I'm sorry I don't know what you paid.*

The stone pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Or like an answer. She couldn't tell which, and that frightened her more than the soldiers at the fish-gate.

* * *

The next morning, Ondrell had cleared the worktable entirely except for a single sheet of blank paper and a pen.

"Write your name," he said.

Gideon picked up the pen. "This is a lesson in calligraphy?"

"This is a lesson in everything. Write it."

She wrote it. *Gideon Amberdale.* Her hand did it automatically, the way it had signed a thousand ledgers, a thousand false blessings, a thousand small legal fictions. The letters came out clean and confident, and looking at them, she felt a strange vertigo—as if she were seeing them from outside, as if the name belonged to someone she had been impersonating for years.

"Do you know," Ondrell said quietly, "what a name is, in the old grammar? Before Vessic, before the Council, before your temple reduced magic to market-stall tricks?"

"Tell me."

"A name is the first covenant. The one your parents made for you, binding you to a lineage, a story, a debt of blood and memory. When the original signatories sealed the Ember-Veil, they did not sign documents. They gave their names into the stone—true names, the whole of them, everything they had been and meant to be—and the Veil took those names and wove them into itself, and that is why it held for two hundred years. Not because the magic was strong. Because the payment was total."

Gideon's mouth had gone dry. "And to reforge it now—"

"Would require a signatory." He met her eyes. "Or the heir of one. Which, according to the stone you carry and the visions it has shown you, is you."

The tower was very quiet. Below, the tide was turning again, and somewhere across the city the drowned god's bells rang the hour, indifferent.

"There must be another way," she said. "A lesser price. Partial payment. The Council—"

"The Council wants the Veil sealed cheaply, or broken profitably, depending on which faction you ask. Neither of them understands that covenants do not haggle." Ondrell began gathering his jars, his movements stiff, ending the lesson the way he ended all of them—abruptly, like a door closing. "Practice your small bindings tonight. Warmth, weight, a whisper held in glass. Learn what giving feels like in your bones, so that when the true price is named, you will recognize it."

"And if I refuse to pay it?"

He paused at the window, silhouetted against the gray harbor light, and for a moment he looked very old and very tired.

"Then the Veil fails, the ember-realm dies, and everything your mother gave is wasted," he said. "Or Vessic breaks it open first and drinks what's left. Those are the alternatives, child. There is no third door." He glanced back at her, milk-pale eyes unreadable. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we bind sound, and you will hate me for it."

* * *

She hated him for it.

Sound, it turned out, was worse than warmth. To bind a whisper into glass, she had to give up the whisper itself—and the whisper Ondrell chose, with his terrible pedagogical precision, was one she supplied herself: a few lines of Brannoc's voice, recited from memory, a teaching cadence she had carried since she was nine years old. *Ink dries faster than regret, little wren. Mind your margins.*

She gave it to the glass. All of it. And when the binding took—when the vial on the table held her mentor's voice like a caught moth, playing it back soft and perfect whenever she uncorked it—she discovered that the memory in her own head had gone silent. She could remember that he used to say it. She could not remember how it sounded.

She sat with the vial for a long time that evening, in the safehouse loft above Tamsin's warehouse, listening to a dead-or-dying man's voice repeat eight words over and over, and she understood, finally and completely, what Ondrell had been teaching her. Not technique. Arithmetic. The arithmetic of the soul: everything bound is subtracted; nothing given returns; the ledger always balances.

Tamsin found her there near midnight, perched on a crate with the vial cupped in her hands. The smuggler leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, hair tied back for sleep, and studied her with those shrewd river-gray eyes that missed less than they admitted.

"That's him, isn't it," Tamsin said. "The old man from the Scriptorium. The one you ran from."

"Yes."

"And the price of hearing him is forgetting how he sounds." It wasn't a question. Tamsin had spent enough evenings watching these lessons from the stairs to have assembled the shape of it. She crossed the loft, took the vial from Gideon's hands with unexpected gentleness, corked it, and set it on the shelf. "Then stop spending him a line at a time. Save what's left."

"I can't save anything, Tamsin. That's the whole lesson. That's the whole *covenant*." Gideon heard her own voice crack and hated it. "Every binding costs. And the big one—the real one—"

"Costs more than a whisper." Tamsin sat down beside her on the crate, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Ondrell won't say it outright, but I've done enough smuggling to smell contraband. Whatever the Veil wants from you, it's big, and he knows, and he's feeding it to you one pebble at a time so you don't run." A pause. "Are you going to run?"

Gideon looked at the shelf, at the little vial holding eight words of a voice she could no longer hear in her own head. At the covenant-stone wrapped in its cloth on the table, patient and warm and waiting. At the harbor lights beyond the window, and beyond them, somewhere east, the thin places where the Veil wore thin, and beyond *those*, a dying world of cooling hearths that had known her mother's name.

"No," she said. And then, because it was true and because saying it made it truer: "I'm going to learn everything he can teach me. Every binding. Every price. Even the ones he won't name yet." She turned to Tamsin, and found her own resolve reflected back at her, sharpened by the other woman's wary, unsentimental care. "Because Vessic isn't going to wait for me to be ready. And neither is the Veil."

Tamsin held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, the way she acknowledged fair terms. "Then we'd best get you ready fast. Word at the docks tonight—soldiers asking questions at the fish-gate, and not the usual kind. Someone gave them a description." She stood, offering Gideon a hand up. "Scribe, female, young, traveling with a river crew. Somebody's been talking."

Gideon took the hand and rose, and the cold dread that had followed her since a charm flared in a market square settled back over her shoulders like a wet cloak. But beneath it, steadying her spine, something new had hardened—the lesson of thirteen failures and one success, the arithmetic she could no longer unknow.

Everything worth binding costs. Very well. She would decide, herself, what the price would buy.

"Then we move tomorrow," she said. "Before they close the gates."

Above the drowned quarter, the drowned god's bells began to ring the midnight hour—and somewhere far off, at the edge of hearing, beneath the bell-tone and the tide-sound, Gideon thought she heard the covenant-stone humming.
