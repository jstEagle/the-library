## 9. The Usurper's Court

The throne room of Ashen Vale had been built for a different kind of king.

Vessic knew this the way he knew the grain of every beam in it, the weight of every banner, the exact number of paces from the great doors to the dais—because he had counted them, in the years before, when the hall belonged to old King Aldous and Vessic was merely a marshal with too many victories and too little patience. Aldous had hung the walls with tapestries of harvests and weddings, of rivers running gold and orchards bowing under fruit. A king of plenty. A king who believed that peace was a thing you inherited rather than made.

Vessic had taken the tapestries down within a week of his coronation. In their place hung maps. Real ones—not the decorative kind cartographers painted for princes, but working charts, inked and re-inked, marked with pins and thread: garrison strengths, granary yields, the river routes where smugglers thought themselves invisible, and along the eastern marches, a long red line that followed the border of the Veil itself.

He stood before that line now, alone, while the evening bells rang out across the occupied city. Below him, through the high windows, Ashen Vale glowed with ten thousand hearthfires, and beyond the city's edge the sky held that faint bruised shimmer that common folk never looked at anymore—the Veil, stretched thin as old parchment, keeping its two worlds apart the way a dam keeps back a sea.

They called him usurper. He let them. The word assumed there had been something worth usurping.

* * *

The doors opened at the hour he had named, neither early nor late. Vessic did not tolerate waiting; waiting was for men who needed other people more than they needed themselves.

Captain Serah Vole entered with the particular stride she had perfected over two years of service—quick enough to show obedience, controlled enough to show she resented showing it. She stopped three paces from the dais, which was one pace closer than protocol required, and Vessic noted it as he noted everything about her. She did not kneel. None of his hunt-captains knelt; he had abolished the practice in his first month, on the grounds that he preferred loyalty that stood on its own feet.

"Your Majesty." Her voice was flat, professional, tired around the edges. She had been in the field for eleven days. He could see it in the dust still caught in the seams of her gauntlets, in the way her eyes moved first to the maps and only then to him.

"Captain Vole." He gestured to the table set below the dais—a field table, oak and iron, deliberately plain in a room of marble. On it lay a single document under a glass case, flanked by two candles. "You took the Scriptorium at Ashen Vale."

"We took the Scriptorium. The scribe escaped. Brannoc Hale is in custody, and—" she paused, and Vessic watched her decide how much truth to spend, "—the archive burned. Partially. My lieutenant got carried away with the firepots."

"And the stone?"

"Not recovered."

Vessic lifted the glass case himself and drew out the document: a ledger page, half-charred at one corner, rescued from the Scriptorium's ruins by men who understood what they were looking for. He laid it on the table between them, turned so she could read it.

She read. He watched her face do what faces always did when they encountered the Ember-Veil covenant for the first time in its original hand—not awe exactly, but the tightening of someone who has walked past a locked door all their life and suddenly been shown the keyhole.

"This is—" she began.

"The founding ledger of the Veil," Vessic said. "Four hundred years old. Signed by nine signatories of the old alliance, binding the ember-realm's fire beneath our world in exchange for our world's warmth flowing back. You'll notice the ninth signature." He tapped the page. "Amberdale. A family name that appears in no genealogy, holds no land, claims no title. A name that was erased from every record by the signatories themselves. Do you know why, Captain?"

"No, Majesty."

"Because a covenant is only as strong as its weakest anchor, and an anchor that can be found can be cut." He straightened. "The Amberdales hid their own existence to protect the binding. Generations of them, living as scribes and charm-sellers and midwives, passing down a single token and a duty they no longer remembered the shape of. And now, Captain, one of them is running down my rivers with the last covenant-stone in her pocket."

Serah's jaw tightened. "The scribe."

"The scribe." Vessic smiled, and knew even as he did it that the expression reached nothing above his cheekbones. People found his smile unsettling. He had stopped minding. "Gideon Amberdale. Though I doubt she knows the name herself yet. Which brings us to why you are here, and why the room is empty, and why what I say next will never be written down."

* * *

He walked her along the map wall, past the pins and threads, to the red line that traced the Veil.

"What do your soldiers eat?" he asked.

She blinked at the non sequitur. "Bread. Salt pork. Whatever the requisitions give us."

"And what do the requisitions give you, this winter? Half-rations in the northern forts. Grain seized from villages that then starve quietly enough not to embarrass anyone. Your soldiers' families queue outside granaries in Duskharbor, and the free cities refuse to trade with us because their councils call me a tyrant." He said it without heat, the way a man reads a supply inventory. "Old King Aldous ruled a realm that was already dying, Captain. Bad harvests. Colder winters. Mines going dry. Rivers shrinking. Everyone blamed taxes, or weather, or each other. No one asked the obvious question."

"Which is?"

"Where does warmth come from?" Vessic touched the red line, and his finger left a faint smudge in the dust of the map. "Not from the sun alone. Not in this world. Four centuries ago, the founders of the Veil made a bargain: the fire of the ember-realm would feed our seasons, our harvests, our hearths—and in return we would send back memory, devotion, story. The ember-realm runs on being remembered, Captain. That is what the shrines were for, before we let them crumble. That is what the tithe-festivals were for, before Aldous's grandfather turned them into drinking holidays. We have been spending a treasury for four hundred years without making a single deposit, and now the account is empty, and the cold is coming due."

Serah was silent a moment. "You're saying the Veil is failing."

"I'm saying the Veil is a dam built across a river that is drying up on one side and flooding on the other. The ember-realm's fire is dying—but dying fire does not vanish, Captain. It pools. It concentrates. Behind that thinning Veil sits more raw heat than this realm has seen since the world was young, and every year the membrane grows weaker and more of it bleeds through as freak warmth, as blight-heat, as the fires that burned the eastern provinces two summers ago." He turned to face her fully. "Aldous's answer was to patch the Veil. Prayers. Renewals. Begging the ember-spirits to keep honoring a contract we had already defaulted on. My answer is different."

"You want it broken."

"I want it *opened*," Vessic corrected, and here his voice changed—dropped its dryness, took on something almost reverent, which was worse. "Deliberately. Under control. Channeled. There are old texts, Captain, in the vaults beneath this palace, describing how the original signing worked and therefore how it can be unmade. A covenant needs a signatory. Break the signatory's line, or better—*use* the last of it—and the binding dissolves, and everything the ember-realm has hoarded flows into ours like a burst reservoir. I am not talking about burning a city. I am talking about ending winter. Permanently. Every hearth in every hovel lit from a source no lord can tax and no priest can withhold. Full granaries. Warm soldiers. A realm that never again kneels to weather."

He watched her take it in. This was the moment he always watched for—the flicker where ambition met arithmetic. Serah Vole was not a fool and not a zealot. She had joined the hunt, he suspected, for the same reason most of his best officers had: not hatred of the gifted, but hunger for a realm that functioned. That hunger was the truest thing in his kingdom, and he fed it carefully.

"And the cost?" she asked. Her voice was careful. "On the other side of the Veil. The spirits. Whatever lives there."

"There is no verified evidence anything lives there," Vessic said. "There are folk tales. Shrine-keepers' stories. Songs." He spread his hands. "I have read every account in the royal archives, Captain. Not one scholar, not one ambassador, not one witness in four hundred years has produced proof of a thinking creature behind the Veil. What we have are stories told by people who needed the dark on the other side to have a face. If something does live there—and I grant the possibility—it is a dying thing in a dying place, and its death will warm forty million living souls. I did not make that bargain. The founders made it, in reverse, four centuries ago, and no one wept for us."

It was a good argument. It was, in fact, the argument he used on himself, on the nights when the arithmetic kept him awake—which it did, sometimes, though he would have executed any man who claimed to know that. He was not a cruel man. Cruelty was inefficient. He was a man who had looked at a freezing kingdom and refused to look away, and if the price of warmth was paid by things that might not even exist, in a country he could not see, for people who were definitely starving—then he would pay it, and history would call him tyrant or savior depending entirely on whether he succeeded.

"Your soldiers' families eat tonight," he said quietly, "because my quartermasters seized three granaries last month. Next winter, with the Veil opened, no one queues. That is the whole of my policy, Captain. Everything else—the hunts, the proclamations, the silver for denunciations—is plumbing."

Something moved behind Serah's eyes. Not agreement—he was too experienced to expect agreement, and didn't need it. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman doing sums she didn't want to do.

"You said the scribe doesn't know her own name," Serah said at last. "That she's carrying the stone without knowing what it is."

"For now. Stones wake near shrines, and shrines are exactly where she's headed—every fugitive gifted runs for Duskharbor, and the road to Duskharbor is lined with the old Veil-shrines. She will learn what she is, Captain, probably sooner than we'd like. A signatory who knows what she's holding becomes either the most dangerous person in my realm or the most useful one." He stepped closer. "Which is why I'm changing the shape of the hunt."

* * *

He returned to the field table and took up a scroll he had sealed that morning with his own hand. He did not offer it ceremonially; ceremony was for men who needed props.

"Hunt-captain general," he said. "Every company, every garrison, every informer network from the coast to the marches, answering to you alone. You'll report to no one but me. You'll have authority to cross provincial borders without writ, to commandeer transport, to spend from the special treasury without itemized accounts."

Serah went very still. Promotion in Vessic's army came fast for competence, but this was not a promotion; this was a handing-over of the knife. "Why me?"

"Because you took the Scriptorium cleanly. Because your denunciation networks actually produce results instead of neighbors settling grudges. Because you don't burn archives out of enthusiasm—or if you do, you admit it, which is rarer and worth more." He held her gaze. "And because you knew the scribe. Before. The records say you shared a tutor, shared books, shared six years of your childhood. I don't believe in coincidence, Captain. I believe in assets. You will understand Gideon Amberdale's mind better than any officer I have, and understanding a quarry is worth fifty horsemen."

For a heartbeat—Vessic saw it, catalogued it—something crossed her face that was not ambition at all. Something older and more tired. Then it was gone, folded away behind the professional blankness she wore like armor, and she accepted the scroll with both hands.

"She was my friend," Serah said. Flatly. As if reporting a tactical detail.

"I know." Vessic allowed himself the ghost of sympathy, the way he allowed himself everything—in precise quantities. "Friendship is not a weakness, Captain. It's intelligence. Use it or be used by it; those are the only options anyone gets. Bring me the stone, and bring me the girl alive if you can. Dead is acceptable. Converted would be ideal—a willing signatory opens the Veil faster and cleaner than a dead one, and a public conversion would break the Council in Duskharbor without a single sword drawn."

"And if she won't convert?"

"Then she is a lock," Vessic said, "and locks exist to be opened or broken. Dismissed, Hunt-Captain General."

She saluted—fist to breastplate, the new style he'd introduced—and walked out with the scroll against her chest and her stride fractionally heavier than when she'd entered. Vessic watched the doors close behind her and stood alone with his maps.

* * *

Later, when the candles had burned low and the palace servants had been dismissed, Vessic unlocked the vault beneath the dais and descended the narrow stair with a single lamp.

The vault held the things he showed no one: the unmaking-texts, bound in hide no tanner would admit to preparing; the survey reports from the eastern provinces where the bleeding heat had already turned fields to slag; and, in an iron case at the far wall, a shard of Veil-glass his grandfather had pried from a ruined shrine, through which—if the old accounts were honest—one could glimpse the far side.

He lifted the shard to the lamplight.

Behind the glass, faint as breath on a window, something glimmered. Not fire, exactly. Embers. A vast dim landscape of banked coals and cooling hearths, and shapes moving among them—slow, patient shapes that might have been drifts of ash and might have been figures, standing very still, the way people stand when they are watching a door.

Vessic looked at them for a long time. He felt no fear. Fear was a luxury of men who believed the world owed them gentleness, and he had stopped believing that at fourteen, in a siege winter that had eaten his entire family except him.

"If you're real," he said to the shapes, conversationally, "then you've had four hundred years of our warmth, and you're welcome to the memory of it. But the account is closed. One way or another, this winter, the Veil comes down."

The embers did not answer. But as he turned to climb the stairs, the shard in its case seemed—for just a moment—to glow a shade brighter, as if somewhere on the far side, something had heard him and begun, very quietly, to prepare.

Above, in the waking world, a hunt-captain general rode out through Ashen Vale's gates with sealed orders and an old friendship folded up inside her like a letter she couldn't stop reading, heading for the river roads where a smuggler's boat carried a scribe, a stone, and the last name of a house that had hidden itself for four hundred years.

Vessic climbed back to his empty throne room, sat down on a chair built for a better man, and began, by lamplight, to draft the proclamation that would promise his realm an end to winter.

By morning, it would be nailed to every door in the occupied lands.

And somewhere downstream, in the dark on the water, a covenant-stone that had slept for a generation stirred against a sleeping woman's palm, warm as a coal breathed upon, as if it too had heard the king's vow—and meant to argue.
