## 10. An Old Debt Called In

The letter came with the morning tide, the way all bad news did in Duskharbor.

Tamsin Roe found it wedged under the *Kestrel's Grin*'s mooring line, folded twice and sealed with grey wax that bore no crest at all—which was itself a kind of signature. Honest correspondence announced itself. Dishonest correspondence preferred to arrive anonymous, like a knife between ribs delivered by post.

She stood on the wet planks of the Saltquay with the letter in her hand for a long moment before she broke it. Around her, the harbor woke in its usual fashion: gulls screaming their eternal grievances over fish guts, dockhands hauling crates of Duskharbor glass and ember-salt, a customs officer arguing with a fishwife about tariffs neither of them understood. The free city smelled of brine and tar and frying onions, and beneath it all—fainter now than it had been even a season ago—the cold mineral breath of the Veil, thinning like old cloth.

She broke the seal. Read it once. Read it again to be sure her eyes weren't lying.

*They know the route. They know the shrine. Get clear or get caught. — You know who owes whom.*

No name. No signature. Just that last line, written in a hand Tamsin knew better than her own mother's, because she'd spent three years paying interest on it.

Corvus Hale.

She looked up from the paper, out across the grey water, past the breakwater, toward the south where the river wound inland through the saltmarshes to the shrine they were meant to reach before the next dark moon. Somewhere along that route, if the letter told true, Vessic's hunters were already moving. And somewhere behind those hunters, holding their leash, was a man Tamsin had once called friend, because debt made strange kinships and stranger betrayals.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, to the gulls, to the sea. "Damn."

* * *

She went to find Corvus first. That surprised her, when she thought about it later—that her feet carried her toward the man instead of away from him. But Tamsin Roe had built her whole life on knowing exactly what she was dealing with before she ran from it, and running blind had killed better smugglers than her.

He kept rooms above a chandler's shop in Rope Row, in the crooked quarter where the buildings leaned together like drunkards holding each other up. She climbed the outside stair two steps at a time, the letter crushed in her fist, and hammered on his door until it opened.

Corvus Hale had been handsome once, in the way of a well-made ship—sound lines, good proportions, everything where it ought to be. Time and debt had weathered him. His hair had gone salt-grey at thirty-five, his hands shook unless he was holding something, and there were shadows under his eyes deep enough to lose a coin in. He looked at her standing on his landing, and she watched him do the arithmetic: *she knows.*

"Tamsin." He didn't step aside. "You look well."

"I look furious. There's a difference." She pushed past him into the room. It was meaner than she remembered—a bed, a chair, a cold hearth, and everywhere the cluttered evidence of a man selling pieces of his own life to keep creditors patient: a missing silver candlestick here, an empty case where a fiddle had been there. "You wrote me a letter."

"Did I." He closed the door. "I don't recall."

"You don't recall telling Vessic's hunters exactly where my crew would be and when?"

Something moved behind his face—not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been easier. What moved there was older and sadder: the particular exhaustion of a man who had run out of doors to close. "I told them about the marsh road," he said quietly. "Not about you. Not by name."

"The marsh road *is* us, you absolute barnacle. There's one crew running that stretch this moon, and we both know whose it is."

He sat down heavily on the bed, as if his legs had been negotiating with him for some time and had finally won. "They have my daughter's name, Tamsin."

The words landed in the small room like a dropped anchor. Tamsin stayed standing, arms crossed, because if she stopped moving she might sit down too, and then they'd be two drowning people holding each other under.

"Maren," she said. "How old is she now? Twelve?"

"Twelve." His voice cracked on it. "And on a list. Vessic's criers read the lists in every market from here to Greywater—names of families with gifted blood, names of families who sheltered the gifted, names of families who *might*, if someone denounces them, be worth investigating. Someone denounced mine. A neighbor. A man I've lent money to, if you can believe the irony." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I went to the hunt-captain's office to plead. Do you know what they said? They said names come off lists for services rendered. Services rendered, Tamsin. And then a very polite clerk slid a piece of paper across the desk with your route on it, and asked me if I recognized the handwriting."

"And you did."

"It was mine," Corvus said. "From three years ago. When I financed your first hull. Every ledger entry, every advance, every interest note—I wrote them all down, because I never trusted memory with money. They had it all. My whole claim on you, laid out like a map." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and hating himself for it. "I owed you eight hundred crowns. I owed my daughter her life. I'm not proud of which debt I paid."

Tamsin turned away and looked out the narrow window at the rooftops of Rope Row, at the smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, at the world that Vessic's machine was grinding down name by name, list by list. She thought about Maren Hale, twelve years old, gap-toothed, forever trailing her father around the docks asking questions about rigging. She thought about Gideon Amberdale, sitting in the cabin of the *Kestrel's Grin* right now with a covenant-stone in her pocket and the weight of two realms pressing down on her shoulders, trusting Tamsin to get her to the shrines.

Loyalty, Tamsin reflected, was a lot like ballast. Wonderful thing to have. Terrible thing to run short of in rough water.

"How much time do I have?" she asked.

"They move tonight. Two boats up the marsh channel, maybe forty men, a witch-finder with them." He hesitated. "There's more. The polite clerk mentioned a captain. Vole. Serah Vole. Apparently she's asked for this hunt personally."

The name went through Tamsin like cold water through a hull breach. She'd heard Gideon say it only once, late at night, in a voice stripped of everything except old grief: *She was my friend. Before any of this.* That was all Gideon ever offered about Serah Vole, and Tamsin had learned not to press. Some cargo you didn't open in transit.

"So it's personal for them too," Tamsin said. "Wonderful. Anything else? Plague locusts? A second sun?"

"Tamsin—" Corvus rose, took a step toward her, hands half-lifted as if to touch her shoulders and afraid to complete the motion. "I'm sorry. Gods, I'm sorry. If there were another way—"

"There's always another way. There's just always a price, and you decided your price was mine." She turned back and looked at him, really looked, and found that underneath the fury there was something worse: understanding. She understood him completely. That was the rot of it. Put her own child's name on a list, put a ledger of her debts in a hunter's hands, and Tamsin Roe would have done the same thing, and hated herself with the same clean thoroughness, and slept badly for the rest of her life. "Keep your sorry. Spend it on Maren. Get her out of the city if you can—there's a fishwife on the Saltquay named Berta Odo, ugly as sin, honest as tide-tables. Tell her Tamsin sent you and she'll take the girl north on the kelp-boats. Consider it eight hundred crowns' worth of forgiveness. We'll call the rest even."

Corvus stared at her. "You're forgiving the debt?"

"I'm buying my conscience back. It's cheaper than you'd think." She was already moving for the door. At the threshold she stopped, without turning. "Corvus. If I find out you sold anything else—anything—you'll wish the hunters had taken you instead. That's not a threat. That's a forecast."

She left him standing in his ruined little room, and she did not let herself cry until she was three streets away, and then only briefly, and then never again, because there wasn't time.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Grin* lay at her berth on the inner quay, low in the water, patched and re-patched, the fastest ugly boat on the coast. Tamsin walked her deck from stem to stern out of habit, touching things—rail, mast, the coiled lines, the hatch cover—as if checking whether the ship could feel how bad the news was.

Gideon was below, as she always was these days, bent over the table with maps and notes spread around her like a general planning a war, except the war was older than any kingdom and the weapon was a stone the size of a plum. The cabin lamp threw warm light across her face, and Tamsin paused in the doorway and simply looked at her for a moment.

Three weeks ago, Gideon Amberdale had been cargo. Fugitive scribe, outrageous fee, keep your nose out of her business—that had been the arrangement, clean and transactional, the way Tamsin liked all her arrangements. Then had come the night on the river when the stone woke near the drowned shrine and lit the whole cabin blue-white, and Gideon had wept without sound while visions played across her skin, and Tamsin had stood in the doorway watching and understood, with the sinking certainty of a hull taking water, that the transactional part of this voyage had ended somewhere back upstream without her noticing.

Now the woman was going to save the world, apparently. And Tamsin was going to help her. And the fee had become, somewhere along the way, irrelevant.

"Gideon."

The scribe looked up. Her eyes had that focused-beyond-the-walls quality they got when she'd been reading too long. "You're back early. Did the chandler have the pitch?"

"Forget the pitch." Tamsin shut the cabin door and lowered herself onto the bench across the table, elbows on the wood. "We have a problem, and I need you to listen without doing that thing where you decide to sacrifice yourself before I finish talking. Can you do that?"

Gideon set down her pen. "That thing I do?"

"That thing. The noble doom thing. Very tiresome." Tamsin laid the letter on the table between them, unfolded flat. "This came this morning. No seal, no name, but I know the hand. Corvus Hale. He financed my first hull, years back—he's held my debt since, and he's been squeezed dry lately, selling his own furniture to make payments. This morning he sold something else." She tapped the paper. "Our route. Our timing. The marsh channel and the shrine approach. Hunters move tonight—two boats, forty men, a witch-finder. And Gideon—" she made herself say it plainly, because plainness was a kindness, "—Serah Vole is leading them. Personally."

He watched the name land. Watched Gideon go still in that particular way, the stillness of a person listening to an old wound reopen. Her hand drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the pocket where the covenant-stone lived, and rested there.

"She knows the marshes," Gideon said finally. Her voice was level. Too level. "She grew up two villages from Ashen Vale. She used to fish that channel with her brothers." A pause. "With me, sometimes. In summer."

"Then she knows exactly where to wait for us."

"Yes." Gideon looked down at the letter, read it through, and Tamsin saw the moment the meaning shifted for her—from danger to something else. "Wait. This is dated knowledge. The route we planned—the shrine sequence—it's the plan as of four days ago. Before I revised it after the vision." She looked up sharply. "Corvus sold them our old route, Tamsin. The dead one. If we take the new approach—the northern cut, through the eel-water—they'll be waiting at a shrine we're no longer going to."

Hope is a dangerous cargo; it shifts in heavy weather and sinks ships. Tamsin made herself weigh it carefully. "Or Corvus knew the revision somehow, and the old route is the bait, and the real trap is wherever we actually go."

"Could he know?"

"He financed my hull. He doesn't finance my decisions. Nobody aboard knew the revision except you, me, and Wick—and Wick can't keep a secret longer than a tankard stays full, but he also hasn't been ashore since Greywater." Tamsin rubbed her jaw. "It's possible. It's not certain. That's the problem."

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. The lamp flame leaned and steadied. Out on the quay, a bell rang the change of watch, and neither of them moved.

"We could run," Tamsin said at last, and hated the words even as she offered them, because offering them was honest and honesty was the least she owed this woman. "North, tonight, on the tide. There are other shrines—your stone showed you others, didn't it? We could be in the Free Reach inside a week, and Vessic's whole army couldn't—"

"No."

"You didn't let me finish the sales pitch."

"There's nothing to sell." Gideon's hand closed around the stone through the fabric of her pocket. "The marsh shrine isn't one option among many, Tamsin. It's the third anchor-point. The rite has to move through them in order, at the dark moon, or the binding won't hold anywhere else. Miss this window and the next alignment is a season away, and Vessic will have broken the Veil by then and drained whatever's left of the ember-realm into his war-machine." She met Tamsin's eyes, and there was no noble-doom in her face, no theatrical sacrifice—just the flat, tired clarity of someone who had done the sums and found no gentler answer. "We go through the marshes. Tonight. Knowing what's waiting."

"Knowing *something's* waiting. Maybe at the wrong shrine, if we're lucky."

"Are we often lucky?"

"Once a voyage," Tamsin said. "Usually at the end."

Something almost like a smile touched Gideon's mouth and didn't survive the crossing. "Then we're due."

Tamsin blew out a long breath and stood, and the decision settled into her bones the way ballast settles—uncomfortable, necessary, steadying. She'd spent her whole adult life calculating odds: wind against current, cargo against customs, risk against fee. Every calculation said run. Every calculation said this woman, this stone, this mad errand was worth ten times her weight in trouble, and trouble was the one commodity Tamsin Roe had sworn off trading in.

But she'd also watched a fugitive scribe hold a dying spirit's hand in a flooded shrine and promise it warmth, and mean it. Some cargoes changed the ship.

"Wick!" she bellowed up through the deck boards, and heard the boy's startled thump overhead. "Wake the others! We sail on this tide—all of us, no shore leave, no arguments! And somebody find out where Berta Odo's kelp-boat is berthed!"

She turned back to Gideon, who was already gathering her maps, already moving, already carrying it.

"One condition," Tamsin said.

Gideon paused. "Name it."

"When the hunters come—and they'll come, tonight or tomorrow, old route or new—we don't split up. Whatever happens, however bad it gets, you don't do the noble-doom thing and slip off alone to save the rest of us. You want to sacrifice yourself, you ask permission first. Crew rules." She held out her hand across the chart-strewn table. "Deal?"

Gideon looked at the offered hand for a heartbeat too long—long enough that Tamsin saw something flicker behind her eyes, some secret weighed and set aside—and then took it. Her grip was warm and ink-stained and steady.

"Deal," Gideon said.

Later—much later, in the burning dark of the saltmarsh, with arrows in the reeds and witch-fire on the water—Tamsin would remember that handshake, and that flicker, and wonder how many warnings she'd been given and failed to read. But that was later. Now there was a tide to catch, and a lie of a route to sail, and forty men in the marshes hunting a woman who meant to give everything she had to save two worlds.

Tamsin Roe cast off the lines of the *Kestrel's Grin* with her own hands, and steered her out of Duskharbor harbor into the grey and gathering dusk, straight toward the teeth of the hunt.
