## 11. Ambush at the Saltmarsh Shrine

The saltmarsh began where the river forgot itself.

Gideon felt the change before she saw it—the water widening, slowing, losing its purpose in a hundred braided channels that wandered toward the sea like old men who had forgotten the way home. The reeds grew tall as spears on either side of the smugglers' channel, and the air took on a taste she had never known in Ashen Vale: salt and sulfur and something older beneath both, something that smelled the way a struck flint smells just before it catches.

"The shrine's half a day inland," Tamsin said, crouched at the prow of the flat-bottomed barge, one hand shading her eyes against the low white sun. "We'll leave the boat with Wren and the others, walk in quiet. Vessic's hunters don't like wet ground. Too slow for their horses, too soft for their boots."

"You sound certain."

"I sound hopeful." Tamsin glanced back at her, and there was something in her face Gideon had learned to read over the weeks on the river—a flicker of the merchant under the smuggler, the woman who weighed every cargo twice. "There's been no word from Duskharbor since we left. No word is good word, usually. But Ondrell taught you the rite needs three shrines, and this is the second. If the Council means to betray us, they'd do it here, where the ground does half their work for them."

Gideon touched the covenant-stone through her shirt, where it hung against her sternum. It had been warm all morning. Not the gentle warmth of the visions on the river—those had come and gone like sunlight through cloud—but a steady, patient heat, like a hand resting on her chest.

*It knows,* she thought. *It knows what we've come to do.*

She said none of this aloud. She had grown practiced at silence; it had been her first craft, before ink, before magic. But Tamsin saw anyway—Tamsin always saw—and her mouth tightened, though she said nothing either.

They grounded the barge among the reeds where a tributary thinned to mud, and six of them went forward on foot: Tamsin, Gideon, Wren the pilot, the brothers Corvo and Anselm who handled the crew's fighting, and old Maren who knew the marsh paths from years of running contraband through places no king's map bothered to name. The ground sucked at their boots. Egrets lifted from the shallows in silent white explosions. And ahead, rising out of the flat green world like the ribcage of some drowned god, stood the shrine.

It was not what Gideon had expected. The shrine at the river bend had been a carved stone in a hollow, humble as a grave-marker. This was architecture—or the bones of it. A ring of standing stones taller than two men, black with salt and age, leaning inward like conspirators. Between them, the air shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers above a summer road, except that here the shimmer ran vertical, a curtain of almost-nothing that Gideon's eyes wanted to slide off of whenever she looked directly at it.

*The Veil,* she realized. *A thin place. The Veil itself, worn thin enough to see.*

Her mother had stood here. The knowledge arrived without evidence, pure and certain as memory. Her mother had stood between these stones with seven others, and the world had been whole.

"Stones are humming," Maren said quietly. She had stopped ten paces back, her weathered face creased. "You hear that? Like a hive."

Gideon listened. Beneath the wind and the whisper of reeds, yes—there it was. A vibration too low for the ear and yet heard somehow anyway, felt in the teeth, in the breastbone. The covenant-stone pulsed against her skin in time with it.

"It knows we're here," Gideon said. "The shrine knows."

"Then let's hope nothing else does." Tamsin drew her knife—not the long blade she wore for show in riverside taverns but the short ugly one she kept for honest work—and jerked her chin toward the stones. "Wren, watch our wake. Corvo, Anselm, flank left through the reeds. Maren, stay with me. Gideon—" She hesitated, and for a moment the transactional smuggler vanished and something almost gentle looked out of her eyes. "Whatever you have to do in there, do it fast."

* * *

The interior of the stone ring was quieter than the day around it, as though they had stepped into a held breath. The shimmering curtain stood at the ring's center, close enough now to see movement within it—shadows, or shapes, drifting past like fish beneath ice. Gideon knelt at the base of the easternmost stone, where Ondrell's instructions said the rite's first mark must be cut, and drew her scribe's knife.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Three shrines, Ondrell had said, each one a knot in the cord that bound the realms together, and at each one a signatory's heir must lay her hand and speak the acknowledgment: *I am of the blood that signed. I remember what was given.* At the river shrine, the words had come easily, almost sleepily, and the stone had warmed and released her. Here, with the Veil itself visible and breathing inches away, the words felt heavier. Words were her trade. She knew what they could build and what they could burn.

She pressed her palm to the cold salt-black surface and spoke.

*I am of the blood that signed. I remember what was given.*

The stone answered.

Heat flooded up her arm—not pain, not quite, but an intimacy so total it was nearly pain, the sensation of being *known* by something vast and patient. The hum swelled. Across the ring, the other stones kindled with veins of dull ember-light, tracing carvings that had been invisible a moment before: spirals, interlocking hands, lines of script in a language Gideon's fingers recognized before her mind did, because it was the script of her mother's ledger, the secret alphabet in the margins of every charm she had ever sold.

And the Veil-shimmer at the center brightened, and through it, briefly, she saw—

Hearths. Thousands of hearths, banked and dimming, tended by figures made of ember and ash who turned toward her as one, and though they had no faces she understood that they were looking, that they had been waiting, that somewhere in their dying country a hope had just been carried across the thin place by a girl with ink-stained fingers—

The vision broke.

Gideon came back to herself kneeling in the mud, gasping, her palm printed red on the stone. The ember-veins were fading. The hum sank back beneath hearing. But the warmth in her chest remained, and with it a certainty that settled over her like a mantle: *the second knot is tied. One shrine remains.*

"By the deep," Maren breathed behind her. "Girl, you're glowing."

Gideon looked down at her own hands. Faint light clung to her fingertips, gold-shot and fading, like the last sparks off a poker. For the first time in her life she had used her gift openly—in front of witnesses, under open sky, with no ledger to hide it behind and no charm to carry the blame. She should have been terrified. Instead, exhilaration moved through her like strong drink, heady and dangerous. *This is what I am. This is what I've been hiding.*

Tamsin caught her arm and hauled her up. "Done? Good. We move n—"

The egrets went up.

All of them at once, the entire marsh's white population rising in a single shrieking cloud, and Tamsin's face changed in the space of a heartbeat from triumph to arithmetic. "Down!" she snarled, and shoved Gideon into the reeds as the first crossbow bolt hissed overhead and buried itself in the turf where she had been standing.

* * *

They came out of the tall grass on three sides at once—Vessic's hunters in their grey-and-crimson, moving with the drilled economy of soldiers who had done this many times. Gideon counted twelve, then fifteen, then lost count as more rose from hiding places that should have been impossible. Someone had known. Someone had told them exactly where and when.

*Tamsin's creditor. Or the Council. Or both.* There would be time to grieve the arithmetic later.

"Scatter and draw!" Tamsin roared, and the marsh exploded into violence.

What followed, Gideon witnessed mostly in fragments, pressed flat in the reeds with mud in her mouth while men died around her. Corvo went down early, a bolt through the thigh, dragging himself into cover while his brother covered him with curses and a hatchet. Maren fought like a woman half her age until she didn't fight anymore. Wren reached the boat and pushed it out into the channel alone, drawing off four hunters who waded after it, screaming orders. And Tamsin—Tamsin was everywhere, knife-work and marsh-craft, using the ground her enemy hated, pulling hunters into the sucking mire where their armor became their coffin.

But there were too many. Gideon watched a hunter corner Tamsin against a fallen stone, watched him raise his blade with the unhurried confidence of a man doing routine work, and something in Gideon's chest tore loose.

Later, she could never describe what happened next as a decision. It was not a decision. It was a dam breaking.

She stood up out of the reeds in full view of both armies' worth of killing, and she opened herself to the gift she had spent her whole life burying, and the marsh *answered*.

Light came off her skin like steam off a kettle. The covenant-stone blazed at her throat, and power poured through her—not summoned but *recognized*, the way a river recognizes the sea—and when she flung her hand toward the hunter above Tamsin, the reeds themselves rose. A wall of living green and root, twenty feet high, erupting from the marsh floor, hurling the man backward as if the land had decided, after long consideration, that he did not belong here.

Silence. Even the battle paused for it.

Every hunter within sight had stopped to stare at the scribe-girl wreathed in gold fire, standing in a ring of bowed reeds. Gideon breathed hard, drunk on it, terrified of it, alive in a way she had never been. *I could do anything,* she thought, and meant it, and knew in the same instant how dangerous that thought was.

Then, across the battlefield, a figure in hunter's grey pushed back her hood.

Serah Vole.

Time went strange. The battle receded to a murmur. Serah stood forty paces away with her sword still drawn and her face gone white as bone, staring at Gideon with an expression Gideon had seen only once before—years ago, in the Scriptorium garden, when Serah had found Brannoc's forbidden book in Gideon's satchel and Gideon had lied to her face about it. It was the look of someone watching a door close on a room they can never enter again.

"No," Serah said. Gideon read the shape of it more than heard it. "No. *Gidy?*"

The old name, the childhood name, the name only Serah had ever used—it crossed the battlefield like a blade finding the gap in armor. Gideon felt it strike home and twist.

"Stand down!" one of Serah's lieutenants shouted. "Captain, the witch is *right there*—"

"Captain Vole." Another voice, from the treeline at the marsh's edge—a hunter in officer's black, one of Vessic's own inquisitors, his voice carrying the flat authority of a man who reported to the throne itself. "Your identity is confirmed. Take the witch alive. That is a direct order."

Serah didn't move. Her sword-point drifted downward, an inch, two. Her eyes never left Gideon's.

And Gideon, glowing, exposed, her gift naked to the sky and every hunter's crossbow trained on her heart, met her oldest friend's gaze across the smoking marsh and understood that whatever happened next, the girl who had shared her books was gone, and only the question remained: *what will Serah do with what she now knows?*

The inquisitor raised his hand to signal the volley.

Serah's sword came up—not pointed at Gideon.

Pointed at him.
