## 12. Serah's Summons

The tide came in gray at Duskharbor, and Serah Vole had learned to hate the color of it.

She stood at the mouth of a salt-stained alley behind the Lanternmongers' Row, where fish-oil lamps burned in windows the color of old honey and the smell of brine and tallow clung to everything like a second skin. The city was loud in the way free cities were loud—bargaining voices, gull-cries, a fiddler somewhere playing a tune with too many notes in it—but this alley was quiet, and quiet was what she needed.

Her scouts had done their work well. The scribe kept habits; that was the thing about scribes, about all careful people. They built routines the way other people built walls, and then they trusted the routines more than the walls. Gideon Amberdale went alone each evening to a chandler's storeroom near the shrine district, where the Council's exiled mage kept his papers and his bitter little lessons. She went alone because she did not yet understand how hunted she was. Or perhaps she understood and could not help herself. That had always been Gideon's way: to walk straight at the thing she feared, chin up, ink on her fingers, as if courage were simply another kind of handwriting.

Serah checked her blade out of habit, though she had no intention of drawing it. Six years in Vessic's hunt had taught her that a drawn blade ended conversations, and tonight she needed a conversation. Tonight she needed something harder than an arrest.

She needed to save her friend from being hanged.

*Friend.* The word sat strangely in her chest, like a coin from a dead kingdom. She turned it over anyway. *Gideon Amberdale. Scribe of Ashen Vale. Signatory's heir. Fugitive. Friend.*

The footsteps came at dusk, quick and light, pausing once at the alley mouth—the way Gideon always paused, listening for pursuit—and then resumed. Serah stepped out of the shadow of a drying rack hung with eel-skins, and said, quietly, "You still walk like you're late for everything."

Gideon stopped so fast her heels scraped stone. For one heartbeat her hand flew to her collarbone, to the place where the covenant-stone hung beneath her shirt, and Serah watched her do it and felt the old ache open under her ribs: *she thinks I've come for the stone. She thinks I've come to take her in chains.*

"Serah." Gideon's voice was steady. Of course it was. She had been practicing steadiness since they were nine years old and Serah had caught her making a dead beetle's legs twitch with nothing but a whispered word and a guilty face. "You're a long way from your soldiers."

"I dismissed them." Serah leaned against the wall, arms folded, deliberately loose. "Two streets back. They think I'm inspecting the harbor watch."

"And are you?"

"No."

* * *

They looked at each other across four feet of wet cobblestone and eleven years of silence, and Serah catalogued her the way she catalogued every quarry: thinner than the ledgers suggested. Shadows under the eyes. Ink stain on the left middle finger, same as always, same as the day they'd met in Brannoc's scriptorium when Serah had been the wardens' charity case and Gideon had been the master's cleverest apprentice, sliding her a stolen sweetmeat under the copying desks and whispering, *"Don't tell anyone I'm kind, it'll ruin my reputation."*

That girl was gone. This woman wore her face.

"You've gotten good," Serah said. "The ambush at the Saltmarsh Shrine. My hunters are still arguing about how you did it. Three of them will carry burns for the rest of their lives."

"They drew first." Gideon's chin lifted a fraction. "Tamsin Roe would be dead otherwise."

"Tamsin Roe is a smuggler."

"Tamsin Roe is the reason I'm standing here instead of in one of Vessic's cages."

"And there it is." Serah pushed off the wall, took one step closer, and watched Gideon hold her ground—which cost her something; Serah could see it in the small tightening around her eyes, the effort of not retreating from a hunt-captain in a dark alley. "You'd die for a smuggler you've known two months. You ran from me without even letting me speak."

"What was there to say?" Gideon's voice cracked, just barely, just enough. "You burned the Scriptorium, Serah. You put Brannoc in irons. He taught us both to read. He gave you books when the wardens said charity children shouldn't waste paper."

"He's alive." The words came out faster than Serah intended, and she realized she had been carrying them for weeks, wrapped and hoarded like contraband. "He's alive, Gideon. Vessic wants signatories' kin questioned, not killed—not yet. Brannoc is in the Ossuary cells in Ashen Vale, warm and fed, because I told them he knew things only a living man could tell them. Every week I delay his interrogation report, he lives another week. So don't—" Her voice snagged. She mastered it. "Don't stand there and tell me what I burned."

Gideon had gone very still. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm going to say something once, and then never again." Serah glanced up the alley, down it. Empty. Eel-skins swaying. The fiddler's tune drifting thin over the rooftops. "Surrender. Quietly. To me, not to the garrison. You come with me to Ashen Vale, you testify before the King's assessors that the Covenant Council in Duskharbor conspired against the crown—that they sheltered you knowingly, that the exiled mage Ondrell has been teaching forbidden binding-work inside their walls. You give them the Council, and Vessic pardons you. Full pardon. Registered talent, licensed, protected. There's a place for people like you now if they kneel. There's no future for people who run."

"That's your offer." Flat. Careful.

"That's my offer."

"And if I refuse?"

Serah held her gaze. "Then in three days the Fourth Hunt comes over the marsh causeway with fire-pots and grapples, and I won't be able to slow them twice."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Somewhere above, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"You came alone," Gideon said slowly, "to a free city you have no authority in, dismissed your escort, and offered to trade the entire Covenant Council for my life." A breath of a laugh, humorless. "Do you know what that makes you, under Vessic's law?"

"Tired," Serah said. "It makes me tired."

"It makes you a traitor."

"It makes me someone who remembers a girl who shared her sweets under the copying desks and asked for nothing back." The words came out rougher than she wanted. She let them. Some truths you had to cut free or they rotted in you. "I feed forty families on the King's silver, Gideon. Soldiers' widows. The garrison orphanage at Thornmark. I have spent six years telling myself the hunt keeps order, keeps the gifted from burning cities, keeps *something* standing after the war. And then you—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "And then at the Saltmarsh Shrine I watched you throw light like a drawn sword to save a smuggler, and every hunter who saw it should have reported it as abomination, and instead half of them can't stop talking about how *beautiful* it was. Do you understand what that means? Even my own people are starting to wonder what exactly we're hunting."

Gideon's hand had drifted to her collarbone again, to the stone beneath her shirt. "Then listen to me," she said, "because I'm going to tell you why none of it matters. Not the pardon, not the kneeling, not the Council. Listen, and then decide whose traitor you are."

"Careful."

"No." For the first time Gideon stepped *toward* her, and Serah saw the fear in her burn off like morning fog, replaced by something older and heavier. "No, you don't get to be careful. You want to save my life? Then know what you're saving it *for*. Vessic doesn't want the Veil sealed. He wants it broken. Openly, wholly, torn down to its roots—and he wants the ember-realm's dying fire channeled through the breach."

Serah frowned. "The Veil is failing regardless. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows it's *failing*. It isn't the same as *breaking*, and it certainly isn't the same as what he intends." Gideon pulled something from inside her coat—a folded packet of papers, travel-worn, water-marked—and held it between them without letting go. "Captured dispatches. His own surveyors' reports, his own engineers' estimates. The ember-realm is dying, Serah. Its hearths are cooling, its spirits fading. But there is still a vast reservoir of covenant-fire banked at its heart, and if the Veil comes down while that fire still burns, it can be drawn across—harnessed. Enough raw heat and light to fuel foundries, siege-forges, whole armies, for a generation. He means to bleed a dying world into his war machine. He means to make sure nothing on the far side ever wakes again."

The alley seemed colder. Serah heard herself say, "That's not possible. The realms aren't—there's no mechanism—"

"There is. It's called a breach, and breaches are made by men." Gideon's voice dropped. "I've seen the far side, Serah. I've walked it. I've stood in a village of hearth-spirits who remembered my mother by name, and watched their fires gutter while we talked. They're not monsters. They're not demons out of the pulpit-books. They're *people*, or close enough to weep over, and they are going out like candles in a draft—and your king means to put his lips to the flame."

Serah stared at the papers. Part of her—the part trained by six years of interrogations—was already looking for the forgery, the slanted phrasing, the planted evidence. But another part, an older part, remembered sitting beside Gideon in the archive stacks while Brannoc read them fragments of the old covenant-laws, and Gideon whispering, *"Someone wrote these. Someone real. Don't you wonder what happened to them?"*

"The documents," Serah said at last. "Give them to me."

"So you can bury them?"

"So I can *read* them." She took the packet. Their fingers touched over it, briefly, and both of them pretended not to notice. "If this is true—if any of this is true—then everything changes. Everything."

"Yes," Gideon said softly. "Now you understand why I can't testify against the Council. Why I can't kneel. The Council is fractured and foolish and half of them would sell the ember-realm for a seat at a better table, but they're the only ones trying to *reforge* the covenant instead of mining it. If I surrender, I die in a cell or worse, and the last living signatory's line ends in Vessic's ledger, and the Veil comes down on his terms. There is no version of your offer where anyone but Vessic lives well, Serah. You must see that."

"I see a woman choosing a losing war over a pardon."

"I see a woman offering her oldest friend a cage with ribbons on it." Gideon's eyes were bright and wet and absolutely unyielding. "I loved you. I love you still, God help me, which is why this hurts like a knife. But I am going to the thin place, and I am going to try to reforge what our grandparents broke, and if you bring your hunters over that causeway in three days, I will stop you. I don't want to. I am asking you—I am *begging* you—don't make me choose between the world and you."

* * *

Serah walked away first. She always had, even as a child—even leaving the scriptorium, even leaving Ashen Vale the night of the raid, walking out through smoke with Brannoc's cries behind her and telling herself *duty, duty, duty* until the word wore smooth as river stone.

She got three steps before Gideon spoke again.

"The widow at Thornmark," Gideon said. "Maren Ostrey. Two children."

Serah froze.

"When you're deciding whose traitor you are," Gideon said, "remember that Vessic's foundries will need stokers too. Remember what he does with people once they've stopped being useful. Maren Ostrey sewed my mother's mourning veil. If your king tears down the Veil and takes the fire, he won't need forty families fed on hunter's silver anymore. He'll need forty thousand obedient, and he knows exactly how to make obedience."

Silence. The eel-skins swayed. Far off, the fiddler had stopped.

Serah didn't turn around. "Three days," she said. "If I find those papers are false—"

"They're not."

"—then I'll come back, and we'll finish this conversation properly."

"And if they're true?"

Serah stood very still in the gray tide-smelling dark, a hunt-captain general of the usurper King Vessic, holding treason in her coat pocket, and heard herself answer in a voice she barely recognized—smaller, younger, the voice of a charity girl with a stolen sweetmeat melting in her palm:

"Then God forgive me, Gideon. I'll hold the causeway myself."

She walked out of the alley and did not look back, because she knew—if she looked back, she would see her friend crying, and if she saw that, she might never leave at all.

Behind her, unheard, Gideon Amberdale pressed her back against the chandler's wall and slid slowly to the cobblestones, the covenant-stone warm against her heart, and wept for the friendship that had just died so that the truth could finally stand up and walk.

And three streets away, in the harbor watch-house, a clerk finished transcribing the evening's reports and added one line at the bottom in a careful hand: *Hunt-Captain Vole seen meeting unknown woman, Lanternmongers' Row. Dismissed escort prior. Recommend inquiry.*

He sealed it, and set it aside for the morning boat to Ashen Vale.
