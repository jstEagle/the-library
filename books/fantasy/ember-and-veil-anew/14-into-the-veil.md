## 14. Into the Veil

The thin place was not where the maps said it would be.

Gideon had learned that much in three days of walking the drowned hills above Saltmarsh, Tamsin grumbling at her elbow about mud and midges and the general ingratitude of clients who paid in promises instead of coin. The old survey charts showed a standing ring of stones on the ridge above the fen; what they found instead was a hollow, a cup of earth where no stone stood at all, ringed by stumps of granite worn smooth as knuckles. The air inside the hollow moved wrong—drifting inward from every direction at once, as if the ground were breathing through its mouth.

"Charming," Tamsin said. "A hole that inhales. I've sailed with crews like that."

Gideon did not laugh. She had taken the covenant-stone from her breast pocket an hour ago, and it had been growing warmer against her sternum with every step, until now it pressed against her ribs like a second heart arguing with the first. In the hollow's center the grass grew pale and fine as hair, and frost glittered on it though the morning was mild, and when she knelt and laid her palm flat against the ground she felt—not cold, exactly. *Attention.* The sensation of being read by something patient.

"It's here," she said. "Under us. The Veil is thin enough here to—" She stopped, because the word for what came next was *tear*, and tearing felt too much like violence for what this was. "To open."

Tamsin crouched beside her, forearms on her knees, and studied the frost-rimed grass with the expression of a woman appraising cargo she suspected was cursed. "And once we're through. What then? You've told me the ember-realm is dying. Dying realms aren't known for their hospitality."

"They remember my mother." Gideon heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. That was the strange arithmetic of the last weeks: every certainty stripped away—the Scriptorium, Brannoc, the Council's fractured half-truths, Ondrell's confession still sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal—and yet some stubborn core of her had hardened around one fact and refused to yield it. Her mother had stood among the signatories. Her mother's blood ran in her veins. Whatever waited beyond the Veil, it was hers by inheritance, and she was done running from inheritances.

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Gideon had learned to read the smuggler's silences the way she'd once read marginalia: the set of her jaw meant calculation, the tilt of her head meant doubt, and when she exhaled slowly through her nose—as she did now—it meant she'd already decided and was only mourning the sensible alternative.

"Well," Tamsin said. "I didn't row you past two checkpoints and a customs barge to stop at the interesting part." She shrugged her pack off and began, absurdly, to repack it—moving the rope forward, shifting the flint kit, rearranging her small hoard of the world with the brisk competence of ritual. "If we're walking into a dying world, I want the rope where I can reach it."

* * *

Tamsin Roe had smuggled salt past toll-keepers, silk past tax-men, and once—and she would deny this until her dying day—a litter of orphaned marsh-cats past a harbor official who hated cats on principle. She knew thresholds. She knew that every crossing worth making cost something, even if the cost was only the person you stopped being on the far side.

But nothing had prepared her for the moment Gideon Amberdale held the covenant-stone up in that breathing hollow and the world opened like a book.

There was no flash. No thunder. The grass simply lay down flat in a spiral, all at once, the way wheat lies down under wind, and the frost rose off it in a column of silver mist, and through the mist Tamsin saw—a hearth. A vast hearth, banked low, coals glowing sullen orange under ash, big enough to walk into. And the hearth was a doorway, and the doorway was the sky of another place, and looking at it made her eyes ache the way deep water aches behind the ears.

"That's not a fire," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "That's a country."

"The ember-realm." Gideon's face was lit from below, coal-orange, and Tamsin saw that she was crying without any of the machinery of grief—just tears, quiet as condensation. "It's so much smaller than it used to be. Can you feel it? It's *smaller*."

Tamsin could feel it, God help her. Standing at the threshold was like standing at a bedside. Something enormous and old was breathing shallowly on the other side, and every breath came a little shorter than the last.

She thought about turning back. She catalogued, quickly and honestly, everything waiting behind her: a warrant with her ship's name on it, a creditor who'd sold their route, a war marching toward Duskharbor. Ahead there was only a dying light and a scribe who had become, somewhere between the river and the saltmarsh, the closest thing to a friend Tamsin had let herself keep in ten years.

"Rope first," Tamsin said, and tied one end around Gideon's waist, and then, after a heartbeat, around her own. "Not because I think you'll fall. Because if you do, I'm contractually obligated to bill your estate for the retrieval."

Gideon laughed—a wet, cracked sound, but real—and stepped into the hearth.

* * *

Crossing felt, to Gideon, like passing through the memory of warmth.

For one long instant she was nowhere—in-between, in the fold of the Veil itself, and she understood suddenly why the old texts called it a veil and not a wall. A wall keeps out. A veil is woven, thread over thread, and she could *see* the weave for that single suspended moment: strands of covenant-light crossing and recrossing, most of them dark, snapped ends drifting like severed rigging, here and there a few threads still glowing faint gold where the original binding held. Her mother's work. Her mother's name was written into these threads—she couldn't read it, but she could feel it the way you feel a familiar handwriting without deciphering the words.

Then her boots found ground, and she was through.

The ember-realm opened around her, and Gideon forgot how to breathe.

She had imagined fire—she realized that now, standing in the amber twilight of a world lit from below. She had imagined furnaces and burning skies. But the sky overhead was a soft deep rose, cloudless, sourceless, the color of the inside of a seashell, and the land rolled away beneath it in gentle hills covered not in grass but in something like moss that glowed faintly where their feet pressed it, each footprint blooming a brief constellation of embers that faded behind them like applause dying out. Rivers ran through the valleys, but the rivers were slow and thick and golden, molten-light moving at the pace of honey, and along their banks stood trees whose leaves were veined with living coal, shedding occasional sparks that drifted upward instead of down.

And everywhere—everywhere—there were hearths.

That was the thing no chronicle had recorded, or perhaps the chroniclers hadn't thought it worth mentioning because it was simply what the realm *was*: every dwelling, every ruin, every waystone had a hearth at its heart, and the hearths were going out. They passed a village of low round houses built of black glassy stone, and in each doorway a hearth glimmered at the dim end of its life, coals grayed at the edges, flames no taller than a candle. An entire civilization's architecture organized around warmth, and the warmth leaving it room by room.

"Oh," Tamsin said softly, behind her. Just that. *Oh.*

They walked. There was nothing else to do. The moss-embers marked their path, and the covenant-stone beat against Gideon's chest like a compass needle finding north, pulling them down a long valley toward a glow on the horizon that might have been a city or might have been the last great fire of the realm, burning itself out in one final act of generosity.

The spirits began to notice them before the spirits became visible.

Gideon felt them first as warmth on her left cheek, always the left, a sensation like standing near an oven door. Then the air would thicken, and shapes would gather—tall, wavering figures like heat-shimmer given posture, faces that were suggestions rather than features, bodies that flickered at the edges the way candleflames gutter. They did not speak, at first. They gathered along the ridgelines and watched the two small solid travelers pass, and their watching felt less like suspicion than like hunger—for news, for novelty, for proof that anything outside still remembered they existed.

The first one who spoke was small. Child-sized, or child-shaped, its shimmer thin as smoke, standing in the doorway of a ruined house with its hands folded.

"You're warm," it said. Its voice was the crackle of a dying fire. "You're both so *warm*. Where did you get all that warmth?"

"We brought it with us," Tamsin said, before Gideon could answer, and Gideon glanced back and saw the smuggler kneeling, holding out her bare hands to the little spirit as if offering feed to a shy horse. "Body heat. Costs you nothing. Take as long as you like."

The spirit drifted closer, hesitated, and settled into the circle of Tamsin's hands like a bird coming to a windowsill. For a moment its edges brightened, steadied, grew briefly more *present*—and Gideon's throat closed, because she understood what she was seeing. The spirit was warming itself on their living heat the way a beggar warms his hands at a stranger's fire. This whole realm was starving, and it was too proud to say so, and it was dying politely.

"What is your name?" Gideon asked the child-spirit gently.

It looked up at her. And then it tilted its burning head, and said, in a voice gone suddenly wondering:

"You smell like *Maren*."

* * *

Tamsin watched the scribe go very still.

She'd seen Gideon still before—still under interrogation in Duskharbor's back rooms, still when Serah Vole's name came up, still through every fresh catastrophe the last months had dealt her. But this was different. This was the stillness of a woman hearing a dead language spoken fluently in her own kitchen.

"Maren," Gideon repeated. The name came out of her like something dislodged. "My mother's name was Maren. She died when I was six. How—"

"She signed," the spirit said. It had drifted up from Tamsin's hands now, hovering at Gideon's eye level, and its shimmer had thickened, gathering substance from somewhere—from the saying of the name itself, maybe, from the act of remembering. "At the First Hearth. When the Veil was young and strong. We all saw her. A woman of the other side, with ink on her fingers"—the spirit reached out one wavering hand and touched, feather-light, the callused middle finger of Gideon's writing hand—"just there. She laughed more than the others. The others were afraid of the binding. She said fear makes bad ink."

Gideon made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite anything else, and pressed her hand against her mouth.

More spirits were coming. Tamsin felt them arriving the way you feel weather change—a rising warmth, a thickening of the rose-colored light. They came down the valley slopes from every direction, dozens of them, then scores, tall elders and small quick ones and shapes so old and vast they seemed less like figures than like standing stones that had learned to drift. They circled at a respectful distance, and the word passed through them visibly, a ripple of brightness traveling the ring: *Maren's daughter. Maren's daughter has come back.*

One elder came to the front of the gathering. It was taller than the rest, its flame burned whiter, almost blue at the core, and when it spoke its voice carried the weight of a banked furnace.

"Daughter of Maren. Bearer of the token." Its gaze—if a shimmer could be said to gaze—settled on the covenant-stone in Gideon's hand, which was glowing now, openly, throwing gold light across her astonished face. "We have waited a long time for a signatory's heir. Longer than your kind measures. Come to the Last Hearth, and we will show you what your mother wove, and what has come unwoven since."

"And what it will cost to weave it again," the elder added, softer, and Tamsin saw Gideon miss it—saw the words slide past her, unheard, swallowed by wonder—while Tamsin herself caught them full in the chest like a thrown stone.

The elder turned and led the way down the valley, and the spirits fell in around the two travelers like an honor guard of candleflames, and Tamsin walked at Gideon's shoulder with her hand resting near the rope knife and the elder's last sentence repeating itself in her skull.

*What it will cost.*

She'd spent her life pricing things. Freight, favors, silence, betrayal—everything had a number, and she'd prided herself on never being surprised by an invoice. But the look on that elder's shimmering face, the particular gentleness of it, reminded her of nothing so much as the face of a physician about to name a price the patient cannot pay.

"Gideon," she said quietly, as the Last Hearth's glow swelled on the horizon like a sunrise that had lost the strength to finish rising. "Whatever they're about to ask of you—make them say it twice. Make them say it slow. Nothing in either world gets given away free, and I've never yet met a gift that didn't hide a ledger."

Gideon looked at her, and the stone-light made her eyes gold, and she smiled the stubborn smile that had gotten them both this far.

"I know," she said. "But Tamsin—look at them. Look what's left. Whatever the price is, someone already paid it once. My mother paid it once." She turned back toward the fading fire of the world, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I just want to know what she bought."

The Last Hearth rose before them, vast and dim, and around its rim the spirits began to gather like moths, and like mourners, and like witnesses called to testify.
