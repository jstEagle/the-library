## 15. The Last Signatory

The elder came to her at the hour the ember-realm called dusk, though there was no sun here to set—only the slow dimming of the great hearth at the world's center, whose light had once been generous and was now a coal cupped in dying hands.

Gideon had been sitting alone on the terrace of fused glass that overlooked the Ashen Terraces, where the spirits of the ember-folk kept their small eternal flames. Each flame sat in its own niche, carved into black stone by hands that had been patient for centuries. Some of the flames burned tall and gold. Others were no larger than a moth's wing, guttering, blue at the edges. And some niches were simply dark—empty, cold, their occupants gone wherever spirits went when their fire failed.

She counted them without meaning to. It was what she did: scribe's habit, counting things, ordering things, as if the world could be held together by arithmetic. Forty-one dark niches out of two hundred and six. When she had arrived three days ago, it had been thirty-eight.

The footsteps behind her made no sound, but she felt them anyway—a warmth against her back, like standing near an oven door. She did not turn.

"You have been counting," the elder said. Its voice was the sound of a log settling in a fire, of embers shifting, of something old rearranging itself with care.

"Is that forbidden?"

"Nothing is forbidden here. That is part of our sickness." The elder settled beside her, folding down into a shape that was almost a body—tall, robed in smoke the color of rust, its face a suggestion rather than a fact. Two points of light where eyes might be, banked low, like coals raked over to last the night. "Counting is a living thing's comfort. We stopped counting long ago, because we did not want to know."

Gideon pulled her cloak tighter. The cold of this realm was not the cold of Ashen Vale winters; it was a cold that came from *within*, from things forgetting how to burn. "Then tell me something you do want to know," she said. "Or something I need to know. You've been circling it for three days, Elder. Ember-spirits may have patience, but I don't. Every dark niche I count makes me more afraid of what you're not saying."

The elder was quiet for a long moment. Below them, a spirit tended one of the smallest flames, cupping its smoky hands around it, breathing warmth into it the way a mother breathes on cold fingers. The flame steadied. The spirit moved on to the next niche, and the next, a gardener walking rows of dying flowers.

"Your mother," the elder said at last, "sat where you are sitting."

Gideon's breath caught. She had known this—had seen it in the visions the covenant-stone gave her at the river shrine, had watched a younger version of Maera Amberdale stand among the signatories with ink-stained fingers and a scribe's stoop, exactly her own stoop, exactly her own ink stains. But knowing a thing in vision and hearing it spoken aloud in this dying place were different weights entirely.

"She came through the thin place thirty years ago," the elder continued. "She asked us the same question you have not yet found the courage to ask. What does the reforging cost?"

"And you told her."

"We told her everything." The elder's face-lights flickered, and for a moment they seemed almost sorrowful. "And she went back across the Veil, and she spent thirty years preparing you, and she never spoke of it, because speaking of it would have meant losing the years she had left with you. Do you understand what I am telling you, daughter-of-Maera? Your mother knew the price. She paid a different price instead—thirty years of silence, carrying it alone so that your childhood would not be shadowed by it. I think perhaps that silence was heavier than anything we asked of her."

Gideon's hands had gone cold in her lap. "Tell me."

"The original covenant was signed by twelve," the elder said. "Twelve souls, six from each side of the Veil. They did not merely write their names into the stone, child. A name written in covenant-ink is not ink. It is *substance*. The Veil between our realms is not a wall—it is a seam, and seams must be sewn with thread, and the only thread strong enough to hold two worlds together is a self. A whole self. Freely given."

"Given how?" But even as she said it, Gideon felt the answer rising in her chest like water filling a lung. She was a scribe. She knew what happened to words written into a binding contract. They ceased to belong to the writer.

"The signatory gives three things," the elder said gently, so gently, the way one speaks to the dying or the bereaved. "Her name, first. Not merely the sound of it—the *thing itself*. Every record of it fades. Every memory of it dims. The mother who bore you would look at your face and feel love and know not why. The friend who shared your books would search her heart and find a shape missing, smooth-edged, like a stone pulled from a riverbed. Second, her memory—not all of it, but all of it that touches who she is. Her history becomes unwritten. Third—" the elder paused, and the pause was terrible— "her self. The thread that says *I am*. It goes into the Veil. It holds the worlds apart and together, forever, and nothing of it remains on either side. Not a body. Not a ghost. Not a story. The covenant consumes what it binds with, and it binds with *you*."

The wind moved across the terraces, and somewhere below, another flame went out. Gideon heard it—a tiny sound, a sigh, a niche going dark—and she thought: forty-two.

She thought: *Tamsin will not remember me.*

She thought: *No one will.*

"There were twelve," she said, and her voice sounded far away to herself, flat, a scribe reading aloud from someone else's ledger. "Twelve signatories. Twelve selves woven into the Veil. And it broke anyway."

"It broke because it was broken," the elder said. "Not from within—from without. Ondrell of Duskharbor drove a wedge into the seam, thinking to draw power through the gap. He did not understand that a seam, once gapped, unravels. Eleven of the twelve threads snapped back to their origins—released, returned, reborn into new lives with no knowledge of what they had been. That is why the Veil has thinned but not torn entire. One thread yet holds. One signatory's self still knits the seam, stretched past bearing, fraying year by year." The elder turned its banked-coal eyes upon her. "You carry her token. You bear her gift. You are the heir of the twelfth signatory, Gideon Amberdale. And an heir can reforge what an originator cannot—but only because the covenant will accept a new thread in place of the old. Only because you are the same thread, continued. Your mother's line. Your mother's gift. Your mother's choice, offered again, one generation late."

Gideon looked down at her hands. Scribe's hands. Ink under the nails that never quite washed out, a callus on the middle finger from the quill, a scar on the left palm from Brannoc's letter-knife, the day he'd taught her to cut vellum and she'd been too eager. Brannoc, taken in the burning Scriptorium. Would he remember her? No. No one would. The ledgers she'd forged, the charms she'd sold, the girl who had hidden magic behind blessings in a market square—all of it would remain, but unattributed, orphaned, like words in a book whose author page had been razored out.

*Tamsin.* Tamsin Roe, who haggled like a knife-fight and laughed like a door kicked open, who had carried her downstream for an outrageous fee and then, without ever admitting it, had stopped charging her at all. Tamsin would walk out of this war with a pocket full of coin and a hole in her memory shaped exactly like a stubborn scribe with ink-stained fingers, and she would never know what the hole was, because knowing requires remembering that there was ever anything to forget.

"I need time," Gideon said.

"You may take it," the elder said. "But not much of it. The last thread frays faster now. Can you feel it? Even here, even across the Veil, you flinch at odd moments—when a fire gutters, when a name is spoken twice. The seam is calling its replacement home. If you refuse, the Veil tears within a season, and both realms drown—yours in the flood of ours, ours in the cold of yours. If you accept..." The elder rose, unfolding upward into smoke. "Then you must decide what to do with the time between now and the reforging. That, too, is a choice. It always has been."

It walked away across the glass terrace, soundless, leaving warmth behind it like a hand lifted from a shoulder.

Gideon stayed until the hearth-light dimmed to true darkness, counting niches, and then she went to find Tamsin.

* * *

She found her in the guest hall of the ember-court, which the spirits had warmed for their visitors with what little heat they could spare—an act of hospitality that shamed Gideon every time she thought of it, like beggars sharing their last bread. Tamsin had commandeered a brazier and was teaching one of the young spirits, a wisp named Cindra who had never seen dice before, to play Knucklebones, and was cheating, and was losing anyway, because Cindra could see through solid objects including cups and palms.

"—that's the third time, you little chimney-sweep, and I want it noted that I am being robbed in my own—" Tamsin looked up, and whatever was on Gideon's face stopped her mouth mid-word. "Hey. Hey, now. What is it?"

"Nothing. I'm fine." The lie came out smooth, worn soft from three days of practice. "Cindra's fleecing you. Good. Someone should."

"Gideon." Tamsin stood, waving off the wisp, who drifted away with the tact of the very young. "Three days you've been going up to that terrace. Three days of talking with the charcoal elder, coming back looking like you've read your own death warrant and found a typo in it. I don't push. You've noticed I don't push—that's why you travel with me instead of some knight who'd ask questions. But I'm asking now. Not pushing. Asking."

And there it was: the open door. Tamsin standing in it, offering, without knowing what she offered. *Tell me,* said her whole stance, her crooked half-smile, her arms crossed but her eyes uncrossed. *Whatever it is, tell me, and I'll carry it too. I've got room. I've got room for you specifically.*

*I will not remember any of this,* Gideon thought. *In a month, maybe less, this woman will look at me—if she ever looks at me at all—and see a stranger. Or worse, see nothing, see past me, because the mind slides off absences. She'll go back to the river and run cargo and haggle and laugh, and somewhere in her will be a smooth-edged hollow, and she'll blame the weather, or bad luck, or a woman she never quite met.*

If she told her now, Tamsin would fight it. Tamsin would rage, would scheme, would try to find another way, would probably try to throw herself into the ritual in Gideon's place and get obliterated for her trouble, because Tamsin Roe had never once in her life declined a fight on behalf of someone she— 

On behalf of a friend.

That was the word. Gideon let herself hold it for one full breath. *Friend.* The last friend she'd had before this one had put irons on her mentor and burned her life's work. Maybe friendship was simply the thing that ended you. Maybe—

No. That was fear talking, and fear was a liar with excellent penmanship.

"Tamsin," she said, "when we go back—when this is done—I want you to take me upriver. Past Ashen Vale. There's a shrine my mother visited, north of the Vale, a little one, hardly used. I never saw it. I want to see it."

Tamsin blinked. "That's it? That's the big secret face? You're planning a pilgrimage?"

"That's it. That's the big secret face." Gideon smiled, and hated how easily it came, and hated more that it needed to come at all. "Come on. Teach me Knucklebones. If Cindra can fleece you, I want a turn."

They played by brazier-light while the ember-realm darkened toward its false dawn. Tamsin cheated. Gideon lost. At some point Tamsin laughed—head back, unguarded, the laugh of a woman who trusted the person across from her completely—and Gideon laughed too, and memorized it. The pitch of it. The way it crinkled the corner of her left eye. The way it made Cindra peek back around the doorway just to listen.

A scribe's habit: recording. Somewhere in her, beneath the terror, a steady hand wrote it all down as though it could be kept.

It couldn't. That was the whole point. That was the price.

But tonight, she decided, watching the dice tumble and the firelight move on Tamsin's face—tonight she would keep it anyway.

* * *

She woke before the false dawn, as she had every morning since crossing the Veil, and dressed in the dark, and took the covenant-stone from beneath her pillow, and climbed alone to the highest terrace where the elder had first shown her the hearth.

The stone was warm in her palm. Her mother's token, worn smooth by thirty years of hiding in a lining, in a floorboard, in a satchel, passed hand to hand along a chain of secrets that ended with a witch-hunter's boot kicking in a Scriptorium door. *Maera Amberdale,* the archives would say, if the archives still said anything. Signatory the twelfth. Sacrifice deferred, not refused.

"I'm not brave," Gideon said aloud, to the stone, to the hearth, to the mother-shaped absence that had raised her without ever explaining why she flinched at fires or hummed songs in a language no one else in Ashen Vale knew. "You should know that, wherever you are. Whatever you are, now. I'm not brave like the stories. I'm going to do it because the arithmetic leaves me no other line, and I hate it, and I'm frightened all the way down. But I'm going to do it."

The stone pulsed once against her palm. Warm. Almost like an answer. Almost like forgiveness.

Below, in the niches, the flames burned on—forty-three dark now, she'd counted, forty-three out of two hundred and six, and the number climbing like water in a flooding hold. Somewhere beyond the Veil, a usurper king was sharpening his ambition against a whetstone of other people's futures. Somewhere beyond the Veil, Serah Vole was wearing a captain's coat and swallowing her doubts, or not swallowing them. Time was spending itself whether Gideon spent it or not.

She closed her hand around the stone and made herself a promise, quietly, in the dark, where no one—not even the stones—could witness it:

*Between now and the end, I will spend every hour like coin. And I will not waste a single one on my own grief.*

Far to the east, beyond the terraces, beyond the thin place, the horizon of the mortal world flickered—once, twice—like a signal fire lit on a distant hill.

The seam was thinning faster than the elder had said.

Something was coming through.
