## 16. Vessic Marches

The road to Duskharbor was a wound in the land, and the army of King Vessic walked along it like pus.

Serah Vole rode at the head of the third column, her hunt-captain's grey cloak snapping in the salt wind, and she counted the things she no longer believed the way other officers counted their men. A hundred banners. Two thousand pikes. Nine siege engines dragged on ox-carts, their iron heads wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. And beneath all of it, folded inside her breastplate where it pressed against her heart like a second, colder heart: three pages torn from a captured courier's satchel.

She had read them forty times. She would read them forty more.

*The ember-realm's residual fire, once the Veil is broken under controlled conditions, may be channeled through the shrine-network into the royal foundries. Projected yield sufficient to arm and provision twelve legions in perpetuity.*

Twelve legions. In perpetuity. Written in Vessic's own cramped, precise hand, initialed at the bottom as casually as a man approving a grain shipment.

She had told herself, when she took the pages from the dead courier's coat, that she was gathering intelligence. That a good hunt-captain general read everything. That knowledge was simply another weapon to be kept clean and oiled and pointed at the enemy.

But weapons did not keep you awake at night. Weapons did not whisper in your mentor's voice — old Commander Hale, who had taught her to track through snowfall, who had died at Vessic's side in the taking of Ashen Vale and been buried with honors — saying, *Serah, girl, what do you think feeds the foundries? Fire is not made from nothing. Nothing in this world is made from nothing.*

The ember-realm was dying. Gideon had said so on the saltmarsh, mud to her knees, light pouring off her hands like water off a millwheel. Serah had called it a lie then. A desperate fabrication from a cornered witch.

Except here were the words in her king's own hand. Except here was the supply train behind her, loaded not with siege provisions but with copper cabling and binding-iron, the materials for channeling. Except here was the order, sealed in green wax, that upon the breaking of the Veil the shrine at Saltmarsh was to be fired first — *to test the flow* — and every spirit-bound structure within the free cities was to be catalogued for conversion.

Conversion. As if hearths and shrines and the small warm gods of ordinary people were so many warehouses awaiting new management.

"Captain General." A rider reined in beside her, young, breathless, his horse lathered. "Scouts report the harbor road is barricaded at the Ternish bridge. Council regulars, maybe four hundred, with militia behind them."

"Any sign of the scribe?"

"No, General. But the fishermen say a smuggler's boat came up the estuary two nights past. The *Kestrel's Debt*, out of—"

"I know the boat." She knew its captain too, by reputation if nothing else: Tamsin Roe, who could run a cargo through a keyhole, who had carried Gideon Amberdale out of Ashen Vale while Serah stood in the burning Scriptorium holding a scrap of half-melted charm-stone and telling herself the guilt was only smoke in her eyes.

"Shall we force the bridge tonight, General?"

Serah looked down the column. Ten thousand men stretched back along the coast road until they dissolved into the grey distance, and every one of them had someone at home — a mother, a daughter, a crew of younger siblings — fed by Vessic's silver, clothed by Vessic's quartermasters, alive because the usurper paid his soldiers better than any crown before him had ever dreamed of paying. She had told herself that too, once. That the silver was clean because the hands it fed were hungry.

Now she thought of the ember-realm's cooling hearths, and the spirits Gideon had described, fading like breath off glass, and she wondered what exactly was being burned to keep ten thousand families warm.

"No," she said. "We encamp. Full pickets. We take the bridge tomorrow, after the artillery arrives." The delay bought Duskharbor a day. It also bought Serah a night, and she intended to spend every hour of it reading those three pages until they either made sense or made her something else entirely.

The young rider saluted and galloped off. Serah touched her breastplate, over the papers, over the cold second heart.

*What do you think feeds the foundries?*

Tomorrow, she decided, she would find out what fed her instead.

* * *

Tamsin Roe had smuggled contraband silk, untaxed brandy, fugitive nobles, one very indignant tiger cub, and twice — gloriously, profitably — herself. She had never smuggled hope, and she was beginning to understand why nobody traded in it. Hope was heavy, perishable, and attracted soldiers.

"They're camped along the whole coast road," Pell reported, sliding down the ladder from the crow's nest with the particular energy he reserved for bad news delivered fast. "Fires like a festival. Siege train coming up from the south — Marta's cousin saw it crossing the ford at Lowmere. Nine engines, maybe ten."

"Ten." Tamsin leaned on the *Kestrel's Debt*'s rail and watched Duskharbor glitter across the dark water. From out here the city looked peaceful — lantern-light strung along the terraces like a necklace, the great sea-lantern on the breakwater turning its slow gold eye. Somewhere in that glitter was a scriptorium-turned-war-room where Gideon Amberdale was learning, apparently, how to lose arguments gracefully. Somewhere in that glitter was a woman who had held Tamsin's face in both hands on the saltmarsh and said, *I have to finish this*, with such terrible gentleness that Tamsin had wanted to shake her and kiss her and sail away with her all at once, and had done none of the three.

"You're making the face again," Pell said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing arithmetic with your conscience."

"My conscience can't count above five." She pushed off the rail. "Get the crew mustered. Quietly. If we're going to be idiots, we'll be organized idiots."

Because that was the arithmetic, wasn't it. Option one: load the *Debt* with everything portable, slip out on the midnight tide, and let Duskharbor burn without them. Sensible. Survivable. The kind of choice that had kept Tamsin Roe alive and solvent through two wars and three price collapses. Option two: stay, ferry ammunition and wounded under fire, watch the only person she'd ever — the word arrived unbidden and unwelcome — *love* walk into a ritual that Ondrell had described, in his dry dying voice, as *the giving-up of everything, my dear, everything, there is no smaller coin*.

Ondrell was dead now. Died in a doorway with hunters' blades in him, buying Gideon thirty seconds with his body, whispering an apology Tamsin hadn't been meant to hear. She'd spent eleven years owing that man money and three weeks liking him despite it, and now the debt was unpayable and the liking was a stone in her chest.

Hope, she thought. Heavy, perishable, attracts soldiers. And yet.

"Captain?" Pell again, hesitant. "There's a boat coming off the water. No lights. Flying no colors I know."

Tamsin turned. A low shape was ghosting across the black harbor water, oars muffled, moving with the economy of someone who had done this before. Her hand went to the knife at her belt out of pure professional courtesy.

Then the shape came close enough for the breakwater lantern to catch it, and she saw the passenger in the stern — broad-shouldered, grey-cloaked, hair cropped short in the style of Vessic's officer corps — and her knife came fully out.

"That's far enough," she called across the water. "One more oar-stroke and you're swimming, Captain Vole."

The oars backed water. Serah Vole raised both hands, empty, palms out — the universal grammar of parley. "I need to speak with the scribe," she said. "Not to arrest her. To warn her. And—" the pause was small but real, a crack in the officer's voice, "—to tell her she was right. About all of it. I have proof. In the king's own hand."

Tamsin stood a long moment with the knife loose in her grip and the word *love* still sitting in her chest like swallowed glass.

"Well," she said at last, gesturing the stranger aboard. "Come up, then. But touch nothing, and if you're lying, know that I've hidden bodies in more creative places than you've ever arrested people."

It was not, perhaps, forgiveness. But it was a gangplank, and lately Tamsin had learned to take whatever gangplanks the world offered.

* * *

Private Dennel Brask had joined the Third Ashen Pikes for the same reason everyone did: his sister's cough needed a physician, and physicians in Vessic's realm cost less than prayers used to. He was nineteen, he was cold, and he was standing night-watch on the picket line above the Ternish bridge, listening to the sea say the one thing it always said, which was nothing.

Across the water, Duskharbor burned its friendly lights. Dennel had never seen the free cities. The recruiters had told him they were dens of witches and worse, that the gifted there sacrificed children to the old shrines, that the war was a holy scrubbing. He had believed it the way you believe a story told by a man holding your enlistment bonus.

But tonight a fisherman's skiff had drifted too close to the picket line, and the sergeant had loosed a bolt into the dark, and there had been a scream — a human scream, high and terrified, the scream of someone's daughter — and then silence, and the sergeant had laughed and said, "One less witch," and Dennel had stood very still in the cold and understood that he did not believe the story anymore.

Witches screamed like sisters. That was the whole lesson. That was everything.

He looked at the city lights across the water. Somewhere over there were people keeping lamps lit for children with coughs. Somewhere over there was a woman named Gideon Amberdale — he'd heard the officers whispering about her, the last signatory, the key to everything — and the officers spoke of her the way you'd speak of a mine seam waiting to be worked. *Once we crack the Veil, the flow comes to us.* Flow. As if worlds bled politely.

"Dennel." His tentmate, Osric, older, quieter, appeared at his shoulder with two cups of the terrible army tea. "You're staring at the water again."

"Just counting their fires."

"You've been counting their fires for three nights." Osric handed him a cup. Their shoulders touched, the way soldiers' shoulders do when the truth is too large for words alone. "Sergeant says we cross the bridge at dawn. Says the general's ordered no quarter for the gifted and their protectors."

Dennel drank the tea. It tasted like bark and betrayal, which was to say like home. "And what does the general say happens to the ember-realm, when we crack the Veil?"

Osric was quiet long enough that the sea filled the silence with its nothing. Then, very low: "You heard the courier papers too."

"Everyone in the third rank heard them. Sergeant reads aloud when he drinks."

"Then you know what I know." Osric looked at the city, at its stubborn golden necklace of lights. "My gran used to leave a bowl of cream on the hearthstone, back home. For the little spirits. Said they kept the fire honest." He turned the cup in his hands. "I didn't come here to make orphans, Dennel. I came here to buy medicine."

Across the water, the sea-lantern turned its slow gold eye, patient as a promise. Dawn was four hours off, and the bridge waited, and somewhere in the dark between the armies a hunter was reading stolen papers by candlelight and deciding, stroke by stroke, whose side the truth belonged to.

Dennel Brask gripped his cup and made the smallest vow of his life, so quiet not even the sea heard it: *when the time comes, I will aim wide.*

Ten thousand men marched on Duskharbor. Not all of them, it was beginning to appear, were marching in the same direction.
