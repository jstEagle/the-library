## 20. Aftermath of a Name

The silence after the Veil closed was the loudest thing Tamsin Roe had ever heard.

She stood at the edge of the shattered shrine, her ears ringing where the world's roar had been, and watched the light die out of the sky like a hearth settling into its coals. The thin place had been a wound in the air above the Saltmarsh Shrine—a ragged seam through which she'd glimpsed, for one impossible heartbeat, another realm burning itself out—and now it was simply *sky*. Clean, whole, indifferent sky. The kind of sky that didn't remember what had torn it.

Around her, the battle was ending in the way battles do: not with a last great clash but with a long unraveling. Vessic's elite had broken when their king's stolen fire turned to ash in his hands—Tamsin had seen it happen, had seen Vessic reach for the covenant's power as it sealed and find nothing there but his own small, mortal greed, suddenly naked before ten thousand witnesses. His own guard had taken him down in the mud, more from terror than loyalty. Somewhere beyond the shrine's outer wall, Serah Vole was accepting the surrender of men who had marched beside her that morning.

Tamsin wasn't thinking about any of that.

"Gideon." Her voice came out cracked, half-swallowed by the ringing. She cleared her throat and tried again, louder, pushing through the drifts of spent ember-light that still glittered on the wet stones like frost. "Gideon! Oy, scribe! You still breathing over there?"

The covenant-stone stood at the center of the shrine where it had always stood—or where she'd been told it had always stood, though now she found she couldn't quite recall who had told her, or when. It was whole again. That was the thing that stopped her mid-stride, boots sliding on char: the stone was *whole*. She remembered—it was one of the few things about these last weeks she remembered cleanly—the crack down its face like a lightning scar, the way Gideon used to rest her palm against the fissure when she thought no one was watching, the way the stone seemed to lean into her touch like a cat.

No crack now. Smooth as river glass, warm to the eye if not quite to the hand, humming with a fullness that made Tamsin's teeth ache.

And no Gideon.

* * *

She searched the shrine the way she'd once searched a capsized barge for survivors: methodically, brutally, refusing the arithmetic of what she might find. Under the collapsed colonnade where the rite had begun. Behind the altar-stone where Ondrell had died two days before, his blood long since washed gray by marsh rain. In the trampled reeds at the waterline, where the fighting had been thickest and the dead lay in rows already being covered with cloaks by Duskharbor's surviving defenders.

"Gideon Amberdale," she said to each of them, lifting hems and edges, checking faces against a memory that felt oddly—she couldn't name how it felt. Oddly smooth. Like a coin worn past its stamping.

She found Serah Vole near the western wall, kneeling beside a boy soldier who was beyond anyone's help, and grabbed her by the pauldron hard enough to leave bruises inside the armor.

"Your witch-friend," Tamsin said. "The scribe. Where did they take her body?"

Serah looked up at her, and Tamsin saw something in the hunt-captain's face that she would spend years trying to describe: not confusion exactly, but a kind of polite blankness, the look of someone being asked to remember a room they've never entered.

"Your passenger," Serah said slowly. "The one you brought downriver. The signatory."

"Yes. *Yes.*"

"I—" Serah's brow furrowed. "I don't actually remember her face."

Something went cold down Tamsin's spine, slow as floodwater.

"What do you mean you don't remember her face? You chased her across half the realm. You cornered her at the saltmarsh. You *defected* for her—you took Vessic's own elite on with your bare sword because—because—"

Because why? The reason was there, Tamsin was sure of it, a heavy shape just behind a door in her mind. She reached for it and the door wouldn't open. She knew Serah had defected. She knew it the way you know a story you've heard so many times it has worn grooves in you. But the *why* of it, the person at the center of it—

"She was my friend," Serah said, and sounded astonished at her own words, as if they'd risen from somewhere deeper than memory. "I don't know why I said that. I don't—I can't see her. When I try to picture her, it's like looking into fog." Her hand drifted up, unconsciously, to the collar of her coat, where a scorch mark seared the fabric in a pattern Tamsin almost recognized. "There was a girl. There must have been a girl. We shared books when we were children—I know that the way I know my own name, and I cannot tell you her name, or her face, or her voice. Only that I loved her, and that she is gone, and that I am grieving something I cannot find."

They stared at each other across the smoking courtyard, two women holding the same wound from opposite sides, neither able to say what had cut them.

"The stone," Tamsin said finally. "Ask the stone. She touched it every day. If anything remembers her—"

But the stone didn't. Tamsin pressed both palms to its warm, seamless face and asked, aloud, in the old words Ondrell had drilled into them all, and the stone answered with a vision that made her stagger back: the original signing, centuries ago, seven figures around this very shrine—and then the reforging, days ago, and in the reforging there were six figures standing witness and a seventh place in the circle that was empty. Not vacant. *Empty*, the way a word is empty after it's been scraped off a page, leaving only the ghost of pressure in the vellum.

The stone remembered that someone had stood there. It could not remember who.

* * *

By evening the stories had come in from all over, and they all agreed in the way that made Tamsin want to break things.

The Council scribes, reconstructing their records, found a gap in the rolls where a signatory's line should have been. The innkeeper at the Drowned Lantern, who had sworn up and down a week ago that he'd never housed a fugitive scribe, now genuinely had no memory of ever doing so—his ledger showed a room let to "no one, paid in advance," in handwriting the ledger-keeper swore was familiar and could not read aloud without weeping and could not say why. Ondrell's apprentices mourned their master and spoke of "the student he was teaching" in the abstract plural, as if a class had existed without students in it.

Even the river remembered wrong. Tamsin's own crew—Brack and little Fen and old Maren at the tiller—nodded along when she told them about the woman they'd smuggled downriver, the fugitive they'd all risked the noose for, and then Brack frowned and said, "Aye, Captain, course we did. What was her name, then?" And when Tamsin opened her mouth, nothing came out. Not a blank. A *nothing*, a hole shaped exactly like a name, and when she tried to speak around it her eyes filled and she didn't know why.

"You're crying, Captain," Fen said, alarmed.

"I know," Tamsin said. "I don't know why. Row."

* * *

It was near midnight when she found it.

She'd gone back to the *Kestrel's Debt*, moored crooked at the shrine dock with a splintered rail and a hold that smelled of smoke, meaning to drink herself blind on the medic's brandy and stop trying to remember what couldn't be remembered. Instead she sat on the deck in the dark, turning over the events of the battle the way you turn a stone in your fingers, and her hand kept going to her pocket.

Half-burned charm. She'd carried it since the saltmarsh—since the ambush, when Gideon had flung her gift open for the first time and the air itself had caught fire, and something small and warm had tumbled from the scribe's sleeve into the reeds where Tamsin crouched. She'd pocketed it without thinking, the reflex of a scavenger, and had forgotten it entirely until now.

She drew it out into the moonlight.

It was an ember-charm—one of the illegal blessings Gideon had once sold disguised as temple work, back in Ashen Vale, in another life. Most of it had burned away in the battle's heat; the wax seal was slagged, the binding-thread gone to ash. But a strip of the inner parchment survived, curled and browned, and on it, in a small, precise, slightly left-slanting hand:

*I will keep the light lit. —G.*

Tamsin read it four times. On the fifth reading her vision blurred, and she understood, all at once and completely, what the handwriting meant. The covenant had taken Gideon's name, her memory, her self—had scraped her out of stone and scroll and skull alike. But it could not unmake ink. Ink was just ink. Dead matter, dried pigment, outside the covenant's jurisdiction. The world had been made to forget Gideon Amberdale, and the world had obeyed, thoroughly, perfectly—

—but a scrap of parchment in a smuggler's pocket still held three words in her hand, and those three words were proof. Evidence, stubborn and physical, that a woman had existed who wrote like that, who signed her charms with a promise instead of a blessing, who had given everything she was so that two realms could go on breathing.

"Oh, you absolute fool," Tamsin whispered, and the tenderness in it broke her open. She laughed, or sobbed, or both, hunched over the little scrap like it was a coal she could shelter with her hands. "You couldn't even vanish properly. Had to leave your handwriting behind. Had to make me the keeper of it."

She pressed the charm to her mouth. It was faintly warm. She chose—with the full deliberate stubbornness of a woman who had survived twenty years on the river by deciding what was true and holding to it—to believe that warmth meant something.

"They'll forget you," she said to the night, to the sealed Veil, to whatever was or wasn't listening beyond it. "Council, stones, ledgers, all of it. Fine. Let them. But I'm a smuggler, love. Lying is my trade, and I'll lie to the whole world if I have to." She folded the scrap with exaggerated care and tucked it into the inner pocket, over her heart, where she kept the things she'd never surrender—not to hunters, not to creditors, not to gods. "I'll tell everyone you existed. Every port, every fire, every fool who'll listen. You'll be the most famous nobody in history. That's a promise, and unlike yours, mine doesn't cost me a thing."

The river moved beneath the hull, dark and patient. Far off across the water, in the direction of the ember-realm that no one could see anymore, the horizon held a faint warmth that hadn't been there before the reforging—a glow like dawn arriving early, or like a hearth relit in a far room of an enormous house.

Tamsin looked at it a long time.

Then, because grief is a cargo that must be moved or it sinks you, she stood, wiped her face on her sleeve, and went below to fetch the good brandy—the bottle she'd been saving for a homecoming that had somehow become a farewell. She poured two cups anyway. Set one on the deck across from her, where a passenger used to sit and ask endless questions about river-craft and write everything down in a small precise hand.

"To Gideon Amberdale," she said, raising the first cup to the empty seat, to the warm horizon, to the hole in the world shaped exactly like a friend. "Last signatory. Worst passenger I ever carried. Best crew I never had."

She drank. The second cup sat untouched, steaming faintly in the cold, and if the steam leaned—just slightly, just impossibly—toward the east and the warmth beyond the Veil, Tamsin Roe was drunk enough, or wise enough, or both, to notice.

And to pour a third cup.

* * *

Dawn came up gold over Duskharbor, the first true gold in a generation, and the city woke to a world remade. Tamsin stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* watching the light come off the water, the charm safe in her pocket, the story already assembling itself in her mind—the story she would tell in every tavern from here to the free cities' farthest edge, polishing it, protecting it, keeping the light lit the way a certain handwriting had promised.

Behind her, on the empty deck, the untouched cup from midnight had not been touched.

But the brandy in it was warm.
