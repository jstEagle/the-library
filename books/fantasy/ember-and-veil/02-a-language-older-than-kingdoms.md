## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The child slept like the dead until well past noon, which Jonah considered deeply unfair, since he himself had slept like a man being slowly pickled in anxiety and had woken at dawn with a crick in his neck and a damp sock.

He had spent the intervening hours doing what he always did when life presented him with an unsolvable problem: he tidied. He stacked his drafting papers into squares so precise they could have been used to calibrate instruments. He sharpened every pencil in the house, including three that had been stubs since before the exile. He swept a floor that had nothing left on it to sweep. And all the while, the child breathed under the good blanket — *his* good blanket, the one he'd been saving for a cold night worth having — with the serene, boneless confidence of someone who had never once worried about anything.

Jonah put water on to boil. Then he took it off again, because he didn't actually want tea, then put it back on, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

The child was maybe nine years old. Small for nine, or large for seven; it was difficult to say. Hair the color of wet riverbank clay, cut unevenly, as though by someone who had been in a hurry or had never seen scissors. A face still round with childhood but already carrying something older around the eyes — the way some harbors look old even when the town above them is new.

And the clothes. Jonah had examined them by candlelight at some shameful hour of the morning, telling himself it was practical concern rather than curiosity. The fabric was wool, but woven in a pattern he had never seen: interlocking spirals that resolved, if you stared too long, into shapes that refused to be stared at. No dye he recognized. No seams sewn the way any tailor in any of the eleven kingdoms would sew them. It was as if someone had described clothing to a person who had never seen clothing, and that person had done their best from memory of the description alone.

He had written all of this down, of course. Observation was the one prayer he knew how to perform.

*Found: one child. Sex indeterminate at distance (I am not a monster). Language unknown. Arrived via flood, which is not how children arrive. Currently occupying my bed.*

He looked at the last line and added: *Currently occupying my bed, and I resent how little I mind.*

The kettle sang. He made tea for one, then, after a moment of internal argument he lost, made tea for two and set the second cup where it would be found.

* * *

The child woke the way cats wake: all at once, mid-motion, sitting upright with eyes already open and already fixed on Jonah, who nearly dropped both cups.

"Good," Jonah said, when his heart had climbed back down out of his throat. "You're alive. That's — good. I'm glad. Do you understand me? Nod if you understand me."

The child did not nod. The child tilted their head, the way you might tilt your head at a dog attempting mathematics.

"I'm going to take that as no." Jonah set the tea down anyway. "My name is Jonah Joubert. This is my house. You are currently in it. There is tea. It's terrible, but it's hot, and hot is most of what tea has to offer."

The child said something.

It was not a word in any language Jonah knew, and Jonah knew — or could at least recognize — a disgraceful number of them. He had spent twelve years in this valley with nothing but paper and ink and the guild's stolen library of phrasebooks, and he had taught himself the sound-shapes of High Vellish and Low Vellish, coastal Trader's Cant, the click-tongues of the southern archipelagos, the tonal court-speech of Sarn-Elameth, even the whistled trade-language of the mountain passes, which he could produce only badly and which made him feel like a teakettle with ambitions.

This was none of them.

It wasn't that the sounds were strange. They were almost painfully ordinary — soft consonants, open vowels, the kind of syllables a mother uses to soothe a baby. What was strange was the *shape*. The words came in long unbroken lines, each flowing into the next without pause, and yet within those lines there was a rhythm, a repetition-with-variation, like waves arriving at a shore: never identical, never unrelated.

Jonah stood very still.

Because he *had* heard that shape before. Once. In a document he should never have opened, in a trunk he should never have inherited, in a language fragment the Guild of Cartographers had catalogued under a number instead of a name — because the Guild did not destroy things, the Guild merely *filed* them somewhere they would never be found again.

"Say that again," Jonah whispered.

The child, sensing perhaps that this was important, or perhaps simply enjoying having an audience, said it again. Longer this time. A whole sentence, unhurried, with the cadence of something recited from memory many times — a prayer, a lesson, a list of names.

Jonah crossed the room, knelt at the trunk at the foot of his bed — the trunk he had sworn, on at least four separate occasions, never to open again — and lifted out the false bottom with hands that were not entirely steady.

Beneath it lay the forbidden folios. And among them, wrapped in oilcloth, the fragment: three pages of transliteration, a dead guildsman's attempt to capture a living tongue, annotated in the margins in his father's handwriting. *Structure non-paratactic. Repetition functions as grammar, not ornament. No word for 'alone.' Repeated attempts to translate the term rendered as 'accompanied-by-the-place-itself.' Nonsense. Beautiful nonsense.*

Jonah laid the pages beside the child, who looked at them, and then at him, and then — with the gravity of a scholar conferring a degree — spoke again, matching the rhythm exactly, and pointed at the page.

"That's it," Jonah said stupidly. "That's the same. That's — you're speaking it. You're actually speaking it. Nobody speaks it. It's a dead language. It's worse than dead, it's a *filed* language, it's—" He stopped, because the child was looking at him with an expression of such patient, pitying kindness that he felt suddenly like the child in the exchange. "You have no idea what I'm saying. Of course you don't. Wonderful. I've finally met someone who speaks the one language I can't, and I'm monologuing at them in two they don't know."

He laughed — a small, cracked sound, surprised out of him. He couldn't remember the last time he'd laughed aloud in this house. The house probably couldn't either.

"All right," he said. "Paper. Obviously. We do this properly."

* * *

What followed was the strangest and best afternoon of Jonah Joubert's adult life.

They built a bridge between languages out of everything the house contained. The child learned *yes* and *no* with suspicious speed — suspiciously fast, Jonah noted, faster than any learner had any right to, as though the words were being remembered rather than acquired. Jonah learned, through pointing and repetition, that the child's name was Sef. He learned it three times before it stuck, because each time Sef said it, the name sounded slightly different — not mispronounced, but *inflected*, as though the name were less a label than a small song that happened to mean *Sef*.

"Sef," Jonah repeated, and Sef beamed at him with such uncomplicated delight that something in Jonah's chest, a door he'd thought long painted shut, creaked open an inch.

Then Sef took the pencil.

Not the charcoal, not the pen — the pencil, chosen with the discernment of a master, and Jonah watched, with the particular horror of a craftsman watching someone handle his tools, as the child turned it over twice, found the correct grip by instinct, and began to draw.

At first it was just lines. But Jonah had spent his whole life reading lines, and within thirty seconds his professional soul sat up and paid attention. These were survey lines. Measured. Confident. Drawn from *memory*, not imagination — imagination wanders, but these strokes went where they went with the certainty of a hand following a road it has walked.

A coastline grew across the page.

Jonah's tea went cold. He didn't notice.

The coastline curved north, split into a double bay — no, not a bay, a drowned river valley, twin arms reaching inland — then ran east along a shelf of cliffs broken by three coves, then swung south in a long shallow arc toward a headland shaped, unmistakably, like a sleeping animal. And there, in the crook of the northern arm, a city: drawn not as blocks but as rings, terraces rising from a harbor, with a causeway running out to an island that held a single tall structure — a tower? a lighthouse? a bell tower, Jonah decided, there was a bell curve to it, a weight at the top that bells have.

His mouth had gone dry.

Because he knew this coastline. Not from any atlas — no atlas held it. He knew it from the inside of his own skull, from the dreams that came to him some nights, the ones he never wrote down because writing them down felt like admitting to something. For twenty years, since boyhood, he had dreamed of a double-bayed coast and a ringed city and a bell tower on an island, and he had woken from those dreams with salt on his lips and grief in his chest for a place he had never been, and he had told himself it was just a mind doing what idle minds do, building countries out of spare parts.

He had never drawn it. He had never dared. A mapmaker who maps his own dreams stops being trusted with real maps.

"You're drawing my dream," he said quietly.

Sef looked up. Held his gaze. And then — deliberately, unmistakably — pointed at the drawing, then at Jonah, then pressed the pencil back into his hand.

*Finish it,* the gesture said. *You know the rest.*

Jonah's hands were trembling again, and this time he let them. He drew the river that fed the northern arm. He drew the road along the cliff shelf. His pencil moved with a fluency that frightened him, filling in details he had never consciously known — a bridge with seven arches here, a market square shaped like a fan there — and every detail landed on the page feeling less invented than *recovered*, like brushing dust off something that had been waiting underneath.

When he finished, the map covered two sheets joined with paste, and it was, by any technical measure, the finest work he had ever done. Better than his commissioned charts. Better than anything. Because it wasn't drawn from observation or report or even skill.

It was drawn from homesickness.

"This isn't possible," he said. His voice came out thin. "There is no country with this coastline. I've read every gazetteer the Guild ever printed. I've memorized the hydrography of eleven kingdoms and the disputed territories besides. This place doesn't exist. It doesn't—"

He stopped.

Because Sef had gone very still, and was looking at the map with an expression Jonah had no framework for — grief and love and recognition braided together, the face of someone seeing a portrait of a dead parent. And Sef touched the ringed city with one finger, light as a blessing, and said a long sentence in that wave-rhythm language, and though Jonah understood not one word of it, he understood the sentence completely.

*Home. This is home. Where is it. Why is it gone.*

"It doesn't exist," Jonah said again, uselessly, because saying it aloud was supposed to make it true and it wasn't working. "It can't exist. If it existed, it would be *mapped*. Everything that exists gets mapped. That's the whole — that's the entire point of—"

And then the cold arrived.

It came up through him slowly, the way winter comes through floorboards. Because there was a third possibility, one he had never let himself think, one that lived in the sealed folios and the numbered catalogue entries and the Edict whose full text he had never read but whose existence he had always known the way you know a scar. The Guild did not only map worlds. The Guild had, once — the records whispered it, the oldest members denied it too smoothly — done the other thing. The thing cartographers were never supposed to do.

Unmap.

Erase a place so thoroughly that no chart showed it, no record named it, no dream — except, apparently, *his* dreams, and why his, why always his — remembered it. File it under a number. Fold it away behind a false bottom.

And here was a child wearing impossible cloth, speaking a filed language, drawing a coastline that existed nowhere on earth, washed up in a valley where magic could not follow — as if the child had been *aimed* here, as if this magicless valley were the one place in the world where whatever was hunting this child could not reach.

As if someone had thrown a lantern into the only dark room in the house.

Sef was watching him. Patient. Trusting. Utterly unaware that the man across the table was heir — heir, the trunk had said so, the seals and the signet and the letters all said *heir* — to the very institution that could answer the question on the child's face, and that the answer might be the worst thing either of them would ever learn.

Jonah looked down at the map. At the bell tower on its island. At the small figure he now realized Sef had drawn on the harbor terrace, standing at the edge of the water, waving.

"Stay here," he heard himself say. "Don't touch the trunk. Don't touch anything. I have to — there are documents. There's a name. There's an edict, and I think—" He swallowed. "I think my family helped make your home disappear."

Sef didn't understand the words. But Sef understood the tone, the way anyone understands a storm coming, and reached across the table and put one small hand flat on the map, between the city and the sea, holding it down. Holding it *here*.

Jonah went to the trunk, knelt before it, and broke a promise he had kept for twelve years.

The false bottom came away, and beneath the forbidden folios, wrapped in black wax-cloth and sealed with the Guild's compass-and-veil sigil, lay a scroll he had never allowed himself to touch.

The Edict of Unmapping.

His father's seal was already broken on it. His father had read it. His father had signed it.

And below the signature, in his father's careful surveyor's hand, a single marginal note, the ink browned with age:

*If anyone ever comes asking about Veylmark — it was real. God forgive us, it was real.*
