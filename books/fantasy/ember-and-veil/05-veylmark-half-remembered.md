## 5. Veylmark, Half-Remembered

### Sef

The bells stopped ringing the moment they crossed the fogline.

Sef had expected them to keep going — bells were bells, and bells in Sef's memory rang whether anyone listened or not. But here, on the near side of the mist, the sound simply ended, cut clean as a thread with scissors, and what replaced it was worse than silence. It was *attention*. The whole valley behind them seemed to lean forward and watch them go.

Jonah's hand was still around theirs. His palm had gone cold and damp, which Sef found strange, because Jonah's hands were always warm — warm as bread, warm as the stove he'd let Sef sleep beside for three nights while the storm wore itself out against his shutters. Warm hands meant safe. Cold hands meant the world had gotten into him.

"Jonah," Sef said. "Your heart is being loud."

"I'm aware," Jonah said. His voice did the thing it did when he was frightened but pretending to be a grown-up about it: it went flat and careful, like a man reading a map aloud so he doesn't have to look at what's actually in front of him. "Stay close. And if I say run—"

"You'll say run."

"And if I say *don't look*—"

"I'll look," Sef said, "but slowly."

That startled a laugh out of him, one short breath through the nose, and Sef felt his hand warm by exactly one degree. Good. Grown-ups cooled down when you made them laugh. Sef had learned that much in three nights and one long walk.

They went down through the fog together.

It wasn't like other fog. Other fog got into your mouth and made everything taste of nothing. This fog tasted of *names*. Sef couldn't have explained it to anyone who hadn't been born half-erased, but walking through it was like walking past houses where everyone was asleep and dreaming out loud — fragments drifting past their ears, a woman laughing at something a man said forty years ago, a dog's name, a recipe read twice because someone kept losing their place, a child being called in from a river at dusk, *Veyla, Veyla, supper, the water's getting cold.*

Sef stopped walking.

"What is it?" Jonah whispered.

"That's my name," Sef said. "Sort of. The other part of it." They hadn't known that until the fog said it. That was how it worked with Sef: they learned themselves the way other people learned weather, from outside, all at once, without warning.

"Veyla," Jonah repeated softly, testing it. Then, more carefully: "Is that — do you want me to call you that?"

Sef considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "No. That's her name. The girl in the dream. I'm Sef. She's — she's the part that got left behind when they cut the country up." They took his hand again. "But she knows the way down. Come on."

The fog thinned, and then, all at once, it wasn't fog anymore.

It was morning.

Not a real morning — Sef could tell the difference now, the way you can tell a painted egg from a live one if you've been both. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, gold and gentle, and it fell on a hillside covered in orchards that hummed. Actually hummed, low and contented, like bees that had decided to be trees instead. Beyond the orchards a road ran down toward a town with red roofs and white walls and a bell tower, and smoke rose from chimneys in slow, thoughtful curls, and everything — everything — was slightly too vivid, colors turned up past the point of honesty, green so green it ached.

"Oh," Jonah said. Just that. He took off his hat, which Sef had never seen him do outdoors, and held it against his chest like a man entering a church.

"It's real," Sef said. "Mostly. It's as real as remembering makes it."

And then, because Sef was six-going-on-ancient and had waited a very long time to show somebody this, they grabbed Jonah's cold hand and pulled him down the hill toward the humming orchards, running, both of them, down through grass that bent to let them pass.

* * *

### Jonah

Later — though *later* was an unstable word here, and he would learn to distrust it — Jonah sat under an apple tree that smelled of his grandmother's kitchen and tried to be a cartographer about the whole thing.

It was not going well. Cartography, he had been taught from the cradle, rested on certain assumptions. That places hold still. That two observers see the same place. That a place does not, under any circumstances, *love you back*.

The orchard loved him back. He was nearly sure of it. Every tree he looked at grew a little more detailed, apples fattening on the branch even as he watched, bark roughening into individuality, because he was looking, because he remembered orchards, because some deep-buried guild lesson in his skull insisted that orchards have ladders and ladders have splinters and splinters have names, and the country was drinking all of it down gratefully, like a guest offered water after a long walk.

"Sef," he said. "The apples just ripened. While I was looking at them."

"Mm." Sef lay in the grass nearby, and Jonah kept glancing over because the sight refused to settle into sense. The child was *glowing*. Not metaphorically — there was actual light under their skin now, faint and golden, like a lamp behind paper. In the valley Sef had been thin and gray and coughing; here every hour made them taller somehow, or rounder, or simply more, cheeks filling out, eyes gone bright as new coins. "Things get more real when you remember them. That's the whole rule. That's the only rule."

"And the reverse?"

Sef rolled over and looked at him, and didn't answer, and the not-answer was worse than any answer.

He got up, mostly to stop looking at the child, and walked to the edge of the orchard where the grass gave out. Here the country simply *stopped* — not a wall, not a cliff, just a place where detail ran out, where the apple trees became sketches of apple trees became suggestions of apple trees became a pale gray nothing that his eye slid off of no matter how he tried to fix it. He stood at the border of the nothing and did what he was made to do: he measured it with his eyes. Slope of the fade. Rate of dissolution. Where the last fence-post stood solid and where its neighbor was already only the idea of a fence-post.

Then he made the mistake of turning around.

Behind him, the orchard stretched away whole and humming, and beyond it the red-roofed town rang its bells, and between him and the town lay a mile of country so dense with remembered life that it hurt — hedgerows, footpaths, a shrine with fresh flowers on it (fresh! who was bringing flowers? memories brought flowers?), washing strung between cottages, snapping in a wind he could feel on his face. All of it built out of nothing but the insistence of people who had loved it. All of it dissolving at the edges, everywhere, always, a candle burning down from every side at once.

He thought: *I know why this place died. My family signed the paper.*

He thought: *I am standing inside the wound my name made.*

His legs sat him down without consulting him. The grass received him kindly, which was somehow unbearable.

"Jonah?" Sef had come to stand beside him, small and luminous, head tipped. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're sorry about something you haven't told me yet."

Jonah laughed, or produced a sound adjacent to laughter. "You can't possibly read faces that well. You've known me four days."

"I've known you since before I knew me," Sef said, matter-of-fact, sitting down next to him and leaning against his arm. "You feel like the map feels. Before I ever saw you, I dreamed of a man drawing coastlines in a cold room, and the coastlines were mine." A pause. "Also your face is easy. It has very big letters on it."

He wanted to ask about the dreams. He wanted, badly, to ask about the dreams. Instead he heard himself say: "Sef. When we find whoever's left here — the council, the archive, whoever keeps this place — there may be people who hate the guild. Who hate cartographers. Who have reason to." He watched the washing snap on its line. "I need you to know that whatever they say about us will probably be true."

Sef was quiet a moment. Then: "Okay. But you carried me out of a flood. So they can hate the guild. You're not the guild. You're the man with the warm hands."

"The hands are cold right now."

"They're warming up," Sef said, and pressed one small palm flat against his knuckles, and Jonah found that he could not argue, because it was true.

* * *

### Sef

The town was called Bellharbor, or had been, or was called that in the strongest surviving memory of itself, and Sef loved it instantly and completely, the way you love a thing you've been homesick for without knowing the address.

The streets behaved properly here — cobbles firm underfoot, doors that opened, cats that regarded them with the specific contempt of cats who have been remembered well. But Sef noticed what Jonah noticed too, because Jonah kept making small wounded noises: gaps. A street that ended mid-cobble in soft gray haze, as if the town had been bitten. A house with only one wall, wallpaper cheerful on the standing side, nothing behind it. A fountain that poured water upward into a sky that forgot to catch it.

"Don't stare at the gaps," Sef whispered, having learned this rule from somewhere inside themselves. "Staring makes them self-conscious."

"Does staring make them *worse*?"

"Worse or better. Depends what you remember when you look." Sef demonstrated: they looked hard at the one-walled house and thought about the dream-girl, Veyla, who had lived in a yellow house with a blue door, and thought about the smell of bread, and the way mothers shout up staircases, and — the wall grew a second wall. Shyly. Like a plant.

Jonah made a noise like a kettle. "Do that again."

"It costs something," Sef said, and didn't explain, because explaining would mean saying *it costs me*, and Jonah had that face again, the big-letter face, and Sef was tired of the face. Besides — it didn't cost much. Not yet. Here, in the country, Sef was rich. Here Sef could feel the whole erased nation pooled around them like groundwater, and every street they walked drank a little of it and stood up straighter.

Look: a fishmonger's stall, solid, smelling gloriously of salt and ice. Look: a schoolhouse with sums chalked on a slate. Look: an old man on a bench — not a person, Sef knew, not really, more like a memory wearing a coat, nodding pleasantly, made of somebody's Tuesdays — who touched his cap to them and said, "Afternoon, little ember. Afternoon, stranger," and Sef's chest went so tight with happiness they could hardly breathe.

An old man had spoken to them. In Veylmark. As if Sef belonged.

"Little ember," Jonah repeated quietly, once the bench was behind them. "She called you that too. Ilsa. On the ridge."

"I know what I am," Sef said. "Here, anyway. I'm the newest thing. Everything else is old remembering. I'm the only *new* memory this country's made since—" Since the erasure. Since the Edict. Since men with instruments had folded a nation up like a letter and burned it. "That's why I'm strong here. And that's why—" They stopped.

"Why what?" Jonah asked, and Sef looked up at him, at the tall anxious kind man with ink on his fingers and guilt in his big-letter face, and decided — not for the first time — that some truths keep better unspoken, like jam.

"Nothing," Sef said. "Why what, what? Look, there's a bakery. It's mostly real. I can smell it."

* * *

### Jonah

The bakery was mostly real, and the bread was entirely impossible, and Jonah ate his slice in the little square with tears standing in his eyes that he blamed, out loud, on onions, despite there being no onions anywhere in evidence. Sef had the grace not to mention it.

He was fading. He'd known it since the orchard, but the square confirmed it the way a ledger confirms a suspicion. It was small things. His boots left prints that filled in a beat too fast, as if the cobbles were healing over him. When he reached for the bench to sit, his fingers passed through the first inch of it before finding purchase, like sinking into shallow water. And once — only once, and he hoped Sef hadn't seen — his own hand went briefly translucent in the sunlight, veins showing through like leaves in a lamp.

The country was eating him. Politely. Gratefully. It was starving, and he was made of details, and every step he took through Veylmark, the country sipped at him — his edges, his excess, whatever part of Jonah Joubert wasn't strictly necessary. He could feel it the way you feel a haircut: nothing missing that you can name, everything a little lighter.

He understood the mechanism, roughly, because he understood guild mechanisms, because he'd been raised on them at his father's knee alongside his letters. Erasure was never destruction. Destruction was crude and left evidence. Erasure was *redistribution*: take a thing apart and give its pieces elsewhere, and the ledger balances, and no one grieves because no one remembers there was anything to grieve for. The empire had unmapped Veylmark by feeding it, piece by piece, into forgetting. And now here was Veylmark, reassembled out of scraps of collective memory, running a deficit, hungry — and here came Jonah, walking pantry-adjacent, full to the brim with vivid particular life.

*Sef grows stronger here,* he thought, watching the child chase pigeons across the square, glowing like a struck match. *I grow weaker. The arithmetic isn't subtle.*

He should tell the child. He believed, professionally and morally, in telling people things.

Instead he took out his journal — his poor sodden, storm-swollen journal, dried now and warped, its pages waving like sea-grass — and began to draw the square. Because that was the other thing he'd figured out, alone, under the humming apple trees, and had not yet said aloud to anyone:

When he drew a thing in this country, the thing lasted longer.

His sketch of the orchard, done in thirty seconds of frantic pencil, had kept twelve apple trees solid through a night that should have eaten ten of them. The country clung to his lines like a drowning man clings to driftwood. Guild training, guild blood, guild curse — whatever it was, his maps were load-bearing here.

So he drew the square. The fountain (he gave it a basin to catch the water; the basin bloomed into being, stone-gray and grateful). The one-walled house (he drew its missing side from memory of a hundred similar houses; somewhere behind him he heard masonry assemble itself with a sound like a sigh). The bench. The pigeons. Sef, mid-chase, arms out, lit from within.

"There," he said, when he finished. "Now it's true twice. Once because everyone remembers it, and once because I wrote it down."

Sef came and looked over his arm, and went very still.

"You drew me," they said.

"I draw everything. It's the job."

"You drew me *first*," Sef said, in the tone of someone receiving a medal, and Jonah, who had survived exile and betrayal and the fog-line and his own family's name, discovered that this was the praise that undid him.

* * *

### Sef

They slept that night in the yellow house with the blue door — Sef's house, or Veyla's, or the memory of one, which amounted to the same thing here. Jonah fixed the door with a drawn hinge. Sef fixed the beds by thinking hard about blankets. The house, shy and pleased, creaked around them like an old dog settling.

From the window, Sef could see the whole town breathing — lamps coming on in windows one by one as the evening remembered lamplight, the bell tower standing black and friendly against a sky that couldn't quite decide on stars. Somewhere out past the edges, the gray nothing gnawed patiently at the borders of everything. Somewhere behind them, along the ridgeline, imperial tripods stood in the living grass, patient as spiders.

And somewhere ahead — Sef felt it in their sternum, a pull like a fishhook of warmth — the capital waited, and the archive, and the Last Door.

"Jonah," Sef said, in the dark. "Are you awake?"

A pause too long for a sleeping man. "I'm awake."

"When we get to the Door. Whatever it wants. We pay it together, okay? Half each. That's the deal. Promise."

Another pause. Longer. Through the floorboards Sef could hear the house listening too.

"Promise," Jonah said, in the exact voice of a man lying beautifully, and Sef, who had very big letters of their own to read by, closed their eyes and let him have it — for now.

Tomorrow they would walk east, toward the capital, following the fishhook-pull in Sef's chest. Tomorrow the roads might forget themselves beneath their feet. Tomorrow, perhaps, the surveyors' instruments would begin to sing.

Tonight, the yellow house held them, and the country breathed, and Sef — newest memory, little ember, last fragment of a murdered nation — slept without dreaming for the first time in their whole small ancient life, because for once, they were already home.
