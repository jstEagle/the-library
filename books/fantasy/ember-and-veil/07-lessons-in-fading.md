## 7. Lessons in Fading

Ilsa taught the way rivers cut stone — patiently, relentlessly, and with a certain contempt for anything soft enough to be moved.

She had claimed the back room of the archive-house for her lessons, a chamber that had once been a schoolroom, if the small desks bolted to the floor were any indication. Their surfaces were carved with generations of children's initials, and every morning Jonah watched those initials fade a little more, the letters going shallow as pond ice in spring. By the time he'd finished his tea, some of them were gone entirely.

"You see it now," Ilsa said, catching him staring at a desk where someone named *Mirelle* had been scratched into the wood so many years ago that only a ghost of the M remained. "That's what unmapping does. It doesn't burn things. Burning is honest — you get smoke, ash, something to bury. Erasure just *thins* everything. The desk forgets it was carved. The carving forgets it was made. And the name—" she tapped the smooth wood where Mirelle had been, "—forgets it was ever spoken."

Jonah set down his cup. "And people?"

"People are just names that learned to walk." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. She was arranging her materials on the largest desk: a slate, a stick of chalk that looked ordinary until you noticed it never wore down, and a stack of paper so old it had gone the color of weak tea. "Sit. You wanted to understand how your guild's machine works. Understanding requires sitting."

He sat. He was learning that Ilsa's insults were a kind of currency, and that being insulted meant she considered you worth spending words on.

"Now." She drew a circle on the slate. "A country, before the Edict. Everything inside it is *mapped* — not by your cartographers, mind, but by living. Every footpath worn by feet. Every door handle polished by hands. Every child who knows the way to grandmother's house without thinking about it. That's mapping too. The truest kind. A country is mapped by everyone in it, all the time, whether they know it or not."

"And the Edict?"

"The Edict did two things." She drew a second circle beside the first, then scraped most of it away with her thumb, leaving a ragged crescent. "First, it struck Veylmark from every imperial map, chart, ledger, and deed. Second — and this is the part your father's guild understood better than the empire did — it struck Veylmark from *memory*. Not all at once. That would have left a wound, and wounds are remembered. No. Slowly. Like the desks."

"Like forgetting where you put your keys," Jonah said.

"Like forgetting you ever owned keys." She fixed him with her pale eyes. "Here is the arithmetic of erasure, cartographer, and I want you to hold it carefully, because it is the cruelest sum ever devised. Every day, an unmapped thing loses one memory of itself. One. Not the biggest memory. Not the most important. A *random* one. The taste of a particular bread. A neighbor's laugh. The color of a door. Each loss is small enough that no one notices, and each one makes the country thinner, and the thinner it gets, the faster it thins. Do you follow?"

Jonah thought of the streets he'd walked in Veylmark — solid where remembered, mist where forgotten. "The mist isn't weather."

"The mist is arithmetic." Ilsa's chalk hovered over the ragged crescent. "Veylmark has been unmapped for sixty-one years. Sixty-one times three hundred and sixty-five days of losses. There are places in the capital where nothing remembers itself at all anymore. You could walk through them and they wouldn't notice you, because noticing requires remembering, and there's nothing left there to do the remembering." She paused. "There are also people like that. We don't speak of them. Or rather — we can't. We've lost the memories required to speak of them."

The room felt colder. Jonah glanced at the doorway, half expecting Sef to be there, because he always half-expected Sef to be there now, the way you expect sunlight from a window.

"And the guild knew this," he said quietly. "When they built the Edict. They knew the arithmetic."

"They *wrote* the arithmetic." Ilsa set down the chalk with a click. "Your ancestors didn't erase Veylmark out of malice, cartographer. Malice I could forgive. They did it out of *craft*. They found the most elegant solution to a political problem and executed it beautifully, and elegance is so much harder to hate than cruelty. Somewhere in your trunk of papers there is probably a letter congratulating them on it."

"There is," Jonah said. "I've read it. It's framed."

Ilsa laughed — a short, sharp sound, surprised out of her. Then her face closed again. "Good. At least you're honest about what you inherited."

* * *

Sef arrived at midday, the way Sef arrived everywhere: suddenly, loudly, and carrying something that should not have fit in a child's pockets.

Today it was a bird. Or the memory of a bird — a wren made of the same half-solid stuff as the streets, perched on Sef's finger and singing a song that Jonah realized, with a lurch, he almost recognized. It sounded like a tune his mother might have hummed, except his mother had never been within a thousand miles of Veylmark.

"It followed me from the bell tower," Sef announced, holding up the wren like a trophy. "It's mine now. I'm keeping it."

"You cannot keep a memory-bird," Ilsa said.

"Why not?"

"Because it will lose a memory of itself tonight, and tomorrow it will be slightly less bird, and in a year it will be a rumor of feathers, and you will have kept nothing. That is how fading works, little fragment. Nothing here can be kept. Only remembered, and remembering is a loan, not a gift."

Sef frowned at the wren. The wren sang its almost-familiar song. Something in Jonah's chest went tight and complicated.

"What about me?" Sef asked. It wasn't a dramatic question. It was asked the way children ask whether it will rain — checking the sky, adjusting plans. "Am I losing memories too?"

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had changed — gentler, though she seemed annoyed at herself for it. "No, Sef. That's what makes you impossible. You're not an old memory thinning out. You're a *new* one. The last new memory Veylmark will ever make. Every day you live here, you add instead of subtract. You're the only thing in this whole country growing."

Sef absorbed this with the serious expression Jonah was beginning to know well — the look of a child doing enormous arithmetic behind their eyes. "So I'm the opposite of the desks."

"In every way that matters."

"Then I'll remember the bird for both of us." Sef cupped the wren in both hands and brought it close to their face, and whispered something to it in that old unrecorded language, and Jonah — who had spent weeks now learning to hear the shape of that tongue even without understanding it — caught the meaning the way you catch a scent through a wall: *I will keep you. I will say your song out loud every morning so it stays.*

The wren trilled once and dissolved into the light coming through the window, and Sef didn't cry, but stood very still for a moment with their empty hands cupped together, keeping the shape of it.

Jonah found he had to look away.

* * *

That evening, Ilsa gave him the second lesson, and it was worse than the first.

They sat in the archive proper, among shelves that held — Ilsa claimed — copies of everything Veylmark had ever been: every deed, every recipe, every letter between sisters, every complaint filed against a baker whose loaves were consistently underweight. The archive was Ilsa's life's work, assembled from the memories of the remnant, transcribed onto paper that existed here and therefore faded here, so that she was forever copying copies of copies, a scribe bailing the sea with a spoon.

"Ask your question," she said. "You've been circling it since noon like a gull after a fishing boat."

Jonah turned his cup in his hands. "Sef said something yesterday. About the Door. They said the council told them the Door needs 'a life's worth of memory' to open. And they said it like it was a price someone else was going to pay." He looked up. "Who's paying, Ilsa?"

Her face did something complicated — a flicker of respect, quickly buried. "You're less stupid than your guild deserves."

"That's not an answer."

"No, it isn't." She rose and walked the shelf line, trailing her fingers along spines of fading books. "The Last Door is the one threshold the Edict couldn't close, because closing it would have required admitting it existed, and the Edict's whole genius was denial. Through that door, Veylmark could be re-anchored — written back into the world's maps, its memories restored, its borders made real again. But a door that was erased cannot simply swing open. It needs a key, and the key must be *memory given freely*, in quantity. A life's worth. All the memories of a single willing soul, poured into the lock."

"And then what happens to the soul?"

"They live." Ilsa's voice was flat. "They walk out the other side with their body, their health, their skills. They simply don't remember giving anything. Or receiving it. Or the road here, or the faces, or the reason their heart aches when it rains. The council calls it mercy. I call it the same arithmetic, wearing kinder clothes."

Jonah sat with that. Outside the archive window, Veylmark's evening was happening — a sunset rendered in colors that flickered slightly, like a painting reconsidering itself. Somewhere a bell rang a hour that might not exist tomorrow.

"And Sef thinks a stranger will pay this price," he said.

"Sef thinks hope is a plan." Ilsa turned. "Listen to me, cartographer, because I will say this once. Your blood makes you dangerous here. Guild-born memory is dense — trained, indexed, *deliberate*. A lifetime of a cartographer's remembering is worth ten lifetimes of a farmer's. If the council learns what you are, they will not ask you. They will take it as their due. So whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything."

"—whatever you are *not* planning," she continued, merciless, "do it far from that child. Because Sef loves easily, and you are easy to love, and love is exactly the sort of memory that gets spent."

* * *

He found Sef on the roof of the archive-house, which was apparently where Sef went when the world got heavy — a habit Jonah recognized with uncomfortable intimacy, having spent thirty years on roofs, hillsides, and other high lonely places himself.

"You're not supposed to be up here," Jonah said, hauling himself through the skylight.

"You're not either."

"I'm a grown man. I'm allowed to be irresponsible."

Sef grinned and scooted over on the ridge tiles, and Jonah settled beside them. Below, the half-city glowed with its uncertain lamplight. Above, stars that sixty-one years of forgetting had thinned to a sparse scatter still managed, stubbornly, to shine.

For a while neither spoke. This was another thing Jonah had learned about Sef: the child could be silent in a way adults rarely managed — not waiting, not plotting the next word, just present.

"Ilsa says I'm the last new memory," Sef said eventually. "Of Veylmark. Did you know that?"

"She told me today."

"Do you think that's why I'm scared of the wrong things?" Sef pulled their knees up. "Everyone here is scared of disappearing. And I'm scared of — I don't know. Of being the *end*. Like a book where the last page is blank and everyone keeps saying, oh, but what lovely paper." They rested their chin on their knees. "I'd rather be a good page than the last one."

Jonah considered several answers — the comforting ones, the deflecting ones, the ones a responsible adult gives a frightened child. What came out instead was the truth.

"When I was exiled to that valley," he said, "I thought my life was over. Finished. A book with no more pages. And then you knocked on my door, soaked through, speaking a language nobody's heard in sixty years, and I want you to know that in that moment I was mostly annoyed."

Sef snorted.

"But underneath the annoyance," Jonah went on, "there was this — this feeling. Like a map with a blank space on it, and then suddenly, a coastline. I'd spent thirty years drawing coastlines I'd never seen, and there you were, an actual shore, arriving at my actual door." He shook his head at himself. "What I mean is: you're not the last page, Sef. You're the first line of a chapter nobody expected. Those are different things entirely."

Sef was quiet. Then, in a smaller voice: "Even if the chapter ends soon?"

"Especially then." Jonah reached over and straightened the child's collar, a fussy, unnecessary gesture that he suspected was mostly for his own benefit. "We write good pages while we have paper. That's the whole job. Ilsa can keep her arithmetic."

"She'd say arithmetic doesn't care about our feelings."

"She would. But she'd say it while copying another book by hand to save it, so I feel comfortable ignoring her."

Sef laughed — a real laugh, bright and startling against the darkening rooftops — and leaned sideways until their head found Jonah's shoulder, the way small creatures decide trust without negotiating it. Jonah went very still, the way you do when a wild thing lands on you, and then, slowly, let his own head tip until it rested against the child's hair.

Below them the half-city flickered. Somewhere in the fogline, surveyors patrolled with instruments designed to unmake. Somewhere in the council chambers, men and women measured a child's life in memories-per-day. Somewhere in his inherited trunk, his father's signature waited on a document that had started all of it.

But on the roof, for this one hour, there was warmth and starlight and the weight of a small head against his shoulder, and Jonah thought: *let the countdown run. I will spend every page well.*

He did not yet know how few pages remained. He did not yet know that the council had already begun asking questions about the stranger with the guild accent — or that by week's end, one faction among them would stop asking and start acting.

But the wren's almost-familiar song was still caught somewhere in his chest, and he hummed it, quietly, into the dark, keeping it alive for both of them.
