## 8. The Empire's Surveyors

The fogline tastes like the inside of a bell.

That is how I would describe it to Jonah, if Jonah could taste what I taste. He says the fogline smells like wet slate and old iron. He says it hums. But Jonah has a body that was built in a valley where magic cannot follow, and so his senses are honest and plain, like bread. Mine were baked in a country made of remembering, and everything I feel comes to me doubled — once as sensation, once as memory of sensation, which is not the same thing at all.

The fogline tastes like the inside of a bell: copper and cold and a ringing you feel in your teeth rather than hear. When we walk along it, heading east toward the capital that used to be called Veylmark-Under-the-Crown before the empire unmade the crown and the mark and most of the *under*, I keep my tongue pressed to the roof of my mouth so the taste cannot get in.

Ilsa walks ahead. She always walks ahead. She says it is because she knows the way, which is true, and also because she does not like walking behind people, which is truer.

Jonah walks beside me, which he manages by walking slightly too fast and pretending he isn't. He has his journal out even now, sketching as we go, one-handed, the way he does everything — as if the world might revoke itself if he stops recording it. Given where we are, this is not an unreasonable fear.

"You're drawing the fog," I say.

"I'm drawing the *edge* of the fog," he says. "There's a difference. Fog is weather. An edge is a decision."

"It looks like a scribble."

"It looks like a scribble to *you*," he says, wounded, "because you have the artistic sensibility of a startled goat."

Ilsa, without turning around: "He's right. You do."

I love them both so much it makes my chest hurt, which Ilsa tells me is a symptom. Everything in Veylmark is a symptom. Hunger here means the land is hungry. Tiredness means the road is tired of carrying you. Love means you are becoming permanent somewhere, and permanence attracts attention.

We had been walking since the grey part of morning — though morning here is less a time than a mood — when Ilsa stopped so suddenly that Jonah walked into her satchel and apologized to it.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Smoke," she said. "Not woodsmoke."

I tasted it then, under the bell-copper: something burnt and sweet and wrong, like sugar held too long over a flame. And beneath that, fainter, a sound I knew from nowhere and everywhere, the way you know your own name — the small precise *scratch-scratch* of a pen on survey paper.

"The surveyors," Ilsa said. Her voice did not change at all, which was how I learned to be afraid. When Ilsa's voice changes, she is merely angry. When it goes flat and quiet, someone is about to be erased. "They're patrolling the line. They must have felt us cross."

Jonah looked at me. In the half-light his face was all angles and worry, a map of a man drawn by someone in a hurry. "Can we go around?"

"There is no around," Ilsa said. "There is only along. The fogline runs through everything we love. That was rather the point of it." She turned, and her eyes found mine, and for one moment they were not flat at all; they were full of something old and furious and grieving. "Sef. Listen to me. If they find you — if they *measure* you —"

"I know," I said. "I'm a new memory. New memories can be unmapped."

"You're more than that," she said. "You're the last new memory. If they take you, there will never be another. Not ever. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

I understood. Understanding it and believing it were two different countries, and I lived in the space between.

* * *

They came out of the fog the way a sentence comes out of silence.

There were six of them, and they were wrong in a way I still cannot fully explain, except to say that everything about them was *ruled*. Their coats had edges sharp enough to trust. Their boots struck the ground in perfect alternation, left-right-left-right, and where they stepped, the ground flattened — grass became gravel became nothing, a little strip of empire laid down behind each heel like a hem being sewn.

Their faces were the worst. Not cruel. Cruelty requires interest. Their faces were *blank the way a form is blank*, waiting to be filled in with whatever needed measuring.

The tallest one carried a brass instrument on a tripod, and when it turned toward me I felt it turn *through* me, a cold thin wire passing from my forehead to the back of my skull.

"Anomaly," it said. Its voice sounded like a ledger closing. "Unregistered presence. Commencing re-erasure."

"Sef," Jonah breathed, and put his hand on my shoulder, and pulled me behind him, which was absurd, because Jonah is a cartographer, a man whose weapons are pens, and these were the empire's surveyors, the same guild that had once employed his father, the men who had taught the world to forget my home.

But he stood there anyway. Broad and mortal and terrified, standing between me and the instrument like a fence post against a flood.

I want to remember that. Whatever else happens, I want to remember that.

"Stand aside," said the surveyor. "This does not concern citizens of record."

"He's a child," Jonah said.

"This is a discrepancy," said the surveyor. "Discrepancies are corrected."

And I felt the wire again, thinner now, threading into me, and I understood with terrible clarity what *correction* meant. It meant being smoothed out. It meant becoming a straight line where I had been a person. Somewhere in the fog behind them I could hear it already — the sound of me, unwriting, a small soft hiss like breath leaving a room.

Then Ilsa stepped forward, and everything changed.

* * *

You should understand something about Ilsa before I tell you what she did.

She is a memory-woman. She was born of Veylmark the way rivers are born of rain — she *is* remembered things given shape, the town baker's laugh and the queen's second-best crown and the way the harbor bells rang on the day the fishing fleet came home early because the sea had been kind. She carries the archive of everything we were, and she guards it the way a body guards blood.

And memories, in Veylmark, can be spent.

She had told us this once, sitting by a fire that burned without wood, in the matter-of-fact tone she used for unbearable things. "A strong memory, freely given, becomes power. The older and dearer the memory, the greater the fire. It is why the empire fears us loving anything. Love is ammunition." Then she had smiled, which she almost never did, and said, "Fortunately, I am very old, and very fond of far too many things."

I had thought she was joking. Ilsa does not joke. Ilsa *devastates*, and sometimes the devastation resembles humor.

Now she walked past Jonah — patted his arm, actually, the way you'd steady a piece of furniture — and planted herself between us and the six surveyors, and she said, in her flattest, quietest voice:

"Gentlemen. You are standing in my country."

"We stand in imperial territory," said the tall one. "Veylmark is unmapped."

"Veylmark," said Ilsa, "is *right here*."

And she reached up — I will see this until I stop existing, however long or short that turns out to be — she reached up and unlaced the collar of her coat, and from around her neck she drew a cord, and on the cord hung a small glass bead, no bigger than a currant, glowing faintly gold.

I knew what it was. Every memory-woman carries one. It was the memory of the harbor bells. The day the fleet came home early. The day the sea was kind. It was the oldest thing she owned, older than her own name, the first thing Veylmark ever gave her, the seed around which all the rest of her had grown.

"No," I said. My voice came out very small. "Ilsa. No. Not that one. Anything but that one."

She glanced back at me, and she smiled — the real smile, the devastating one — and she said, "Child, what do you think memories are *for*?"

The surveyors raised their instruments. Six wires of cold light swung toward her.

Ilsa closed her hand around the bead.

"Harbor bells," she said softly, to herself, to the memory itself, the way you'd speak to a beloved child before sending them off alone into the world. "Ring loud, my darlings. Ring loud enough to wake the dead country."

She opened her hand, and the bead went up like a spark going home to its fire, and the world *rang*.

* * *

I cannot describe the sound honestly, because it was not a sound. It was every bell in a harbor that no longer exists, ringing all at once, and underneath the ringing was the smell of salt and tar and frying fish, and underneath that was the feeling of a whole town turning toward the water at the same moment, ten thousand faces glad at once, and all of it poured outward in a golden circle that hit the surveyors like a wave hits sandcastles.

The instruments shattered. The ruled coats unraveled into ordinary cloth, then into thread, then into nothing. The surveyors themselves — I saw their blank faces crack, just for a heartbeat, into something almost like wonder, as if the memory of our gladness had gotten inside them and shown them a door they hadn't known their forms contained.

Then the wave passed, and they were gone. All six. Unwritten, unmeasured, unrecorded. The empire keeps no census of its erasers.

And Ilsa —

Ilsa stood swaying in the middle of the road, and she was *less*. I don't know how else to say it. There had been a great deal of her before, layer upon layer, a woman made of a whole country's remembering, and now a hole had been burned through the center of her, clean and round, like a coin punched out of a page. She still had her shape. She still had her voice. But when she turned to look at me, something behind her eyes was missing, some deep warm cellar where the oldest wine had been kept, and I could see straight down into the emptiness where the harbor bells used to live.

"Well," she said. Her voice wobbled, just once, and steadied. "That was wasteful of me. We should move on before the empire sends more. They always send more. Bureaucracy is the one thing they've never managed to erase."

"Ilsa," Jonah said. He had come forward and taken her elbow, gently, the way you touch something you're afraid might break. "What did it cost? What did you lose?"

She considered him for a long moment. And then, horribly, wonderfully, she began to laugh — a real laugh, ragged at the edges.

"That's the thing," she said. "I don't know. I can't remember what I can't remember. I know I lost something enormous. I know it mattered more than anything. I can feel the shape of the absence, like a tooth gone from a familiar mouth." She touched her sternum, where the empty cord hung. "But the bells themselves — the day, the sea, the gladness — gone. Spent. Spent *well*, mind you. Six erasers unwritten with one morning's joy. The empire spends whole regiments to buy less."

"But you'll never get it back," I said. I was crying. I hadn't noticed starting. "It was the first thing. It was the oldest thing you had, and you'll never —"

"Sef." She crouched down to my height, which she almost never did, and took my face in her hands, and her palms smelled of paper and rain. "Listen to me, last-new-memory, and listen well, because I will only ever say this once, and I will not be able to say it twice, because I won't remember saying it." Her eyes were fierce and wet. "A memory spent in love is not lost. It is *planted*. Somewhere out there, six imperial surveyors died full of wonder, and that wonder is loose in the world now, doing who knows what work. And somewhere in you is a harbor you never saw, ringing with bells you'll never hear, and that is *also* true, and both of those things are worth more than my comfort." She kissed my forehead, hard, like sealing a letter. "Grieve quickly, child. We haven't far to go and the light is failing, and the road ahead forgets itself after dark."

* * *

We walked. The fogline ran beside us like a dog that had done something wrong and didn't want to talk about it.

Jonah carried Ilsa's pack without asking, and she let him, which from Ilsa was a surrender deeper than any battle. For a long while none of us spoke. The burnt-sweet smell faded. The bell-copper taste slowly drained out of the air, leaving only evening, ordinary evening, the grey-gold kind that happens everywhere and belongs to no empire.

It was Jonah who finally broke the silence, because Jonah cannot bear silence the way other men cannot bear heights.

"She's right, you know," he said quietly, to me, while Ilsa walked a few paces ahead pretending deafness. "About the planting. I've been thinking about what my father did — what the guild did — and I keep coming back to the fact that they couldn't actually destroy anything. Erasure isn't destruction. It's *displacement*. Everything they unmapped had to go somewhere. All those bells, all those names, all those glad mornings — they're not gone, Sef. They're folded up small and hidden in places the empire doesn't keep ledgers." He tapped his own chest, then, hesitantly, mine. "In people. In children. In—" He stopped himself, ears going red, and pretended to consult his journal.

"In you?" I said.

"In whoever asks nicely," he muttered.

Ahead of us, Ilsa laughed — short and cracked, missing something in the middle, but a laugh. "Save your poetry for the capital, cartographer. And walk faster. The light's nearly —"

She stopped.

So did the road.

I felt it before I understood it: the ground under my feet shifting its mind, the way it does here, the way it had begun doing since we'd left the archive hills. The road ahead of us, which had been running straight and honest toward the distant smudge of the capital, *hesitated* — and then bent, smoothly, deliberately, like a snake deciding to be a river, and curved away north into the fog.

"That's not right," Jonah said. His cartographer's voice, the flat professional one. "Roads don't do that. Roads especially don't do that *toward* a city. A road that avoids its own destination is —"

"Is a road that's been told to," Ilsa finished grimly. She squinted north, into the thickening fog, and I watched her read it the way Jonah reads maps — and I watched her face change. "Someone's been through here recently. Someone with authority over the paths. Someone who wanted the way to the capital made... difficult."

"For whom?" Jonah asked. "For us?"

"No," Ilsa said slowly. "For everyone. Which is worse." She turned to look at us both, and the hole in her seemed to deepen as the light failed, a darkness behind her eyes with a bell-shaped bottom. "The council faction. The ones who want the Door for themselves. They've reached the road first — and they've begun renaming it."

Above us, in the last grey light, the old milestone at the roadside flickered. The worn letters that had read, for four hundred years of remembering, *VEYLMARK-UNDER-THE-CROWN, 40 LEAGUES*, shivered like a reflection in disturbed water — and settled into new letters, crisp and wrong:

*NOWHERE IN PARTICULAR, 0 LEAGUES.*

Jonah stared at it. And then, to my astonishment, he smiled — a small, fierce, entirely un-cartographer-like smile, the smile of a man who has just been handed a puzzle instead of a punishment.

"Renaming roads," he said, pulling out his journal, flipping to a fresh page, uncapping his pen with his teeth. "Against *me*. Oh, they shouldn't have done that."

The fog ahead sighed, and shifted, and began, very softly, to forget which way it was going.

We followed Jonah into it.
