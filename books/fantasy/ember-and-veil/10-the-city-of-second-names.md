## 10. The City of Second Names

I had not meant to bring them to the capital. I want that written down somewhere, in some archive that outlasts me: I did *not* mean to bring them.

But the road to the Last Door runs through Veylmark's heart the way a scar runs through a palm — you cannot go around it without going over it — and so on the third morning of our walking, when the mist thinned and the towers rose up out of it like the ribs of something enormous and patient, I stopped at the crest of the hill and let them see it first.

"Well," Jonah said, in the voice of a man who has spent his life drawing cities he will never visit and is now being asked to visit one drawn by grief. "That's..."

"Ruined," I said. "Yes."

"I was going to say *beautiful*."

"It's both. That's rather the problem." I adjusted my shawl — a memory-shawl, woven from my grandmother's habit of worrying corners, which is why it never sat straight — and looked down at Sef, who had gone very still in the way that children do when they are trying to be brave about something too large for their bodies. "The city has a proper name," I told them. "It had one before the Empire came, and it will have it again after we're done. But for now everyone calls it what it is. The City of Second Names."

"Because people took new ones," Jonah said quietly.

"Because people took new ones." I started down the hill. "Come along. And whatever you see in there — however strange it looks — do not stare. We're all staring at each other constantly as it is. It's the one civic courtesy we've abandoned."

* * *

The gates were gone. In their place stood two pillars of fog shaped, badly, into the suggestion of archways, and between them a woman sat on a stool knitting something that kept unraveling whenever she looked away from it. She looked up at us, then at Sef, and her needles went still.

"That's Marta," I said under my breath. "She knits the gate. Or she knits the memory of the gate. Same thing here."

"What happened to the real gate?" Sef asked.

"The real gate was made of iron and pride, and the Empire melted the iron and kept the pride." I nodded to Marta, who nodded back with the elaborate indifference of someone who would be asking me everything within the hour. "Good morning, Marta."

"Mm," said Marta, and resumed knitting, and watched us the entire time, which is a skill you develop quickly in a country made of attention.

Inside, the capital unfolded the way dreams unfold when they're trying very hard to be streets. There were houses with only their fronts filled in — façades propped up like theater flats, because no one alive remembered what the backs looked like. There was a fountain that ran with the sound of water and, if you didn't look directly at it, the suggestion of water too; look straight on and it was only light, bending politely. A baker's stall sold bread that tasted exactly like bread tastes in memory, which is to say better than any actual bread, and which vanished from your stomach an hour later, leaving you hungry but oddly comforted.

And everywhere, everywhere, the names.

I watched Jonah notice it. He was a cartographer; he noticed things in order of how much they frightened him, and this frightened him plenty. Over every door hung a small painted board, and on every board a name, and none of the names matched anything. *The House of Tallow and Kindness.* *Ondrell's.* *This Is Definitely a Shoemaker's.* (That last one belonged to a man named Pem who had been, in the old days, a bell-founder, and who had chosen his second name with the defiant honesty of the truly exhausted.)

"They renamed themselves," Jonah murmured.

"They renamed themselves to survive." I kept walking. "When the Edict fell, the country didn't die all at once. It died the way a fire dies — corner by corner, room by room. And the people learned something terrible and wonderful: the erasure takes what is *named*. A thing called Veylmark could be unmapped. But a thing called *Ondrell's*, a thing that insists it has always been a shoemaker's, a thing with no history worth erasing—" I shrugged. "Smallness became armor. Forgetfulness became a costume. They made themselves too humble to bother killing."

"And it worked?"

"For some. For a while. For others—" I gestured ahead, where a figure stood motionless in a doorway, and as we passed I saw that the doorway remembered him more than he remembered himself; his outline flickered at the edges, a man going soft at the borders. "Others held on to their true names too long. Grief makes people stubborn. Stubbornness makes people mist."

Sef had stopped walking. I turned and found the child standing in the middle of the street, turning slowly, taking it in — and I saw what I had been afraid I would see. Color coming into the cheeks. Steadiness coming into the step. Here, in the half-remembered heart of the half-remembered country, Sef burned brighter than anywhere else we had walked. The child was a lamp and this city was dark, and everyone was about to notice.

They noticed within four minutes. I counted.

* * *

It began with the baker, whose name was — well, her board said *Breadmother*, and that was as much as anyone knew or needed to. She came out from behind her stall wiping her hands and said, "Oh," in the voice of a woman seeing spring after a decade of winter, and then she said, "Oh, *look at you*," and then she gave Sef a roll that Sef ate and did not vanish from, because Sef's stomach, unlike ours, could hold things properly. Word travels fast in a city where everyone watches everyone else to make sure they still exist. By the time we reached the square outside the Council hall — a building that was mostly suggestion, its dome a rumor of a dome — there were perhaps forty people trailing us at a respectful distance, the way you follow a candle through a cellar.

"They're looking at Sef like—" Jonah began.

"Like hope," I said. "Yes. I know. Enjoy it for the moment, because hope in this city comes in two flavors, and you're about to meet the other one."

The other one arrived wearing a coat too fine for the ruin it lived in, with a smile calibrated to the degree. His board read *A Friend of the District*, which told me everything. In the old country he had been somebody minor and ambitious; in the new one he had discovered that ambition survives erasure even when nothing else does.

"Cousin Ilsa," he said warmly, though we were no kin and both knew it. "You've brought such an interesting little guest."

"Sef is a child, Aldous, not a guest. Children don't need invitations to their own country."

"Don't they?" He crouched to Sef's level, and I will grant him this: his kindness, when he performed it, was indistinguishable from the real thing, which is precisely what made him dangerous. "Do you know what you are, little one? Do they tell you? You're the last new thing this place ever made. Everything else here is a copy of a copy of a memory, wearing thin. But you—" he touched, without quite touching, the air near Sef's shoulder— "you're *original*. Do you understand what that's worth?"

"I understand you should stand up," Sef said, "because my friend doesn't like it when tall men loom."

Jonah made a sound that he converted, barely, into a cough. I decided, provisionally, to keep him.

Aldous rose, smiling wider. "Of course. Forgive me. But the offer stands, cousin. There are those among us who have kept themselves together for thirty years on scraps and stubbornness, and we are *tired*, and along comes a child made of living country, and you expect us not to wonder?" He spread his hands. "We only want to talk. At the Council. Where talking happens."

"Where talking happens," I agreed, "and where, historically, so does everything else."

He bowed and drifted off, and I watched the crowd around Sef divide itself invisibly — some faces open, some calculating, most doing both at once, because desperation is not a pure emotion, whatever the poets claim. It mixes. Hope complicated by hunger. Welcome shadowed by want. That was the whole city in a single expression, and now it was aimed at a child.

"Jonah," I said quietly, "hold Sef's hand. Not for comfort. For logistics."

* * *

The Council received us that evening in a hall lit by lanterns that burned without oil — they burned on recitation; somewhere in the city, three old women spent their evenings saying *"the lights of the capital, the lights of the capital"* over and over, and it was enough, it had always been enough, until recently.

There were nine councilors. Eight of them were memories of officials, dignified and threadbare, and the ninth was a woman named Verre who had never been an official at all but had simply refused, for thirty years, to stop attending meetings, until the meetings assumed she belonged. I liked her best.

"You've brought the fragment," said the eldest councilor, whose name had been worn smooth as a river stone by use.

"I've brought Sef," I corrected. "Who is a person, and tired, and standing right here."

"Sef," the councilor amended graciously, the way powerful people amend things. "The Council is grateful. The Council wishes to express that gratitude concretely. There are quarters prepared. Food. Safety."

"Say the rest," I said.

Verre snorted. The eldest glared at her and continued. "The Council also wishes to discuss... arrangements. The Last Door requires a key of memory, as you know. A life's worth. And there are those among us who believe that a certain young person's memories — being new, being potent, being *native* — might serve as a shared resource. A communal reserve. Nothing would be *taken*, precisely. Only... pooled."

"Pooled," Jonah repeated.

"Distributed," said another councilor.

"Eaten," said Verre, cheerfully, "is the honest word, and I'd thank this Council to use it. They want to drink the child like broth and call it community spirit." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, and fixed the eldest with a look. "Tell them the other part. Tell them what happens to the pooled."

Silence, of the kind that answers questions.

"The Door takes a whole life or it takes nothing," Verre said at last, to us, not to them. "If they pour forty half-remembered lives into it, it opens a crack, spits them out, and keeps the change. If they pour in one child — a child who *is* the country — it opens wide. Wide enough for whoever's standing closest." Her eyes moved across her colleagues, mild and merciless. "Guess who plans to be standing closest."

"This is speculation," said the eldest, with dignity.

"This is arithmetic," said Verre. "I was a schoolteacher. I know the difference."

* * *

They gave us rooms in a house whose board read *Somewhere Warm*, and it was, mostly, warm, in the way of things believed in firmly. Jonah sat by the window sketching the street below by moonlight-that-was-only-habit, and Sef sat beside him pointing out errors ("that roof is wrong, it used to be blue, everyone knows it was blue"), and I stood in the doorway watching the two of them and feeling the particular ache of a guardian who has run out of clever options and knows it.

"They'll try something," Jonah said, not looking up. "Aldous. The faction. Whatever we call them."

"Tonight or tomorrow night. Festivals are good cover. And this city loves a festival — it's the one event where forgetting yourself is considered virtuous instead of fatal."

"What festival?"

I smiled, and it wasn't entirely a happy smile. "Tomorrow night. The Night of Lanterns. We light them for everything already forgotten — every street that lost its name, every song nobody hums anymore. You'll see. It's beautiful." I looked at Sef, drowsing against Jonah's shoulder, still bright even in sleep, a coal refusing the dark. "It's beautiful, and half this city will spend it deciding whether the child in our care is a miracle or an ingredient."

Jonah closed his sketchbook gently. "Then we don't sleep much tomorrow."

"We don't sleep much any night," I said. "Welcome to Veylmark."

Below us, in the street, a lantern flared early — unlit by any hand, burning on nothing, on somebody's sudden fierce remembering of a night long ago — and went out again just as fast, like a heartbeat testing itself.

And in the doorway of the house across the lane, faint in the habit-moonlight, stood Aldous, looking up at our window, smiling his calibrated smile, counting our lanterns before the festival had even begun.
