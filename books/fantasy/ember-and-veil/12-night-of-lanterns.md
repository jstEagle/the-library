## 12. Night of Lanterns

The lanterns were made of paper the color of old teeth, and Sef thought they were beautiful anyway.

They hung from every balcony and broken archway in the City of Second Names — hundreds of them, thousands, strung on wires that sagged between buildings like garlands of small patient moons. Each one carried a name written on its skin in ink that glowed faintly amber. Not the names people had now. The names they had lost.

Sef stood at a window in Ilsa's archive-house with their chin on their fists, watching the city below fill up with people. It was the first time since arriving in Veylmark that the streets had looked full rather than haunted. There was a baker setting out trays of honey-bread on a trestle table, and his hands flickered — there, then not-quite-there, then there again — but he kept working as though steadiness were simply a matter of deciding to have it. Children ran between the legs of adults, chasing a hoop, and every time one of them laughed, three or four lanterns overhead brightened by a degree, as if laughter were a kind of oil.

"They light them for things already forgotten," Ilsa had said that morning, when she'd explained the festival in her dry, careful voice. "A grandmother's maiden name. A dog. A river that used to run through the eastern quarter before it was unmapped. You write what you can't remember anymore, and you hang it up, and for one night the city pretends it remembers."

"And does it work?" Sef had asked.

Ilsa had looked at them for a long moment with those eyes like water over slate. "No," she said. "But pretending is how we survived this long. Don't knock it until you've tried it."

Now, at dusk, the first lanterns caught, and the whole city exhaled into gold.

* * *

Jonah found Sef on the stairs, wearing a coat that someone had pressed on him at the door — deep green, embroidered at the collar with a pattern of interlocking rivers, which was apparently the old Veylmark fashion and which made him look like a very dignified heron.

"You're staring," Jonah said.

"I'm appreciating," Sef said. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Appreciating is scholarly."

"Ah. And what are you appreciating?"

"The fact that you own exactly one expression and tonight it's the confused one."

Jonah's hand went to his collar, defensive. "The woman who gave me this coat wouldn't take no for an answer. She said I had the shoulders of a river-warden and it would be an insult to the ancestors to let them go unadorned."

"Do you know what a river-warden is?"

"No."

"Neither do I." Sef grinned and hopped down the last three steps. "But you look like one. Very warden-y. Extremely responsible around bodies of water."

Jonah made the small huffing sound he made when he was trying not to laugh, which was one of Sef's favorite sounds in any country, remembered or otherwise. "Where's Ilsa?"

"Gone ahead. She said festivals are for people who still believe pretending works." Sef's grin faded a little at the edges. "She's coming, though. She always comes. She just likes to arrive late so she can pretend she doesn't care."

"She's very good at pretending," Jonah said quietly.

They went out together into the gold.

* * *

The streets smelled of woodsmoke and honey and something green and growing that Sef couldn't name — because, Sef realized with a small cold jolt, no one could name it anymore. That was probably why it had been written on a lantern somewhere: *the smell after rain on the eastern hills*, or whatever it had been, glowing up there among the lost things.

But tonight the city did its best. Musicians had gathered on the fountain steps — the fountain itself was only mist and suggestion, half-forgotten, but the steps were solid enough, remembered by every child who had ever been told *don't climb on the fountain*. They played instruments Sef's body knew and Sef's mind didn't: a bowed thing with too many strings, a flute carved from reed-grass, a drum that sounded less like a drum than like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

People danced. Badly, mostly. The memory-women and men of the city moved with a strange double rhythm, half of them dancing steps their feet remembered and their minds didn't, colliding gently, apologizing, laughing. An old man spun alone in perfect form, weeping without stopping, and everyone gave him room and respect, the way you give room to weather.

"Is it always like this?" Jonah asked. He had gone still in the way he went still around anything that mattered — maps, coastlines, Sef himself, though he didn't seem to know about that last one yet.

"I don't know," Sef said honestly. "I think maybe I do know, and I can't reach it. Everything here is like a word on the tip of your tongue." They looked up at the lanterns drifting past on their wires. "Except worse. Because with a word, somebody else might say it for you."

Jonah looked down at them. In the lantern-light his face was all warm planes and shadow, and there was something in his expression that Sef had learned to read across weeks of road and ruin: the cartographer's look, the one that meant *this is important, mark it, remember where it goes.*

"Sef," he started.

"Don't," Sef said, and surprised themselves with how fast it came out. "Don't do the face. The face where you're about to be sensible at me."

"I'm never sensible at you."

"You're *always* sensible at me. You're doing it right now. Your eyebrows are being sensible."

"My eyebrows—"

"Come dance," Sef said, and grabbed his hands before he could deploy further eyebrow-based reason. "You owe me a dance. You owe me several. You've been saving yourself for maps since the day I met you."

"That's not—" Jonah let himself be pulled, stumbling, protesting in the mild way that meant he wasn't really protesting at all. "That's a slanderous characterization of my relationship with maps."

* * *

Here is what Sef knew, and had known for some time, and had not said:

Sef was going to die. Or something adjacent to dying — something the council discussed in low voices over charts of fading streets, something Ilsa's silences confirmed. Every unmapped thing loses a memory daily, Ilsa had taught them, and Sef was the last new memory Veylmark would ever make, and new memories burn fastest. Sef had done the arithmetic. Sef was good at arithmetic; it was one of the few subjects that existed in both the language older than kingdoms and the language of empires, and it came out the same in both.

And here was the other thing Sef knew, the smaller and sharper thing, the splinter under the skin:

Sef was not afraid of it.

Or — not of that part. When Sef lay awake in the archive-house listening to the city settle and creak like a ship, they searched themselves for fear of ending and found instead a much more ordinary terror, dressed in plain clothes, waiting patiently to be noticed:

*I don't want to be unloved.*

Not unloved in general. Sef had been loved — was loved, by Ilsa in her flinty way, by the jailer-hearted city itself, which brightened its lanterns whenever Sef walked beneath them, as if the whole country leaned toward its last child like a plant toward a window. But there was one specific love Sef wanted, wanted with a greed that embarrassed them, and it belonged to a tall heron-shouldered man who was currently stepping on their foot for the third time.

"Sorry," Jonah said. "Sorry. I'm told I have the natural grace of a surveyor's tripod."

"You're improving," Sef lied generously.

"I'm not. We both know I'm not."

"We do," Sef admitted, laughing, and the laugh lit two lanterns overhead — Sef saw it happen, saw the amber swell, and felt the old cold jolt again, because even joy was fuel here, even joy got spent.

The music slowed. The musicians shifted into something older, a tune in a mode that made Sef's chest ache in a way they suspected was homesickness for a home that hadn't finished existing yet. Around them the dancers drew closer together, pairs and trios and one confident cluster of five, and the crowd became a slow tide, turning.

Jonah's hands settled — one at Sef's shoulder-blade, one holding their hand, formal and careful, the grip of a man handling something he believed was fragile.

"I'm not fragile," Sef said.

"I know."

"You're holding me like I'm wet paper."

"I'm holding you like you're the last copy of a map of a country that doesn't exist," Jonah said, "which, as far as I can tell, is accurate."

Sef looked up at him. The lanterns turned above them in slow amber constellations, and each one was a lost thing, and Sef thought: *he will be a lost thing too, someday, if I don't say it. Everyone becomes a lantern eventually. Say it while you're still a person saying it.*

"Jonah."

"Mm?"

"I need to tell you something and I need you to not be sensible about it."

Jonah's mouth twitched. "I'll try."

"I'm scared," Sef said. "But not of what you think. Not of — the end, or the Door, or burning up like a lamp. I've made peace with that. Mostly. On Tuesdays." Jonah's grip tightened a fraction; Sef pressed on before the sensible eyebrows could mobilize. "What I'm scared of is smaller and stupider. I'm scared of being unloved. Specifically—" and here Sef's voice did something traitorous and wobbled, "—specifically by you. I'm scared that when I go, I'll go having never said it, and you'll stand there with your tripod grace and your one expression, and you won't know. And that would be worse than dissolving. That would be worse than the fog."

The music played. The tide of dancers turned. Somewhere above them a lantern bearing the name of a dog swayed gently, remembering nothing.

"I love you," Sef said. Plainly. The way you set a stone on a map to hold down a corner in wind. "Not the way children love, or the way you love a stray that follows you home. I love you the way Veylmark loves its rivers, which is to say completely, structurally, in a way the geography depends on. I love you and I wanted you to know it while I still have a mouth to know things with."

For a moment Jonah said nothing at all, and Sef felt the terrible freefall of having spoken, the vertigo of the unsayable said.

Then Jonah stopped dancing.

He stopped in the middle of the turning tide, and took Sef's face in both hands — the same hands that drew coastlines, ink-stained, steady — and he knelt, actually knelt, right there on the fountain steps, heedless of the dancers flowing around them like water around a stone, so that they were eye to eye.

"Sef," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the name, which Sef would keep forever, wherever forever happened to be located. "I have spent my whole life loving places I couldn't enter. Whole countries, from the outside, through glass. And then you knocked on my door half-drowned and speaking a language older than kingdoms, and I finally understood that every map I ever drew was just practice. Practice for finding my way to you." His thumb brushed Sef's cheekbone, careful, deliberate, memorizing. "I love you. Structurally. Geographically. In a way the terrain depends on. I am not sensible about you. I have never once been sensible about you. Ask my eyebrows."

Sef laughed and cried at the same time, which was undignified and which they did not care about even slightly.

"You knelt," Sef managed. "In front of everyone."

"The ancestors demanded it. River-warden's honor."

Above them, dozens of lanterns flared — not the gentle swelling of laughter this time but a great soft roaring bloom of amber, as if the whole city's lost things had turned to look, as if the forgotten names had leaned in over the wires to witness. People nearby glanced up, murmuring. An old woman touched Sef's shoulder as she passed and said, in the old tongue, words Sef understood without trying: *the country sees you, little ember.*

"Everyone's looking," Jonah whispered.

"Good," Sef said. "Let them. If I'm going to be a lantern someday, I want the whole city to know whose I was."

They danced until the music ended and past it, humming the missing parts, and the tide turned around them, and the honey-bread man brought them sweet things without being asked, and for one whole night the city pretended so hard that it almost worked.

* * *

It was very late — lantern-oil running low, the gold dimming to ember-color — when Sef, drowsy against Jonah's shoulder on the fountain steps, saw the figures moving along the eastern colonnade.

Three of them. Council robes, hooded, walking with the particular purposeful quiet of people who do not wish to be counted. One of them carried a case, long and narrow, of the kind used for transporting documents that mattered.

They were heading toward the archive-house.

Toward Ilsa.

Sef sat up. Jonah followed their gaze, and Sef watched his cartographer's eyes do what they always did — measure, mark, remember where it goes.

"That's the faction," Jonah said quietly. "Maren's people. The ones who voted against telling us about the Door's price."

"Yes," Sef said, and the warmth of the night curdled slowly in their chest, and far above them a lantern guttered out — just one, just a small one, a dog's name or a maiden's, going dark over the sleeping city like a held breath finally released.
