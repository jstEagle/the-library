## 13. Betrayal in the Archive

Ilsa had guarded the archive of Veylmark for longer than she could precisely say — which was, of course, the whole trouble with being made of memory. You remembered everything except how long you'd been remembering it.

She was cataloguing that morning's small losses — a baker's name gone from the Breadmakers' Ledger overnight, a whole street in Lowmarsh gone soft at its edges — when she heard footsteps in the Hall of Second Names that did not belong to any of the council.

She knew every footfall in this city. That was not a boast; it was simply what happened when you had nothing to do for decades but listen. Councillor Vess walked like a man apologizing to the floor. Old Tam walked like the floor owed him money. The child Sef ran everywhere, heels striking like rain on a tin roof, and Jonah Joubert walked like a man reading a map with his feet — careful, deliberate, always half a step toward whatever he wasn't looking at.

These footsteps were none of those. These were six people walking in perfect unison, which no one in Veylmark ever did, because unanimity had been the empire's trick, and the whole point of surviving erasure was learning to disagree loudly and often.

Ilsa set down her pen. She blew out her candle, though there was no reason to — the archive lit itself from the memories shelved within it, a warm amber glow that never flickered — but some habits predated her death-in-life, and hiding in the dark was one of them.

The footsteps stopped at the Doorward Archive. The inner vault. The one that held the Last Door's keys: not metal keys, but memories — the oldest ones, the heaviest, the first things Veylmark had ever been.

She heard the vault seal break.

It sounded, she thought absurdly, like a sigh.

* * *

By the time she reached the vault — running through corridors built of other people's recollections, past shelves where a wedding once lived and a harvest festival and the smell of a particular grandmother's kitchen — they were gone. All six of them. And so was Sef.

There was a note pinned to the empty cradle of memory where the child had been sleeping these past weeks, growing stronger, growing realer, growing *loved*, which Ilsa privately suspected was the strongest magic of all.

The note read:

*We are sorry. We are few, and we are tired, and we have waited so long. The Door will open for the child. It will not open for all of us. Forgive us. — The Faithful Remnant*

"Sorry," Ilsa said aloud, to the empty room. "They wrote *sorry*."

She noticed, distantly, that her hands were shaking. She hadn't shaken since the day the maps came — the day the guild's surveyors drew their clean lines across the world and Veylmark slid out of existence like a coin between sofa cushions. She had thought she'd used up all her shaking then.

Then she heard the second set of footsteps. Heavy. Careful. Half a step toward whatever he wasn't looking at.

Jonah Joubert stood in the vault doorway, breathless, his coat buttoned wrong — two buttons off, which meant he'd dressed in the dark, which meant he'd woken in a panic, which meant—

"You felt it too," she said.

"I woke up and the house was quieter." He looked around the vault, at the broken seal, at the note. "Where is she? Where's Sef?"

"Gone. Stolen. By six of our own." Ilsa held up the note. "Read your colleagues' apology."

"They're not my—" He stopped. He read it. His face did something complicated. "The Door will open for the child," he repeated slowly. "But the council said—"

"The council said the Door demanded a life's worth of memory from whoever opened it. One life. One key." Ilsa watched him very carefully as she said the next part. "And you told us, Joubert, didn't you, that you were nobody. A cartographer. Exiled. Guildless."

Something crossed his face. It was quick, but Ilsa had spent an unlife learning to read faces in bad light, in fog, in the moment before someone lied.

"I am," he said.

"Show me your hands."

"What?"

"Your hands. Show me."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and held them out. And there, on the inside of his left wrist, faint as a watermark, was the mark she had hoped never to see again: the surveyor's sigil. The compass-rose-and-line. The brand every guild cartographer took when they swore the oath she still remembered being sworn, from the far side of the fogline, on the last day anyone in Veylmark had a tomorrow.

"That's not possible," Jonah whispered. "That's not— I've never seen that before. I've never—"

"No," Ilsa agreed. "You've never seen it. But your skin has. Your blood has. It's in you deeper than seeing, Joubert. It's in the part of you that got written before you were old enough to hold a pen." She stepped closer. "Whose son are you?"

"My father was Aldous Joubert. He was a mapmaker, he was exiled same as me, he died when I was nineteen, and he never—" Jonah's voice cracked. "He never spoke of the guild. Not once. I found his papers only weeks ago. I found the Edict. I found his *name* on it."

"Yes," Ilsa said softly. "I know. I've known since the night you arrived. I let you into my archive, I taught you how erasure works, I watched you hold that child's hand in the lantern-light, and I told myself: perhaps the son is not the father. Perhaps grief makes better people than blood does." She laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "And then I wake to find the child gone and a note signed by cowards, and I think: who else knew the Door's true price? Who else knew that guild-blooded memory burns brighter than any other? Who else has been asking careful little questions about the vault seals?"

"I asked about the seals because I'm a *cartographer*—"

"You asked about them the way a man asks about a lock he intends to pick!"

The amber light of the archive seemed to dim around them, or perhaps that was just Ilsa's anger pressing against the walls of herself. Somewhere above, a bell that remembered being rung for festivals gave a single mournful hum, as if the city itself were eavesdropping.

"I did not take her," Jonah said, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that frightened Ilsa more than shouting. Quiet meant certain. "I would die before I let anyone spend her. Ilsa — I have spent three chapters of my life, if you'll forgive the phrasing, learning exactly what my family cost this country. Do you truly believe I'd finish the job?"

"I believe," Ilsa said, "that a man can love a thing and still be its instrument. I believe the guild understood that better than anyone. They didn't erase us with malice, Joubert. They erased us with *procedure*. With signatures. With men who went home to their suppers afterward and slept soundly." She jabbed a finger at his wrist. "And now the most dangerous memory in Veylmark is missing, and the man standing next to me wears the guild under his skin, and I don't know what to believe anymore. That's the truth. I don't know."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Jonah did something unexpected. He sat down. Right there on the cold memory-stone floor, amid the dust of a country's forgetting, and put his head in his hands.

"She called me *Jonah*," he said, muffled. "Not 'cartographer.' Not 'the heir.' Just — Jonah. She saved me the crusts off her bread because she'd decided I liked them best, and I do, I do like them best, and I never told her that, she just *knew*—" He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "I didn't take her. But I know who might have. Because I've been reading my father's master map, Ilsa. Every night. And there are six names written in its margins. Six names in a cipher I haven't cracked yet. But I recognized one of the hands tonight, on that note."

Ilsa felt her anger tilt, reluctantly, like a ship catching new wind. "Whose?"

"Councillor Vess."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, and it was strange, Ilsa thought, how quickly alliance reassembles itself when there's a common enemy. Anger doesn't vanish — hers sat in her chest like a swallowed coal — but it can be set aside, the way you set aside a hot pan to deal with a fire in the next room.

Jonah spread his father's master map across the archive's great table. Ilsa had seen it before and hated it: the beautiful, terrible document that had orchestrated Veylmark's unmapping, every line a knife-stroke. But now Jonah traced it differently, hunting the margins, the near-invisible annotations.

"Here." His finger stopped on a cluster of symbols near the capital's old surveyor's college. "Six marks. And beneath them, a phrase in guild cipher. I've been working on it for days. It's not standard cipher — it's older. Household cipher. The kind a man teaches his children." His voice caught. "The kind my father taught *me*, as a game, when I was small. I thought it was nonsense. I thought he was just — playing."

"And?" Ilsa prompted, when he didn't continue.

"It says: *If the door must open, let it open for everyone, or let it stay shut forever.*" Jonah exhaled. "My father didn't just sign the Edict. He planned its reversal. He hid instructions in the very map they used to kill this place. And someone found the margin notes — someone who read them backwards. Someone who decided *one* should walk through instead of *all*."

"Vess," Ilsa said. "Vess was a guild clerk, before. Did you know that? One of the minor ones. He survived erasure by hiding in a records office, memorizing filing systems until the empire forgot he existed." She shook her head bitterly. "A man made of paperwork. Of course he'd steal a child to power his own escape. To Vess, Sef isn't a person. She's a *key*. And keys are meant to be used."

"Where would they take her?"

"The hollow hills," Ilsa said immediately. "There's an old Door-approach beneath them, from before the erasure, when the Door was a door and not a wound. If Vess knows the margin notes, he knows the approach." She was already moving, pulling a traveling cloak from a shelf where a memory of winter had been stored — she borrowed its warmth the way she borrowed everything, secondhand. "How fast can you read a landscape, cartographer?"

"Faster than it can lie to me."

"Good. Because the road to the hollows forgets itself, and I can't remember it anymore — I spent that memory years ago, buying passage for refugees." She paused at the archive door and looked back at him, and the coal in her chest shifted, uncomfortably, into something almost like honesty. "Joubert. If I find out you had any hand in this — any hand at all —"

"You won't," he said. "And if I find out you doubted me for good reason, I'll hand you the knife myself."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true."

Despite everything — despite the stolen child, despite the broken seal, despite the sigil burning faint and hateful on his wrist — Ilsa felt the corner of her mouth move. "Your father," she said, "must have been a very difficult man."

"He was," Jonah said, shouldering his pack. "I'm told I'm worse."

* * *

They were halfway to the city gates when Ilsa saw it: chalked on the wall of the Bellfounders' Row, small and neat, in a hand she almost didn't recognize because it was shaking.

*Sef.*

Just the name. Nothing else. Chalked low on the stone, at the height a child would reach.

And beside it, drawn hastily, a little compass rose — crooked, lopsided, with the north point deliberately bent eastward. Toward the hollow hills.

"She left us a sign," Jonah breathed. "Before they took her — she fought them, or she guessed, and she marked the way."

Ilsa stared at the crooked little star and felt something crack open in her that had been sealed since the erasure. Hope, probably. It was rusted. It hurt coming loose.

"She's cleverer than the both of us," Ilsa managed. "Come on, cartographer. Let's go commit some treason against the Faithful Remnant."

Above them, the remembering bell rang out — once, twice, three times — though no hand had touched it, as if the city itself had finally decided whose side it was on.
