## 15. Through the Hollow Hills

The dark under the hills was not empty dark. It was *full* dark — the kind Sef had only ever felt in the corners of Veylmark where memory ran thin, the kind that pressed against your skin like a hand deciding whether to hold you or let you fall.

They had carried Sef down here in a lantern's worth of light and a wagonload of lies. *For your own good,* the woman with the gray braid had said, which was what everyone said right before they did something unforgivable. The wagon had gone down a road that didn't exist on any map Jonah had drawn — Sef knew this because they had memorized every map Jonah owned, not out of duty but out of love, the way other children memorized songs.

Now there was a cell. Not stone — stone would have been honest. The walls were made of *forgottenness*, that gray felt-like substance that gathered wherever a thing stopped being remembered. Sef had touched it once, back in the city, on the wall of a house whose family no one could name anymore. It had felt like touching sleep.

Sef sat cross-legged in the middle of the cell and refused to be afraid.

This lasted approximately as long as it takes a candle to notice it has no wick.

Then the fear came, and it came like weather, and Sef let it rain. Ilsa had taught them that much: *Don't fight the feeling. Feel it all the way through, and it moves on faster.* Ilsa. Whose face Sef could still see if they closed their eyes, though every hour it seemed to cost a little more to keep her there. That was the cruelty of this place, Sef realized. It wasn't just that the faction had stolen them. It was that being stolen from everything you loved made the loving itself start to fray.

*Jonah,* Sef thought, deliberately, the way you'd strike flint. *Jonah Joubert, who maps things he can't enter. Who hums when he draws coastlines. Who held my hand crossing the fogline even though his own were shaking.*

The thought caught. Held. Good.

"I'm going to get out of here," Sef told the dark, out loud, because out loud was more real than inside-the-head, and in a country made of memory, real was a resource you spent carefully.

The dark, rudely, did not answer.

But something else did.

* * *

It came at what Sef guessed was morning, though morning down here was a rumor. A shuffling, a clink, a smell like old bread and older ink, and then a face at the little barred window of the door — a round, worried face belonging to a man who seemed assembled slightly wrong, the way dreams assemble people. His left ear was on the right side of his head. His shadow sat down before he did.

"Are you the prisoner?" he asked.

"No," said Sef, "I'm the guest of honor. They just haven't brought the cake."

The man blinked. Then, wonderingly: "That's a joke. Nobody's made a joke at me in—" He counted on fingers that had too many knuckles. "A very long time."

"Then you're overdue." Sef stood and came to the window. Up close, the jailer resolved into someone who might once have been a baker, or a schoolmaster, or both; his apron had flour ghosts on it that no washing would lift because the flour itself had been forgotten. "What's your name?"

"That's the trouble," the man said, apologetic. "I don't entirely have one. I'm a memory, you see. One of the ones the country kept to do its chores. Somebody has to remember how locks work, and doors, and which end of a broom is which. So Veylmark keeps a few of us running. But names are expensive." He smiled, and it was a good smile, crooked and kind. "You can call me Warden. Or Baker. Or Master Underfoot. I've answered to all three."

"Warden-Baker," Sef decided. "Master Underfoot sounds like an insult wearing a hat."

Warden-Baker laughed — a great honking laugh that echoed strangely off the forgotten-walls, as if the walls themselves were surprised by it. "Oh, you're *him*. The child. The last new memory." He peered closer, ear swiveling (the wrong one). "I wondered. You look smaller than the rumors."

"The rumors are mostly about my appetite."

"And is the rumor true?"

"Completely false," said Sef. "I eat far more than the rumors say."

* * *

Warden-Baker came back. That was the first miracle. He brought bread that tasted like the idea of bread, which was better than it sounded, and he stayed to talk, which was better than everything.

He wasn't supposed to, Sef gathered. Jailer-memories had duties. But duties, Warden-Baker explained over the third meal, were like recipes — meant to be improved upon by anyone with taste.

"They told me not to speak to you," he said, lowering his voice even though there was no one for miles except hills full of hollow. "Orders from the Gray Braid herself. *Feed him, water him, don't let him near the Door.* But she didn't say anything about *talking*. People always forget to forbid talking. It's the first thing erasure eats, you know. Conversation. Two people remembering the same evening differently until neither remembers it at all."

"Is that what happened to your name?"

Warden-Baker went quiet. His shadow, seated against the wall, went quieter.

"There was a boy," he said at last. "I think I was somebody's memory *of* a boy. A baker's son, maybe. Or the baker himself, remembered by his son. It's hard to tell, from the inside, which way the remembering goes." He turned the bread over in his hands. "Somebody loved me enough to remember me for a long time. And then they stopped, or they died, or they were erased, and I fell out of their head and into the country's, and the country said, *well, we need someone to mind the locks.*"

Sef's chest hurt. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be *here*." Warden-Baker looked up, and his eyes were wet and bright. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone asked me a question about myself? The faction members walk past my door like I'm furniture. The council forgot I existed years ago — which, down here, is practically a death sentence. And then along comes a child who asks my *name*." He shook his head. "You've already given me more than they ever will. Whatever they're planning to take from you, they can't take that back. Remembering someone is a gift, little one. It doesn't work in reverse."

Sef filed that away, next to Jonah's humming and Ilsa's laugh, in the room inside themselves where they kept the things worth keeping.

* * *

It was on the fifth day — or the fiftieth; time down here pooled rather than flowed — that Sef learned what the faction actually intended.

Warden-Baker hadn't meant to tell them. Sef was sure of that. He'd come to the window distracted, jumpy, his shadow pacing when he stood still.

"You should eat," he said. "You should eat and sleep and be ready."

"Ready for what?"

"For nothing. For nothing at all." He pushed the bread through too fast and it crumbled. "Sorry. Sorry. I'm not good at secrets. I was never the one who kept them. In whatever life I'm a memory of, I was probably the one who *told* them."

Sef came close to the bars. "Warden-Baker. Look at me."

He looked. It was clearly a mistake, from the way his face folded.

"If they're going to use me up," Sef said quietly, "don't I deserve to know how? Everyone else in my life has decided things for me 'for my own good.' My whole country got erased for its own good. I'd like, just once, to be trusted with the truth while I can still do something about it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, water dripped through a hill that couldn't decide whether it existed.

"They mean to open the Last Door," Warden-Baker whispered. "Just them. Just the Gray Braid and her circle. Without the council's vote, without the price paid properly." His hands twisted together. "The Door wants a life's worth of memory freely given — that's the old law, the true law. But there's a *cheaper* key. A newer one. Something the Door has never tasted." He swallowed. "New memory. Memory that was made *inside* Veylmark, after the erasure. There's only one such memory in all the world, child, and it's you. If they feed you to the Door — not willingly, not wholly, just — just *enough* —"

"It opens anyway," Sef said. Their voice sounded very far away. "And I—"

"Gone. All of you. Every part of you that exists because of this country, which is every part of you at all." Warden-Baker's voice cracked. "They'll step through into a restored Veylmark and leave nothing behind but the shape where a child used to be."

The fear came again then, and this time Sef didn't let it rain politely. It flooded. It filled the cell to the ceiling and squeezed. *Gone. Not dead — worse than dead. Unmade. The way Veylmark was unmade, except Veylmark had millions of rememberers and Sef had only themselves.*

And underneath the flood, cold and clear, a single thought:

*Jonah will come.*

Not hope. Certainty. Jonah, who had crossed a fogline with shaking hands. Jonah, who read his father's terrible map by candlelight until his eyes bled. Jonah, who had promised — not in words, Jonah didn't waste words — but in the particular way he'd started sleeping facing the door.

"They'll come for me," Sef said.

"Who, child?"

"My people." Sef stood up straight, wiped their face with their sleeve, and found — to their own surprise — that their legs held. "The cartographer and the archivist. The two most stubborn people your country ever grew. And when they come, Warden-Baker, I'm going to need to know everything you know about these locks."

Warden-Baker stared. Then that crooked smile came back, wider than before, and something in it looked less like a jailer and more like whoever he had been, all those years ago, in somebody's loving head.

"Ohh," he said softly. "There you are. There's the ember." He glanced down the corridor, then back, quick as a thief. "The main gate answers to a word. The Gray Braid changes it weekly, but she changes it *aloud*, to herself, walking past — vanity, or prayer, I've never learned which. If you can hear a word spoken once and keep it—"

"I'm a living memory," Sef said. "Keeping words is the one thing I do perfectly."

"Then listen close, and pray the hills have bad hearing." Warden-Baker leaned in until his crooked ear nearly touched the bars, and began, very quietly, to teach.

* * *

Far above, muffled through forgotten stone, Sef heard it: a sound like a bell, if a bell had been dropped down a well and had opinions about it.

Once. Twice.

Then a third ring — and Sef knew that rhythm anywhere, because Jonah had tapped it absentmindedly on every map table between the valley and the capital. Three-and-a-rest. Three-and-a-rest. The sound of a man thinking his way toward you.

Sef pressed both palms flat against the forgotten-wall and grinned into the dark with all their teeth.

"Hold on, Jonah," they whispered. "I'm keeping your seat warm."
