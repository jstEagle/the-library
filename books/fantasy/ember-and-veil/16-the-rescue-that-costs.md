## 16. The Rescue That Costs

### JONAH

The hollow hills did not appear on any map, which was fitting, because they were the sort of place that resented being found.

Jonah crouched at the mouth of the first tunnel with Ilsa beside him and the wind of the fogline at his back. Above them the hills rose like sleeping animals — smooth, grey, wrong in a way his cartographer's eye kept snagging on. Their slopes had no shadows. He'd noticed it an hour ago and hadn't stopped noticing since. A hill without a shadow was a lie told by the landscape, and he hated lies.

"Say your plan again," Ilsa said. "Slowly. As though you respect me."

"I say my plan again: we go in, we find Sef, we come out."

"Mm. And the collapsing part?"

"The collapsing is theoretical."

"Everything about this country is theoretical until it kills you." She checked the strap on her satchel — the archive-satchel, heavy with bottled memories she'd refused to leave behind, each one a small amber sun. "The faction took the child four hours ago. The council's scouts say the Door is beneath the third hill. They also say the tunnels are 'unstable,' which in Veylmark means the ground remembers being somewhere else and gets confused."

"Then we move fast and don't correct it," Jonah said. "Never argue with terrain. First rule of surveying."

"What's the second rule?"

"Don't survey anything that argues back."

For a moment — one brief, unguarded moment — she almost smiled. It transformed her face from a locked door into a door someone had merely forgotten to lock, and Jonah understood, not for the first time, why the memory-women of Veylmark were said to be dangerous. Not because they knew things. Because for one second you believed they might let you know them.

Then the smile was gone, filed away, and she nodded at the tunnel. "After you, heir to the guild that murdered my country."

"You could let that go, just once."

"I could. I choose not to. It keeps me sharp."

They went in.

* * *

### ILSA

The tunnels smelled of chalk and rain that had never fallen anywhere.

Ilsa had walked a thousand remembered roads in her long half-life as a memory given legs, and she knew what forgetting felt like from the inside: a softening at the edges, a name going vague, a face becoming "someone, surely, someone." These tunnels were forgetting made architectural. The walls rippled where no one recalled their shape. In places the stone simply gave up mid-arch and became suggestion.

She kept her hand on the wall anyway. Superstition, maybe. Or maybe because everything in Veylmark deserved to be touched by someone who meant to remember it.

Ahead, Jonah moved like a man reading. Of course he did. She'd watched him work since the fogline — watched him take the country's chaos and lay it flat in his mind, streets and loops and renamings, and find the pattern underneath. It was obscene, how good he was. It was the guild's gift in him, the inheritance she'd spent chapters of her existence despising.

It was also, she admitted in the privacy of her own fading head, the only reason they were still alive.

"Left here," he murmured.

"There is no left here. There's a wall."

"There's a wall that used to be a corridor. Feel the draft." He pressed his palm flat against the stone. "Air moving means space beyond. Space beyond means the map has a gap, and gaps go somewhere." He glanced back at her, and there was something boyish in it, something that belonged to a Jonah before exile, before the trunk of guild papers, before the Edict with his father's signature. "Trust me?"

"I trust the draft," Ilsa said. "You're an accessory."

He laughed — actually laughed, down here, in the dark, with the child they both loved stolen away beneath a hill that hated existing. And Ilsa felt something crack in her chest that had nothing to do with erasure.

* * *

### JONAH

The passage beyond the false wall held, and Jonah allowed himself three full seconds of smugness before the ceiling groaned.

"That's the sound," Ilsa said flatly, "of terrain arguing back."

They ran. The tunnel behind them slumped like a tired shoulder; dust breathed out of cracks; somewhere far off, stone settled into a shape it preferred. Jonah's lungs burned and his mind raced ahead of his feet, mapping as he went — junction, junction, slope, the smell of deeper air — building the hollow hills inside himself faster than they could unbuild themselves around him.

Then the tunnel opened, without warning, into a vaulted chamber lit by lanterns of green glass, and Jonah saw the jailer.

It was a memory-man, tall and kind-faced and slightly translucent, wearing the uniform of a prison guard from a country that no longer existed, holding a ring of keys to doors that had been erased so long ago the keys had forgotten what locking was. He looked at them with weary, gentle eyes.

"You're the second rescue party," he said. "The first was a woman with a lantern and a very bad opinion of councils."

Ilsa went still. "Maren. Maren came alone?"

"She came, she argued beautifully, and then they put her somewhere even I can't unlock." The jailer sighed. "But the child — the little one, Sef — is two corridors east, behind a door of guild-iron. Guild-iron answers to guild-blood." His gaze settled on Jonah, apologetic and ancient. "That would be you."

"They knew," Jonah said quietly. "The faction didn't take Sef despite me. They took Sef knowing I'd come. I'm the key to the key."

"Yes," said the jailer, with the tactlessness of the very old or the very transparent. "Rather clever of them, really."

"Later," Ilsa said, "we can all sit down and admire how clever everyone is. Move."

* * *

### ILSA

Two corridors east, the guild-iron door stood in the rock like a verdict.

Ilsa watched Jonah approach it and saw the moment land on him — the moment she'd been waiting for since the fogline, since the archive, since she'd pressed the Edict into his hands and watched his world rearrange itself around his father's signature. His blood on the iron. His family's craft holding the cage shut. Every sin of the guild, distilled to a single lock waiting for his hand.

He pressed his palm to the metal. It recognized him. Of course it did. Doors always recognize the people who built them; it's the people they hurt who have to learn.

The lock turned with a sound like a small apology.

Inside, curled on a cot in the lamplight, Sef lifted their head — and Ilsa's whole long borrowed life narrowed to that one point of light. The child looked thinner. Paler, if a fragment of a country could be said to have color at all. But alive. Awake. And when their eyes found Jonah first — found him first, before her, before anyone — Ilsa felt a grief so old and so specific that she nearly laughed at herself.

*Jealous of a child,* she thought. *Of a child, over a man who maps things.*

"Sef," Jonah breathed, and crossed the cell in three strides, and gathered the child up, and Sef clung to him with the fierce, wordless grip of someone who had been counting hours. Over the small shoulder, Sef's eyes met Ilsa's, and the child mouthed her name — *Ilsa* — and reached out one hand toward her, and she took it, and that was enough. That was more than enough. She was a memory; she had learned to live on enough.

"We have to go now," Sef whispered. "They're opening the Door tonight. The tall one — Corvess — he says the country doesn't need all of us. Just the Door, and a key, and—" The child's voice cracked. "He said I'm the key, Jonah. He said I'm what burns."

"No one is burning," Jonah said, in the voice Ilsa had privately named *the mapmaker's calm*, the one he used for floods and border disputes and other people's panics. "We're leaving. Ilsa — route?"

And here Ilsa did the thing she had sworn not to do, the thing she had been saving, hoarding, rationing like the last water in a dry country.

She told the truth.

"The direct way is gone — it collapsed behind us. There's one passage left, the old processional under the third hill. It runs straight to the fogline." She met Jonah's eyes. "It's dying, Jonah. Nobody remembers it anymore. It's held together by exactly one memory, and I am carrying that memory in my satchel, and the moment I pour it into the stone, the passage will hold — but not on its own. Someone has to stay at its heart and keep remembering it, out loud, the whole way through. Keep it shaped. Keep it *real* while three people run along it."

A silence. Even the tunnel seemed to hold its breath.

"And how much does that cost?" Jonah asked, very quietly, in the voice of a man who already knew.

Ilsa smiled at him — the unlocked-door smile, the real one, saved for last.

"Everything after the pouring," she said. "Which, for a woman made entirely of after, is rather a lot."

* * *

### JONAH

"No," Jonah said. "No. Find another way. There's always another way — that's the entire point of maps—"

"This isn't a map problem." Ilsa was already uncorking the first bottle, and her hands were steady, and that steadiness frightened him more than the collapsing hills. "This is a Veylmark problem, and Veylmark problems end one of two ways: something is forgotten, or something is chosen. I've been forgotten for two hundred years, Jonah. Let me be *chosen* instead. It's a better story."

Sef was crying. Small, furious tears, the tears of a child who had already lost one country and refused to lose a person. "You promised," Sef said. "In the city, at the festival — you said memories are how we love people. You said—"

"I said memories are how we love people *who are gone*," Ilsa said gently, kneeling to the child's height. "So love me hard, little ember. Love me so thoroughly that I become one of those. That's not a loss. That's a promotion."

Jonah grabbed her wrist. "There has to be another—"

"Jonah." She looked at him, and her eyes were already beginning to thin at the edges, silver creeping in like frost across a window. "I guarded an archive of everything my country ever was, and do you know what I learned? The guild didn't erase Veylmark with armies. They erased it with *one signature*. One act, freely given, by a man who thought he was doing right." Her grip tightened on his arm. "Your father gave his signature to unmake us. Give yours — give *yourself* — to making us again. That's the trade. That's the whole trade. Don't waste my death arguing about the exchange rate."

He wanted to argue. God, he wanted to argue; he was built for arguing with terrain, with empires, with the dead. But the tunnel behind them groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a country clearing its throat to forget, and Sef's hand was in his, and Ilsa was already walking toward the heart of the passage with a bottle in each hand like a woman carrying candles into a church.

"Ilsa," he said.

She paused. Didn't turn.

"I'm sorry. For the Edict. For my father. For every year you stood guard over ashes while my family slept soundly in a house paid for with your name." His voice broke on it, finally, uselessly, too late. "Forgive me. Please. Say you forgive me."

For a long moment she was silent. Then, softly, without turning:

"I forgave you in the archive, the day you cried over a coastline. I just didn't want to ruin the argument." She glanced back, one last time, silver-eyed and smiling. "Now run, cartographer. And when you get to the Door — whatever they offer you, whatever it costs — remember that some things are worth more than a name."

She poured the first bottle into the stones, and began to speak.

* * *

### ILSA

She spoke the processional road back into being.

"—and here stood the arch of the Weavers' Guild, blue and gold, and the bells rang on feast days until the pigeons fled the towers—"

The tunnel firmed around her words. Stone remembered its arches. Lamps that had never hung there bloomed into lamplight, because she remembered the lamplight, because she had catalogued it, archived it, loved it, bottle by bottle, for two centuries.

"—and here the children ran races on Founding Day, and old Tam Vetch sold honey-bread at the third turning, and he always gave the smallest child an extra piece and pretended he hadn't—"

Behind her, running: footsteps. Two sets. One small and quick, one heavier, stumbling, refusing to look back. Good. Refusing to look back was the only way through.

"—and here the river Vael ran clear in spring, and the boats went down to the sea with red sails, red sails, red sails—"

Her hands were going. She could see the lamplight through them now, pretty and terrible, like stained glass learning to be window. It didn't hurt. That was the strange mercy of it. Forgetting, done willingly, felt like setting down something heavy after a very long walk.

She poured the last bottle. The road blazed into fullness — banners, cobbles, the ghost-smell of honey-bread — and she felt the whole of herself pour out after it, every year, every name, every archived morning of the country she had guarded with her stubborn, borrowed life.

The last thing she remembered choosing to remember was a man's face. Not her father's, not the archivists', none of the two hundred years of scholars and soldiers and bell-ringers.

His. The cartographer's. Crying over a coastline.

*Oh,* she thought, going soft at the edges, going bright. *So that's what I was keeping, all this time.*

* * *

### JONAH

The passage held just long enough.

They came out of the third hill into grey daylight and clean wind, Sef sobbing against his side, the fogline roaring behind them — and Jonah turned, because he couldn't not turn, because some part of him had been listening to her voice the whole way, memorizing it, mapping it—

—and caught her as she came apart.

She weighed nothing. That was the unbearable thing. Two hundred years of guarding a nation, and she weighed nothing, a drift of silver in the shape of a woman, her eyes finding his with the last focus she had left.

"You made it," she whispered. Her voice was already wind. "Good. Good. Listen — the Door opens for willing memory, freely given. Remember that. Whatever Corvess tells you. Whatever he threatens. The Door knows the difference between taking and giving, and it will not be fooled, and neither—" the smile again, faint as breath, "—neither should you be."

"Ilsa—"

"Love the child," she said. "Love the country. And when you stand at the Door and they ask you what you're willing to pay—" her fingers tightened on his sleeve, or tried to; there was hardly enough of her left to try, "—don't you dare hesitate, heir. Don't you dare be your father."

"I forgive you," Jonah said, stupidly, desperately, getting it backwards, because grief makes liars of us all. "Ilsa, I forgive you, I—"

"I know," said the mist, kindly. "I know. That was never the debt."

And she was gone — not violently, not tragically, but the way morning fog goes when the sun decides the day should begin: gradually, then all at once, then everywhere. The wind carried a smell of honey-bread and red sails past his face, and then nothing, and then the ordinary silence of a valley's edge.

Sef was crying into his coat. Jonah knelt and held the child and looked at his own empty hands.

Somewhere beneath the third hill, a Door was waiting. Somewhere above it, a man named Corvess was preparing to open it with a stolen child and a stolen country, and he would fail, because Ilsa had spent her eternity making sure Jonah would arrive in time to stop him.

"Come on," Jonah said hoarsely, standing, wiping Sef's face with a thumb that shook. "We have a Door to reach. And Sef — whatever it asks of me—"

He didn't finish the sentence. He couldn't. But the child looked up at him with eyes the color of a coastline drawn from a dream, and something passed between them that needed no words at all.

The Last Door waited. And Jonah Joubert, heir to the guild that had unmapped a nation, walked toward it with the last new memory Veylmark would ever make, holding his hand.
