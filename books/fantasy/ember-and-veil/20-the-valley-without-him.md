## 20. The Valley Without Him

The rain came back in the third week of autumn, the way it always did, sliding down the valley walls like a curtain drawn by some patient hand. Jonah Joubert watched it from his worktable with a cup of tea going cold beside him and the particular emptiness of a man who has forgotten to be lonely and remembers it all at once.

He had been ill. That was the story he'd settled on, because it was the only one that fit. A fever, perhaps, caught on one of his long walks along the fogline — the boundary where the valley ended and the world's magic began again, though *began* was a generous word for it. He remembered walking out there. He remembered the mist sitting low and grey over the pass, thick as wool. And then—

Then nothing. A gap, clean as a page torn from a ledger.

He had woken in his own bed three days ago with mud on his boots and an ache behind his breastbone that no physician could name. Old Marta from the village had clucked over him, fed him broth, and pronounced him lucky.

"Lucky," Jonah repeated now, to the empty room, and the room did not disagree.

He turned back to his work. This was the arrangement of his life: the valley refused magic, so he supplied maps of places that ran on nothing else. Kingdoms where rivers changed their minds mid-course. Archipelagos that migrated south each winter like birds. He drew them all in fine ink on good paper, and once a season a trader carried them over the pass, and once a year a payment came back, and between those two events Jonah lived inside his own careful lines.

It was a good life. He told himself that often enough that the words had worn smooth, like stones in a stream.

He reached for his journal — the current one, the fat one with the cracked spine — to note the rainfall, because a cartographer notes everything, even weather, even grief, especially the things he cannot draw.

The pages were wrong.

* * *

Jonah sat very still for a long moment, the way he did when a map contradicted itself, because panic was useless and precision was a kind of prayer.

His handwriting filled the last third of the book, as expected. But the entries stopped. Not trailed off — *stopped*, mid-sentence, at the top of a page dated the ninth day of the harvest month:

*I have been thinking about what Ilsa said—*

And then nothing. The rest of the page blank. The next page blank. Forty blank pages after that, and then, near the back, in his hand but looser, faster, almost desperate, a single line:

*If you are reading this, you were right to keep writing. Keep writing.*

He read it four times. His own words, and they meant nothing to him. It was like finding a letter addressed to a stranger who happened to share his name.

*Ilsa,* he thought. *Who is Ilsa?*

The name sat in him strangely, the way a key sits in a lock it doesn't fit — familiar in shape, useless in function. He searched the earlier journals. The name appeared nowhere. Neither did anything else from the missing weeks: no sketches, no observations, no complaints about the food or the rain, which Jonah considered the two great subjects of any honest diary.

There were gaps in the older books too, he realized. Small ones. A week here, three days there, scattered through the past months like moths through cloth. He had assumed busyness. Now, holding the torn-edge silence of that final entry, he understood that he had not been busy. He had been *elsewhere*. And whatever had happened elsewhere had been important enough to write about, and terrible enough — or precious enough — that someone had taken it out of him like a splinter, leaving only the ache of the wound.

"Keep writing," he said aloud. "Right. Gladly. Of what?"

The house answered with rain.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he always did with problems too large for a single frame: he made an inventory.

The journals went on the left side of the table. On the right, everything in the house that seemed newly strange. The inventory grew longer than he liked.

Item: a second teacup in the cupboard, chipped at the rim, that Jonah had no memory of buying. He drank from it anyway. It felt correct in his hand, which unsettled him more than if it hadn't.

Item: a child's wooden top, tucked behind the kindling box. No children lived within a day's walk of the valley. He turned it over twice and set it on the shelf above the fire, where it looked, absurdly, at home.

Item: the smell of woodsmoke and something sweeter — honey-cakes, maybe — that kept arriving in rooms he'd already cleaned, like a guest who wouldn't leave.

Item, and this was the one he circled back to all afternoon: the map.

He found it in the trunk. Not the guild trunk — that stayed locked in the corner where it had always stayed, full of his father's papers that Jonah had never had the stomach to sort — but a plain cedar chest at the foot of his bed that he could have sworn held only blankets. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that made his throat tighten before he even unrolled it, lay a finished map.

Not a sketch. Not a draft. A *finished* map, inked and colored and bordered, the coastlines confident, the mountain hachures dense and lovely, the rivers named in a flowing script that was unmistakably, undeniably his own hand. He knew his own hand the way he knew his own heartbeat. Every quirk of it. The way his *r*s leaned. The little hook he put on the compass rose's north point, a habit inherited from his father.

The country on the map did not exist.

Jonah knew this the way other men knew their prayers. He had spent thirty years cataloguing the world's edges; he had drawn coasts no living explorer had seen, because the guild's old charts whispered them to him in dreams. There was no nation called —

He read the title cartouche, and his mouth went dry.

**VEYLMARK**

The word hit him somewhere below thought. His eyes stung. He actually pressed the heel of his hand against them, annoyed at himself, the way you'd be annoyed at a draughty window — because why would a map make him weep? Whose tears were these? They felt borrowed. They felt like they belonged to a version of him standing in this exact spot, looking at this exact map, and grieving something enormous.

Veylmark. He said it under his breath and the valley seemed to lean away from the sound.

He spread the map across the whole table and studied it as he studied everything: methodically, corner to corner, letting the details accumulate into understanding. The capital sat on a river fork — *Corvessa*, the label read, in letters slightly smudged, as if the ink had been written while the writer's hands shook. Roads radiated outward. One of them, running west toward the mountains, toward the fogline, toward *here*, was drawn heavier than the others. Traveled often. Or traveled once, hard, by someone who wanted to remember every stone of it.

Along that road, in the margin, in tiny script, someone had noted distances. And beside one bend in the river, where the hills went hollow, a small annotation: *careful here — the ground forgets.*

"The ground forgets," Jonah murmured. "Well. Apparently so do I."

There were lanterns marked on the capital. Dozens of them, tiny circles of gold leaf, clustered in the squares like fallen stars. He touched one with his fingertip and felt — nothing, obviously, it was gold leaf on paper, and yet his chest did that thing again, that hollow expanding thing, like a bell rung in an empty church.

Somewhere in this city, someone had lit a lantern for something forgotten. He didn't know how he knew that. He knew it the way he knew the weight of his own pen.

* * *

Toward evening the rain eased and Jonah did what he always did when the house grew too full of questions: he walked.

The path along the valley floor was mud and slate, hedged with wet gorse. The sheep watched him pass with their usual contempt. At the valley's edge the land climbed in a long grey shoulder toward the pass, and at the top of that shoulder, always, hung the fogline — the border where the world resumed its enchantments and the valley's clean nothingness gave way. Villagers didn't go up there. Jonah went up there constantly, telling himself it was for the views, knowing it was because the fogline was the closest thing his exile had to a horizon.

Tonight the mist sat low and still. Unnaturally still. It lay over the pass like a sleeping animal, and Jonah stood at its edge with his collar up and his breath steaming, and felt the ache behind his breastbone shift and turn over, like something waking.

"You know something," he told the fog. "I don't imagine you'll tell me."

The fog, being fog, declined.

But standing there, he noticed things. A patch of ground just past the boundary where the grass grew flattened in two narrow lines, boot-width apart, leading in. Leading *out*, rather — the prints pointed down-valley, toward his door, though they were weeks old and half-healed by rain. Someone had come out of the mist and walked to his house. Someone had knocked.

He remembered no knocking. He remembered fever, and waking, and mud on his boots.

And there — this was the thing that finally made the cold walk all the way through him — there was a smaller print beside the larger ones. Child-sized. Side by side the whole way down, big steps and little steps, like a pair of compass legs drawing one line.

Jonah crouched and laid his palm flat on the wet grass, as if the ground might hand him the memory directly. It offered only cold, and the faintest sweetness of woodsmoke, and underneath that, impossibly, honey-cakes.

"I'm missing something," he said quietly. "Something large."

The ache behind his breastbone agreed. It had been agreeing for weeks. It was the strangest grief he'd ever carried — grief with no object, mourning with no grave, love with no face. Some nights he woke with a name half-formed on his lips, a short bright syllable, and by the time he lit the candle it was gone, leaving only the shape of where it had been, the way a removed picture leaves a paler rectangle on a sun-faded wall.

He had started, without deciding to, keeping the second teacup filled. Just in case. He'd caught himself doing it twice and both times had decided, firmly, not to examine it. A man in his position learned which mysteries to leave folded.

* * *

Back home, the fire had burned low. Jonah relit the lamps, made fresh tea — two cups, one he ignored with great discipline — and returned to the map.

He worked by lamplight the way he'd worked his whole life, and slowly the work did what work always did for him: it became a conversation. The map talked. He listened. Coastlines confessed their histories; rivers explained themselves. It was the nearest thing to magic his valley allowed, and he had never once questioned the gift.

Except tonight the map was saying something he couldn't parse.

In the far northwest, where the mountains marched off the parchment's edge, the cartographer — *he*, damn it, his own hand — had done something Jonah never did. He had drawn beyond the border of the known. The neat frame line of the map simply opened, a deliberate gap in the border, and through it the hills continued in lighter ink, fading, unfinished, trailing off toward a mark in the very corner of the sheet.

A door. A tiny, careful drawing of a door, standing free among the hills, with light coming through the crack of it in fine rays of gold leaf.

Beneath it, in that same shaken script: *the Last Door. It opens for what is freely given.*

Jonah sat back. The lamp flickered. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, the ordinary sounds of an ordinary house, and none of it accounted for the fact that his eyes were burning again, or that his hand had risen halfway to his own chest as though reaching for someone who used to stand there.

"Freely given," he repeated. "Given to whom? Given *for* what?"

No answer. Only the map, glowing softly under the lamp, patient as all his maps were patient, waiting for him to catch up.

He blew out the lamps at last and stood a while in the dark, listening to the rain begin again against the shutters. In the fireplace the embers shifted, and one of them — he would remember this, later, though he'd never know why — flared briefly the color of a lantern.

He slept badly. He dreamed of a road through hollow hills, of bells ringing in a city he'd never seen, of a small hand in his, and woke with wet cheeks and no memory of why, and got up, and made tea, and sat down at his table, and — because a voice he didn't remember had told him to, and because it was the only instruction in the world he trusted — he opened a fresh journal and began to write.

*Day four since the fever. Rain. Found a map of a country called Veylmark in my own hand. I don't know it. I miss it. Those two facts should not be able to live in the same man, and yet here we are.*

He wrote until the light came. And on the fifth day, and the sixth, he wrote more, filling pages with questions, and on the seventh day the rain stopped, and the fog over the pass lifted for the first time in weeks, and Jonah Joubert looked up from his table because he heard — faint, impossible, coming down the long road from the fogline — footsteps.

Two sets of them. One long. One short.

He was at the door before he had decided to move, heart hammering a rhythm he didn't recognize, watching a figure resolve out of the clearing mist with something glinting at their chest like a struck ember — and every nerve in his body said, with a certainty that bypassed all reason, all memory, all sense:

*You know them. You have always known them.*
