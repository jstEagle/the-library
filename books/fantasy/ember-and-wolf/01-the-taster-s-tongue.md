## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The trick to surviving a poisoned breakfast, Jude Villalobos had learned over nine years of service, was to arrive hungry.

A hungry tongue paid attention. A full one grew lazy, and lazy tongues ended up in the ground behind the tasters' barracks with a nice little marker and a pension for nobody, because tasters were not permitted families. The crown considered that a kindness. Families complicated grief.

So Jude rose before the bells, skipped the bread and honey set out in the servants' hall, and walked the long colonnade to the usurper's kitchens with an empty stomach and a mind like a ledger: clean pages, sharp columns, nothing written on them yet.

"Morning, Villalobos," said Cook Marta, who had hands like hams and a heart she kept hidden under all that flour the way other people hid knives.

"Marta."

"He wants the eggs with the river trout again." She said it the way priests said *amen* — flat, resigned, slightly accusing the heavens.

"The trout's from the southern marshes this week?"

"Fresh catch. Came up yesterday with the salt barges."

Jude nodded and filed that away. Southern marshes meant three days in a barrel of brine, which meant certain alkaloids could bloom in the flesh if the barrel had been sealed wrong, which happened every spring when the bargemen got greedy about weight. Not poison, exactly. Just the marsh's own small malice, concentrated. Jude had tasted it twice before and spent both afternoons quietly vomiting behind the chapel while being praised for vigilance.

They took the silver spoon from the warming drawer — tasters' spoons never left their bodies, a superstition everyone observed and no one admitted to — and began.

Eggs first. Butter, chive, a whisper of smoke from the wood fire, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang of truth. That was the thing Jude never told anyone, the thing they kept folded away beneath nine years of careful mediocrity: every dish spoke to them. Not in words. In certainties. The eggs said *this butter is honest*. The bread said *the miller waters his flour, but only a little, and he is sorry about it*. Food could not lie to Jude. It was the one gift the gods had bothered to give them, and it was so small, so useless-seeming, that even the Ember Guild's testers had shrugged and stamped their papers *ungifted* twenty years ago.

Which was precisely why it was useful. A person who could taste truth in a spoonful could taste it in a confession, in a bribe, in a treaty clause dissolved into wine. The Guild had figured that out eventually. Ovid had figured it out fastest, which was why Ovid now owned Jude's nights while the crown owned their mornings.

The trout came last. Jude cut a sliver from the flank, let it sit on the tongue, and waited for the fish to introduce itself.

It did not introduce itself politely.

Heat bloomed at the back of the throat — not spice, not the marsh's mineral sourness, but something older and more deliberate, a sweetness gone wrong, like honey poured over a grave. Jude's tongue went numb. Their vision narrowed to a bright pinhole. Somewhere very far away, Marta was saying their name.

*Hemlock derivative,* thought Jude, with the strange clarity of the nearly dead. *Prepared properly, too. Someone studied.*

Then the world tilted, and Jude caught the edge of the table, and the second thing happened — the thing that would ruin everything if anyone saw.

Their body healed.

Not dramatically. No glow, no theater. Just the numbness receding like a tide, the pinhole widening, the heartbeat steadying itself as though someone had reached inside Jude's chest and smoothed the arrhythmia flat with a thumb. Nine years of microdoses had built an armor in their blood, yes — that was the official story, the story every taster told — but this had been a proper dose, a killing dose, and proper doses left even armored men gray and shaking for a day.

Jude felt fine. Better than fine. They felt *rested*, which no one in this palace ever was.

Marta was gripping their elbow. "Villalobos. You've gone white as the tablecloth."

"Bad oyster," Jude said, because the trout was right there and lying about it seemed rude. "I'm well. Clear the dish."

"You're sure—"

"*Clear the dish*, Marta."

She cleared it. She also, Jude noted with the cold arithmetic part of their brain, looked toward the door — where a man in wolf-grey leathers had been standing for some minutes, arms crossed, watching.

Wolf-hunters wore grey because grey was the color of ash, and ash was what they promised to leave of the gifted. This one was young, broad through the shoulders, with the patient stillness of a hunting dog that has found a scent and is deciding whether it's worth waking its master. He'd been watching Jude heal. Jude was almost certain he'd been watching Jude heal.

Almost certain was a word Jude lived inside, most days. It fit like an old coat.

"I'll want a report on that dish," the hunter said, strolling closer. His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant, the way a snare is pleasant until it closes. "Name's Corvane. Ser Corvane, lately of the Gift Hunt."

"Taster Villalobos. Lately of breakfast." Jude smiled the smile that had kept them alive through two kings and one usurpation: modest, forgettable, faintly dim. "You'll have my report through the steward's office, same as always."

"The steward says you recovered remarkably fast."

"The steward should eat more oysters."

Corvane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, entirely false. "You know," he said, leaning in just enough that Jude could smell the leather and iron of him, "the new edict says any soul harboring a gift must be named within the fortnight. Any soul *concealing* one—" he tapped the table where the trout had been, "—well. Ash keeps better than secrets."

"I wouldn't know. I keep neither." Jude held his gaze with the mild bovine patience of a man thinking about lunch. "Was there anything else? His Grace wakes hungry, and I've a schedule."

Something flickered in Corvane's eyes — disappointment, maybe, or the beginning of doubt about his own certainty. A forgettable man was hard to accuse. Jude had spent decades becoming furniture, and furniture was never burned.

"For now," Corvane said, and strolled out whistling.

Jude stood alone in the kitchen for a long moment, one hand pressed flat against the cool stone of the counter, counting breaths until their heart agreed to behave like an ordinary heart.

*He saw.* Or he didn't. Either way, the margins had just gotten thinner.

* * *

The Ember Guild kept its offices above a chandler's shop in Tallow Row, where the smell of boiling fat covered the smell of ink, and no one respectable ever visited. Jude arrived at dusk with a basket of "kitchen scraps" — under which, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the day's true cargo: a list of names moving through the capital, cross-referenced against the usurper's new household appointments.

Ovid was waiting in the back room, as he always was, a small round man shaped like a teapot, currently pouring himself tea with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"You're late."

"I was nearly murdered at breakfast. Forgive me."

"Again?" Ovid set down the pot. "That's the third time this year. You're getting careless."

"I'm getting *popular*. There's a difference." Jude unwrapped the list and laid it on the scarred oak table between them. "Someone in the usurper's own kitchens is buying hemlock derivatives. Properly prepared. Which means either he's poisoning his enemies—"

"—or someone's poisoning *him*, and you're standing in the way." Ovid peered at the list over his spectacles. "And the wolf-hunters noticed your recovery."

"One of them. Corvane. He's new."

"They're all new. The Hunt doubled last month." Ovid removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which was what he did instead of sighing. "Which brings us to tonight's work. The shipment came in from the eastern road. You'll want to see it before we catalogue it."

The "shipment" occupied three crates in the cellar, and it was, as shipments went, a fairly ordinary collection of impossible things: a woman who could unpick locks by singing to them; twin boys whose shadows moved independently and could carry messages across the city faster than any rider; a farmer's daughter who coaxed blight out of wheat fields with her bare hands, leaving the grain golden. Each had a page in the guild's ledger — name, gift, route, safehouse, the fee paid or forgiven. Each would be gone within the week, folded into the caravans heading east toward the free cities, where gifted folk were taxed rather than burned.

Jude catalogued them with the same care they gave the usurper's breakfast: precise columns, unremarkable handwriting, nothing that would raise an eyebrow if seized. Except that if these pages were seized, forty people would burn.

"You've heard the proclamation," Ovid said. It wasn't a question. Bells had rung it from every tower that morning: *the hunt is open, the gifted are outlawed, sheltering one is treason.*

"I heard it."

"Then you understand the ledgers can't stay here. Every name in those books is a death warrant waiting for a signature. We move them to the free cities — all of them, the whole archive — before the month turns."

"All of them?" Jude looked up. The ledgers weren't just records. They were eleven years of Jude's life, every smuggled soul, every forged paper, every risk. "Ovid, those books are the guild's whole history. If we lose them—"

"If we *keep* them, we lose everything they name." He tapped the table. "You'll carry the master volumes yourself. You're the only courier the Hunt doesn't watch, because you're the only courier in this city too dull to be interesting."

"Furniture," Jude agreed mildly. "The most dangerous kind."

Ovid almost smiled. Then he opened the current ledger to make a final entry, ran his finger down the column of names scheduled for the eastern caravan, and stopped.

His face did something complicated.

"What," Jude said.

"Nothing. An old entry. Cross-referenced oddly." But he turned the book around, and there it was, in Jude's own handwriting, dated six years back:

**VANE, S. — gift: ember-sight (sees heat, tracks kindled flame). Status: recruited, declined. Note: subject declined for reasons personal. Do not approach again.**

Serah Vane. Who had stood in a rain-soaked alley at nineteen and shown Jude her hands glowing like banked coals, and asked, terrified and hopeful, whether the guild could use her. Who had declined — because of Jude, because of what Jude hadn't been brave enough to say — and walked out of that alley and into the crown's service, where a girl who could see fire in the dark was worth ten soldiers.

Who was, as of last spring's appointments, First Hunter of the Gift Hunt.

"She's leading it now," Jude said. Their voice sounded fine. Voices were easy to train. "Ovid. The woman leading the hunt against us is—"

"I know who she is." Ovid closed the ledger gently, the way one closed a door on a sickroom. "Which is why you'll take the eastern road yourself, and why you'll leave tonight if my advice means anything. Because Serah Vane knows exactly how interesting you are — and she's the one person alive who knows you were never furniture at all."

Outside, the bells rang the hour, and somewhere across the darkening city, Jude knew, wolf-hunters were lighting lamps and opening files.

One of those files, very soon, would have Jude Villalobos's name in it. Written, perhaps, in a hand Jude had once loved enough to memorize.
