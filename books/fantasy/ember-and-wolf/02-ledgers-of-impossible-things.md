## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The Ember Guild kept its books the way other people kept secrets: badly, and in code that only made sense after wine.

Jude had been balancing them for six years, hunched over a desk in the back room of a candlemaker's shop that never seemed to sell any candles. The shop was a front, obviously. The candles were real enough—wax and wick and honest flame—but nobody bought them, because the shopkeeper, a broad-shouldered woman named Petra who could kindle fire from her fingertips, had a habit of lighting every sample on display simultaneously whenever a customer lingered too long.

"Bad for business," Jude had told her once.

"Good for honesty," Petra had said, and lit another one.

So it was Jude alone, that gray morning, with the ledgers spread out like a confession: three volumes bound in oxblood leather, their pages dense with the guild's particular arithmetic. Not coin, mostly. Names. Dates. Routes. The impossible things people could do, written down in Jude's small, careful hand so that if the worst happened—if a safehouse burned, if a smuggler vanished mid-crossing—the guild would know who to grieve for, and where.

*Ilse Marrow, kindler of frost. Moved north via the salt road, 14th of the Harvest Moon. Fee waived; she paid in bread, which was excellent.*

*The twins Ferro and Bram, who could speak to locks until the locks opened out of sheer politeness. Currently lodged above the tannery. Do not let them near the tannery's locks.*

Jude dipped the pen and wrote, in the margin beside Ilse's name: *send her more bread.*

It was, all things considered, a good morning. The usurper's breakfast had been merely unpleasant rather than lethal—cold partridge, a wine with ambitions—and Jude's tongue had reported nothing worse than resentment. Their own body felt ordinary. Unremarkable. Exactly as designed.

Then the bell above the shop door rang, and Ovid came in wearing his funeral coat.

Ovid owned two coats: a brown one for days when the world was merely inconvenient, and a black one, heavy with silver thread at the cuffs, for days when he intended to deliver news that would ruin someone's week. He wore the black one perhaps four times a year. Jude put down the pen.

"Out with it," they said. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you wear when you're about to say something terrible and want credit for saying it gently."

Ovid sat down across from the ledgers, folded his hands, and said gently: "The Gift Hunt has been declared."

The words landed in the room and just—stayed there. Somewhere in the shopfront, Petra lit a candle by accident. Or possibly not by accident.

"When?" Jude asked.

"This morning. Proclamation on every gate. *By decree of the rightful crown, all persons of unnatural gift are enemies of the realm, to be hunted, taken, and judged.*" Ovid's mouth twisted around the word *rightful*. "He's calling them wolf-hunters. The ones who do the taking. New order, new badges, new pay. He's recruiting from the old king's knights, the ones with debts or grudges."

"And the ledgers?"

"Are now a list of targets." Ovid laid one broad finger on the nearest volume, almost tenderly. "Every name in these books is a person the crown can find, if the books are found. Which is why the books are going to the free cities. All of them. You'll carry them yourself."

"I always carry them myself."

"You'll carry them *out of the capital*, Jude. Across the salt road, past the checkpoints, into Vhalen's harbor, and you will not come back until I tell you to come back." He paused. "If I tell you to come back."

Jude looked at the ledgers. Six years of names. Six years of small mercies recorded in a dead language of their own invention. "And you?"

"I have letters to write and a council to mislead," Ovid said, rising. "I'm very good at misleading councils. It's the one gift I have that isn't illegal yet." At the door he stopped, and his voice dropped its performance entirely. "Jude. If the hunt finds you before you find the road—burn them. Don't be noble about it. Paper can be rewritten. People can't."

Then he was gone, and the bell rang behind him like a small, nervous applause.

* * *

**From the private papers of Ovid, guildmaster of the Ember Guild, written that same night:**

*I lied to the boy.*

*Not about the hunt—that is true enough, gods rot the man's black heart—but about the reason the ledgers must go west. Yes, the names must be sheltered. But there is a page in the third volume, in Jude's own hand, that I did not have the heart to point out. A name entered last spring, when we smuggled a knight's daughter through the undercity and Jude asked, as they always ask, how the fee was paid.*

*They wrote the answer down without looking at it closely. I watched them do it. I said nothing, because I am a coward in exactly one direction, and it has always been toward kindness.*

*Serah Vane. Paid in full, in crown silver, by Serah Vane, to move her younger brother out of the capital before his gift could be discovered.*

*The same Serah Vane who, as of this morning, wears the badge of the wolf-hunters.*

*Jude will find the name tonight, because Jude finds everything—it is their curse and their trade. And when they find it, they will understand what I understood the moment I read it back: that the woman hunting the gifted once emptied her purse to save one. That is either a lever or a wound, and I am too old to decide which.*

*Let the road decide. Roads are wiser than I am.*

* * *

Jude did not find the name that night. They found it at dawn, because they were stubborn about grief and insisted on finishing the third volume first, page by page, the way you finish a meal even when the cook has died.

The candle had burned to a stub. The shop was silent except for Petra snoring upstairs, dreaming, presumably, of arson. Jude turned the page, pen hovering, ready to enter the night's final entry—a glassblower's apprentice hidden in a shipment of mirrors—and there it was.

*Fee paid in full, crown silver, by S. Vane. Purpose: passage for her brother, Tomas Vane, age eleven, gift: he makes plants grow where nothing should grow. Moved south, 3rd of the Planting Moon.*

Jude read it twice. Three times. The fourth time was not reading; it was bracing.

*Serah Vane.*

The name arrived the way a dog arrives—you heard it before you saw it, and then it was on you with its whole weight.

Six years ago. A kitchen in the old palace, before the old king went cold, before the usurper learned to smile with all his teeth. Jude had been nineteen and newly promoted to taster, terrified and pretending not to be, and Serah had been a knight-candidate assigned to watch them eat—which, in retrospect, was the most romantic assignment the crown ever issued. She'd stood by the door in her half-polished armor and watched Jude taste seven courses of food that might kill them, and at the seventh course she'd said, quietly:

"You flinch before the bad flavors. Did you know?"

"No," Jude had said, which was a lie, because they flinched constantly. Every dish spoke to them. Honeyed lies tasted of copper. Poison, real poison, tasted of snow. And truth—truth tasted like bread still warm from the oven, and Serah Vane, complaining about her boots, had tasted like the warmest bread Jude had ever eaten.

"You're the bravest coward I've ever met," she'd said, and laughed, and Jude had fallen in love the way you fall downstairs—all at once, painfully, with no dignity whatsoever.

For two years they'd stolen hours. She'd taught Jude to hold a sword badly; Jude had taught her to hold still while they read the truth off her tongue, which she'd found unsettling and then indispensable. *Tell me if my commander is lying to me,* she'd said once, deadly serious, and Jude had tasted the commander's report—warm bread—and told her so, and she'd looked at them afterward like they were something rare.

Then the old king died. Then the usurper came. Then Serah stopped coming to the kitchens, and the last thing she ever said to Jude was *don't wait for me*, delivered flat as a coin, tasting of nothing at all.

Jude had assumed the nothing meant she'd stopped loving them. It had taken six years and this ledger page to consider the alternative: that the nothing was armor. That she'd emptied her purse in crown silver to smuggle her little brother—the boy who made weeds bloom in cracks, the boy whose gift would have gotten him culled—to safety, and then gone back to serving the crown that would have killed him, wearing its badge, carrying its orders.

*Don't wait for me.*

"Oh, you absolute idiot," Jude said aloud, to a woman a hundred rooftops away, and their voice cracked in the middle of *idiot*.

Petra appeared in the doorway in her nightshirt, hair like a haystack on fire. "Are you talking to the books again?"

"The books are talking to me."

"That's worse." Petra squinted at the ledger, at Jude's face, and wisely decided against asking. Instead she lit the stove with a snap of her fingers, because comfort, in Petra's grammar, was always expressed in heat. "Tea?"

"Tea," Jude agreed, and closed the ledger, and did not close it fast enough to keep from seeing the name one more time.

Here was the arithmetic, then, laid out plain as a balance sheet. The woman they had loved was now the crown's wolf. The ledgers in their arms named everyone the wolves were hunting. And somewhere between here and the free cities, those two facts were going to meet on the same road, because roads, as Ovid liked to say, were wiser than people, and this road had clearly been planning something for six years.

Jude packed the ledgers into a false-bottomed flour sack, ate a breakfast they didn't taste, and left the candlemaker's shop for the last time.

Behind them, Petra lit every candle in the window—one for each name in the book, though she didn't know it—and the whole street glowed like a warning.

Three days later, on the salt road, Jude would hear hooves behind them and turn to see a rider in wolf-grey, and the rider would pull off her helmet, and it would be Serah Vane, older and harder and tasting—when their treacherous gift finally got close enough—of warm bread.

But that was three days away. For now there was only the road, and the weight of names, and the past arriving, patient as winter, with teeth.
