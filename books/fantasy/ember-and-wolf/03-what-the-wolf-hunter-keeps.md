## 3. What the Wolf-Hunter Keeps

The smuggler ran across the rooftops of Copperlane like she was born to it, which, Serah reflected as she gained on her, was probably the point.

Gifted folk always ran like that—like the impossible thing inside them had taught their bodies a different grammar. This one moved in long, laughing strides over slate and tile, her braid streaming behind her, and where her bare feet touched the shingles, frost bloomed in delicate ferns and melted again before Serah's boots could find purchase. Twice Serah nearly went down on ice that hadn't existed a heartbeat earlier. It was, she had to admit, excellent work.

"Stop!" she shouted, because the orders said to shout it, and because shouting gave her something to do with the part of her that wanted very much not to catch this woman at all.

The smuggler glanced back, grinned—a real grin, bright and unafraid—and stepped off the edge of the dye-house roof into empty air.

Serah reached the edge two strides later and looked down in time to see the woman land in a passing cart of wet wool, sink to her shoulders in it, and pop up like a delighted otter. The cart driver began yelling. The wool would hide her scent, her trail, everything. By the time anyone thought to search the carts leaving Copperlane's dyers' row, she'd be three miles gone with a head full of someone else's laundry.

Serah stood on the roofline, breathing hard, and let her hand fall away from her sword.

*She got away,* she told herself. *Slipped me. Happens.*

It didn't happen. Not to her. Six years a wolf-hunter, forty-one captures, zero escapes, and every hunter in the keep knew the tally hung on the training-yard wall like a butcher's chart. Serah Vane did not lose people.

Except tonight she had, deliberately, and now she had to go home and write down a lie in her own neat, careful hand, and the worst part—the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone—was that lying had started to feel less like betrayal and more like breathing.

She climbed down, questioned the cart driver with appropriate ferocity, confiscated his hat for obstruction (he'd been wearing it; it seemed rude), and reported to Captain Dree at the ninth bell with frostbite on her fingers and a straight face.

"The Ashwick courier," Dree said, not looking up from his maps. "The one who freezes things."

"Lost her in Copperlane." Serah set the confiscated hat on his desk. "Roofwork. She knew the district better than I did."

Dree grunted. He was a tired man shaped like a dropped sack of flour, and he had never once in six years asked Serah why she hunted so well or what she did with the quiet hours after. She suspected he knew. She suspected everyone knew, the way garrisons know things: wordlessly, completely, and without mercy.

"Write it up," he said. "And Vane—" Now he looked up. His eyes were small and brown and missed nothing. "The Lord Protector wants you tomorrow. Personally. Something about the Ember Guild."

Serah's stomach performed a slow, practiced rotation, like a diver turning over.

"Yes, sir," she said, and went to write her lie.

* * *

The falsified report took her twenty minutes, most of which she spent sharpening a quill that didn't need it.

*Pursued suspect through Copperlane rooftops. Suspect demonstrated evasive familiarity with district. Lost contact near the dye-houses at dusk. Recommend expanded patrols along the canal.*

Every word true except the shape of them. That was the trick she'd learned, the only trick that mattered: never lie outright. Just arrange the truth like furniture until the room says what you need it to say. The smuggler *had* known the district better than Serah. Serah *had* lost contact. Nobody needed to know that Serah had watched a cart of wet wool carry a gifted woman to freedom while she stood on a rooftop memorizing the color of her grin so she could describe it badly in an official document.

*Suspect appeared to be in her thirties. Unremarkable. Brown hair.*

Her hair had been copper. It had caught the last of the sunset and burned.

Serah put down the quill and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

There was a version of her life, she sometimes thought, where she'd stayed in Millbrook. Where she'd married the cooper's son or the miller's daughter, whichever would have her, and grown broad and comfortable and dull, and never learned what her own heart could do when it decided, all on its own, to commit treason.

Because that was the thing nobody warned you about love. They warned you about everything else—about fire, about wolves, about the crown's long arm—but nobody told you that loving someone was itself a kind of gift, unregistered and unlawful, and that once you had it you were smuggling it for the rest of your life.

* * *

Jude Villalobos had been seventeen the first time Serah saw them, standing behind the pastry stall at the Millbrook market with flour on their cheekbones and an expression of profound skepticism directed at a tray of honey buns.

"You're going to buy one anyway," Jude had said, without being asked. "You always look like that right before you buy one."

"I don't *look* like anything."

"You look like a person negotiating with herself and losing." Jude had wrapped a honey bun in paper, held it out, and added, conversationally: "Also, the baker waters the glaze. You can taste it. Third water, maybe fourth. He's cheap about it."

Serah had bitten into the bun and tasted it—clearly, undeniably, the thinness of a stretched glaze—and felt the world tilt slightly on its axis. Because everyone in Millbrook knew Jude Villalobos had a good palate. A *famous* palate. But this wasn't good. This was impossible. This was a person tasting truth out of food like other people read weather in clouds.

"You're gifted," she'd whispered, horrified, thrilled, holding the honey bun like a live coal.

"I'm *employed*," Jude had said. "There's a difference. Sit down. Eat your bun. And if you ever tell anyone what I just said, I will deny it so thoroughly and so boringly that you'll doubt your own memory."

They were nineteen when they fell in love, properly, in the way of small towns where there is nothing to do but walk the river path and learn each other. Jude had shown her the ledger then—not the guild's, the first one, a schoolroom copybook where they recorded every dish they'd ever tasted and what was hidden in it. Cheated weights. Watered wine. A magistrate's cook dosing his rival's soup with something bitter and slow.

"Why do you keep it?" Serah had asked.

"Because somebody has to remember what actually happened," Jude said. "Truth goes bad fast, Serah. Like fish. Somebody has to taste it fresh and write it down before everyone agrees on a more convenient flavor."

"And if the truth is dangerous?"

Jude had smiled at her across the river path, that crooked, flour-dusted smile, and said, "Then I suppose somebody dangerous has to keep it."

She should have known then. She should have known that a person like that doesn't stay in Millbrook, doesn't stay *safe*, doesn't stay hers. But she was nineteen too, and in love, and she'd believed the ridiculous thing that young people believe: that the world would make room for them.

The world made room for the crown instead.

* * *

Serah's father had owed the old king's tax collectors eleven crowns and a mule. When he couldn't pay, the collectors took the mule, and then, because the collectors in those days were creative men, they took Serah's older brother for the army and her father's health for good measure, and her mother died the following winter of a fever that a single physician's visit might have broken, if physicians came to Millbrook, which they did not.

So when the wolf-hunters came recruiting—offering wage, rank, and immunity for her family's debts—Serah signed. Not out of loyalty. Out of arithmetic. Her father lived four more years in comfort he couldn't have bought otherwise. Her brother came home missing an arm and hating her, which she'd decided was fair.

That was the cage, and she'd built it herself, bar by bar, and called each bar *duty* so it wouldn't rattle so loud at night. Serve the crown that broke your family, protect the family it left, and tell yourself the work means something because the alternative is admitting you sold six years of your life for eleven crowns and a mule.

And through all of it, Jude. Jude writing twice a year from the capital, letters full of jokes and recipes and absolutely no information, because Jude understood exactly what Serah had become and loved her anyway, carefully, from a distance, the way you love a country you can't return to.

Until the letters stopped. Two years ago. No explanation. Just silence, and then, eventually, a rumor that Jude Villalobos had taken a position in the Lord Protector's own household, tasting for the usurper's table, and Serah had read that rumor forty times and understood it perfectly and hated how well she understood it: Jude had gotten close to the crown the only way a taster could—by putting poison between themselves and it, daily, forever.

Serving the same master she served. From the other side of the knife.

* * *

The summons came at dawn, and it was worse than she'd feared.

Not Dree. Not the war office. The Lord Protector's spymaster himself, Master Corvane, received her in a room with no windows and a table like a slab of night, and he did not offer her a chair.

"Vane." He pronounced it like a verdict. "Forty-one captures. Your record is the finest in the service."

"Thank you, my lord."

"Mm." He slid a folder across the black table. "Your report on the Ashwick courier. Read it again."

She read it again. Her own handwriting looked suddenly childish, the arranged furniture suddenly obvious, and she kept her face still through sheer animal discipline.

"Informative," Corvane said. "Thorough. And yet my other sources tell me the courier boarded a grain barge at the dyers' wharf an hour after you lost her, bound downriver, carrying guild correspondence sewn into her collar." He folded his hands. "I am not accusing you of anything, Vane. Accusation requires proof, and you are far too valuable for me to waste on a guess. Instead, I am going to give you the Ember Guild. All of it. Ledgers, routes, names. The Lord Protector wants the whole network by season's end, and I want you to bring it to him—because you are the best hunter I have, and because, frankly, I am curious whether the best hunter I have is hunting for us or against us."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, bells marked the hour, indifferent.

"You'll have full authority," Corvane continued. "Warrants, informants, the works. Start with the guild's money. Every network leaks somewhere, and money leaks fastest. Find me their books, Vane, and you'll find every name they've ever sheltered."

Every name. Serah stood very still, and beneath the stillness her heart was doing something complicated and terrible, because she knew—had always known, in the place where she kept the things she couldn't say—that somewhere in the capital, Jude Villalobos kept ledgers. Had probably always kept ledgers. Had once told her, on a river path, with flour on their face, that somebody dangerous had to keep the truth.

If the guild's books named its people, and Jude kept the guild's books—

"Find me their books," Corvane said again, softly, "and perhaps we'll both learn what you're made of."

Serah bowed. It was the deepest bow she could manage without her knees giving out, and she walked out of the windowless room into the grey morning light of a city that was about to become a hunting ground, carrying her new orders like a stone in her chest.

By season's end, she would either destroy the Ember Guild or betray the crown. There was no third door.

Except—she stopped in the courtyard, watching the frost melt off the training-yard wall, off the butcher's chart of her own captures—and thought of copper hair catching the sunset, of a cart of wet wool rolling toward freedom, of a honey bun and a boy who tasted truth.

There was one third door. It was just the kind that opened onto nothing at all.

She went to saddle her horse, and begin.
