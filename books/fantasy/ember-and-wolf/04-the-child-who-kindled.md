## 4. The Child Who Kindled

The safehouse above the chandler's shop smelled of tallow and burnt sugar, which Jude had always considered a fair trade for safety. Tallow meant the chandler was working; burnt sugar meant Wren was baking again, badly, with the fierce concentration of a child who had decided that if she could not yet control fire, she would at least feed it.

"You've put salt in the honey cakes," Jude said from the doorway.

"I put *hope* in the honey cakes."

"Hope is not a seasoning."

"It is when you're poor." Wren licked her thumb, considered it, and added more salt. She was eight years old, small for it, with singed eyebrows that never quite grew back and a way of looking at Jude that made them feel like a person worth looking at. "Ovid says I'll learn to hold it soon. The fire. He says it's like holding a bird — too tight and you crush it, too loose and it flies off and sets something on fire."

"Ovid says many things. Ovid once told me the free cities have honest tax collectors."

"What happened to him?"

"He was laughed out of the room by a man with no teeth."

Wren giggled, and the oven flared — just a breath of orange along its seams, gone before the chandler downstairs could notice. Jude felt their stomach do the thing it always did around the child: a slow, cold drop, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

Because they knew what Wren was. Not a kitchen-fire gift, not a candle-kindler like half the guild's children. The guildmaster had read the old records twice and gone gray both times. *Dormant ember wakes where she stands,* he'd written in cipher, and then, underneath, as if the ink itself were afraid: *armies.*

Somewhere in the capital tonight there were wolf-hunters who would burn down a city block for one line of that record. And here she was, salting honey cakes.

"Eat your failures," Jude said, taking one anyway. It was terrible. They ate two.

* * *

The ledgers lived under the floorboards beneath Jude's cot, wrapped in oilcloth, three volumes of names and routes and prices paid in silence. Jude checked them every night the way other people prayed — kneeling, hands flat on the boards, counting heartbeats until the fear became routine enough to sleep through.

Tonight the check took longer than usual, because tonight Jude could not stop thinking about the name they'd found two nights past, written in Ovid's careful hand in the margin of the Vane Street route:

*Serah Vane. Wolf-hunter. Do not approach. Debt of honor, unpaid.*

Jude had read it eleven times. Had touched the ink once, lightly, as though it might still be wet after four years. Serah. Who used to steal plums from the palace orchards and blame the crows. Who had kissed Jude behind the tannery and tasted, she'd said, like secrets. Who had joined the crown's hunters the same season Jude joined the guild, each of them choosing a side of the same wall without ever saying so aloud.

*Do not approach.* As if Jude needed telling. As if some part of them hadn't already been approaching for years, in dreams, in the space between tasting the usurper's breakfast and swallowing it.

A floorboard creaked below. Then another — wrong rhythm, too heavy for the chandler, who walked like an apology.

Jude went still. Listened. There it was, under the creaking: the particular silence of many people trying to be quiet. Boots. A whisper with iron in it. And beneath everything, faint as a held breath, the sound Jude had learned to dread above all others in four years of double living —

— a wolf-hunter's whistle, low, three notes, the signal for *found.*

"Wren." No time for gentle. Jude crossed the room in four strides and pulled the child up by both hands. "Back door. Now. Don't ask."

"But my cakes—"

"*Now.*"

They got as far as the landing. Below, the chandler's shop door burst inward with a sound like the world cracking its knuckles, and lantern-light climbed the stairs ahead of the boots, and Jude did the arithmetic they'd spent four years avoiding: back door blocked, window onto the awning, ledgers under the cot, Ovid asleep in the back room with his bad hip and worse stubbornness.

"Window," Jude said. "Awning. Roll when you land."

"I can't—"

"You can set an oven on fire with your *feelings*, Wren. You can roll."

She went through the window like a cat thrown from a lap — outraged, airborne, briefly magnificent. The awning caught her with a whump of canvas. Jude turned for the cot, for the floorboards, for three volumes of everyone Jude had ever kept alive—

And heard Ovid's voice from the back room, calm as a ledger line: "Gentlemen. If you're going to arrest me, at least let me put on boots. A man should be arrested standing up."

The choice arrived all at once, the way such things do — not as a thought but as a weight settling on the chest. Ledgers or Ovid. The names of forty gifted people scattered across the capital, or the crooked-backed old man who had taught Jude to keep accounts of human lives because, he said, *numbers are just love wearing work clothes.*

Boots on the stairs. Two flights' worth of seconds, maybe three.

Jude grabbed the ledgers.

Then stood there, hating themselves with a completeness that felt almost restful, and ran for the window.

* * *

They went over the rooftops because the streets belonged to wolves. Wren's hand in Jude's was slick and burning-hot, and Jude didn't flinch, because flinching teaches children their hands are wrong.

"Where are we going?" Wren whispered, scrambling across a gap between roofs, brave and terrified in equal measure.

"Away."

"That's not a place."

"It's the only place anyone's ever going."

Below them, the safehouse bloomed orange. Not Wren's doing — the hunters, torching it on principle, erasing the evidence of their own trespass. Jude watched the chandler's shop eat itself and thought of Ovid putting on his boots, standing straight so they couldn't take him bent, and made a promise to the smoke: *I'm coming back for you. I don't know how. But ledgers balance, old man. Every debt gets paid.*

They dropped into the tanner's quarter near midnight, into streets that smelled of Jude's childhood, and Wren finally stopped running and started shaking.

"I want Ovid," she said. Small voice. Smaller than eight years old.

"I know."

"They took him because of me. Because of what I am."

"No." Jude crouched to her height, took her face in both hands, and lied with the fluency of a professional. "They took him because cruel men are cruel, and because he refused to run fast enough. You are not the reason, Wren. You are the excuse. Those are different animals entirely, and one day I will teach you the difference, and you will use it to make powerful men feel very stupid."

Something flickered in her face — not a smile, but the ground where a smile might grow. "You talk funny when you're scared."

"I talk funny all the time. Fear just stops me editing."

She laughed — one wet, cracked note of it — and leaned her forehead against Jude's chest, and Jude held her and looked out over the dark city and understood, with the clarity of poison entering the blood, that they were now responsible for a child who could start an ancient war by sneezing, carrying three ledgers full of condemned names, hunted by wolves led by the only person Jude had ever loved.

It was, Jude reflected, going to be very difficult to keep this unremarkable.

"We need a plan," Wren said into their coat.

"Yes."

"Do you have one?"

"I have a direction and a strong opinion about survival. Historically, that's how most plans begin."

* * *

They holed up before dawn in the drying loft of the tannery, among hanging hides that swayed like patient ghosts. Jude counted the ledgers — three, intact, dry — and then counted Wren, who had fallen asleep sitting up mid-sentence, her head tipping against a salt-cured deer hide, one hand curled loosely open. Even asleep, faint heat came off her palms, like bread fresh from the oven. Like a hearth someone had forgotten to bank.

Jude sat watching her and did the accounting they were best at.

Assets: one child beyond price. Three ledgers worth forty lives. One taster's tongue, trained to detect poison, treachery, and — though Jude told no one this, had told no one ever — truth itself, which sat on the palate like copper and rain.

Debts: one guildmaster, held by the crown, certain to be questioned, certain to hold out for exactly as long as his pride lasted, which knowing Ovid was perhaps two days. One first love, hunting them. One usurper who wanted a spark to stamp out and would burn any amount of city to find it.

The trade was obvious. That was the horror of it. Any sensible smuggler would walk into the spymaster's office tomorrow and say: *the ledgers for the old man,* and walk out with Ovid and a pardon, and the forty names would go to the fire instead, and Wren — Wren would go somewhere Jude wasn't, because no sane crown lets a war-spark live.

Jude looked at the sleeping child. At the small heat of her hands. At the future folded up inside her like a letter no one had dared open for three hundred years.

"Every debt gets paid," Jude murmured. "But nobody said I get to choose the currency."

Above the tannery, far off across the rooftops, a whistle sounded — three notes, low, carried on the wind like a question.

*Found.*

Jude gathered the ledgers, woke the child gently, and took her hand.

"Time to be remarkable," they whispered. "Just this once. Just long enough to disappear."
