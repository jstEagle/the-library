## 5. Road of Salt and Ash

### Jude

The road out of the capital smelled like salt and old fires, which was fitting, because we were fleeing both.

Wren walked beside me with the solemn dignity of a child who had been told, three times in one morning, not to touch anything. The ledgers rode against my ribs in a satchel I'd taken to sleeping with like a lover. Behind us, the capital's smoke still smudged the horizon like a bruise that wouldn't heal.

"Are we almost there?" Wren asked. It was the fourth time they'd asked since dawn. I had begun to suspect it wasn't really a question about distance.

"The free cities are nine days' walk," I said. "Eight, if you stop asking."

"That's not how walking works."

"You'd be surprised." I handed them a strip of dried pear from my pack, because a hungry child is a chatty child, and a fed one will at least chew quietly for a while. "Eat."

They ate. Then, inevitably: "What's in the free cities?"

"People who don't burn things."

"I didn't *mean* to burn the safehouse."

"I know."

"It just got big."

"They usually do," I said, and Wren looked up at me with an expression of such naked relief that I had to look away down the road, at the salt flats shimmering, at nothing.

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a gifted child across hostile country: it is exactly like carrying a lit lantern through a hay barn, except the lantern talks, asks questions, and has opinions about dried pears.

We made good time until midday, when the road narrowed toward the first checkpoint — a toll gate strung across the pass with crown banners hanging off it like wet laundry. Two soldiers, maybe three. Bored ones. Bored soldiers are either the best kind or the worst kind, depending on how much they want to be interesting.

I crouched and took Wren by the shoulders. "Listen carefully. You're my nephew. Your name is Pip. You are extremely ordinary. Can you be extremely ordinary?"

"I'm very good at ordinary," Wren said, with the confidence of a child whose entire existence was currently a felony.

"Attaboy."

The soldier at the gate looked us over with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own name carved on a wall. "Business?"

"Salt merchant," I said, and here my gift did its quiet work. Every dish I've ever tasted tells me its truth — the arsenic in the wine, the lie in the broth, the confession folded into a sauce. Words, when people speak them at me, come with flavors too. This soldier's words tasted of boredom and boiled mutton and nothing hidden at all. Safe. Mostly.

"Papers?"

I produced papers. Ovid's forgers were artists; the guild had never smuggled anyone out on bad documents, because Ovid said a forgery should be able to survive dinner with its own mother.

The soldier stamped our passage. And that would have been the end of it, if the second soldier hadn't decided to be interesting.

"Nice weather for a walk," he said, crouching to Wren's level with a smile that tasted, when he spoke, of sour milk and something sharper underneath. Cruelty, maybe. Or just the itch men get when they smell fear. "You look nervous, little man."

"I'm not nervous," said Wren, who was, in fact, vibrating like a struck bell.

"Just tired," I said smoothly. "Long road."

"Mm." The soldier reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward something you already intend to take — and touched Wren's hair. "Pretty coloring. Unusual."

And Wren's eyes went gold.

I have seen ember-gift do many things. I have seen a woman knit fire into a wedding shawl. I have seen a boy light every candle in a cathedral with a sneeze. But I had never seen it the way it came off Wren then — not a flame but a *pressure*, heat rolling off them in a wave that made the salt dust dance, and the soldier snatched his hand back with a yelp, and the dry grass at the roadside went up all at once, and then the checkpoint banner, and then, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, the whole toll gate.

"Run," I told Wren, and we ran.

Behind us the soldiers shouted, and one of them — I'd remember this later, tasting the memory like ash — shouted a word I'd been dreading for days: *"Gifted! Gifted on the south road!"* And somewhere beyond the smoke, I swear I heard a horn. A hunting horn. Long, low, patient.

Wolf-hunters.

### Wren

Jude walks fast when they're scared. I know because I counted their steps while we hid in the ditch, and they were going *much* faster than yesterday, when they were only regular-scared instead of wolf-scared.

"We can't stop," Jude said. "Not till dark."

"My legs are tired."

"Your legs can file a complaint with the free cities."

I didn't know what a complaint was, but it sounded boring, so I kept walking. The ditch smelled like salt and like the burnt grass smell that comes off me sometimes, except this time it wasn't only mine. That was the worst part. The soldiers were going to tell everyone about the burning, and everyone was going to come looking, and it was going to be my fault, again, always, the way it was my fault at the safehouse and my fault before that too, back before I could even say long words.

"Hey." Jude crouched down in front of me, right there in the ditch, salt dust on their knees. They had gray in their hair and a face like a door that had been locked a long time but might open if you knocked nicely. "Look at me. What happened back there wasn't your fault."

"He touched my hair."

"Yes."

"I got angry."

"You got *scared*, and scared came out as fire. That's not a crime, Wren. That's just being eight."

"It burned a whole gate."

"One gate. There are more gates. Gates are replaceable." They tapped my chest, gently, right where my heart thumps. "You are not."

I thought about that for a long time, walking. The sun went orange. My legs stopped filing complaints.

At dark we found a hollow under a leaning rock, and Jude built no fire, which seemed rude, so I asked why, and Jude looked at me for a moment and then did something strange. They sat down across from me, cross-legged, like the teachers used to do at the safehouse before everything burned.

"Lesson one," they said.

"Of what?"

"Of holding it in. Not forever — holding it in forever would kill you, probably, and make a very large hole. But holding it when holding matters." They held out their hand, palm up. "Give me your anger. Just a little. Picture the soldier touching your hair, and give it to my hand."

"That's weird."

"All teaching is weird. Do it anyway."

So I pictured it. The sour-milk smile. The hand in my hair. The hot rising feeling, like soup boiling over. I pushed it at Jude's palm the way you blow on a spark.

Nothing happened. No fire. No gold. Just Jude sitting there, calm as bread.

"Huh," I said.

"Now picture it coming out your *feet*, into the ground. Slow. Like water draining."

I did. And the hot feeling in my chest — the one that lives there all the time now, ever since the safehouse, ever since I understood what I am — went down through my legs and into the dirt, and the dirt drank it, and I felt lighter. Hungry, actually. Really hungry.

"Good," Jude said softly. "That's the trick. Fire wants out the fastest door. Your job is to build it better doors."

"How do you know all this? You're not gifted."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Out in the dark, some night bird made a sound like a spoon tapping a pot.

"Aren't I?" they said.

* * *

### Jude

I hadn't meant to say it. Twenty years of tasting the crown's food, twenty years of swallowing secrets along with the poison, and I had kept my gift folded up so small even I sometimes forgot it was there. Telling Ovid had nearly killed me. Telling a child felt like handing a lit match to a library.

But Wren was looking at me with those ember-bright eyes, waiting, and lying to children is the one flavor I refuse.

"I taste truth," I said. "In food. In drink. In what people say. Every dish tells me what's really in it. Every sentence comes with a flavor underneath — the true one. It's why I survived twenty years as the usurper's taster. Poison announces itself to me like a guest wearing the wrong coat."

Wren considered this with enormous seriousness. "So you can't be lied to."

"Not successfully."

"So when you said my legs could file a complaint—"

"Boring truth. Yes."

"And when you said we'd be safe—"

I paused. "That one was half a lie. Nobody's safe. But I believe we'll make it, and belief is the truest thing I own."

Wren scooted closer, until their shoulder pressed against my arm, warm as a hearthstone. "Does it taste bad? Lies?"

"Lies taste like copper and old milk. Truth tastes different depending on who says it. Kindness tastes like honey. Fear tastes like tin." I hesitated. "Love tastes like bread, fresh from the oven. Which is inconvenient, because bread is also just bread."

"What do I taste like?"

"You," I said, "taste like the first day of summer. Hot stone and green growing things. Don't let it go to your head."

Too late. Wren grinned wide enough to show the gap where a tooth had recently surrendered, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the safehouse raid loosened by one careful degree. Found family, Ovid called it once, drunk on plum wine. The family you build out of strangers and emergencies. I'd laughed at him then. I was not laughing now. I was sitting in a ditch with a child made of wildfire leaning on my shoulder, and I would have burned down every checkpoint between here and the sea to keep that shoulder warm.

"Wren," I said. "One more lesson. Lesson two."

"Is it about doors?"

"It's about running. When I say *down*, you go down. When I say *run*, you run and don't look back, not for me, not for anything. Agreed?"

"Why wouldn't I look back for you?"

"Because looking back is how people get caught, and caught children get put in cages, and I have seen the cages." I kept my voice steady, which cost me. "Agreed?"

"...Agreed," Wren said, in the tone of a child agreeing to eat vegetables.

We slept in shifts. In the small hours I woke to find Wren watching the southern horizon, where a line of torches had appeared on the ridge behind us — too orderly for soldiers, too patient for panic. Moving the way wolves move. Not following our trail yet.

Fanning out across it.

"Wren," I whispered. "*Down.*"

They went down without arguing, which I chose to count as progress. I lay flat beside them in the cold salt grass, heart hammering, and watched the torches begin, unhurried, to close like a hand around the road ahead.

And at the head of them, riding a gray horse I would have known anywhere, even after ten years, even in the dark —

Serah Vane.
