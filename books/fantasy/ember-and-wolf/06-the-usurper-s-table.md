## 6. The Usurper's Table

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet her eyes.

Serah Vane read the wax seal twice—the usurper's own crest, not the spymaster's—and felt the particular cold that lived behind her ribs stir and stretch. In four years of service she had been summoned to the Iron Hall perhaps a dozen times. Twice had been promotions. Once had been a funeral. The rest had been men asking her to do things they preferred not to say aloud in writing.

She dressed in her wolf-hunter's grays, braided back the hair that Jude used to say was the color of a struck match—*you always did burn brightest before someone got hurt*—and buckled on the sword she had never once needed against the gifted. The gifted didn't fight. That was the whole tragedy of them. They ran, or they hid, or they wept, and then you put iron on their wrists and wrote down what they could do, as though a person could be filed like grain receipts.

Her report sat in her satchel. The true one and the false one were, by now, the same document; she had spent three nights sanding the truth out of it until only duty remained. *Smuggler escaped through the tannery district. Trail lost at the salt gate. No further leads.* A clean, competent failure. She had even smudged the ink convincingly.

"You look terrible," said Brannic, falling into step beside her in the courtyard. Her second-in-command was a mountain of a man with the soul of a worried aunt. "Did you sleep?"

"I slept."

"Liar. You've got the same face you had when Captain Dole died. The one where you're deciding whether to be sad now or later."

"Later," Serah said. "Possibly never. It's very efficient."

He snorted, but his eyes stayed on her a moment longer than jokes required. Then the horns sounded, and they walked into the Iron Hall together, and there was no more room for anything but what was coming.

* * *

The usurper, Lord Regent Cassian Vael, received them standing, which he believed made him look vigorous and instead made him look like a man who distrusted chairs. He was not tall. He was not short. He was the sort of person whose appearance slid off the memory like rain off oilcloth, which Serah had privately decided was its own kind of gift—the only kind he'd ever shown.

"The Ember Guild," Vael said, without preamble. He gestured, and a clerk unrolled a map of the capital studded with red pins. "Fourteen years this infection has been in my city. Fourteen years my predecessor called it *tolerance*. We are done tolerating." His finger traced the pins. "Your hunters have taken eleven of them in six days, Captain Vane. Eleven. I'm told that's a record."

"It's a slaughter," Serah said, and kept her voice level enough to pass for pride.

"It's a beginning." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Today it becomes a lesson. The executions will be public. Noon, the Meridian Square. All eleven. Let the city see what happens to people who hide impossible things under their floors."

Serah's mouth was dry. "Public execution tends to make martyrs, my lord. The free cities will—"

"The free cities will send a delegation next month to beg for peace, and I intend to arrive at that table smelling of smoke." He turned from the map at last, and something in his gaze sharpened, focused, became almost human. "There is one matter more. My spymaster tells me the guild kept ledgers. Names, routes, payments. Every gifted soul smuggled out in fourteen years, and every hand that helped them." He let that hang. "Find me those books, Captain. I am told they are worth more than the people in them."

*Worth more than the people in them.* Serah heard the sentence twice: once as an officer hearing orders, once as a girl who had once hidden a stolen peach in her sleeve for a boy who counted everything, because counting things was how he loved the world.

"Ledgers can be copied," she said carefully. "Burned. Scattered. Books are difficult quarry."

"Then it's fortunate," Vael said, "that I have a hunter who never loses her quarry." He looked at her a moment too long. "Or so your reputation claims. Your last report suggests otherwise."

The false report. Of course. She bowed. "The tannery district is a maze, my lord. I regret the failure."

"Mm." He waved her toward the door, then paused. "Captain. One more thing. Among the condemned today—" the clerk handed him a slip of paper, and he consulted it with theatrical care— "there is an old man. Ovid Marsh, guildmaster. Interrogation says he's the one who moved the ledgers. Wherever he sent them, whoever carried them—I want that thread pulled until it screams. Watch the crowd today. Guild sympathizers always come to watch their own die. Grief makes people stupid, and stupid people follow the books."

"Yes, my lord," said Serah Vane, who had already followed the books halfway across the capital in her imagination, and hated herself for how easily the lie assembled itself in her mouth. "I'll watch."

* * *

Meridian Square at noon smelled of hot stone and fear-sweat and the honey-cakes a vendor was still stubbornly selling near the fountain, because commerce is the last faith to abandon any gathering. Scaffolding had been raised around the executioner's block—or rather, around eleven blocks, because the usurper wanted efficiency as well as spectacle. Banners hung from the colonnades: the crown's wolf sigil, jaws open over a crossed sword and quill. Someone in the heraldry office had a sense of humor. Serah wished they didn't.

Brannic stationed the hunters along the square's edges. "Crowd's thick," he murmured. "Thicker than a tax day. Half these people aren't here for the show—they're here to mourn."

"Then watch for mourning," Serah said. "Anyone crying too hard. Anyone leaving early."

"And you?"

"I'll be watching the condemned."

They brought the prisoners out in chains at half past noon, and the square went quiet in that awful way crowds do—not silent, but hushed, a thousand breaths held at once. Serah stood on the scaffold steps with her hands clasped behind her back, parade-still, and made herself look at each face. This was the tax she paid for her survival: she looked at all of them. The young woman with ink-stained fingers—a scribe, gods help them all, gifted with perfect memory probably, executed for remembering. The twins who wouldn't stop holding hands. The old fisherman who kept trying to lead the others in some song the wind kept stealing.

And seventh in the line, leaning on a guard's arm because his legs had been broken during questioning and reset badly—

Ovid Marsh looked up at the sky, found it wanting, and said something to his guard that made the man blush. Even now. Even chained. The old guildmaster treated his own execution like a poorly organized dinner party, and Serah felt her throat close around a laugh that would have gotten her killed.

*Where are the ledgers, old man?* she thought at him. *What did you do with fourteen years of names?*

Ovid's gaze drifted across the square, unhurried, cataloguing—she could see him doing it, the merchant's habit of inventorying everything—and snagged, briefly, on a figure near the fountain. Nothing in his face changed. But his eyes came back to that spot once, twice, the way a man checks a purse he's just been told is light.

Serah followed the glance.

Near the fountain, hood up, standing with the terrible stillness of someone who has taught themselves to be furniture—stood a person of medium height and unremarkable build, the exact shape of nobody. And beneath the hood, when the sun caught the angle of the jaw, when the head turned a quarter-inch to track the guards' hands—

Jude.

Jude Villalobos, who had once fallen asleep counting the freckles on Serah's shoulder and woken up apologizing for miscounting. Jude, who tasted poison for a living and lied about nothing except everything. Jude, who was supposed to be three hundred miles away by now, or dead, or anything other than standing in the usurper's square watching the man who trained them go to the block.

The world narrowed to a point. Serah heard Vael's chamberlain reading charges—*consorting, smuggling, the crime of impossible gifts*—heard the crowd's low moan, heard Brannic beside her saying her name, and none of it arrived properly. Because she had finally understood, all in one cold swallow, what the usurper had been circling for weeks without naming.

It was never the smugglers. Smugglers were vermin; you stepped on them and forgot. It was the ledgers. Fourteen years of names—every gifted child, every hiding family, every sympathetic clerk and guard and *taster* written down in a careful hand. A book like that wasn't evidence. It was a harvest list. And somewhere in that book, in that same careful hand, was a name Serah had spent four years making sure no one ever connected to her: the name of a girl from the river district who had let one smuggler go through sheer cowardice, and then another through love, and had falsified reports until the falseness was load-bearing.

*Worth more than the people in them,* Vael had said. He was wrong, and he was right. The book was worth more than the people in it—to him, because it would give him all of them. And to her, because it held her.

On the scaffold, the drums began.

"Noon bell first," the chamberlain announced. "By custom."

The noon bell. Which meant, by the same custom, a final interval in which petitions could be shouted, pardons begged, miracles attempted. Serah's eyes went from Ovid to the hooded figure by the fountain, and she saw—she would swear on her mother's grave she saw—Jude's hand move inside the cloak, small and deliberate, the way it used to move across a page. Counting. Timing. *Eleven blocks, twelve guards, one bell.*

"Captain?" Brannic said quietly. "You've gone white as salt. What do you see?"

What did she see. She saw her whole life balanced on the head of a pin: the crown that fed her, the oath that caged her, the love she'd buried so deep she'd convinced herself it was compost. She saw that loyalty was a house she'd built brick by brick over four years, and that Jude Villalobos had just walked through the wall without touching it.

"I see," Serah said, drawing her sword—not toward the scaffold, but resting naked and bright against her shoulder, a hunter's signal, *eyes on me*— "a crowd that's about to become a problem. Positions. Now."

The noon bell began to swing.

And by the fountain, the hooded figure took one step toward the scaffold, and Ovid Marsh, seven blocks from death, started laughing.
