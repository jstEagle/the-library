## 7. A Bargain in Iron

The spymaster's summons came the way poison comes: sweet at first, then too late to spit out.

It arrived as a boy with a baker's tray and a note folded inside a honey roll, which was either a joke or a threat. *The Salt Cellar. Midnight. Come alone. Bring what we both know you have.* No signature, but the wax seal pressed into the paper bore a wolf's head with its jaws closed — the mark of Master Corvane, the usurper's spymaster, a man who collected secrets the way other men collected debts.

Jude read it twice, then ate the honey roll, because wasting good pastry felt like one more small treason against a week that had already taken everything.

Ovid was in a cell beneath the Iron Keep. Wren was asleep in the hayloft above them, curled around a stub of candle like a dragon around its hoard, and every hour Jude delayed was an hour closer to the child's name climbing some new list.

So: midnight. The Salt Cellar.

* * *

The Salt Cellar was not a cellar and had never sold salt. It was a gambling den built into the ribs of an old grain barge, moored where the canal met the river, and it smelled of tallow smoke, wet rope, and the particular desperation of men betting money they'd already lost. Jude arrived early, ordered nothing, and sat with their back to the wall and their hands visible, because a taster learns that the only thing more dangerous than hidden weapons is hidden hands.

Corvane came at midnight exactly, which told Jude he'd been waiting nearby since dusk. He was smaller than his reputation — a neat, grey man with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of someone who had read other people's letters for so long he'd forgotten how to be surprised by anything.

"Villalobos." He sat without being asked. "You look terrible."

"I've been traveling."

"You've been running." He laced his fingers on the table. "Let's not season this. You have the Ember Guild's ledgers. Every safehouse, every route, every gifted soul smuggled out of my capital in the last six years. And I have Ovid Marsh, who has been remarkably unhelpful, in the way old men are remarkably unhelpful when they've decided to die politely."

"He's not dying politely," Jude said. "He's being rude. There's a difference. Ask anyone who's shared a meal with him."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way a fish ghosts under ice. "Here is my offer, since His Radiance prefers I make offers before I make examples. The ledgers, complete, and your testimony about how they were kept. In exchange, the old man walks free, and you receive a pardon under seal."

"And the child?"

"What child?"

And there it was — the trap lying flat in the open, so obvious it was almost insulting. Corvane knew about Wren. Of course he did. A checkpoint burned to glass on the salt road; hunters don't need ledgers to do that arithmetic. The question wasn't whether he knew. The question was whether he knew *where*, and everything depended on the answer.

"There's no child," Jude said, and their tongue — their treacherous, trained, truth-tasting tongue — lay perfectly still in their mouth, because this was not a question, it was a statement, and statements could be lied into all day long.

"No child," Corvane repeated mildly. "Interesting. Because my hunters report ash on the wind and a burned checkpoint, and no bodies. And because Serah Vane's last three reports contain gaps wide enough to drive a cart through." He leaned forward. "You should know something about Serah Vane, Villalobos. She is very good at her job. That is precisely why I know when she isn't doing it."

Jude kept their face still. Somewhere behind their ribs, an old wound opened and quietly began to bleed.

"The ledgers," Corvane said, "for the old man. Tomorrow night, the ferry landing at Tanners' Reach. Bring the books. Leave the sentimentality at home." He rose, straightened his coat, and paused. "Oh — and Villalobos. If the child surfaces anywhere near this transaction, the bargain dies, and I stop making offers. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," said Jude, who understood him better than he knew. "One condition of mine."

Corvane raised an eyebrow.

"Ovid eats well between now and then. I'll prepare the food myself. You can watch me taste it."

"Afraid we'll poison him?"

"Afraid you'll do it badly," Jude said. "I have standards."

* * *

Wren was awake when Jude got back, sitting cross-legged on the hay with the candle stub guttering, watching the flame with the fierce concentration of a child doing arithmetic with fire.

"It keeps going out," Wren said accusingly.

"Candles do that. It's their whole personality."

"Not when I *ask* them." The flame leaned toward the child like a dog hearing its name, then shrank guiltily. Wren looked up, and in the dark their eyes held a spark that had nothing to do with the wick. "Did you sell us?"

Jue sat down in the straw. "No."

"To the wolf-man?"

"To the spider-man, actually. Different animal entirely." They pulled off their boots, wincing. "I made a trade. Ovid for the books. You stay hidden until it's done, and then we go to the free cities, and you learn to read, and set fire to nothing larger than a birthday candle for at least a year."

Wren considered this. "What if it's a really big cake?"

"We'll negotiate." Jude blew out the candle. In the dark, the child's warmth found their side like a small furnace, and Jude lay awake listening to the river and thinking about tomorrow, and about Serah Vane's name in a ledger written in their own careful hand, and about how every road out of this city seemed to run through the past.

* * *

Tanners' Reach at night was all rot and lamplight — tannery vats breathing their sour stink over the water, the ferry landing slick with lime, two lanterns hung on the posts like patient eyes. Jude came down the plank road with the ledgers in a oilcloth satchel against their chest, feeling every step of the weight of six years of names.

Corvane waited on the landing with four guards and a covered cart. Beside the cart stood Ovid — thinner, greyer, one eye swollen shut, but upright, and when he saw Jude he rolled the good eye with magnificent contempt.

"They served me *cabbage*, Jude. Cabbage. I want it noted in whatever book you're writing that I suffered."

"Noted," Jude said, throat tight. "In the margins."

"Satchel," said Corvane.

Jude held it a moment longer than necessary. Six years of names. Routes. Safehouses. Serah's name. Their own hand on every page. Then they tossed it, and a guard caught it, and Corvane opened it right there under the lantern, flipping pages with those ink-stained fingers while Ovid limped across the planks to Jude's side.

"Ledgers confirmed," Corvane said at last, and nodded, and the guards moved to the cart—

—and the night broke open.

Lanterns flared along the bank — not the amber of city watch but the cold white of hunter-lamps, and figures dropped from the warehouse roof with the soundless grace of people who had done this a hundred times. Wolf-hunters. Six of them, ringed around the landing, and at their center, sword drawn but pointed at the ground, stood Serah Vane.

"Master Corvane," she said. "By order of the Radiant Throne, this exchange is concluded. Everyone here is under arrest for trafficking in the Gifted — including, I'm afraid, the Throne's own spymaster, whose private purchase of guild records constitutes—" she glanced at a paper in her free hand— "considerable embarrassment to certain parties at court."

Corvane went very still. "Serah. These orders are forged."

"Yes," Serah agreed pleasantly. "I forged them. It took me four hours. I'm rather proud of the seal."

Jude's heart was doing something complicated and painful. "Serah—"

"Don't," she said, without looking at them. "Not yet. Let me finish the crime first."

Corvane's guards reached for steel. What happened next took eleven seconds and Jude would remember all of it: Ovid, bless his ancient treacherous heart, sweeping a tannery hook off its bracket and laying out the nearest guard with the economy of a man who had been brawling since before the usurper learned to walk; the guards faltering; and Serah stepping forward, sword finally rising, moving through the two nearest hunters like water finding its level — disarming, not cutting, always disarming, her blade flat and her eyes apologetic.

Then it was Corvane alone on the landing, and Serah standing over him, and she took the satchel of ledgers from the frozen guard's grip and held it out — not to Corvane, not to her hunters, but to Jude.

"Your books," she said.

Jude didn't take them. "Those orders will hang you."

"They were going to hang me anyway. Corvane's had questions about my reports for a month. I simply decided to answer them early." She drew a folded paper from her coat — her true orders, the ones she'd been carrying all night, the ones commanding her to secure the ledgers and the child at any cost — and held it to the nearest lantern.

The paper caught, flared, and was gone. Ash drifted out over the black water.

"Serah Vane," Corvane said from the ground, and his voice had gone soft and dreadful, "you are finished. When the Throne hears—"

"When the Throne hears," Serah said, crouching to his level, "it will hear that its spymaster bought stolen guild records for reasons I intend to discover, loudly, in front of witnesses. You taught me that, sir. Secrets are leverage. Consider yourself leveraged." She stood. "Hunters! We're leaving. Anyone who wishes to report me may do so at the next garrison. Anyone who wishes to keep their pension will develop a sudden memory problem."

Six hunters looked at each other. Five of them had followed her through burning checkpoints and rooftop chases and worse, and five of them, after a long moment, mounted up and said nothing at all.

Ovid sidled up to Jude, watching Serah douse the lanterns. "That," he murmured, "is the woman whose name is in your ledger under 'do not involve.'"

"She was never supposed to be involved."

"Mm. And yet." He patted Jude's shoulder with a hand like a dried riverbed. "Love, child, is the one smuggling operation nobody audits."

They rode out of Tanners' Reach with the ledgers back against Jude's chest and Serah's horse close enough that their stirrups nearly touched, and neither of them spoke until the tanneries were far behind and the road bent toward the hills and the free cities beyond.

"You knew I'd come," Jude finally said. "The forged orders. You were waiting for me to make the trade so you could—what? Arrest everyone? Take the books yourself?"

"I was waiting," Serah said, eyes on the dark road, "to see what you'd choose. You gave up six years of work — names, routes, everything — for one grumpy old man. That told me everything I needed to know about what's in the rest of you." A pause. The horses' breath steamed. "I've spent nine years choosing the crown over you, Jude. Tonight I got tired of losing."

Jude's chest ached in a way no poison had ever managed. "Wren's waiting at the millhouse," they heard themselves say. "Half a mile ahead. I hid them there before the meeting — Corvane asked about a child, and I lied, and I've been sick about it since sundown."

Serah laughed — short, startled, real. "You lied to the spymaster's face."

"I'm a professional."

Ahead, the millhouse window flickered — once, twice — a small light blinking in the dark like a signal, or a greeting, or a warning. Serah's hand went to her sword. Jude squinted, and their stomach dropped, because that was not Wren's candle rhythm.

That was the signal Ovid had taught them both, years ago, for one word only:

*hunters.*
