## 8. The Hunt Turns

**Serah**

The road to the free cities was three days of salt marsh and burned checkpoint, and Serah spent all of it learning a new arithmetic: one horse, two fugitives, one child who occasionally glowed like a struck flint, and no possible way to divide rations fairly when Jude kept giving theirs away.

"You're supposed to be the practical one," she said on the second night, watching Jude hand Wren the last of the dried plums with the solemnity of a man surrendering a kingdom.

"I'm the taster," Jude said. "My whole profession is knowing what's worth swallowing."

"That's not what that means."

"It's exactly what that means. You've just never listened."

Wren, wrapped in Serah's cloak with only her singed eyebrows visible, giggled into her knees. The fire between them was small and ordinary — Wren had learned to keep it that way, mostly — and its light made Jude's face look younger than it had any right to, given everything they'd carried out of the capital in a satchel against their ribs.

Serah should have been watching the dark for wolf-hunters. Instead she was watching Jude, and hating how easy it still was.

* * *

**Jude**

The free cities announced themselves by smell before sight: brine and tar and woodsmoke that belonged to hearths instead of pyres. Jude had smuggled people toward that smell for eleven years and had never once come at it as cargo themselves. It was humbling. It was also, they decided somewhere past the last mile-marker, deeply unfair that refugees got no bread on arrival.

The gates of Vell took them without ceremony — a bored guard, a stamp of chalk on Wren's hand, a shrug at "papers" that were actually three pages of Ovid's ledger copied in Jude's smallest handwriting. Freedom, it turned out, smelled like fish.

They found the Ember Guild's remnant in a candlemaker's cellar beneath the Saltmarket: six frightened guildhands, a map of the coast, and a council summons already waiting, because word traveled faster than horses when the word was *the usurper wants the child*.

"They're meeting tonight," said Maren, the eldest, wringing her wax-stained hands. "The Council of Nine. They know you're coming, taster. They know what you carry."

Jude patted the satchel. Eleven years of names, routes, payments, bribes — and one entry older than all the rest, written in a younger hand, describing a taste Jude had never forgotten: bitter almond under honey wine, the old king's final cup, and the silence Jude had sold to keep their own skin.

"They know," Jude said. "That's the problem."

* * *

**Serah**

The Council of Nine met in a round room with nine chairs and, apparently, no spines among them.

Serah stood at the back with her hunter's coat still smelling of smoke, arms crossed, while the councilors debated trading an eight-year-old girl to a usurper in the measured tones of men discussing grain tariffs. Chancellor Ivo — soft-bellied, silver-ringed, the kind of man who'd never tasted anything he didn't order — called it "a pragmatic peace."

"The child is a weapon," he said. "The usurper fears weapons. Return the weapon, and his armies stop at the salt line. Thousands live."

"She's a *child*," Serah said.

Ivo smiled at her the way powerful men smile at inconvenient women. "And you are a defector, Serah Vane, formerly of the crown's hunt. Forgive us if we weigh your convictions lightly."

Jude stepped forward from the shadows where they'd been lurking with the professional patience of someone used to waiting for poison to work. "Then don't weigh my convictions. Weigh my ledger."

* * *

**Jude**

Here is what nobody tells you about confession: it is much easier to rehearse than to perform.

Jude had rehearsed this speech across three days of marsh, in the dark, while Wren slept against Serah's shoulder. It had been eloquent. It had been dignified. What came out instead, standing before nine chairs in a round room, was:

"I tasted it. The old king's cup. Bitter almond under the honey. I knew, and I wrote it down, and then I hid the page for six years because telling the truth would have killed me faster than the poison would have." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they let it. "Your peace rests on a regicide. Your pragmatism is built on my cowardice. Here — read it yourself."

They set the ledger page on the council table, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone realizes the floor is thinner than they thought.

Chancellor Ivo picked up the page. Read it. Set it down with fingers that were, Jude noted with distant satisfaction, not entirely steady.

"This proves nothing," he said. "A taster's suspicion. Ink and grievance."

"It proves the vintage," Jude said. "It proves the cup. It proves the apothecary's mark on the poison vial, which I copied, which matches the seal on your own trade agreements with the capital. I have spent my life tasting truth, Chancellor. Do not insult both of us by pretending you can't smell it too."

* * *

**Serah**

She watched Jude stand there — thin, exhausted, hands trembling slightly around the strap of a satchel that had cost them everything — and felt something in her chest turn over like a key.

Six years. Six years Jude had carried that page. Six years of tasting the usurper's breakfast, smiling, bowing, swallowing the knowledge of a murdered king along with the almond. And now they'd laid it out before strangers because a child needed protecting more than Jude needed safety.

Serah had burned her orders in a stable yard to prove she loved this person. Jude had just done something harder: they'd proven it in front of witnesses, with no fire at all.

"I can verify the handwriting," Serah heard herself say. Every head turned. She walked forward anyway. "I served the crown's hunt for nine years. I processed the old king's household after his death. The apothecary's mark on this page matches the vial inventory I personally catalogued — and which was subsequently sealed by order of the man who now calls himself king." She looked at Ivo. "Two witnesses, Chancellor. One of them me. Unless you'd like to explain to the free cities why a known traitor's testimony counts for less than a trade agreement."

Ivo's smile had developed cracks. "You would have us go to war over ink."

"I would have you stop calling it peace," Serah said, "when what you mean is ransom."

* * *

**Jude**

The council did not decide anything that night, because councils never do; they decided to "deliberate," which Jude understood, from long experience with powerful tables, to mean *we will do the cowardly thing unless publicly prevented.*

So Jude did what they'd always done best. They worked the room afterward.

Maren got the ledger copies distributed to three printing houses before dawn. Wren — who had followed the whole proceeding with the unnerving attentiveness of a child who'd watched adults fail her before — sat on the cellar steps eating bread and asked, "Did it help? Saying it out loud?"

Jude sat down beside her. "No. Yes. Both. Truth is like medicine, little ember. It tastes awful, and half the time it doesn't work, and you take it anyway because the alternative is worse."

"Were you scared? In there?"

"Terrified."

Wren considered this with great seriousness. "But you did it anyway."

"That's the entire job description of being a person, as far as I can tell."

Wren leaned her small, warm weight against Jude's side, and Jude thought: *this is why we burn the ledgers if we must. This, exactly this.* Some entries were worth more than the book.

* * *

**Serah**

The answer came at dawn, and it came the way bad news always did in Serah's experience: wearing good manners.

Chancellor Ivo sent a runner to the candlemaker's cellar with a letter on cream paper. *The Council has deliberated. In the interest of regional stability, the Council will open negotiations with the capital regarding the custody of the gifted child, with assurances of humane treatment—*

"Humane treatment," Serah read aloud, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "He means a cell. He means a leash. He means handing a little girl to the man who murdered his own king, and calling it diplomacy."

Jude was already pulling on their coat. "How long do we have?"

"Negotiations take weeks. But Ivo will want to demonstrate good faith early. A gesture." Serah's stomach dropped through the floor. "Jude. A gesture."

They looked at each other, and the same thought arrived in both faces at once, and behind them on the steps Wren looked up from her bread and asked, with terrible eight-year-old directness:

"What's a gesture?"

* * *

**Jude**

By noon, the cellar was empty, the printing houses were warned, and Jude stood in the Saltmarket crowd with a satchel full of copies and a plan so reckless that even Ovid — currently rotting in a capital dungeon — would have called it excessive.

If the council wouldn't publish the truth, the city would. If the law wouldn't protect Wren, then the law could be made to blush. Jude had spent a lifetime being unremarkable in service of other people's survival. It was, perhaps, time to be remarkable in service of their own.

"Tell me again why we're doing it this way?" Serah murmured beside him, hood up, hand near her sword.

"Because the council meets at midday," Jude said, "and because every printer in Vell owes the Guild a favor, and because by the time Ivo finishes his opening remarks, every wall in this city will be papered with the name of the man who poisoned a king — and the name of the taster who stayed silent." He exhaled. "Both names, Serah. Mine first. Guilt works better when it's honest."

Her hand found his in the crowd, brief and fierce. "And if it doesn't work?"

"Then we run again. Third time's traditional."

Above the market bells began to ring the hour, and down the boulevard, the doors of the Council Hall opened onto the steps where Chancellor Ivo stood to address his city — just as the first sheets of paper went up on the walls behind him, ink still wet, and the crowd began, very quietly, to read.
