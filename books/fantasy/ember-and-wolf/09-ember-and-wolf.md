## 9. Ember and Wolf

**Jude**

The peace parley was held in the Salt Court, a hall built by people who had believed, once, that treaties could be made beautiful. Banners of the free cities hung along one wall; the usurper's black-and-gold hung along the other; and between them stood a long table of pale wood that had never yet hosted an agreement anyone kept.

Jude sat at it because no one could think of a reason to make them leave. They were, technically, evidence.

Wren sat beside them, scrubbed and solemn in borrowed clothes, swinging legs that didn't reach the floor. The child had been told to stay quiet. Wren had interpreted this as *stay quiet unless something is extremely important*, which was, Jude reflected, exactly how they themselves had survived twenty years at court.

The usurper arrived last, as usurpers do. He was smaller than his statues. He wore poison-gray silk, and when he passed Jude he paused, just slightly, the way a man pauses near a smell he can't place.

"Taster," he said. "You're still alive."

"Stubborn constitution," Jude said pleasantly. "It's in my file."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the particular discomfort of a man who has ordered many files burned and is never certain he got them all. He took his seat. His wolf-hunters lined the walls, gray cloaks, gray faces, and Serah stood among them with her hands folded like a woman attending her own funeral.

The council of the free cities opened with pleasantries. The usurper let them have four minutes of pleasantries before he set both hands flat on the table and said:

"Give me the child."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere a banner rope tapped against stone.

Wren stopped swinging their legs.

"The Accord—" began Councilor Ives.

"There will be no Accord." The usurper's voice was almost gentle, which was how you knew. "There will be a border, and a tax, and your cities may keep their little freedoms so long as every gifted soul within them is registered. Registered, or rendered. And this one—" he looked at Wren the way men look at weapons, not children "—this one comes to me now, or my army comes to all of you by morning."

Jude's mouth tasted of copper. Not fear. Memory. They knew this flavor. They had tasted it once, years ago, in a cup of spiced wine meant for the old king, and had said nothing, and had carried the silence ever since like a stone swallowed whole.

They looked at Serah. Serah was already looking at them.

*Well,* Jude thought. *Here we are.*

* * *

**Serah**

Serah had imagined this moment for weeks — the parley, the demand, the choice — and in every imagining she had drawn her sword and said something magnificent. In practice, her hand simply moved to her hilt and stayed there, trembling, while eleven other hunters did not move at all, because they were watching her, waiting to see what a Vane did.

The council refused. Of course they refused. Ives said something about law and shelter and the word *never*, delivered with the mild firmness of a grandmother refusing to sell a house. Serah loved the free cities a little, in that moment, from across a war.

The usurper smiled. That was the worst part. He had come expecting refusal; refusal was the script. He gestured, and horns sounded outside, and through the high windows Serah saw the front ranks of his army wheel into view on the salt plain — ten thousand men arranged in the shape of an argument no one could answer.

"And since we cannot agree," the usurper said, "we will conclude with a demonstration. Bring the cup."

Two soldiers brought it forward: a small silver cup on a small black cushion, and beside it a vial of something green-gold that caught the light like trapped summer. Serah knew the vial. Every hunter knew the vial. Emberbane — distilled grief, the only poison that killed a gifted body faster than its own fire could heal it.

"You cannot," said Ives.

"I can," said the usurper, "and I will, here, where your councils can watch. Let them see what rebellion costs. The child drinks. The child dies. And then perhaps we discuss registration again, with everyone in a more reasonable temper."

They came for Wren.

And Wren — eight years old, three days unwashed, brave in the way that broke Serah's heart sideways — stood up on the chair and said, "I'm not going to drink it, but thank you," which was somehow worse than screaming.

Serah stepped out of the line.

She heard her own boots on the stone and thought, distantly, *so this is what it sounds like.* Twenty years of orders, and it had taken her until now to learn the sound of disobeying them. She put herself between the soldiers and the chair.

"Serah Vane." The usurper's voice dropped its gentleness. "Stand down or stand condemned."

"My lord," Serah said, "I've executed forty-one people on your word. I checked each order twice. This one I've checked three times, and it's wrong, and I find I can't carry it, and I'd rather be wrong with them than right with you."

Silence. Then, softly, from the end of the table, Jude said: "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me, and I once watched you fight a smuggler in the rain."

"Not now, Jude."

"It's been eleven years of *not now*, Serah."

* * *

**Jude**

The soldiers hesitated — wolves without a hand to guide them, because their captain had just become a different kind of animal entirely — and in that hesitation Jude did the thing they had spent a lifetime training their body to survive.

They picked up the cup.

"Actually," Jude said, "I'll drink it."

Every head turned. The usurper's brow creased in genuine puzzlement, which Jude found deeply satisfying after everything.

"You're not the condemned."

"No, but I am the taster." Jude held the cup up to the light, swirled it, and gave the hall their professional smile — the one that had reassured two kings and one usurper through ten thousand suspicious meals. "And it occurs to me that none of you know what's in this cup. You take his word. I don't take anyone's word about anything anymore. So I'll taste it, as is my office, and report."

"That's absurd—"

"It's tradition," Jude said. "You're very fond of tradition. It's how you justify the army."

And they drank.

Emberbane hit like winter. Jude had spent twenty years building immunity in careful, measured doses — a sip here, a crumb there, cataloguing every toxin the crown favored — but nothing had prepared them for this, and nothing could have. Fire went down their throat and bloomed outward, and their knees buckled against the table, and somewhere very far away Wren was screaming their name.

But — and this was the secret, the ledger written in their own blood — they did not die. Their body fought. Slowly, insultingly slowly, the way it always had, healing the burns even as the poison made them. They had bought minutes. Maybe five. Maybe less.

Five minutes was a great deal, if you spent it well.

Jude gripped the table, white-knuckled, and spoke through teeth that would not quite obey. "My lords and ladies of the free cities. My lords and ladies of the crown. I am Jude Villalobos, taster to the throne, and I have tasted every meal this man has eaten for three years." A breath. Copper and ash. "Three years ago, in the west wing, I tasted a cup of spiced wine meant for King Aldous. It contained emberbane. I reported nothing. I was afraid. I have been afraid every day since, and I am telling you now, dying if necessary, so that my silence dies with me: *this man poisoned the king.*"

The hall detonated. Councilors on their feet. Crown officers reaching for swords and finding, everywhere they reached, free-city guards already closer. The usurper rose, shouting for his hunters —

And his hunters did not come.

* * *

**Serah**

Because Serah had spent the last minute doing the second bravest thing of her life, which was walking down the line of gray cloaks and quietly saying, one by one, six words apiece: "Your family's name is in the ledgers."

She had read those ledgers. She knew which names. Half the hunters had sisters, sons, cousins among the gifted — smuggled out, hidden, alive, because the Ember Guild had saved them while the crown paid the hunters to pretend otherwise. She had known it for days and said nothing, hoarding it like a blade. Now she spent it all at once.

Hunter Brannoc lowered his crossbow. Hunter Ashe turned to face the throne. One by one, the wolves of the crown stopped being the crown's.

The usurper looked at his empty wall of protection and, for the first time, looked small enough to be a statue of himself.

Then the light changed.

* * *

**Jude**

It came from Wren.

Not fire — Jude had braced for fire, had half-risen despite the poison to shield the child from their own gift. But what woke in Wren was older than fire. The air above the Salt Court filled with light, and in the light, images: the old king's cup, the green-gold vial, the usurper's hand pouring. The guild safehouse burning. Ovid in chains, calm as a library. The hunt lists. The graves. Everything the ledgers held, everything Jude had carried, projected vast and undeniable onto the walls and the sky itself, so that ten thousand soldiers on the salt plain stopped mid-step and looked up and *saw*.

Wren's gift did not wake armies. It woke witnesses.

"That's my boy," Jude whispered, and then corrected, "my *child*," because accuracy mattered even while dying.

The truth poured over the hall like weather. And under it, Serah crossed to Jude, caught them as their legs finally gave, and lowered them gently to the cold stone floor.

"You absolute idiot," she said, cradling their head. "You beautiful, stubborn, impossible idiot."

"Took you eleven years," Jude managed, "to say something romantic back."

Above them, the light held. Below them, the usurper's own council closed around him like a book shutting. And Jude, tasting blood and ash and — faintly, unmistakably — the clean green flavor of morning, felt the poison lose its argument, one slow heartbeat at a time.

"Hold on," Serah said. "Hold on, Jude. Stay with me."

Jude smiled, and let go — not of life, not yet, but of the weight. The ledgers were spoken. The names were seen. Whatever came next, they had, for the first time in twenty years, nothing left to hide.

The last thing they heard, sinking under, was Wren's voice, fierce and bright above the fading light:

"You tell them you're staying. You *tell* them."
