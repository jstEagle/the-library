## 10. A Law Written in Ash

I woke to the smell of bread, which was how I knew I wasn't dead. The dead, in my experience—and my experience of the dead was professional—did not get bread.

The room was small and clean and full of morning light, the kind of light you only got in the free cities, where nobody had ever thought to tax a window. My hands lay on top of the blanket like two pale strangers who had wandered in off the road. They shook when I lifted them. Everything about me had been diminished: my strength, my appetite, the fine-tuned instrument of my tongue that had spent fifteen years cataloguing every poison the crown could brew. I put my tongue against the roof of my mouth and tasted nothing but sleep and salt. It was, I decided, the closest thing to peace I would ever know.

"You're supposed to be resting," said Wren, from the doorway, with the authority of an eight-year-old who had recently learned that adults could be managed.

"I am resting."

"You're sitting up and making faces at your hands."

"That's advanced resting." I patted the bed beside me. "Come here. Tell me what day it is."

"Day nine," she said, climbing up with the careful gravity of someone who had been told not to jostle me and intended to obey for exactly four more seconds. "Serah says if you slept past noon again she was going to pinch you. Ovid says you're too stubborn to die of anything as honest as poison. And the council signed it yesterday. The Accord. They read the whole thing out loud in the square and everybody cheered except one man who fell asleep standing up, which Serah said was fair."

"The whole thing?"

"All of it. Even the boring parts. There are *lots* of boring parts. Ovid says laws are mostly boring on purpose so that kings can't sneak things into them." She settled against my shoulder, careful of my ribs, and went quiet in the way she did when something was working behind her eyes. "It has your name in it."

"It does not."

"It does. Third paragraph. *The Villalobos Provision.*" She pronounced it with tremendous care, having clearly rehearsed. "Ovid says they argued about whether to name it after you while you were still unconscious, because you'd have argued back."

Ovid knew me well. I had spent my whole life being unremarkable on purpose; there was something obscene about becoming a paragraph. But I looked down at Wren's dark head against my shoulder, at the small scar on her wrist where the executioner's cup had been meant to touch her skin instead of mine, and I found I didn't have the strength to be embarrassed. Perhaps I never would again. Diminished men were allowed their names in paragraphs.

"What else happened while I was sleeping?" I asked.

"Wren," came Serah's voice from the hall, "you were going to let him rest."

"He is resting. He's talking to me."

"That's not rest, that's interrogation."

Wren slid off the bed with the swift dignity of the guilty and fled past Serah, who filled the doorway the way she always had—as though doors had been invented specifically to frame her. Her hair was shorter than it had been in the capital. Her wolf-hunter's blacks were gone, traded for the plain grey of the free cities, and she wore them like a woman trying on a new country. In her hand she carried a tray: bread, soft cheese, a cup of something steaming.

She set it down and looked at it, and then at me, and I watched her do the thing she had done every day for nine days—hesitate over the cup.

"It's just tea," I said gently.

"I know what's in it. I watched them make it."

"Then drink half of mine first. For tradition's sake."

Her mouth twitched. That was our old joke, from another life, another crown—the taster and the soldier, sharing stolen figs in a palace garden, Jude tasting everything first because it was funny, before it became a job, before it became a cage. She sat on the edge of the bed, took the cup, drank, and handed it back, and we pretended this was a normal thing that normal people did, and it almost was.

"The council wants you at the reading of the final clause," she said. "Next week. They want you standing."

"They can have me leaning dramatically."

"Jude."

"Serah." I set the tea down and took her hand. It was calloused and warm and it did not pull away, which after everything felt like a miracle performed daily by no one. "I'm alive. Badly, but genuinely. You don't have to check my pulse every time you pass the door."

"I'm not checking your pulse."

"You've checked it eleven times since I woke up."

"Twelve," she admitted. "You were asleep for the twelfth."

* * *

The poison had a name, it turned out. Of course it did; everything in the usurper's kitchens had a name, a ledger line, a price. *Greyveil*, the free cities' physicians called it, when I described the taste that had nearly ended me—a taste I now remembered only as cold, like swallowing winter. Slow enough to look like grief. Quiet enough to bury a child without scandal. The same family of draughts, the physicians confirmed, that had killed the old king two winters past, the taste I had known on my tongue and swallowed my silence along with it.

That silence had bought me immunity, once. It had cost me everything else. Now the ledgers—my ledgers, carried across the salt roads in a satchel that had survived fire, wolves, and one very determined checkpoint—lay open on the council's table, and every page of my careful double-entry bookkeeping stood testimony. What the crown bought. Who it poisoned. What it paid for silence, and to whom.

The usurper's own council read those pages. That was the end of him—not armies, not Wren's impossible gift, though the girl had shown them truth until they could not look away. Just arithmetic. Poisoners keep excellent records; it is their one virtue. And when his captains counted the gold he had promised them against the gallows he had prepared for anyone who displeased him, they chose, as men usually do, the option with better odds.

They say he wept at the end. I hope it's true. I hope he tasted something bitter in his last hour and understood, finally, what I had tasted every morning of my working life.

* * *

On the eleventh day I walked to the guildhouse on my own feet, slowly, with Serah pretending not to hover and doing it beautifully. Ovid met us at the door. He looked older—the weeks in the usurper's cells had silvered him—but his eyes were the same dry, amused instruments they had always been, and he looked me over the way he used to look over shipments of impossible things.

"Hm," he said. "You'll live. Annoyingly."

"Did you doubt it?"

"I never doubted it. I budgeted for it." He stepped aside to let us in. "There's something upstairs I want you to see. Bring your tea. You're supposed to be drinking constantly. Doctor's orders, and I outrank doctors."

Upstairs, in the long room where the guild's books had lived behind false shelves for thirty years, the old ledgers were gone—shipped to the council archives, every name in them now a matter of law rather than contraband. The shelves stood empty. Except one.

On the center table lay a single new book, bound in grey leather, its pages blank and heavy and waiting. On its cover, in Ovid's handwriting, three words:

*Ledger of Returns.*

"We smuggled people out for thirty years," Ovid said. "Names in, names hidden, names erased from every record the crown kept. Necessary work. But erasure was always the second-best thing we could give them." He tapped the cover. "The war's over. The Accord holds—for now, and I intend it to hold longer than for now. So the work changes. We bring them home, Jude. Every gifted soul who ran, who hid, who buried their names in the salt flats and the far islands. We write them back into the world. First page is already started."

He opened it. One entry, in careful script:

*Wren, called the Kindler. Returned.*

And beneath it, in a shakier hand, added later:

*Jude Villalobos. Returned.* — *W.*

I laughed, and it hurt my ribs, and I laughed anyway.

* * *

Winter came gently to the free cities, the way it did when no one was burning checkpoints behind you. Wren learned her letters at the kitchen table of the guildhouse, hunched over a slate with the ferocious concentration she usually reserved for holding fire in her chest. She was slow at it and furious about being slow, and then one evening in the third week she read a whole sentence aloud without stopping—*the wolf sleeps by the ember and is warm*—a sentence Serah had written for her, obviously, because Serah wrote nothing that wasn't either tactical or tender, and often both.

"There," Wren said, triumphant, chalk dust to her elbows. "Now I can read the ledger."

"You can read *one sentence* of the ledger."

"A sentence is how all of them start." She said it with such certainty that I wrote it down later, verbatim, in the margin of the returns page. Children should be quoted; they are the only honest historians.

Serah stayed. I want to record that plainly, because for years I recorded everything in code and shadow, and plainness is the luxury I intend to spend the rest of my life enjoying. She resigned her commission in a letter so brief it was practically poetry. She took a room in the guildhouse. She trained the free cities' new watch in the mornings and read to Wren in the evenings and sat with me at night on the roof, where the city glowed below us with a hundred unhidden fires—hearths, lamps, forge-flames, and here and there, if you knew how to look, the small bright signatures of people who no longer had to pretend their candles burned a little truer than other people's.

"Do you miss it?" she asked me one night. "The tasting. Knowing things before anyone else."

I considered honestly. My tongue was quieter now; the poison had taken some of its fine edge, and I grieved it the way you grieve a tool that was also a wound. But I had tasted truth in every dish for fifteen years and told no one, and I had learned the exact weight of a secret held alone.

"No," I said. "I'm done knowing things by myself. From now on, everything gets shared. Starting with the bread."

"The bread is mine."

"Half of yours is mine. Tradition."

She broke the loaf and gave me the bigger piece, and below us the city burned its ten thousand harmless fires, and in the guildhouse on the table by the window the Ledger of Returns waited with its blank pages, patient as morning.

We had a great many names to write.
