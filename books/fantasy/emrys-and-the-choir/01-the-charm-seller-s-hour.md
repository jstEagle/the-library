## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The lamp in Ivo Whitlock's stall guttered before she even lit it, as if the flame already knew what hour this was and wanted no part of it.

It was the charm seller's hour — that grey sliver between the last cart leaving the night market and the first bell of morning, when Vellamark belonged to no one. The smoke hung low enough to taste, coal-smoke and tallow and the sour breath of the river, and beneath it all the city's older smell: wet stone, old iron, the faint animal sweetness of ten thousand lives pressed too close together. In fifteen years Ivo had learned every layer of that smell the way a blind woman learns a room. It was how she knew when someone had been crying before they reached her stall. Grief had a scent. So did fear. So did the particular sharpness of a person carrying a secret they could not put down.

She lit the lamp with a spill of paper, cupping the flame from the wind that came funnelling down Tanner's Row, and set out her wares in their careful order. Knots of red thread for fidelity. Dried lavender sewn into linen hearts for sleep. Copper coins bored through and strung on leather — against fever, against ill luck, against the evil eye, though Ivo privately suspected the evil eye was mostly other people's poverty looking sideways at yours. Little glass vials of rose oil cut with something cheaper. And at the back, where the lamplight went soft and uncertain, a small clay pot sealed with wax, unlabelled.

The love charm. Not a real one. There was no such thing anymore, not since the Sundering, not since magic was outlawed and burned out of the city like a fever burned out of a body — badly, and with scars. What Ivo sold was rosemary and honey and a drop of wine, and instructions whispered so softly they sounded like prayer: *speak his name over it at dusk, seven nights, and if he is meant for you he will turn toward you.* It worked about as often as anything works on the human heart, which is to say sometimes, and never for the reason the buyer believed. But it cost three coppers and gave a person somewhere to put their hope, and hope, Ivo had learned, was the only commodity in Vellamark that never spoiled.

Her rules were simple, and she recited them each dawn like a litany, though she'd have denied it under torture that any such litany existed.

*Never speak to an animal where a human can see your lips move.*

*Never sell what cannot be explained away as herbcraft.*

*Never let them learn your name — your true name — and never, ever say it aloud yourself, not even alone, not even in the dark, because the dark has ears and some of those ears belong below.*

*And never, never take payment in anything but coin.*

Fifteen years ago there had been another rule, the first one, the one that had made all the others necessary. She did not recite that one. She kept it where she kept everything else that mattered: folded small, buried deep, wrapped in silence so thick she sometimes mistook it for peace.

The bell rang. Six strokes, dull and cracked, from the chapel of Saint Verity whose bell had been cracked since before anyone living could remember. The market lurched awake around her — shutters banging up, fishwives arguing with fishmongers, a child shrieking about a lost mitten. Ivo arranged her face into its working expression: pleasant, attentive, faintly amused, entirely unremarkable. A woman who sold charms was a woman who dealt in nonsense, and nonsense attracted no attention from the Inquisition. That was the whole architecture of her survival, built brick by brick over half a lifetime: be harmless. Be boring. Be *nothing*.

Then she saw the widow coming, and knew before the woman said a word what she would want.

Marta Hesse walked Tanner's Row the way people walk when they are carrying something heavier than their arms can show. Black wool, freshly dyed — you could smell the dye, sharp and vegetal, still bleeding into the weave. Her hands were clasped at her waist like a woman holding a door shut. Her eyes went past the lavender and the red thread and fixed, immediately, helplessly, on the little clay pot at the back of the stall.

Ivo's chest did the thing it always did, the slow tightening, the ache that was almost tenderness and almost dread. Because Marta Hesse was not here for a love charm. Love charms were for the beginning of things. This woman wanted the end of things — wanted one more word with a man who was past words, and had come to the only shop in Vellamark foolish enough to pretend it might arrange that.

"Madam Whitlock." Her voice was steady the way ice over deep water is steady. "They told me at the shrine — they told me there's nothing. No rites left for the dead to speak. Fifteen years since the gods stopped answering, they said, and the Hearthmother's fire is just fire now."

"That's what they tell everyone," Ivo said gently.

"But you're not the shrine." Marta's hands tightened. "You're the charm seller. People say—" She stopped herself, embarrassed, the way people are embarrassed by their own hope. "People say you know things. Small things. Whether a child will live through the winter. Where a lost ring went. Whether a man truly loved his wife before he died."

There it was. The question underneath the question, naked and terrible: *did he love me?* Ivo had heard it a hundred times in fifteen years, in a hundred mouths, and it never got easier, because the answer was never hers to give. Or rather — it *was* hers to give, that was the curse of her, the whole rotten gift of her. She could ask. She could ask the cat sitting on the dead man's chair. She could ask the dog that slept by his bed. She could ask the sparrows in the eaves who had watched him hang laundry and complain about the price of bread. Beasts remembered. Beasts told the truth, because beasts had never learned to lie.

But asking was speaking, and speaking was the thing she could not do where anyone could see.

"I sell charms," Ivo said, and her voice took on the flat warmth of the professional mask. "For love, for luck, for sleep. I don't traffic with the dead, madam. Nobody does. Whatever the priests told you is true — the dead keep their own counsel now."

Marta's face did not crumple. That was the worst part. It *stiffened*, the way faces do when grief has been rehearsed too long in private and has hardened into something load-bearing. "Three coppers," she said. "For the pot. For whatever's in it. I'll speak his name over it, seven nights, like you say. And if he turns toward me—" her voice caught on the impossible verb — "if he turns toward me, I'll know."

"You'll know," Ivo agreed, taking the coppers, feeling their weight, hating herself a little more efficiently than usual. "Speak it at dusk. Face east. And madam — whatever you feel on the seventh night, whether it's him or only the wind — let it be enough. Hope isn't a lie. It's just a door we build ourselves, when the real ones are locked."

Marta almost smiled. Then she turned away down Tanner's Row, black wool dissolving into the smoke, and Ivo sat alone with three coppers and a stolen hour.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she could never say, never sell, never explain: the widow's cat had followed her to the market.

A great grey tom, scarred at the ear, missing half a tail — a veteran of Vellamark's alleys, the kind of cat that survives by knowing everything and loving nothing. He came trotting down the row behind Marta Hesse like a dark thought given fur, and when the widow turned off toward the shrine district, the cat did not follow her. He came to Ivo's stall instead, and sat down in the shadow of the flour sacks across the lane, and looked at her.

Not the way cats look at people. Not the flat golden indifference, not the calculation of *feed me*. He looked at her the way one clerk looks at another across a crowded office: recognition, patience, business pending.

Ivo knew that look. She had known it since she was six years old, since the day a sparrow landed on the well-sweep beside her and said, in the plain voice of a bird, *your mother is crying again,* and Ivo — small, stupid, honest Ivo — had repeated it out loud, and the world had begun, very slowly, to close around her family like a hand.

She waited until the market noise peaked — the fishwife screaming about herring, the cart horse backing into the cooper's barrels, the moment when every human ear within thirty feet was occupied with human things. Then she turned her head a fraction toward the shadow of the sacks, and let her lips barely part, and breathed rather than spoke:

"He's gone, then. Your man."

The cat's whiskers twitched. His answer came not in sound but in that strange pressure-behind-the-ears way that beast-speech arrives, the way she had heard it all her life, the way no one else in Vellamark seemed to hear it at all: *Gone. Cold three days. They burned him at the common pyre because the fever took the house and the shrine wouldn't risk the ground.*

"Fever," Ivo breathed. "That's what they said?"

*What they said,* the cat agreed, and then, with the merciless precision of animals, *and what they said was true as far as it went. Fever took him. But the fever came from the water. From the cistern under the tannery. He drank there every day at noon, and the rats won't drink there now, and rats will drink anything.*

Ivo went very still. Stillness was her oldest discipline; her body had learned it before her mind understood why. A woman staring at nothing, lips closed, breathing slow — that was nothing. That was a charm seller resting her eyes. But inside, cold moved through her like water through a cracked wall.

Poisoned water. A dead man. A widow buying false hope with three coppers while the truth sat in the shadows wearing fur.

"Does she know?" Ivo asked the air. "Does the widow know about the water?"

*She knows the water tastes wrong,* the cat said. *She boils it twice. But she doesn't know it killed him, and she won't. Telling her would be telling the tannery, and the tannery pays the ward watch, and the ward watch asks questions, and questions in this city go down and never come back up.*

The cat washed one paw, unhurried, and added, with the dry cruelty only the honest can afford: *You could tell her. You could also stop being a charm seller and start being a corpse. You've survived fifteen years by knowing the difference.*

He was right. He was right and Ivo hated him for it, the way you hate a mirror.

This was the shape of her life, the whole shape, the shape she had worn smooth as a river stone: she knew things. She knew the baker's dog grieved for the baker's dead son. She knew the carthorse in Tanner's Row was going lame and would be rendered by spring unless someone intervened. She knew the sparrows had stopped nesting on the south side of the shrine street this year, and she did not know why, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. She knew everything the beasts knew, and the beasts knew everything worth knowing, and none of it could be spoken, and the weight of all that unspoken truth was the actual texture of her days — heavier than the stall, heavier than the lamp, heavier than the three coppers in her apron pocket that were not really payment but absolution, purchased falsely, spent wrongly.

She was tired. God below, she was tired, in the marrow way, in the way that sleep doesn't touch. Fifteen years of being a bridge that pretends to be a wall. Fifteen years of hearing the world's honest voices and answering them with a mask.

The cat stood, stretched, and regarded her a last time.

*One more thing,* he said, and his tone changed, and Ivo felt the change in her bones before her mind caught up — the way animals go quiet before weather, before earthquake, before the thing beneath. *The rats have stopped talking.*

Ivo's breath snagged. "Stopped?"

*Three nights now. All of them. Every rat from the river gates to the tannery rows. They don't beg. They don't quarrel. They sit in the walls with their whiskers forward and they listen downward, and once — only once, two nights past — the old rat under the shrine steps screamed one word into the dark, over and over, until the cats came and the screaming stopped.*

"What word?"

The cat looked at her, and for the first time in their long acquaintance, the scarred old tom looked afraid.

*"Coming."*

The market noise swelled around them — herring, barrels, bells, the ordinary gorgeous racket of the living — and Ivo Whitlock sat behind her little stall of harmless lies with the word *coming* lodged in her skull like a splinter, and did not move, and did not speak, and waited for the seventh night of a widow's hope to arrive and pass, and wondered what, exactly, was coming, and from where, and why every small honest mouth in Vellamark had gone silent to listen for it.

The lamp guttered again. She did not relight it.
