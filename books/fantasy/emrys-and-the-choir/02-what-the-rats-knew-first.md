## 2. What the Rats Knew First

The rats stopped speaking on a Tuesday.

Ivo knew it the way you know a clock has died — not by looking at it, but by the sudden weight of all the silence it had been holding back. For fifteen years the rats of Vellamark had been her weather-glass and her almanac, her gossip-merchants and her early-warning system, chattering in their dry rusted voices from the walls of every cellar she'd ever charmed her way into. *Bread on Cutter's Lane is weeviled. The tanner's boy leaves scraps behind the north wall. A man with soft boots walks the docks asking after charm-sellers.* They had never once been quiet.

Now they were quiet, and the quiet had texture. It had teeth.

She noticed it first at dawn, standing over her washbasin in the room above her shop, listening to the gap where the wall-rats should have been complaining about the cold. She noticed it second in the market, when the gulls that usually screamed insults at the fishmongers wheeled overhead in perfect, unnatural silence — a hundred white shapes turning on the wind like a thought no one would say aloud. And she noticed it third, and worst, when old Marta's terrier — a dog Ivo had spoken with twice, a cheerful, stupid, honest animal who thought the moon was another dog that kept getting away — pressed itself flat against its owner's skirts and trembled, and would not look at the drains.

Dogs looked at drains the way people looked at open graves. That was old knowledge, older than the Sundering, older than the covenant. But dogs did not *tremble*. Dogs were too proud to tremble.

Ivo closed her stall early. She told herself it was the damp in her joints, which was true, and also a lie, and she was careful to keep the two truths in separate pockets the way she kept everything else.

* * *

By evening the rats had found their voices again, and Ivo wished to every god she no longer believed in that they hadn't.

They came up through the floor of her cellar shop in ones and twos at first, then in dozens, ignoring the crumbs she kept for them, ignoring the little wax charms she'd set out as courtesy-tokens years ago. They gathered along the baseboards in a grey, shifting tide, and every one of them was saying the same thing, in the same raw scraped tone, the way a crowd says a name when the news has just arrived.

*"Coming. Coming. Coming."*

Not *he*. Not *she*. Not *it*. The rats did not have words for gods or monsters; they had words for cats and grain and warm pipes and the smell of death under doors. Whatever they were hearing, it fit into none of their categories, and so they had stripped it down to the only word left, the oldest one, the one that lived in the marrow of every burrowing thing since the world was young.

"Coming from where?" Ivo asked, kneeling, keeping her voice low and even, the voice she used for frightened animals and frightened customers alike. "Under the river? Under the cathedral? Tell me where."

The nearest rat — a big scarred female with half an ear gone, one of the cellar's elders — turned her black eyes on Ivo, and what Ivo saw in them made her stomach drop through the floorboards into the dark beneath the city.

The rat wasn't afraid *of* something. The rat was afraid *for* her.

*"Coming,"* the elder said again, softer now, almost gentle, the way you repeat bad news to someone who hasn't understood it yet. *"Coming. Down-down-down. Old-old-old. Under the singing place. Under the dead god's house. It walks where the prayers used to fall. It eats where the prayers used to fall. Coming."*

The singing place. The dead god's house. Ivo's mouth went dry. There was only one building in Vellamark that the beasts called that — the Cathedral of Whispers, gutted in the Sundering, sealed for fifteen years, its bells silenced by Inquisitorial decree and its doors chained with iron that had been blessed until the blessing wore grooves in it.

Prayers used to fall there. Fifteen years of them. Every unanswered prayer in a city of ninety thousand souls, falling like ash into the dark under the cathedral, going nowhere, answered by nothing.

Going somewhere, maybe. Answered by something.

"Thank you," Ivo said to the rat, because her mother had taught her manners before her mother taught her fear, and because her hands were shaking and saying thank-you gave them something to do. "Go home. All of you. Get high and stay high, and if anything asks you questions down there — anything at all — don't answer. Do you hear me? Silence is a door. Keep it shut."

The elder rat looked at her for a long moment with those wet black eyes, and then she did something Ivo had never seen a rat do in fifteen years of listening to them.

She bowed. A small, quick dip of the head, formal as a courtier, and then the whole grey tide of them poured backward into the walls and was gone, and the cellar was silent in the ordinary way again, and Ivo sat down hard on her stool among her harmless charms and put her face in her hands.

Down-down-down. Under the singing place.

Something was climbing toward Vellamark, and the small honest mouths of the city had heard it first.

* * *

In a narrow office above the Inquisition's Hall of Records, Third Clerk Ambrose Pell dipped his pen for the eleventh time and tried to make sense of the day's reports.

Pell was thirty-one years old, precise, unambitious, and quietly convinced that the Inquisition's true purpose was not the hunting of magic but the filing of it — that somewhere in the great ledgers of the Order, properly cross-referenced, the chaos of the world could be made to sit still. He believed this the way some men believe in the Hearthmother: without evidence, but with great comfort.

The reports were not sitting still today.

*Item:* Gull flocks over Fishmarket observed flying in formation, silent, duration forty minutes. No cause identified.

*Item:* Sewer-work crew (Gallowgate section) abandoned post citing "voices in the water." Two men dismissed for drunkenness; foreman insists neither had drunk.

*Item:* Stray dogs congregating at storm-grates throughout the Lower City, numbering in excess of two hundred at Cordwainer's Square alone. Animals non-aggressive. Animals refusing food. Animals facing, uniformly, downward.

That last phrase had snagged in Pell's mind like a thread on a nail. *Facing, uniformly, downward.* He had read it four times. Two hundred dogs did not agree on anything. Two hundred dogs could not be organized to face the same direction by any natural means, and yet there it was in Constable Renn's careful hand, with the constable's seal beside it, and Renn was not a man who embellished. Renn was a man who once filed a report consisting entirely of the sentence *Cat present at scene. Cat uncooperative.*

Pell dipped his pen again and added the final item, the one that had come up from the night-watch an hour ago, the one he had already copied twice because the first two copies seemed insufficiently real:

*Item:* Bells of the Cathedral of Whispers heard ringing, unassisted, at approximately the third hour past midnight. Duration: three strokes. Cathedral remains sealed. Chains intact. No persons observed within or without. Three residents of Cloister Row report the sound. One resident reports that following the third stroke, "all the rats in the lane came up out of the ground at once and stood in the street, looking at the cathedral, until dawn."

He sat back. Outside his window, Vellamark smoked and clattered and went about its business, the lamplighters making their rounds, the ordinary gorgeous racket of the living doing what it always did, which was drown everything quieter than itself.

Three strokes. The Sundering had taken fifteen years ago, almost to the season, and in all that time the cathedral's bells had hung dead as teeth. Pell had personally catalogued the chains. He had walked the perimeter with his ledger and his lamp and recorded the rust-bloom on each link, and the seals on each door, and the particular quality of the silence inside — a silence he had noted, in a moment of weakness he had never repeated, as *listening*.

He took a fresh sheet and began a memorandum to the Office of Inquiry, because that was his function: to notice, to record, to pass upward. He wrote the date. He wrote the items. Then he paused, pen hovering, and considered whether to include the observation that had been sitting at the bottom of his thoughts all day like a stone in a shoe.

The observation was this: every report, from every quarter of the city, described the same phenomenon in different shapes. Animals gathering. Animals silent. Animals attentive. As if the beasts of Vellamark had received an instruction that the humans had not — or as if they had heard something coming, from below, and were watching the doors for it.

Pell was a clerk. Clerks did not speculate. Clerks transcribed. But his pen hovered a long time over the paper, and in the end he wrote, in his smallest and most defensible hand:

*Recommend inquiry into subterranean activity beneath the Cathedral of Whispers. Recommend further that said inquiry be conducted promptly.*

He blotted it, sealed it, and set it in the tray for morning collection, and if his hand trembled slightly as he did so, well — the ink had been thin all week. Everyone knew that.

Below him, down through six floors of records and stone and founded earth, the city's roots went down into the dark, and the dark went down farther than any ledger recorded, and something in it was counting the bells.

* * *

Ivo did not sleep. She told herself it was inventory, and lit every lamp she owned, and sat in her cellar shop surrounded by small bright flames, doing sums on charms she had already counted twice.

The trouble with hearing beasts was that you could never stop hearing them, and the trouble with tonight was that the beasts would not stop saying it. Even muffled through stone and street and the thick dark between, the word came up out of the city's bones like water through chalk. From the mice in the granaries. From the moles in the garden plots above the Gallowgate. From something vast and slow-moving in the sewers that might have been a colony of rats the size of cats, speaking in chorus, a congregation of grey voices all chanting the same syllable into the dark.

*Coming. Coming. Coming.*

And underneath it — fainter, deeper, so far down it might have been her imagination except that imagination had never frightened her like this — something else. Not a word. Not yet. A rhythm. A slow, patient tread, like footsteps in a stairwell that never reached the top, that never even seemed to get closer, and were somehow more terrible for that. Because things that climbed got tired. Things that climbed could be waited out. This was not climbing. This was *approaching*, the way winter approaches, the way grief approaches — not moving through space but simply arriving, moment by moment, nearer and nearer to a day that was already fixed.

Ivo wrapped her arms around herself and made herself think it through, because fear unexamined grew teeth, and examined fear was just information.

The beasts were hearing something beneath the city. Beneath — specifically — the cathedral. The dead god's house, the singing place, the place where fifteen years of prayers had fallen like ash into the dark. The Sundering had cut heaven off from earth; everyone knew that much, whatever version of the story they preferred. Prayers went up and nothing came down. The gods had gone silent, the wild realms had drawn behind their veils, and the covenant — if there had ever truly been such a thing, and Ivo's mother had always said there had — lay broken in the rubble.

But prayers did not stop being prayed just because no one answered them. Ninety thousand people, fifteen years, every fear and hope and bargain and beggary, all of it falling into the same dark place, piling up, year on year, like snow in a pass that never melts.

What happened to snow in a pass, given enough years?

It became a flood. Or it became an avalanche. Either way, it came down eventually, and it came down *all at once*, and no one standing in the valley was ever the same.

"Oh," Ivo said aloud, very quietly, to the empty shop full of small flames. "Oh, you stupid, beautiful, broken city. You've been praying into a grave, and the grave is full."

The lamps guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

And in the silence that followed, clear across the cellar, from the crack beneath the door where the elder rat had gone, came one last voice — small, dry, exhausted, the voice of a creature that had been shouting warnings all day and had finally run out of strength to shout them loudly.

*"Coming,"* the rat whispered. And then, worse, in the tone animals used for the oldest truths, the ones they didn't have words for and had to bend other words around: *"Coming. And it knows your smell."*

Ivo sat very still.

The lamps burned. The sums lay unfinished on the counter. Somewhere above, the city slept on, dreaming its ninety thousand dreams and praying its ninety thousand prayers, not one soul among them knowing that the dark beneath the cathedral had learned to listen — or that of all the scents drifting down through fifteen years of stone and silence, the one the dark had chosen to remember, the one it was climbing toward through the deep roots of the world, was hers.

At dawn, the crows began to gather on her roof.
