## 3. A Prayer Answered in Ash

The shrine of the Hearthmother stood at the crook of Tanner's Row and the Ashen Stair, a squat stone building the color of old bread, with a squat stone goddess above its door whose face had been chiseled smooth during the Reformation so that no one could accuse her of looking back. Fifteen years she had been blind. Fifteen years her altar flame had been nothing but a flame — oil and wick and heat, tended by a priest who was less a priest than a caretaker, a soft-bellied man named Brother Aldous who swept the steps and sold candles and asked no questions, because asking questions was how you ended up on a list.

Ivo went to the shrine every seventh day, not to pray but to watch.

That was one of her rules, though it wasn't written anywhere: know where the faithful gather, because that is where the frightened gather, and the frightened talk, and sometimes what they say is dangerous. Another rule: never light a candle at an official shrine; candle-smoke carries intention, and intention can be read by people paid to read it. She broke that rule exactly once a year, on the anniversary of a fire she did not speak about, and she always bought the candle from Brother Aldous himself, always with her left hand, always while telling him a small lie about her aunt in Greyford who liked the honey-scented ones.

On this particular morning, the seventh day of the month the city called Emberfall, Ivo came early, before the crowds, because she wanted to speak with the shrine cats. There were three of them — a gray matriarch with a torn ear, a long orange fool who let children pull his tail, and a black one who belonged to no one and slept in the cold hearth behind the altar as if he were keeping the absence warm. The black one was the only one worth talking to. Cats who chose shrines chose them for reasons, and reasons were information.

*Anything?* she asked him, crouching by the hearth, keeping her lips still, her voice going out along the old quiet channel that no human ear had ever caught. *The rats have been strange all week.*

The cat opened one eye. He was an ugly, dignified animal, ribs like a washboard, fur gone coarse at the shoulders. *Everything is strange,* he said. *The stones hum. The ash tastes sweet. My dreams have doors in them.*

*Doors?*

*Doors that were bricked over,* said the cat, *and are not anymore.*

Ivo sat back on her heels. Behind the altar, the Hearthmother's flame burned low and blue-hearted, the way it always did at that hour, and she looked at it the way she looked at all flames now — with respect, and with an old scar of memory underneath the respect — and she thought, not for the first time, that the world had been too quiet for fifteen years and quiet was never natural. Quiet was held. Quiet cost something, somewhere, and someone was paying.

She bought her honey-scented candle. She told Brother Aldous the lie about her aunt. And she was halfway down the Ashen Stair, thinking about rats and doors and whether it was finally time to leave Vellamark for the marsh country where there were fewer ears and more eels, when the screaming started.

Not the cats. Not the rats.

People.

---

By the time she fought her way back up the stair — against the current of bodies pouring down, elbows and bundles and one dropped basket of onions rolling free like panicked little moons — the shrine's forecourt was packed shoulder to shoulder, and the sound coming out of it was not quite screaming anymore. It had changed key. It had gone reverent.

Ivo was small and quick and knew how to be unnoticed even in a crush; she slipped along the shrine's outer wall, through the gap between a bread cart and a beggar's lean-to, and came up at the edge of the crowd where she could see over the shoulders of a fishwife and under the arm of a guardsman who should have been holding the line and was instead on his knees.

The door of the shrine stood open. And the fire had come out to meet the morning.

It poured up through the altar-flame like water finding its old riverbed — a column of it, gold-shot with green at the base, standing in the doorway without burning anything, without needing anything, and inside the column, moving slow as honey, were shapes. Words. No — not words yet. *Prayers.* Ivo could feel them the way she felt the speech of beasts, a pressure against a sense most people didn't know they had: hundreds of prayers, thousands, pressed flat and stacked like linens in a chest, and now the chest was open and they were rising, unfolding, catching light.

A woman near the front had fallen to her knees. Ivo recognized her — Maren Cottle, the dyer's widow, the one whose prayer had been a small dry thing repeated every seventh day for eleven years: *let my son write to me, let my son write to me, let my son—*

The flame took Maren's prayer and burned it into being.

There was no other way to describe what happened. The words rose out of the column like sparks given grammar, hung in the air above the crowd in letters of living gold, and everyone read them at once, and everyone heard them at once, in the voice of the woman who had prayed them — *let my son write to me* — and then, horribly, wonderfully, the flame answered. Not in words. In warmth. It reached out — Ivo felt it pass over her like the breath of an enormous sleeping animal — and somewhere across the sea, in a place none of them could name, a pen touched paper.

Maren Cottle made a sound Ivo had never heard a human make. It was not joy. It was eleven years of swallowed grief tearing itself open all at once, and half the crowd went down with her, weeping, laughing, clawing at the air toward the flame, and the other half began to scream the other kind of screaming, the kind that runs.

Because everyone in Vellamark knew the law. Everyone knew the Sundering, the Reformation, the fifteen years of silence since the gods had been bound away from answering — *bound*, the priests said, *for your protection*, though no one could say what the binding protected them from except hope, which apparently needed guarding like a weapon. Prayers went up and prayers stayed unanswered; that was the covenant's shape now, the hole where heaven used to be. Every child learned it before they learned their letters: *the gods do not hear. Do not ask them to. Asking is a crime.*

And now the Hearthmother had answered a washerwoman's prayer in front of two hundred witnesses, and the sky had not fallen, and the Inquisition had not descended, and the flame kept rising, kept opening, kept taking prayers like a mother taking up children one by one—

The riot began the way a fever breaks: all at once, from the middle outward.

---

Later, Ivo would try to piece the hour together and find it would not hold a shape. Memory refused the task. There was only the crush, and the heat, and the noise — the crowd surging toward the flame like a tide toward something drowning, some to touch it, some to feed it, some to put it out, and the guardsmen forgetting their pikes in favor of their fists, and someone's stall overturning in a bright spill of dyed wool, saffron and madder red running together in the gutter like a wound. She was carried three feet off her own path by pure mass of bodies. A man's elbow found her cheekbone. A child screamed somewhere below the level of the noise, which meant the child was down, which meant Ivo stopped being a woman trying to escape and became a woman hunting, driving her shoulder into legs and hips, carving a line through the crush toward the sound.

She got the child — a boy, four maybe, face smeared with blood that turned out to be his own nose — pinned him against her chest inside the cage of her arms, and turned her back to the worst of it, and stood in the lee of the bread cart while Vellamark tore itself apart around a miracle.

The boy was sobbing. She put her mouth against his ear and did the thing she had done a hundred times in alleys and stables and flooded cellars: she borrowed calm. Not from herself — she had none to spare — but from the shrine cats, who were watching the riot from the wall with the flat, unimpressed eyes of creatures who had seen empires come and go and considered this one unremarkable. *Steady,* the gray matriarch sent down the line between them, lazy as sunning herself, and Ivo took the feeling and wrapped it around the boy like a blanket, and his sobs wound down to hiccups, and his fists unclenched from her collar.

*There,* she breathed. *You're all right. Where's your mother?*

*Everyone's shouting,* the boy said, which was true and useless, and then, because children and animals lived in the same honest country: *The fire talked. Mam says the fire talked.*

*I know,* Ivo said. *Keep your head down.*

She should have run then. That was the whole architecture of her life — fifteen years built on the simple load-bearing truth that when miracles happened, you left. But the crowd had welded itself shut around her, and the boy was shaking, and the flame in the doorway had begun, very softly, to sing.

That was the part no one else seemed to hear. The crowd heard the prayers read aloud; the guards heard their own fear; the priests, arriving late and pale, shouted about order and sacrilege in voices pitched to carry. But underneath all of it, in the register where Ivo had lived her whole secret life — the register of beast-speech and rat-scream and the hum in stones — the flame was singing, and the song was old, older than the Reformation, older than the Sundering, a lullaby-shaped thing with grief in the bottom of it, the way a well has water.

And then, in the middle of the singing, the flame said her name.

Not *Ivo Whitlock*. Never that; that name was armor, a coat she'd sewn herself from stolen cloth, and no god had ever spoken it because no god had ever known it mattered. What the flame said was the name underneath — the true one, the buried one, the one she had not spoken aloud in fifteen years, not once, not alone, not in sleep, the name she had starved and buried and salted the earth over — and it said it the way her mother used to say it, at dusk, from a doorway, calling a child home:

Softly. Twice. With love in it.

The world went narrow and ringing. The riot became a painting of a riot. The boy in her arms became a weight, a warmth, a fact she anchored to like a root in a flood. Because a name is a hook, and hers had been in the water fifteen years without a bite, and she had told herself — she had *built herself* on the telling — that the line had rotted through, that whatever had cast it was dead or bound or had forgotten.

The flame said it again. Third time. The way you call when you know the child heard you the first time and is deciding.

*Ivo,* said the Hearthmother's fire, in her mother's voice, in the voice of a god wearing her mother's memory like a borrowed shawl, *come home.*

Every hair on her body rose. The shrine cats on the wall went stiff as carvings, staring at her — at her specifically, all three heads turning together, and the black one's mouth opened and he said, aloud, in front of God and Vellamark, in the silent channel only she could hear:

*It knows you. Little seller-of-charms. It has always known you.*

The crowd surged. A guardsman went down bleeding two yards away. Somewhere a bell began to ring the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, and the sound of it cracked the moment open like ice, and Ivo moved — shoved the boy upward into the arms of a big dye-stained woman who caught him on reflex, and ran, low and fast, out of the forecourt, down the Ashen Stair, into the smoke and the shouting, with her heart slamming and her true name ringing in her skull like a struck bell that would not finish fading.

Behind her, above her, the flame stood in the shrine doorway and did not go out.

She made six streets before she understood, with the cold certainty of a woman who has survived by knowing things early, that running was already too late. The prayer had been read. The name had been spoken. Whatever bargain her mother had made — whatever debt lay coiled under fifteen years of small charms and careful lies — had just been called due, publicly, in fire, in front of the whole city.

And on the corner of Gullmarket, pasted up fresh, the paste still wet at the edges, was a notice bearing the seal of the Inquisition. It offered a reward for information regarding unlawful manifestations. It listed, among the persons sought for questioning, no name at all — only a description.

*A woman, small, dark-haired, known to frequent the shrine of the Hearthmother.*

Ivo stood in the wet-shadowed street with the notice in front of her and the bell still ringing three-and-one, three-and-one, and felt the past close its hand around her ankle, gentle as a cat, patient as a god.

They were coming for her. All of them — the Inquisition with its lists, and the flame with its lullaby, and whatever waited beneath the city that had taught the rats to scream *coming*.

She pulled her collar up, turned her face into the crowd, and walked — not fast, never fast; fast was guilt and guilt was visible — toward the one person in Vellamark who might know what her mother had done.

The shrine cats watched her go. And far beneath Tanner's Row, in the dark where the bricks had come unstitched, something old turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to count her footsteps home.
