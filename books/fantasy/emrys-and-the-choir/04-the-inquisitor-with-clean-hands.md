## 4. The Inquisitor with Clean Hands

They came for her on a morning so gray the gulls seemed to fly through smoke that had forgotten how to rise.

Ivo was at her stall, arranging the day's wares with the particular care she gave to days that felt wrong — the little glass bottles of colored water labeled *Courage*, *Sweet Dreams*, *Forget Him*, the knotted red threads sold as protection against bad luck, the dried lavender that did nothing but smell like summer and cost nothing to grow. Harmless things. The whole stall was an argument she made daily to the city of Vellamark: *this woman deals in nothing. This woman is safe.*

The two Inquisition officers did not argue back. They simply stood at the edge of the market square, one on either side of the lane, as if they had grown there overnight, and waited for her to notice them. That was the Inquisition's way. They never rushed. Rushing implied urgency, and urgency implied fear, and the Inquisition was never afraid. They were the reason everyone else was.

"Watch the stall," Ivo told the baker's boy who loitered nearby hoping for a copper, and her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. She noticed that. She catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything now — the tremor, the dry mouth, the way her heart knocked like something wanting out of a locked room. Fifteen years of practice had taught her this: fear was not the enemy. Fear unmanaged was.

She walked toward the officers because walking toward them was the only move that didn't look like running.

"You're wanted," said the taller one. He had a face like a closed ledger. "Questions regarding yesterday's disturbance at the Hearthmother's shrine."

"A riot," Ivo said. "I heard. Terrible thing."

"Were you there?"

"I sell charms, not opinions." She smiled the smile she'd polished over fifteen years — warm, slightly tired, utterly forgettable. "But if the Inquisition wants my opinion anyway, I'm honored."

Neither officer smiled. The tall one gestured down the lane, toward the white tower at the city's heart that everyone in Vellamark made a small private religion of not looking at.

They did not bind her hands. They did not need to. That was the first lesson of the walk: they considered her already caught.

* * *

The Inquisition's interview rooms were not in the tower's dungeons. That surprised people, Ivo knew from the market gossip — the ones who came back from questioning, the rare few, always described the same thing. A comfortable room. A fire. Tea.

The horror was the comfort. Screams said *we have decided about you*. Comfort said *we are still deciding*, and could be drawn out for years.

The room they gave her had a window, high and barred, letting in a column of pale light. A low fire burned though the day was mild. Two chairs faced each other across a small table, and on the table sat a porcelain teapot, two cups, and a plate of honey cakes arranged with geometric precision.

Inquisitor Dara Voss stood at the window with her hands folded behind her back, watching the street below as if it were a garden she was considering purchasing.

She was younger than Ivo had expected, or perhaps that was only the stories. They said Voss had cleared the smuggler-cults of the Salt Quarter in a single winter. They said she'd talked a hedge-witch into hanging herself with nothing but questions asked gently over three afternoons. They said her hands were always clean — literally clean, immaculately clean, that she washed them before and after every interview, and no one had ever learned what she thought she was washing away.

"Ivo Whitlock," Voss said, without turning. "Charm-seller. Licensed since—" she paused, delicately, "—fifteen years ago. Almost to the day."

"Yes."

"Fifteen years is a long time to be so consistently unremarkable." Now she turned, and Ivo saw why the stories were all about her eyes. They were light gray, nearly colorless, and they held the particular patience of something that could wait longer than you could breathe. "Sit, please. The tea is decent. The cakes are not mine — I don't eat during interviews. It seems unfair, somehow. Taking nourishment while another person's future sits on a table between us."

Ivo sat. Her body performed sitting the way it had practiced: relaxed shoulders, easy hands, the posture of a woman with nothing behind her ribs but ordinary fatigue. Inside, every instinct she owned was pressed flat against the walls of her, listening.

Voss poured tea for both of them anyway. Steam curled. Somewhere above, a pigeon muttered on the windowsill, and Ivo caught the edge of its small gray mind — *ledge warm, ledge warm, bread-man late* — and shut the door on it hard. Not here. Not with those colorless eyes anywhere in the room.

"You were at the shrine yesterday," Voss said, settling into the opposite chair. She didn't pick up her cup. She just let the tea sit there, cooling, a small domestic clock ticking between them. "During the burning."

"So was half of Copperlane. The Hearthmother's feast draws a crowd even in a bad year."

"It drew a crowd. You drew closer than most. Witnesses place you within ten feet of the brazier when the prayer ignited."

"The crowd surged. Everyone was pushing toward it. A prayer catching flame after fifteen years — people wanted to see."

"Mm." Voss opened a leather folder on the table. Ivo kept her eyes on the folder's plainness, its bureaucratic dullness, and not on what might be inside it. "Tell me what you saw."

"I saw an old woman's prayer burn green instead of guttering. I saw people panic. Panic does the rest."

"And what did you feel?"

The question slid in sideways, the way Voss's questions apparently always would. Ivo took a sip of tea to buy a moment. It was decent, as promised. "Frightened," she said. "Awestruck. Confused. Pick any of them; I felt all three, most of the time in overlapping order."

"Did you hear anything? At the moment of the burning?"

Silence stretched. Outside, the pigeon shifted its feet. *Bread-man late, bread-man late.*

"I heard screaming," Ivo said. "Screaming, and then bells."

"Hm." Voss turned a page without looking at it. It was a performance, Ivo realized — the page-turning, the folder, the whole apparatus of record-keeping. Voss wasn't consulting notes. She was letting Ivo watch her *not* consult notes, so that Ivo would understand the notes were unnecessary. Everything worth knowing, that gesture said, is already in my head. "You know, Mistress Whitlock, there's a version of this conversation where we talk about the riot for an hour and I thank you for your civic cooperation and you go home to your little bottles of colored water. I'd prefer that version. I suspect you would too."

"But?"

"But." Voss finally lifted her cup and drank, once, precisely. "There's another version, and which one we get depends entirely on honesty, and I should warn you — I am very good at recognizing it. It has a texture. Most people's truths feel like this tea. Warm, slightly bitter, going down easy. Lies feel different. Thicker. Like drinking cream that's turned." She set the cup down with a soft click. "So. Let's try again. What did you hear when the god spoke?"

*I heard it say my name.* The words rose in her throat like floodwater and Ivo pressed them down with both hands. Because that was the truth, and the truth was the one thing she could not give this woman. A god had said *Ivo Whitlock* in a voice like a struck bell, and if Dara Voss knew that — if anyone in the Inquisition knew that — then the quiet life died on the spot, and probably Ivo with it.

"Screaming," Ivo said again. "And bells."

Something flickered across Voss's face. Not anger. Something worse: interest, the way a scholar looks at a text with a smudged line.

* * *

The second hour was worse than the first, because the second hour was when Voss stopped asking about the riot.

She asked instead about Ivo's mother, dead fifteen years — *almost exactly fifteen years, isn't that a coincidence* — and about the fire that had taken her, officially attributed to a lamp left burning. She asked about the family Ivo had lived with afterward, the tanner's household in Greyford, both of whom had since moved away, one of whom had died. She asked these questions in the tone of a woman discussing weather, and each one landed like a stone dropped into a well Ivo had spent fifteen years pretending was bottomless.

"You've built a careful life," Voss observed, somewhere in the third hour. She had risen at some point to stand by the fire, and the light moved on her face without warming it. "Licensed trade. Taxes paid early, every year, which is unusual in Copperlane and suggests either remarkable virtue or a desire to be memorable only for virtue. No husband. No children. One close friend, a fishwife named Maren Oduya, whom you see weekly. You attend services at four different shrines in rotation — never the same one twice in a month, never missing more than two in a row. Devout, but generically devout. The piety of a woman making sure she's seen being pious rather than a woman with actual faith."

"That's quite a portrait for someone who sells love charms."

"I make portraits. It's most of the work." Voss turned from the fire. "Here's what troubles me about yours, Mistress Whitlock. Every detail is correct. And the whole picture is wrong. People aren't smooth, Ivo Whitlock. Real lives have burrs on them. Yours has been sanded. Sanded things are made, not born, and I find myself wondering what shape you were before the sanding."

The room was very warm. Or Ivo was. She thought about the cat from two nights past, the widow's great gray tom, and what he'd told her about the husband — *the river took him, the river and the debt-men together, he went under owing money to men who count bodies* — and how easily that knowledge could be twisted into evidence of witchcraft if it ever surfaced. Every kindness she'd ever done with her gift was also a confession waiting to be read aloud.

"I don't know what you want from me," Ivo said quietly.

"Honesty. Just once. As an experiment." Voss returned to her chair but did not sit; she rested her fingertips on the table's edge and leaned, slightly, into the space between them. "You were near the brazier. The god spoke. And I believe — this is only a belief, I collect beliefs the way other women collect buttons — I believe that whatever it said, it said to *you*. Not to the crowd. To you."

"Why would a god speak to a charm-seller?"

"Because gods are returning, Mistress Whitlock, and returning things reach first for what was theirs." Voss's voice stayed gentle, almost kind, which was the most frightening register it had yet achieved. "And because fifteen years ago, a woman named Sera Whitlock was recorded in our archives as a suspected beast-speaker. Unconfirmed. The investigation was closed when she died in the lamp-fire. She had a daughter. The daughter was never tested."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above, the pigeon flapped off the sill, and Ivo felt its departure like a door closing.

"My mother sold fish," Ivo said. "And mended nets. And died badly, like poor women do."

"Perhaps." Voss straightened. And then, unhurried, with the deliberation of a surgeon laying out instruments, she opened the folder properly for the first time and withdrew a single sheet of paper. She did not turn it around. She did not need to; she read from it aloud, in the level voice of a clerk reciting inventory.

"'Persons of interest, category three: unverified gift-bearers, priority assessment pending.' There are forty-one names on this list, Mistress Whitlock. Bakers. Ferrymen. A midwife. A child of nine in the Salt Quarter who talks to gulls — we're watching the gulls, incidentally; they've gone strange lately, all of them, have you noticed?" She looked up, and her colorless eyes found Ivo's and held them with terrible patience. "Your mother is on it. Posthumously. And beneath her name, in the column marked *known associates, blood relations*, there is one more name. Written in my own hand, eleven months ago, based on nothing but a portrait that didn't fit. Your name, Ivo Whitlock. Priority assessment pending."

The word *pending* hung in the air like smoke.

"Eleven months," Ivo managed. Her voice sounded far away, someone else's voice. "You've suspected for eleven months, and you waited until now?"

"I wait until now with everyone. Patience is the Inquisition's sharpest tool — sharper than fire, cheaper than torture, and it never leaves marks that upset the magistrates." Voss folded the list away again, and Ivo understood suddenly that showing it had been the entire purpose of the day. Not the questions. The list. *We know. We have always known. We can afford to be gentle, because gentleness costs us nothing and time costs you everything.* "Yesterday changed the arithmetic, though. Gods answering prayers. Beasts behaving strangely — the rats especially; my clerks report the rats in the undercity have stopped their usual habits entirely. When the world tilts, hidden things slide toward the light. Including gifts. Including yours, if you have one."

"I don't."

"No," Voss agreed softly. "Of course not. You sell colored water." She came around the table and opened the door herself — no guards summoned, no chains, the courtesy of a cat releasing a mouse it intends to catch later, at leisure, for sport or sustenance as the mood takes her. "Go home, Mistress Whitlock. Sell your charms. Light no lamps you can't account for. And think about the fact that I washed my hands before this interview and haven't needed to wash them after. Do you know what that means?"

Ivo stood. Her legs held. "No."

"It means you haven't done anything yet." Voss smiled — a small, genuine, horrifying smile. "Everyone on that list eventually does something. Guilty or innocent, they all do *something*, because a watched life cannot stay still. I'll see you soon, I expect. The city is getting interesting, and I do hate to miss interesting things."

* * *

The walk home took twice as long as it should have, because Ivo took three wrong turnings without noticing, her body moving on habit while her mind circled the same burning point: *she knows. Eleven months. My mother's name. Pending.*

Copperlane received her with its ordinary noise, carts and cries and the smell of frying onions, and none of it reached her. She unlocked her shop, stepped inside, and stood in the dim among her harmless wares while the afternoon light went thin.

It was the crows who broke through. Three of them landed on the sill outside her back window — her regulars, the ones she fed crusts to in winter — and their minds pressed against hers, urgent, ragged:

*Down. Down-under. The wet-dark one. The antlered one. It waits where the water doesn't move. It knows your name too, crow-feeder. It knew it before the crows did.*

Ivo closed her eyes. Behind them, in the black under the city, she could almost see it: a drowned stag standing in still water, antlers like drowned branches, waiting with the same bottomless patience as the woman in the white tower.

Everyone, it seemed, had been waiting for her. The only question left was which hunger would stop waiting first.

She pulled her coat back on, blew out the lamp she hadn't lit, and went down toward the cisterns.
