## 5. The Stag in the Cistern

The rats had gone quiet again, and that was worse than the screaming.

Ivo told herself it was a mercy as she descended the maintenance stair behind the tannery row, lantern held high, her boots finding the slick stone steps by memory. Three days since the word *coming* had torn through the undercity in a thousand small voices. Three days of silence from the beasts below, and silence, in Ivo's experience, was never empty. It was a held breath. It was a door someone stood behind.

She should not have come. Every rule she'd built in fifteen years said so: *never go to them; let them come to you; never be seen seeking.* But the rules had been fraying since the Hearthmother's shrine burned its prayer into living flame, since a god's voice had said her name — not *Ivo Whitlock*, the charm-seller with the crooked stall on Cooper's Lane, but something older, something that had made her spine go cold and her hands go steady all at once. She needed to know what was beneath the city. She needed to know before it came up on its own.

The stair ended at the old cistern, a vaulted cavern the size of a church, built by masons three hundred years dead who had believed water deserved architecture. The Inquisition had sealed it after the Sundering — or claimed to. The seals were chalk lines and brass plates now, mostly stolen, and the water had crept back through cracks in the world the way water always did, patient and indifferent to law.

Her lantern light reached maybe twenty feet into the dark. Beyond that, the cistern went black, and the black breathed.

Ivo stopped at the last dry step and did what she always did when she was afraid, which was listen.

At first there was only the drip of water, the groan of settling stone, the far-off industrial cough of Vellamark's pumps. Then, threaded beneath it, so faint she might have invented it: breathing. Slow. Wet. Labored, the way breath sounds when lungs are full of something they were never meant to hold.

"Hello," she said to the dark, in the voice she used for frightened animals — low, even, unhurried. "I'm not armed. I'm not Inquisition. I just want to know what you are."

The breathing stopped.

In the silence that followed, Ivo felt the peculiar pressure she had felt only twice before in her life — once as a child, when a dying horse had looked at her with terrible comprehension, and once at the shrine, in the crush of the rioting crowd. The sensation of being *regarded*. Not watched. Regarded, the way a jeweler regards a stone, turning it over, checking for flaws.

Then the water moved.

It didn't splash. That was the wrongness of it — a body the size of a cart horse rising out of black water without a single ripple, as though the water were simply giving it up, reluctantly, the way a fist opens. Her lantern caught antlers first: a great crown of them, wide as a doorway, dripping. Then the head beneath them, elongated, equine but wrong, the skull too long, the eyes set too far forward and too large, and the eyes were the worst part, because they were not animal eyes. They were drowned eyes. Clouded white, filmed over like the eyes of a thing that had been underwater a very long time, and yet they saw her. She knew they saw her. Everything about her.

A stag. A stag that had drowned and declined to stop existing. Water poured off its flanks in ropes, and where it touched the stone, the stone darkened and wept.

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. She made herself speak anyway, because the alternative was running, and running from a god seemed like the sort of mistake one didn't get to learn from.

"You're one of them," she said. "One of the old ones. You're awake."

The stag lowered its head. Antlers scraped stone somewhere above her, a sound like a ship's keel. When it spoke, the voice did not come from its throat. It came from the water, from the walls, from the wet dark all around her, resonant and slow, each word arriving like something surfacing from a great depth.

*I have been awake for eleven days. Do you know what eleven days is, to one who slept two hundred years?*

"Long," Ivo said carefully.

*A heartbeat. A heartbeat, and I woke drowning still, in my own house, in my own water, and the first thing I heard — do you know the first thing I heard?* The stag's nostrils flared, drawing her scent in, and Ivo felt it like a hand passing over her skin. *Prayers. Thousands of them. Fifteen years of them, sunk down through the stones of this city like silt, and every one of them addressed to no one.*

"The gods were sealed," Ivo said. "After the Sundering. Everyone knows—"

*Everyone knows what they were taught.* The stag stepped closer, and the water gave way before its hooves with that same terrible reluctance. Up close, Ivo could see that its hide was not merely wet. It was *inhabited*. Small pale things moved beneath the skin along its neck, eel-like, blind, turning. She made herself keep looking at its face instead. *You sell charms, little speaker. Love charms. Luck charms. Charms against colic and creditors. Tell me — when the widow buys your charm, does she believe?*

Ivo hesitated. "She wants to believe."

*Yes. And wanting is a kind of prayer, is it not? Wanting is prayer with its coat off, ready to work.* The drowned eyes regarded her without malice and without mercy, which was somehow more frightening than either. *For fifteen years this city has wanted, and grieved, and begged, and bargained, and every want fell downward through the dark like a coin down a well, and no one caught it. No one. Do you understand what that does to a god, little speaker? To hear the knocking and be unable to open the door?*

Something in Ivo's chest twisted. She thought of the rats screaming *coming*, and understood, suddenly, that they hadn't been warning her about the gods waking. They'd been warning her about this — the weight of fifteen years of unanswered prayer, pressing up from underneath like floodwater against a door.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why did you call me here?"

*Call you.* Something moved in the ruined face that might, on a living creature, have been amusement. *I called every speaker in Vellamark. Most did not hear. Some heard and ran — wisely. Twelve will come when the summons comes, in their own time, dragged by their own gifts. But you — you I wished to see alone. Because you are not only a speaker, Ivo Whitlock.*

Hearing the name spoken aloud in that voice, in that place, made her flinch. "That's not my—"

*It is the name you wear, and names worn long enough grow roots.* The stag's head swung closer, antlers parting the darkness overhead like branches. *But it is not the name your mother gave you, is it?*

The words landed like ice water down her back. Ivo took a step backward and hated herself for it. "My mother is dead."

*Your mother is dead,* the god agreed, gentle as deep water. *And before she died, she came to me. Or rather — she came to what I was, before the Sundering finished its work and I sank. You were six years old. You had already been betrayed once by then — the friend, the sold secret, the fire. Yes, I know. The water keeps records. Your mother waded into the flooded quarry at Greyford at moonset, carrying you asleep on her back, and she knelt in the shallows, and she made a bargain.*

"I don't believe you." Her voice came out flat and hard, and she was grateful for it. "My mother wouldn't go near magic. She scrubbed our floors with lye after anyone visited. She—"

*She scrubbed because she knew what clung,* the stag said. *People think the ones who fear the gift are the ones without it. Never once has that been true. Your mother had the hearing too, Ivo Whitlock. Faint. A whisper of it, where yours is a shout. And she knew what would happen to a child with a shout in a city that had outlawed whispering. So she bargained.*

"Bargained what?" The question tore out of her before she could weigh it. "Bargained *what*?"

*Protection,* said the god. *Yours. She asked that the beasts of this city never give you up — that no rat, no crow, no cat, no beast of feather or fur or scale would ever betray you to mortal ears, even under torture, even under enchantment, even unto death. That your secret would live in their keeping, unspoken, forever.*

Ivo stood very still. Fifteen years of small mercies rearranged themselves in her memory with a slow, sickening click: the cat who had warned her of the Inquisitor's rounds; the pigeons who had scattered, deliberately, to hide her from a watchman's sightline; the rats who had screamed *coming* — *coming* — not as threat, but as warning, *to her*, because some bargain older than her memory obligated them to spend their voices on her safety.

"She paid for that," Ivo said slowly. "Bargains have prices. What did she pay?"

The stag was silent a moment. Water dripped from its antlers, each drop loud as a hammer.

*That,* it said finally, *is the second matter I wished to discuss. And it is why you must speak for me.*

"Speak for you." Ivo laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "I don't — I sell love charms. I talk to cats about whose husband drinks. I am not a — an ambassador. A priestess. Whatever it is you want."

*The pantheon is waking,* the stag said, ignoring her, or perhaps simply having waited two hundred years and having no patience left for human modesty. *One by one, like lamps relit in a dark house. And when the last lamp burns, there will be a reckoning — a court, a council, whatever shape the old laws take when they remember themselves. Claims will be laid upon this city. Rivers will want their channels back. Fields will want their tithes. Storms will want appeasement. And mortals will need a voice among us, little speaker, or they will be carved up like a carcass by things that loved them once and have forgotten how.*

"So find another speaker. You said twelve are coming."

*Twelve speakers, yes. But the covenant requires a broker — one who stands between, who belongs wholly to neither side. And there is a claim on you already.* The drowned eyes did not blink. *Your mother's bargain did not end with protection. Protection was the first clause. There was a second.*

The cistern seemed to get colder. Ivo wrapped her arms around herself. "Tell me."

*When the gods returned — when any of us woke and found a speaker of true blood in this city — she bound you to serve as that speaker's voice in the courts of the returning pantheon. Whichever god found you first would hold your service. I found you first, Ivo Whitlock. Eleven days ago, when the seals thinned and I rose drowning from my own bed, the first prayer I heard clearly enough to answer was hers — the last prayer she ever prayed, prayed fifteen years ago and sinking toward me ever since, waiting for me to wake and catch it.*

The floor tilted, or seemed to. Ivo put a hand against the wet stone wall. Her mother, kneeling in the shallows at Greyford, a sleeping child on her back, trading away pieces of her daughter's future one clause at a time because she loved her and because love, Ivo was beginning to understand, was just another word for spending someone.

"What was the second clause?" she whispered. "What did she pay?"

*She paid the debt of the bargain itself,* the stag said. *A life for a keeping. Her life, timed against yours — the protection would hold until you no longer needed it, and she wagered, correctly, that you would need it your whole life. But the second clause binds differently. It binds you.*

"To you."

*To whichever of us wakes first. Me.* The stag lowered its great drowned head until its clouded eyes were level with hers, close enough that she could smell the water on it — river water, quarry water, and beneath that something older, mineral and cold, the smell of the inside of the earth. *I do not say this cruelly. I say it as one drowning creature to another. I need a voice, and you are owed nothing but what you were given, and what you were given was given by someone who is dead and cannot renegotiate.*

"That's not—" Ivo's voice shook. "That's not fair."

*No,* the god agreed, and there was something in its voice that might have been grief, if grief could drown. *Fairness died with the covenant. That is rather the point.*

Ivo pressed her back against the wall. Above her, antlers blotted out the lantern light. She thought about running. She thought about the twelve other speakers, about Tam Ossler with his weather-sense and his easy grin, about the Inquisitor with clean hands who had a list with her name on it. Hunted above, bound below. Her mother had built her a cage out of love and called it protection, and Ivo had lived in it fifteen years thinking it was a life.

"If I refuse?" she asked.

*Then I release you from the first clause.* The words fell into the cistern like stones into a well. *The beasts will owe you nothing. The next crow that sees you working a charm may sing of it to whoever it likes. The next rat may carry your name to the Inquisition in exchange for crumbs. You will be exactly what you have always pretended to be: a woman alone, with a secret and no allies, in a city filling up with gods and inquisitors.*

"And if I accept?"

*Then when the summons comes — and it will come within days, to all twelve of you — you will stand for me in the courts of the pantheon. You will speak my claims. And I will protect you as your mother asked: with fang and wing and fin and the whole listening dark, for as long as you live.*

The lantern flickered. Somewhere far above, Vellamark went on with its evening — carts and quarrels and suppers and prayers, thousands of them, falling silently down through the stones like silt, like coins down a well, landing unheard on the backs of waking gods.

Ivo closed her eyes. She saw her mother's face — and realized, with a small cold shock, that she had to work to see it now, that fifteen years had blurred it, that she remembered the lye-smell of her hands better than the color of her eyes. Everything her mother had done, she had done in the dark, unwitnessed, unthanked, and unpaid except in the coin of her own life. And everything she had bought with it was standing in front of Ivo now, dripping, crowned in antlers, waiting for an answer.

"You knew her," Ivo said quietly. "Before. When you weren't — this." She gestured at the drowned hide, the pale eels beneath the skin. "What was she like, to you?"

The stag was silent for a long moment. When it answered, the voice came softer, from deeper down.

*She was the last honest prayer I ever heard,* it said. *Until you walked down those stairs tonight.*

Ivo opened her eyes. The decision, when it came, did not feel like a decision. It felt like a door she had been leaning against for fifteen years finally swinging inward.

"All right," she said. "I'll speak for you. For now. Until I learn whether you're worth speaking for." She lifted her chin, and was surprised to find her hands had gone steady, the way they did at the shrine. "But I set terms of my own, stag. I don't lie for you. Not to the pantheon, not to the Inquisition, not to anyone. If you want a voice, you get a truthful one, and truth doesn't bend for gods any more than it bent for my mother."

The drowned eyes regarded her. Beneath the stag's skin, the pale eels turned and turned.

*Terms accepted,* the god said. *Now run home, little speaker, quickly, and lock your door, and do not open it for anyone wearing a face you love. Because the others are waking faster than I told you — and one of them woke angry, and hungry, and it has already begun asking the water where you sleep.*

The lantern went out.

Ivo stood in absolute blackness, heart slamming, and heard — unmistakably, from everywhere at once — the sound of the stag sinking back down into water that did not ripple, taking its crown of antlers and its drowned patience with it, leaving her alone in the dark with a bargain older than her memory and a warning ringing in her ears like a struck bell.

Somewhere above her, faint through thirty feet of stone, a bell in the Temple quarter began to toll.

Not the hour bell. The alarm.

* * *

She climbed the maintenance stair two steps at a time, one hand on the wall, the dark pressing at her back like a tide. By the time she shouldered through the hatch behind the tannery row, the sky over Vellamark was the wrong color — a bruised orange, shot through with veins of green, as though the sunset itself had been answered by something that shouldn't have.

Crows boiled off the Temple spires in a black, screaming cloud. Down in the streets, people stood in their doorways looking up, and Ivo heard them — not with her gift, with her ordinary ears — murmuring the only prayer anyone in Vellamark remembered anymore, the one they said at funerals: *keep us, keep us, keep us.*

And for the first time in fifteen years, something answered.

Every crow in the sky turned as one, and spoke with a single vast voice that rolled down over the rooftops like thunder given grammar:

**"WE ARE KEPT. NOW WHO KEEPS YOU?"**

Ivo stood in the tannery yard with her heart hammering against her ribs, soaked in river-smell and god-smell, her mother's bargain heavy on her shoulders like a yoke, and understood that whatever court was convening above the city, it had already begun —

and that somewhere in that boiling black chorus, a voice she recognized was calling her true name, the one no human tongue had spoken in fifteen years, calling it across the whole sky of Vellamark like a summons she could not refuse.
