## 7. The Choir Assembles

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the voice of a pigeon.

Ivo was haggling with a fishmonger over the price of eels — not because she wanted eels, but because the stall sat beside the canal and the water rats had been muttering all morning about something moving beneath the pilings — when a grey bird dropped onto the awning above her head and said, in a voice like gravel poured through a child's throat:

*Whitlock. The Cathedral of Whispers. When the bell that does not exist rings.*

She did not flinch. Fifteen years of practice had taught her that much: never flinch where mortals can see. She paid for two eels she didn't want, wrapped them in brown paper, and walked home at an ordinary pace while her heart hammered out a rhythm no honest drummer would keep.

The pigeon kept pace along the rooftops, hopping from ridge to ridge, its head cocked.

"You could have written," she murmured under the noise of the market.

*The old ones don't write,* said the pigeon. *They remember aloud. And they remembered you by name.*

"Which name?"

The bird ruffled its feathers, and for a moment its eyes were not the flat amber of a city pigeon but something older, deeper, the color of pond ice over black water. *All of them,* it said, and flew away.

---

That night Ivo did not sleep. She sat at her workbench with a single candle burning and took inventory of her life the way a miser counts coins before a robbery.

Her shop on Candle Lane, with its jars of dried lavender and its charms that were ninety percent craft and ten percent prayer — the safe kind, addressed to no one, whispered into knots of thread so quietly that even heaven would have to strain to hear them. Her reputation as a charm-seller of modest but reliable talent. Her name. Not the true one, never the true one, but the one she had built like a wall around herself, brick by brick, until even she sometimes forgot there had ever been anything behind it.

*Ivo Whitlock.* A good solid name. A name that belonged to a woman who sold love charms to grieving widows and talked to cats only when the shutters were closed.

And now the gods knew it. The gods, or whatever was stirring beneath Vellamark's smoke and stone, had reached into the ordinary machinery of the city and plucked her name out like a splinter.

She thought about running. She had run once before, at fourteen, with ash still in her hair and her mother's hand going cold in hers, and she had learned the lesson thoroughly: you cannot outrun a thing that knows your name. Names are hooks. Names are leashes. Somewhere in the dark, something held hers now, and every mile she put between herself and the Cathedral of Whispers would only tighten the line.

Besides — and this was the thought that frightened her most, so she turned it over slowly, examining it like a coin she suspected was clipped — besides, the beasts had been screaming *coming* for three weeks. The rats beneath the canal. The crows on the temple roofs. Even the fat, complacent dogs of the merchant quarter had taken to howling at nothing between the second and third bells. Something was arriving. It would arrive whether or not Ivo Whitlock stood in the ruins to meet it.

Better, perhaps, to be standing when it came. Better to see its face.

She snuffed the candle and began, methodically, to pack.

---

The Cathedral of Whispers stood on the highest ground in Vellamark, or what had once been the highest ground; after the Sundering the whole district had sunk, slow as a body settling into a grave, until the cathedral's spires leaned like drunks against one another and its doors opened onto a street four feet below their sills. No congregation had worshipped there in fifteen years. The Inquisition had sealed it, then unsealed it, then simply forgotten it, the way the city forgot everything that stopped costing money.

Ivo arrived before dawn, because the summons had said *when the bell that does not exist rings*, and she had reasoned that a bell which did not exist would not be hurried. She climbed the tilted steps, ducked under the leaning lintel, and stepped into a darkness that breathed.

Not wind. Breath. The cathedral's interior was vast and hollow and it *inhaled*, slow and enormous, and the air that moved past her carried the smell of cold stone and older things: incense long burned to memory, tallow, rain on dust, and underneath it all a faint sweet rot, like fruit left too long in a locked room.

"Hello," she said softly, to the dark, because it seemed rude not to.

The dark said nothing. But somewhere high above, in the ruined belfry where no bell hung, a sound began — not ringing, exactly, but the *memory* of ringing, a bronze resonance with no metal behind it, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than heard. Once. Twice. Twelve times.

And they came.

They came alone and in pairs, out of the dark doorways and down the broken nave, and Ivo pressed herself against a pillar and watched them the way she had learned to watch everything: cataloguing, assessing, measuring each one for the shape of the danger they carried.

A woman in a butcher's apron, blood stiff on the leather, who walked with her eyes closed and her hands out like a blind woman, though her eyes moved beneath the lids, quick and reading. A boy of maybe sixteen, dockside thin, whose hair moved in a wind Ivo could not feel. An old man in the grey robes of a laundress' guild, carrying a birdcage covered in cloth, from which came no sound at all — and yet Ivo's gift prickled at it, the way a tooth aches near a fire. Two sisters, twins, holding hands so tightly their knuckles whitened, speaking to each other without opening their mouths. A tall man with a soldier's bearing and a farmer's hands. A girl who left wet footprints on dry stone.

Twelve of them, counting herself. Thirteen.

Every one of them, Ivo realized, with a chill that started in her spine and spread outward like frost, was doing what she was doing: hiding against a pillar, hugging the walls, making themselves small. A dozen people who had spent their whole lives being careful, gathered in one room by a bell that did not exist.

*A dozen hidden things,* said a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone itself, from the breath of the cathedral, *called out of hiding at last.*

The whispering voice was vast and gentle and utterly indifferent to fear, the way winter is indifferent. Ivo felt it pass through her like light through glass.

*You were never meant to hide. You were meant to sing.*

"What are we?" someone asked — the butcher woman, her voice raw, her eyes still closed. "What did you call us here *for*?"

*The covenant had a voice,* the stone said. *When the world was younger and the walls between realms were young, every honest prayer passed through mortal throats on its way to heaven. You are that throat. You are the Choir.*

Nobody sang. Nobody moved. The word hung in the dark like a lamp nobody dared light.

Ivo became aware, gradually, that the man beside her — the tall one, farmer's hands, soldier's stance — was watching her watch the others. He was perhaps thirty, with a face that might have been handsome if it had ever relaxed, and a scar through one eyebrow, and eyes the grey-green of weather coming. When he caught her looking, he didn't look away. He smiled instead, crooked and knowing, and leaned closer, and said in a low voice pitched only for her:

"Charm-seller off Candle Lane. Love charms, luck knots, the usual harmless rubbish." He tapped his own ear. "But your voice carries, friend. Dogs talk to you in the street. Rats tell you things. I've watched you do it for two years."

Ice went through Ivo's veins, slow and total. Every rule. Every careful year. Her mouth opened to deny it — *you're mistaken, friend, I sell thread and dried flowers* — and then closed, because what was the point? They were all standing in the same trap. The bell that did not exist had rung for all of them.

"And you?" she said instead. "What do they call you?"

"Tam. Tam Ossler." He stuck out a hand, easy as a man greeting a neighbor across a fence. "Weather-speaker. Storms mostly. Rain, if I'm feeling humble." His grin widened. "Don't look so horrified. We're colleagues now, apparently. The Choir of Vellamark." He said it lightly, but Ivo heard the thing underneath the lightness — the same thing she felt underneath her own fear. A hunger. Fifteen years of hiding, and here, suddenly, was a room where hiding wasn't necessary. The hunger to belong was a dangerous thing. She recognized it because she was fighting it herself, right now, in her own chest, and losing.

"Don't get comfortable," she said quietly. "Gods that summon you by force of a bell aren't gods that mean you well."

"No," Tam agreed cheerfully. "Probably not. But my brother's dying, Ivo Whitlock, and I have spent six years talking to clouds about it, and the clouds don't care." His grin stayed fixed but his eyes went hard and bright as hail. "So if something in this ruin wants to use me — well. Let it stand in line."

Before she could answer, the cathedral breathed again, deeper, and the whispering voice gathered itself the way a wave gathers before it breaks.

*Hear the terms of your gathering,* it said. *The covenant is broken. The prayers of this city go unanswered, and worse — they go unheard, and something feeds upon the silence they leave. You have been called because the covenant cannot mend itself. It must be spoken back together. By throats. By yours.*

Around the nave, twelve people flinched as one.

*Rest tonight within these walls,* the stone said, softer now, almost tender, and that tenderness frightened Ivo more than any threat could have. *No harm will come to you here. The Inquisition has forgotten this place. Your secrets are safe among singers.*

And then the presence withdrew, like a tide going out, and the cathedral was only a cathedral again — cold, ruined, smelling of dust and dead incense — except that it was full of strangers who could do impossible things, and every one of them was looking at every other one of them, and no one knew what to say.

It was the butcher woman who finally spoke, eyes still closed, hands still reaching. "I hear the dead cattle," she said, to no one, to everyone. "Every animal slaughtered in this city for fifteen years. I hear them. Do any of you—" Her voice cracked. "Does anyone else *hear things*?"

"I hear the river," said the boy with the moving hair.

"The moths," whispered the girl with the wet footprints. "The moths tell me where lost things are."

"My birds," said the old man with the cage, and pulled the cloth off it. The cage was empty. "My birds are dead. All nine of them. But they still sing to me, every morning, and I have never told a living soul."

Silence. Then Tam Ossler laughed — not cruelly, but with a wild, disbelieving edge, the laugh of a man falling and finding out mid-fall that he can fly. "Fifteen years," he said. "Fifteen years I've been mad alone, and it turns out the whole city is mad together."

Ivo did not laugh. She stood against her pillar with her arms crossed tight, and she felt the terrible thing happening inside her and could not stop it: the calcified grief of fifteen years developing a hairline crack. Because these people — the butcher who heard the dead, the boy the river loved, the old man keeping a cage full of ghosts — these people were *her*. They were the shape of her secret, multiplied. And some starved part of her, the part she had buried with her mother and bricked over with the name Ivo Whitlock, looked around that ruined nave and whispered: *home.*

Terrified belonging. That was what this was. Belonging the way a nail belongs to a magnet — not by choice, not gently, but because something larger and colder had decided what you were for.

She found a dry corner near the broken altar, wrapped her cloak around herself, and tried to sleep. Sleep came in shallow doses, punctuated by the sounds of the others settling: the butcher weeping quietly; the twins murmuring their mouthless conversation; Tam Ossler sitting awake on the altar steps, watching the dark belfry with his hail-bright eyes, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic with his soul.

Near midnight — or what Ivo judged was midnight, since no window in the cathedral admitted light — the crows found her.

Three of them, slipping in through a gap in the roof, folding down through the dark to land on the broken altar beside her head. She knew them. The three who had been following her for weeks, ever since the rats began to scream. They arranged themselves with ceremonial precision, and the eldest — missing a toe, scarred across the cere — dipped its head close to her ear.

*You should not have come, bridge-child,* it said.

"I didn't have a choice."

*There is always a choice. That is what the gods fear.* The crow's eye, black and wet, reflected the nothing of the cathedral. *Listen. Among the thirteen, one has already made a bargain. One has already sold something that is not theirs to sell. Before the singing starts, find the traitor, or the first note will be your death.*

Ivo's blood went cold. Her eyes went, before she could stop them, to the altar steps — to Tam Ossler, still awake, still watching, still smiling faintly at the dark as though it had told him a joke.

The crow followed her gaze and made a sound that might, in a less dignified creature, have been a chuckle.

*Clever,* it said. *But the storm-speaker is not the only one with secrets. Ask yourself, bridge-child: why did the old ones know your mother's name?*

And the crows lifted, all three at once, silent as spilled ink, and were gone through the gap in the roof, leaving Ivo alone in the breathing dark with a question she had spent fifteen years making sure no one would ever ask her.
