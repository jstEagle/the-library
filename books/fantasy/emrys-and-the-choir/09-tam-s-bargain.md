## 9. Tam's Bargain

The storm came to Tam Ossler the way it always did — uninvited, patient, and smelling of iron.

He felt it first in his teeth. That was how his gift announced itself, had always announced itself, since he was a boy of seven standing in his mother's garden with hail melting down the back of his collar and a taste like copper coins on his tongue. The weather spoke to him before it arrived. It told him its intentions the way a dog tells you it means to bite: not in words, but in pressure, in the sudden wrongness of the air against your skin.

Tonight the air over Vellamark was wrong in every direction at once.

Tam stood on the roof of the tannery on Corvis Street, where he'd gone to be alone, or as alone as a man could be with a god circling him like a gull after offal. Below him the city lay in its usual shroud — coal smoke and river fog braided together, the lamps along Greyford Bridge strung like dim orange beads. Somewhere beneath all that, in the drowned dark under the cathedral, eleven other people who could speak to things that should not be spoken to were sleeping, or pretending to sleep, or lying awake doing exactly what Tam was doing now: thinking about what they'd been asked to do, and what it would cost.

*Reforging,* the threshold god had said, in its voice like a door swinging shut. *A price none of you can yet name.*

Tam thought he might have an idea of the price. He just didn't intend to pay it himself.

His teeth ached. He opened his mouth and let the storm in.

It poured through him the way water pours through a cracked jug — he felt every contour of it, the cold front sliding down from the northern hills, the warm wet breath rising off the sea, the two of them grinding against each other above the city like millstones. And threaded through it, familiar now after three nights of this, the presence. Vast. Amused. Attentive the way a cat is attentive to a mouse that has made the mistake of being interesting.

*You came up to the roof again,* the storm said. It didn't use sound. It used pressure, dropping barometric whispers directly into the hollow of his skull. *You keep coming up to high places when you want to talk to me. You think I can't hear you from the ground?*

"I know you can hear me anywhere," Tam said aloud. His voice came out rough. He hadn't spoken to another living person since the Choir dispersed at dusk. "I come up here because it's harder for the others to see me."

*Ah.* The pressure shifted, sharpened. *The others. Your little choir of liars and hiders. The woman who talks to rats. The man who hears what dead wood remembers. The girl who—*

"You know what they are."

*I know everything the wind carries, weather-speaker. Which is more than any of them know about each other.* A rumble of thunder, far off to the north, rolling toward the city like something being dragged. *And still none of them knows about you yet. About what you've been doing on rooftops while they sleep.*

Tam's hands curled on the wet slate. "That's why I'm here."

*Say it, then.* The wind picked at his hair, his collar, gentle as a pickpocket. *You've been circling it for three nights. Say the thing you climbed up here to say.*

Below him, a tannery dog began to bark at nothing. Tam watched its breath steam in the lamplight and thought: *this is the last night I am only a traitor in my own heart. After tonight I'll be one in fact.* He found, to his surprise, that the thought didn't frighten him. It steadied him. Treachery, it turned out, was a kind of floor to stand on. You couldn't fall any further once you'd decided to fall.

"My brother is dying," Tam said.

The storm went very quiet. Even the distant thunder stopped, as if the whole sky were leaning in.

"His name is Wren. He's nineteen. He works the dye-works on Salt Lane, and eight months ago he breathed in something he shouldn't have — mordant fumes, the foreman says, though the foreman lies — and now there's water gathering around his heart. Slowly. The physicians call it dropsy of the chest and charge a fortune to tell you nothing." Tam's voice stayed level. He'd rehearsed this. He'd rehearsed it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones. "He coughs blood in the mornings. He jokes about it. He's the funny one. I was always the strange one, the one who knew rain was coming before the clouds did, and he covered for me our whole childhood. Every time. When I froze a well solid by accident, when I called lightning down on Master Hedley's barn — he took the blame, or he lied, or he made a joke until everyone forgot there was anything to explain."

*And now you would repay him.*

"And now I would repay him." Tam turned to face north, where the clouds were massing, black on black, lit from within by silent flickers. "I've heard what the gods are offering. Protection. Favor. A place at the table when the covenant is reforged. Every speaker in that cathedral is going to bargain with some god or other — don't tell me they aren't. The stag-god already has its hooks in Whitlock; half the city saw her name written in fire. So I'm not asking for anything they won't ask for."

*No,* the storm agreed. *But you're asking for it first. And secretly. And offering something they haven't offered.* The pressure tightened around him, delighted, terrible. *Their loyalty. All of them. Spoken to me, sworn to me, before they even know there's a choice to make.*

"The Choir's loyalty," Tam said. "In exchange for Wren's life. You heal him — fully, no relapse, no debt called in later — and when the Choir stands before the pantheon to reforge the covenant, they stand as yours. Their voices answer to you first."

The silence stretched so long Tam wondered if the god had left. Then the whole sky laughed.

It was not a sound. It was a *condition* — the air itself shivering with amusement, the hairs on his arms lifting, static crawling across the slate under his palms. Lightning walked without sound along the ridge of clouds to the north, a long slow vein of white, like something opening one eye.

*A mortal,* the storm said, *offering me other mortals. As if they were his to give.*

"They are mine to give." Tam heard his own voice go hard, and let it. "You don't understand loyalty, maybe, up there in the wind. Loyalty isn't a thing people hand over wrapped in ribbon. It's a thing you *spend*. I've spent my whole life spending it on Wren — every secret kept, every lie told, every time I swallowed my gift and said nothing because saying something would have gotten us both burned. Fifteen years I've been paying for him out of my own hide. This is just the last payment, and it's larger than the others, that's all."

*And if they refuse? If your choir will not sing for me?*

"Then you'll have their refusal too, and you'll have learned it honestly, which is more than the covenant ever gave anyone." He paused. "But they won't refuse. Not most of them. They're frightened, and tired, and they've been hiding so long that someone offering them a place in the open — even a place bought with a chain — will look like rescue. I know them. Three days among them and I know them better than they know each other. The old woman with the bees already prays to you when her hives swarm. The boy who speaks to fish would drown himself before he refused a god. And Whitlock—" he hesitated, and the storm pounced on the hesitation like a hawk.

*Whitlock,* it repeated, tasting the name. *The beast-speaker. The one the stag wants. You don't trust her.*

"I don't know her. Nobody knows her. She walks into a room like she's deciding how much of herself to leave at the door." Tam looked down at his hands, at the faint blue-white sparks jumping between his fingers where the god's attention ran through him like current through wire. "But she's the one the others look to. She's been doing this longer, hiding longer, and hiding teaches you things. If she stands against you, half the Choir stands with her. So I'm telling you now, plainly, as part of the bargain: whatever you do with them, do it *before* she binds them to her. Or buy her. Or break her. I don't care which. But I want Wren breathing free air next spring either way."

There. It was said. All of it, laid out on the wet slate between him and the sky like a butcher's display: his brother's life on one side, twelve souls on the other, and himself standing in the middle holding the knife.

He expected to feel worse. He mostly felt *lighter*, the way a fever breaks — awful and clean at once.

The storm considered him. He could feel it doing so, a vast slow turning of attention, the way the sea considers a swimmer who has gone out past the sandbar.

*You understand,* it said at last, softer now, which was somehow worse, *that the covenant, reforged, will bind gods to honesty as much as mortals. If I take your bargain and give you your brother, I must give him truly. No rot deferred. No death rescheduled. He lives, and lives whole, and owes neither of us anything.*

"Yes."

*And you understand what you are, weather-speaker. What you will be, afterward. There is a word for a man who sells his companions before the battle is joined. The other gods will know it. The Choir will know it, eventually — they always do, these things surface like drowned bodies. And when they know it, no reforged covenant will protect you from their grief and their anger. You will have your brother and lose every other human bond you might have made. Is the arithmetic acceptable?*

Tam thought of Wren at the kitchen table that morning, gray-faced, laughing at some idiocy, one hand pressed flat against his chest as if holding a door shut. He thought of fifteen years of small treacheries already committed — every silence, every lie, every morning he'd dressed his gift in innocence and walked out into a city that would kill him for it. He had been betraying someone or other since he was seven years old. At least this betrayal had a shape. At least this one bought something.

"The arithmetic is acceptable," he said.

*Then witness it,* said the storm, and the sky came down.

Not rain — not yet. But the pressure dropped so fast his ears popped, and every lamp on Corvis Street guttered at once, and the tannery dog fell silent mid-bark. Above him the cloud-mass tore open along a seam of blue-white light, and the god stepped through — not in body, gods didn't have bodies, but in *attention*, a column of gathered wind and ozone and the smell of the sea before wrecks, and Tam felt the oath settle onto him like a yoke onto an ox. Heavy. Fitted. Permanent.

*Sworn and witnessed,* the storm said, in every drop of moisture in the air, in the blood-warmth behind his eyes. *Your brother lives. Your choir is promised. Go down, weather-speaker, and smile at them tomorrow. Sing beautifully.*

The presence withdrew. The clouds closed. Ordinary darkness resumed, and Tam Ossler stood alone on the rooftop with his ears ringing and the taste of lightning in his mouth, feeling the oath sit inside his chest like a swallowed stone.

He got down off the roof on shaking legs. In the street below, the first fat drops of rain were beginning to fall — the storm keeping its word already, in its way, watering the city it had just been paid in souls. Tam pulled his collar up and walked home through it, and told himself the wetness on his face was only weather.

At the door of the lodging house on Salt Lane, he paused. Through the shuttered window, faintly, he could hear Wren coughing — that deep wet rattle that had become the soundtrack of their lives. Eight months of it. Eight months of listening to his brother drown by inches and being able to do nothing but boil water and lie about how bad it sounded.

By morning, the cough would be gone. By morning, Wren would wake clear-lunged and hungry and complaining about the porridge, and never know what it had cost, and never know what his older brother had sold to buy it.

Tam put his forehead against the cold doorframe for a moment. Just a moment.

Then he heard it — so soft he almost missed it, threading down through the rain from somewhere above the roofs. Not thunder. Not wind. A sound like a great many wings, and beneath the wings, thin and dry and carrying strangely through the storm, the voice of crows.

They were speaking to each other. And what they were saying, over and over, in their rusted-hinge voices, was a name.

Not his name.

*Whitlock. Whitlock. Whitlock.*

Tam stood in the rain and felt the stone of the oath shift in his chest, and understood that the beasts of Vellamark had been on the rooftops tonight too — that they were always on the rooftops, always watching, always telling each other everything — and that whatever the crows knew, they were already carrying it to the woman whose name they sang.

He had until dawn. Maybe less.

Tam Ossler went inside to sit by his brother's bed, and waited for the coughing to stop, and listened to the rain come down like applause.
