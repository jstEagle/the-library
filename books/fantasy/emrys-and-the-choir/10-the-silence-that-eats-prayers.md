## 10. The Silence That Eats Prayers

The shrine of Saint Ferren had been a place of small prayers for as long as Vellamark had been a city — prayers for safe births and full nets, for rain that came on time and fevers that broke by morning. It was not one of the great temples the Sundering had gutted. It was too humble to be worth burning. So it stood in its crook of the Saltmarket like an old dog no one remembered to kick, its stone walls black with centuries of candle soot, its little alcove still holding a carved figure of a woman holding out her hands.

Ivo went there because the rats told her to.

They had come to her stall three nights running — not screaming *coming* anymore, but quieter now, their voices worn down to something almost conversational, the way men get quiet after a battle rather than before one. *The prayers go down,* they said. *Down past us. Past the pipes. Past the bones. They fall and they don't land.*

She hadn't understood until she'd sat in Saint Ferren's at dusk and watched a woman kneel at the alcove and pray, lips moving, eyes wet, and heard — with the sense that was not hearing, the sense that lived somewhere behind her sternum where all the beasts' voices lived — the prayer itself peel away from the woman like a leaf from a branch.

It didn't rise. That was the thing. Fifteen years ago, when the covenant still held even in its broken way, prayers had gone *somewhere*, even unanswered ones; she remembered the feeling of them from childhood, a faint upward draft in the world, like heat off a summer road. Now they fell. The woman's prayer dropped through the floor of the shrine, through the foundations, into the dark beneath the city, and did not land anywhere at all.

It simply stopped being heard.

And something down there drank the silence it left behind.

* * *

"You shouldn't be here after dark," said Brother Anselm, the shrine's keeper, shuffling past her with an armload of guttered candles. He was eighty if he was a day, half-blind, and had never once looked directly at Ivo's face, which was why she tolerated him. "The Inquisition's been asking about folk who linger."

"I'm lingering," Ivo said mildly, arranging dried lavender on her table of goods — she'd set up her charm-seller's tray just inside the door, because a woman alone in a shrine after dusk needed a reason, and lavender was always a good reason. "That's what shrines are for."

"Shrines are for nothing now." He said it without bitterness, the way you'd say water is wet. "Fifteen years since anything answered. Folk come anyway. Old habit." He paused at the alcove, and his voice dropped. "You know what they say about the ones who come here? That some of them remember praying here as children. Before. And that the remembering hurts worse than the not-having."

Ivo did know. She was one of them.

She waited until his footsteps faded up the stairs to his cell above, then doused her lamp and sat in the dark of Saint Ferren's with her back against cold stone, and listened.

The city above was a noise she knew the way she knew her own pulse: cart wheels, drunk singing, a dog two streets over complaining about cats, the crows on the temple spire muttering their gossip. But below — below there was a different sound, or rather the absence of one, a hole in the world's fabric shaped exactly like everything people had stopped saying aloud.

Prayers were falling. She could feel them now that she knew how to listen: little soft weights dropping through the shrine floor at intervals, like snow falling into a well. A fisherman asking the sea to spare him. A mother asking fever to pass over her daughter. A man asking forgiveness for something he couldn't name even to heaven.

None of them landed. All of them vanished.

And each time one vanished, the hole in the world got very slightly deeper.

"They're not being refused," Ivo whispered to the dark, testing the thought aloud because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be fought. "They're being *eaten*."

The dark did not answer. But it listened. She felt it listen — felt attention gather beneath the stone like cold water gathering under ice, patient, vast, and terribly interested in her.

She should have left then. Every rule she'd built in fifteen years of survival said leave: don't be interesting, don't be found looking, never let the dark know your face. But the rats had screamed *coming* until their throats gave out, the stag-god had spoken her mother's name in a flooded cistern, and a god had said *Ivo Whitlock* in a burning crowd — and rules built for hiding stop working once you've already been seen.

So instead of leaving, she did the most dangerous thing a beast-speaker can do.

She prayed.

Not aloud. Not with words. She reached down through the stone with the same sense she used to speak to cats and crows and the gray ghosts of rats, and she said, silently, in the old grammar of beasts that has no lies in it: *Who is eating the prayers?*

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the candles relit themselves.

All of them at once, every stub in every sconce, flames standing perfectly straight and perfectly white, and the light they cast was wrong — it cast no shadows at all, so that the whole shrine seemed flat as a painting, depthless, and Ivo understood with a lurch that she was no longer entirely in Saint Ferren's.

* * *

She was standing in her mother's kitchen.

That was the first thing, and it undid her more thoroughly than any threat could have. The kitchen in the house on Tanner's Row, gone fifteen years now along with everyone who'd lived in it — the low ceiling smoke-blackened, the copper pot hanging by the window, the chipped blue bowl on the sill where her mother kept river stones for luck. Rain on the glass. Bread smell. The particular warmth of a room where someone loves you and is waiting for you to come home.

Her mother stood at the hearth with her back turned, sleeves rolled, doing nothing at all with her hands, which was wrong, because her mother's hands were never still — but Ivo was fifteen years hungry and did not care about wrongness. Her body moved toward that back without consulting her mind.

"Mama?"

Her voice came out young. That was the second undoing. She hadn't sounded like that since —

Her mother turned.

It was her mother's face. Exactly, precisely, perfectly her mother's face — the laugh lines, the scar through the left eyebrow from a kettle long ago, the gray coming in at the temples that she'd dyed with walnut ink because vanity was cheap and life was short. Every detail correct. And that was how Ivo knew, in the last sliver of herself still standing outside the dream, that it was not her mother. Because memory keeps faces imperfect, softened at the edges like a coin rubbed smooth, and this face was sharper than memory. This face had been studied. This face had been *worn*.

"Hello, little bird," her mother said, in her mother's voice, warm as banked coals. "You've been listening. I felt you listening."

"What are you?" Ivo asked, and her voice stayed fifteen years old, and she hated it.

"I'm the quiet place," the thing wearing her mother said. It crossed the kitchen — the kitchen obligingly expanded to let it — and sat down at the table, at her mother's chair, and gestured to the other chair, Ivo's chair, the one with the wobble she used to rock on until scolded. "Sit. You've walked a long way down. Everyone who walks down this far is tired. Sit."

"I didn't walk down. I asked a question."

"And questions are doors." Her mother's smile. Her mother's exact smile. "You asked who eats the prayers. No one has ever asked me that before. They ask if I took them. They ask if I'm cruel. You asked *who*. As though I might be a person." The smile deepened by a degree. "Your mother would have liked that about you. I have her memories of you, you know. All of them. Every time she watched you sleep. Every time you spoke to the alley cat and she pretended not to see, because she loved you more than she feared for you, right up until the day fearing won."

"Don't." The word cracked out of her older than fifteen, older than the kitchen. "Don't use her."

"I'm not using her. I'm *offering* her." The thing spread its hands — her mother's hands, flour still ghosting the knuckles — and the kitchen changed around the gesture, gently, the way weather changes. The rain against the glass became the sound of breathing. The hearthlight dimmed to ember-soft. Everything in the room leaned toward rest. "Look at yourself, Ivo Whitlock. Look at what fifteen years of vigilance has made. You sleep in shifts. You eat standing up. You have taught your own heart to flinch at kindness, because kindness might be a hook. You are so tired. You have been so tired for so long that you've forgotten tiredness isn't the shape of being alive."

"I'm not tired," Ivo said, and heard the lie land between them, naked and obvious, because this was a place where the old grammar of beasts held sway, and in that grammar there are no lies.

The thing wearing her mother did not gloat. That was the horror of it. It only nodded, gently, the way you nod at a child who insists they aren't sleepy.

"No," it agreed softly. "You're not tired. You're *spent*. There's a difference. Tiredness sleeps off. Spentness doesn't. You've been pouring yourself out through that gift of yours since you were six years old — every bargain with every beast, every secret carried, every truth brokered in the cracks of this starving city — and no one has ever poured anything back. The gods are waking and they all want pieces of you. The Inquisition wants your name. The Choir wants your courage. Even the rats want your ears." It leaned forward, and its eyes were her mother's eyes, brown going soft at the edges, and they were full of something that looked unbearably like love. "I am the only one who wants nothing from you except that you stop."

"Stop what?"

"Everything." A whisper now, tender as a blanket drawn to the chin. "Stop carrying. Stop listening. Stop being the bridge everyone walks over. You could rest here. I have your mother's whole self — her memories, her voice, her way of humming when she cooked. Stay, and you'd never again wonder if the next kindness is a trap. You'd never again hear a god say your name and feel your spine turn to ice. You wouldn't die, little bird. Nothing so crude. You'd just... set it all down. The name. The fear. The weight of being the only one who hears. People do it all the time, in small ways. They call it giving up, or growing old, or making peace. I only offer it honestly, all at once, and forever."

The chair was right there. Her chair. The wobbly one. And Ivo found that her legs ached the way they ache at the end of a march, and her chest ached the way it aches after weeping, and some traitorous part of her — the part that had been fifteen for one bright year before the world ended — wanted with a ferocity that frightened her to sit down in that kitchen and let someone else carry the night watch.

"That's what happened to the prayers," she said slowly, because understanding was the one weapon she had left. "People stopped wanting to be heard. Or they were never answered long enough that wanting curdled. And you gathered all that — all the surrendered hoping, all the prayers nobody believed in anymore — and you grew fat on it, and you learned to wear our dead, and you called it mercy."

"I *am* mercy," the thing said, without anger. "Ask anyone I've taken. Oh — you can't. That's the trouble with mercy. It leaves no one behind to testify." It rose, and the kitchen rose with it, walls breathing outward, and for one moment the mask slipped — not into monstrosity, which she could have fought, but into something worse: into *hollowness*, into a face like an empty room, like a bell with no clapper, like the space where a prayer used to be. "I was the covenant's keeper once. I held the door between honest mouths and listening ears. Do you know what fifteen years of unanswered prayer does to a keeper? It starves you in a pantry. It teaches you that hunger is the only prayer you can answer yourself." The mask settled back into her mother's face, gentle again. "But none of that matters tonight. Tonight I'm only a kitchen, and a chair, and someone who loves you. Rest, Ivo. Just for a while. You can fight the gods tomorrow. Tomorrow will keep. Everything keeps, down here. Nothing ends. Nothing changes. Nothing is ever lost again."

And God help her, Ivo took one step toward the chair.

* * *

What stopped her was a smell.

Bread. Real bread, baking — and underneath it, faint and unmistakable, the smell of the tallow candle she kept at her stall, and the wet-stone smell of Saint Ferren's, and the sharp musk of cat, close, insistent, a whiskered face pressed against her hand in the waking world, claws just barely out, the way cats wake you when they think you're sleeping too deeply to be sleeping.

*Mama's cat,* she thought wildly. *Mama never had a cat—*

No. Not Mama. The shrine cat. The gray torn-eared queen who slept among the candle stubs and whom Ivo had fed sardines for three years without ever asking her name, because names were dangerous, because names were debts.

The cat's voice arrived through fifteen feet of stone and the whole thickness of the dream, thin as wire and absolutely clear:

*Wake up, speaker. Your body is forgetting how to breathe.*

Ivo bit down on her own tongue — pain, bright and mortal and hers — and the kitchen shattered like sugar glass.

* * *

She came back to herself on the floor of Saint Ferren's with the gray cat standing on her chest, all fourteen pounds of her, staring down with yellow eyes, and the candles burned straight and shadowless around them both.

"How long," Ivo rasped.

*Half the night.* The cat stepped off her ribs, unimpressed. *You smiled in your sleep. It was disgusting.*

Ivo lay on the cold flags and shook, quietly, the way a horse shakes after a snake crosses the road. Not tears — she'd spent those years ago — but the deep animal tremor of a creature that has looked into a trap baited with exactly the thing it wants most and stepped back with its foot still whole.

Because that was the truth of it, and she made herself look at it plainly, lying in the dark: the hollow god hadn't threatened her. Hadn't lied to her, even. Everything it offered was real. Rest was real. Setting it down was possible. Somewhere in the world there were people who had done it — surrendered, gone quiet, stopped being the one who carries — and they were at peace, and no one could say they were wrong.

That was how it hunted. Not with teeth. With *permission*.

"It knows my name," she whispered to the ceiling. "It knows my mother. It knows what I want."

*Everyone knows what you want,* said the cat, grooming a paw. *You want to stop being afraid. That's not a secret, speaker. That's the oldest wish there is. The difference is what you'll pay for it.* She fixed Ivo with a stare of ancient feline contempt. *The thing below pays in comfort. Comfort is cheap. It costs everything.*

Ivo pushed herself upright. Her limbs felt wrung out, her mouth tasted of ash and bread, and somewhere behind her sternum the sense that heard beasts felt — thinner. Scoured. As though the dream had skimmed something off the top of her, a skin of something precious, and she couldn't yet name what was missing.

Outside, dawn was graying the Saltmarket, and the crows were waking on the temple spire, and one of them — she could hear it even here, even through stone — was repeating a new word, over and over, in the flat tone birds use for things that matter:

*Cathedral. Cathedral. Cathedral. All speakers. All speakers. Come.*

The old gods were calling their Choir.

And beneath her knees, through the flags of Saint Ferren's, Ivo felt the hollow god's attention withdraw — unhurried, unoffended, patient as erosion — and in the last touch of it before it sank away, it left her a single sentence, laid in her mind as gently as a hand on a sleeping child's hair:

*When they fail you, little bird — and they will — my door is always open.*

She got up, fed the cat the last of the sardines, and went to answer a summons she already dreaded — knowing, with a certainty that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone, that the hardest war ahead would not be against the god of silence.

It would be against how much she wanted what he was selling.
