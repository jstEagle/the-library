## 11. Voss Closes In

They came for the candlemaker first.

Ivo heard it before she saw it — the way news traveled through Vellamark's beasts faster than any crier could run. The gulls on her roofline went up in a ragged chorus at dawn, and the old tomcat who slept in her window told her, in his dry sandpaper voice, *Men in grey coats took the wax-woman from her shop. She did not scream. She walked out between them like a lady going to church.*

Maren. Maren of the tallow and wicks, who spoke to moths and had once wept over a drowned moth as if it were her own child. Ivo stood at her window with her hands pressed flat against the cold glass and watched the street below, empty now, and felt the fear arrive not as a spike but as a slow flooding, the way water finds every seam in a wall.

*She didn't run,* the cat observed, washing one paw. *That's either courage or stupidity.*

*It's love,* Ivo said. *She wouldn't leave her shop. Her whole life is in that shop.*

*Then she's a fool,* said the cat, who had survived eleven years on Vellamark's streets by being exactly that kind of honest. *Are you going to be a fool too?*

Ivo didn't answer. She was already counting what she'd need if she had to vanish — and hating herself for how short the list was, after fifteen years of pretending she would never need it.

* * *

The second arrest came at noon, and this time Ivo was close enough to see it.

She had gone to the fish market because the eels had been muttering all morning about men in grey, and she wanted to see with her own eyes. She got more than she wanted. They came down Cordwainer's Row six abreast, boots striking stone in unison, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around a keel — not willingly, but because refusing would mean touching them.

In the middle of them walked Hollis Fen.

Hollis, who talked to pigeons and fed them from his own bread ration even in the hungry years. Hollis, who was seventy years old and moved like a man made of folded paper. He wasn't struggling. He was looking at the crowd, at each face in turn, and Ivo understood with a sick lurch that he was memorizing them — not to name names later, but so that he would remember what ordinary people looked like, wherever they were taking him.

His eyes found hers across the market square. He knew. Of course he knew; the pigeons would have told him long ago who else in Vellamark shared his affliction. He looked at her for one long second, and then he smiled — small, crooked, almost apologetic — and shook his head once.

*Don't,* the gesture said. *Don't do anything foolish on my account.*

Then the grey coats swallowed him, and the crowd closed behind them like water over a dropped stone, and everyone went back to their haggling because that was what survival looked like in Vellamark: you watched a man be taken and you bought your mackerel and you told yourself there was nothing you could have done.

Ivo bought no mackerel. She walked home with her heart hammering out a single word against her ribs: *list*. Voss had said she kept a list. And now the list was being read aloud, two names at a time, and Ivo's own name was written somewhere in that careful clerk's hand, waiting its turn like a debt coming due.

* * *

The summons came that evening, and it came politely, which was somehow worse than chains would have been.

A boy in Inquisitorial grey knocked at her door — a boy no older than fourteen, with a face like a closed ledger — and handed her a card of heavy cream stock. On it, in beautiful copperplate: *Inquisitor Dara Voss requests the pleasure of Mistress Whitlock's company. Tomorrow, the ninth hour. No escort will be provided. You may come freely.*

*You may come freely.* As if freedom were a thing anyone in Vellamark still possessed. As if the absence of an escort weren't itself a message: *we know you'll come. We don't need to drag you.*

The cat read the card upside down while she held it — he couldn't read, but he liked the smell of the ink — and asked, *Will you go?*

*I have to.*

*No,* said the cat. *You don't. There are boats. There are roads. Rats travel light.*

*And leave Maren and Hollis to whatever's coming? Leave the Choir to face Voss alone?* She set the card on the table and stared at it until the letters blurred. *Besides. If I run, I confirm everything. A woman with nothing to hide doesn't flee at midnight.*

*A woman with something to hide doesn't walk into the Inquisition's house at nine in the morning,* the cat countered. *But then, cats and women rarely agree on strategy.*

She slept badly, which was to say she dreamed of her mother's face wearing someone else's smile, and woke twice with the taste of ash in her mouth.

* * *

The Inquisition's chapter house had been a granary before the Sundering, and it still smelled faintly of grain — a hundred years of stored wheat ghosting through the stone, overlaid now with ink and lamp oil and the particular antiseptic bite of lime wash. Ivo noticed these things because noticing was how she survived; when terror comes, catalog the room. Count the exits. Name the smells. A mind busy with inventory has less room for panic.

Voss received her in a small room with one window, high and barred, through which morning light fell in a dusty column. There was a table. Two chairs. A pot of tea, steaming, with two cups.

Two cups.

"Sit, please," Voss said. She was not in uniform today — just a plain grey dress, her dark hair pinned simply, her hands bare. Without the coat and insignia she might have been a schoolmistress, or a widow, or anyone. That was the trick of her, Ivo thought. Dara Voss wore power the way other people wore perfume: invisibly, until you were already breathing it.

Ivo sat. Voss poured the tea herself, and slid the cup across, and waited until Ivo had taken a sip — she noted that; she noted everything — before she began.

"Do you know why I became an Inquisitor?"

"I imagine it pays better than most work."

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth. Not quite a smile. "It pays terribly. No — I became an Inquisitor because when I was nine years old, my grandmother died, and I wanted to ask her where she'd hidden the deed to the house. And I discovered that dead things answer me, if I ask correctly." She said it flatly, without drama, the way one reports weather. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Mistress Whitlock?"

Ivo kept her face still. Inside, everything lurched. *The dead.* Voss heard the dead. The Inquisitor with clean hands had a gift of her own, buried under a lifetime of lime wash and procedure — and she was confessing it to Ivo across a tea table, which meant either extraordinary trust or an extraordinarily elaborate trap.

"That must have been frightening," Ivo said carefully. "For a child."

"It was clarifying. It taught me that the world is full of things that speak, and that most people cannot hear them, and that the ones who can are either destroyed by the state or employed by it." Voss folded her hands. "There is no third option. I've spent twenty years looking for one."

"And have you found it?"

"No." For a moment — half a heartbeat — something moved behind Voss's eyes, something tired and old and horribly familiar. Then it was gone, filed away, and the Inquisitor sat in its place. "Which brings us to business."

She opened a folder on the table. Ivo made herself look at it. Photographs — the new silver-plate process, expensive — and beneath them, pages of neat script.

"Maren Ostwick, chandler. Taken yesterday morning. Cooperative. Confessed within the hour to 'conversing with insects,' though she wept throughout and recanted twice." Voss turned a page. "Hollis Fen, retired ropemaker. Taken yesterday noon. Has confessed to nothing, said nothing, and asked only that we feed his pigeons. He is," and here her voice did something almost gentle, "seventy-one years old, and he is making my clerks very frustrated."

"Because he won't talk?"

"Because he keeps asking *them* questions. About their mothers." Voss closed the folder. "You understand the position I'm in."

"I'm not sure I do."

"The law requires me to hold them. The law requires me to determine whether they bear proscribed gifts. The law requires me, if they do, to process them." Each sentence landed like a coin on a counter. "The law does not require me to enjoy it. And the law, Mistress Whitlock, has recently become... complicated."

"Complicated."

"Gods are answering prayers again. Shrines are burning. Rats are screaming words in the streets. My superiors want explanations, and the explanations they want involve lists of names, and trials, and examples made." Voss leaned forward slightly. "I am offering you a different explanation."

Here it was. Ivo wrapped her hands around the teacup because they wanted to shake, and warmth was easier to fake than calm. "Which is?"

"You give me the others. The ones who were called to the cathedral — yes, I know about the cathedral; I've known since the week after it happened, I have ears in places you'd find embarrassing. You give me their names, their trades, their habits, their haunts. In exchange, Maren and Hollis are released into my personal custody — quietly, unofficially, as witnesses too confused to prosecute. And your own name comes off my list." Voss's voice stayed level, courteous, terrible. "You keep your shop. You keep your life. You keep your freedom. Three lives preserved for the price of a few words. It is, I think, the cheapest transaction you will ever be offered."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, a gull cried against the barred window, and Ivo heard what it said — *eggs, eggs, the cliffs are far, the wind is wrong* — and thought absurdly that even the birds' problems were simpler than hers.

"You're asking me to betray twelve people," she said.

"I'm asking you to save three."

"They're the same thing."

"Yes," Voss agreed. "They usually are. That's what makes it interesting." She turned the folder around and pushed it toward Ivo. On top lay a photograph of a woman Ivo didn't know — broad-faced, middle-aged, a baker perhaps by the flour dust on her collar. "Sister Anselm. Taken this morning, an hour before I sent for you. That makes four. By tomorrow there will be six. I don't choose the pace, Mistress Whitlock. I only choose whether some of them walk free."

And there it was, laid out on cream paper with copperplate precision: the arithmetic of betrayal, dressed as mercy. Ivo looked at the photograph and thought about Hollis asking clerks about their mothers. Thought about Maren weeping over a moth. Thought about Tam Ossler's sharp grin and the storm-god's secret humming in his bones, about the threshold god's patient voice, about a stag drowning in a cistern who had told her *your mother bargained for you before you were born.*

Thought about her mother's face, hollowed out and offered to her in dreams like a bowl of rest.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a stone at the bottom of a well, she thought about her own name — the true one, the buried one, the one she had not spoken aloud in fifteen years, the one that lived in her chest like a coal banked under ash. If she gave the Choir to Voss, she could keep that name forever. Keep the shop, the charms, the careful small life. Keep being Ivo Whitlock, who was nobody, who was safe.

"Take your time," Voss said. "But not much of it."

Ivo lifted her eyes. "I'll tell you what I know," she said.

Voss went very still. It was the stillness of a hunter who has heard the trap-spring click — hope and suspicion in equal measure. "Go on."

"There were thirteen of us summoned to the cathedral." Ivo let her voice take on the flatness of confession, the weariness of a woman giving up. "But you should know the truth of it: most of us aren't what you think. Half the people called that night were frauds. People who'd heard rumors the gods were back and wanted a piece of it. Fortune-tellers, faith-healers, a man who claimed he could talk to his dead wife — he couldn't; he was a con artist working the ferry docks." She shook her head slowly. "The real ones — there were only ever a handful, and I can't tell you who they were, because I don't know. We were kept apart. Hooded, some of us. Called by voice, not by name."

"Liar," Voss said softly.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water.

And Ivo felt it happen — felt the lie leave her mouth and strike something inside her that she hadn't known was listening. It was not pain, exactly. It was subtraction. Like a tooth pulled from the jaw: the socket remains, the tongue goes probing into the absence, and the absence aches with a shape that used to be full.

Somewhere in the deep architecture of her gift, a door closed.

She didn't know which door. Not yet. But she felt the draft of it, the sudden thinning of a current that had run through her since childhood — the constant low murmur of the world's beasts, the background choir she had never once been without. It was still there. But quieter. Dimmer. As if someone had carried away one instrument from an orchestra mid-song, and the music continued, and yet the music was less.

Voss was watching her. Watching her the way a jeweler watches a stone under lamplight, turning it, hunting the flaw. "Your pupils just changed," she said. "Interesting. Whatever you're doing right now — whatever you're holding back — it has a cost, doesn't it? I can see it costing you."

"I told you the truth," Ivo said, and the second lie came easier, which frightened her more than the first, and cost her another door — she felt this one distinctly, a cold snap somewhere behind her sternum, like ice cracking on a pond. The murmur of the beasts thinned again. Somewhere out in the city, a dog's voice went silent to her. A rat's chatter dropped below the waterline of hearing. Small losses. Permanent ones. She could feel their permanence the way you feel a scar forming.

"Thirteen summoned," Voss repeated. "Mostly frauds. Kept apart. Hooded."

"Some of us," Ivo said, and stopped, because the third lie would not come. It rose in her throat and met something there — not a wall, but a weight, vast and patient and *listening*, something older than the Inquisition, older than the covenant, something woven into the fabric of honest speech since the world was young. The god of thresholds had told them: *the covenant binds gods to hear honest prayer. It binds mortals to speak honestly to be heard.* And Ivo understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that her gift itself was a form of prayer — that every word she spoke to beasts passed through the same ancient machinery, and the machinery had begun, tonight, to collect its toll.

She could lie to Dara Voss. She could lie to the Inquisition, to the law, to the whole grey apparatus of human fear.

But she could not lie through her gift. And her gift, apparently, was listening to everything she said.

"I can see you're deciding how much more to tell me," Voss said. She rose, collected the folder, straightened its edges with two precise taps against the table. "So let me make the decision easier. You have until tomorrow night. If I haven't heard from you, Maren Ostwick goes to processing. Hollis follows. And I begin arresting the rest of the list alphabetically, which means, Mistress Whitlock—" she paused at the door, hand on the latch, and for one unguarded moment her voice lost its polish entirely, "—it means I come for you last, and I will hate every minute of it, and I will do it anyway. Because that's what the law is. That's what I am."

She opened the door. Grey corridor. Grey light.

"One more thing," Voss said, not turning around. "Whatever you're protecting them from — whatever you think happens in processing — you should know that I have seen what comes out of the deep cells. And you're right to be afraid. Just... be afraid accurately."

Then she was gone, and Ivo sat alone in the little room with the cooling tea and the column of dusty light, taking inventory of herself.

She flexed her fingers. Pressed her palms to the table. Felt for the murmur.

It answered — thinner. A sparrow on the sill outside spoke to her of crumbs and territory, and its voice came to her clear as ever, but beyond it, where there should have been the great rolling surf of the city's animal life — ten thousand dogs and rats and horses and gulls all murmuring at once — there was now a gap. A hole shaped like a hound. A hole shaped like a stable of horses. Small absences, scattered through the music, permanent as pulled teeth.

*That's the price,* she thought. *That's the invoice attached to the hope.* Loyalty wasn't a feeling. It was a currency, and she had just paid out a fistful of her own soul to buy twelve strangers another day.

She walked home through streets that sounded, for the first time in her life, partially silent. At her door, the old tomcat sat waiting, and when she knelt to him his voice reached her fine — *you smell of ink and fear and bad decisions* — and she laughed, or made a sound like laughing, and scratched his ears.

*What did it cost?* he asked, because cats always know.

*Some of them,* she said. *Some of the voices. I lied to protect the Choir, and the covenant took payment out of my hide.*

The cat considered this with his tail curled around his feet. *Was it worth it?*

*I don't know yet.*

*Wrong answer,* said the cat. *The correct answer is always yes, and then you spend the rest of your life finding out why.*

That night she lit no lamps. She sat in the dark of her shop among the harmless charms — the luck-knots and the sleep-sachets, the little braided promises of an ordinary life — and she listened to the diminished ocean of the city's beasts, mapping her losses one by one. The hounds of Tanner's Row: gone. The carthorses of the south gate: gone. The bats under the eaves of the cathedral itself: gone, and that one frightened her worst, because the bats had been her eyes in the Choir's meeting place, and now that window was shut.

And then, near midnight, as she was drifting into exhausted half-sleep, the remaining voices changed.

All at once. Every beast still audible to her — sparrows, gulls, the tomcat, the rats in the walls, the eels far off in the river mud — fell silent together, in the same instant, as if a conductor had raised a hand.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere, from under the floorboards and above the roof tiles and inside her own chest, they spoke. All of them. One word, in the voice of every creature that still trusted her:

*"Under."*

The word rolled through the dark like thunder through a valley. The rats in her walls repeated it, urgent, trembling: *under, under, under.* The gulls wheeled crying it against the moon. Even the tomcat had risen, hackles lifted, staring at the floor as though the boards might open.

Ivo sat up in the dark, her remaining gift ringing like a struck bell, and understood that the city beneath the city — the deep places, the old places, the root of the covenant itself — had begun to move.

And something down there knew her name.
