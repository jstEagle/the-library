## 12. The Market of Small Gods

The way down began behind a tannery on Sallow Row, where the smell was so thick it could have been sold by the pound.

Ivo had known about the door for years without ever using it — every charm-seller in Vellamark knew about it, the way sailors knew about wrecks, the way children knew about the dark under beds. You learned its location and you swore never to go. The door itself was nothing: a plank set into the tannery's foundation wall, iron-banded, marked with a brand so old no one remembered what god it had once honored. The trick was not finding it. The trick was knocking correctly.

Three knocks, then a pause long enough to say a prayer you didn't believe in, then two more.

She stood in the reek of rendered hides with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she made herself knock.

The pause was the worst part. In that pause she heard everything: the tannery vats gurgling behind the wall, the rats in the rafters above her — silent now, still silent, three days silent except when they screamed *coming* — and beneath all of it, faint as a pulse through stone, the sound of the market waking below. Not footsteps. Not voices. A kind of rustling, like a city of small things turning over in their sleep.

The plank swung inward on darkness and the smell of cold river water.

"You're late," said the door.

Doors in the Market of Small Gods talked. She should have been prepared for that. She wasn't.

"I didn't know there was an appointed hour."

"There is always an appointed hour," said the door, in a voice like a hinge remembering oil. "You simply hadn't arrived at yours yet." It opened wider. "Mind the third step. It minds you back."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the city had depth to give them. Ivo counted two hundred steps and then stopped counting, because counting was a way of measuring how far she was putting herself beyond help, and some knowledge was better left unmeasured. The walls sweated. Torchlight came from nowhere — or rather, from small flames that lived in niches like captive birds, guttering when she passed, leaning toward her the way flowers lean toward sun, and she understood with a lurch that they were leaning toward what was left of her gift, the way dogs lean toward anyone who smells of home.

She tried not to think about how little of it was left.

Three days ago she had lied to Dara Voss — lied smoothly, beautifully, with a god of thresholds standing witness inside her skin — and the lie had gone out of her mouth like a coin from a purse, and something had gone with it. She had woken the next morning and spoken to the gulls on her roof, the way she'd spoken to gulls since she was six years old, and the gulls had looked at her with flat yellow eyes and seen only a woman. A stranger. Nothing.

One bird, an old herring gull with a scarred beak, had cocked its head and made a sound that might have been pity.

She had sat down on the roof tiles and shaken until the sun came fully up.

That was why she was here. Not courage. Not destiny. Desperation wearing courage's coat, which was the only kind of courage she had ever owned.

The stairs ended at a curtain of hanging chains, thousands of them, thin as hair and black with age, and when she pushed through they did not clatter. They chimed. Each link gave off one clear note, and the notes braided together into something almost like speech, almost like a word she almost knew, and then she was through, and the Market of Small Gods opened around her like a lung inhaling.

It was built in a cavern that could not have fit under Vellamark, which meant it did not fit under Vellamark, which meant the geometry here was a courtesy rather than a fact. Stalls of black wood and bone crowded a central channel where a river ran uphill. Lanterns burned every color fire was not supposed to burn. And everywhere, everywhere, the vendors.

They were small. That was the first thing. The stories said gods, and these were not gods; these were what remained when gods shed skins, or what grew in the cracks where divinity had leaked, or perhaps what gods dreamed when they dreamed small. A thing like a salamander made of banked coals sold warmth against winter, one ember per coin. A woman whose face was a blank oval of river clay sold forgetfulness by the drop. Something that was mostly teeth and mostly polite sold luck, and something that was mostly shadow sold the opposite of luck, and both stalls seemed to do steady trade.

"First time?" said a voice at her elbow.

Ivo flinched. The speaker was a creature the size of a child's doll, gray and wrinkled as an elephant's knee, with eyes like two drops of tar. It sat on a barrel of nails. Everything here sat on barrels of nails; she would learn later that the nails were a ward, though against what, no vendor would say.

"Yes," she said.

"Mm. You smell of beast-speech. Faded." The tar eyes blinked slowly. "Spent too much truth telling a lie. Classic. Half the people who come down here are you."

"I need—" Her voice caught. She started again, quieter. "I need to buy back what I lost. Or something like it. A mending."

The gray thing laughed, and the laugh sounded like sand pouring. "Buy back. Oh, sweet. Nobody buys back. Down here we don't mend, we *trade*. You want your gift loud again, you pay in the only coin that matters." It leaned forward, and its tar eyes reflected her face back at her, tiny and upside down. "Yourself. Piece by piece. Everyone pays themselves. That's the whole market, girl. It's just people selling bits of their souls to small gods who are very good at pretending the price is reasonable."

"Then I'll pay it."

"Will you." It hopped off the barrel, joints crackling like green wood in fire. "Come on, then. You want the big stalls, the ones near the uphill river. But walk slow. Look at everything. The Market punishes hurry, and it *loves* a customer who doesn't read the label."

* * *

She walked slow. She looked at everything.

She saw a young man paying a coal-salamander for warmth and watched him hand over, not coins, but the memory of his father's laugh — she saw it leave him, saw it as a faint gold thread drawn out of his open mouth, and she saw his face afterward: relieved and lighter and somehow smaller, a house with one room bricked shut. He walked away smiling. That was the horror of it. They all walked away smiling.

She saw a woman buy courage and pay with the taste of her grandmother's bread. She saw a boy sell his fear of drowning and come away unable to remember why he'd ever loved the river. Every stall was a small amputation performed with perfect tenderness. The vendors were gentle. That was the second horror. Nothing here was cruel. Cruelty would have been easier to refuse.

At the river's edge, where the water climbed the air in a slow silver rope, the big stalls stood. She passed a spirit that traded in names — not hers, never hers, she checked twice, three times, the old reflex — and a spirit that sold second chances at a markup she couldn't calculate, and a spirit that was only a voice, selling apologies to the dead, and she made herself keep walking past that one, though her feet argued.

Because she knew what she had come to spend. She had known since the roof, since the gull's pitying eye. There was only one currency in her purse heavy enough to buy back a piece of her soul.

The stall at the end of the row was the smallest. A table of driftwood. Behind it sat a figure wrapped in gray wool, faceless but for two patient hands folded on the table, and between those hands lay a shallow dish of water so still it looked solid.

"Beast-speaker," it said. Its voice came from the dish, from the water, soft as rain on a window. "Or what remains of one. Sit."

She sat. The stool was warm, which frightened her more than cold would have.

"What do you sell?"

"Everything the others sell. But I deal in *faces*." The woolen shape tilted. "Memories of faces, specifically. They're dense coin. A face holds a whole person folded small. Pay me a face and I can give you back a great deal of gift."

Ivo's hands went cold in her lap. "Which face?"

"That's your choice. That's always the cruelty of it. We never take what you offer freely. We make you choose." The dish rippled, though nothing touched it. "Though I confess I already know what you've brought. You've been carrying it up front since you walked in, the way a man carries his last coin in his fist. Your mother. The real one. Not the face you've rebuilt from grief and bad light and fifteen years of forgetting. The true one."

The cavern noise fell away. Or she fell away from it.

"How do you—"

"Water remembers faces," said the vendor simply. "All water. Every tear you cried over her went somewhere, child. Some of it came here."

Ivo looked into the dish. The water showed her a face — her mother's face, true and whole, the strong jaw and the crooked smile and the small scar over the left brow that Ivo had forgotten she'd forgotten — and the sight of it hit her like a blow to the chest. Fifteen years, and the real face had been eroding all that time, sanded down by retelling, until what she carried was a sketch of a sketch. This was the original. This was the last true copy in the world, and it lived in a dish in a hole under the world, and if she paid it away—

"If I give you this," Ivo said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "what do I get?"

"Loudness," said the vendor. "The beasts will hear you again. Not all of what you were — a lie told before a god leaves a scar, and scars don't lift — but enough. Enough to matter, in the days coming. And they are coming days, beast-speaker. Even the small gods feel it. Even we are afraid." The hands unfolded, palms up, empty. Waiting. "But understand the terms. You will not lose her entirely. You will lose the *true* face. What remains will be the sketch. You'll remember that you loved her. You won't quite remember what made loving her inevitable. Grief without its reason. A lock without a key, hung on a door you can't find."

"And I'll agree anyway," Ivo said quietly. "That's the third horror, isn't it. Everyone agrees anyway."

"Everyone agrees anyway," the vendor agreed, gently.

She thought of her mother's hands kneading bread. She thought of the night soldiers came, of the word *run*, of fifteen years of running. She thought of the gull looking through her as if she were glass.

And she thought — this was the thought that decided her, and she hated it — that she could not afford to be half a bridge. The Choir needed her whole. The city needed her whole. Somewhere beneath the cathedral something vast and hungry was hoarding prayers, and the rats were screaming *coming*, and a woman with a faded gift was a woman who would fail at the moment it mattered most. Survival had always meant spending pieces of herself. This was only the largest bill yet.

"The face," Ivo said. "Take the face."

"Breathe out," said the vendor. "And don't close your eyes. We don't allow closing eyes. It's kinder if you watch. Kinder later, when you try to remember what you lost."

She breathed out. She watched.

The memory rose in her like water rising in a well — she felt it lift, felt the true face shimmer up through her, and it did not hurt exactly; it was worse than hurt, it was *relief*, the relief of setting down a weight carried so long the shoulders had grown around it. The face passed out of her mouth as a breath of silver, drifted across the table, and settled onto the surface of the dish.

For one instant the water held her mother perfectly — the crooked smile, the scar, the eyes that had looked at young Ivo with a love so fierce it had felt like a warning —

—and then the image sank, and smoothed, and was only water.

Ivo sat very still. She tested the place where the memory had been and found smoothness, a healed-over absence, the sketch remaining and the original gone. She could still see her mother. She could no longer see *her*. The difference was a room she could no longer enter, standing in a house she could no longer find.

"Grief without its reason," the vendor said softly, and there was something in its voice that might, in a larger being, have been called sorrow. "As promised."

Warmth moved up through Ivo then, starting in her sternum, spreading along her arms like dawn along a ridge. The gift, returning — diminished, scarred, but *loud*. She could feel the rats of Vellamark above her, hundreds of hearts beating in the dark. She could feel the crows.

The crows were already awake. The crows were already watching her.

"Go," said the vendor, folding its hands again. "Spend yourself carefully. You have less self left than you think, and more road than you know."

* * *

She was halfway back up the endless stairs when the crows spoke.

Not aloud. Not in words. It came up through the returned gift like a current through wire — a chorus, dozens of voices braided into one, and they were not screaming *coming* the way the rats screamed it. They were saying something else. Something older.

*Ivo,* the crows said. *Ivo, Ivo, Ivo.*

Except that was not what they said. The sound they made shaped itself around a different name, a longer one, a name with roots in it, and the strange thing — the thing that stopped her dead on the two-hundredth step with the torch-flames leaning toward her like a congregation — was that the name fit. The way a key fits. The way her mother's true face had fit.

They had known it all along, she realized. All fifteen years. Every crow on every rooftop in Vellamark had been carrying it for her, keeping it safe the way she kept charms safe for other people, and now that her hearing was restored she could finally hear what they had been saying all along, every morning, every dusk, in the chorus of the city's black wings:

*We have kept it for you. We have kept it for you. Come and take it back.*
