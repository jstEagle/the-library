## 13. What the Beasts Call Her

The crows came for her at dawn, and they came all at once.

Ivo had opened her shutters to the grey light over the tannery roofs out of habit more than hope — fifteen years of mornings had taught her that Vellamark's sky was a ledger of debts, each day owing the last — and the sound hit her before the cold did. Not the usual argumentative racket of the city's carrion-birds, squabbling over a gutter-corpse or a dropped sausage. This was structured. Deliberate. A hundred black throats producing one sound.

Her name.

Not *Ivo*. Not *Whitlock*, not *charm-seller*, not any of the safe, hollow syllables she wore like a second skin. The other one. The true one. The word her mother had breathed into her ear in a kitchen that no longer existed, the word she had not spoken aloud since she was nine years old, since the night her friend Marta's loose mouth had cost her family everything.

It came down off the rooftops like a stone dropped into still water, and every hair on Ivo's arms rose to meet it.

She stood in the window with her hands gripping the sill until the wood bit back, and she listened to a hundred crows say the unsayable thing, over and over, in unison, with the terrible patience of creatures who have rehearsed.

*Come,* they said then, breaking their chorus at last, wings scattering like torn paper against the pale sky. *Come and take it back.*

---

She did not go immediately. That was the discipline, and the discipline was all she had left. She banked her fire, fed the kettle, laid out her charms on the morning cloth — the little knotted cords and river-stones and bottles of ash-water that paid her rent and kept her invisible — and she worked through her first three customers of the day with steady hands while the crows perched along her eaves and watched her through the glass with eyes like wet ink.

Old Bren the cooper wanted a charm against damp in his stores. She gave him one and charged him fairly and did not tell him that the real cure was moving his barrels off the north wall, because the rats had told her about the wall two winters ago and some knowledge was cheaper kept than sold. A girl of maybe twelve bought a luck-knot for her brother going down the mines. Ivo tied it herself, murmuring nothing, letting the knot do its plain honest work, and when the girl asked if it would really help, Ivo said, "He should carry it where he can touch it," which was true, and truth was the only currency she permitted herself anymore. Not after the lie to Voss. Not after feeling the gift tear like wet paper in her chest, a whole octave of the world's voices gone suddenly mute.

The beasts were quieter now. That was the wound under the wound. Where once the city had hummed to her — dogs grumbling their opinions, pigeons gossiping like washerwomen, the great slow thoughts of carthorses rolling past like fog — there was now a thinness, a muffled quality, as though she heard them through a wall. The rats still spoke, but flatly, without their old sly music. The cat who lived under her stairs had taken to looking at her with something close to pity.

But the crows. The crows came through clear as bells.

"Tell me why," she said to them, when the shop was empty and the door was barred. They crowded the sill outside, a parliament of black feathers, and the eldest among them — a ragged veteran missing an eye, whom she had known for years without ever learning his history — hopped forward and fixed her with his good gaze.

"We never learned your name," he said, in the croaking grammar of crow-thought that only she could hear. "We were given it."

"Given." Her voice came out strange. "By whom?"

"By her." The old crow tilted his head. "The woman who walked the river road at dusk. The one who smelled of bread and thunderstorms. She came to us the winter you were born — no. Before. She came to us when she knew what you would be, and she made us a bargain, the way your kind makes bargains, except hers was clean. *Keep this word for my daughter,* she said. *Keep it where no man can reach it and no friend can sell it. Keep it until the day keeping it does her more harm than good.*"

The kettle began to whistle and Ivo did not move. Steam curled up past her face and she let it scald, faintly, because pain was simple and this was not.

"My mother," she said. "Spoke to crows."

"Your mother spoke to everything," the crow said. "You think you invented the listening? Child, you are the echo of a much older voice. We have known your name since before you could walk. We have carried it through fifteen years of winters, through the Sundering's silence, through the Inquisition's lists. Do you know how heavy a true name is? It is heavier than a stolen lamb. Heavier than a dead fledgling. We carried it anyway, because we promised, and crows keep promises longer than mountains keep their shapes."

"Why?" The question tore out of her raw. "Why would she give it to *you* and not leave it in me?"

"Because names in the body can be tortured out," the crow said simply. "Names in the body can be tricked out by a smiling friend on a summer afternoon. She had seen it happen. She knew what was coming for you — she always knew things coming — so she took the most dangerous thing you owned and she hid it in the one place the Inquisition never thinks to look." His good eye glittered. "In us. In the ignored ones. In the scavengers and the vermin and the small dark lives that your kind walks past every day without seeing."

Ivo sat down slowly on her stool among her harmless charms, and the shop seemed to tilt around her, fifteen years of assumptions sliding like goods off a broken shelf.

All those years she had believed the silence around her name was her own achievement. Her own iron discipline, her own careful erasure, her own grief calcified into survival. She had built a fortress out of never-speaking and called it strength, and all the while —

"You kept it," she whispered. "All this time. You knew me. Every charm I sold, every market day, every time I walked past your roosts — you knew exactly who I was."

"We knew," said the crows, all together now, a hundred voices braided into one. "We watched you grow from a frightened cub into a cunning one. We grieved when your gift tore. We argued — oh, how we argued — about whether the day had come to give it back. And then the hollow god stirred, and the prayers began to burn, and the stag walked up out of the drowned places, and we knew: the day has come. The harm of keeping has outweighed the harm of telling."

* * *

She walked out of the city that afternoon without meaning to, following the crows flight by flight, north and west toward the high pasture-land above Greyford where the sheep grazed on hills too steep for plows. She told herself she was going to clear her head. She told herself a dozen useful lies, because lying to oneself was the last luxury left to a woman whose honesty had a price tag on it now.

The wind up there smelled of grass and distant rain and something older underneath, something mineral and patient, like the inside of a mountain thinking. The crows settled in a hawthorn at the hill's crown and waited for her with the composure of creditors.

"Say it," she said.

They looked at her.

"I've spent fifteen years not hearing it. If you're going to give it back, give it back. Don't make me beg in front of sheep."

The old one-eyed crow ruffled his feathers, and for a moment he seemed almost gentle, in the way that wild things are gentle — without softness, without apology, but with a kind of fierce respect.

"It isn't ours to say," he said. "That was the shape of the bargain. We keep; we don't spend. You must call it out of us yourself. Say the calling-words your mother taught you — you remember them, they're in you deeper than fear — and we will open our beaks and let it fly home."

And she did remember. That was the worst of it. Somewhere beneath fifteen years of caution, beneath the scar tissue and the rules and the careful flattened life, the words were waiting exactly where a mother's voice had planted them, worn smooth as a river stone from being turned over in the dark so many times. Words about giving back. About carrying no longer. About a bird opening its beak.

Ivo Whitlock stood on a bare hill above the city that had eaten her family and remade her into a seller of harmless things, and she was afraid.

Not of the Inquisition. Not of gods. She was afraid of the oldest thing she knew: that to be named is to be graspable. That everything Marta's betrayal had taught her was true — that to be known is to be held, and to be held is to be hurt, and the only safety in this world is to pass through it unnamed, unwitnessed, a rumor in your own life.

But the covenant was broken. The hollow god was wearing her mother's face in dreams and offering her rest like a knife offered handle-first. Tam had already sold a piece of the Choir. Voss knew her name — the false one, the armor-name — and had written it on a list. The fortress of silence had not saved her. It had only guaranteed that she would die alone behind walls that had never once been stormed, because no one had ever been let close enough to want to.

"Give it back," she said to the crows, and her voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "Please. I'm tired of being nobody. I'd rather be hunted as myself than safe as no one."

The hawthorn exploded upward. A hundred wings, a wheeling black constellation against the grey sky, and they began to sing — not the harsh cawing of carrion birds but something structured and ancient, a chorus with harmonies no human throat could make, and in the middle of it, wrapped in it, carried on it like a boat on a flood, came the word.

Her name.

Her true name, spoken by a hundred voices that had guarded it through frost and hunger and the long dark years of the Sundering's silence, spoken with such care and such ferocity that it did not sound like a word at all. It sounded like a key turning. It sounded like a door she had nailed shut from the inside swinging open onto a room full of light.

It went into her the way rain goes into drought-cracked earth — painfully, deeply, finding channels she had forgotten she had. She felt it settle along her bones. She felt the beasts of the hillside lift their heads — the sheep turning their blunt faces, a fox freezing at the edge of the bracken, a kestrel hanging motionless in the wind — and she felt them *recognize* her, all at once, the way you recognize someone across a crowded room whose face you have only ever seen in portraits.

"Oh," she said, and it came out as a sob. "Oh, you knew. All of you. All this time."

A ewe near the wall regarded her with horizontal-pupiled calm. *"We all knew,"* said the sheep, in the slow deep way of grass-eaters, for whom even speech is a form of grazing. *"The rats knew. The fish in the Greyford pools knew. Your mother told the whole living world, child, the week you were born. Only men were left ignorant, and men don't listen anyway."*

"The whole world," Ivo repeated. She laughed, and it came out broken, and then it came out real. "Fifteen years. Fifteen years I thought I was hiding. I was the only one who didn't know I was hidden."

*"Being hidden and being kept are different things,"* said the fox from the bracken, quick and sly and unexpectedly kind. *"She didn't hide you from the world. She hid you *in* it. There's a difference, cub. Ask any burrow."*

The crows came down at last and settled around her in the grass, a ring of black feathers, and the old one-eyed one hopped onto her knee with the familiarity of fifteen years' acquaintance finally admitted.

"She also left a message," he said. "To be delivered only when you had your name back. She made us memorize it. Crows have good memories, but that one weighed on us, I'll tell you — fifteen years of carrying a sentence we couldn't understand."

Ivo went very still. "What message?"

The crow drew himself up, and the whole chorus of them went silent, and the wind held its breath over the hill, and in the voice of one ragged scavenger bird speaking words a dead woman had chosen, he said:

"*When the silence comes to offer you rest, tell it that rest was never mine to give away. Tell it I spent mine buying you time, and I would spend it again. And tell my daughter — tell her the covenant was never meant to be carried by one voice. That's why it broke. Find the others. Sing together or not at all.*"

The wind came back. The sheep resumed grazing. Somewhere below, Vellamark smoked and muttered, unaware that anything had changed.

Ivo knelt in the wet grass with her true name warm in her chest like a swallowed coal, and she wept — not the dry, controlled weeping of a woman managing her grief, but the ugly, graceless weeping of a child, because her mother had been dead fifteen years and had still, somehow, gotten there first. Had still known what was coming. Had still left her exactly what she needed, hidden in the one place no inquisitor would ever search: the ordinary, overlooked, despised hearts of beasts.

When she could speak again, she wiped her face with her sleeve and looked at the old crow.

"Find the others," she said. "Sing together or not at all." She stared down at the city. "The Choir. She knew about the Choir."

"She knew about everything," the crow said. "Including the thing you need to know next, which is this: the hollow god has noticed that your name has come home. Names make noise in the deep places, daughter-of-our-woman. And the thing beneath the cathedral has been listening for yours a very long time."

Far below, beyond the smoking rooftops, the Cathedral of Whispers stood against the darkening sky — and as the sun went down behind the western hills, every bell in its tower began, very softly, to ring.

No rope pulled them. No hand touched them.

They rang the way a throat clears before speaking.
