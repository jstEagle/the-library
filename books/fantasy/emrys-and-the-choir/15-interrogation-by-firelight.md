## 15. Interrogation by Firelight

Dara Voss had conducted one hundred and forty-one interrogations in her eleven years with the Inquisition, and she had a private taxonomy for them. There were the blusterers, who filled the room with noise to hide their guilt. There were the weepers, whose tears were honest more often than not. There were the silent ones, who said nothing at all and were usually innocent of everything except being poor in the wrong district on the wrong night.

Ivo Whitlock was none of these.

Voss had watched her for three days now, through the narrow window of the holding cell in the Undercroft of the Bastion Serene — not a cell built for criminals but for suspects of the particular kind the Inquisition handled, warded against magic that had not been seen in Vellamark since before Voss was born. Whitlock sat on the cot as though it were a chair in her own shop. She ate the food. She slept, or appeared to. Twice she had asked the guard, politely, whether she might have paper and ink, and when refused, had simply folded her hands and looked at the wall as if it might eventually say something interesting.

She did not pace. She did not pray. Voss noted that especially. Everyone prayed in the dark, even people who claimed they didn't believe; she'd seen hardened smugglers mouthing petitions to gods that hadn't answered in fifteen years. But Whitlock's lips never moved. Her stillness was not the stillness of fear. It was the stillness of someone conserving something.

*Conserving what?* Voss wondered, and then, because she was honest with herself in the way that made her good at her work and miserable in everything else: *conserving it from me.*

The evidence lay in a leather folder on the desk before her, and she had read it four times, and each reading made the Inquisition look less like an institution and more like a man swinging a sword in the dark at sounds he couldn't name.

Greyford Bridge, three nights past. A structural survey commissioned by the Office of Works reported scorching along the bridge's undercarriage in patterns no fire could make — spirals, concentric rings, a geometry that repeated itself like a sentence spoken over and over by something learning to speak. The mortar between two stones had fused into glass. And embedded in that glass, recovered by a clerk with steady hands and a promotion he would never receive because he would be reassigned within the week: a fragment of bone, human, burned from the inside out.

The dead speaker had been identified by morning. Merrin Caul, twenty-six, a laundress from Tanner's Row. No family. No record. On the Inquisition's list of suspected gift-bearers since she was nineteen, flagged by a neighbor's report that pigeons followed her home "in formation, like soldiers."

Two other names from the list had been arrested in connection with the rite. They had said nothing. One of them, a fishmonger named Aldous Renk, had looked at Voss during his processing with an expression she kept turning over in her mind like a coin she couldn't identify the currency of. It wasn't defiance. It wasn't fear.

It was pity.

*She pities us,* Voss had thought, walking away from the cell. *Why does she pity us?*

And beneath that question, patient as water finding a crack, another one waited. One she had carried for eleven years without ever letting it become words.

Tonight she would ask it aloud, or she would drown in it. There was no third option left. The city was coming apart at the seams — prayers manifesting in the streets like burst pipes, wishes rotting into miracles gone wrong, the rats screaming a single word from every wall until the exterminators stopped charging for their services and started drinking. The Inquisition's answer was arrests, and the arrests were catching nothing, and the nothing they caught was multiplying.

She took the folder. She told the duty sergeant she would conduct the interrogation personally, alone, off the record — a request so irregular that he blinked at her twice before deciding that whatever was happening was above his pay grade, which was the correct instinct and also the only thing keeping him safe.

Then she went down to the Undercroft, carrying a lantern instead of using the ward-lights, because firelight was honest in a way that enchanted light was not. Firelight flickered. Firelight cast shadows that moved. If you were going to confess something, you wanted light that admitted it could tremble.

* * *

Whitlock looked up when the door opened, and Voss saw her catalog everything in the space of a breath: the lantern, the absence of guards, the folder, the two chairs Voss carried in under one arm. The charm-seller's eyes were quick and gray and very tired, and Voss recognized the tiredness the way you recognize your own handwriting.

"Inquisitor." Whitlock's voice was even. "Have you come to charge me, or to talk? I've found those are different things, in your profession."

"Both." Voss set the chairs facing each other, close, closer than regulation. She hung the lantern on its hook and sat. "Neither, possibly. Sit down."

Whitlock sat. Up close, without the counter between them, without the crowd of the riot and the smoke and the screaming shrine, she was smaller than Voss had imagined and older. There was a scar on her jaw, old and thin, and her hands — Voss had learned to read hands the way other people learned to read faces — her hands were a tradeswoman's hands, nicked and burn-marked, the hands of someone who worked with wax and wire and small sharp things.

"You've been sitting in this cell for three days," Voss said, "and you haven't once asked what you're charged with."

"I know what I'm charged with. Everything. Nothing provable." A ghost of a smile. "It's how your institution works. I've studied it."

"From inside a cell."

"From outside one, mostly. Cells are just the study carrels of your faith."

Despite herself — despite eleven years of discipline, of clean hands and cleaner ledgers — Voss felt her mouth twitch. She killed it. She opened the folder and laid the evidence out piece by piece on the floor between them, where the lantern-light could reach it: the survey sketches of the scorched geometry, the clerk's sworn statement, the photograph of the glassed mortar, and last, wrapped in cloth, the fragment of bone.

"Merrin Caul," Voss said. "Twenty-six. Laundress. She died at Greyford Bridge three nights ago doing whatever it is you and yours attempted there. Her body burned from the inside. Do you want to tell me about it?"

Whitlock looked at the bone for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the shopkeeper's polish. "Her name was Merrin. She stuttered. Did you know that? Not when she spoke to animals. Only to people. She joined the Choir because it was the first place anyone ever listened to her finish a sentence." She looked up. "You want me to tell you about the rite. I'll tell you about the rite. But first you're going to tell me why there are no guards behind that door, why you brought two chairs instead of calling me to an interview room, and why you're conducting this off the record. Because I've been in enough rooms with your kind to know what an arrest looks like, Inquisitor Voss, and this isn't one. This is something else. And I don't answer questions in something else until I know what it is."

There it was again. That steadiness. Most people, faced with the folder, with the bone, with the weight of the Bastion Serene above them, would have begun talking — talking was what guilty people and innocent people alike did when the machinery of the state turned toward them, talking was how you stayed a person instead of becoming a file. Whitlock treated the machinery like a merchant across a table.

Voss should have found it infuriating. Instead she found it familiar, and that frightened her more than anything the last month had produced.

"The record stays closed," Voss said, "because what I'm about to say cannot exist in it. Not yet. Maybe not ever." She paused. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself. "Eleven years ago, I stood where you're standing. Different room. Same stone. I was twenty-two, newly sworn, and my commanding officer brought me a case. A child in Saltmarket who'd been overheard talking to the gulls. The gulls, apparently, talked back. My job was to verify and process."

Whitlock had gone very still. Not the conserved stillness from before — a different quality entirely, the stillness of someone watching a door they'd assumed was a wall begin to open.

"I interviewed the child," Voss said. "Alone, because protocol. She was eight. She told me the gulls warned her about storms before the storms came, and she'd told her mother, and her mother had told her never to say it again, and the mother was right, because here I was." Voss's hands were flat on her knees. She noticed she was pressing hard enough to whiten the knuckles and made herself stop. "What I didn't put in my report — what I have never put in any report — is that while the child was telling me this, the gulls landed on the window ledge of the interview room. Four of them. Facing inward. Watching her. And when she began to cry, they made a sound I have never heard gulls make before or since. It wasn't alarm. It was comfort. It was a lullaby in a language I don't speak, and I understood it anyway, the way you understand crying without needing it translated."

The silence in the cell was total. Even the lantern seemed to burn quieter.

"What did you write in your report?" Whitlock asked softly.

"That the child showed no demonstrable gift. That the gulls were attracted by crumbs from the guard's lunch. I recommended release and monitoring." Voss exhaled slowly. "The child went home. I got promoted. And every day for eleven years I have known exactly one thing with perfect certainty: that I knew those gulls were talking, because some part of me heard them. Not the way she heard them. Fainter. Like music through a wall. But I heard it, Ivo Whitlock. I have the gift. Buried, stunted, barely a seed — but I have it. And I built the Inquisition's list of suspected bearers with these hands." She held them up, palms out, the way you show a court you hold no weapon. "Every name on it is a name I can hear, faintly, humming. Including yours. Including mine, if anyone ever thought to test me."

Whitlock stared at her. And then she did something Voss had not seen her do in three days of observation: she laughed. It was a short, rough sound, half disbelief, half grief.

"A hunter with the quarry's blood," she said. "Oh, that's — the gods have a sense of humor after all. They just save it for people like us." She shook her head. "Why are you telling me this? You could hang for it. Concealment of gift-bearing is a capital offense, and you're not just concealing, you're the chief architect of the hunt."

"Because the hunt has failed." Voss said it plainly, because dressing it up would dishonor it. "Because Merrin Caul is dead and the bridge is scarred and the rats are screaming and my institution is arresting laundresses while the actual catastrophe walks around wearing whatever face it likes. Because I have spent eleven years being very good at a job that I now believe is not merely wrong but impossible — you cannot stamp out a river by arresting the raindrops. And because—" Here she stopped. Here was the part she had rehearsed on the stairs and abandoned twice, because saying it made it real. "Because I am tired, Whitlock. I am so tired of listening through walls. I want to open the door."

For a long moment Whitlock said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone, the city went on making its damaged noises — bells, screams, the strange sweet chimes of prayers answering themselves in the streets.

Then Whitlock leaned forward, elbows on knees, and Voss saw the exhaustion underneath the competence at last, plain as a bruise.

"Let me tell you what happened at Greyford Bridge," she said. "All of it. What we tried, what broke it, what killed Merrin. And then let me tell you what's actually underneath this city, and what it wants, and why your Inquisition — why *we*, all of us — have been feeding it for fifteen years without knowing." Her gray eyes caught the firelight. "But understand what you're asking for, Inquisitor. Once you open that door, it doesn't close. I spent fifteen years keeping mine shut. I know exactly what comes through."

"I shot the lock off my own door eleven years ago," Voss said. "I've just been pretending the draft was weather."

Something moved across Whitlock's face — recognition, wariness, and beneath both, cautiously, the first pale edge of trust. She glanced at the door, then back.

"All right," she said. "But not here. These walls have ears of the ordinary kind, and worse kinds besides. There's a place — a cistern chamber under Chandler's Lane, dry, disused. The beasts keep watch there for me. Crows, mostly. They see more than your whole Bastion." She stood, and offered — hesitantly, as if the gesture cost her something — her hand. "Come at moonset. Come unarmed, come alone, and come having decided, finally, which side of your own list you stand on."

Voss looked at the hand. Eleven years of clean hands. Hands that had signed a hundred and forty-one interrogations, that had written *crumbs from the guard's lunch* over the truth, that had built the cage and lived comfortably beside it, telling themselves the bars were for others.

She took it.

"One condition," Voss said. "When this is over — however it ends — you help me dismantle what I built. Name by name. Every person on that list gets their file burned by my own hand."

Whitlock's grip tightened briefly. "That's not a condition. That's the price of admission. Welcome to the Choir, Inquisitor."

They released her before dawn — paperwork error, missing signature, a clerk who would be flogged if anyone traced it, and no one would, because Voss would make sure of it. She watched from the Bastion steps as the charm-seller melted into the grey streets like water into sand, and felt the strangest sensation, standing there in her immaculate coat with her immaculate record: lighter. Terrified, and lighter.

In her pocket, the folder with Merrin Caul's name weighed nothing at all.

Above the rooftops, unseen by anyone but her, four gulls wheeled once against the darkening sky — and then, in formation, flew west, toward Chandler's Lane, as if they had somewhere to be and all the time in the world to wait for her to follow.
