## 17. Every Prayer at Once

Ivo felt it before she heard it — a pressure behind her eyes like the moment before a sneeze, or before weeping, or before the sky splits. She was three streets from Greyford Bridge, hurrying through the fish-market's pre-dawn emptiness, when every gull in Vellamark went silent at once.

Fifteen years of silence, and then the sky opened its throat.

The first prayer came down like weather. It struck the harbor in a column of golden light and answered itself: *let my son come home from the sea* — and the sea obliged, lifting a fishing boat that had gone down eleven winters ago out of the deep water whole and dripping, its drowned crew standing at the rails, salt-cured and smiling, waving to the widow who was already running down the quay with her arms out, screaming a word that was not joy.

Then another. And another. They fell on the city the way rain falls on a fire — everywhere at once, indiscriminate, hissing into every dry corner of Vellamark where someone had once whispered a wish to a god who wasn't listening.

*Let me be beautiful.* The laundress on Tanner's Row became beautiful so thoroughly that her own mother walked past her in the street without recognition, and the woman clawed at her new face trying to find her old one underneath.

*Let the pain stop.* The whole of the Rope-walk district went numb — not just the fevered and the broken but everyone, every hand, every tongue, so that a man could put his palm flat against a stove and feel nothing at all, which was its own kind of horror.

*Let him love me again.* A carpenter named Aldous Fell woke in his bed with his dead wife beside him, warm, smelling of bread and river water, and he lay very still for a long time because some part of him understood exactly what he was holding, and what it would cost to let go.

Ivo stood in the middle of the market square with her hands over her ears, which did nothing, because the prayers were not sound. They were happening inside her the way they were happening inside everything — inside the cobbles, inside the gulls, inside the fish gutted and gleaming on their slabs of ice. The beasts were screaming. Not words now, not even *coming*, just raw animal terror pouring through the bond she'd spent fifteen years strangling thin, flooding her until she tasted blood.

She ran for the bridge.

* * *

Dara Voss was awake because she had never really been asleep — sleep had become, in the last months, a country she visited briefly under armed guard — when the ceiling of her office began to answer prayers.

It started with the candle. *Give me light enough to finish this,* she had muttered at it an hour ago, half to herself, the way a tired woman talks to objects, meaning nothing by it. The candle had heard. Now it burned without consuming itself, a flame the size of a fist, white-hot, and it would not go out. She had smothered it under a book. It burned through the book. She had thrown it out the window; it hung in the air outside her window like a small furious star, waiting for her to need it again.

Voss stood in the wreckage of her office — papers lifted and swirling in a wind that came from nowhere, ink rising off the page in black threads and rearranging itself into words she had written once, years ago, in a report she had prayed no one would ever read — and understood, with the cold clarity that had made her a good Inquisitor, that the world had stopped pretending.

Every careless wish. Every bitter mutter. Every prayer spoken in grief or spite or boredom over fifteen years, hoarded in the dark by something patient and starving, all of it coming due at once, with interest.

She took her coat, her iron, and the list — the list with Ivo Whitlock's name on it, which had stopped being a weapon weeks ago and become something more like a map — and went out into the city of answered wishes.

The streets were full of miracles, and every one of them was a wound.

A fountain in Sable Court flowed upward, carrying a drowned child back toward the surface in slow rotation, and around it knelt forty people praying in opposite directions — some begging it to finish, some begging it to stop. A baker's shop had become a shrine of endless bread, loaves multiplying on the shelves faster than they could be carried out, while the baker sat in the doorway laughing and laughing and could not stop, because he had wished, once, drunk, after his wife left, that he would never be hungry for anything again. Two men who had hated each other for twenty years were fused at the shoulder by a prayer neither remembered making — *make us brothers again* — walking the street together in horrible tandem, learning each other's stride.

And above it all, faint as smoke, the hollow god's absence hummed. Voss couldn't hear it the way a speaker could. But she could feel it the way you feel a missing tooth: the shape of something gone, rubbing raw against everything around it.

She turned toward the Cathedral of Whispers, because if the Choir was anywhere, it would be there, and if Ivo Whitlock was anywhere, she would be between the Choir and whatever was coming to eat them.

* * *

The bridge was drowning in light.

Greyford Bridge spanned the threshold-god's territory — the liminal water beneath, the arches that were doorways whether you knew it or not — and the hollow god had chosen it deliberately, Ivo realized as she fought her way up the near slope. What better place to pour out fifteen years of stolen prayers than across the one seam in the world the covenant still sealed? Every miracle landing here cracked the mortar further.

The Choir was holding. Barely.

Twelve speakers stood along the span in a ragged line, hands linked, singing — not with music but with intention, each of them pushing back against the flood with the only thing they had, which was honesty. Old Marta the bone-speaker had collapsed to her knees and was being held upright by the twins. Tam Ossler stood at the center with his arms raised and storm-light crawling under his skin, hurling himself against the tide of answered wishes like a man bailing a sinking ship with his bare hands.

Ivo reached the foot of the bridge and the beasts' terror hit her fully, and she understood what they were screaming about.

The prayers weren't random. They were *aimed*. The hollow god was spending its hoard the way a besieger spends fire — not to destroy the city, but to break the seal. Every answered wish landed on the covenant's root and gnawed. Fifteen years of human longing, weaponized. The most powerful force in the world, and it had always been this: people wanting things badly enough to kneel.

"Ivo!" Tam's voice cracked across the chaos. "The root — it's under the cathedral — we can't hold the bridge and reach it—"

"Then don't hold the bridge," Ivo shouted back. "Fall back! All of you! You can't stop a flood, you can only get out of its way!"

"And do what?" Marta screamed, tears cutting through the dust on her face. "There is nothing else!"

There was. There had always been only one other thing, and Ivo had been walking toward it since the night the rats first said *coming*. But she didn't say it. Instead she grabbed the nearest speaker — young Pell, whose gift was for moths, poor terrified boy — and hauled him off the line, and the line bent, and the others understood, and they broke.

The moment they broke, the bridge gave.

Light poured through the arches like water through a burst dam, and the last seal on the covenant's outer gate tore with a sound Ivo felt in her teeth — a sound like a name being said wrong.

* * *

Voss found the crowd outside the cathedral first, and almost died among them.

They had come running from every quarter of the city, drawn not by the miracles but by the hole the miracles left — the humming absence overhead, which had begun, in the last hour, to make promises. Voss heard it herself as she shoved through the press: a voice like the space between heartbeats, saying *rest* in a tone that made rest sound like the only reasonable thing left in the world. Put down your fear. Put down your wanting. Stop praying. Be quiet, and be quiet forever, and be at peace.

Half the crowd was already kneeling. Their lips moved without sound. Their eyes had gone smooth as river stones.

Voss put her iron to the cobblestones and spoke the old Inquisitorial cadence — the one designed to cut through enchantment, though the Inquisition had never known why it worked, and Voss suspected now that it worked because it was honest, because it said *stand up, you are being lied to,* and lies cannot survive being named — and a ring of people shuddered back to themselves, weeping, furious, alive.

"You," she said, pointing at a boy of maybe fourteen. "Cathedral. Is anyone inside?"

"The speakers," he gasped. "They went in. And the light followed them in."

Of course it did. Voss looked up at the cathedral — its spires black against a sky that churned with unspent prayer, auroras of gold and green coiling like smoke trapped under glass — and understood the shape of the trap. The hollow god had spent fifteen years learning hunger. Now it had learned strategy too. It had flooded the city to drive the speakers to ground, and ground was exactly where it wanted them: in the one building in Vellamark built over the covenant's root.

She went in after them. Alone, because everyone she might have ordered to come with her was either kneeling, weeping, or on fire.

* * *

Inside, the cathedral was full of faces.

They hung in the air like lanterns — thousands of them, soft-lit, gentle, the faces of everyone whose prayers had ever gone unanswered in Vellamark. A child's face. An old man's. A face Ivo recognized with a lurch that dropped her to her knees: her mother, younger than memory, smiling down with an expression of infinite patience.

"Don't look at them," Ivo rasped to the Choir, huddled at the altar end of the nave. "It's wearing them. None of them are real."

"Neither are we, anymore," Tam said quietly. He was gray-faced, drained to the marrow, his gift flickering in him like a guttering lamp. "Ivo. It's not attacking us. Look."

She looked. The hollow god was not tearing at the speakers. It was *offering*. Every face in the air wore the same invitation, and the prayer-light pooled around the Choir like warm water, and Ivo felt it working on her too — the seduction of it, the terrible reasonableness. *You are so tired. You have hidden for fifteen years. You have spent yourself in scraps and fragments, selling charms to widows, lying low, lying always. I am offering you the end of lying. Just stop. Just be quiet. Everyone gets what they wanted, in the end, by wanting nothing.*

"It's not lying," Ivo said aloud, horrified. "That's the worst part. It means every word. It genuinely thinks this is mercy."

"Then it can be mercied right back into the pit," snarled a new voice, and Dara Voss came striding up the nave with her iron drawn and her coat smoking at the hem, looking like the last sane sentence in a mad sermon.

"Voss." Ivo stared. "You're supposed to arrest me."

"I'm supposed to do a great many things." Voss reached the altar steps, scanned the twelve exhausted speakers, the hanging faces, the churning dark above the transept where the root of the covenant pulsed like a buried heart. "Inquisitor's assessment: we have minutes, not hours. Whatever it's doing to the root, it's nearly done. So one of you is going to tell me how to stop it, and then we're going to do that, and you can arrest me afterward if you're feeling sentimental."

Marta laughed — a cracked, hysterical sound. "Stop it? Child, the covenant isn't a wall you defend. It's a promise. It was broken by a promise, and it can only be remade by one."

"What kind of promise?" Voss asked.

Above them, the thousand faces smiled wider, all together, and the hollow god's voice filled the nave like cold water filling a lung:

*A true one.*

And Ivo, looking up at her mother's borrowed face, at the patience and the pity in it, finally understood what kind of promise, and what it would cost, and who in that room could pay it.

The light began to descend.
