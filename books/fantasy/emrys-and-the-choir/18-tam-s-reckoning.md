## 18. Tam's Reckoning

The rain had not stopped since Greyford Bridge.

It came down soft and grey over the Cathedral of Whispers, threading through the broken roof in silver ropes, pooling in the hollows of the shattered nave until the whole floor gleamed like a dark mirror. Tam Ossler stood at the edge of that mirror and watched the water tremble with each drop, and he thought: *this is what I did. This is the shape of it.* Water, falling. A storm-god's leavings. Maren's blood thinned to pink and carried away through cracks in the stone, going wherever dead things' rain goes.

Three days. Three days since the rite had torn itself apart beneath his feet, since the threshold god's bridge had screamed and bucked and thrown the Choir's voices back at them like a fistful of knives. Three days since Maren Solle — quiet Maren, who spoke to moths, who kept sugar-water in a tin cup in her pocket for them, who had once told Tam her gift was the smallest in the Choir and therefore the most honest — had been unmade on the bridge stones while the storm-god's bargain curdled in Tam's chest like swallowed lightning.

He had not slept. Sleep was a country whose border he could no longer cross; every time he approached it, he heard the storm-god's voice again, warm as summer thunder: *Your brother's life for their loyalty. They will never know. What is loyalty worth if it costs you nothing?*

They knew now. That was the worst of it. Not Voss's arrest warrants, not the hollow god's prayer-flood still drowning half of Vellamark in answered wishes gone rotten — those were horrors, but they were *outside* horrors, weather to be endured. The worst was Ivo Whitlock's face when she'd pulled him out of the floodwater on the second night, his secret already loose in the Choir's mouths, and looked at him not with fury but with something far more terrible: recognition. As if she had always known. As if betrayal were a language she'd learned young and could hear spoken even in whispers.

The others were gathering. He could hear them coming through the cathedral's ruined doors — the shuffle of wet boots, the low animal murmurs of people who spoke to beasts and had learned to speak quietly among themselves too. Old Fen with her hounds. The twins, Pell and Sorrel, whose sparrows had been screaming *coming* for weeks and now only screamed *here*. And Ivo, last, as always, moving through the doorway like a woman walking into a room where she expected to be shot.

Tam's hands would not stop shaking. He pressed them flat against his thighs and made himself look at the place where Maren had died — or rather, where the rite had died and taken her with it, because he still did not have words for what exactly had happened to her, only that one moment there had been a small round woman with moth-dust on her sleeves, and the next there was a coat, and shoes, and a silence so complete it had its own weight.

*You cannot unmake,* the storm-god had told him once, in the first sweet hour of the bargain, when it had still sounded like mercy. *You can only pay.*

He was here to pay.

* * *

Fen lit a fire in the old brazier near the altar, because the Choir had decided — without ever saying it aloud — that they would not meet in this place in darkness again. The flames threw orange light up the shattered pillars, and the shadows of the broken saints leaned and swayed overhead like a congregation that had stayed past the end of the world.

"Say it," Pell said. He was the younger twin, the angry one, and his voice cracked across the nave like a whip. "Say it out loud, Ossler. We've all heard it secondhand. We've all heard the crows gossip about it. I want it from your mouth."

"Pell." Sorrel put a hand on her brother's arm. Her sparrows sat in her hood, feathers slicked down by the rain, watching Tam with little black eyes that missed nothing. Birds always missed nothing. That had been the joke between him and Sorrel once, before: *you keep the sparrows so you'll never be lied to.* He wondered if she felt it now as a wound rather than a comfort.

"No," Tam said. "He's right. It should come from my mouth."

His voice came out steadier than he deserved. He stepped forward into the firelight, and the whole Choir turned to him — twelve minus one, eleven faces, and every one of them holding some small bright thing inside them that he had tried to sell.

"I went to the storm-god before we ever assembled here," he said. "Before the stag found Ivo. Before any of us knew what the covenant was or what reforging it would cost. My brother Joss was dying. Fever, then the lung-rot, then the fever again. The physicians gave him a week. And I—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "I have spoken to weather since I was six years old. It was the one thing I had. And I took the one thing I had and I offered it — offered *us* — to a god, in exchange for my brother's life."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the roofless dark above, rain struck stone in a rhythm almost like applause, almost like jeering.

"The bargain was this," Tam went on, because stopping now would be the coward's mercy and he had spent enough of those. "The Choir's loyalty to the storm-god, delivered when the covenant was reforged — our voices bent to its claims first, before the pantheon's, whatever those claims might be. In exchange, Joss lives. And I—" he laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, "I told myself it wasn't treachery. I told myself the storm-god is an old god, a true god, that its claims would be just ones. I told myself I was securing us an ally. I told myself every lie a man tells when he's trading something that isn't his to trade."

"And at the bridge?" Fen asked. Her voice was gravel and grief. One of her hounds pressed against her leg, hackles up, staring at Tam the way dogs stare at meat that has gone bad. "What did you tell yourself when the rite broke?"

"That I didn't know," Tam said. "That's the truth, and I hate how much it sounds like an excuse. I didn't know the bargain would poison the rite. But I knew — *knew*, in my gut, the way you know weather coming — that something of mine was tangled in the working. I felt it pulling. I felt the covenant snag on me like cloth on a nail. And I said nothing. I stood on that bridge with Maren beside me, and I felt the whole rite start to tear, and I kept my mouth shut because speaking would have cost me Joss."

He looked at each of them. He made himself do it. Sorrel's face was white and still. Pell's was twisted with disgust. Fen looked old. And Ivo — Ivo watched him from the edge of the firelight with those grey eyes that saw everything, and said nothing, and let him hang himself honestly, which was more courtesy than he had earned.

"Maren is dead because I valued my brother's life over hers," he said. "Not because the storm-god tricked me. Not because the hollow god interfered. Because when the moment came, I chose, and I chose wrong, and she paid. There is no version of this where I am not the man who killed her. I need you all to know that I know it."

Silence, except the rain.

Then Pell moved — fast, furious, fists first — and it was Fen who caught him, and Sorrel who stepped between, and Tam who stood still and took the shape of the blow without flinching, because he had decided on the walk here that whatever the Choir gave him, he would accept. It was the least of the payments due.

"Enough," Ivo said.

She hadn't raised her voice. She almost never did. But the word went through the nave like a current through wire, and Pell stopped struggling, and even the fire seemed to lean toward her. Tam realized, with a strange distant clarity, that the animals had gone quiet too — the hounds, the sparrows, the rats that had begun creeping back into the cathedral's shadows since the prayer-flood. Everything in the room was listening to her. Everything always listened to her.

"What do you want, Tam?" Ivo asked. "Truly. Before we decide anything — what do you want?"

And there it was. The question under all questions. He had rehearsed answers on the long wet walk: *forgiveness, punishment, a chance to help fix it.* All true, all insufficient.

"I want to pay," he said. "Not suffer — pay. There's a difference. Suffering is just pain with nowhere to go. Payment goes somewhere. It buys something back." His hands were shaking again and he let them. "Maren is beyond buying back. I know that. But the covenant isn't. The hollow god is still drowning the city in fifteen years of rotten wishes, and the storm-god still holds a claim on this Choir that I handed it like a thief handing over stolen goods. So I'm asking you to let me settle my debt. All of it. Now."

"How?" Sorrel asked softly.

Tam reached into his shirt and drew out the token the storm-god had given him — a sliver of fulgurite, lightning-fused sand, still faintly warm against his skin after three days, humming with a patience that made his teeth ache. He held it up so the firelight caught it.

"I call it here," he said. "In front of all of you. And I offer it my gift. Not the Choir's loyalty — that bargain dies tonight, voided, witnessed. Just mine. The weather-speaking. Six-and-twenty years of it. Every storm I've ever talked to, every wind I've ever begged off a harvest, every hailstorm I've turned from my neighbors' fields. It can take the whole of it, as payment for breaking faith. And then I'll be what Maren always said was the most honest thing a person can be." His voice finally broke, and he let it. "Ordinary."

"You'd be giving up your brother's protection too," Fen said. "If the god takes your gift instead of your loyalty, maybe it takes the bargain entire. Maybe Joss dies anyway."

"I know." Tam closed his hand around the fulgurite until the edges bit. "I thought about that for three days. On the second day I went to see him. He's mending — the god's work is holding, whatever else happens. And I sat by his bed and I thought: if the price of his life is that I spend the rest of mine as a liar among liars, having sold eleven people who trusted me, then I'd be keeping him alive in a world I helped make uglier, and he'd grow up and someday learn what his life cost, and he'd have to live with being that price. I won't do that to him. If the god takes my gift and the bargain with it — then Joss's fate belongs to God and chance and medicine, like everyone else's brother. At least I'll have paid honestly. At least he'll be able to live with me afterward, if he learns the truth. At least *I* will."

He knelt then, on the wet stone, in front of the fire and the Choir and the listening dark, and set the fulgurite on the floor between his palms like an offering on an altar.

"Storm-father," he said, and his voice rang strangely in the ruined nave, the old formal cadence rising out of him unbidden, the way prayers do when they're older than the person praying. "You hold my oath and my shame. Come and count them."

* * *

The god came the way storms come: not gradually, but all at once, everywhere, arriving already arrived.

The rain above the broken roof stopped falling and began to *hang*, ten thousand drops suspended in the air like beads on invisible threads, and through them the light turned green-grey and bruised. The fire in the brazier stretched tall and thin and silent. And in the hanging rain, in the space between the drops, something vast turned its attention upon the kneeling man.

Tam felt it the way he had always felt weather — in the pressure behind his eyes, in the ache of old breaks, in the fine hairs of his arms lifting. But he had never felt it this close, this *focused*. It was like standing inside the eye of a hurricane and knowing the eye was looking at you.

*Little speaker,* the storm-god said, and its voice was thunder heard from a valley away, rolling and patient and old. *You called. I counted. Speak your payment.*

"My gift," Tam said. His voice was very small under the hanging sky, and he made it steady anyway. "All of it. Freely given, in settlement of my false bargain. Take the oath with it. Void the claim on the Choir. Witnessed by the fire and the faithful and the beasts." He glanced sideways, briefly, at the hounds and sparrows and the small dark shapes of rats along the walls, all of them watching. "Witnessed by everything that hears."

*And your brother?*

Tam's breath caught. Of course it would ask. Gods were not cruel the way men were cruel; they were cruel the way weather was cruel, indifferent and exacting, and it wanted the terms stated plainly so that no one could later claim ambush.

"If the bargain dies, his protection dies with it," Tam said. "I accept that. I ask nothing for him. I only ask that what I pay be *counted* — that you weigh it truly, and find the debt settled, and carry no grudge forward into the new covenant. Can you do that, storm-father? Can you let a debt die clean?"

The hanging rain trembled. For a long moment the only sound in the world was the suspended shimmer of ten thousand held drops, and Tam kneeling in the middle of it with his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

*It will be counted,* the storm-god said at last. *Freely offered, freely taken. This is the oldest arithmetic, little speaker, and the only one the covenant honors: you cannot unmake. You can only pay. Rise, and be emptied.*

It came into him like a river reversing.

Tam had spent twenty-six years with weather in his blood — the pressure-sense, the wind-whisper, the deep animal knowledge of when the sky meant to break. He had thought of it as a well, deep and cold. Now he felt the well picked up and poured out, every drop of it drawn upward through his chest and out through his open mouth in a long exhalation that was not quite breath. Storms he had calmed left him. Winds he had courted left him. The memory of thunder's grammar, the syntax of gales, the sweet dark vocabulary of rain — all of it streamed out of him into the green-grey light, and with it went something he had not known was part of the gift: the way the horizon had always *meant* something to him. The way the sky had always been a room he could read. After, he understood, it would only be a ceiling.

He wept without deciding to. It was not grief exactly, and not relief, but the strange flat sorrow of a man watching his own house burn and knowing he set the fire and knowing the fire is just.

When it was done, the rain fell again — all at once, ordinary, cold, drumming on the cathedral stones — and the light returned to the color of evening, and Tam Ossler knelt on the wet floor of the Cathedral of Whispers and was nobody.

He tested the emptiness the way a man tests a missing tooth. Reached for the pressure-sense and found nothing. Listened for the wind's voice and heard only wind. Looked at the hanging clouds through the broken roof and saw — weather. Clouds. Rain. Things that happened to you, not things that spoke.

It was the quietest his head had been since he was six years old. He sat in that quiet and let it hurt, because it should hurt, because Maren would never feel anything again and he would feel this small loss forever and the disproportion was the point. You cannot unmake. You can only pay. And the paying never balances; it only ends.

"TAM!"

Sorrel's scream tore him out of the quiet. He looked up, blinking rain from his eyes, and followed her pointing arm to the brazier —

— where the fire had gone out.

Not guttered. *Out*, snuffed in a single instant, though the coals still glowed. And standing in the sudden dark, where no one had entered, where no door had opened, was a figure made of the absence of sound. It wore a shape like a tall woman, and its face was smooth as an unwritten page, and around it the rain made no noise at all — the drops fell silently, the drips from the broken roof fell silently, even the Choir's ragged breathing seemed to fall into it and vanish.

The hollow god. Here. In the one place the Choir had believed safe.

Its blank face turned — slowly, gently, the way a mother turns toward a crying child — and settled on Ivo Whitlock.

And when it spoke, though no sound crossed the air, every one of them heard it inside their skulls, soft as a secret:

*Ivo. Your mother's face is for sale in the Market of Small Gods. I have bought it. Come below, daughter of the broken seal, and I will show you what she truly did — and what she truly wanted for you.*

Beside Tam, Ivo Whitlock went white as the drowned.
