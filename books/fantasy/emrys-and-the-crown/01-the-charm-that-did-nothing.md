## 1. The Charm That Did Nothing

The rain came down on Greyhollow like it had a grudge, and Felix Fairweather stood under his awning in the Drowned Market and sold hope by the ounce.

"Brass and wishful thinking," he said to the child in front of him, because that was the truth, and the truth was the only luxury he allowed himself these days. "That's all this is. You understand?"

The girl couldn't have been more than eight. She clutched a coin in her fist so tight her knuckles had gone white as candle wax, and she kept glancing over her shoulder at the alley mouth where her mother had told her to wait. Her hair was plastered flat with rain. Her lips were blue at the edges.

"It's for my da," she said. "He works the kilns. He coughs."

Felix looked down at the charm between them—the one hanging from his stall's crossbar among forty of its brothers, each one turned that morning from scrap brass, filed smooth, stamped with the Fairweather mark: a small sun over an open door. The stamp had been his grandfather's design. The sun meant luck. The door meant welcome home. Neither of them meant anything, not anymore, not for three generations, maybe never.

"He coughs," Felix repeated. He reached up and took the charm down. It was warm from the brazier he kept behind the stall, which was the closest thing to magic it would ever contain. "This won't fix him. I want you to hear me say that plainly, because I'm a liar about most things, but not this. It's just brass. If you spend your coin on it, you're buying a story to hold onto, nothing more."

The girl's chin came up. Rain ran off it. "Ma said you were honest. She said that's why we come to you instead of Old Maddox, who swears his charms are blessed by the river saints."

"Maddox is a fraud and a thief."

"You're a fraud too, though." She said it without malice, the way children say true things. "You just say so first."

Felix laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed. He crouched to her level, elbows on his knees, and held the charm out on his open palm. Rain dripped off the awning edge between them like a curtain of beads.

"That's right," he said. "I'm a fraud who tells you he's a fraud. Which makes me either the worst liar in Greyhollow or the best, depending on how you count."

She took the charm. Her fingers were small and cold and careful, the way people handle things they believe in.

"How much?" she asked.

He should have said two coppers. Two coppers was the price; two coppers was what kept the rent half-paid and the brazier lit and the landlord's knuckles off the door another week. The guild was dying—had been dying since before Felix inherited its corpse—and every copper mattered the way blood matters to a man bleeding out: desperately, and without any real hope of making a difference.

"One copper," Felix heard himself say. "And here—" he fished in the drawer beneath the counter and came up with a second charm, identical to the first, "—a spare. In case you lose the first one, or in case your da wants one for his pocket and one over the bed. They do nothing, mind. But sometimes doing nothing is better than doing harm, and there's precious little else in this city I can promise."

The girl pressed her single copper into his hand with the gravity of a queen paying a tithe. Then she did something worse than paying. She smiled at him—gap-toothed, soaked, utterly certain—and said, "Thank you, mister Fairweather. Ma says your family used to make real ones."

Something turned over in Felix's chest, slow and heavy, like a stone rolling in a streambed.

"Your ma says a lot of things," he managed.

"She remembers. She's old enough." The girl tucked both charms into her shirt, against her skin, where the rain couldn't get at them. "She says before the Purges, Fairweather charms actually worked. She says that's why the hunters watch your shop."

"Your ma," Felix said lightly, "should learn to keep her memories to herself."

But she was already backing away into the crowd, and the crowd swallowed her, and Felix was left standing in the wet grey light with one copper in his palm and the taste of an old lie in his mouth—not his lie, but his family's, worn smooth as the brass he sold.

* * *

The guild had been bankrupt for years. It simply hadn't admitted it yet.

Felix counted the day's take behind the counter while the market murmured around him: eleven coppers, a bent nail, and a button. The button was mother-of-pearl and probably worth more than everything else combined, which said everything worth saying about Greyhollow's economy. Tomorrow the landlord would come for the quarter's rent. Tomorrow Felix would have perhaps thirty coppers to give him against a debt of four silver marks. The day after tomorrow, if the arithmetic held—and it always held, that was the cruelty of arithmetic—the Fairweather sign would come down off its hook for the last time, and the last charm-shop in the Drowned Market would become a fishmonger's or a pawnbroker's or whatever the city decided to pour into the empty shape of it.

Ten years he'd kept the corpse breathing. Ten years since his grandfather died with the ledger open on his knees and his last words slurred and strange: *Keep the anchors warm, boy. Keep them warm until someone comes.* Felix had been nineteen and grieving and had assumed the old man meant the brazier.

Now he was twenty-nine, and he still wasn't sure he'd been wrong to assume that.

He pulled the ledger out from its hiding place—under a loose floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath a decoy box of worthless inventory—and set it on the counter. It was a heavy thing, bound in cracked green leather gone black at the corners, and it was the only object in the shop that Felix had never once tried to sell, even in the worst winter, even the year he ate boiled leather and dandelion roots for a month.

To anyone flipping through it, the ledger would look like what it pretended to be: accounts. Sales records. *Two dozen luck-charms, spring market, 4 coppers each. One warding sigil (decorative), sold to the harbormaster's wife, 12 coppers.* Decades of it. His grandfather's cramped hand, then his father's, then, in the last pages, his own.

But between the account pages, stitched into the binding itself the way a surgeon stitches a wound, were other pages. Thinner. Older. Written in ink that had gone brown as dried blood, in a hand so fine it might have been done with a needle.

Felix didn't read those pages anymore. Reading them hurt. They talked about bindings and anchors and *the long keeping*, and they used words like *Emberline* and *the door below*, and once—only once, on a page he'd found at fifteen and never found again, though he'd torn the book nearly apart looking—they described something buried beneath the capital that the first binders had *caged, not killed, God help us, only caged.*

His grandfather had called it the guild's true inheritance. Felix called it the reason his family had spent three generations selling nothing to people who needed everything, and he hated the ledger and loved it and could no longer tell the difference.

A shadow fell across the counter.

"For the last time, Fairweather, I'm not buying a love-charm, and if you ask again I'll report you to the—"

It was Tansy Cobb, the eel-seller, arms crossed, rain steaming faintly off her shoulders. Sixty years old, forearms like mooring rope, a woman who had watched Felix grow up in this market and had appointed herself his aunt, conscience, and chief critic without ever consulting him on the matter.

"I wasn't going to ask," Felix said. "I was going to offer you a discount on a headache charm."

"You don't sell headaches charms."

"I sell brass and wishful thinking. What you imagine it does is between you and your imagination."

Tansy snorted. Then her eyes flicked past him, down the market lane, and the humor went out of her face the way water goes out of a pulled plug. "Boots," she said quietly. "Twelve of them. Grey cloaks. Coming up from the Fishgate end."

Felix's hands went still on the counter.

Witch-hunters didn't come to the Drowned Market for eels.

* * *

Later—much later, in the dark, with his heart still hammering—Felix would try to reconstruct those next few minutes and find them full of holes. But some pieces stayed sharp as glass:

Tansy's hand closing on his wrist, hard, saying *not yet, not yet, walk, don't run.*

The market around them changing texture—vendors who had been shouting prices suddenly folding their stalls with practiced speed, mothers gathering children, a whole street of people performing innocence with the smoothness of long rehearsal.

The grey cloaks coming on anyway, unhurried, spreading out at the lane ends like spilled ink finding its level.

And at the center of them, tall and dry despite the rain—as though the weather had agreed to negotiate with her separately—a woman in a hunter's coat with silver buttons, carrying a long iron rod banded with rings, and wearing an expression of patient, professional disappointment.

Captain Vess. Everyone in Greyhollow knew the name the way they knew the name of the plague.

"Market inspection," Vess announced, in a voice built to carry without effort. "By order of the Crown Office of Purity. All licensed vendors will present their goods for examination. Unlicensed practitioners of the hidden arts will step forward now, and will be treated with the mercy the law provides."

Nobody stepped forward. Nobody ever stepped forward. The mercy the law provided was a pyre in Wicker Square, and everyone knew that too.

The search began. And here was the piece Felix could never explain afterward, the piece that felt less like memory and more like instruction: he heard his grandfather's voice, clear as a bell across ten dead years, saying *keep the anchors warm,* and his body moved without consulting him.

He swept the ledger off the counter and into the oilcloth. He dropped the floorboard back into place and dragged the decoy box over it. Then—because twelve hunters were already working down the lane and the cellar hatch was behind the counter and the cellar's river-grate exit was flooded twice a year and smelled like drowned cat—he took the ledger with him anyway, wrapped against his chest like a child, and went down into the dark.

Above him, boots crossed his floor. A voice—Vess's voice, calm as tide—said, "This one sells charms. Start here."

An iron rod rang against brass. Somewhere in the shop, forty useless trinkets chimed together like a struck bell, and Felix, huddled in the black beneath his own floorboards with his grandfather's lies against his heart, understood with sudden terrible clarity that the hunters' rods weren't ringing because the charms were magical.

They were ringing because the charms were *resonant*. Because somewhere under all that dead brass, something was listening.

The floorboard above his head creaked. Light knifed down through the gap—lantern-light, moving, methodical.

"Hollow space under here," a hunter said.

"Then dig," said Captain Vess.

Felix pressed the ledger tighter to his chest and, in the dark, with the sound of iron prying at the boards above him, did the only prayer he knew: he whispered the first line of the oldest page, the one written in brown ink, the one he'd memorized at fifteen and never understood.

*"What is kept must be kept warm."*

The lantern-light stopped moving.

For three full seconds, nothing in the world made a sound—not the rain, not the market, not the hunters' boots.

Then, from directly above him, Captain Vess said quietly, almost to herself: "Well. Hello, Fairweather."

And the floorboards began to come up.
