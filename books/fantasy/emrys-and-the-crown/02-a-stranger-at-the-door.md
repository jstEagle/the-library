## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The rain came down on Greyhollow like a debt being collected.

Felix Fairweather watched it through the warped glass of his shop window, counting the coins in the till for the third time and getting the same answer each time: not enough. Tomorrow the guild's final creditor would come with his ink-stained fingers and his pitying smile, and by evening the sign above the door—*FAIRWEATHER CHARMS & CURIOSITIES*, the letters flaking gold like old skin—would belong to someone else. A chandler, probably. Chandeliers did honest business in Greyhollow. Charms did not, because charms, as Felix told every customer with a straight face and a salesman's warmth, were just brass and wishful thinking.

He was good at saying it. He'd had ten years of practice.

The shop smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular staleness of things no one wanted: luck-knots braided from river grass, iron nails painted blue against nightmares, little brass suns that were supposed to warm a cold room and never had. His grandfather had made them all before Felix was born, back when the Fairweather name still meant something, back when people crossed three districts to buy from them instead of crossing the street to laugh.

Now there was one customer left who mattered, and she was due any moment.

He heard her before he saw her—the quick slap of small shoes on wet cobbles, then a hesitation at the threshold that he knew by heart. Children always hesitated. The world taught them early that shops like this one were where hope went to be overcharged.

"Come in, then," Felix called, not looking up. "It's dry, which is more than the street can say."

She crept in like a cat entering a dog's yard. Nine years old, maybe ten, hair plastered dark against her forehead, a shawl that had been white sometime in the last decade clutched around her shoulders. Her eyes went everywhere at once—to the shelves of tarnished trinkets, to the ledger on the counter, to Felix's face—and settled nowhere.

"You're the charm man," she said. "My gran says you're a fraud."

"Your gran sounds like an excellent judge of character." He set down the coin tray. "What does she want?"

"Not her." The girl's chin came up, and beneath the fear Felix saw something stubborn take root. "Me."

"Ah." He leaned on the counter, making himself smaller, less a shopkeeper and more a conspirator. This part he knew like a prayer. "And what does *you* want?"

Her hands twisted in the shawl. "There's a man," she said. "He comes to our building sometimes. He stands in the stairwell and doesn't say anything, and last week my mam found him outside our door, just... standing. Listening." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Mam says it's nothing. Mam says lots of things are nothing lately."

Felix's chest did something complicated and unwelcome. He smothered it. Sentiment was a luxury; sentiment didn't pay creditors.

"The Watchward charm," he said, turning to the shelf behind him. Third row, behind the love-lures nobody bought, sat a small iron nail wrapped in red thread, sealed in wax, priced at exactly what a frightened child might scrape together. "Old craft. Very old. You hang it inside your door, and ill-intent—" he tapped the counter twice for drama— "*knocks louder.* That's all it does. It makes bad intentions loud enough to hear coming. It won't stop anything. It's just brass and wishful thinking."

"It's iron," the girl said.

Felix blinked. "What?"

"It's iron, not brass. I can see it."

A grin escaped him before he could stop it. "Sharp eyes. Iron, then, and wishful thinking. Still want it?"

She dug in her pocket and produced a small cloth bundle, unfolding it with the ceremony of a queen revealing crown jewels. Three coppers and a button shaped like a fish.

"That's everything I have," she said, daring him to laugh.

He looked at the coins for a long moment. Then he took two coppers and pushed the rest back across the counter, along with the charm.

"Guild discount," he said. "Don't tell your gran."

She took it the way children take things they're afraid will be snatched away—fast, both hands, already turning to go. At the door she stopped.

"Does it work?" she asked, without looking at him. "Really?"

And here was the lie he'd told ten thousand times, worn smooth as a river stone: *it's just brass and wishful thinking*. It kept him safe. It kept everyone safe. Magic was outlawed, magic was ash and gallows, magic was what had gotten half the guilds in this city burned out of their cellars before Felix could walk. Useless charms were legal charms. Useless charms were *alive* charms.

But the girl stood dripping in his doorway with her whole future held in one closed fist, and Felix Fairweather heard himself say:

"Hang it inside the door. Not outside. Inside. And if the knocking ever comes, don't open it, no matter what voice is on the other side."

She looked at him then, really looked, and nodded once, solemn as a funeral. Then she was gone into the rain, and Felix stood alone in his dying shop feeling like a man who had just handed someone a lit candle in a house full of straw.

*Just wishful thinking,* he told himself. *That's all it ever was.*

Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the bells of the Assize rang the hour: six o'clock. Closing time. Time to count what couldn't be counted, lock what couldn't be locked, and sleep badly in the room above the shop for one of the last nights he'd ever own it.

He was reaching for the shutters when the woman fell through his door.

* * *

She came in sideways, like something thrown, and hit the floorboards hard enough to rattle the shelf of dream-bottles. Felix was around the counter before he'd decided to move.

"Madam—here, now—" He caught her under the arms. She was soaked through, but not with rain. Rain wasn't warm. Rain didn't soak a person in slow pulses from somewhere low on their left side.

Blood. Gods, so much blood.

"Door," she rasped. Her hand shot up and gripped his collar with shocking strength, dragging his face down to hers. She was older than he'd first thought, fifty or more, grey-streaked hair escaping its braid, a face built for weathering and weathered past its warranty. Her eyes were the problem. They were too clear, too awake, burning with a purpose that had carried her here through whatever had opened her side like a letter.

"Bar the door," she said. "Then listen. We have very little time."

Every instinct Felix owned screamed at him to prop her up, run for the surgeon, shout for the watch. But her grip said *listen*, and something in her voice—a flat certainty he'd only ever heard in his grandfather's stories—made his hands do what she asked instead. He shoved the bolt home. Turned the deadlock. Pulled the shutter closed over the window with a bang that made them both flinch.

When he turned back she had dragged herself upright against the counter, one hand pressed to her left side, leaving a smear of red across the wood like a signature. In her other hand she held something wrapped in oilcloth, and she held it out to him the way the girl had held out her coppers: fast, both hands, afraid it would be taken.

"Take it."

"I'll fetch a surgeon—"

"There's no time for surgeons and you know it." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "You're a Fairweather. I saw the sign. Don't insult us both."

The name landed in Felix's stomach like a dropped stone. Nobody knew that name anymore. He'd spent a decade making sure of it.

"How do you—"

"*Take it.*"

He took it. The oilcloth was slick with blood and rain. Inside was a map—not paper but something finer, vellum gone soft with age, folded into a shape that fit the palm. A blob of black wax sealed it, stamped with a sigil he didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a door seen edge-on. And tucked against the map, threaded onto a cord, a ring of heavy silver set with a stone the color of deep water.

"What is this?" His voice came out strange. Thin. "Who are you?"

"My name was Maren." She slid down the counter an inch, caught herself. "Emberline. Remember that word if you remember nothing else. *Emberline.* Find the chalk mark under the tannery bridge. Show them the seal. They'll know."

"The Emberline? What is—" He stopped. He'd heard the word once, years ago, whispered by his grandfather over the ledger he kept locked in the cellar, the ledger Felix had never been allowed to read. *If anything happens to me, boy, find the Emberline.* He'd assumed it was a tavern.

"They'll know," Maren repeated. Her breathing had gone ragged, whistling at the edges. "And Felix Fairweather—you listen to me now, because I am about to spend my last breath and I would rather it buy something." She seized his wrist. Her fingers were ice. "The king is not the king."

"...What?"

"Aldric sits the throne. Aldric smiles and waves and signs his name. But Aldric went into the Glass Court three winters ago and something else walked out wearing his face." Her eyes bored into his, and Felix felt the vertigo rise, the floor tilting under a life that had been simple an hour ago. "They call it the Regent. It wears him like a glove. And in nine days it stops wearing him and starts *being* him, permanent, and every hedge-mage from here to the coast dies in the fires it lights to celebrate."

"This is madness," Felix whispered. "This is treason. This is—I sell *luck-charms*. To children. I'm a fraud. My grandfather made pretty junk and I sell it at a markup and tomorrow I lose the shop and none of it—none of it—has ever mattered to anyone—"

"Your grandfather," Maren said, and the words cost her visibly, each one hauled up from somewhere deep, "kept a ledger. Black cover. Brass corners. He hid it because the truth in it would have burned this city down twice over. You've seen it."

Felix's mouth went dry. The cellar. The locked strongbox under the stairs that Grandfather had made him swear on his deathbed never to open until he had no choice left.

"He told me it was debts," Felix heard himself say. "He said it was the guild's debts."

"It's the guild's *purpose*," Maren said. "And yours. The Fairweathers weren't sellers, boy. They were binders. The last of them. And the thing under the capital is waking up because the binding is failing, and the binding fails because there's no Fairweather left to tend it." A bubble of blood rose at the corner of her mouth; she wiped it away impatiently, furious at her own body. "So. No choice left yet?"

Boots. Outside. Many of them, moving fast through the rain, and beneath the drumming of them a sound that turned Felix's blood to well-water: the low, carrying note of a hunter's horn. Once. Twice. The second call cut off abruptly, close, very close, right outside—

"They tracked me," Maren breathed. "Of course they tracked me." She pushed off the counter and stood, swaying, one hand pressed to her wound, and drew from her belt a knife that Felix realized with distant horror was not for defense. "Back door. Cellar. Now."

"Maren—"

"Don't waste my name on grief, spend it on memory. The map. The ring. The bridge. *Go.*"

The front window exploded inward.

Glass rained across the charms, across the counter, across Maren, and through the jagged hole came a gauntleted fist that wrenched the shattered frame aside like paper. A witch-hunter stepped through—grey cloak, steel mask shaped like a smooth blank face, the Crown's inverted torch blazing in white enamel on his breastplate. Behind him in the rain stood four more, crossbows leveled, and beyond them the street filled with neighbors' faces in windows, watching, memorizing, already rehearsing how they'd swear they'd seen nothing.

"Fugitive Maren of the Emberline," the hunter said. His voice was filtered flat through the mask, neither male nor female, a voice like a verdict. "By warrant of the Crown, you are under arrest for heresy, conspiracy, and trafficking in proscribed arts. Surrender the artifact."

Felix's hand had closed around the map without his deciding to. It was slick and warm with her blood.

Maren smiled at him—actually smiled, blood on her teeth, rain-light in her eyes—and mouthed one word: *bridge.*

Then she moved. Not at the hunters—at the lamp.

Her knife took the oil reservoir clean off its chain and her bleeding hand hurled it into the shelf of dream-bottles, and the world went up in a bloom of colored fire, lavender and green and gold, burning spirits and shattered glass and twenty years of useless wonders feeding the blaze. Felix was already moving, down behind the counter, through the curtain, into the back room, Maren's scream—or was it laughter—chasing him through the smoke.

The cellar stairs he took three at a time. The strongbox was where it had always been, under the loose flagstone, and his hands shook so badly the key chattered against the lock like teeth. Black cover. Brass corners. He crammed it inside his coat alongside map and ring, and the weight of all three together was impossible, unbearable, *true*.

Above him, boots thundered across the floorboards. A voice shouted—*"the cellar, the cellar, check the cellar!"*—and another answered, closer, descending.

The coal chute. Grandfather's coal chute, a rusted iron hatch barely wide enough for a boy of fifteen, and Felix was thirty and had eaten too well on too little for a decade. He wedged his shoulder against it anyway, and it groaned, and stuck, and groaned again, and gave with a shriek of rust that he was certain every hunter in Greyhollow heard.

Rain. Mud. The alley behind the shop, narrow and black, running along the backs of the chandler's and the dyer's toward the river. He ran. Smoke billowed from his own roof behind him, orange light strobing off the wet walls, and he did not look back because looking back was a thing done by people with something to return to.

Behind him, hunters poured into the alley. Ahead, the alley forked, and Felix chose left on pure animal instinct and immediately regretted it—a dead end, a wall, a locked gate—and wheeled to find the first masked figure filling the alley mouth, crossbow rising, unhurried, certain.

The bolt loosed.

It never landed. From the rooftop above the gate something small and fast and shrieking dropped onto the hunter's arm, and the bolt went wide, and the figure—small, hooded, wrapped in rags that moved like live things—hit the ground between Felix and the hunters and turned a face toward him. Young. Wild. Eyes like banked coals.

"Well?" the stranger snapped, wrenching the gate's lock apart with bare hands that smoked faintly at the knuckles. "Are you the Fairweather or aren't you? Because if you're not, I just committed arson for nothing, and I'll be *furious*."

Somewhere behind them, Maren's shop—his shop—collapsed inward in a gout of sparks.

Felix Fairweather, fraud, bankrupt, orphaned twice in one night, clutched a dead woman's ring against his heart and ran through the open gate into the dark.
