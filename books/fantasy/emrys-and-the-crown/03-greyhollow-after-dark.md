## 3. Greyhollow After Dark

The witch-hunters came through the front door of the Fairweather shop at the stroke of midnight, and Felix was already three streets away, running with his lungs full of knives.

He had gone out the cellar. That much he owed to ten years of paranoia and a grandfather who'd built escape routes into everything—the trapdoor under the rug, the coal chute that opened onto a tanner's yard, the loose brick in the garden wall with a hollow behind it where the ledger now sat wrapped in oilcloth. Grandfather Fairweather had been a man who expected the world to come for him eventually. Felix had always thought that was just the old man's gloom talking. It turned out it had been a prophecy with a fifty-year fuse.

He crouched in the shadow of a cooper's shed, chest heaving, and made himself count to one hundred. Above him, Greyhollow climbed the hillside in crooked tiers, its thousand chimneys breathing smoke into a moonless sky. Somewhere up there, lanterns were moving. Not many. Three, maybe four, moving in the disciplined way hunters moved—not searching, *herding*. They knew which way a frightened man would run. They were leaving those ways open on purpose.

Felix pressed his back against the shed and took stock. He had the coat on his back, eleven coppers, a half-eaten sausage in his pocket, a dead woman's signet ring threaded on a cord around his neck, and a map sealed in wax that weighed more than any object he'd ever carried. The weight wasn't in the paper. It was in what she'd said with her last breath, her blood soaking into his floorboards: *the king is not the king.*

He should have thrown it all in the river. That was the sensible thing. Sell the ring, keep the coppers, be nobody again by morning. Ten years of being nobody had kept him alive when every other charm-seller in Greyhollow had been dragged to the pyres or the gallows or the slow gray death of the hunter's brand.

But the woman had looked at him—looked at him the way you look at a door when the house is burning—and she had chosen him. Chosen the shop that sold nothing. Chosen the apprentice of a guild everyone said was finished.

*They'll know why,* she'd whispered. *Fairweathers always knew.*

And so Felix did the least sensible thing he had ever done in a life built entirely on sensible cowardice. He turned away from the river and ran uphill, toward the rooftops, toward the dark, carrying a dead woman's errand like a lit fuse.

* * *

The roofs of Greyhollow were a country unto themselves, and Felix had grown up poor enough to know its borders. As a boy he'd raced other gutter-rats along the ridge-tiles for pennies, and later, when the guild died by inches, he'd learned the higher routes for reasons that had nothing to do with play: a man who could cross the city without touching a street could not be followed by men who only knew how to walk streets.

He went up the cooper's drainpipe hand over hand, boots finding the old notches his own feet had worn, and came out onto the slate spine of Tanner's Row. The wind up there smelled of smoke and river and the sour reek of the tannery pits below. He moved fast and low, weight on the balls of his feet, avoiding the loose tiles he knew by heart the way other men knew the names of streets.

Behind him, a lantern flared at the mouth of Cooper's Lane.

Felix froze against a chimney stack, pressing himself into the cold brick. Below, three figures in the grey-and-scarlet of the Crown's hunt fanned out across the lane. And at their center walked a woman taller than the rest, unhurried, her lantern held high as if she were inspecting a property rather than hunting a man.

Captain Vess.

Felix had seen her twice before, from a distance. Everyone in Greyhollow had. She was the reason the charm-burnings had gotten efficient. There were stories about her—that she could smell magic the way hounds smelled blood; that she'd once tracked a hedge-witch for nine days through the marshes with no food and no sleep; that she read people's faces like ledgers and never wrote down a wrong number. Watching her now, head tilted, studying the gap between two rooftops as though the architecture itself might confess, Felix believed every story.

She stopped. She raised one hand, and her hunters stilled like cut strings.

Then, slowly, deliberately, Captain Vess looked straight up—at the chimney stack, at the darkness above it—and smiled.

Felix didn't wait to learn whether she could actually see him or whether it was theater. He ran.

Slate sang under his boots. He vaulted the gap over Gin Court, hit the next roof rolling, came up sprinting. Behind him a whistle blew—two short notes, then a third, some hunter's cant he couldn't parse—and answering whistles bloomed across the city like a net being cast. They weren't chasing him anymore. They were *fencing* him, driving him, letting the rooftops funnel him down toward the streets where the net would close.

Fine. Let them think they were herding a rabbit.

Felix reached the edge of the tanners' quarter, where the roofs gave out and the ground began, and instead of descending he dropped flat, slid down the last slope of shingles on his heels, and let himself fall into the black water of the drainage channel below.

The cold hit him like a slap from a giant. He surfaced gasping, mouth full of the taste of the whole city's filth, and swam for the arched mouth of the sewer outfall before the ripples of his entry had faded. Behind and above, he heard Vess's voice, clear and cold across the water:

"He's gone to ground. Seal the grates on Miller Street and Cordwainer Row. Nobody leaves the quarter tonight." A pause. "He's still in my city. That's enough."

Felix hung in the black water inside the tunnel's mouth, teeth chattering, and thought: *she talks about me like I'm worth the trouble.* Some small, stupid part of him was almost flattered. The rest of him was busy remembering that the things worth trouble were the things that got burned.

He waded into the dark.

* * *

The sewers of Greyhollow were older than the sewers of most cities, because Greyhollow had been built on top of something older than itself, and the first builders had simply borrowed the tunnels and called them theirs. Felix knew the main galleries the way any shopkeeper near the river knew them—which ones flooded with the tide, which ones the rat-catchers worked, which grate led up behind the fishmongers' hall. But the map wanted none of those.

He found a dry ledge where an old storm-drain met the main channel, wrung out his coat as best he could, and unrolled the dead woman's map by the light of a stub of candle he kept in a waterproofed tin—for a charm-seller who spent his nights avoiding the law, a little fire that couldn't be seen from a window was the closest thing to honest magic he owned.

The map was beautiful and mad. It showed Greyhollow, but not the Greyhollow above—it showed the bones beneath, tunnels and chambers drawn in faded iron-gall ink, and here and there small symbols he didn't recognize, each one numbered. At the top, in a cramped antique hand, a single line: *From the first door, walk backward through the kingdom.*

The first marker was close. That was either luck or design, and Felix was too tired and too scared to decide which frightened him more. A symbol—a circle crossed by three descending strokes, like a claw raking downward—was inked at a junction beneath the tannery district, with a note beside it in the same cramped hand: *the bridge that smells of salt and rot. Look under, not over.*

Under the old tannery bridge. Felix knew it. Every child in the quarter knew it—the humped stone span where the tannery waste met the river, crusted white with generations of chemical scab, the one place in Greyhollow even the beggars avoided after dark. Of course the outlaw mages would meet there. No hunter in the Crown's service would search a place they wouldn't touch with a boot.

He memorized the route, pinched out the candle, and walked.

It took him the better part of two hours, wading and climbing through the dark, counting side-tunnels by the map's instructions, twice flattening himself into alcoves while distant light swept past—hunters' lanterns, down in the sewers, which meant Vess hadn't believed her own order to seal the streets. She was looking under the city too. The thought put ice in his spine that had nothing to do with the water.

At last the tunnel he followed began to change. The brickwork grew older, mortared with something dark and glassy. The air shifted—less filth, more salt, and under the salt a faint mineral tang like struck flint. And then, ahead, a gray suggestion of open air, and the sound of the river, and the enormous dripping shadow of the tannery bridge overhead, its underside furred with white crust, exactly as foul as legend promised.

Felix stood in the shallows beneath the arch and felt suddenly, absurdly self-conscious. He was soaked, filthy, exhausted, holding a dead woman's map in a haunted tunnel, about to announce himself to a fellowship of outlawed mages—the kind of people whose existence he'd spent ten years pretending not to believe in.

"Hello?" he said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, which rather ruined the effect.

Nothing answered. Water dripped. Somewhere far off, a rat expressed an opinion.

"I was told—" He stopped. He hadn't been told anything. The woman had died before she could tell him anything except that the king was not the king. He had a symbol and a seal and a ring, and a hope thin as thread. He lifted the signet ring on its cord, holding it up toward the pale gray light. "I have this. From a woman who died in my shop. She said—she said you'd know."

Still nothing. Long enough that Felix's shoulders began to drop, that the exhaustion started whispering its old sweet lie—*go home, give it up, be nobody, nobody survives being somebody*—

"You're standing in her blood, you know."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, low and dry as sand shifting. Felix spun, heart slamming. On the ledge above the waterline, in a fold of shadow he would have sworn had been empty, sat a figure. As his eyes adjusted he made out a woman—young, maybe his age, maybe a few years older—dressed in dark leathers patched with river-clay, a short blade lying across her knees with the ease of long habit. She had a sharp face, sharp eyes, and hair cropped close to her skull, and she was looking at him the way a fishmonger looks at a fish: assessing freshness, weighing price.

"The floorboards," she said. "Of your little shop on Candlemakers' Walk. Blood soaks down. Blood remembers. We've been reading your floor for two days." She tilted her head. "You buried her in Potter's Field with no name and paid for it yourself. Then you went home and slept in the room where she died. Either very brave or very stupid."

"Both," Felix said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Who are you?"

"Sable." She said it like a knife laid on a table. "And you're the Fairweather boy. The one who sells charms that don't work."

"They work," Felix said, before he could stop himself, some old guild pride rising out of the grave of him. Then, honestly: "No. They don't. Brass and wishful thinking. That's what I tell people."

"And is it true?"

Felix looked at her. In the gray light under the bridge, with the river muttering beyond, he decided—why not—to stop lying for one minute of his life. "I don't know anymore. Two days ago I'd have said yes. Tonight I've got a map that says my family's guild was keeping something caged, and a captain of witch-hunters smiling at me through a chimney stack, and a dead woman's ring that apparently opens doors. So no. I don't know what's true. I only know I'm not giving this back." He tightened his fist around the ring. "If you've come to rob me, do it quickly. I've got eleven coppers and a sausage."

Something flickered across Sable's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. It might have been surprise. It might, almost, have been approval.

"Eleven coppers," she repeated. "You broke quarantine on your whole life for eleven coppers and a sausage."

"For a dead woman who trusted me with her last breath," Felix said. "That part's not for sale."

Sable slid down from the ledge, landing soft as spilled ink. She circled him once, close enough that he caught river-mud and woodsmoke and something sharper underneath—tar, maybe, or pitch. She stopped in front of him and held out her hand, palm up. Not for the ring. For the map.

Felix hesitated. Every instinct honed by ten years in Greyhollow screamed at him: *never let go of the thing they want, because the moment they have it, you're worth nothing.* But the woman had died pressing it into his hands, and Sable had watched him bury her unnamed and pay for the grave, and had said *brave or stupid* in a voice that made both sound like virtues.

He gave her the map.

She unrolled it with practiced fingers, tilting it to catch the gray light, and when her eyes found the wax seal at its corner—the same seal stamped into the ring at Felix's throat—she went very still. It lasted only a heartbeat. Then her face closed like a shutter.

"Where did she say to go?" Sable asked quietly.

"She didn't get the chance. Just—the king is not the king. And that Fairweathers would know why."

Sable rolled the map with the same care one shows a wounded bird and handed it back. "There's a place downriver," she said. "People who can answer what that map asks. People who knew her." Her eyes flicked to the ring at his throat, and something moved behind them, deep down, like a shape passing under ice. "People who will want to meet the man she chose."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take your eleven coppers and your sausage and leave you to Captain Vess, who has already offered forty crowns for you alive and doesn't strike me as a woman who needs the money." Sable shrugged. "But you didn't crawl through a sewer at night to say no. You crawled through a sewer because for the first time in your careful little life, somebody handed you a truth bigger than your fear. Am I wrong?"

Felix opened his mouth to argue and found, to his considerable irritation, nothing there to argue with.

"There's a barge," Sable said, already moving, fluid and certain, back toward the river mouth of the tunnel. "Leaves on the ebb tide, an hour before dawn. Cargo wool, mostly, and one passenger who doesn't exist. If you're coming, don't be late, and don't be followed." She paused at the edge of the light, silhouetted against the gray breath of the river, and looked back at him. "Fairweather. Why did you sleep in the room where she died?"

Felix considered lying. It was his trade, after all; he was very good at it.

"Because she died alone in a stranger's house," he said, "and I didn't want her to be alone for it. Even after."

For a moment Sable said nothing. The river filled the silence, patient and enormous.

"That," she said finally, "is the stupidest, bravest thing I've heard all year." And there—just for an instant, there and gone like the fish-turn of her earlier smile—was something in her face that was not sharpness at all. "Ebb tide. Don't be late."

Then she was gone into the dark, silent as water finding its level, and Felix stood alone under the tannery bridge with a dead woman's ring against his throat and a living woman's voice still ringing in his ears.

Above him, far off across the sleeping city, a whistle sounded—two short notes, then a third. Closer than before. Captain Vess, walking her city, patient as winter, certain that everything hidden in Greyhollow would eventually come up for air.

Felix looked downriver, toward the ebb tide and the barge and whatever waited at the end of the map's first line.

An hour before dawn. Don't be late. Don't be followed.

He pulled his soaked coat tight around the map, and went to find out how a man who didn't exist traveled downriver.
