## 4. The Emberline

Sable had smuggled a lot of things down the Vell in her years on the water: untaxed brandy, fugitive printers, a caged lynx once, for reasons she'd never fully understood. She had never smuggled a boy who looked like he might apologize to her boat.

"Sit still," she said, without turning around. "You're rocking her."

Felix Fairweather froze mid-fidget, one hand hovering over the gunwale as if the wood might bite. "Sorry. Sorry. I just—do you usually go this fast?"

"I usually go faster." She eased the tiller and let the current take them around the bend where the river widened and went quiet, sliding between banks of black reeds. Greyhollow's smoke was behind them now, a bruise of grey against the last light. Ahead there was nothing but water going the color of pewter and the first stars coming out over the fens. "The hunters will have your description by midnight. By morning they'll have dogs. So we make the quarry before either."

"The quarry," Felix said. "The flooded one. The one everyone says is cursed."

"Everyone says that because it's convenient." Sable shipped the small lantern she kept unlit at her feet—no light until they were past the watch-station at Millward Reach—and let the dark do its work. "Cursed places don't get searched. Cursed places get *avoided*. Best neighbors a person can have."

He was quiet for a while after that. She could hear him thinking, which was itself a kind of noise—the boy thought loudly, the way green things did. She'd known him for less than a day and already she could map his moods by sound: the rustle of fear, the creak of questions he was too polite to ask, and underneath it all something stubborn and banked, like a coal under ash.

She'd seen his face when she'd recognized the seal on the dead woman's map. He'd expected her to rob him. She'd watched him brace for it, watched him decide, in the space of half a breath, that if she tried he would fight her with whatever useless brass trinkets he had in his pockets. It had been, frankly, the most honest thing about him.

"You knew her," he said at last. "The woman. You said you recognized the seal."

Sable pulled an oar stroke and didn't answer immediately. The reeds whispered past. Somewhere off to the east a marsh-bird made its idiot crying sound.

"Merrin Vale," she said finally. "That was her name. She ran messages for people who couldn't afford to be seen running them. Including me." She adjusted her grip on the tiller. "I haven't seen her in two years. She had a laugh like a door hinge and she cheated at cards so badly it was almost religious. And now some witch-hunter's clerk is writing *unidentified female, deceased* in a ledger somewhere, and that's all she gets to be."

Felix was silent. Then, quietly: "I held her hand. At the end. I didn't know what else to do."

"That's more than most people get," Sable said. "Most people die alone in ditches waiting for someone brave enough to stop." She heard him swallow. Good. Let him carry a little of it. He'd carry less alone than he would pretending it hadn't happened.

The watch-station light slid by far off to their left—a single yellow eye above the water, bored, half-asleep. Sable kept the boat in the deep shadow of the eastern bank and breathed again only when it was well behind them.

"Now," she said, "while we've got hours and nothing to look at: tell me about the map. Not where it goes. What you saw when you opened it."

"It's wax-sealed. Blood in the seal—that's hers, I think, or someone's. Inside there's a route marked in stages, but not place names. Symbols. A bridge with a chalk mark. A tree split by lightning. A—" he hesitated, and she heard him turn pages in his memory, careful, respectful, "—a door. Drawn twice. Once open, once shut. And words around the border in a script I can't read, except one word keeps repeating. *Emrys.*"

Sable's hands went very still on the tiller.

She felt it happen and hated that it happened—that old cold slide down the back of the neck, the body remembering things the mind wanted to file away. *Emrys.* The binder-word. The word the old families used before the Crown burned the old families. She hadn't heard it spoken aloud since she was nine years old, crouched under her grandmother's table while boots came up the stairs.

"You know it," Felix said. Not a question. He was learning, at least, when something mattered.

"I know it's a word you don't say on open water," she said. "Or closed water. Or any water. Row."

* * *

They reached the quarry an hour past full dark.

It announced itself first as a change in the air—the river smell giving way to something mineral and cold, wet stone and standing water, the particular silence of a place where the ground had been torn out and never quite healed. Then the trees thickened, old alders leaning over the channel, and the channel itself narrowed until Sable had to ship the oars entirely and pull them along by hand through branches that dragged at their hair like curious fingers.

"There's no path," Felix whispered.

"There's a path. You're looking with your eyes." She found the marker she was listening for—a hollowed stone post, just breaking the surface, worn smooth by decades of rope—and hooked her line around it. "Eyes are how hunters find things. We don't use eyes here."

Beyond the alders the world simply ended. That was the trick of the quarry: from the water it looked like a wall of black rock rising sheer into the night, forty feet of it, seamless. Every map in Greyhollow showed the same thing—an exhausted mine, flooded to the rim, abandoned sixty years ago when the last crew walked out and the pumps failed and the Crown decided the hole wasn't worth filling or fencing.

The Crown, as usual, had been wrong about what was worth keeping.

Sable brought the boat flat against the cliff base and held two fingers to her lips. "When I knock," she murmured, "you say nothing. Whatever you see, whoever you see—you say *nothing* until Mother Quill asks you a direct question. Then you answer it honestly. She'll know if you don't. Everyone thinks they can lie to her. Nobody can. It's embarrassing, actually. Grown men, hardened criminals, reduced to stammering."

"What happens if I stammer?"

"She'll feed you. Stammerers get extra bread. It's a whole system."

She reached up along the rock face in the dark, counting by touch—third ledge, hand's width right, the seam that wasn't a seam—and knocked. Four times, pause, twice. The pattern fell into the stone and vanished, swallowed, and for three long breaths nothing answered.

Then the cliff opened.

There was no grinding of gears, no dramatic rumble. A section of rock simply swung inward on greased pivots, silent as a page turning, and warm yellow light spilled out across the black water along with the smell of woodsmoke, bread, and drying herbs. A girl of maybe fifteen stood in the gap with a crossbow held with the easy boredom of long practice, looked at Sable, looked at Felix, and stepped aside.

"In," she said. "Boat comes too. There's a hoist."

* * *

Inside, the quarry was not drowned at all—it was *kept*, which was different.

The floodwater sat forty feet below the rim, black and mirror-still, and around its edge, built into ledges and galleries cut into the living rock, the Emberline had grown like ivy over sixty years. Walkways of salvaged timber zigzagged between cave-mouths fitted with doors and shutters. Lamplight glowed behind oiled windows. Laundry lines crossed the upper galleries. Somewhere below, a hammer rang on an anvil; somewhere else, someone was singing badly and being told so by several voices at once. Water dripped somewhere in the deeps with the patience of a clock.

Felix stood on the landing platform and stared, and Sable watched him stare, and understood exactly what was happening behind his face. Ten years selling lies in a market stall. Ten years believing he was the last ember of a dead thing. And now this: a hundred lives, hidden in a wound in the earth, burning quietly the whole time.

"It's real," he said. His voice cracked on the second word.

"Don't get sentimental on the platform, it's slippery." But she said it gently, and steered him toward the central stair.

The great hearth-hall occupied the largest gallery, a cavern tall enough to hold smoke comfortably, floored in packed earth and river stone. A fire burned in a proper stone hearth—real fire, unhidden, the kind of extravagance that would have gotten every soul in the room hanged within the week—and around it perhaps thirty people sat working at the thousand small tasks of survival: mending nets, grinding herbs, arguing over a ledger, teaching children letters on slates. Hedge-mages, every one of them, or kin to hedge-mages. People whose crime was existing with a gift in their blood.

Conversations dipped as they entered—not silence, the Emberline was too practiced for silence, but a lowering, a collective tilt of attention. Sable felt Felix shrink half a step toward her and resisted the urge to roll her eyes. He'd have to learn to be looked at eventually.

And then the crowd parted, and Mother Quill came through it, and everything else in the hall became scenery.

Sable had met powerful people. She'd sold contraband to magistrates and outridden a customs captain who'd once made a baron cry. None of them carried weight the way Quill did. She was not large—a small, round, grey-haired woman in an apron, flour to the elbows, the kind of grandmother who appeared in paintings holding pies—but she moved through her hall the way the tide moves through a harbor, and every head turned to her without seeming to decide to.

Her eyes found Sable first. "You're late."

"River was slow. Also I brought you a guest." Sable stepped aside. "Mother Quill—Felix Fairweather. Of the Fairweathers."

The name moved through the hall like wind through wheat. *Fairweather.* Sable heard it repeated in whispers, saw an old man by the fire set down his mug with a shaking hand. She'd guessed at the reaction on the river and been wrong about its size. They weren't surprised. They were *waiting*. Some of these people had been waiting their whole lives.

Quill looked at Felix for a long moment. Looked at him the way she looked at everything—thoroughly, kindly, and without a single blind spot.

"So," she said. "The last apprentice. You have your grandfather's hands."

Felix glanced down at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him. "I—I don't understand. How could you—"

"Later. All of it later." Quill wiped her hands on her apron, and then, businesslike: "You carry something that belongs to me. May I?"

He hesitated—Sable saw the war move across his face, ten years of Greyhollow caution against something older and hungrier—and then he reached into his coat and drew out the dead woman's signet ring, and held it out on his open palm.

What happened next, Sable would remember for the rest of her life.

Mother Quill looked at the ring. And the tide went out of her.

It was small, the collapse—she didn't fall, didn't even sway visibly. But the whole hall saw it, the way a field sees weather change. Her hand rose halfway and stopped. Her lips formed a name and for a moment no sound came out of her at all, and this woman who never lost a single thread of composure stood in front of thirty of her people holding absolutely still, the way people do when moving even slightly will break something that cannot be repaired.

"Merrin," she said.

The name dropped into the hall like a stone into the flooded dark below them.

"My daughter," Mother Quill said, and her voice was steady and terrible and completely ruined all at once. "My daughter's ring."

Nobody spoke. The hammering below had stopped. Even the bad singer had gone quiet. In the hearth, a log settled and sent up a fountain of sparks, and the light moved across Quill's face and showed Sable what she'd missed at first glance: the flour-dusted hands weren't shaking from age. They were shaking from the effort of not closing around nothing.

"How?" Quill asked Felix, softly.

"She came to my shop. Bleeding. Hunters behind her. She gave me the map and the ring and she said—" Felix's voice wavered, and he steadied it with visible effort, and Sable thought: *good, boy, hold it, she needs you to hold it* — "she said, *the king is not the king.* And then she died. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I held her hand but I couldn't—I didn't know how to help her. I sell charms that don't work. I couldn't do anything."

Something shifted in the hall at that. Sable felt it rather than heard it—a ripple through the crowd, heads turning to each other, an old woman pressing her knuckles to her mouth. Because everyone here knew what the Fairweathers had been. Everyone here knew what *charms that don't work* meant, and what it meant that the last one of them was standing in their hall saying it like a confession.

Quill took the ring at last. Closed her hand around it. Brought it to her lips, briefly, like a kiss given to a forehead.

Then she straightened, and the tide came back in, and she was Mother Quill again—except for her eyes, which stayed wet and furious and grieving, and would stay that way, Sable suspected, for a very long time.

"You held her hand," Quill said. "In her last hour, in a strange city, hunted and dying, my daughter was not alone. Do you understand what that is worth? Do you have any idea?" She didn't wait for an answer. She took Felix's face in both floury hands, the ring pressed between them, and looked into him the way she looked into everyone, all the way down. "You will eat tonight. You will sleep under a roof that has never once been found. And tomorrow, boy, we are going to talk about your grandfather's ledger, and about what the word *Emrys* means, and about why the Crown has spent sixty years making sure the last Fairweather believed himself worthless."

She released him. Turned to the hall. Raised her voice, and it filled the cavern the way the firelight did, touching every corner.

"Merrin Vale is dead," she said, "killed by the Crown's hounds, and the last Fairweather stands in our hearth. Grieve tomorrow. Tonight we feed a guest."

And the hall roared back at her—grief and fury and welcome braided into one sound—and Felix Fairweather flinched at the noise of it, and then, slowly, stopped flinching.

* * *

Later, much later, when the bread had been eaten and the songs sung badly on purpose and Felix had been claimed by a bunk in the east gallery by three separate grandmothers arguing over who got to feed him breakfast, Sable found Quill alone at the hearth, turning the signet ring over and over in her fingers.

Sable sat down beside her without asking. That was allowed. That had always been allowed.

"He doesn't know," Quill said. It wasn't a question.

"Not a scrap. He thinks his charms are junk. He thinks his guild was con artists. He apologized to me for existing, roughly, four times on the river."

Quill huffed something that was almost a laugh. The firelight caught the ring's seal: a door, drawn open, wreathed in a script older than the kingdom. "Merrin wrote to me, you know. Last spring. Said she'd found something in the capital archives. Said the binding under the palace was failing, and that the signs were starting to show." Her thumb traced the little carved door. "I told her to come home. I told her it could keep another season."

Sable said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie.

Quill closed her fist around the ring and looked into the fire, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic with lives.

"They'll come for him, Sable. The hunters. Maybe not tomorrow, maybe not this month, but they'll come, because a Fairweather alive is a loose thread in a garment the Crown has spent sixty years sewing shut. And when they come, everything my daughter died carrying rests on whether that frightened boy in the east gallery can become what his blood says he is." She turned her head. Her eyes were dry now, and bright as coins. "So tomorrow we begin his lessons. Wake him up. Teach him what his hands are for."

"And if he can't learn?"

Quill looked back at the fire. Above the mantel, half-hidden in shadow, hung an ancient iron key on a leather cord, black with age, that Sable had never once seen anyone touch.

"Then," Mother Quill said quietly, "we had better pray the door holds longer than Merrin thought it would."
