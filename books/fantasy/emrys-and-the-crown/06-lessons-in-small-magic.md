## 6. Lessons in Small Magic

The flooded quarry smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and for the first three days Felix was certain he would die there.

Not by violence—by drowning. Mother Quill's first lesson was to stand chest-deep in the quarry's green water at dawn and hold a single brass charm above the surface without letting it wake. "A binder who cannot be still," she said, "is a lit fuse in a powder store." The water was cold enough to make his teeth chatter like dice in a cup, and the charm sat in his palm doing absolutely nothing, as it had done nothing his entire life, and he stood there shivering and thinking: *ten years of this. Ten years of selling nothing to people who needed something, and the whole time it was sleeping.*

On the fourth morning, the charm woke.

He hadn't meant it to. He'd been thinking about Sable's laugh—the way she laughed *at* him, mostly, sharp as a filleting knife—and about the warmth of the hearth-hall, and about how the dead courier's blood had felt tacky against his palm when she pressed the map into it, and the brass disc in his hand went warm as a held bird. Light moved under its surface like a fish turning in shallow water. The quarry pool around him bloomed with pale rings, one after another, spreading out from his chest.

Quill, on the bank, dropped her tea.

"Stop," she said. "Felix. *Stop.*"

"I'm not doing anything!"

"That," said Quill, wading in fully clothed to pry the charm from his fingers, "is rather the point."

* * *

The Emberline kept its hearth in the quarry's old pump-house, a low stone building half-swallowed by the hillside, its rooms carved deeper over generations until no one could say where the masonry ended and the cave began. There were perhaps forty of them. Hedge-mages, river-witches, a former cathedral bell-ringer who could sing stone into shapes, twin sisters who read futures in the drift of smoke. All of them outlawed. All of them, Quill said, alive because they were small.

"Small magic leaves small footprints," she told Felix that evening, laying his grandfather's ledger open on the trestle table between them. "The Crown hunts fire-speakers and storm-callers because they're loud. A warming charm, a luck-knot, a ward stitched into a coat hem—those slip beneath notice. Your guild understood that better than anyone. You didn't sell trinkets, boy. You seeded the whole city with anchors and called them junk."

The ledger's pages were dense with his grandfather's cramped hand—not inventory, as Felix had always believed, but instructions. *Anchor, third order. Waking word withheld. Bloodline only.* Diagrams of charms Felix had sold by the hundred: the brass discs, the knotted red cords, the little tin birds that were supposed to bring safe travel. Each entry ended with the same notation, repeated so often it had become almost a prayer:

*Sleeps until a Fairweather speaks.*

"Your grandfather didn't hide the craft from you," Quill said gently. "He hid you from the craft. There's a difference."

Felix stared at the page until the letters swam. Ten years. Ten years of lying to frightened children, of watching people walk away with brass and wishful thinking while he hated himself for the smile he wore doing it—and every piece of it had been true. Every useless charm had been a loaded spring, waiting. He thought of the girl from the market raid, the one who'd bought the luck-charm with her last two coppers. Somewhere in Greyhollow she was still carrying it. Still carrying a piece of a spell older than the kingdom, and never knowing.

"What happens if I speak to all of them?" he asked quietly. "Every charm I ever sold?"

Quill was silent a long moment. "Then Greyhollow becomes the most heavily warded city in the world overnight," she said. "And every witch-hunter in the province feels it happen." She closed the ledger. "One thing at a time."

* * *

Wren found him that night, which was how Wren did everything—found him, arrived at him, erupted into his vicinity like weather.

She was sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and scorched hems, with hair cropped raggedly short and eyes the color of banked coals. She'd been watching him for days from rafters and doorways; he'd catch the orange flicker of her reflection in windows and turn to find nothing but a heat-shimmer and the smell of struck flint.

"You're the Fairweather," she said now, dropping from the pump-house roof into his path with a sound like a sack of cats.

"I'm aware."

"You made the pool glow." She fell into step beside him, uninvited, radiating heat like a forge-door. "Tam says you did it backwards. Says you woke it by accident instead of on purpose, which means your doors don't latch. That's either very bad or very good."

"Which is it?"

"Dunno yet." She grinned, and her teeth were very white in the dark. "Show me a charm."

He hesitated, then fished one from his pocket—a tin bird, wings folded, meant for safe passage. He'd carried it for years the way other men carried a saint's medal. He held it out.

Wren didn't take it. She leaned close and breathed on it, a long slow exhale, and the air around her lips shimmered—and the tin bird twitched. Once. Twice. Then it stretched its stamped wings with a groan of metal and lifted off Felix's palm in a lazy spiral, trailing sparks, and circled his head twice before landing on Wren's shoulder and folding itself primly closed.

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "It's never done that."

"It's never been near me before." She shrugged, dislodging the bird, which fluttered back to Felix as if embarrassed. "Fire wakes things. That's why they burned my village." She said it flatly, the way you'd report the price of bread. "Anyway. Tam wants to meet you. He doesn't want anything, actually, he can't want things out loud anymore, but he's been scratching your name on his slate all day, which is basically shouting."

* * *

Tam lived at the far end of the pump-house, in a room that smelled of cedar oil and lamp-black. He was old the way roots are old—gnarled, patient, apparently immovable—and when Felix entered he was already sitting at his workbench, slate balanced on his knee, chalk in hand.

*So,* the slate read. *The last Fairweather.*

"You know my name."

*Everyone knows your name. Quill talks in her sleep.* He set down the chalk and turned, and Felix saw it then: the brand. A high collar of scar tissue wrapped the old man's throat, shiny and ridged, the mark the witch-hunters pressed into the flesh of mages whose voices could carry spells. It had taken his voice with it. He'd been silent, Wren had told Felix, for thirty years.

*I was a singer,* the slate said, when Felix's eyes lingered too long. *Before. Stone-songs. My family built bridges that are still standing, though no one remembers our name. The hunters remembered.*

"I'm sorry," Felix said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

Tam studied him for a moment, then beckoned. On the bench before him lay a charm Felix recognized down to the scratch on its rim—one of his own. He'd sold it in Greyhollow four years ago, a warming disc, five coppers, to a dockhand with a cough. *How—*

*Traded through six hands and upriver. Things find their way home here. Everything does.* Tam tapped the disc. *Wake it. Properly this time. Quill will have taught you the first word by now.*

"She has. I just—" Felix turned the disc over in his fingers. "Every time I try, either nothing happens or everything happens. There's no middle."

*There is no middle,* Tam wrote. *There is only control, and control is boring, and boring takes years. But you don't have years. So we cheat.* He tapped his own branded throat, then pointed at Felix's mouth. *You'll be my voice. And I'll be your leash. Sit.*

What followed was the strangest hour of Felix's life. Tam would write an instruction—*breathe out, not in, when you speak the word*—and Felix would obey, and the warming disc would flare hot enough to scorch the bench, and Tam would rap his knuckles with the chalk and write *again, softer.* They worked until midnight. The disc went from bonfire to hearth to candle to the exact warmth of a held hand, and when Felix finally set it down, steady and humming and *obedient*, Tam looked at it for a long time.

Then he wrote, slowly: *Your grandfather bound things the whole world was afraid of. Do you understand what that means?*

"That he was powerful?"

*No.* Tam underlined the next words twice. *That he was trusted. Power is common. Trust is the rare metal. The Emberline has forty mages and maybe three the others would follow into a dark place. If your blood runs true, we may need you in a dark place soon, Fairweather. I'd like to know now if you'll hold.*

Felix looked at the scarred throat, the patient hands, the slate dusted with chalk like the first snow of winter. "I sold lies for ten years," he said. "I'd like to spend whatever comes next holding true things."

Something moved across the old man's face that might once, before the brand, have been a smile. He wiped the slate clean and wrote one final line:

*Good. Dawn tomorrow. Bring the bird.*

* * *

The weeks took on a rhythm, and the rhythm became a life.

Dawns belonged to Tam: breath and word and restraint, Felix learning to hold a working the way you hold a full cup crossing a crowded room. Mornings belonged to Quill and the ledger: binding-grammar, anchor-craft, the deep architecture of spells laid down before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather wore the crown. Afternoons belonged to Wren, who appointed herself his self-declared sparring partner on the grounds that "someone has to find out what breaks you before the hunters do, and I like breaking things."

She broke a great deal. She melted two of his practice charms, singed off his left eyebrow, and once set the entire herb-drying rack alight while trying to demonstrate a technique she described as "basically safe." In return, Felix learned to weave wards fast enough to turn her flame—sometimes. Their sessions always drew an audience, because watching the quiet charm-seller and the feral fire-girl circle each other in the quarry yard was the best entertainment the Emberline had produced in years.

"You're getting faster," Wren panted one afternoon, smoke curling off her sleeves, both of them grinning like idiots.

"You're getting predictable."

"Liar." She threw a spark at his head purely on principle, and he caught it—actually caught it, snaring the little flame midair in a lattice of woven charm-light and closing his fist around it until it died warm and harmless in his palm. The audience cheered. Wren stared at his closed fist with an expression Felix had never seen on her before: wonder, naked and unguarded.

"Do that again," she whispered.

"No. Trade secret." He opened his hand and let the last warmth drift free. "I'll teach you, though. If you teach me how you breathe fire without burning your own face off."

"Deal." She spat in her palm and held it out, and after a moment's hesitation Felix spat in his and shook it, and that was that. That was how it was done here. He was learning the customs.

Evenings belonged to the hearth-hall, and those were the hours Felix came to treasure most and trust least, because happiness still felt to him like a borrowed coat—he kept waiting for someone to demand it back. Sable would appear with cards and cheat magnificently. The bell-ringer sang the stones of the ceiling into slow-turning patterns overhead, constellations of their own invention. Tam sat by the fire writing jokes on his slate that made Wren laugh so hard she ignited the cushions, twice. And Felix sat among them with his hands around a mug of nettle tea, and thought: *this is what the shop was supposed to feel like. This is what I was selling, all along, and didn't know it.*

One night, near the embers, Tam touched his elbow and held up the slate.

*You sleep easier than you did.*

"I do?" 

*First week, you slept like a man listening for boots on stairs. Now you sleep like a person.* The chalk hovered. *Whatever you were carrying in Greyhollow—you can put some of it down here. We'll keep watch. That's the arrangement. That's what the Emberline is.*

Felix looked around the hall—at Sable arguing furiously over a hand of cards she was definitely cheating at, at Wren asleep in a heap of singed blankets with a tin bird perched on her knee, at Quill in the corner mending a net with the same patience she brought to everything—and found that his eyes had gone hot and his throat tight, and that for once he didn't mind anyone seeing.

"Thank you," he said.

Tam erased the slate and wrote: *Don't thank me yet. Quill's been reading courier reports since sundown and she hasn't smiled once. Whatever's coming out of the capital, it isn't good.*

Across the hall, Mother Quill looked up from the papers in her lap. Her eyes found Felix's across the firelight, and held, and did not look away.

The warmth in the room seemed, all at once, a great distance away.
