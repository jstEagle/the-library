## 7. The Hollow King

Wren felt the spy before she saw him.

That was the thing about Brannock—old as he was, gray-stubbled and limping from a knee that had been broken twice in two different cities—he came back into the quarry like weather. A pressure change. The hearth-smoke bent sideways. The ember-lamps along the flooded quarry's rim guttered in sequence, one after another, like a row of candles bowing to something passing through them, and Wren set down the kettle she'd been scalding clean and said, "He's here," before anyone else had even looked up.

"You always know," Tam signed at her, his hands quick and dry. He'd lost his voice to a hunter's brand three winters ago, but his hands never stopped talking, not once, not even in sleep—Wren had seen him signing dreams against the blanket.

"Fire knows fire," Wren said, which wasn't an answer, or maybe it was the only answer there was. She'd been a feral thing when Mother Quill found her, all sparks and scorched doorframes, and she still burned too hot and too fast for most people's comfort. But Brannock understood. Brannock moved slow and cold on the outside and kept everything banked down deep, the way old coals do, and when he finally climbed the last of the quarry steps into the lamplight, Wren saw that his coals were nearly out.

She'd seen tired men before. This was different. This was a man who had looked at something for eleven days straight and couldn't stop seeing it behind his eyes.

The whole hearth went quiet. Felix Fairweather froze mid-argument with Sable over whose turn it was to gut fish, and even the argument died, because everyone in the Emberline knew what Brannock's return meant. Eleven days in the capital. Eleven days inside the gilded rot, wearing a tinker's cart and a pilgrim's stoop, counting guards and copying decrees off public boards with a charcoal stub hidden in his sleeve.

Mother Quill rose from her chair by the great hearth. She was not a tall woman, and she was not a young one, and she moved like a woman made of knotted rope and iron nails, and when she said "Report," the word fell into the silence like a stone into a well.

Brannock sat down first. That was how Wren knew it was bad. Brannock never sat before reporting; he said it was a habit of tradecraft, stand while you speak so you can run while you lie. He sat, and he put his hands flat on his knees, and he looked at the fire for a long moment.

Then he reached into his coat and drew out a folded square of paper, sealed with a blob of black wax, and laid it on the table between them.

"A decree," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Posted this morning on the Chancery board. Read aloud by a herald, as law requires." He paused. "I copied it. Word for word."

Quill broke the seal. Her eyes went down the page, and Wren watched her face do something Wren had almost never seen it do: nothing. Total stillness, like water freezing from the top.

"Read it aloud," Quill said quietly.

"'By order of the Crown,'" Brannock recited, and his voice shook on the second word, "'all persons practicing the art vulgarly called charm-craft within the city walls shall present themselves at the Hall of Reckoning within thirty days, to be registered, examined, and remanded. Those failing to present themselves shall be taken without trial. Their families shall be held accountable for their concealment.'" He stopped. Swallowed. "There's more. The last line."

"Say it."

"It's signed *Regent Aldric, Protector of the Realm.*" Brannock's hands were flat on his knees again, pressing down, as if holding himself to the chair. "Mother, I stood in that crowd for twenty years of decrees. I knew Aldric's hand before I knew my wife's. That signature is not his. It isn't even close. And the herald—" He stopped again, jaw working. "The herald read it in a voice that wasn't quite right either. Flat. Like a man reading words in a language he doesn't speak, sounds he's only ever heard described."

*Families held accountable.* Wren felt the heat rise in her chest before she could stop it, the old dangerous heat, and somewhere across the hearth a lamp flared bright orange and popped. Nobody looked at her. Everybody always pretended not to look at her. She clenched her fists under the table until her palms smoked faintly and the smell of singed wool drifted up, and she breathed the way Quill had taught her—in through the nose, four counts, hold, four counts—until the flame settled.

"The king," Felix said. He was standing near the doorway with a fish knife still in his hand, looking younger than his years and older than his grief, both at once. "You're saying you saw him. You're saying you actually saw the king."

"I did." Brannock closed his eyes. "Feast of Saint Corvel. The king processes from the palace to the Great Temple, as he does every year. Twenty thousand people lining the streets. I was pressed against a barrier not ten feet from the open carriage." He opened his eyes, and Wren saw that they were wet, and that he seemed furious about it. "Aldric Vane. I remember him from the old days, before the hunt got bad. He used to walk among the guilds at midsummer. Laughed easily. Big man, warm hands, remembered names. This—" His breath caught. "This wore his face."

*This.* Not *he*. Wren noticed. Everyone noticed.

"Tell it," Quill said gently. "All of it."

"He waved," Brannock said. "The arm moved correctly. The smile sat where a smile sits. But the eyes—" He lifted one hand and turned it over, palm up, as if showing them something invisible lying in it. "Glass-grey. Not blind-grey, not cataract-grey. Grey like a window at night when the lamps are lit inside and you can't see in anymore. And they didn't *move*, Mother. People cheered and threw flowers and a child broke loose from the crowd and ran alongside the carriage laughing, and those eyes tracked nothing. They pointed forward like painted eyes on a temple idol. And then, just once, just for a heartbeat as the carriage passed me—the left eye twitched. Twitched and rolled toward me, toward the crowd, and for half a second there was something *in* there looking out, something drowning, something screaming behind glass—and then it smoothed over and the painted look came back and the carriage went on."

The hearth-fire crackled. Somewhere below, water lapped at the quarry stones, patient as it had been for a hundred years.

"And the child?" Wren asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Waved back at him," Brannock said. "Children love a procession." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Gods forgive us. Children love a procession."

* * *

They talked until the small hours.

Sable spread maps of the capital across the long table, pinning them down with dagger points, and Brannock walked them through what he'd counted: guard rotations, the new checkpoints on the river gates, the scaffold being built in Coronation Square. Because that was the other news, the news that had chased him home faster than caution allowed—the Regent had announced a coronation. Not its own. Its heir's.

"Prince Maerin," Sable said, tapping the palace district with one dagger tip. "Aldric's boy. Fifteen years old."

"Fifteen," Quill repeated, and the word dropped like lead.

"There's a ceremony set for the turn of the season. Anointing, oath, crowning—the full rite, first time in a generation. Every noble house summoned. Every guild master required to attend." Brannock's mouth twisted. "And here's the part I saved for last, because I only confirmed it my final night, and I nearly didn't get out with my skin. I paid a temple clerk—a frightened man, but a greedy one—for a look at the liturgical drafts. The coronation rite has been *altered*. There are additions. Old language. Language that isn't in any rite of any crown of this kingdom, because I made the clerk check the archives." He leaned forward. "It's binding language. Old binding language. The kind we don't use anymore because it *works*."

Wren watched Felix go white. She'd gotten to know the charm-seller these past weeks—his nervous jokes, his stubborn decency, the way he flinched whenever anyone mentioned his family's ledger and the way he read it anyway, every night, by lamp-light, like a man picking at a wound. Now he said, slowly, "They're going to bind something into the royal line. Permanently. At the crowning."

"Not into the line," Brannock said. "*Through* it. If the rite does what I think it does, whatever wears Aldric now will root itself into Maerin the moment the crown touches his head. And then into Maerin's children. And theirs. A dynasty of gloves." He looked around the table, at each of them in turn. "We've been assuming the Regent is a squatter. It's not planning to squat. It's planning to *inherit*."

Tam's hands cut the air, sharp: *How long?*

"Forty-one days," Brannock said. "Maybe less. They may move the date. Things that wear kings don't like giving crowds time to think."

Silence again. Wren found herself staring into the hearth, watching the flames, and the flames were watching her back the way they always did, and for once their whispering gave her no comfort. Fire told her true things. Fire said: *forty-one days, forty-one days,* like a drumbeat, like a countdown scratched into the world.

"So we run," someone said—one of the quarry-folk, a mason named Hesk whose family had been Emberline for three generations. "We fold up the hearth and go north, past the border marshes, start again where the hunt is thin. We've done it before."

"We've run before," Quill said. "Yes." She stood, and walked to the edge of the hearthlight, and looked out over the black water of the quarry, and when she spoke again her voice was low and level and terrible. "And every time we've run, the hunt has followed, and every time the hunt has followed, we've lost someone. My daughter died running. My son is dead to me. Half the faces at this hearth are here because running cost them everything else they had." She turned. "And now the thing that hunts us is about to make itself permanent. Not a regent. Not a tyrant we might outlive. A *lineage*. Do you understand? We can run north, and our grandchildren will be born hunted, and their grandchildren, and the crown will never die because the crown will never again be worn by a *person*."

Hesk shifted. "What would you have us do, Mother? Storm the palace with hedge-magic and kitchen knives?"

"No," Quill said. "We do what the Fairweathers were founded to do."

Every head turned to Felix.

Wren watched him take it like a physical blow. He'd been sitting apart, as he often did—not unwelcome, just unsure, a man still learning whether the ground under him would hold. Now the whole room's attention landed on him and he stood up too fast, knocking his stool over, and didn't bother to right it.

"I sold luck-charms," he said. "Brass and wishful thinking. For ten years. I *told* people that. I told myself that. I'm not—I'm an apprentice. I'm the last apprentice of a bankrupt shop. I've known how to wake a charm for six weeks. You want me to—" He gestured helplessly at the maps, the daggers, the capital. "Whatever the guild was for, the guild is *dead*. It's been dying since my grandfather's grandfather. There's no one left who—"

"There's you," Quill said.

"One man!"

"One binder," Quill corrected, and crossed the room to him, and took his face in her two rope-and-nail hands the way she'd taken Wren's face once, years ago, the night Wren had burned down the miller's barn and sobbed that she was a monster. "One binder with blood that runs thick with the old craft. One binder carrying a ledger three generations of your family bled to keep. One binder who walked into my hearth with a dead woman's ring in his pocket and didn't sell it, didn't flee, didn't save himself. Listen to me, Felix Fairweather. The first binders built the cage under the capital. Your line kept the lock oiled for two hundred years. Whatever is leaking out of that cage found a king to wear because the lock grew weak—and if it roots itself into the heir, no binding made after that day will ever be strong enough to undo it. Forty-one days. There is no one else. There is only ever, in the end, whoever is willing."

Felix's jaw worked. Wren saw the fear in him, plain as lamplight—but under the fear, banked deep, that stubborn ember she'd seen the night he'd arrived, the one that had made her decide, privately and immediately, that she liked him.

"And if I fail?" he asked quietly.

"Then we fail together," Quill said, "which is what family is *for*."

Something went through the hearth at that—not a sound, exactly, but a settling, like a house shifting on its foundations. Wren looked around and saw it land on every face: Hesk's grim nod, Sable's crooked half-smile, Tam's hands rising to sign *together* and then simply resting, palm down, on the table. Even Brannock, hollowed out by eleven days of horror, straightened his spine.

Wren stood up. She hadn't planned to. Her legs just decided.

"I'll burn anything you need burned," she said.

Felix laughed—a cracked, startled, real laugh, the first one she'd heard out of him in days. "That's very generous."

"I mean it. Gates. Scaffolds. Whoever built that scaffold, I'll give them a very bad evening."

"That's enough, spark," Sable said, but she was smiling.

* * *

Later, when the plans had gone round and round and the hearth had burned down to a red heart of coals, Wren climbed out onto the quarry rim where she went when the fire in her chest got too loud. The night was clear and cold, and far to the east, past the hills, the sky above the capital glowed faintly gold—the city never truly went dark anymore, not with the Regent's new lamps burning in every square, lamps that never flickered, lamps that some of the quarry-folk swore gave off no heat at all.

Footsteps on the stone. Felix, wrapped in a coat too big for him, lowering himself down beside her with none of a city-boy's grace.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said.

"Fire doesn't sleep."

"Right. Yes. Of course." He sat with her in silence for a while. Below them, the hearth-smoke curled up out of the quarry in a thin grey ribbon. "Forty-one days," he said finally. "Do you think we can really do it? Get to him?"

Wren considered the question honestly, because fire hated lies. "No," she said. "Not the way things stand. We're twelve people with kitchen knives and one apprentice binder against a kingdom."

Felix exhaled something that was half laugh, half groan. "Wonderful."

"But," Wren said, and held up one finger, and let a small flame stand on the fingertip, steady in the wind, "fire doesn't care about odds. Fire cares about fuel. And there's more fuel out there than Brannock saw. Every charmer hiding in a cellar. Every family the decree just made into enemies. Every person in that crowd who saw the king's eyes and felt something crawl up their spine and didn't know why." She closed her fist and the flame went out. "The Regent thinks a crown makes it safe. Crowns are just kindling shaped fancy."

Felix stared at her. "Has anyone ever told you that you're terrifying?"

"My whole life," Wren said, pleased. "It's my best quality."

He smiled, and then the smile faded, and he looked east at the golden glow, and she watched the fear come back into his face—but differently now. Steadier. A coal instead of a flare.

"Forty-one days," he said again, softly, like a vow.

Far away, in the city that never went dark, something behind a king's glass-grey eyes was also counting.
