## 8. Captain Vess

The rain had not stopped in three days, and Captain Ilse Vess had stopped noticing it somewhere around the second funeral.

She stood in the doorway of a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, watching her sergeant bully an old woman whose hands shook so badly she could barely hold the cup of tea he'd demanded. The shop smelled of tallow and wet wool and fear—that last one was the smell Vess knew best, the smell that followed the Crown's witch-hunters through every door they kicked or knocked upon, and lately it had begun to turn her stomach in ways she could no longer pretend were indigestion.

"Again, mistress," Sergeant Broch said, leaning close enough that his breath fogged the woman's spectacles. "The boy who keeps the charm-shop. Fairweather. When did you last see him?"

"I told you." The old woman's voice was thin as onion skin. "He sells trinkets. Brass bits and ribbon. My granddaughter bought a luck-charm from him at Midwinter and it did nothing at all."

"Nothing," Broch repeated, writing it down as though the word were evidence.

"That's what I said."

Vess moved into the room then, and the old woman flinched—everyone flinched, it was the uniform, the grey coat with its stitched sigil of the Crown's eye, the iron badge at the breast that every child in Greyhollow learned to dread before they learned their letters. Vess had once believed the flinch was necessary. Fear kept the peace. Fear kept hedge-mages from burning villages and blood-cults from raising things best left buried. Her father had died holding that line; she'd taken the oath at sixteen with his badge pinned over her heart.

Ten years of doorways like this one had worn the belief down to something she kept in a drawer and did not look at.

"Mistress—" she began.

"Hetta," the woman said. "Hetta Cobb. And I've nothing to tell you, captain, because there's nothing to tell. He's a sad little man selling sad little lies to people too poor to know better. If that's witchcraft, then half this city is guilty and you'll need a bigger pyre."

Broch's hand went to his baton. Vess lifted two fingers and he subsided, sulking.

"When did you last see him?" Vess asked again, gentler.

"Two nights past. Market day. He was packing up early—the raid scattered everyone." Hetta's eyes went, for just a moment, toward the back of the shop, toward the cellar hatch visible behind a hanging curtain of dried herbs. It was the smallest flicker, gone almost before it came, but Vess had spent fifteen years reading faces the way other people read ledgers, and she did not miss it.

"The cellar," Vess said.

"There's nothing down there but pickled onions."

"Then you won't mind if we look."

* * *

There was nothing down there but pickled onions, a broken chair, and a scrap of paper caught in the hinge of the hatch, as if someone had fled upward in a great hurry and torn loose a page in passing.

Vess crouched over it while Broch clattered about above, overturning jars. The paper was cheap stock, water-stained, but the ink was fine and careful—a ledger hand, cramped and precise. Most of the page was columns: dates, sums, entries marked *sold* or *unsold*. But across the top, in a different ink, older, was a heading that made the cold climb up Vess's spine and settle behind her teeth:

**Fairweather Binding Ledger — Vol. III — continued**

And beneath it, half a line, before the page tore away:

*...the anchors must be renewed each season, for the doors are old and the things behind them are patient...*

She read it four times. Around her the cellar dripped. Above her Broch called down something about contraband candles, and she did not answer.

Binding ledger. She knew the word *binding* only from the oldest training texts, the ones kept under lock in the Chapterhouse archive, the ones written by the first witch-hunters themselves—men and women who had hunted not hedge-witches with hexes and love-potions but *binders*, the old guilds who had once, according to the histories, kept dangerous things caged beneath the earth. The histories said the binders were destroyed generations ago. Purged root and branch. That was the founding myth of the hunt itself: that the last binder had burned, and the world had been safe ever since.

So why was a page from a binder's ledger caught in the cellar hatch of a charm-seller in Greyhollow?

Unless the purge had missed a root.

"Captain?" Broch stood at the top of the ladder, silhouetted against the weak light. "Anything?"

Vess folded the page small and put it inside her glove, against her palm, where the leather hid it. "Candles," she said. "Confiscate the candles."

* * *

That evening she sat alone in her office at the Chapterhouse with the page flattened under a paperweight and a lamp turned low, and she did not read it again so much as stare at it until the words swam.

Fairweather. She turned the name over. There had been a Fairweather case in the archive—she remembered now, dimly, from her earliest years, a file so thin it was nearly a joke. *Charm-sellers, itinerant, harmless.* A note in her predecessor's hand: *No action warranted. Products inert.* Someone had investigated the family decades ago and concluded exactly what Hetta Cobb had said: brass bits and wishful thinking.

But the ledger page didn't read like wishful thinking. It read like maintenance notes. Like a man keeping records of a duty.

Her father's voice, from long ago, from a kitchen that smelled of bread and lamp-oil: *Ilse. The law is a wall, and we are the wall's stones. You don't get to choose which side of yourself the wall protects.*

She had been nine. She had believed it the way children believe their fathers are ten feet tall.

A knock. Broch put his head in without waiting. "Found where the charm-seller went, near enough. Cellar hatch opens onto the sewer cut. And captain—the dead woman from the shop. We've a name now. Sera Quill, traveling under papers as a wool merchant. No wool merchant in any registry. And this was sewn into her coat." He dropped a cloth bundle on the desk.

Inside lay a signet ring, plain silver, worn smooth, stamped with a mark Vess didn't recognize: a quill crossed with a key.

"And here's the interesting part," Broch said, leaning on the desk, enjoying himself the way he always enjoyed himself, which was one of the hundred reasons Vess had never let him past sergeant. "The Quill woman was seen alive in the flooded quarry country two years back, asking after river-smugglers. Smugglers, captain. Charm-runners. This isn't a lone madwoman. There's a line running from Greyhollow to the quarry and I want warrants for the quarry settlements. Full sweep. Burn the lot if they're marked."

"The quarry settlements are fishing families," Vess said. "Half of them children."

"Half of them *marked*, if we're lucky." Broch grinned. "Warrants by morning?"

Vess looked at the ring. At the ledger page hidden in her glove. At Broch's face, open and hungry and utterly certain, the face she herself had worn for fifteen years.

"Warrants take time," she said. "Procedure. I'll draft them myself."

His grin faltered. "Since when do you slow-walk a sweep?"

"Since always. It's why I'm captain and you're not." She closed the bundle. "Out, sergeant. I work better alone."

When the door shut, she took the ledger page out of her glove and smoothed it flat, and read the torn line once more. *The anchors must be renewed each season, for the doors are old and the things behind them are patient.*

Things behind them. Patient. For how long? Patient since when?

She thought of the training texts again, the ones under lock, and of a passage she had memorized at nineteen because it had frightened her beautifully, the way deep water frightens: *Of the old bindings we know only that they were many, and that our order was raised not to make the world safe, but to keep watch upon the locks after the locksmiths were slain.*

After the locksmiths were slain. She had always read that as triumph. Tonight, with a dead woman's ring on her desk and a binder's page under her lamp, it read differently. It read like a wall built on a grave, guarded by people who had forgotten there was anything underneath.

* * *

She should have slept. Instead she walked.

Greyhollow at night in the rain, hood up, out of uniform, which was itself an offense she'd have arrested anyone else for. She walked without deciding to walk, and her feet chose the streets, and it took her some time to understand where they were taking her, and when she understood she let them.

Lantern Street. The corner house with the blue door.

Nineteen years ago. She had been eleven, and her mother had been dying of the lung-rot, coughing red into rags while the physicians charged silver to shrug, and her father had been away on the northern circuit hunting mages to pay those same physicians, and Ilse had done the thing desperate children do: she had gone looking for magic.

Everyone knew the corner house with the blue door. Everyone knew the charm-seller was a fraud, and everyone went anyway, because hope is not a respecter of evidence. She had knocked with her mother's last coins knotted in a handkerchief, and a young man with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers had opened the door—not Aldric Fairweather the elder, whom she'd expected, but a son, home from a journey—and she had stammered out her errand, ashamed, ready to be laughed at.

He hadn't laughed. He had looked at her a long moment, and then he had said, "I can't sell you what you're asking. But come in out of the cold, and we'll see."

What happened in that house she had never told anyone, never fully admitted even to herself in the dark watches. Because what she remembered was impossible, and she had spent nineteen years filing it under grief-hallucination, child's fancy, fever-dream. But tonight, standing in the rain before the blue door with a binder's ledger page burning against her ribs, the memory would not stay filed.

The young man had set a brass charm on the table—the same worthless luck-piece sold in every market stall—and held her mother's name in his mouth like something fragile, and hummed three low notes, and the charm had *warmed*. Not glowed. Warmed, like a stone left by a fire, and he had pressed it into her hands and said, "It won't cure her. Nothing will cure her. But it will ease the nights, and easing is not nothing."

Her mother had lived four more months instead of four more weeks, and those months had been gentle ones, sleeping warm, dreaming easily, and when she died it was with her hand around a piece of brass that had stayed faintly, impossibly warm until the day they buried her.

Illegal charm. Unlicensed working. Every article of the code Vess had sworn to uphold, broken kindly in a kitchen on Lantern Street by a Fairweather who asked nothing, reported nothing, harmed no one—and eased the dying.

She had never connected it. Nineteen years, and she had kept the memory in one drawer and the oath in another and never once opened them in the same hour. Because connecting them meant choosing, and she had built her whole life on not choosing, on being the wall's stone, on letting the law decide right and wrong so her conscience could sleep.

Now the drawers were open whether she liked it or not. The Fairweather boy fleeing through a sewer cut with a dead courier's map. A binding ledger. A signet ring. A thing patient behind old doors. And somewhere in the quarry country, fishing families who were about to learn what the Crown's mercy looked like if Broch got his warrants signed.

Vess stood in the rain until she was soaked through, looking at the blue door, which was shuttered and dusty and bore the faded ghost of a painted sign: *FAIRWEATHER CHARM-SELLERS — EST. BY ROYAL LICENSE, RENEWED EACH REIGN.*

Renewed each reign. By whose hand, she wondered, when the king was not the king? Or had the renewals simply stopped, quietly, the way duties stop when the last person who remembers them dies?

She thought of the boy. Felix Fairweather. Ten years in Greyhollow selling nothing, lying kindly, keeping a ledger he perhaps didn't understand, maintaining—without knowing it—anchors against things patient in the dark. If the ledger was true, then the boy wasn't a criminal. He was a lock-keeper who'd lost the keys and kept polishing the lock anyway.

And she was the hunter sent to drag him off it.

* * *

Morning came grey. Vess sat at her desk with the warrant drafts before her and Broch's list of quarry names beside them, and she wrote, and crossed out, and wrote again.

In the end she did what she could live with and no more: she narrowed the sweep to three structures flagged for unlicensed trade, all of them empty storehouses, all of them chosen because Broch's informants were lazy and she had fed the list herself. It bought time. It bought maybe days. It was not mercy; it was a stone rolled a few feet uphill.

Then she unlocked the drawer where she kept the things she did not look at, and she added the ledger page to it—no. She took it out again. Filed pages could be found. Instead she copied the torn line into her private notebook, in her own hand, and burned the original in the lamp flame, watching the old ink curl and go.

*The anchors must be renewed each season.*

Someone had to renew them. Someone had to remember there was anything underneath the wall.

She finished the warrants, signed them, and sent Broch to the quarry with orders to observe and report before any arrest—orders he would resent and obey, for now. Then she sat alone with her father's badge on her breast and the memory of a warm brass charm in her hands, and felt the crack run through the wall of her, quiet and structural, the kind of crack that does not announce itself but changes everything built upon it.

Duty, she had always believed, was knowing which orders to follow. It occurred to her, sitting in the grey light, that she had gotten it backwards. Duty was knowing which orders to refuse—and being willing to stand in the doorway when refusal came due.

Somewhere out there, a boy with his grandfather's eyes was running through the dark with a dead woman's map, and the full machinery of the Crown was turning to grind him fine, and she was the hand on the lever.

Vess opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in the careful hand she used for reports that would outlive her:

*Felix Fairweather. Last of the binders' line. Find him before Broch does.*

She looked at the words a long moment. Then she closed the book, put it in her coat over her heart, and went to find out what the quarry families knew—alone, out of uniform, before the hunt she served caught up with the conscience she had neglected for nineteen years.

Outside, the rain finally stopped. It did not feel like an ending.
