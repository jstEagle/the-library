## 9. Road Through Ashfen

The pilgrim's cloak itched.

Felix had worn a hundred disguises in ten years of selling nothing to nobody, and he could say with authority that the itch was always the tell. A good disguise fit like your own skin. This one—coarse grey wool, hood, rope belt, a wooden badge of the Pilgrims of the Quiet Road stamped with a saint Felix was fairly sure had never existed—itched at the collar like a lie trying to get out.

"You keep scratching," Sable murmured beside him, "and every hunter from here to the capital will know you're fraud."

"I'm a fraud either way."

"Yes, but a comfortable one. There's a difference. Comfortable frauds live long lives."

They walked in a line of six along the river road, the Emberline moving as pilgrims bound for the shrine at Saint Verity's Ford—a real shrine, Mother Quill insisted, which made the best kind of cover because no one bothered to check on pilgrims going somewhere dull. Quill led, leaning on her staff, playing the part of a grandmother so well that Felix sometimes forgot she could boil a man's blood with a word. Behind her came Corvin, quiet and competent, mending a strap on his pack as he walked. Then Wren, who had been given strict instructions about fire and had taken them with the sullen grace of a cat being told not to knock things off tables. Tam brought up the rear, slate hanging from his neck, chalk in hand, watching the road behind them the way he always did—with the patience of a man who had already died once and found it overrated.

Felix walked beside Sable because walking beside Sable had become, without any decision on his part, simply where he walked.

"Tell me about Ashfen," he said.

Her stride didn't change, but something in her did—a tightening, small as a stitch pulled wrong. "Why?"

"The map route goes through it. Quill said we'd reach it by tomorrow night. You went quiet when she said the name."

"I go quiet about lots of things. It's one of my virtues."

"Sable."

She was silent for twenty paces. The river ran low and brown on their left, and somewhere ahead a heron lifted off the shallows like a thrown rag.

"Ashfen is where I'm from," she said finally. "Or where I was from, before. Peat town. Smoke town. Everyone coughed and everyone stayed anyway, because the peat cuttings were work and work was life." She adjusted the strap of her crossbow, slung under her pilgrim's cloak where no pilgrim's god would approve. "There was a fever when I was eleven. Sweating sickness. It came up the river with the barges and it took one house in three. My mother built charms. Little ones—warming charms, breath-easing charms, the kind of thing your guild used to sell before it learned to sell nothing." She glanced at him, sharp-eyed, half-smiling, letting him know the barb was affection. "She wasn't licensed. None of us were. When the fever passed, the hunters came through and burned the charm-stock and took the charm-makers. My mother among them. I watched the cart go from the top of the peat stack. She waved at me. That's the worst part. She waved."

Felix didn't say anything. There wasn't anything that fit.

"So," Sable said, and her voice was light again, door closed, "when we pass through Ashfen, we walk through. We don't stop, we don't gawk, we don't perform acts of charity for the sick and dying. We are six anonymous pilgrims going to look at a very boring saint. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Felix said.

He meant it, too. That was the thing he would turn over later, in the dark, in the fevered hours after: he had meant it.

* * *

They smelled Ashfen before they saw it.

Peat smoke, yes—that was the town's own smell, the smell of its living—but under it something sweeter and fouler, the smell of sickness and of the lye they threw down on the streets to fight it. The sweating sickness again, or its cousin; the barges still came up the river, and the river didn't care what rode on it.

The town gates stood open. Nobody checked papers anymore; nobody wanted to touch what travelers might carry. Inside, Ashfen moved slow and hunched, shutters drawn against the afternoon, doors marked with white crosses in chalk—the old sign, *fever within, mercy without*. Children should have been in the streets. There were almost none. The few Felix saw were wrapped in blankets in doorways, watching the road with eyes too old for their faces.

Quill led them straight through, head bowed, staff tapping. "Saint Verity's Ford by nightfall if we push," she said softly. "We take the east road past the market and out. No stops."

It was Wren who broke first, because it was always going to be Wren.

The boy stopped dead in the middle of the street, staring at a doorway. Felix followed his gaze. In the crook of the door, on a pallet dragged halfway into the open air because the house behind it was too hot with fever to hold another body, lay two children. A girl of maybe eight and a boy younger, both grey-lipped, both shivering so hard the pallet frame rattled. Their mother knelt beside them doing the only thing left to do—rubbing their hands, rubbing their feet, trying to rub warmth back into bodies that were spending it faster than love could replace it.

"They're cold," Wren said. Not a question. Just the fact, stated in that flat, dangerous way he had, the way that meant the fact had gotten inside him and was burning there.

"We're all cold," Quill said gently. "Keep walking, spark."

"They're *dying* cold. That's different. I know dying cold." Wren's hands had curled at his sides, and Felix saw the faint shimmer of heat around the boy's knuckles, like air above a candle. "You can fix dying cold with a warming charm. One charm. I've seen Felix do it in half a breath since he learned to wake them."

"And then the hunters come," Quill said. "And then everyone in this street dies with them instead of just these two. Keep. Walking."

Wren kept walking. He was young, but he'd been an Emberline child longer than Felix had been anything, and he knew what orders meant. He kept walking, and his fists uncurled, and the shimmer died.

It was Felix who stopped.

Later he would try to find the moment it happened—the exact seam in himself where the sensible man ended and the other one began—and he never could. There was no moment. There was only the mother's hands, cracked and raw from rubbing, and the little rattling shiver of the pallet, and the memory of ten years of selling brass and wishful thinking to people who needed miracles, and the ledger in his grandfather's handwriting that said, in effect: *you were always capable of this, you coward, you just never tried.*

"Felix," Sable said quietly. Warning in it. His name in her mouth was usually the nicest sound in his day; now it was a hand on his collar.

"One charm," he said. "One. They'll think it's a saint's blessing. Pilgrims give blessings. It's practically regulation."

"It's a death warrant with good intentions."

"Sable—"

"If you do this," she said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was how he knew she was truly frightened, "I can't fix it. Vess isn't behind us, Felix, she's *ahead* of us—Tam heard it at the last village, hunters quartered at the ford, sweeping the road. If you light even a candle's worth of craft in this town, she will smell it, and she will come, and everything we're carrying to the capital dies here in a peat town nobody will remember."

He looked at the children. The girl had stopped shivering. That was worse than the shivering. Shivering meant the body was still fighting.

"I know," Felix said. And woke the charm.

It was one of his grandfather's—the old brass disc on a leather cord, the one with the worn spiral, the one Felix had sold four hundred times as a luck-piece and never once felt answer him. Now it answered. Warmth poured out of it like water from a tipped jug, spreading across the pallet, sinking into two small grey bodies, and Felix fed it with the binding-craft Quill had taught him, holding the flow steady, easing it into their lungs and their blood, chasing the fever's chill out through their heels. The girl gasped—a whole, deep, living gasp—and opened her eyes. The boy turned toward the warmth like a plant toward sun.

The mother stared at Felix. At the badge on his chest. "Saints," she whispered. "Saints be praised—" and she reached for his hand to kiss it, and he let her, because stopping her would have required a speech he couldn't give.

"Say a wandering pilgrim blessed them," Felix said. "Nothing else. Please."

Then Sable had his arm and was hauling him down the street, and Quill was saying nothing at all in a voice that was louder than shouting, and Wren was grinning at him like he'd hung the moon, and Tam had gone pale beneath his weathering and was writing fast on his slate, turning it toward them:

*Hunters. Now. Run.*

* * *

They nearly made the east road.

That was the cruel arithmetic of it, the thing Felix would count and recount forever: they nearly made it. They were fifty yards from the gate, forty, thirty, the fields opening gold beyond it, when the horn sounded from the market square behind them—one long note, then two short, the hunter's call Felix had known since childhood as the sound that emptied markets.

"Halt! In the crown's name!"

Vess.

She came out of the side lane with six hunters at her back, and even in a pilgrim's cloak Felix would have known her anywhere—that tall, unhurried walk, the crossbow held easy and level, the face like a locked door. Her eyes went down the line of them, one by one, and Felix watched her do the arithmetic too: six pilgrims, wrong time of year for the Ford shrine, and one of them—her gaze snagged on Felix, and stayed.

"Fairweather," she said.

"Captain Vess." He spread his empty hands. "Just a pilgrim passing through."

"There's a warming charm active in this town. Fresh. Strong." She came closer, boots deliberate on the lye-scattered cobbles. "I've felt three workings today. Two were hedge-slop, breath-easing, barely worth the rope they'll hang from. The third—" something flickered across the locked door of her face, quickly shut away, "—the third was Fairweather binding-craft. I'd know it anywhere. I grew up next door to a woman who used it."

"Captain," Quill said, stepping forward, grandmotherly and mild, "we're late for the shrine—"

"Search them," Vess said. "All of them. Carefully."

And that was when Sable moved.

Felix never got the whole of it, even afterward. He saw her stumble—saw her pitch against Tam with a cry, clutching her stomach, and Tam's slate clattered to the cobbles and Tam went down with her, and the hunters' eyes went to the commotion the way eyes do, and in that half-second Sable's hand came out from under her cloak and her crossbow spoke twice.

Both bolts hit the lead hunter's shield. Deliberate. A warning shot, a chaos-maker, nothing lethal—because Sable did not kill unless killing was the only door left, and she spent her whole life making sure other doors stayed open.

"Run!" she shouted, and shoved Felix hard toward the gate, and the street exploded.

Hunters scattered for cover. Quill did something with her staff that made the lye-water in the gutter leap up in a blinding white sheet between them and the crossbows. Wren—strictly forbidden, gloriously disobedient—put his fist against a rain barrel and boiled it, and steam swallowed the lane. Felix ran. They all ran, six shapes plunging through the white cloud toward the gate, and they might have made it clean, all of them, if the world hadn't slowed at exactly the wrong moment.

Felix tripped.

A cobble, loose, treacherous—he went down hard on his knees in the gateway itself, daylight and open country blazing beyond him, and behind him he heard Vess's voice crack across the square: "*Bolt!*"

He turned. Time went thick. He saw the hunter at the corner of the lye-house, crossbow leveled at his chest, finger tightening. He saw it all with terrible clarity, the way you see things when there's nothing whatsoever you can do about them.

Sable saw it too.

She was three steps behind him. She covered the distance like it was nothing, like she'd crossed worse distances for less reason, and she hit him sideways—both hands, full weight, knocking him flat through the gate into the mud of the road ditch—as the bolt snapped past overhead and took her high in the back, below the shoulder blade, angling down.

The sound she made was small. That was the worst of it. All that sharpness, all those closed doors and dry remarks, and the sound was small.

Then the company was around them like a closing fist—Quill's staff cracking the earth so a wall of peat-smoke rolled up screaming from the ditches, Wren dragging Felix up by the collar with strength no eight-year-old body should have had, Tam standing in the gate with his arms spread wide and his voiceless throat working around words no one would ever hear, buying seconds the only way he had left. They ran. The fields took them. The smoke took them. Somewhere behind, horns tangled with each other in confusion, and Vess's voice rang out ordering pursuit, and then the wind took that too.

They carried Sable through the fen paths until dark, Quill's craft slowing the bleeding, Wren running ahead to break trail. She was conscious part of the time. She spent most of it insulting Felix's running form, each remark fainter than the last, until even her insults ran out and left only her breathing, quick and shallow, in the dark.

They reached the drover's hut at midnight—an abandoned turf shelter on a hummock above the marsh, walls of stacked sod, a hearth that hadn't burned in years. Quill lit it with a word. They laid Sable down by the fire, and Felix knelt beside her and looked at the bolt's entry wound and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever happened next was his fault, entirely and specifically his fault, purchased at the price of one broken promise on a street in Ashfen.

"Get the bolt-head out," Quill said quietly. "Then hold her together. Binding-craft, Felix. Everything you have."

Felix rolled up his sleeves.

Outside, far off across the black fen, a hunting horn sounded—then another, answering, closer than the first. Vess was not done. Vess would never be done. And tomorrow, if Sable lived, they would have to run again through country that was learning their names.

Felix put his hands over the wound, and began.

* * *

The bolt-head came out at dawn.

Quill guided his hands—*there, feel the iron's hunger, pull it the way you'd pull a splinter from a sleeping man's dream*—while Wren held Sable's shoulders and Corvin boiled water and Tam stood watch at the door, slate forgotten, his whole body bent toward the table like a man listening through a wall. Sable did not scream. She bit down on a rolled strip of blanket and made sounds through her nose, furious, economical, as though even agony were an inefficiency she resented.

When the iron head clinked into the basin, Felix closed his eyes and did the second part.

Binding-craft was not healing. Quill had told him that a dozen times in lessons: *we do not mend flesh, we hold edges together while flesh remembers its own work.* So Felix held. He wove the waking warmth of his charms through the torn muscle, anchored thread-lines of craft across the wound the way his grandfather's ledger described anchoring a failing bridge, and he held them, hour after hour, kneeling on the cold floor while the fire burned down and the fen outside filled with light. His hands shook. His vision swam. Twice he lost the weave and felt Sable's heartbeat stutter under his palms, and twice he found it again, teeth gritted, whispering the binder-words his grandfather had written in margins too small for any honest eye.

Near noon, her fever broke. Near dusk, she slept—truly slept, deep and even, color creeping back into her lips like ink returning to a faded page.

Felix sat back on his heels and discovered he could not stand. Wren caught him under the arms and lowered him against the wall, and the boy sat beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"You saved them," Wren said at last. "The kids in Ashfen."

"I killed Tam's chance of ever going home," Felix said. "I burned our route. I nearly killed her." His voice came out flat and strange. "One charm, Wren. I promised one street's worth of silence and I couldn't even keep it for a hundred yards."

Wren considered this with the terrifying seriousness of the young. "My mother burned," he said. "Hunters. Our whole camp, when I was six. I hid in a culvert and watched." He poked the fire up with a stick. "If someone had broken cover for us—if one person had decided we were worth the cost—I'd have grown up somewhere warm." He shrugged. "So. Worth the cost."

Felix looked at him. The boy's face was orange in the firelight, solemn and soot-streaked and utterly certain, and Felix felt something in his chest crack—not painfully, or not only painfully. More the way ice cracks on a river in spring: dangerously, and with spring underneath it.

Behind them, from the table, a voice like gravel dragged over gravel:

"If you two are finished being profound... I can hear you."

Felix was on his feet before he knew he'd moved. Sable's eyes were open—grey and clear and annoyed, which was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. She was pale as the bed-turf, propped on one elbow, and she was looking directly at him with an expression that promised a great deal of trouble.

"You tripped," she said.

"You took a crossbow bolt."

"You *tripped*, Felix. On a cobble. In a gateway. I have seen you climb a roof in a hailstorm with a stolen goose under one arm." She winced, resettled her elbow. "I took a bolt for the single clumsiest man in Greyhollow, and history will never let me forget it."

"Sable—" His throat closed. Everything he had rehearsed through the long hours of the weaving—all of it, the guilt and the gratitude and the thing underneath both that he refused to name while she could still die of it—came up against her eyes and jammed there. What came out instead was: "I'm sorry. About Ashfen. About all of it. I broke my word."

"Yes," she said. "You did. Cheerfully. In front of witnesses." She held his gaze, and something in it softened by a degree, one degree, carefully measured. "The children lived, I hear."

"The children lived."

"Then we'll argue about whether it was worth it when I can sit up." She lay back, closed her eyes, and added, mostly asleep already, "For the record, Fairweather—it was worth it. But if you ever trip again, I'm leaving you to the hunters."

Felix laughed. It hurt, and he laughed anyway, and Wren laughed, and from the doorway Tam silently held up his slate, on which he had written, sometime during the night in his patient chalk hand:

*She hasn't left anyone yet. Don't be the first.*

But outside the hut, in the blue dark coming down over the fen, the horns were sounding again—three of them now, in a pattern Felix didn't recognize, a pattern that made Quill stand at the door a long time before she turned and said, grim as winter:

"They're not searching anymore. They're herding. Someone has told Vess exactly where we're going—and the only road to the capital runs straight through the Weir."
