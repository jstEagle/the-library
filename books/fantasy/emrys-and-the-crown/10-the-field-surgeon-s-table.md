## 10. The Field Surgeon's Table

The bolt went in below her ribs, and the world went out like a pinched candle.

Sable knew rivers. She knew them the way other people knew their mothers' faces—every bend, every shallow, every place where the current turned lazy and fat. So when the pain took her at the weir's edge and the world tilted, she did not think *I am dying*. She thought *the river is high for this time of year*, and *the crossing will flood by morning*, and *Felix is shouting my name in that useless cracked voice of his*, and then there was no more thinking at all.

There was only drifting after that. Drifting and fever, which are cousins, or maybe the same thing wearing different coats.

She dreamed of her mother teaching her to read water: *see where the foam gathers, love, see where it won't go*. She dreamed of a hundred harbors, a hundred names given and taken back. She dreamed of a boy with ink-stained fingers holding a brass charm over her wound and saying, in a voice that shook but did not stop, "I don't actually know what I'm doing."

*That's a lie,* she tried to tell him. *You're a Fairweather. You know exactly what you're doing. You just never believed it.*

But her mouth wouldn't work. Her mouth was full of river.

* * *

Felix had never worked magic that mattered before, and he chose the worst possible night to start.

They had carried her upstream through the reeds while Tam held the crossing—Tam who was not holding it anymore, Tam whose silence would be a wound in all of them forever—and they had found an abandoned eel-fisher's hut with a sagging roof and a hearth full of old ash. Quill stripped Sable to the waist and looked at the bolt hole with the flat expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Bolt's still in," she said. "Barbed. If we pull it wrong, she bleeds out in a minute. If we leave it, she's dead by dawn anyway." She looked up at Felix, and her eyes were red-rimmed and terrible. "I've seen surgeons try this. They cut, and they pray, and half the time they kill them faster."

"I can feel it," Felix said.

Quill blinked. "Feel what?"

"The—the edges of it. The wound." His hands were hovering over Sable's side without his permission, trembling, and beneath the terror he could feel something else, something that felt like reading a page in the dark: the shape of the damage, the torn places, the barbed iron sitting crooked against her ribs like a nail driven by a drunk carpenter. "It's like—it's like a broken charm. Torn threads. I can see where it's supposed to knit back together."

"That's binding-sight," Quill said quietly. "Fairweather blood. I've read about it. I've never seen it." She gripped his shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Felix. Listen to me. What you're about to do has no book. No teacher. You'll be inventing it as you go, on the one person in this company you cannot afford to lose. Do you understand me?"

He understood. That was the problem. He understood perfectly, and he knelt down beside Sable anyway, because the alternative was watching her die, and he had spent ten years watching things die slowly in Greyhollow and he was done. He was done with slow deaths and useless charms and being careful.

"Wren," he said. "Boil water. All of it. Tam's kit is in the second pack—there's a bone needle wrapped in oilcloth. Don't drop it. Don't look at it either, if you can help it."

Wren's face was grey and wet-streaked, but she nodded and went, and did not argue, which from Wren was its own kind of prayer.

Felix opened his satchel. Inside, rattling against each other like dead men's teeth, lay forty-odd charms he had sold lies about for a decade. Luck-charms. Warding-charms. A little brass turtle that was supposed to keep travelers' feet dry. He picked out six—the ones that hummed faintly when he held them now, the ones that had started singing since Quill taught him to listen—and laid them in a row along Sable's bare, blood-slicked skin.

"Alright," he whispered, to her, though she couldn't hear. "You once told me you'd robbed three temples and a bishop, so I figure you owe the gods a favor or two. Call this in. Call it even."

He reached into the wound.

The pain of doing it right was worse than any pain he had known, and he had burned his hands learning fire-charms at fourteen. Binding magic did not flow; it *held*, and holding meant taking the strain into your own body. When he closed his fingers around the barbed head of the bolt and began to draw it backward along the path it had made, the tearing in Sable's flesh became a tearing in *him*—a hot wire pulled through his chest, his arms, behind his eyes. He heard himself make a sound. He kept pulling.

The bolt came free with a sound like a boot leaving mud.

Sable screamed—a raw, distant, drowning scream—and Felix said, "I know, I know, I'm sorry, I'm sorry," to a woman who could not hear him, and set the bolt aside, and put both hands over the wound, and began to stitch.

Not with thread. Not at first. With the charms.

Each one woke under his fingers like a bird unclenching. The first was a warding-knot, and he wove it across the deepest tear, and the bleeding slowed from pouring to welling. The second was a luck-charm, and he laughed—a horrible cracked laugh—because luck was exactly what she needed and he had sold hundreds of these to people who needed exactly that, and none of them had ever known what they were buying. He wove it into the tissue's edge, coaxing, persuading, *binding* flesh to flesh the way his grandfather's ledger described binding spirit to stone.

The third charm fought him. It was old, older than the others, brass gone green, and it did not want to hold a human body together—it wanted to hold a door shut, it wanted to hold a cage, it wanted to hold *anything except this small warm mortal mess*. Felix bent over it, sweat dropping onto Sable's skin, and growled at it the way he imagined his grandfather must have growled at stubborn work two hundred years gone.

"You'll do what I tell you," he said. "We're Fairweathers. We hold doors. Tonight the door is her."

The charm yielded. And when it did, something came up through Felix from somewhere deeper than training or craft—from the ledger, from the bloodline, from ten years of lying about trinkets that had been waiting all along for someone to mean it—and the last three charms woke all at once, singing, and the wound began to close.

It took until dawn. He stitched with the bone needle when the charms needed anchoring, sewing real thread through real flesh with hands that no longer shook, because shaking was a luxury and Sable's breathing was thin as paper. Wren sat against the wall the whole night with her knees drawn up, feeding the fire stick by stick, watching him with the wide black eyes of a child watching a miracle she didn't dare name. Once, near the lowest hour, she whispered, "Is she going to live?"

"I don't know," Felix said honestly. "But she's not going to die tonight."

Quill sat on Sable's other side and held her daughter-figure's hand—not Corvin's hand, never again Corvin's hand—and murmured old Emberline words that Felix didn't know, low and steady, a rope thrown into dark water.

And when the sky outside the broken shutter went from black to iron to the pale bruised grey of almost-morning, Felix tied off the final knot—a knot of nothing visible, a knot of pure held intention, the first great working of his life—and collapsed forward with his forehead resting gently against Sable's shoulder, and slept like the dead.

* * *

Sable woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone else's blood, and for a long, luxurious moment she thought she was dead, because dead people surely got to lie still.

Then the pain arrived, deep and hot below her ribs, and she revised her opinion: not dead. Worse. *Alive*, and alive hurt.

She turned her head. Felix was asleep sitting up against the wall, chin on chest, hands still faintly glowing—or maybe that was the fever, maybe everything was glowing, maybe the whole world had gone soft at the edges. His fingers were stained to the wrist with her blood, dried brown, and there was a bone needle on the floor beside him like a fallen soldier.

The fever broke that afternoon. She knew because the shivering stopped and the world stopped swimming and she could suddenly count the cracks in the ceiling—eleven, twelve, thirteen—and counting things was how Sable knew she was herself again. Names changed. Rivers changed. Cracks in ceilings stayed counted.

Felix was there when she surfaced properly, sitting close, peeling an apple with a knife in long unbroken spirals because apparently that was a thing he did now, that people did, domestic and absurd.

"You're supposed to be resting," he said.

"You're supposed to be dead of exhaustion." Her voice came out a rasp. "What happened."

His hands stilled on the apple. "You took a crossbow bolt that was addressed to me."

"I know what I took. I want to know what you did. I remember—" She frowned, chasing the fever-dream. "I remember you saying you didn't know what you were doing."

"I didn't." He set down the apple and the knife and looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I do now, a bit. Quill says it's in the blood. Fairweather blood." He laughed, short and bitter. "Ten years, Sable. Ten years I sold those things and apologized for them. 'Just brass, miss. Just wishful thinking.' And the whole time—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The whole time they were true. Or they could have been. If anyone had ever *meant* them."

Sable watched him. This was the part she was good at—watching, reading, seeing where the current ran under a person's surface. And what she saw under Felix Fairweather's surface, in the grey morning light of an eel-fisher's hut, was a man standing in the ruins of his own story, trying to decide whether the rubble was a grave or a foundation.

"You saved my life," she said.

"You gave yours for mine. Seems we're trading at a loss, both of us."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. I'm terrified." He met her eyes then, and his were wet and furious and honest. "When you went down—I've never—Sable, I have lost everyone slowly. My grandfather. The guild. The shop, piece by piece, customer by customer. Slow losses you can get used to. But you went down in one second, and the world just—" His hands made a shape in the air, helpless, closing around nothing. "There was no getting used to it. There was no *after*. There was just the bolt, and the blood, and me kneeling in it."

"Sable," he said again, softer, and then seemed to run out of words entirely.

She should have deflected. It was her gift, deflection—quips like smoke, exits like water. Every crew she'd ever sailed with, every harbor she'd ever loved, she had left them or buried them, and she had learned young that the only safe distance was arm's length, and the only safe number was one. One person travels light. One person grieves fast. One person owes nothing.

Instead she heard herself say: "My mother died when I was seven. Fever, same as this. I remember the sweat cooling on her."

Felix went very still.

"My father lasted two more years, then the river took him—properly took him, not like your smuggler stories. I was raised by a lock-keeper who beat me and a priest who didn't notice me, and the first crew that took me on, I was nine, and they were kind, and they all drowned within the decade. Every crew after that. Every friend. Marta, who taught me locks—hanged. Old Joss—fever. Little Pip, who was fifteen and faster than anyone I ever knew—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and kept going, because she had decided somewhere in the last minute to say all of it and Sable had never once left a job half-done. "Everyone leaves or dies, Felix. Everyone. So I stopped staying. I stopped letting myself stay. You keep moving, you keep one bag packed, you never sleep facing the door, and nobody gets close enough to be a wound when they go."

"And yet," Felix said quietly, "you threw yourself in front of a bolt for me."

"Yes," Sable said. "I've been wondering about that myself."

The silence between them filled up with woodsmoke and the drip of the eaves and the far-off sound of Wren arguing with the river about fish.

"Why do you never stay anywhere?" Felix asked. Not accusing. Asking, the way you'd ask about a scar.

"Because everyone leaves or dies," she said. "I told you."

"Not this time."

She looked at him. He was pale, wrung-out, still crusted with her blood, and he said it the way he'd knelt over her wound all night—not knowing if it was true, meaning it anyway, holding the door.

"You can't promise that," she said. "Nobody can. We're riding toward a coronation to fight a parasite wearing a king. People are going to die. Maybe lots of people. Maybe us."

"I know."

"Then don't say it. Don't you dare say things you can't bind."

Something crossed his face—hurt, then understanding, then that stubborn ember she'd seen the first night under the tannery bridge, when instead of robbing him she'd offered passage and hated herself for it.

"Alright," he said. "Then here's what I can bind. I can't promise to live. But I can promise that whatever happens—bolt, hunter, Regent, crown—I will spend every day I've got choosing to stay. Not because it's safe. Because you're worth the risk of the wound." He picked the apple back up. His hands were steady. "Everyone leaves or dies. Fine. But some people, before they go, *stay*. Really stay. And I'd rather have that with you for a year than safety without you for fifty."

Sable stared at the ceiling. Thirteen cracks. She counted them twice, because her eyes were burning and counting was how she stayed herself.

"You're very bad at this," she said finally, thickly. "Any decent smuggler would have lied to me. Promised forever. Easy sell."

"I spent ten years selling lies," Felix said. "I'm retired."

She laughed, and it hurt below her ribs, and she laughed anyway, and then she reached over and took his blood-crusted hand in hers, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, because some things, once admitted sideways, do not bear looking at straight on. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But the hand stayed, and the fire stayed, and outside the hut the river ran high and cold and indifferent, carrying spring downstream toward a capital that did not yet know what was coming for it.

Near dusk, Quill came in quietly, looked at their joined hands, and said nothing about it, which was its own kind of blessing. What she said instead, in a voice scraped hollow by grief for Tam and mercy for a traitor son, was:

"The scouts are back from the capital. Felix—you need to hear this. The king was paraded through the festival square today." Her jaw tightened. "And halfway through the procession, Aldric looked straight at the crowd—at *us*, at our man in the crowd—and wept. Without stopping. Without knowing why."

Felix rose. Sable, aching, swung her legs off the cot to rise with him, and when he moved to stop her she fixed him with a look that ended the argument before it started.

"We ride at first light," Quill said. "All of us. Coronation's in eight days—and the Regent means to make itself eternal."
