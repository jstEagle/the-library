## 11. The Traitor's Mark

Tam had lost his voice eleven years ago, on a night much like this one, and he had learned since then that silence was not the same as having nothing to say.

He sat at the edge of the marsh camp with his slate across his knees, chalk in his hand, watching the fire the way he always watched fires — for what they lit, and for what they left dark. The Emberline had made camp in a bowl of black reeds where the river slowed to a brown crawl, close enough to the weir road that Sable said they could be moving within an hour if Vess's hunters came sniffing. Tam did not like how close that was. He liked it less since Ashfen, where the hunters had come down on them with a precision no tracker should have managed through plague-locked streets.

He had been turning it over for three days now, the way you turn over a stone in your hand, feeling for the wrongness underneath.

Wren came and dropped beside him cross-legged, all elbows and singed cuffs, her hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a strip of banner cloth she'd stolen off a shrine in Ashfen. She was fourteen going on wildfire, and she was the only one in camp who read his slate without making him feel like a child sounding out letters.

"Well?" she said. "You've got your thinking face on. The ugly one."

*The hunters knew,* Tam wrote. *Ashfen. They came straight for us.*

"Bad luck," Wren said, but she said it too fast, the way people say things when they've already thought the worse thing themselves.

*We don't have bad luck. We have a route. And routes are written down.*

Wren was quiet. Across the fire, Felix sat hunched over Quill's maps with Corvin leaning over his shoulder, the two of them arguing softly about the crossing at the weir — which bank, which hour, whether the eel-fishers' stakes would hold a rope. Corvin had his hand on Felix's shoulder when he laughed at something. It was a good laugh. Tam had known Corvin since the boy was small enough to ride Mother Quill's hip, and it was a good laugh, warm and easy, the laugh of a man who had grown up half-wild in the marshes and never quite learned to hide anything.

That was what troubled Tam most. Because either Corvin was exactly what he seemed — or he had learned to hide everything very well indeed.

*Someone told them,* Tam wrote, and turned the slate so Wren could read it by firelight.

Her jaw set. "You think so too." It wasn't a question. She glanced toward the fire, toward Corvin's hand on Felix's shoulder, and Tam watched the thought arrive in her like a spark landing in dry grass.

"Don't," Tam wrote quickly. *Don't yet.*

"I'm not doing anything," Wren said, doing it with her whole face.

---

The camp woke cold, with mist lying on the reeds like a shroud someone had forgotten to lift. Tam went his morning rounds, because rounds were what kept him whole — checking the ward-lines Quill had strung between the alder stumps, counting the stores, scratching marks on his slate that only he and Wren could read. He had been a quartermaster once, before the brand, before the burning of the Fenwick cell. Order was the last thing they hadn't taken from him.

It was on his rounds that he found the mark.

Not a sigil. Not anything a hedge-mage would read. A hunter's trail-mark, cut into the bark of an alder at knee height, three shallow strokes with a hook at the end — the kind Crown trackers used to sign a trail for the company behind them. Fresh. The sap still wept.

Tam stood very still for a long moment, listening to the marsh breathe. Then he took out his knife and cut the marked bark away entire, wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it inside his coat against his chest, where it felt like carrying a coal.

He did not show anyone else. Not yet. Because there were only four people who had walked this stretch of the line since moonset — himself, Wren, Sable, and Corvin, who had taken the dawn watch along the water.

And Tam wanted to be wrong. He wanted it so badly his chest hurt around the wrapped bark.

---

Quill called the council at midday, in the lee of the overturned ferry hull they used as a shelter. Tam sat where he always sat, at Quill's right hand, slate ready. Felix sat opposite, looking like he hadn't slept, which he hadn't; since Ashfen and Sable's fever the boy carried guilt the way other men carried coin, spending it everywhere he went. Sable herself was up and moving now, pale but upright, her bandaged shoulder held stiff, her eyes doing their usual work of missing nothing.

Quill laid it out plainly, because she was not a woman who dressed hard news in soft cloth. "The hunters at Ashfen didn't find us. They were waiting for us. And this morning I'm told there was a mark on our own ward-line." Her eyes went around the circle. "So we say it plain, once: someone is feeding the Crown our road."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water.

"It's not possible," Felix said. "We've been careful—"

"We've been careful," Sable said flatly, "since Ashfen. Before Ashfen we were comfortable. Comfortable is where knives go in."

"You think it's one of us." Felix's voice climbed. "Sable, these people took me in when the Crown wanted me dead. Wren, Tam, Quill — Corvin grew up here—"

"And who do the hunters want more than anyone in this camp?" Wren said.

She said it quietly, which was somehow worse than shouting. Everyone looked at her. Tam saw her hands shaking and her chin up anyway, and loved her for it and grieved for her in the same breath, because he knew what it cost a child to learn suspicion.

"The Fairweather name is on every hunter's list from here to the capital," Wren went on. "Vess has been hunting Felix since Greyhollow. Every place we've gone since he joined us, they've come. Greyhollow. The quarry road. Ashfen."

"That's madness," Felix said. "I was running alone before any of you. They were hunting me before I ever met the Emberline—"

"Were they?" Wren said. "Or were they hunting the map? And who led them to the map?"

"I would die before—" Felix started hotly, and then stopped, because everyone heard it, the shape of the argument underneath: *so would any of us.* That was the poison of it. Once the word *traitor* was loose in a camp, innocence proved nothing. Everyone had reasons. Everyone had a face they'd swear by.

"Enough," Quill said, and the word fell like a stone in a well. But it was Corvin who spoke next, and Tam watched him do it, watched every detail the way he'd taught himself to watch after the brand took his voice — because a silent man sees what talkers miss.

"This is exactly what they want," Corvin said, reasonable, calm, his hands open on his knees. "They can't beat us in the field, so they turn us on each other. You're doing their work for them." He smiled at Wren, gentle, big-brother gentle. "Nobody here is a traitor, little spark. Look around. These are your people."

And it should have landed. It was a good speech, well-made, the kind that had settled a dozen camp arguments before. But Tam had spent eleven years learning the grammar of faces, and something in him flinched.

Because Corvin had answered a question no one had asked him.

Wren had named Felix. Sable had blamed comfort, not a person. No one had accused Corvin of anything. And there he was, denying it — smoothly, warmly, completely — like a man stepping in front of an arrow that hadn't been loosed.

*Told you,* Tam wrote, and turned the slate toward Wren.

Her eyes went from the slate to Corvin, and Tam watched the spark catch.

"You're right," Wren said slowly, standing. "Nobody said traitor. So why did you?"

The camp went very quiet.

Corvin's smile stayed exactly where it was, and that was the second wrongness, because smiles that don't move when the ground moves under them are painted on. "Wren—"

"You denied it," she said. "Before anyone accused you. You stood up and denied it with your whole chest, and nobody even looked at you." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Unless you already knew what was coming."

"That's enough," Quill said sharply. "Wren, sit down."

"He's your son," Wren said, wheeling on her, and the words came out crueler than she probably meant, or maybe exactly as cruel as she meant, because grief makes children cruel. "Of course you don't want to see it. Your daughter died for this fellowship and now your son is selling the rest of us—"

"*Enough.*" Quill's voice cracked across the marsh like ice giving way. For a moment the old woman looked ancient, carved from peat and river-stone, and Tam hated what he was part of doing to her.

But Corvin — Corvin laughed. Softly. Shook his head. "This is what fear does," he said sadly, to the circle, to Felix especially. "It makes us eat ourselves. Don't let it. I'm with you. All the way." And he put his hand over his heart, the old Emberline gesture, the one they made when swearing on the hearth.

Too smooth. Too complete. Tam wrote it on his slate and showed only Wren: *Innocent men are clumsy. Guilty men rehearse.*

Felix stood up then, and Tam's heart sank, because he could see it coming the way you see weather coming.

"Felix," Tam wrote, quick, warning. *Sit down.*

But Felix was already talking. "No. I've heard enough." The boy stood in the middle of the circle, thin and stubborn and lit up with that ember he carried under all his weariness, the one that had made Tam like him from the first week. "I know what it is to be accused of what you are. Ten years, the whole city looked at me and saw a charm-seller, saw a fraud, saw whatever was easiest to hate. And I know what it does to you." His voice shook. "Corvin has bled for this fellowship. He pulled me out of the weir current at the ford when I went under — he dove in after me without a thought. You want a traitor? Look harder. Because I'll vouch for him with my life, and I mean that literally, because apparently my life is the currency we're all trading in now."

Silence again. Different silence. Wren looked at Felix with something breaking behind her eyes — betrayal number two in a single morning, and both of them from people she trusted.

Corvin put his arm around Felix's shoulders. "Thank you," he said quietly. "Brother."

And Tam, watching, saw Corvin's thumb move once against Felix's shoulder — a small pat, soothing, proprietary — and saw that Corvin's eyes, over the boy's head, were not on Wren, or Quill, or anyone else in the circle.

They were on the treeline. On the north path. Counting, the way a man counts when he's expecting something to arrive.

---

Tam did not sleep that night. He sat up with the marked bark unwrapped on his knees, studying the cuts by ember-light, and let the marsh tell him its small sounds until near moonset, when it told him a new one: footsteps, soft, going out along the north path.

He followed at a distance, barefoot on the cold mud, an old man's trick — walk where the reeds grow thickest, move when the wind moves. Ahead, a shape paused at the ward-line, at the alder, and stood a long moment with its head bowed, like a man praying. Or like a man saying goodbye to a place he knows he won't see again.

Then the shape knelt and cut something into the bark, low, near the roots, where a man on rounds might miss it and a hunter reading low would find it.

Tam waited until the shape had gone back to its bedroll. Then he went to the tree, and read the fresh mark by touch, because he didn't dare strike light.

Three strokes. A hook. And beneath them, a new sign he'd only seen once before, years ago, on a burned cell's doorpost: the mark that meant *tomorrow.*

He stood in the dark with his heart going like a drum, and made the hardest choice of his silent years. Because he could wake the camp. He could write CORVIN on his slate and hold it up to the fire and split the fellowship down the seam tonight, with no proof but bark and a feeling — and if he was wrong, he'd be the old mute fool who tore them apart on the eve of everything. Quill would have to choose between her son and her people, and whichever way she chose, the Emberline would bleed.

Or he could wait for daylight, and proof, and pray that tomorrow meant something other than what it meant.

Tam chose daylight. He wrapped the evidence, went back to his bedroll, and lay staring at the stars through the reeds, composing and recomposing the sentence he would write at dawn.

He never got the chance to write it.

Because dawn came early, and it came with horns — three of them, spaced like a hunter's cadence, from the north, the east, and the water. And the first thing Tam saw, stumbling from his bedroll into the grey light of chaos, was Corvin already awake, already armed, already moving — not toward the fight.

Toward the weir. Toward the crossing. Shouting for Felix to follow him, voice cracking with perfectly pitched panic, *this way, brother, this way's clear* —

Toward the one path that wasn't.
