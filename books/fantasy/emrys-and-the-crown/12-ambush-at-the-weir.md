## 12. Ambush at the Weir

The river told them the truth three days before anyone listened to it.

Felix noticed first, because noticing was the one skill ten years of selling nothing had sharpened in him. You learned to read a customer's hands, the flicker of their eyes toward the door, the way a lie sat differently on different tongues. The river spoke the same language. For two mornings running, the herons that should have fished the shallows below the mill ruins were gone. On the third morning, the water itself changed—ran a finger's width lower than it should have, as if something upstream were drinking it.

He said nothing. He was learning, slowly and painfully, how much of belonging meant knowing when not to speak.

They traveled as pilgrims still, though Ashfen had burned that disguise down to ash of its own. Quill walked at the head with her staff and her widow's grey, Sable ranged ahead through the alder thickets like a rumor, Wren drifted at the rear humming songs she invented as she went, and Tam—Tam walked beside Felix, silent as ever, his slate hanging from his neck on a leather cord. Every few miles he'd scratch something on it and hold it up for Felix to read. *Your charmwork is getting cleaner.* Or: *You still breathe wrong when you cast. Fill the lungs. Magic is just breath that decided to stay.*

Felix had come to love those scraps of chalk more than he could say. A man who had lost his voice to a hunter's brand, who could no longer speak a single word above a whisper, who taught anyway—who *insisted* on teaching, jabbing his chalk at Felix's clumsy sigils until they stopped being clumsy. Tam had been a binder once, before the purges. Before the brand. He knew what Felix's blood was worth, and he spent himself freely passing it on, as if knowledge were a purse he wanted empty by the time he died.

*The weir tomorrow,* he wrote that evening, as they made cold camp in a stand of birches. *Cross at dawn. Low water, easy stones. Then marsh all the way to the capital roads.*

Felix nodded and banked the fire, and if a cold thread wound through him—the missing herons, the shy water—he told himself it was only the season turning.

He would spend the rest of his life wishing he had spoken.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, mist lying on the river like a sheet over a corpse.

The weir was an old thing, older than the kingdom's current name—a spine of fitted stones thrown across the current so bargemen could walk their cargo over the shallows. Moss-slicked, waist-broken in places, but crossable. Sable scouted it twice from both banks and came back satisfied, which from Sable was nearly a speech of praise.

"Clear," she reported, crouching by the cold fire pit. "No sign of hunters. No fresh tracks. We cross single file, fast and quiet, and we're in the marsh before the mist burns off."

Quill studied the river with eyes that had outlived three purges. "Too easy," she murmured, and Felix's heart stumbled, because he'd thought the same and said nothing.

"The water's low," he said quietly. "It's been dropping for three days. And there are no birds."

Sable looked at him. Something moved behind her eyes—not doubt of him, never that anymore, but the swift recalculation of a woman re-reading a map. "Say that again."

"No herons. Two days. The shallows are emptier than they should be." He swallowed. "Something upstream is holding the water back."

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Quill said, flat and certain, "A sluice. They've dammed the river above the gorge to drop the crossing stones into walking depth." Her knuckles whitened on her staff. "That's not a patrol stumbling onto us. That's a trap laid with our own route in its teeth."

"They can't know our route," Sable said. But her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was afraid and refusing to show it.

"We move now," Quill decided. "Before the mist lifts. Single file. Tam, you're with me."

They never got the chance.

The first crossbow bolt took the birch trunk a hand's width from Wren's head, and the world came apart.

* * *

Later, Felix would try to remember it as a battle, with lines and orders and the clean geometry soldiers claimed wars had. It wasn't. It was noise and white mist and the screaming of alder leaves, hunters rising out of the ground as if the marsh itself had birthed them—grey-cloaked, witch-marked, moving with a precision that turned his stomach cold. Not six men on a lucky patrol. Fourteen, maybe sixteen, placed like pieces on a board: two cutting the downstream bank, a line of them strung along the high ground where the gorge narrowed, and a squad already wading the shallows from the far side, closing the last door.

They had known. Not guessed—*known*. Known the crossing point, known the hour, known the order of march. The ambush closed around them like a fist around a moth.

"Wren!" Sable's voice cracked across the chaos. "Fire, left bank, *now!*"

Wren didn't need telling twice. The girl flung both arms out and the wet birches on the left slope went up in a roar of green-wood flame, steam and smoke boiling into the mist. Hunters scattered from the heat, and for thirty heartbeats the fist loosened.

"Upstream!" Quill's staff blazed with pale light as she struck a bolt from the air. "The gorge path! Move!"

They ran. Felix ran with the ledger pressed against his ribs and charms waking hot along his sleeves without his willing them—the old anchors answering fear with light. A bolt hissed past his ear. He threw a warding sigil behind him without looking, felt it catch and shatter, bought himself half a second.

And then the far squad rose out of the shallows between them and the gorge path, and the way was shut.

Six hunters, wet to the waist, crossbows leveled. Behind them, the mist was lifting, and Felix saw the full shape of the trap at last: the dropped water, the placed men, the timing tuned to the minute. Somewhere a horn sounded twice, short-short, and the hunters advanced in step.

They were going to die at a weir, in the mist, for a king most of them had never seen.

Then Tam stepped forward.

* * *

He didn't run. That was the thing Felix could never make peace with afterward—that Tam didn't run. He walked out ahead of them, unhurried, an old man in a patched coat with a slate on a cord and no voice to shout a warning or a farewell. He set his feet on the mossy spine of the weir itself, dead center, the current sliding thin and quick around his ankles.

And he began to bind.

Felix felt it before he understood it—a deep hum in his bones, in his teeth, in the water underfoot. Tam's hands moved through shapes Felix had spent weeks learning, but at a speed and surety that made them look like a child's chalk scratches next to scripture. The old binder reached down—not to the river, *into* it—and the river answered.

The shallow water over the crossing stones stood up.

It rose in a wall, ten feet, fifteen, a curtain of grey-green glass hung in the morning air, and behind it the hunters' formation simply ceased to exist as a threat. Bolts fired into it dropped like tired flies. Men wading toward it staggered, caught, were folded under and spat out downstream, coughing and weaponless. Tam stood at the center of it with his arms spread and his coat whipping, and though his ruined throat made no sound, Felix heard him anyway—heard him in the grinding song of the stone, in the pressure against his eardrums, in every anchor-charm on his body ringing like a struck bell.

*Go,* the water said, in Tam's stolen voice. *GO.*

"Tam—" Felix started forward and Quill's hand clamped his shoulder like iron.

"He holds the crossing," she said, and her voice was terrible and steady. "It's what he was for, boy. What we were all for. *Move.*"

"But—"

"*MOVE!*"

They ran, upstream along the gorge path while the wall of water thundered behind them and Tam held it, held it, held it. Felix looked back exactly once. He wished with everything in him that he hadn't. Tam had turned his head, just slightly, just enough—and through the veiling spray his eyes found Felix's, and the old man smiled.

It was the smile of a teacher whose student has finally filled his lungs correctly.

Then the hunters on the high ground found their range, and the first bolt took Tam through the chest, and the second, and the wall of water shuddered, sagged, held—held another ten heartbeats, twenty, long enough, always long enough—and came down.

The sound it made followed Felix for miles.

* * *

They fled through the marsh until the gorge was a memory and the mist had burned away and there was nothing left in any of them but the animal arithmetic of putting one foot in front of another. They lost the hunters in the peat-cuttings, doubled back twice on Sable's instinct, and finally holed up in a drover's ruin where the walls were turf and the roof was half gone and nobody suggested a fire.

Wren sat with her knees pulled up and her forehead on them, small and shaking, the fire-glow gone out of her. Sable crouched at the doorway with a crossbow across her lap, watching the reeds, saying nothing, because Sable's grief came out as vigilance. Quill stood in the middle of the ruin like a woman who had forgotten what legs were for.

Felix sat against the wall and stared at his hands.

*I'm sorry,* Tam had written on his slate, once, early on. *I can't say the words anymore. So I'll write them instead:* and beneath it, in careful chalk letters he'd never wiped away: *You were worth teaching.*

The slate was gone. It had gone into the river with him, and Felix would have given every charm he owned, every secret in the ledger, every year he had left, to fish it out.

"He knew," Quill said at last. Her voice came out sanded flat. "They knew the crossing. The hour. The order of march. That is not luck, and it is not good scouting." She turned, slowly, and looked at each of them in turn. "Someone told them."

The words landed in the ruin like a dropped blade.

"No," Wren said, lifting her head. "No, nobody—it's not—"

"Fourteen hunters," Sable said quietly, still watching the reeds. "Placed like coins on a board. Sluice gates opened on schedule. Someone drew them a map, Wren. Someone who knew everything."

Silence. The reeds whispered. Felix felt the cold thought arrive in him the way frost arrives on glass—slowly, then all at once, spreading from a single point.

Corvin.

He pushed it away. Corvin was Quill's son; Corvin had argued for caution when caution was needed; Corvin had carried Wren four miles through the Ashfen fever-country when she collapsed. Corvin was family. Family was the whole point of everything they'd bled for—

But the frost kept spreading. Because Corvin had been on rear guard that morning. Because Corvin had argued, three nights ago, for this exact crossing, this exact hour, when Sable had wanted to push north to the ford. Because Corvin had been too smooth about it, too reasonable, and Felix had backed him—had *vouched* for him, warm and certain, because defending people was the one thing Felix Fairweather had always believed himself to be good at.

"I saw something," Felix heard himself say.

Everyone turned. His mouth was dry. Once the words were out, they could not be taken back, and he knew—with the same sick certainty he'd felt reading the dying courier's map—that they would change everything.

"At the weir. When we broke for the gorge path." He made himself keep going. "On the high ground. I saw someone signal the hunters. Arm raised, three slow sweeps. The kind of thing you do when you want them to know exactly where we're running." He swallowed. "It was Corvin."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Wren's face went white, then blotchy red. Sable turned from the doorway at last, and her eyes met Felix's, and there was no surprise in them at all—which somehow made it worse, because it meant she had been counting the same frost-spreading arithmetic and waiting for someone else to say it aloud.

"You're sure," Quill said. Not a question. A request, almost. *Tell me you're sure. Tell me my son didn't kill Tam.*

"I'm sure it was his build. I'm sure of the coat. I'm sure—" Felix's voice cracked and he hated it. "I defended him. When Wren accused him. I told you all he was true. So if I'm wrong, then I've done worse than lose a friend—I've cost us Tam."

Quill closed her eyes. When she opened them, the mother was gone and something older remained, something from the deep years of the Emberline, and it was worse than grief because it was *decided*.

"Then we hold judgment," she said. "Outside the law, as we have always done. In the marsh, before witnesses." She lifted her staff, and the pale light along it did not flicker. "Find my son."

* * *

They found him an hour later, alone at the edge of the peat-cuttings, washing his hands in a black pool as if the water could take something off them that would not come off.

He looked up at the circle of them—at his mother's staff, at Sable's leveled crossbow, at Wren burning low and blue with a fury too cold for flames—and Felix watched Corvin's face do the thing that confirmed everything: it smoothed. Too quickly. Too completely. A guilty man would have panicked. An innocent man would have raged. Corvin arranged his features like a shopkeeper arranging goods, and said, "Whatever you've heard—"

"Three sweeps," Felix said. "High ground. I saw you."

And Corvin looked at him—looked at the man who had vouched for him, who had spent his trust like a fool spends coin—and smiled.

It was the smile Felix would carry into the capital, into the dark under the palace, into every fight that came after: small, apologetic, and utterly without shame.

"You should have let Wren hang me at camp," Corvin said softly. "You'd all have slept better."

Behind him, the marsh wind moved through the reeds, and somewhere far downstream, in water none of them would ever fish again, a slate lay in the silt with its last message still written in chalk, waiting for a rain that would never reach it.
