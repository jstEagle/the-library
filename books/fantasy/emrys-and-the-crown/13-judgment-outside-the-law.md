## 13. Judgment Outside the Law

The marshes did not care who had betrayed whom.

That was the first mercy of them, Mother Quill thought as she stood ankle-deep in black water and watched the fellowship gather on the hummocks around her. The reeds bent in the wind and straightened again without opinion. The herons went on hunting. The mud held every footstep with the same indifferent grip, whether it belonged to a loyalist or a liar. In Greyhollow, in the capital, in every court and constabulary from here to the coast, judgment was a performance—gallows dressed in bunting, verdicts written before the accused was ever named. Here there was only water, and the people who had chosen to stand in it together.

And one of them had sold them to the Crown.

Tam's body lay wrapped in oilcloth on the widest hummock, weighted with river stones because they could not burn him—not here, not with hunters still combing the weirs downstream for their trail. They would carry him to the flooded quarry when it was safe, and give him to the hearth-fire as the Emberline gave all its dead. But that rite would wait. First there was this.

Corvin knelt in the shallows twenty paces from his mother, his hands bound behind him with the same kind of cord he'd used, no doubt, to signal the hunters at the weir. His face was swollen where Wren had struck him. He did not look at anyone. He looked at the water, as if answers might rise out of it like frogs.

Quill felt the weight of every eye upon her. Forty-one souls left of what had been forty-three yesterday morning. Tam was under the oilcloth. Corvin was in the water. And she was the one they were waiting for, because she had built this fellowship out of nothing—out of fugitives and hedge-witches and smugglers and children too strange for their villages—and everything the Emberline was, it was because she had decided it would be so. She had never once, in nineteen years, been unsure of herself in front of them.

She was unsure now.

"Bring him up," she said.

Sable hauled Corvin to his feet by the collar and shoved him onto the dry crown of the hummock where Quill stood. He stumbled, caught himself badly with his bound hands, and stayed down on his knees—which was fitting, though Quill took no satisfaction in it. Felix Fairweather moved as if to step forward, and Sable put out an arm and stopped him without looking away from the prisoner. The boy had defended Corvin two nights ago, when the accusations first flew. He looked like a man watching a wound he'd bandaged turn septic.

Wren sat apart on a fallen willow, knees drawn up, fire coiled sullenly in her cupped palms so that the rain hissed off them. She had not spoken since the weir. She had not needed to. She had seen Corvin's lantern flash three times across the water, same as Felix had, and she had said only one word since—"told"—and then gone quiet in the way of young things deciding whether grief or rage would win.

Quill let the silence stretch until even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"Corvin Emberline," she said, using the name she had given him herself, the name that meant he belonged to something larger than blood. "You are accused of passing word of our route, our numbers, and our crossing to Captain Vess of the Crown's witch-hunters. By your signal, Tam Voss died holding the weir. By your signal, six more of us nearly followed him. Speak."

"I have nothing to say." His voice was flat. Too smooth. Quill had heard that smoothness before—in men lying about card debts, in women denying stolen bread. It was the voice of someone who had rehearsed.

"You have everything to say," Quill answered. "You have the rest of your life to say it in, however long that proves. I am asking you once, as your mother and as your hearth-mother: why?"

Something cracked in him then. Not dramatically—no sobbing confession, no collapse. Just a hairline fracture running through the smoothness, visible only to someone who had known his face since it was small enough to fit in one hand. His jaw worked. He stared at the water.

"They have my daughter," he said.

* * *

The words fell into the marsh and sank, and for a long moment nobody moved.

"Mara," Quill said. The name came out of her like a stone coughed up from the chest. Her granddaughter. Eight years old, gap-toothed, forever drawing chalk sigils on the quarry walls that did nothing but were beautiful anyway. "They took Mara."

"Six weeks ago." Corvin's voice came faster now, the dam broken, the rehearsed calm drowned. "From her mother's house in Millbrook. No note. No demand. Then a man found me at the ferry crossing—a hunter, plainclothes, but I knew the boots—and he said the captain wanted information, and if the information was good, Mara would eat well. If it stopped being good—" He swallowed. "I sent word twice before Ashfen. Small things. Times and places. I told myself it was nothing they could use, that I was playing for time, learning where they kept her. Then you announced the crossing at the weir, and I knew—I *knew*—that this time they'd come in force, and I signaled anyway, because the man had shown me a letter in her handwriting. *Papa I ate plums.* Her little crooked letters. And I thought, they'll scatter, they'll get away, they always get away, and I'll get my daughter back—"

"Tam didn't get away," Wren said. One word became four, hurled like stones into the water. She stood up on the willow log, fire flaring off her fists. "Old Tam, who taught me letters. Who couldn't even *shout* when they came, because your friends burned his voice out of him ten years ago, and he still stood on the crossing and held thirty hunters off with a slingshot and a bad leg—"

"Wren." Quill's voice was quiet, and it cut through the girl's fury like a blade through wet canvas. Not yet. Let him finish. Justice that interrupted itself was just another brawl.

But Corvin had stopped talking. He knelt in the mud with his mouth open, and whatever else he had rehearsed, he had not prepared for Tam's name spoken aloud in the rain.

"He couldn't call out," Wren went on, lower now, shaking. "So he whistled. Did you know that? Through his teeth, the way he used to whistle us home to supper. That's how we knew to run. He whistled us safe and they shot him off the weir and you lit your little lantern and watched."

Corvin bowed his head until his forehead nearly touched the mud. "Yes," he whispered. "I watched."

* * *

Quill turned away from him and faced the fellowship.

Forty faces looked back at her through the drizzle, and she read them the way she read weather. Sable stood with arms crossed and a knife already in her hand—not brandished, just present, the way a fact is present. Felix Fairweather stood pale and stricken beside her, and Quill saw in his face the particular misery of a man whose instinct for mercy had just been proven expensive. Old Marta of the reed-cutters had her arms folded and her lips pressed white. Young Osric, barely sixteen, was crying without seeming to know it. Half the fellowship wore vengeance openly; the other half wore fear, because if Corvin could be turned by a hostage, any of them could be, and mercy for him might look like permission.

"The law of the Emberline is written in three sentences," Quill said, loud enough to carry over the reeds. "We keep each other alive. We keep each other's secrets. We do not sell each other. Corvin has broken the third sentence, and the price of the third sentence has always been paid in full." She paused. "You know what I'm saying. I won't dress it in softer cloth. The old law says death."

"It says more than says," Sable said. Her voice was level, almost gentle, which was how Sable sounded when she was at her most dangerous. "It says rope or river, hearth-mother. Your own words, the night we wrote them. You made me swear it, and I'd sworn nothing to anyone in ten years before that."

"I remember my own words, Sable."

"Then say them."

And there it was—the whole trial compressed into three syllables. Quill looked out over the water. Rain needled the surface into a million small wounds that healed instantly. Somewhere a bittern boomed, low and mournful, like the marsh itself clearing its throat.

Nineteen years ago, she had founded the Emberline in a flooded quarry with eleven half-starved mages and one principle: that outlawed people must govern themselves justly, or the Crown's lies about them would become true. No gallows. No crown courts. No burning. Whatever justice they practiced had to be justice the Crown could never practice—justice that remembered the person under the crime. She had said those words standing on a barrel with her boots soaked through, and every soul in the Emberline had learned them as children learn prayers.

But Tam was dead. And Tam had been hers since the beginning too—since before the beginning, since the quarry, since the barrel. Tam, who had lost his voice to a hunter's brand rather than name a single soul among them, and who had spent ten years speaking on a slate, and whose last act had been to whistle his family to safety while the arrows found him.

Her daughter had betrayed that man. Her grandson-in-law of the heart, her own flesh, kneeling in the mud with river water soaking through his trousers.

Justice weighed against vengeance, and both pans hung level, and she was the fulcrum.

"Corvin," she said. "Look at me."

He raised his head. His eyes were wrecked. Whatever smoothness remained had washed away entirely, and what was underneath was just a tired man of thirty-four years who had loved his daughter more than his honor, which was a sin she understood down to the marrow.

"If I order your death," Quill said, "every soul here will carry it. Some will be glad. Some will be sickened. All of them will know that the Emberline kills its own, and the Crown will know it too, and they will use it—they will paint us as exactly the savages they already claim we are, and some frightened hedge-witch in some village will think twice before knocking on our door, because we are people who kill our brother when he stumbles." She drew breath. "And if I spare you, Tam's slate stays blank forever, and Wren grows up learning that betrayal costs a boat ride, and the next one of you with a hostage daughter will do the arithmetic and find it comes out cheap."

"Then what's the answer?" Sable demanded. "You can't have both, hearth-mother."

"No," Quill said. "But I can refuse the question as it's asked. The Crown asks: rope or river? Kill him or cage him? Those are the only judgments they understand, because the Crown does not deal in people—it deals in examples." She turned back to her son. "Stand up, Corvin."

He rose, unsteady, bound hands dripping.

"Here is my judgment, and let every ear carry it to every hearth of the Emberline, and let them argue about it for a hundred years if they must." Her voice hardened into the register she used for oaths. "Corvin Emberline is cast out. Struck from the roll. His name is taken from him—he is Corvin of nowhere, son of no hearth, owed nothing by any member of this fellowship ever again. Any hearth that shelters him chooses exile alongside him. He keeps his life because the Emberline does not make examples of its people—but he loses everything else, and everything else was the whole of it."

A sound went through the gathered company—not agreement, not quite protest. A shifting, like wind through wheat.

"And Mara?" Felix asked quietly. He alone had dared to speak the name everyone was thinking. "If he goes out alone, they'll never release her. Or worse."

Quill closed her eyes briefly. Yes. Worse. She had thought of nothing else since the words *they have my daughter* left his mouth. Exile was not mercy if it signed a child's death warrant; it was cruelty wearing mercy's coat.

"He goes out alone," she said, "and he goes out with one task, and one chance. He finds his daughter. And when he has her—if he has her—he sends word by the sign we agree on tonight, and the Emberline will decide, hearth by hearth, whether a man who whistled hunters onto Tam Voss has earned a second hearing." She looked around the circle. "That is not forgiveness. That is a door left unlocked. Whether anyone walks through it is another matter entirely."

* * *

They argued until dark.

Of course they did. Quill had expected obedience—she had earned that much, hadn't she?—but what she got instead was the fellowship doing exactly what she had trained it to do: thinking for itself, and saying so.

Sable led the hard line. "Mercy is a luxury bought with other people's blood," she said, standing in the firelight on the largest hummock, rain streaming off her jaw. "I've buried friends sold out by informers before. Every time, the informer lived to inform again. Cast him out today, and in six months Vess has him back in her pocket with a new hostage story, and we lose the next ambush too."

"He confessed!" Felix shot back. He was soaked through and hoarse and had apparently decided that defending Corvin was a debt he intended to keep paying past all reason. "He didn't deny it, didn't lie, didn't blame anyone else. When did a Crown court last get a confession like that? We're supposed to be better than them, not just better-armed!"

"Better than them doesn't mean stupid," Sable snapped. "You've known him five weeks, charm-seller. Some of us have known him fifteen years, which means we also know how good he is at telling people what they need to hear."

"He's my *brother*," Felix said, and then flushed, because it wasn't literally true and was somehow more true than anything literal.

Osric spoke for the middle: trembling, tear-streaked, sixteen. "I don't want him killed. I don't want him forgiven. I don't know what I want." A few heads nodded at that, because most of the Emberline, if honest, wanted the same impossible thing.

And Wren said nothing at all, until the very end, when the fire had burned down to coals sheltered under an oilskin and the vote—or rather, the weighing, for Quill refused a show of hands on a matter of life—had all but settled. Then the girl stood, walked to the edge of the firelight, and looked at Corvin across the dark water.

"My grandmother taught me that fire isn't good or evil," Wren said. "It's just hunger, and what you feed it. You fed yours the wrong thing." She turned to Quill. "Exile. But hearth-mother—if he signals hunters again, if one more of us dies of him, I don't care what the law says. I'll find him. And I won't leave anything to argue about."

No one contradicted her. There are threats that land harder for being calm, and a feral fire-speaker stating a fact qualified.

* * *

They released him at dawn, at the marsh's eastern edge, where the ground firmed toward the drover's road.

Quill performed the unmaking herself. She took the cord from his wrists, then took his name—spoke the words of striking that had only ever been spoken twice before in the Emberline's history, both times for murderers—and watched him flinch as though each syllable were a physical blow. When she finished, he stood straighter and somehow smaller, a man erased.

"Your mother's ring," she said, holding out her palm. He had worn his father's signet since he was seventeen. He worked it off his finger with numb fingers and set it in her hand, and Quill closed her fist around warm metal and cold morning and did not let her face move at all.

"Mara's mother," Corvin said hoarsely. "Tell her—"

"Tell her yourself," Quill said. "When you send the sign. That's part of the price."

He nodded. He took three steps toward the road, then stopped, his back to her, shoulders rigid against the grey light.

"She draws sigils on everything," he said, without turning. "Chalk, mud, flour spilled on a table. They don't do anything. They're just beautiful." His voice broke cleanly in half. "Like her mother said about mine, once."

Then he walked, and the mist took him, and Quill stood in the reeds until Sable came to stand beside her.

"You did the right thing," Sable said, in the tone of a woman reporting a rumor she didn't believe.

"I did the *hard* thing," Quill answered. "Whether it was right, we'll know in a year, or we'll be dead and it won't matter." She opened her fist and looked at the ring. "Half the fellowship thinks I'm soft. Half thinks I'm cruel. That's usually the shape of a correct decision."

"And the capital?" Sable asked. "We've lost Tam, we've lost time, we've lost trust—and the coronation of the Regent's heir is nine days out. We're a wounded company walking into the lion's mouth."

Quill turned east, where beyond the marsh and the ash-country and the plague-towns lay the gilded rot of the capital, and somewhere inside it a hollow king who wept without knowing why, and a thing beneath the palace older than the kingdom, scratching at a door her grandfather's grandfather had helped seal.

"Then we walk faster," she said, "and we trust each other harder, because the alternative is dying separately." She pocketed the ring. "Break camp, Sable. We go to the capital."

Behind them, on the far hummock, Wren fed the morning fire, and the flames stood unnaturally tall and steady in the wind—as if even the fire had opinions about what was coming.
