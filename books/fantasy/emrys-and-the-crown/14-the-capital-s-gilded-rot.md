## 14. The Capital's Gilded Rot

The capital announced itself by smell before it ever showed its face.

Felix had expected rot—every city rotted somewhere—but this was different. Beneath the woodsmoke and roasting chestnuts of festival week, under the garlands strung between lampposts and the honeyed reek of spilled wine, there was a thin, sweet wrongness in the air. Like flowers left too long in a sickroom. Like something burning that wasn't quite wood.

"Charm-smoke," Sable said quietly beside him. She'd pulled her hood low, and her voice came out flattened, careful. "They've been burning for three days straight. You can smell it from the river."

Felix breathed it in again and felt his stomach turn over. He knew what burning charms smelled like now. Ten years he'd sold brass and wishful thinking, but since Ashfen—since the warming charm he'd cupped around dying children's hands until his own fingers blistered—he knew what real magic smelled like when it woke. And he knew what it smelled like when it died.

It smelled like this. Like a hundred small hopes going up at once.

"Don't look like a pilgrim who's about to be sick," Wren muttered on his other side. She was wearing borrowed robes two sizes too big, sleeves rolled six times, and she kept touching the rope belt like it might bite her. Of all of them, she looked least like a penitent and most like a child playing dress-up, which was perhaps why no one gave them a second glance. Pilgrims came in every shape during festival week. The roads into the capital were thick with them.

"We look like everyone else," Quill said from the front of their little line. She walked with a pilgrim's staff and a pilgrim's stoop, gray-haired and bent, and only Felix could see how her eyes never stopped moving. "Tired, dusty, devout. Keep your eyes down and your mouths shut, and we'll be just five more faces in a crowd of thousands."

Five faces. Tam should have made six. Felix caught himself counting him anyway, the way you catch your tongue probing a missing tooth. At the weir, Tam had stood on the crossing with his slate held high—*GO. I'LL HOLD THE WATER.*—and the water had obeyed him one last time, rising up in a wall to swallow the hunters' arrows and buy them thirty heartbeats. They'd found his body downstream two days later, and they'd buried him in the marshes with river stones on his eyes, because Corvin was already gone by then, exiled into the fog with Vess's daughter's name carved on a token around his neck, and no one had wanted to wait.

Felix still didn't know if defending Corvin had been mercy or blindness. He suspected he'd carry that question longer than he carried anything else.

"Gate ahead," Sable murmured. "Heads down. Smile like you love the Crown."

The gate of the capital was called the King's Mouth, and it lived up to the name: a vast arching maw of pale stone, teeth of iron portcullis, guards in silver-and-black livery standing in the throat of it. Festival week meant open gates—the Regent wanted the city full, wanted it *loud*, wanted ten thousand witnesses to the coronation of his heir—and so the queue moved quickly, guards waving pilgrims through with barely a glance.

But there was a table set up beside the gate, and at the table sat a woman with a brazier, and into the brazier each traveler was invited to drop a charm.

"It's voluntary," the woman droned, in the voice of someone reciting a script for the thousandth time. "In honor of His Majesty's coronation vigil, all loyal subjects are invited to surrender unlicensed talismans. A blessing will be recorded in your name."

A blessing recorded in your name. Felix watched a farmer ahead of them hesitate, then dig in his coat and produce a knotted thread—a fertility knot, probably, or a fever-knot, the kind of thing grandmothers had been tying since before there were kings. He dropped it in the brazier. It flared green for half a heartbeat before the flame went orange, and the woman made a mark on a ledger without looking up.

"They're cataloguing," Quill breathed. "Look at the ledger. They're not just burning—they're *recording*. Who gives up what. Who hesitates." Her hand tightened on her staff. "That's not a pyre. That's a census."

Then they were at the table themselves, and there was no time to think.

"In honor of the vigil," the woman said, holding out the brazier.

Felix's hand went to his collar, where three charms hung against his skin—the waking-stone, the door-knot, and the little brass disc that had been the first thing he'd ever woken, back in the quarry, when Mother Quill had folded his fingers around it and said *now, Fairweather, now*. Giving them up wasn't even a question. But refusing the brazier entirely would mark them worse than hesitation.

So he did what a Fairweather did best. He lied.

"I have nothing but my prayers," he said, and let his shoulders slump into the shape of honest poverty, and looked the woman full in the face with the mild, empty eyes of a man who has never once thought an interesting thought. Beside him, Wren turned out her empty pockets with theatrical dejection, and Sable pressed a copper coin into the brazier—"for the blessing, mother"—and the woman marked them down as clean.

Only Quill dropped something in. A plain wooden bead, worn smooth. It flared soft blue and died, and Felix saw her jaw tighten as she watched it go. Something of hers. Something old. A tithe paid to keep them moving.

Then the King's Mouth swallowed them, and they were inside.

* * *

The capital was beautiful the way a corpse dressed for burial is beautiful.

Felix had grown up in Greyhollow, where beauty meant a dry roof and a full till, and he'd assumed the stories were exaggerations—the marble terraces, the canals spanned by bridges carved with kings, the palace on its hill with banners running down its flank like a spill of blood and gold. But the stories hadn't exaggerated. The city really was lovely. That was the horror of it. Every street they walked was more beautiful than the last, and every street bore the same marks: white-washed doorways where notices had been nailed up, corners where chalk sigils had been scrubbed away so hard the stone was pitted, lampposts with fresh scorch-marks at shoulder height where someone had burned something—or been burned.

And everywhere, everywhere, the Regent's face.

It hung from balconies on silk banners. It stared from shop windows in gilt-framed portraits. Children sold woodcut prints of it in the streets, three for a penny. It was a handsome face, stern and serene, framed by the crown, and beneath it ran the same words on every print, every banner, every wall:

*THE CROWN PROVIDES. THE CROWN PROTECTS. THE CROWN IS ETERNAL.*

"He's everywhere," Wren whispered, staring up at a banner big enough to sail a ship under. "You'd think people would get tired of looking at him."

"People get tired of anything," Sable said. "Except being told they're safe."

They lodged that night in a pilgrim hospice run by a widow with kind eyes and a locked cellar, and after dark she brought them down into it and fed them stew, and told them what the city had become in whispers, because even here, even among the poor, you didn't speak above a murmur.

"The burnings started last spring," she said, stirring the pot without looking at anyone. "Little ones, at first. Charms confiscated at the gates. Then they started taking the charm-makers. Not arresting—taking. Whole families, some of them. There's a list posted outside the Hall of Justice every week. New names in black ink." She paused. "My sister's name is on it. Third week of summer."

No one spoke. The stew steamed.

"And tomorrow," she went on, quieter, "is the Procession. The king rides through the square to the cathedral for the vigil. Everyone must attend. Everyone must cheer." She finally looked up, and her kind eyes were hard as river stones. "You'll see him. The whole city sees him. And I'll tell you what I tell every pilgrim who asks: don't look too long at his eyes. Folk who look too long start saying things. And folk who say things end up on lists."

* * *

The Procession began at noon, and the city packed itself into Coronation Square like grain into a sack.

Felix had never seen so many people. They stood shoulder to shoulder from the fountain steps to the cathedral doors, ten thousand deep, and the strange thing—the thing that crawled up the back of his neck and stayed there—was how quiet they were. Festival week, and nobody sang. Bunting overhead, wine-sellers crying their wares in voices pitched carefully flat, children perched on fathers' shoulders, and underneath it all a silence like held breath.

"They're afraid," Quill said. She'd found them a place against a colonnade, half in shadow, close enough to see and far enough to move. "Ten thousand people, and listen. No one wants to be the first to cheer."

Trumpets split the air.

The Procession came through the great eastern arch in a blaze of gold: outriders in silver-and-black, heralds throwing coins that no one scrambled for, priests swinging censers whose smoke lay heavy and sweet over the crowd—that was the smell, Felix realized, the sickroom sweetness, censers burning day and night to cover the charmburn—and then, on a white horse led slowly by two grooms, the king.

King Aldric the Second, Shield of the Realm, Father of His People.

Felix had seen his face on coins his whole life. He'd expected age, pomp, distance. What he had not expected was how *small* the man looked.

Aldric sat his horse the way a coat sits on a hook. His back was straight because someone had arranged it straight; his hand lifted to bless the crowd in slow, perfect intervals, like a clockwork figure wound for the occasion. He wore the crown, and the crown seemed to sit strangely on him—not resting on his head so much as *gripping* it, its points sinking in past the hair, and Felix told himself it was shadow, it was the angle, it was distance playing tricks—

And then the horse passed close, twenty feet away, close enough to see the pores of the king's skin, and Felix looked into Aldric's eyes.

Glass-grey. Flat as a winter pond. No light behind them at all.

*Eyes gone dull as slag,* his grandfather's ledger had written, in the cramped hand Felix had spent ten years thinking was just an old man's crabbed script. *Third time this season the court physician bleeds him and swears the blood runs thin and cold. The guild must wake the anchors. Whatever sleeps beneath the palace is stirring, and it drinks through the crown.*

Felix had read that entry a hundred times in the quarry, by candlelight, and it had always been history. Ancient grief, ancient warning, words about dead men. Now he stood in a living crowd watching those words wear a living face, and his mouth went dry as ash.

Because that wasn't the worst of it.

The worst came as the king reached the center of the square, where a platform had been raised and priests waited with the vigil relics. The grooms halted the horse. Trumpets blared. Ten thousand people cheered on command, a roar like surf, and the king lifted his hand to bless them—

—and stopped.

His hand hung in the air. His head tilted, very slightly, the way a man tilts his head when he hears his name called from another room. And then King Aldric the Second, Shield of the Realm, began to weep.

Silently. Without changing expression. Tears simply slid down that serene, distant face, one after another, while the smile beneath them stayed fixed, and the hand went on making its slow blessing gesture, and the crowd roared its joy, and not one soul in ten thousand dared acknowledge what they were seeing.

One of the grooms stepped in close, produced a cloth, dabbed the king's cheeks with the brisk tenderness of a stablehand wiping down a horse, and withdrew. The trumpets sounded again. The Procession moved on.

Felix became aware that Wren was gripping his sleeve hard enough to tear it. Her face was white. "He was crying," she whispered. "Felix. He was *crying* and he didn't know it. He didn't—there was nothing behind his face doing it. It was like—" She stopped, hunting for the word, and found the worst one. "Like me. Before. When the fire came out and I couldn't stop it and I didn't know why."

Felix looked at her—at this feral, brilliant child who knew exactly what it was to be ridden by something your body did without asking you—and felt the horror come home. This was what Quill had tried to warn them of in the marshes, around Tam's grave: *this isn't a monster story, it's a theft story.* Somewhere under all that gold, a man was trapped in his own skull, weeping through eyes that no longer belonged to him, while his kingdom cheered his jailer.

"That's our king," Sable said softly. "That's what we're walking into the palace to save." She laughed, short and bitter. "Half of me wonders if we shouldn't let the poor man rest."

"No," Quill said. It came out like a hammerfall. Her eyes were fixed on the retreating Procession, and there was something in her face Felix had never seen there before—not grief, not anger, but recognition, old and personal and terrible. "No. Look again. Look at how he stopped. Look at *when*."

Felix looked. Replayed it. The king's hand hanging, the head tilting, the tears—

"He heard something," Felix said slowly. "Inside. For a second, he heard something."

"Aldric heard something," Quill corrected. "Or Aldric *said* something, and the thing wearing him had to spend strength to hold his mouth shut. Either way—" she turned, and gripped Felix's arm with fingers like iron, "—he's still in there, boy. Fighting. After however many years of that glass-eyed dark, he is *still fighting*, and today, in front of ten thousand people, he slipped his leash for three seconds." Her grip tightened. "Three seconds is a crack. And cracks, Fairweather, are what your family breaks."

Around them the crowd was dispersing, obedient and silent, flowing toward the evening's festivals like water finding its level. Overhead the banners snapped in the wind—*THE CROWN IS ETERNAL*—and somewhere across the city a new pyre was surely being lit, sweet smoke rising over the spires.

Quill released his arm and straightened, and when she spoke again her voice was quiet, and steady, and final.

"Rest tonight. All of you. Tomorrow we watch the vigil, and learn the guard rotations, and find our way down." She looked up at the palace on its hill, crowned in torchlight against the darkening sky, and her old eyes narrowed. "Because whatever sleeps beneath that hill felt three seconds of daylight today. And come tomorrow night, Fairweather—we're going to give it a great deal more."

Behind them, in the emptying square, a lone crier took up his post and began, in a voice trained to brightness, to announce the morrow's glories: the vigil, the oath, the crowning of the heir.

And beneath the crier's feet, unnoticed by ten thousand departing feet, the old flagstones were warm.
