## 15. Beneath the Crown

Wren had never liked being small. Small meant overlooked, and overlooked meant invisible, and invisible meant that when she was eight and the tithe-collectors burned her mother's cart with everything they owned inside it, nobody looked at the girl crouched in the ditch with sparks still dancing on her fingertips like guilty little stars. Being small had taught her one thing worth knowing: fire doesn't care if you're seen. Fire only cares what it touches.

So she let herself be small now. Small as a candle flame cupped in two hands. She walked three paces behind Felix Fairweather through the festival-crowded streets of the capital, wearing a pilgrim's grey hood and carrying a bundle of blessed candles she'd bought from an actual priest, which felt like the funniest joke she'd ever been part of, and nobody in all that glittering rot of a city knew she could burn their festival to the ground with a sneeze.

"You're grinning," Felix murmured without turning his head.

"I'm devout," Wren said. "It's a holy expression."

"It's a *suspicious* expression."

"Everything about you is suspicious, Fairweather. You walk like a man apologizing to the ground."

That earned her half a smile, there and gone, the kind he'd been rationing since Ashfen. Since Sable's wound. Since Tam's slate went cold and Quill carried it wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping bird. Wren didn't say any of that. Grief in the Emberline was passed around quiet, like bread at a table where everyone was hungry.

The palace rose ahead of them out of the festival haze, white stone gone honey-gold in the lantern light, banners of the crown hanging fat and motionless in the windless evening. Coronation eve. The whole city smelled of roasting meat and hot sugar and, underneath it, faint as a bad memory, smoke — because tomorrow morning they were burning another cartload of charms in Coronation Square, and the Regent wanted the air seasoned for it.

Wren touched the signet ring through her shirt, where it hung on a cord against her sternum.

*The dead woman's ring.* The courier who had died in Felix's shop ten years' worth of trouble ago. Mother Quill's daughter. The ring was warm against Wren's skin, or she imagined it was, the way she imagined every lock was already open if you just asked it nicely enough with heat.

Felix stopped at the mouth of the Lanterners' Row, where the palace wall met the old cistern district, and pretended to adjust his sandal strap. "Guard change is soon," he said. "Vess said the undercroft stair is only watched during the changeover. Four minutes of nothing."

"Four minutes." Wren cracked her knuckles. "I've boiled eggs longer than that."

"That's not reassuring."

"I wasn't trying to reassure you. I was trying to make you hungry so you stop thinking."

He laughed — actually laughed, short and startled out of him — and for a moment he was just a tired young man in a borrowed robe instead of the last Fairweather, the door-holder, the one whose blood ran thick with binding craft and whose shoulders had gotten narrower every day since he'd volunteered. Wren had watched him volunteer. She'd watched everyone else open their mouths to argue and watched Quill close hers first, and Wren understood why: some things you couldn't argue a person out of. You could only stand beside them while they did it.

She intended to stand very close beside him. Close enough to burn anything that came through that door.

* * *

The cistern gate was older than the wall around it, iron gone to lace with rust, and it took Wren eleven seconds and one held breath to persuade the lock that it had always been open. Heat was honest work. You fed a lock's metal until it forgot its shape, gently, gently, the way you coaxed a stubborn kettle. Her mother had taught her that much before the burning: *fire is patient if you are.*

Inside was wet dark and the smell of a hundred years of standing water. Their footsteps echoed wrong — too many footsteps, as if the dark kept its own company down here. Felix unrolled the map by feel, and Wren lit the smallest flame she knew how to make, a bead of light no bigger than a ladybird, hovering over her palm. It was the first thing she'd ever taught herself, that bead. Everything else had come after, wilder.

"The map says the undercroft stair is behind the third arch," Felix whispered. "There should be a seal. A Fairweather seal."

"And your ring opens it?"

"My ring and—" He hesitated. "And me. That's what Quill thinks. The locks my guild made don't care about keys. They care about *who's asking*."

They found the second arch flooded ankle-deep, the third choked with festival rubbish someone had dumped through a grate — broken mask frames, a shredded banner, wine casks. Wren burned a path through the trash with distaste. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, the festival drums began the night-watch rhythm, and the sound came down into the dark like a heartbeat belonging to some enormous buried animal.

Behind the third arch: a door.

Not a door like other doors. Wren had expected timber, iron, something a person had built. This was a single slab of grey stone set flush into the living rock, taller than two men, and it had no handle, no hinges she could find, no keyhole. It had *words*. Lines of them, cut deep into the stone in a script that predated anything Wren had ever seen scratched on a smuggler's chart or a hedge-mage's slate — angular, dense, running in bands like the growth rings of a tree.

Felix went very still in front of it.

"What?" Wren whispered.

"It's the same as the ledger." His voice had gone strange, flattened, like a man reading his own death notice. "The old pages. Grandfather's ledger had samples of the deep script — I thought they were decoration. They're the same words. It's the same *hand*." He raised his hand, and Wren saw it was shaking, and saw him force it steady. "My family wrote this door."

"Then your family can open it."

He pressed his palm flat against the stone, and the signet ring — no. Wait. Wren had the ring. She'd carried it since the quarry. But when Felix touched the door, the ring on its cord against her chest grew suddenly, unmistakably warm, then hot, then almost too hot to bear, and she yanked it free and it was glowing, softly, gold-red, like a coal remembering fire.

"Give it here," Felix said.

She handed it over and the moment his fingers closed around it the glow steadied, and the carved words began to move.

Wren had seen magic do a lot of things. She'd seen Tam write jokes on his slate faster than Wren could read them. She'd seen Quill pull a storm apart like bread. But she had never seen writing *wake up* — the cut lines filling with slow golden light, each character lifting a hair's breadth off the stone, rearranging, flowing downward like water finding its level, until the whole surface of the door was a river of light and the sound coming off it was a low chord, felt in the teeth more than heard.

The door opened inward by the width of a hand. Cold air breathed out.

Cold was the wrong word. Wren had grown up in northern winters; she knew cold. This was *absence*. The air beyond the door had no temperature at all, the way a hole in a page has no words. It moved against her face and her skin simply reported *nothing*, and every instinct she owned, every feral spark in her blood, screamed at once: *away*.

"Felix," she said.

"I know."

"Do you? Because I know fire, and this isn't—"

"*I know.*" He said it quietly, and stepped through anyway, and Wren swore at him in three dialects and followed, because that was the whole job now, wasn't it. Standing beside him.

* * *

The undercroft beyond the door was not a room. It was a throat.

The passage sloped down in wide shallow steps, each one a single slab of that same grey stone, and the walls were covered — floor to unseen ceiling — with binding script, band after band after band, thousands upon thousands of characters, and Wren understood with a lurch that she was looking at years of work. Decades. Generations. Someone had stood down here with chisels and patience and poured their whole life into these walls, and then someone else had come after, and someone after that, each new layer of script laid over the old like bandages on a wound that would not close.

"How far down does it go?" she whispered. Her voice came back wrong, flattened, swallowed.

"The ledger said nine levels," Felix said. His bead of borrowed courage — no, that was her flame, still hovering at his shoulder because he'd asked and she'd allowed it — threw his shadow long ahead of them. "Nine levels, and the last one isn't carved. The last one is *sealed*."

They counted. One level, two. The air got worse — not colder, just less, thinner somehow, as though the passage were drinking it. At the third level Wren noticed the script had changed. The upper bands had been confident, deep-cut, sure-handed. Here the strokes were shallower. Hurried. And here and there whole sections had been gouged out and recarved, patched like a road repaired after a flood.

At the fifth level, the patches started failing.

A section of wall the size of a shield had been recarved and then recarved again, and beneath the newest layer the old stone showed through — and the old stone was *blistered*. Pushed outward from behind, smooth and glassy, like skin stretched over something swelling. As Wren's flame-light slid across it, the blister moved. Not much. A slow ripple, the way a sack of grain shifts when something inside turns over.

"Wren." Felix's voice was barely there. "Don't touch it."

"I wasn't going to touch it. Why would I touch it. Nobody touches that."

But she was staring, and staring was its own kind of touching, because as she looked the blistered stone seemed to look back — a pressure behind her eyes, a vast slow attention turning over like something waking in the bottom of a well. Her flame shrank to a pinprick. She made it grow again through sheer spite.

Sixth level. Seventh. The script here was desperate — overlapping, misspelled, characters crammed into gaps where no character belonged, the handwriting of people working in the dark against a deadline that was winning. And between the bands of writing, in places where the stone had been worn smooth by centuries of hands, there were marks that weren't script at all.

Scratches.

Long parallel gouges, four together, dragged downward. Then lower on the wall, another set. And another. All of them pointing the same direction: *down*. Toward the ninth level. Toward the sealed place.

Wren stopped walking.

"Felix. Look at the scratches."

He looked. She watched him look, watched his face do the arithmetic she'd already finished, and hated every number in it.

"Those are fingermarks," he said.

"They're *claw* marks."

"No." He crouched, brought the light close, and his jaw tightened. "No, look — four fingers, no thumb, and the spacing. It's a hand. A big hand, but a hand." He straightened slowly. "Something in there has been scratching at the door. From the inside. For a long time."

The chord-sound from the entrance had faded miles ago, but now Wren realized she could hear something else underneath the silence. A rhythm. Faint, arrhythmic, patient. Scrape. Scrape-scrape. Scrape. Like a prisoner counting days on a wall, except the prisoner was below them and the wall was between them and it, and the counting had been going on since before the kingdom had a name.

"We should go back," Wren said. She was surprised at how calm her voice came out. Inside she was a bonfire in a high wind. "We get Quill, we get everyone, we come back with the full Emberline and every charm we own and we burn the whole ninth level into glass—"

"And leave the king in there?" Felix said. "Leave Aldric?"

"Aldric's upstairs being a puppet! Whatever's down here is the puppeteer!"

"Is it?" He turned to her, and in the tiny light his face looked hollowed, older, like the walls around them. "Quill said the Regent wears him. Everyone assumes the Regent *made* itself a king. But what if it didn't? What if the thing the first binders buried down here has been leaking for centuries — thin as smoke, slow as water — and all it ever managed to reach was one man on a throne? What if the Regent isn't the monster?" He gestured at the descending dark. "What if the Regent is just its *finger*?"

Scrape. Scrape-scrape.

The sound was closer. Or louder. Wren couldn't tell which was worse.

They went down anyway. Of course they did. Eighth level: the script gave up pretending. Whole walls were bare, the bindings abandoned mid-stroke, chisels' worth of work left unfinished by hands that had run out of time or hope or both. The blisters in the stone were bigger here, some of them burst, and the burst ones weren't empty — they were lined with something pale and fine, like frost, like ash, like the residue of a fire that had never been lit. Wren's flame leaned toward them hungrily and she reined it in hard, because her fire *wanted* that residue, wanted it the way a dog wants meat gone green, and she did not trust anything her fire wanted down here.

The ninth level was a single chamber, and the chamber was mostly door.

The sealing filled the entire far wall — a plug of fused stone and metal and glassy slag, layered like the inside of a geode, and across its face ran the final binding: one line of script, enormous, each character taller than Wren, cut so deep the edges still held a knife-sharpness after all the centuries. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen and the most frightening, because even she could read the intent in it without knowing a single word: *this holds. this holds. this holds.*

Except.

In the center of the great seal, at the height of a man's heart, the fused layers had thinned. Worn through. And through the thin place, the darkness on the other side was not dark the way a cellar is dark. It was dark the way the pupil of an eye is dark, and it was the size of a coin, and as Wren's flame trembled near it, the coin-sized darkness *dilated*.

"Oh," Wren said. Very quietly. "Oh, it knows we're here."

The whisper came through the thin place like wind through a keyhole, and it used a voice Wren recognized, and that was the worst thing yet, because it was Felix's grandfather's voice, the voice from the ledger's marginalia, warm and dry and kind, a voice that had been dead for thirty years:

***Fairweather.***

Beside her, Felix made a sound like a man taking a blade between the ribs. The signet ring flared white-hot in his grip and he didn't drop it, didn't cry out, just stood there with tears steaming off his cheeks and his spine straight as a chisel, and Wren grabbed his arm because if she didn't hold onto something she was going to run, and she had decided — furiously, absolutely — that she was not leaving him alone down here with the thing that wore dead men's voices.

***Little binder.*** The whisper curled through the coin of darkness, savoring. ***You opened the door so beautifully. Your family always did such beautiful work. Come closer. Let me see what ten generations of keeping has bought me.***

"It's lying," Wren said. "Felix. It lies. That's all it is, that's all it *does*—"

***Is it lying?*** The voice shifted, softened, became a woman's — and Wren's stomach dropped through the floor, because she didn't know the voice, but Felix did, she could see it in the way his whole body flinched. ***Ask him what he's always wanted. Ask him what he told the smuggler girl by the river. Ask him what he dreams, every night, about the shop that never failed—***

"Stop it." Felix's voice cracked down the middle. "Stop it, stop it—"

***I am the thing your ancestors chained,*** the whisper said, gentle as a nurse, ***and I have had such a long time to learn patience. Tomorrow they will crown my heir, and the binding will be complete, and none of this will matter. But tonight — tonight you came all this way, little door-holder. Tonight, the least I can do is show you what's really behind me.***

The coin of darkness widened. An inch. Two. Through it, impossibly, Wren saw — not the chamber beyond, but something else, something shown, offered, *staged*: a street she knew, Greyhollow's market lane, sunlit, crowded, alive. And walking toward her out of that impossible sunlight came faces. Tam, slate in hand, laughing silently. Sable, unhurt, smirking. The courier woman, Quill's daughter, alive and waving. A city where the witch-hunters' pyres had never been lit, where charms glittered legally in every window, where—

The vision reached for her, sweet as sugar smoke, and Wren did the only thing she trusted anymore.

She burned it.

Her flame leapt off her palm and struck the thin place dead center, a lance of white fire hotter than anything she'd ever made, and the vision shrieked — not the whisper-voice, the *darkness itself*, a sound like a mountain tearing — and the coin of darkness snapped shut, and the great seal groaned, and somewhere far above them, muffled through nine levels of stone, the festival drums stopped mid-beat.

Silence. Real silence, finally.

Wren stood panting, her hand smoking, her knees water. Felix was staring at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — awe, plain and undisguised, and fear right behind it, and she honestly didn't know which half was aimed at her.

"You burned a god," he said hoarsely.

"I burned a *lie*." Her voice shook. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, bells began to ring — not the festival peal, something shorter, sharper, repeated. Alarm. And under the bells, threading down through the stone like ice water through a crack, came the sound Wren had dreaded since the weir, since the marshes, since the word *traitor* had entered their lives:

Boots. Many boots. Descending.

Felix pressed his scorched hand flat against the great seal, and through gritted teeth he said the words that turned Wren's blood to winter:

"The coronation isn't tomorrow. It's *tonight*. They're moving early — and they know exactly where we are."
