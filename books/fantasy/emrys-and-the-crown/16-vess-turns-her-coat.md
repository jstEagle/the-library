## 16. Vess Turns Her Coat

The orders came at dawn, written in the Regent's own hand—or in whatever hand the Regent wore these days—and Vess read them three times before the words stopped sliding off her.

*By decree of the Crown: the village of Marrow's Bend harbors unlicensed practitioners of forbidden craft. Captain Vess is commanded to proceed with a company of twelve, to seize all persons of interest, and to burn all materials of sorcery. Let the ash be a lesson.*

Marrow's Bend. She knew it. A fishing village on the lower weir road, forty roofs and a crooked bell tower, the kind of place that paid its taxes in smoked eel and never gave anyone trouble. The kind of place that had, according to the intelligence report folded beneath the decree, been quietly mending nets with stitched charms for three generations.

Stitched charms. Knots and thread and whispered words over a fisherman's cracked knuckles. That was what the Crown wanted burned now.

Vess stood in the garrison yard at Ashford Keep and watched the sun come up red through the smoke haze from the capital, and she thought about a winter night eleven years ago when she had been nobody—a corporal's daughter with a fever that was eating her lungs, and her father kneeling in the snow outside a charm-seller's door, begging.

She thought about the woman who had opened that door. Old, bent, missing two fingers on her left hand. She thought about the little brass disc pressed into Vess's palm, warm as a living thing, and the voice like dry leaves: *Hold it and breathe, girl. Breathe until morning.*

She had breathed until morning. She had lived. And she had spent eleven years hunting people exactly like the one who saved her, telling herself the law was the law, telling herself that most charm-mongers were frauds and swindlers anyway, telling herself that the ones who weren't—the real ones—were dangerous, were different, were *other*.

Then she had found a ledger page in a Greyhollow gutter with a Fairweather name on it, and she had started pulling a thread, and the thread had not stopped pulling since.

"Captain?" Sergeant Hale stood at her shoulder, helmet under his arm, waiting. "The men are mounted."

Vess folded the decree along its creases, very carefully, as if it might bite. "How many prisoners are we holding from the Greyhollow sweep?"

Hale blinked. "The charm-keepers? Fourteen, Captain. In the west cells. Why?"

"Because I want them loaded on the supply wagon before we ride."

*

The west cells smelled of straw and old fear. Vess walked the row alone, lantern in hand, and looked through each barred door at the people the Crown had swept up in its nets over the past season. A grandmother who sold weather-knots to farmers. Two brothers who ran a healing stall behind a tannery. A boy of maybe fifteen whose only crime, by the confession extracted from him, was carrying his dead mother's luck-charm without reporting it.

They looked back at her the way people look at the executioner: with a terrible, patient understanding. They had known, all of them, that someone like her would come eventually.

The boy was awake. He always seemed to be awake. He came to the bars when her lantern light touched him and said, in a voice scraped raw, "Are you here to burn us now? Is that why they moved us?"

"Moved you?"

"To the deep cells. Last night. The turnkey said—" He swallowed. "The turnkey said there'd be a fire soon. That they always burn the stock before a big arrest. So the evidence doesn't talk."

Vess felt the floor tilt under her, though the floor was perfectly still. *Burn the stock.* The decree had said *burn all materials of sorcery.* It had not said anything about prisoners. But decrees were written by clerks, and clerks wrote what their masters meant, and the Regent's meaning had been getting clearer every month, hadn't it? Clearer in the way a noose gets clearer.

"Who signed the transfer order?" she asked.

"The new magistrate. The one with the grey eyes."

Grey eyes. Flat as bottle glass. Everyone who came out of the capital lately had that look, or was learning to wear it.

Vess put her lantern down on the flagstones, took the ring of keys from her belt, and stood there for a long moment listening to her own heartbeat. Eleven years of service. A captaincy bought with sweat and obedience and the slow burial of everything her father had taught her about right and wrong. If she turned this key, none of it would survive. There would be no going back to being the woman who followed orders and slept soundly.

There was, she realized, no going back anyway. Not really. She had read the interrogation transcripts. She had watched Aldric's decrees come down the river like black ice, freezing everything they touched. She had burned a bookshop in Greyhollow with a family still hiding in the cellar loft, and told herself for four months that she hadn't known they were there, and then one night in the dark she had admitted that she had known. She had known.

She turned the key.

The lock sounded very loud in the dark, like something breaking—which, she supposed, it was.

*

Getting fourteen prisoners out of Ashford Keep required either a miracle or a captain's signature, and Vess had learned long ago that a captain's signature could fake most miracles.

She wrote requisitions. Prisoners transferred to Marrow's Bend for public sentencing, per decree—witnesses to the lesson, the logic went, made the lesson stick. She signed the wagon manifest herself. She detailed six of her twelve riders to escort duty and sent the other six ahead to secure the village square, which got them out of the way, which was the point.

Sergeant Hale rode beside the wagon. He was the one man in the company she half-trusted, mostly because he was too stubborn to lie well and too decent to enjoy cruelty. Somewhere on the weir road, with the river running brown and fast on their left, he leaned over and said quietly:

"Captain, those aren't witnesses. You know what happens to them at Marrow's Bend."

"I know exactly what happens at Marrow's Bend," Vess said.

"And you're going to let it happen?"

The question hung between their horses like a blade. Vess watched the water. She thought about the Emberline—about the smuggler Sable, whom she'd chased twice and caught once and released both times for reasons she hadn't examined; about Mother Quill, whose name appeared in three separate intelligence files and whose face appeared in none; about the Fairweather apprentice who had slipped through her fingers in Greyhollow like smoke with a heartbeat.

She had been circling them for months. Telling herself it was pursuit.

"No," she said.

Hale stared at her. "Captain—"

"Hale. Look at me." She turned in the saddle and let him see whatever was on her face, because he deserved to see it before he decided whether to follow her into ruin. "I'm not letting it happen. I'm ending my career in about an hour, and possibly my life, and I need to know if you're with me or if you're going to draw steel when I open that wagon."

He was quiet for a hundred yards. The wheels creaked. Somewhere ahead, Marrow's Bend's crooked bell tower rose over the willows, and beyond it, faint on the wind, woodsmoke—and under the woodsmoke, if you knew what to smell for, lamp-oil. Her advance riders had already begun preparing the pyres.

"My sister died of lungfever when we were children," Hale said finally. "We couldn't afford a charmer. Couldn't afford a doctor either. My mother held her all night." He wiped his mouth with the back of his glove. "I've been telling myself for twenty years that the law protects people from swindlers. I don't think I believe it anymore."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes, Captain. Gods help us both."

*

What happened at Marrow's Bend entered the Crown's records as an ambush by outlaw partisans, and that version was even mostly true.

Vess rode into the village square with her escort, dismounted, and ordered the pyre-building stopped. Her advance sergeant—an ambitious man named Corl, the kind who kept copies of his superiors' correspondence against future need—asked to see her authority. She showed him her sword instead, and when he reached for his whistle she broke his wrist, and after that it was simply fast and ugly: six riders against two, in a square full of screaming fishermen, while Hale cut the wagon locks and fourteen prisoners spilled out into the light blinking like creatures raised underground.

One of the prisoners—the grandmother, the weather-knot seller—took one look at the burning oil-soaked pyre timbers and began, incredibly, to laugh. Then to cry. Then she grabbed Vess's gauntlet with both hands and pressed it to her forehead and said something in an old dialect Vess didn't know, and didn't need to know, because some things translate themselves.

"Ride," Vess told the freed prisoners. "South along the weir road, past the drowned quarry. There are people there who'll take you in. Tell them Captain Vess sends her regards and her apology." She pressed her purse into the grandmother's hands—every coin she carried, a year's wages. "Go. Now. Don't stop for anything wearing a crown."

Corl lay groaning by the well with his broken wrist, and Vess knelt beside him long enough to say, "You'll tell them I turned traitor. Good. Make it loud. The louder the better—it'll keep their eyes on me and off the road south."

"You're mad," Corl spat. "You're dead, Vess. You know that? Dead by sundown."

"Sundown's a long way off," she said, "and I've got a lot to do before then."

*

Because that was the thing. Freeing the prisoners was only half the debt. Half the reason.

The other half was a palace.

She had spent eight days inside the capital's walls the previous month, delivering reports, and she had done what a good hunter does: she had watched. She had watched the guard rotations change in ways that made no tactical sense—watched the undercroft approaches stripped of patrols while the throne room tripled its watch, as if whatever the Regent feared lived below rather than above. She had watched the coronation preparations swallow the Glass Court whole: scaffolds, banners, a second crown being fitted in a jeweler's locked workshop, smaller than the first, sized for a younger head. She had copied the rotation schedules onto the inside of her left gauntlet cuff in ink so fine it looked like shadow, because some instinct older than her loyalty had told her to.

Now she rode for the flooded quarry with that gauntlet hidden inside her coat, pursued by the sound of her own name turning to poison in every garrison east of the river.

*Traitor.* They'd have it on posters by week's end. *Vess the Turncoat.* Maybe they'd make a ballad. Maybe the ballad would even be true.

She didn't care. Or rather—she cared enormously, and the caring had finally changed shape. For eleven years she had cared about her name, her rank, her clean record, the careful architecture of a life built on following the rules of a kingdom that had stopped having rules and started having appetites. Now she cared about a debt she owed a dead woman with two missing fingers, and about a boy in a cell who had committed no crime but grief, and about a king she'd seen once from across a parade ground, weeping silently at his own birthday feast while the court applauded, his eyes flat grey glass, his mouth moving to words he did not seem to choose.

Nobody saves a king by obeying the thing that wears him.

*

The quarry received her the way it must have received all the hunted: without questions, at first.

A girl met her at the tree line—young, wild-haired, walking barefoot on frozen mud as if it were summer grass, and Vess's skin prickled with the heat coming off her like off a forge. Behind her, half-hidden in the flooded pit's terraced shadows, lamplight moved among stone galleries where dozens of people lived in defiance of every law Vess had enforced.

"You're the hunter," the girl said. Not a question either. Her eyes were the color of banked coals. "Sable says if you're here to arrest anyone, I get to do the arresting."

"Wren," someone called sharply from below—a woman's voice, worn smooth as river stone. "Let her walk."

Vess walked. Down the switchback paths, past hanging gardens growing out of quarry walls, past a smithy, a schoolroom, children—actual children—chalking sigils on slate. At the bottom, beside a hearth built into the raw rock itself, a broad old woman with ink-stained fingers waited with her arms crossed, flanked by a lean smuggler type with a bandaged shoulder and a young man whose face Vess recognized from a dozen wanted sketches and whose hands were trembling.

Mother Quill. Sable. Felix Fairweather.

"So," Quill said. "The witch-hunter captain walks into my house with a price on her head. Give me one reason not to feed you to the river."

Vess reached into her coat slowly—slowly enough that the smuggler's knife cleared its sheath—and drew out her left gauntlet, and turned the cuff inside out, and held it up to the firelight so the fine grey lines of ink became legible.

"Palace guard rotations," she said. "Current as of nine days ago, updated by courier schedule. Undercroft approach patterns. Coronation timelines. The location of the second crown." Her voice held steady, which surprised her; inside, she was falling. "And the names of every informant the Crown has planted in your fellowship. Yes—I know about the leak. I've arrested two of them personally. The third one signs his reports with a quill-and-crown mark. Ask your son."

The silence that followed had weight, like water. Felix Fairweather stared at her as if she were a puzzle with a missing piece. Sable's knife hadn't lowered, but her eyes had changed—hunter reading hunter, one professional recognizing another's work.

Quill took the gauntlet. Read it. Read it again. When she looked up, her face was unreadable, which Vess understood to be its own kind of mercy.

"There was a charm-seller in the north country," Quill said quietly. "Eleven years ago. Missing two fingers. Saved a soldier's daughter from lungfever and never told a soul, because telling would have cost her her life."

Vess's throat closed. "You knew her."

"She was my sister." Quill set the gauntlet down on the hearthstone, gently, the way one sets down something sacred. "She died in a Crown cell five winters back. They asked her to name the people she'd healed. She wouldn't." The old woman's eyes were wet and absolutely fierce. "So. You're paying a debt to a dead woman, Captain Vess. To my Maren. Fine. Consider it acknowledged." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to iron. "But understand what you're buying into. Tomorrow night we go through the gates you're going to open for us, and we go to pull a king out of a monster's mouth, and some of us won't come back. There's no pardon at the end of this road. There's no going back to being what you were."

Vess looked around the firelit quarry—at the children chalking their letters, at the wounded smuggler, at the trembling apprentice who was apparently the linchpin of the entire mad enterprise, at the old woman who had lost a daughter and a sister to the same machine Vess had served.

"I burned a bookshop in Greyhollow," she heard herself say. "There were people inside. I told myself I didn't know." She swallowed. "I can't undo that. None of it. But I can stand at the gate tomorrow night and hold it against every man I ever called brother, and if that's the only penance I'm ever granted, I'll take it."

Quill studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—not warmly, but truly, the smile of a woman weighing something heavy and finding it honest.

"Penance is just another word for work," she said. "Welcome to the Emberline. Now sit down, Captain, and tell us everything you know about the undercrofts. We've got a king to steal back, and tomorrow comes whether we're ready or not."

Above them, far above, the first stars were coming out over the rim of the quarry—and somewhere beyond them, in the gilded rot of the capital, the Regent was fitting a smaller crown to a younger head, and the clock of the coronation was ticking down toward the hour when all of them, hunter and hunted alike, would have to keep the door together or die apart.
