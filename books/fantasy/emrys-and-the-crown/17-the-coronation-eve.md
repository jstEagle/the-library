## 17. The Coronation Eve

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, and nobody had slept.

Felix knew it the way he'd learned to know everything in the last months—by the small honest magics that had replaced a lifetime of lies. The candle flames in the room burned too steady, fed by Wren's restless heat leaking off her skin. The wards Quill had hung at every door hummed a half-tone flat, the sound they made when the hands that wove them were shaking. And Sable sat too still on the windowsill, watching the street below with her knife drawn and her thumb resting on the flat of the blade, which was what Sable did instead of pacing.

Above them all, through the grime of the window, the capital glowed.

Festival week had turned the city into a lantern. Every balcony in the Glass Quarter dripped with colored glass lamps; barges on the river carried choirs singing the coronation hymns; and over it all, on the palace mount, the spires of the royal residence stood lit white as bone. Tomorrow at dawn, the bells would ring three hundred times, and Aldric's heir—the Regent's heir, though no one dared say so—would be crowned before a crowd of forty thousand cheering subjects who had never once looked their king in the eyes.

*Bind itself into the bloodline,* Tam's slate had read tonight, in Wren's careful transcription of the spy's report. *The rite tomorrow is not a coronation. It is a wedding. The Regent means to marry itself to the royal line forever. After that, there will be no pulling it out. There will be no king to save. There will only be it, wearing them one after another, until the line fails or the world does.*

Felix turned the words over again as he sat at the scarred table, his grandfather's ledger open before him, the wax-sealed map beside it, and the dead courier's signet ring on its chain around his neck. He had read the same page four times. The letters kept sliding out from under him, because his mind kept going somewhere else entirely—to a shop in Greyhollow that smelled of brass polish and dust, where he had spent ten years selling nothing to people who needed something, and telling himself the lie so often it had worn smooth as a river stone: *it's just brass and wishful thinking.*

He was not thinking about the shop. He was thinking about the door beneath the palace.

* * *

Quill called them together an hour past midnight, when the festival noise below had softened to a distant roar like the sea.

She stood by the hearth, and Felix saw for the first time how old she looked. Not weak—never that—but spent, the way a candle looks near the end of a long night, burning down to give light to the last of the work. Her daughter's ring hung on a cord at her throat now, next to Felix's own keepsakes. She had lost one child to the Crown's hunters and another to fear, and tomorrow she meant to spend whatever she had left.

"You've all seen the report," she said. No preamble. Mother Quill wasted nothing, least of all time. "Tomorrow the Regent completes itself. If we do nothing, we lose—not this fight, but every fight after it, forever. So we will not do nothing."

She unrolled a copy of the palace plans across the table, weighted at the corners with charm-stones that glowed faint amber. "Wren fires the granary at the third bell before dawn. Sable takes the undercroft stairs while the guard runs to the flames. Vess holds the gate—she has the rotations, and she has given her word." A pause, weighted. "I will be in the crowd at the coronation square with our sympathizers among the guildsmen. When the king is pulled free, the court must see it happen. They must see their Regent for what it is, or we win nothing but a longer war."

"And me?" Felix asked.

Quill looked at him. Everyone looked at him. He felt the weight of it settle on his shoulders like a coat made of lead and expectation.

"You," Quill said gently, "go down."

* * *

She laid it out plainly, because she was Quill, and mercy was not in her nature when the truth would serve better.

Beneath the palace undercrofts, behind the sealed door older than the kingdom, lay the original binding site—the anchor-point where the first binders had caged the thing that kings had been guarding, and feeding, and finally forgetting for a thousand years. The binding was woven through the crown itself; that was why the Regent could wear Aldric, why it leaked through the seal, why it needed the bloodline. To pull it out of the king, someone had to stand at the door and hold it open—a Fairweather, a binder of the old line, working the craft his family had kept alive without knowing it for ten generations.

"Unbinding is easy," Quill said. "Any hedge-witch can unravel. Holding is the craft. While you work, the door cannot close. Whatever comes through it—and things *will* come through it, Felix, the Regent will fight, and the seal will fight, and the deep thing beneath will wake and want—you must keep the way open until the king is clear and the Regent is fully drawn out. You cannot let go. You cannot step aside. You cannot stop."

"And after?" Sable's voice, from the windowsill. Flat. Dangerous. "After he pulls it out. Then what? He walks away?"

Quill was quiet a moment too long.

That silence told Felix everything, and he found, strangely, that he wasn't surprised. He had known since the undercroft, since he'd pressed his palm to that ancient sealed door and felt the scratching on the other side like a heartbeat made of fingernails. Some knowledge arrives already old.

"The binding wants an anchor," Quill said at last. "A living will, holding the shape of the door. When the working ends—" She stopped. Started again, more quietly. "When the working ends, the door will want to take the hand that held it. It can be done. Binders have done it. But it is not—" her voice caught, just barely, on the word— "*safe.*"

"Not safe," Wren repeated, and laughed, high and sharp, the laugh she made when she was frightened. "You mean he might die. Say it. We're all going to die someday, I set fire to a granary tomorrow, say the word."

"He may die," Quill said. "Yes."

The room went very still. The candles burned. Somewhere below, a festival crowd roared at some small joy, forty thousand people cheering for a crown that was eating their king.

Felix looked at the ledger. His grandfather's handwriting marched down the page in neat brown ink, patient as rain: *the holder holds. This is the whole of the office. This is the whole of the honor.* Ten generations of Fairweathers had written variations on those lines, and every one of them had understood what Felix was only now understanding—that the guild had never been about selling charms. It had been about standing at doors. About being the hinge. About holding the way open so that other people could pass through to safety.

His whole life, he had thought himself the last of a line of frauds.

He was the last of a line of door-holders. And tomorrow there was a door.

* * *

"I'll do it," Felix said.

It came out quiet, and that surprised him most. He had expected to shout it, or weep it, or dress it up in speeches. Instead it came out the way he used to name prices across a counter—calm, certain, final.

"No," said Sable.

She was off the windowsill and across the room before anyone else had drawn breath, and she took his arm hard enough to bruise, turning him to face her. Up close her eyes were bright and furious and wet.

"No," she said again. "Find another way. There's always another way. You taught me that—you, with your useless brass and your wishful thinking, you found ways out of everything your whole miserable life—"

"Sable—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked clean through. "Don't you dare stand there looking peaceful about it. Don't you dare make peace with dying in a hole under a palace while I'm upstairs setting fires for you. We didn't—I didn't—" She stopped. Swallowed. He watched her rebuild herself piece by piece, the way she did, the smuggler's discipline, walls going up course by course. When she spoke again her voice was low and level and worse than the shouting. "Not this time. You said that. You don't get to break it the same week."

Felix covered her hand with his. Her knuckles were scarred from ropes and locks and years of hard living, and they were trembling.

"I'm not breaking it," he said. "Listen to me. All my life I sold people hope in brass casings and told them it was nothing. And the worst part—the part I never said out loud—is that I wanted it to be true. I wanted to matter. I wanted one thing I'd ever touched to actually *work.*" He smiled, and found that it wasn't a bitter smile at all. "Tomorrow I get that. One real thing. One door, held open, so a king can walk out of his own prison and a city can see the thing that's been wearing him. That's not dying, Sable. That's the first day of my life that counts double."

"It counts double if you live through it," she snapped.

"Then I'll live through it," he said, and made it sound like a promise, and hated himself a little for how easily the lie came—ten years of practice, after all. "But either way, I'm the one who stands there. There's no one else. You know it. Quill knows it. The ledger knows it." He lifted his eyes to the room. "I'm a Fairweather. Holding doors is what we're *for.*"

Wren had gone quiet by the hearth, firelight moving on her face. Old Tam sat with his slate in his lap, and slowly, deliberately, he wrote upon it and turned it around:

*My voice went to a hunter's brand so others could walk free. It was worth it. Hold the door, boy. We will be loud enough for both of us.*

Felix laughed—a real laugh, cracked and wet—and had to look away.

Quill came to him last. She took his face in her two old hands, the way she might have held a son's, and studied him with eyes that had buried a daughter and exiled a child and outlived every law that tried to break her.

"When my girl gave me that ring," she said softly, "she told me the Emberline's oldest rule. Do you know it?"

"Keep the hearth lit?"

"That's the second." Quill's thumb brushed his cheekbone. "The first is: *no one holds alone.* Tomorrow, whatever happens behind that door, you will not be alone in it. We will come as far as we can go, and then we will hold the world open for you. That is our part of the binding. Let us do it."

Felix nodded, because speech had gone somewhere beyond him.

Outside, far off across the sleeping-bright city, the first bell of the vigil began to toll. Then the second. The whole capital was counting down to its coronation, bell by bell, and none of them knew—they couldn't know—that the most important ceremony of the morrow would happen underground, in the dark, at a scratched and ancient door, held open by the last apprentice of the Fairweather charm-sellers.

Sable hadn't let go of his arm. He turned back to her, and she was looking at him with an expression he'd only seen once before, on a night when fever nearly took her and she'd fought it off out of pure spite.

"If you die down there," she said, "I'm coming down after you, and I'm bringing fire."

"I know," Felix said. "That's why I plan to hold on."

Above them, the bells rang on, counting down the hours. Below the palace, in the dark beneath the dark, something ancient felt the approach of a Fairweather and began, very softly, to scratch.

* * *

They worked through the small hours, because grief and dread are poor beds and busy hands are better than any prayer.

Felix prepared his charms one by one, waking each with a thread of the craft Quill had taught him, feeling them answer now—truly answer—as they never had in ten years of Greyhollow. The luck-charm warmed in his palm like a small bird. The warding knots tightened at his touch. His grandfather's ledger, laid open on the table, seemed almost to lean toward him, generations of binders leaning through the ink.

Near dawn, Wren appeared at his elbow and thrust something at him: a scrap of red ribbon, singed at one end.

"For luck," she said. "It's real. I checked."

He tied it around his wrist beside the signet ring's chain, and did not tell her that his hands were shaking, and she pretended not to see, and between them passed the kind of understanding that needs no slate and no voice.

Sable sharpened her knives. Vess sent word from the city: *gates will open. God forgive me.* Quill stood at the window watching the sky pale over the palace mount, and her lips moved silently, whether in prayer or in the old binder-tongues, no one asked.

And Felix Fairweather sat down at the table where he'd learned the truth of his name, and wrote a letter he did not send—to a shop in Greyhollow, to a child who'd once bought a luck-charm, to everyone he had ever lied to kindly—and at the bottom of it, instead of a signature, he drew the old guild mark: two hands, and between them, a door.

Then the bells began.

Three hundred strokes, rolling across the capital like a tide, and under their cover the Emberline moved—out of the safehouse, into the festival crowds, toward the fire and the gates and the gilded rot above and the older darkness below.

Felix walked at the center of them, wrist bound in red ribbon, ring at his throat, ledger-heart steady, and thought: *hold the door.*

The first thing he heard, when they reached the mouth of the undercroft stair and the bells were still ringing, was the scratching.

It had stopped waiting.

It was coming up to meet him.
