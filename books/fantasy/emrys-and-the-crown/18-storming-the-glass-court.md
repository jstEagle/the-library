## 18. Storming the Glass Court

**WREN**

The granary smelled of spilled wheat and old rat poison, and Wren loved it immediately, because it was going to burn so beautifully.

She crouched in the loft above the sacks, watching the coronation vigil crowd stream past below—lanterns bobbing like drowned stars, everyone walking toward the Glass Court to gawk at the Regent's heir being paraded through the streets. Nobody looked up. Nobody ever looked up at Wren. That was the whole trick of her, and she'd finally found a use for it.

*When the bells ring nine,* Sable had said, *you give them a fire they can't ignore.*

Wren pressed her palm flat against a sack of grain and let the heat come. It came the way it always did—not summoned but *invited*, like a dog she'd raised from a pup, tail wagging, teeth too sharp. The burlap browned. Smoldered. A thread of smoke curled up sweet as bread baking.

Below her, a guard yawned.

"Sorry," Wren whispered to him, meaning it not at all. "You should have picked a better king to guard."

The sack caught with a soft *whump*, and fire went through the granary like gossip through a small town. She was out the window and onto the tiles before the first shout went up, running the rooftops toward the palace district with the glow climbing behind her and every bell in Greyhollow's capital—no, *the* capital, the city of Aldric and ash—beginning to scream.

Nine o'clock. Right on time.

She could hear them all the way from here: the Emberline moving through the panicked streets, forty hedge-mages dressed as festival drunks and pilgrims and one very convincing fruit-seller, flowing toward the Glass Court while every soldier in the district turned to face a burning mountain of grain.

Wren paused on a chimney, lit by her own fire like a saint in a window painting, and grinned with all her teeth.

"Come on, Felix," she said to the smoke. "Hold the door."

Then she dropped into the dark and ran.

* * *

**SABLE**

The puppeteer-strings of a court are not made of rope. Sable had learned that young, learning which lords could be bought and which only rented: the strings were debts, and secrets, and fear, and tonight she meant to cut every one she could reach.

The chancellery annex sat behind the Glass Court like a hunched servant, and its records office held twenty years of the Regent's leavings—signed orders, sealed bribes, the quiet paper machinery that kept a hollowed king upright. Quill's plan called it *cutting the strings*. Sable called it arson for people who liked ink better than fire.

She went through the window Tam would have picked—he'd scouted it before the weir, gods rot his silent ghost—and dropped into a room that smelled of dust and tallow and something else underneath, something faintly wrong, like meat left too long near ice.

The clerk was still at his desk. Of course he was. Vigil night, and the Regent's clerks never slept; someone had to keep the strings wound.

He looked up. He saw a woman in a festival mask holding a knife, and he did the arithmetic quickly, because clerks always do.

"The strongbox is—" he began.

"I don't want your coin." Sable pulled the mask down. "I want you to watch me work."

She worked fast. Into the fire she fed the levy rolls that had emptied three villages to build the Regent's new guard. The correspondence with Captain Vess's superiors—the ones ordering charm-burnings, the ones Vess had copied for them, bless her traitor heart. The letters signed in a hand no courtier recognized, the hand that had appeared the winter the king's eyes went grey.

The clerk watched, pale as tallow himself. "Do you know what happens to you when they catch you?"

"They'd have to catch me." A page curled and died. "And they're all looking at a granary right now."

"That wasn't us," the clerk said, and something in his voice made her stop. "The fire. We didn't set it. It started in a *sealed* room."

Sable went very still. "What do you mean, sealed?"

"The old archive. Under the court." His eyes were wide now, wider than fear of her could make them. "It's been sealed since the king's grandfather. And tonight the wax seals on the door started weeping. Black wax, running like—like it's *melting from inside*. They sent for the Regent an hour ago. The Regent went down instead of sending someone."

The knife suddenly felt very light and very useless in Sable's hand.

Felix was heading for the undercrofts. Toward a door that was already opening.

"Change of plan," she told the clerk, and knocked him out gently with the pommel, because she was trying to be a better person and it was remarkably inconvenient. Then she ran for the stairs, cutting strings forgotten, running toward the one string that mattered—the boy who held the door.

* * *

**VESS**

Captain Vess stood at the Glass Court gates with thirty of her hunters at her back and her whole life balanced on the edge of a blade she'd spent twenty years sharpening.

The granary fire painted the sky orange. Refugees of the vigil streamed past, and somewhere in that crowd were forty outlaws she'd sworn to hunt, carrying the fate of the kingdom in their pockets, and her orders were written plain on the parchment in her breast pocket: *hold the gates. Let no one pass. Burn any who carry charms.*

Burn any who carry charms.

She thought of a market stall, ten years ago. A fever that would have killed her. A brass charm pressed into her hand by a frightened old man who shouldn't have done it, and did anyway, and vanished into the crowd before she could thank him or arrest him. She'd carried that charm until it wore smooth, and she'd carried the guilt longer.

"Hunters!" she called, and thirty faces turned to her. Some of them she'd trained herself. Some of them had burned villages beside her. All of them trusted her, which was the cruelest part of what came next.

"Incoming crowd from the river quarter," she said. "They'll claim to be festival-goers cut off by the fire. Some of them will be lying. Some of them will be carrying outlawed magic."

A ripple through the ranks. Hands on hilts.

"And your orders, Captain?" asked Sergeant Hale, her second, a good soldier with a wife and two daughters and no idea what he was asking.

Vess looked at the burning sky. At the gates. At the men and women who had been her family, in the way that institutions replace families when you let them.

"My orders," she said, "are to hold these gates against anyone who means the court harm."

She drew her sword—but pointed it, deliberately, visibly, back down the street they'd come from.

"So if anyone comes up that road meaning harm, we fight them together. But I've read tonight's intelligence reports, Sergeant. I wrote half of them. There is no army coming up that road. There are no invaders. There is a granary fire and a lot of frightened people, and I will not spend hunter blood chasing shadows while whatever is under that court tears the kingdom open."

Hale frowned. "Under the—Captain, what's under the court?"

"That," said Vess, "is exactly what I intend to find out. Open the gates."

For one long breath, nothing moved. Thirty hunters looked at their captain, and their captain looked back, and the fire roared behind them all.

It was Hale who moved first. He unhooked the gate bar himself, and set it down carefully, like a man laying down something heavy he'd carried too long.

"Open the gates," he repeated, and the Emberline poured through.

* * *

**FELIX**

Felix had fought exactly twice in his life, both times badly, and neither time had involved actual swords. So it was with some surprise that he discovered fighting your way through a palace, during a crisis, wearing a guard's stolen cloak, was mostly about *walking like you belonged there*.

"Charm detail!" he barked at a pair of actual guards hurrying the other way, holding up the signet ring and doing his best impression of a man with authority. "Regent's orders! Undercroft inspection!"

They didn't even slow down. One saluted. Felix saluted back and nearly wept with relief.

The palace around him was coming apart beautifully. Bells clanging, smoke-stained light through the windows, servants running in knots, and over it all a sound he felt more than heard—a low vibration in the stones, rising from below, like a note held on an organ too large for any cathedral. The deeper he went, the louder it got, and the colder, and the more the torches guttered in a wind that had no source.

The signet ring burned cold against his chest. The dead courier's ring. *The king is not the king,* she'd said, and died, and here he was, ten years of useless charms rattling in his pockets, each one awake now and humming like a struck bell, anchors straining toward something far beneath the floor.

He passed the great doors of the throne room—they stood open, abandoned, the vigil crowd long fled—and he should have kept walking. The binding site was below; Quill's plan was clear; every second mattered.

But the throne room was glowing.

Not lamplight. Not firelight. A grey, glassy radiance, like moonlight through frost, spilling out across the marble, and from inside came a voice. Two voices, actually, layered one atop the other, speaking in perfect unison, and one of them was King Aldric's voice, worn and human and *terrified*, and the other was smooth as poured glass.

Felix stopped in the doorway.

The throne room was empty except for the king. Aldric knelt on the dais steps in his nightclothes, crownless, hands pressed flat to the floor, and the grey light poured *out of him*—out of his mouth, his eyes, the seams of him, pooling around his knees like spilled mercury. Above him, the empty throne seemed almost to lean forward, listening.

"You have served so faithfully," said the two voices, from the king's own throat. "So many years, Aldric. Let me finish the work. Tomorrow the heir is crowned, and the vessel passes on, and you may rest. Or—" the voice softened, honeyed, horrible, "—or you resist again, and I wear you another fifty years. Your body holds up well. I see no reason to hurry."

"No," Aldric whispered. Just that. Just *no*, over and over, a man holding a door with his fingernails.

Every lesson Felix had learned in the flooded quarry rose up in him at once. He didn't think. He reached into his coat, pulled out the first charm his fingers found—a luck-charm, brass and wishful thinking, sold to a frightened child a lifetime ago—and woke it.

Light snapped down his arm. The grey pool recoiled from him like water from a hot iron, and King Aldric lifted his head, and for one moment the glass-grey eyes cleared to a tired, ordinary brown.

"You," the king breathed. "You're a *Fairweather*."

Felix's whole body went cold. "How do you—"

"My grandfather's journals." Aldric laughed—a broken, disbelieving sound. "He wrote about your family. *If the binders fail, pray a Fairweather lives.* Ten years I wondered why the thing inside me flinched whenever a peddler passed the palace gates." His gaze dropped to the charm blazing in Felix's fist, and hope crossed his ruined face like sunrise crossing a battlefield—beautiful, and far too late. "Child. Whatever you came to do. Do it *quickly*. It knows you're here now."

The grey light stopped recoiling.

It gathered.

And the two voices spoke again, no longer from Aldric's mouth alone but from the walls, the floor, the throne itself, everywhere at once, delighted and patient and vast:

"*There* you are. The last binder. I have been waiting for you since before you were born."

The doors slammed shut behind Felix with a sound like a tomb closing.

* * *

**WREN**

Wren hit the palace district at a dead run and found the gates standing open and hunters waving people *through*, which made no sense until she spotted a tall woman in a captain's coat directing traffic like a general, sword drawn, pointing it at her own side of the street.

Vess. The turncoat. Sable's impossible gamble, actually working.

Wren vaulted a fountain, wove through the fleeing crowd, and skidded to a stop in front of the captain, who looked down at this soot-streaked child with firelight in her hair and said, "You're one of theirs."

"I'm the diversion," Wren said. "Where's the undercroft entrance?"

"Below the throne room. Why—"

"Because the thing down there just noticed Felix, and when things notice Felix, Felix does something stupid and brave, and I'm the only one fast enough to be stupid and brave *with* him."

Something flickered across Vess's face—recognition, maybe, of a certain kind of loyalty. She jerked her chin toward the great doors. "Guard rotation's collapsed. Half my hunters are holding the gates with me. You'll have the stairs to yourself."

Wren was already moving. Over her shoulder she called back:

"Hey, Captain! When this is over, somebody owes you a really good charm!"

And she dove through the doors into the grey light, following the hum of forty waking anchors down into the dark, toward the friend who held the door—and the sound that rose up to meet her, from everywhere and nowhere, was laughter.
