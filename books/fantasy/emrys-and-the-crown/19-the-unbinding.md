## 19. The Unbinding

The door was older than the kingdom.

Felix had known that the moment he touched it, though knowing was a strange word for what happened when his palm met the stone. It was more like remembering something he'd never learned. The Fairweather blood in him woke all at once, every charm he'd ever woken humming in sympathy, and the door spoke to him the way his grandfather's ledger used to speak—slowly, patiently, in a language of pressure and patience and *hold*.

Scratches covered it from top to bottom. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Some were old enough to have gone glassy with age; some looked fresh as this morning's bread. All of them were made from the inside.

"Tell me you feel that," Wren whispered behind him.

"I feel it." Felix's voice came out steadier than he deserved. His hands were shaking anyway. "Everyone back. Everyone. If this goes wrong, I don't want any of you close enough to be pulled in."

"No one's leaving," Sable said. She said it the way she said most things—flat, final, like a knife laid on a table. She'd come down into the undercrofts with a crossbow wound barely three weeks healed and an expression that dared him to argue. He didn't. There was no time to argue, and besides, he'd already lost that fight twice on the stairs.

The coronation vigil was burning above them. He could hear it even here, through forty feet of palace stone: bells, choirs, the great organ groaning through some hymn about the crown eternal. In six hours, if Quill's plan failed, the Regent would bind itself permanently into the royal bloodline—not just wearing Aldric anymore, but written into the succession itself, into whatever child came next, and the one after that. A dynasty of gloves. A kingdom worn forever.

Quill's plan required a Fairweather to stand at this door while Wren and Tam's slate-work unraveled the king from above. The binding site and the binding host had to be worked together, or Aldric would tear apart like wet paper. And whoever held the door—

*May not walk away.* That was what Quill had said, in her flat, terrible honesty. *The door takes its toll. It always has.*

Felix pressed both palms flat against the scratched stone and closed his eyes.

* * *

The first thing he felt was hunger.

Not his own. Something vast and patient and coiled beneath the door like a spring compressed for a thousand years, pressing outward against the binding with a force so constant it had worn grooves into the rock. The scratches weren't random. They were fingers. They were *attempts*. Every scratch was a moment where the thing behind the door had found a seam in the weave and pushed, and the binders above—or below, or wherever they'd been—had patched it, and patched it, and patched it.

Until there were no binders left but one.

"Fairweather." The word arrived without sound, without direction, blooming inside his skull like ink dropped in water. It knew his name. Of course it did. It had been listening to his bloodline since before Greyhollow had walls. "Little anchor. Little lock. You've come to turn yourself."

"I've come to finish the job," Felix said aloud, because speaking aloud helped, because his own voice in his own ears was proof he was still himself.

"You misunderstand what the job *was*."

The binding flared under his hands—gold light seeping up between the stones, the old weave showing itself to him at last. And oh, it was beautiful. Whoever the first binders had been, they'd built this the way cathedral builders built cathedrals: layer upon layer upon layer, each generation adding their own knots and anchors, each knot keyed to a name. He found them as he read downward. Emberline founders. Hedge-kings whose names history had burned. And near the top, woven last and woven best, in a hand so like his own it made his chest ache:

*Fairweather.*

Dozens of them. Generations of them. His grandfather's signature was there, still bright, still holding—the last living thread, exactly as Mother Quill had told him. Ten years of selling brass and wishful thinking, and all along the real work had been waiting inside him like a seed in frozen ground.

"The guild did not imprison me," the Regent said, and now it sounded almost gentle, almost sorrowful, a grandfather's voice, a father's voice. "The guild *contained* me. There is a difference, little anchor. I am not a plague. I am a harvest. Everything your kind fears about power—I am simply all of it, gathered in one place instead of scattered and wasted and *burned* by frightened men with brands and pyres."

Felix thought of the charm-burnings in the square. He thought of Tam's ruined throat. He thought of Ashfen, and the children, and the bolt meant for his chest that Sable had taken instead.

"Don't," he said. "Don't pretend you care about us."

"I am the only one who has ever cared about you *correctly*."

And then it stopped talking and started *showing*, and that was worse.

* * *

It showed him a shop.

Not his shop—a grand one, fronting onto the Processional Way in the capital, tall windows full of light, shelves stacked with charms that *worked*. Charms that sang. Customers three deep at the counter, and every one of them leaving with something true.

It showed him the guild restored. Apprentices by the dozen—young faces, eager faces, learning small honest magic openly, legally, taught by masters whose names were carved in marble instead of scratched out of ledgers. It showed him a charter signed by royal decree, magic unbound across the whole kingdom, hedge-witches walking out of hiding by the thousand, no more pyres, no more brands, no more children dying in plague-towns because warming charms were contraband.

It showed him his father.

That was the knife. That was the part designed to go under his ribs and twist. His father, older, greyer, standing in the doorway of that bright impossible shop with pride written across his whole face—the pride Felix had chased for fifteen years and never once caught, the pride that had walked out of Greyhollow with a caravan and never sent so much as a letter. His father put a hand on his shoulder. His father said, *I always knew you'd amount to something, lad. I always knew it was you.*

"This is not a lie," the Regent murmured, and the terrible thing was that it wasn't, quite. "I can make it real. I have worn kings, little anchor. Do you imagine reshaping one city's laws is beyond me? Say yes. Say yes and hold the door open instead of shut, and every dream your sleeping heart has ever rehearsed will be waiting for you tomorrow morning. Your father. Your guild. Your *name*."

Felix stood in the dark with his hands on a door scratched hollow by ten thousand years of wanting out, and he let himself want it.

He let himself want it fully, honestly, the way you're supposed to look at a thing before you refuse it. He wanted his father's pride so badly his teeth ached. He wanted the shop. He wanted the apprentices, the charter, the city where Wren wouldn't have to hide her fire and Tam would never have been branded at all. He wanted it the way a starving man wants bread, and the Regent wrapped around that wanting like warmth around a cold hand, patient, certain, *sure*.

Then he thought about how the offer had been made.

Because that was the tell. That was the flaw running through the whole glittering thing like a hairline crack through glass. The Regent hadn't asked him. It hadn't negotiated, hadn't bargained, hadn't left anything for him to decide. It had simply *shown* him the future it intended to give him, the way it showed Aldric his decrees, the way it showed the court its certainties. Even in the act of offering him everything, it couldn't help wearing him like a glove.

His father's pride, delivered by a parasite, wasn't his father's pride. It was a puppet's mouth moving.

"No," Felix said.

The darkness went very still.

"He's not here," Felix said, and his voice cracked and he let it crack, because it was *his* voice, cracked and scared and stubborn, and that was the whole point. "My father. Whatever face you're wearing right now—it isn't him. He left. He chose to leave, and it broke something in me, and I carried it for fifteen years, and you know what? The people who stayed were better than the dream of him ever was. A smuggler who took a bolt for me. An old woman who grieves her daughter and still gets up every morning and fights. A kid who burns things when she's happy. They're *flawed*, do you understand? They're difficult and stubborn and half of them can't cook, and they chose me. Nobody had to puppet them into it."

He drew a breath.

"And you—" his hands tightened on the door, and the gold weave blazed under his palms, "—you are going back where you belong. Not into a king. Not into a bloodline. Into the smallest, darkest, tightest box anyone has ever built. I'm the last Fairweather. This is my guild's work. And I'm *very* good at making things that hold."

* * *

The unraveling began at the top thread.

His grandfather's knot, bright and waiting, keyed to his own blood—and Felix reached into the weave the way Wren reached into fire, clumsy and fearless, and pulled. The knot resisted for one heartbeat, recognizing him, testing him, and then it opened like a fist unclenching, and the whole binding shuddered.

Above him, far above, through stone and earth and the noise of the vigil, he felt Wren begin her work on the king. He felt Aldric—what was left of Aldric—stir like a man waking from a long fever. He felt the Regent's attention split, torn between the glove it wore and the door it was caged behind, and in that split second of divided hunger, Felix pulled the second thread.

The door screamed.

There was no other word for it. Stone does not scream, but this stone did, a sound that was less heard than endured, vibrating up through his bones and out through his teeth. The scratches writhed. The gold weave flared white-hot. And through the widening seam, the Regent poured toward him—not a shape, not yet, more a pressure, a tide of cold wanting with ten thousand years of patience behind it.

*You cannot hold me,* it said, in a hundred voices, in his father's voice, in his own. *I have worn emperors. I have worn saints. I will wear you, and I will wear your smuggler, and I will wear your little fire-child, and—*

"You won't," Felix said, "because I'm not giving you anywhere to wear."

He had spent the whole journey here turning the problem over, and it was Sable who'd solved it, in the end, without meaning to. Three nights ago, around a fire in the marshes, she'd said: *A crown's just a cage everyone agrees to admire.* And Felix had lain awake staring at the stars and understood.

The Regent needed a vessel. Kings were good vessels—crowned, anointed, already half-open doors. But there was one vessel in the world more perfect than any king. One object crowned and anointed and worshipped for centuries, saturated with oaths and obedience and the weight of a thousand coronations, sitting not thirty feet away in the vault above the undercroft, waiting to be set on a new head at dawn.

The crown itself.

Felix turned the final thread of his grandfather's knot into a loop, and the loop into a snare, and the snare into a door—one door, one opening, shaped exactly like the thing straining against his palms.

"Come on, then," he whispered. "You want in. Here's in."

The Regent hesitated. Even now, even ravenous, it sensed the shape of the trap. It pressed against the seam and probed it and found only one destination offered, one vessel, one crown—and no king attached, no bloodline, no succession, nothing to spread through. Just gold, and weight, and the long dark.

*You would seal me in a symbol,* it said, and for the first time there was something in its voice that might, in a lesser creature, have been fear.

"I'd seal you in a *lesson*," Felix said. "Let them keep the crown. Let them polish it and parade it and put it on a cushion in some museum, and let every king to come walk past it and remember what it costs to wear a thing that wears you back. That's not a cage. That's a *warning*. My family made charms that did nothing for ten years, and you know what I learned? Sometimes the value of a thing isn't what it does. It's what people believe when they look at it."

He pulled the last thread home.

* * *

Afterward, he could never describe those few seconds except as weather. A storm passing through a keyhole. The Regent fought—he would carry the bruises of that fight in his marrow for the rest of his life—but it fought the way a flood fights a dam, all force and no finesse, and Felix had been built for exactly this, generations of Fairweathers distilled down to one terrified young man with his hands full of light. The tide hit the snare. The snare closed. And the thing that had worn kings was drawn out of the seam, out of the dark, up through the stone, through the vault, into the crown waiting on its velvet cushion—and bound.

The silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

Felix slid down the door and sat on the cold floor, shaking, empty, lighter than he had ever been in his life. Somewhere above, faintly, he heard shouting—joyful shouting, Wren's whoop unmistakable even through forty feet of rock—and he started laughing, or crying, or both, he honestly couldn't have said which.

Boots on the stairs. Then Sable was there, dropping to her knees beside him, her hands on his face, checking him over with the brisk roughness she used instead of tenderness. Behind her, Wren, soot-faced and blazing-eyed, dragging a grey-faced man in a nightshirt who wept without any idea why.

"It's done?" Sable demanded. "Felix. Look at me. Is it done?"

"He's out," Felix managed. His voice was a ruin. He held up his hands—empty, scorched, human. "The king's out. And the Regent's..."

He nodded upward, toward the vault, toward the cushion, toward the gleaming circlet that no one would ever safely wear again.

"...in the crown."

Wren stared at him. Then at the ceiling. Then she sat down abruptly on the stairs and started laughing, wild and disbelieving, and after a moment Sable laughed too, low and cracked, and pulled Felix's head against her shoulder and held it there, and Felix let her.

King Aldric knelt on the stones of his own undercroft, a thin, hollowed man in a nightshirt, and looked up at the three of them with eyes that were coming slowly back to life.

"What," the King of Callowmere croaked, "happened to my crown?"

Felix opened his mouth—and stopped. Because from far above, muffled by stone and distance, came a sound that froze the blood in all of them at once: not bells, not choirs, but the deep boom of the palace gates being forced, and the rising roar of a crowd, and someone—many someones—screaming the Emberline's name.

The vigil had noticed them.

"They'll have Vess pinned at the gates," Sable said, already hauling Felix upright by the collar. "Move. All of you move *now*—we're not free of this palace yet."
