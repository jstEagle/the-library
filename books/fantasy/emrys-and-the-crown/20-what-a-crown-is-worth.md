## 20. What a Crown Is Worth

Mother Quill had buried a daughter, exiled a son, and outlived three kings, and she had thought herself past the point where anything could make her hands shake.

She was wrong. They shook now, watching from the gallery of the Hall of Commons as Aldric, King of Vaelin and its broken territories, rose from the throne for the last time.

He looked older than his years—older than he had looked in the portraits that hung in every tavern, before. The Regent had worn him thin, like a glove turned inside out too many times. His hair had gone white at the temples in patches, as though frost had taken him unevenly. But his eyes were his own again. Half of them, anyway. There was a grey film at the back of them still, Quill could see it even from this distance, like smoke trapped behind glass—the residue of ten years' occupation, the part of Aldric that might never come home.

Felix Fairweather stood beside the empty throne, small and soot-streaked and swaying on his feet. He had not slept in two days. None of them had. When they had carried him up from the undercrofts, he had been clutching the crown against his chest with both arms, the way a man carries a wounded animal or a sleeping child, and he would not let anyone else touch it. Not Wren, who had burned half a granary district to buy them their opening. Not Sable, who had a fresh bandage under her shirt and blood loss enough to fell an ox. Not Quill herself.

*It has to be kept somewhere no one can reach it,* he had said, hoarse as a crow. *And I'm the only one who can touch it without it touching back.*

So the crown sat now on a cushion of black velvet on the steps below the throne, wrapped in seven layers of binding-charm, each one stitched by Felix's own hands in the hours after the battle while Sable shouted at him to stop working and sleep. It did not gleam. It did not call to anyone. It sat like a stone, like a dead thing, like a wasp sealed in amber—and yet every eye in the hall kept sliding toward it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Aldric raised his hands for silence, and got it, because whatever else the man was now, he was still the shape of a king, and the hall remembered how to kneel even when it chose not to.

"My lords. My ladies. Guildmasters. Commoners of Vaelin." His voice came out cracked, and he stopped, and started again. "I have held this crown for twenty-two years. For the last ten of them, I do not remember holding it at all."

A murmur ran through the benches. Some of them knew. Most of them only knew rumors: the flat-eyed king, the decrees signed in an unknown hand, the charm-burnings, the witch-hunters, the festival week when the sky over the palace had lit green and the bells had rung themselves. Rumors were safer than truth. Truth required believing that a kingdom could be stolen from inside a man's skull.

"I am told," Aldric went on, "that what wore me is bound now into this crown, and that the binding will hold so long as the crown goes unworn." He looked down at the black-wrapped thing on its cushion, and Quill saw something pass across his face that she recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person measuring how much of themselves is left, and finding the sum uncertain. "I am also told there are those who would test that binding. Who believe a king must have a crown, and that any risk is worth the having."

His gaze found the front bench, where Lord Chancellor Merrivale sat with his hands folded and his face arranged into patience. Merrivale had arrived at the hall that morning with forty retainers and a proposal already drafted: the crown should pass to a regency council pending the king's recovery, chaired, naturally, by Merrivale. He had smiled at Felix Fairweather the way a man smiles at a useful tool.

"I will not wear it again," Aldric said. "And I will not hand it to anyone who wants it. Wanting it is the disqualification."

The murmur became a roar. Quill watched Merrivale's smile hold perfectly still, which told her everything.

* * *

They argued until dark.

Quill had been invited—not summoned, *invited*, which was itself a small revolution—to sit with the commons' delegation, and she spent most of the day listening while tanners and weavers and river-pilots and one extremely loud fishwife debated the future of a kingdom none of them had ever been permitted a voice in. A herald read Aldric's abdication aloud. Then the arguments began, and they did not stop for bread or water or sense.

"An empty throne is an invitation," said a wool-merchant with ink-stained cuffs. "Some duke will fill it within the year. Better we choose now, choose someone clean—"

"Choose *who?*" demanded the fishwife. "Half the nobility signed the Regent's decrees with a smile. You want to crown one of *them*?"

"Then a council. Rule by council, as the boy suggests—"

"The *boy*," said a sour old judge near the front, "is a convicted charm-seller whose guild operated outside the law for a century. Forgive me if I don't take constitutional instruction from a criminal."

Quill felt the words land in her chest like a thrown stone. She was on her feet before she decided to stand.

"The Fairweather guild broke your law," she said, and her voice carried the way it always had, the way that had made forty hedge-mages follow her into a flooded quarry and call it home. "Your law said magic belongs to the crown alone. Your law built the burnings in Festival Square. Your law hunted my daughter through the streets of Greyhollow and put a crossbow bolt through her heart, and I will tell you what your law bought you: ten years of a hollowed king signing whatever was put in front of him, while the men who wrote those decrees grew fat. The guilds kept faith with the people when the crown did not. That is not a crime. That is the only reason any of you are free to stand in this room and argue."

Silence. Then the fishwife began to clap, slowly, deliberately, and the sound spread along the commons' benches like fire catching dry grass.

Merrivale rose to answer her. Quill did not bother to listen. She had heard men like him her whole life—men who mistook the smoothness of their voices for the rightness of their cause—and she knew already how the day would end, because she had seen it coming since the moment Felix had staggered out of the undercrofts with a bound crown in his arms and refused, absolutely refused, to give it to anyone.

No one could wear it. So no one would.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the council was named.

Not elected—there had been no time for elections, and everyone knew it, and everyone accepted it anyway because the alternative was Merrivale's regency, and Merrivale's smile had grown visibly thinner with every hour. Instead, Aldric himself read the list, standing without his crown, leaning on a plain oak staff like a farmer at market.

"A council of nine," he said. "Three drawn from the great guilds. Three from the commons of the capital and the provinces. One seat for the Emberline"—and here the hall went very quiet indeed, because the Emberline had been a hanging offense for sixty years—"one seat for the Crown's own officers, military and civil, who kept faith; and one seat, non-voting, for the Order of Binders, newly chartered, whose charge shall be the keeping of the crown itself."

Nine names. A tanner. The fishwife, whose name was Odell Brack and who received the announcement with a bark of laughter that startled the ushers. A river-pilot from Ashfen, chosen because Ashfen had suffered first and loudest. Master Corliss of the Weavers. An old soldier named Hask who had refused three orders to fire on crowds during the festival week. And for the Emberline—

"Fenwick Quill," Aldric read.

Quill's breath stopped.

She had not given her name to any authority in thirty years. She had buried it the day the first warrant went up with her face sketched beneath it, and she had taught her children to do the same, and her daughter had died under a false name on a courier's papers because the true name was worth more than the bounty on it. To hear it spoken aloud in the Hall of Commons, by a king, as an honor—

She stood. Her knees were not entirely steady. Around her, the commons' delegation rose too, and then, in ones and twos and then all together, the whole hall rose, even some of the lords, even the sour old judge, because whatever else was true, everyone in that room understood they were watching something end and something else begin, and standing was the least they could do.

"I accept," Quill said, "on behalf of every name that never made it onto a list like this one."

Later—much later, in the grey hour before dawn, when the hall had emptied and the candles had drowned in their own wax—she found Felix sitting on the steps beside the bound crown, eating cold bread with the concentration of a man performing surgery.

"You put my name forward," she said.

"Wren put your name forward. Loudly. In front of witnesses." He brushed crumbs from his fingers. "I just agreed with her. Which, if you've ever tried disagreeing with her, you'll understand was the path of least resistance."

"Felix."

He looked up. In the candlelight his face was young and old at once, ten years of Greyhollow carved into it, and underneath the exhaustion something raw and unguarded that he would have hated to know was showing.

"She'd have wanted it," he said quietly. "Your daughter. The courier. She died getting the map to me. Everything after that was just... finishing her errand." He touched the black velvet wrapping, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "This thing killed her. Well—it didn't, but the thing *inside* it did, and the man who let it in, and the whole rotten system that decided some people were fuel and others were fire. So it doesn't get to be a crown anymore. It gets to be a lesson. And someone has to teach it."

Quill sat down beside him on the cold stone steps, which no Mother of the Emberline had ever done for anyone, and found that she did not mind.

"Your grandfather kept a ledger," she said. "Every charm the guild ever sold, every binding it maintained, going back two hundred years. Do you know what the first entry says?"

Felix shook his head.

"*We keep what must be kept, so that others may live freely.*" She smiled, tired and crooked. "Two hundred years, and the guild finally finished the job. You may want to add an epilogue."

* * *

The first act of the new Council of Nine, passed at dawn on its first morning by a vote of eight to nothing (Hask abstained, on principle, having not yet slept), was a single sentence long:

*Let it be recorded that Captain Elara Vess of the Witch-Hunters, having turned her coat at ruinous cost in service of the realm, is pardoned of all offenses, real or imagined, and restored to the rank of Commander of the City Watch.*

Quill seconded the motion herself.

She had reasons, and she gave them plainly to the chamber: Vess had held the palace gates against her own brothers-in-arms and taken a sword-cut doing it. Vess had delivered the guard rotations that made the assault possible. Vess had, by Felix's account, once been saved by an illegal charm as a child and had spent her whole career trying not to remember it, until remembering became unavoidable. These were the public reasons, and they were true.

There was a private reason too, and Quill spoke it only afterward, in the council chamber's anteroom, where Vess stood waiting in a borrowed uniform with her old captain's insignia torn off and her hands clasped behind her back like a woman awaiting sentencing.

"You doubted," Quill said. "For months, you doubted. You could have buried the doubt and climbed higher—you're clever enough, and the Regent rewarded cleverness. Instead you fed it until it ate you alive. That's rarer than loyalty, Commander. Loyalty can be bought. Doubt has to be earned the hard way."

Vess's jaw tightened. "I hunted your people for six years."

"Yes."

"I burned the tannery quarter. I branded Tam so deep he never spoke again. I—" She stopped. Her eyes, which Quill had expected to be hard, were wet and furious about being wet. "Pardon isn't enough. It's not—I don't want absolution. I want work. Give me something to do with what I know."

Quill studied her for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the anteroom's narrow window, the city was waking to its first morning without a king on the throne, and the bells were ringing—not the alarm pattern, not the coronation peal, but something looser and stranger that the bell-ringers had apparently invented overnight, and the sound of it was like weather breaking after a long drought.

"There are four hundred witch-hunters on the rolls," Quill said. "Some will surrender. Some will run. Some will decide the Regent's binding is a rumor and the old order is recoverable, and those are the dangerous ones. I need someone who knows their faces, their methods, and their excuses." She extended her hand. "Commander, I need a hunter who hunts hunters. Are you offering?"

Vess took the hand. Her grip was strong and her eyes were steady at last.

"For the rest of my life," she said, "or however long the pardon lasts."

"It'll last," Quill said. "I'll see to it personally. I'm told I'm a councilwoman now. Apparently I get to be respectable." She allowed herself a dry smile. "My daughter would find that hilarious."

* * *

They called it treason in the fine houses on the hill. Quill heard the word used twice in the council's first week, always in whispers, always by men who had signed the Regent's decrees and now discovered a passionate belief in the sacred continuity of the monarchy. Let them whisper. Treason, properly weighed, meant giving one's allegiance to something other than the crown—and if the alternative to the crown was the commons, the guilds, the flooded quarries and the smugglers' bridges and the feral fire-speakers and the charm-sellers who sold brass and wishful thinking to frightened children, then Quill pleaded guilty and would plead it again.

But the streets called it something else.

She walked the city on the seventh morning, alone, hood down for the first time in thirty years, and saw the word chalked on walls and doorposts and the flanks of the palace itself, in a hundred different hands:

*DAWN.*

Someone had painted it across the base of the empty throne dais in letters a yard high, gold over the old royal blue, and no one had ordered it removed, and no one would.

At the corner of Tanner's Row, a crowd had gathered around a crier reading the council's second act aloud: the repeal of the Charm Statutes, all of them, every clause, with compensation ordered for the families of the burned. A woman in the crowd was weeping. A boy of maybe twelve stood on a barrel, showing off a luck-charm on a leather cord to anyone who'd look—a crude thing, badly cast, brass and wishful thinking—and Quill stopped, and watched, and felt something in her chest that was too large and too complicated to be called joy but lived in joy's neighborhood.

The charm worked. Of course it worked. It was a Fairweather pattern; she could see the anchor-knots from six feet away. The guild's craft, loose in the world again, legal and small and honest, warming someone's pockets on a cold morning.

She was still standing there when the boy noticed her looking and grinned, gap-toothed, and held the charm up for her inspection. "Got it off a peddler," he said. "Cost me a button. D'you think it's real?"

Quill considered the question with the gravity it deserved.

"It's real," she said, "if you keep it."

He frowned, deciding whether this was wisdom or nonsense, and ran off to show someone else, and Quill laughed—actually laughed, out loud, alone in a street in a city she had spent her life hiding from—and turned toward the palace, where the council would be convening, and where the hard part was only beginning.

Because the crown still sat in its vault beneath the Hall, wrapped in seven layers of binding, and it was patient. Bound things always were. And already there were letters arriving—sealed, obsequious, from lords and foreign envoys and at least one self-described prophet—requesting audience regarding "the question of succession," as though the question were a vacancy to be filled rather than a wound to be left open.

And there was another matter, smaller, that she had not yet raised with the council. A report from the Watch, filed by Commander Vess herself, flagged urgent: among the witch-hunters who had fled the city after the gates fell, three had gone north together, toward the old quarry country, carrying with them a stolen relic from the palace armory.

A key. Old iron, older than the kingdom, recovered from the undercrofts during the fighting and logged, incorrectly, as scrap.

The sealed door beneath the palace had been Felix's to open, and he had opened it, and closed it, and bound it. But it was not the only door the first binders had made. Quill had known that her whole life. It was the reason the Emberline kept its hearth in a flooded quarry, of all places, far from anywhere—because the water was not there by accident, and neither was she.

She pulled her hood up after all, out of old habit, and quickened her pace toward the hall.

Dawn had come. Now came the work of making sure it stayed.
