## 21. The Fairweather Sign

The shop smelled of dust and old brass when Felix pushed the door open for the first time in a year.

He stood on the threshold a long moment, one hand still on the latch, letting the smell of it come back to him. Dust and brass, and beneath that the ghost of his grandfather's pipe smoke, sunk so deep into the floorboards that no amount of sweeping would ever get it out. The morning light came through the front window in a grey Greyhollow slant, catching motes of dust that drifted like slow snow. The shelves stood where he'd left them, mostly. A few had been knocked over during the raid—hunters had torn through here too, eventually, looking for him—and their contents lay scattered across the counter like the aftermath of a small, sad battle.

Luck-charms. Wart-wards. A jar of "genuine dragon's breath" that was really dried marigold and wishful thinking. Ten years of nothing, all of it lies, all of it now the most dangerous inventory in the city.

"Smaller than I imagined," Sable said behind him.

"You imagined it at all?"

"I imagine everything." She shouldered past him into the shop, boots ringing on the boards, and turned a slow circle with her hands on her hips, cataloguing exits the way she always did. Back door through the cellar. Window over the alley, wide enough for Wren, not for either of them. Loft above, reachable from the neighbor's roof if you were desperate or stupid. She nodded once, satisfied, as though the building had passed some private inspection. "It'll do."

"It'll do," Felix echoed, and something in his chest went loose and strange.

* * *

They had come back to Greyhollow because there was nowhere else that made sense.

The capital was rebuilding itself around the empty throne—or rather, around the council table where the throne used to sit—and it had no use for a charm-seller who had unmade a king's crown. Aldric lived out his days in a country house by the sea, half-hollowed still, tending bees, weeping sometimes without knowing why. Felix had gone to see him once. They had sat together in silence for an hour, two men who had both been held by the same thing, and parted without quite knowing what to say. He didn't think he'd go again.

Quill stayed south, holding the Emberline together while it decided what to become now that being outlawed was optional. Corvin's exile had left a wound in the fellowship that hadn't closed; mercy always leaves a scar on somebody. But people were coming to them now instead of hiding from them. Hedge-mages who had spent lifetimes pretending to be herb-wives and farriers and midwives were knocking on quarry doors, asking, hesitantly, whether the hearth was still lit.

And Felix—Felix had a shop.

That was the part that kept catching him off guard, usually at night, usually when he woke in the loft bed with Sable's breathing steady beside him and forgot, for three blessed seconds, everything that had happened. Then he'd remember: the binding door sealed behind him. Tam's slate buried under an oak outside Ashfen. The crown locked in a warded vault beneath the council hall, untouchable, unwearable, waiting for no one. And himself, alive, whole, standing in the wreckage of the only life he'd ever understood, expected to build a new one out of it.

His grandfather's ledger lay open on the counter. He'd brought it back from hiding the day they arrived. The pages that had once been ciphered nonsense now read plainly to him—the bindings waking in his blood had unlocked the script, Quill said, the way a key recognizes its own lock. Fairweather, apprentice of the third order, keeper of the small doors. Every charm he'd ever sold, every trinket he'd sworn was brass and wishful thinking, was listed here in his grandfather's careful hand: *anchor, dormant. anchor, dormant. anchor, dormant.*

A guild of sleepers, keeping a thousand small doors closed by doing nothing at all.

Well. Not nothing anymore.

* * *

The first customer came on the fourth day, before Felix had even swept the floors properly.

She was a washerwoman named Marta, broad-shouldered and suspicious, and she stood in the doorway wringing her cap in her hands like she wasn't sure the shop was real. "They say," she began, stopped, started again. "They say the Fairweather boy's back. And they say—" Her eyes darted to the window, to the street, to the faded scorch-mark on the cobbles outside where the hunters had burned confiscated charms a season ago. "They say you're teaching. Real teaching."

Felix set down his broom. "Who says?"

"Everyone." A nervous almost-laugh. "Everyone says."

He looked at her—at the cracked knuckles, the burn scar on her forearm that he recognized because he'd seen a hundred like it, the mark of someone who'd dabbled in hedge-craft and gotten burned for it, literally, sometime in the last ten frightened years. He thought about the ledger. About ten years of lying to children about luck-charms because the truth would have gotten them all killed.

"The lesson's free," he said. "The materials cost what you can pay. If you can't pay, you sweep."

Marta blinked. Then she laughed—a real one this time, startled out of her—and stepped inside like a woman crossing a threshold she'd been circling for years.

She couldn't do much. Almost nobody could, at first; that was the point, that was the whole philosophy Quill had hammered into him until he could recite it in his sleep. *Small magic for small doors. You don't tear down walls, you don't raise storms, you keep the little hinges of the world oiled.* Marta learned to warm water—not boil it, just warm it, enough that her hands stopped aching on cold washdays. It took her six lessons and when it finally worked, when the basin steamed faintly under her palms, she burst into tears and had to sit down on a crate for a quarter hour.

Felix sat beside her and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the warm water hadn't already said better.

By the end of the month there were nine students. By the end of the second month, twenty-three, and he'd moved the luck-charm stock off the shelves entirely to make room for practice basins, chalk, bundles of copper wire, and jars of honest marigold labeled honestly as marigold.

* * *

"We need a sign," Sable announced one evening, dropping onto the counter hard enough to rattle the wire spools. "This is a business now. Businesses have signs."

"This was a business before. It had a sign."

"A sign that said FAIRWEATHER CHARM-SELLER — LUCK, LOVE, FORTUNE." She wrinkled her nose. "Which was a lie stacked on a lie, since you sold neither luck nor love nor fortune, and also it's hanging by one chain and looks like something a gull died on."

"That sign belonged to my grandfather."

"That sign belongs in a fire." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. Sable never argued about anything that didn't matter. "You're not selling lies anymore, Felix. You can't hang the lie over the door and expect people to believe the truth underneath it."

He opened his mouth to argue and found, annoyingly, that he agreed with her. That was the trouble with Sable. She was right roughly ninety percent of the time and insufferable about it roughly one hundred percent of the time.

"Fine," he said. "What do you propose? FAIRWEATHER HONEST MAGIC — NO REFUNDS, NO MIRACLES?"

"See, you joke, but that's genuinely better than what's up there now." She swung her legs, thinking. "Something plain. Something that says anyone can walk in. Not *charm-seller*—that word means smuggler and fraud and worse, depending on which street you ask. Something like—" She snapped her fingers. "*Lessons.* Just that. Put the name underneath, smaller. Let them wonder."

"*Fairweather Lessons,*" Felix tried. "In a city where lessons got people branded?"

"In a city where lessons just helped unmake a crown." She hopped off the counter and kissed him quickly, the way she did when she'd won an argument and wanted to leave before he noticed. "Paint it yourself. I'm not artistic. I'm many things, but I'm not artistic."

* * *

Wren burned the first batch of stock on a Tuesday.

To be fair to Wren—which Felix often found himself doing, in the patient, exhausted tone of a man defending a large destructive child—it was nearly an accident. Nearly. Wren had been practicing flame-shaping on the workbench, coaxing a candle-flame into shapes as Tam had taught him before the end: a bird, a boat, a little running dog. And Felix had left a tray of unfinished charm-housings on the bench, forty small brass casings meant for warming-charms, and Wren had gotten excited about the dog, and the dog had gotten excited about the brass.

The fire was out in ten seconds. The damage took considerably longer to assess.

"I said I was sorry," Wren muttered, arms crossed, hair singed at one temple, radiating the specific grievance of a fifteen-year-old who has been yelled at and feels the yelling was disproportionate.

"You said 'whoops,'" Felix said. "You said 'whoops' in a very small voice and then you tried to hide the melted tray under the rug."

"It was a very convincing whoops."

Felix pinched the bridge of his nose. Behind Wren, Sable was making no effort whatsoever to hide her grin. "Those were two months of wages, Wren. Brass isn't cheap. Nothing's cheap. We're a school run on sweeping and goodwill."

Wren's shoulders dropped. Underneath the bluster there was the other thing, the thing that was actually true: Wren had never had a tray of anything. Wren had grown up in the ash-towns burning trash for warmth until the Emberline found him, and every possession he'd ever owned had either been taken or burned, and now he'd burned someone else's, someone who'd let him stay, and Felix watched the guilt land on him like a coat two sizes too big.

"Hey." Felix picked the ruined tray out from under the rug and turned it over. The brass had fused into a single lumpy mass, pitted and blackened, frankly hideous. He weighed it in his hand. Then, because he was a teacher now and teachers were supposed to turn disasters into lessons, and because some traitor part of him found the thing interesting: "Come here. Look at this."

Wren shuffled over, sulky and curious.

"When metal melts together like that, it remembers. All those separate pieces are one piece now—you can't unpour it. But—" Felix wrapped his hand around the lump and reached, gently, with the sense Quill called *the binder's ear*, listening for the seams. There. Forty faint lines of former separateness, still humming under the surface. He breathed out and pulled, the way Tam had taught him to pull, the way he'd pulled a parasite out of a king, and the lump shivered—and split, neatly, along every old seam, into forty misshapen but intact housings, smoking slightly, each one stamped with a tiny scorch-mark shaped exactly like a running dog.

Silence.

"That," said Sable from the doorway, "is the ugliest inventory I have ever seen."

"They'll sell," Felix said. "Ugly sells. People trust ugly." He handed one to Wren, who turned it over in his fingers, thumb finding the dog-shaped burn, and slowly, unwillingly, started to smile.

"Every one of them's got your dog on it," Felix said. "Congratulations. You're a maker now. Congratulations also on owing me two months of wages, which you will repay in swept floors."

Wren laughed—the bright, startling laugh that had become, without Felix quite noticing, one of the sounds that meant *home*—and clutched the scorched charm to his chest like a medal.

They sold all forty within the week. People asked for the ones with the mark on them specifically. Within a month, customers were requesting *dog-branded* goods, and Wren, insufferable, had begun signing his work with a flourish.

* * *

They painted the sign on a bright cold morning near the end of autumn, all of them, because it turned out that was simply how it was going to be done.

Sable had procured the board—an old door from a demolished warehouse, sanded smooth—and Felix had sketched the letters in chalk while Marta and two of the senior students mixed paint in the yard. Wren was banned from open flame within ten feet of the project, a ruling he appealed loudly and lost. Neighbors drifted over the way neighbors do when something interesting is happening, and then drifted closer, and then someone handed someone else a brush, and that was that.

FAIRWEATHER went on first, in Felix's careful hand, gold on deep green—the old guild colors, pulled from the ledger's flyleaf where his grandfather had pressed a sprig of evergreen sixty years ago. Beneath it, smaller, in Sable's blunt practical lettering: LESSONS IN SMALL MAGIC. ALL WHO KNOCK MAY ENTER.

Then it stopped being any single person's sign.

Marta added a border of little waves, because she was a washerwoman and water was her craft. The cooper's son painted a barrel. A girl from the tannery rows painted a bridge—*the* bridge, everyone knew which bridge—and stood back looking fierce, daring anyone to comment. Old Hew the farrier, who had taken Felix's third-ever lesson and discovered at fifty-eight years of age that he could make iron hold warmth like a stove, painted a horseshoe, upright, for luck, and dared anyone to call it a useless charm.

Wren painted a small running dog in the bottom corner. Nobody objected.

And at the very end, when the paint was tacky and the crowd had thinned to the ones who belonged there, Sable took the smallest brush and added one final thing beneath the name, low, where you'd only see it if you were looking: a thin line of white, like a chalk sigil under a bridge. Like a marker on a dead woman's map.

Felix watched her paint it and didn't say anything, and she knew he'd seen, and that was the whole conversation.

They raised it together—Felix and Wren on the ladder, Sable directing operations from the ground with the authority of a woman who had captained river barges through blockades and considered a shop sign a modest logistical challenge. The chains were new. The brackets were new. Hew had forged them himself and refused payment, saying the horseshoe was payment enough, which made no sense and was therefore perfect.

The sign swung up into the grey-gold light and caught the wind and turned, slowly, flashing green and gold over the street where charms had once been burned in a pile.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Down the lane, life went on—a cart rattling past, a fishwife calling her prices, a child pointing up at the sign and tugging her mother's sleeve. Ordinary noise. The sound of a city that had decided, mostly, to keep going.

"Well," Sable said at last, hands on her hips, squinting up at it critically. "It's crooked."

"It's not crooked."

"It's crooked by a finger-width and everyone will know it was you."

"My grandfather's sign hung crooked for thirty years," Felix said. "It's traditional."

Wren snorted. Marta laughed. Somewhere in the little crowd, someone started applauding, and then everyone was, embarrassed and warm, and Felix stood on the ladder with paint on his hands and his grandfather's name over his head and felt the last cold place inside him finally, quietly thaw.

*Homecoming*, he thought. It wasn't a place. It had never been a place. It was this: paint under your fingernails, a crooked sign, people who stayed.

* * *

They opened the doors the next morning, and the line was already down the street.

Felix stood in the doorway with the key in his hand and looked at them—all of them. Washerwomen and farriers and a widow with a hedge-craft burn she'd hidden for twenty years. Two city guardsmen, off-duty, hats in their hands, glancing nervously up and down the street. A boy of maybe ten, holding a stick of chalk like it might bite him. And at the end of the line, hooded, travel-stained, unmistakably a stranger—someone who had walked a long way to find this door, the way a courier once had, bleeding, on another closing-time in another life.

Felix felt Sable come to stand at his shoulder. Felt Wren press close on his other side, smelling faintly of smoke.

He turned the key, and opened the door, and the bell above it rang—brass, honest, slightly out of tune—for the first student of the rest of his life.

"Come in," Felix Fairweather said. "All who knock may enter. First lesson's free."
