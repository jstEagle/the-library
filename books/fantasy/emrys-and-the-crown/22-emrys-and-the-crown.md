## 22. Emrys and the Crown

The rain came to Greyhollow the way it always did in autumn—not falling so much as settling, a grey wool wrapped around the rooftops, beading on the shingles of the charm-shop that had stood at the crook of Tanner's Lane for three generations. Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and cedar smoke and the particular warm-metal scent of charms waking. Felix Fairweather stood at the counter with his sleeves rolled up, showing a girl of perhaps eleven how to hold a luck-charm without strangling it.

"You're gripping it like it owes you money," he said.

"It might," said the girl. Her name was Pip, and she had arrived at the shop six months ago with singed eyebrows, an unlicensed fire-speaker's gift she couldn't control, and the look of someone who expected to be thrown out of every door in Greyhollow before winter. Felix had given her tea instead. Then a broom. Then lessons.

"Loosen your fingers," he said. "A binding isn't a cage you crush shut. It's a knot you tie because you mean to untie it again someday. You have to leave yourself room."

Pip loosened her fingers. The little brass disc—dormant for ten years in a drawer, woken last spring by Felix's own blood-warm hands—began to turn slowly above her palm, humming a note like a struck wineglass. Her whole face changed. It was the same face Felix saw on every student the first time: wonder arriving like sunrise over a country they'd been told was only ever night.

"That's me doing that," she whispered.

"That's you doing that."

The bell above the door chimed. Sable came in shaking rain from her shoulders, carrying a paper parcel of buns and wearing the expression of a woman who had lost an argument and intended to resume it.

"The sign," she said, without greeting. "The *sign*, Felix."

"What about the sign?"

"'Fairweather Charms & Small Honest Magic.' It's too long. Nobody can read it from across the street. I told you we should have gone with just 'Fairweather.'"

"People know the name."

"People knew the name when it meant brass rubbish sold to frightened children at extortionate prices. Now it means something else. Let the sign say so." She set the buns down, and her voice softened in the way it only ever did sideways, when she wasn't looking at him directly. "Besides. There's another word down there nobody's explained to half our students yet."

Felix followed her glance through the front window, out into the wet lane. Above the door hung the freshly painted board—FAIRWEATHER, in his grandfather's old lettering style, which Wren had reproduced with alarming accuracy and one small flame-scorch that they'd agreed to call character. And beneath it, bolted to the lintel where anyone passing could see if they thought to look down, a second sign. Smaller. Older wood, older carving, darkened by decades of weather though it had hung there less than five years.

*Emrys.*

* * *

It was Pip who asked, two days later, when the rain had cleared and the afternoon light came slanting gold through the shop's dusty windows. She'd been sweeping—badly, cheerfully—and she stopped mid-stroke, broom propped against the counter, and pointed at the lower sign with her chin.

"Why are there two names?"

Felix looked up from the ledger he was keeping—the new ledger, honest columns, real prices, a page in the back where students signed their names when they were ready to practice unsupervised. "Because the shop has two names. Like some people do."

"But what does it *mean?* Emrys. Tam's slate had that word on it once—I saw it. He wrote it under a drawing of a door."

Of course he had. Tam had never spoken a word since the hunter's brand took his voice, but he had written more than most men said in a lifetime, and near the end of his life he had filled three slates with drawings of doors—doors with locks, doors with chains, doors scratched from the inside. And beneath every door, the same word.

Felix set down his pen. Outside, Greyhollow went about its evening: carts rattling toward the market square, the bakery's chimney breathing out its smell of tomorrow's bread, somewhere a child laughing the way children laughed now, loudly, without checking first whether laughter was allowed. Ten years ago this city had been a held breath. Five years ago it had been a battlefield. Now it was just a city, ordinary and loud and alive, and every ordinary day still felt to Felix like a coin found in an old coat.

"Sit down, Pip," he said. "This is a long story."

"I like long stories."

"No, you don't. You fidget."

"I'll try not to."

She sat on the stool behind the counter, the student's stool, the one worn smooth by a decade of learners, and folded her hands with exaggerated patience. Felix looked at her—at the faint scorch marks on her knuckles, at the stubborn set of her jaw—and felt the old ache and the old warmth arrive together, as they always did now, twins.

"A long time ago," he began, "there was a king named Aldric, and there was a thing beneath the capital that wore him like a glove."

* * *

He told it whole, for the first time. Not the version the ballads told—the ones Sable collected with professional interest and corrected with professional contempt, in which Felix Fairweather was seven feet tall and fought the Regent with a sword of morning light. Not the version the council told in its careful histories, all dates and decrees. He told it the way it had actually happened: a frightened man selling lies in a dying shop; a bleeding stranger dying on his floor with a map in her hands; a smuggler with a sharp tongue who should have robbed him and didn't; a mother in a flooded quarry who opened her arms to a stranger because grief shared is the only kind that lightens.

He told her about Mother Quill, who had buried a daughter and then a son—one to hunters, one to mercy—and who had chosen mercy anyway, knowing it would fracture everything she'd built, because she believed a fellowship that ruled by fear was just a crown with a different shape. Quill lived now in the marshes still, old and unbowed, teaching hedge-magic to anyone who rowed out to her. She sent letters twice a year. They were always three lines long and always made Felix cry.

He told her about Wren, who had burned her first granary at fourteen and her first wedding cake at twenty-two—an accident, mostly—and who now ran the Emberline's northern hearth and terrified tax collectors and adopted strays the way other people collected buttons. Wren had come to the reopening of the shop with an armful of stock to sell and had burned the entire first batch within the hour, and Felix had laughed until his ribs hurt, because some things never change and shouldn't.

He told her about Captain Vess, who had spent years hunting people like them and had turned her coat at ruinous cost, and whose pardon—the new council's very first act—had not been enough for her, so she had asked for a job instead. She trained the city watch now. Her recruits learned arrest procedure and also how to tell a warming charm from a weapon, and any recruit who sneered at either got extra drills.

He told her about Tam, who had held the crossing at the weir so the others could live, and whose last written word—Wren read it aloud at the funeral, her voice breaking on the second syllable—had been *enough*. He told her about the courier whose name they never learned, and how they'd carved it anyway onto the memorial stone by the quarry: *SHE KNEW THE KING WAS NOT THE KING.* He told her about Corvin, and about Vess holding his daughter hostage, and about how mercy and betrayal can wear the same coat, and how you forgive the person even when you cannot forgive the act—or sometimes the other way around, or sometimes neither, and you carry it, because carrying things is what people do.

"And the Regent?" Pip asked, when he paused for breath. "The thing in the crown. What happened to it?"

Felix was quiet a moment. Even now, five years on, he could feel it if he let himself: the cold of the undercroft stair, the sealed door older than the kingdom, the scratches on its inner face. He could feel the moment the ancient binding unraveled under his hands like a knot finally understood, and the thing pouring out of Aldric like smoke from a snuffed candle—and then turning, and offering. A full guild. His father's pride, resurrected. A city where magic rang lawful from every tower. Everything he had wanted since he was a boy kneeling beside his grandfather's workbench, hungry for a word of approval that never came.

"It offered me everything," he said. "And I looked at what it was offering, and I thought: none of those people would choose me. They'd be mine because the thing made them mine. Bought, not given." He tapped the counter, where the new ledger lay open. "Then I thought about a smuggler who stayed when staying cost her. A fire-speaker who called me family before I'd earned it. An old woman who gave a stranger her dead daughter's chair. That's the difference, Pip. That's the whole difference. What love buys isn't love."

"So you bound it into the crown."

"So I bound it into the crown. The oldest binding my guild ever kept, redone by the last apprentice, using—" he smiled— "charms that didn't do anything. Every trinket I ever sold in this shop was an anchor. Dormant thread in a spell older than the kingdom. My grandfather's ledger wasn't an inventory. It was instructions. He died thinking he'd failed, selling nothing to no one, keeping a secret so well he kept it from himself. I think about that a lot."

Pip was leaning forward now, elbows on the counter, all pretense of patience abandoned. "And the king? King Aldric?"

"He lived. Half-hollowed, like a house after a fire—still standing, but you can see sky through parts of the roof. He couldn't rule. Maybe he never really had, these last years; maybe none of us had been ruled by anything but the thing's hunger. So he abdicated. Stood up in the Glass Court with his flat grey eyes going slowly human again, and he said—" Felix closed his own eyes to get it right— "he said, 'I wore a crown that was never mine alone. Let no one wear it alone again.' And he named a council. Guilds and commons. Farmers and hedge-mages and a reformed witch-hunter captain. Some people called it treason. Most people called it dawn."

"And the crown itself? Where is it now?"

"In a vault beneath the palace, behind a door that only opens for a binder of the old line. Which currently means me, and eventually—" he nodded at her— "means whoever I teach well enough. It'll sit there forever, sealed, singing its one cold note to itself. No one will wear it. No one *can*." He paused. "That's why the second sign exists."

Pip glanced at the window, at the dark old word beneath the bright new one. "*Emrys,*" she said carefully. "Tam drew it under his doors."

"Emrys is a binder-word. Older than the kingdom, older than the guild. It doesn't translate clean, but the closest meaning is *the one who holds the door*. Not the one who owns it. Not the one who guards it with a sword. The one who holds it—keeps it, tends it, stays." Felix ran his thumb along the edge of the ledger. "When we put the shop back together, Sable repainted the Fairweather sign, and Wren carved Emrys underneath without asking anyone. When I asked her why, she said—" and here Felix laughed, softly, the way you laugh at the dead when the missing has become warm— "she said, 'Because you held the door, idiot. Someone ought to write it down.'"

* * *

Evening settled. Pip lit the lamps with a snap of her fingers—a controlled snap, a *practiced* snap, and Felix noted with quiet pride that not one curtain in the shop so much as browned. Sable came back down from the loft, where she'd been arguing by letter with a ballad-maker in the capital who insisted the Regent had been a dragon. Wren's latest letter lay open on the table, smelling faintly of smoke: *Northern hearth thriving. Adopted three more strays. One bites. Come visit or I'm naming a goat after you.*

Felix stood at the window a while, looking out at Tanner's Lane, at the city that had tried to forget him and had learned, instead, to knock on his door. Students came every week now. Some had gifts; most just had hunger—for wonder, for belonging, for the radical idea that small magic honestly done was worth more than grand magic hoarded. His grandfather had sold nothing and called it kindness. Felix taught everything and called it the same thing.

"Pip," he said. "Why did you ask about the sign?"

She shrugged, suddenly shy. "Dunno. Felt like it mattered. Like it was a question somebody was supposed to ask eventually, and nobody had."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached beneath the counter and brought out something wrapped in oilcloth: a fresh piece of oak, sanded smooth, blank. Beside it, a carving knife with a worn handle.

"Then you'd better learn to carve," he said. "Because signs wear out. Names fade. Somebody has to cut them deep enough to last." He set both on the counter between them. "Emrys means the one who holds the door. One day—years from yet, but one day—that'll be you. Or whoever you teach. The door doesn't care whose hands hold it. Only that they're steady, and that they chose to stay."

Pip picked up the knife. Her hands, so often restless, were perfectly still.

"And the crown?" she asked. "The story. Does it end there? With the vault and the council and the sign?"

Felix smiled and looked out at the lamplit lane, at Sable arguing cheerfully with the evening, at the city breathing easy around them, at the two names over his door—one inherited, one earned, both true.

"No," he said. "It ends where it started. In a shop on Tanner's Lane, with a boy selling nothing and hoping for everything, learning the only lesson that was ever worth the price of the ledger: crowns get hollowed, kings get replaced, empires rot from the top down. But some things are kept not by kings at all. They're kept by the people who choose each other—over and over, in the rain, in the dark, at the door." He turned back to his student, and handed her the knife properly, grip first, the way trust is handed on. "Now. First lesson in carving. We start with the E."
