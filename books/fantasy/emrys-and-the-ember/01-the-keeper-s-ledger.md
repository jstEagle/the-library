## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The Ember Gate has been my responsibility for twenty years, and in all that time it has never once thanked me.

I want to be clear about that at the outset, because the ballads—if anyone ever wrote ballads about keepers, which they don't—would have you believe there is some warm communion between warden and ward. There is not. The Gate is a slab of black stone set into the mountain's shoulder like a splinter, tall as three men and wide as a barn door, carved with script older than the kingdom that outlawed reading it. It does not glow. It does not hum. For a hundred years it has done precisely nothing, and keeping it has mostly meant climbing a mountain four times a year to confirm that nothing continues to happen.

This was the centennial check. The big one. The one where I was supposed to measure everything twice, recite the old formulas, and enter a full report in the ledger—the ledger being a fat, water-stained book my grandmother started and my mother continued and I have kept going out of what I tell myself is duty and what is probably just habit wearing duty's coat.

So: the fourth of Thawmonth, cold enough that my breath froze in my beard before it finished leaving, and me standing in front of a door that leads nowhere, doing sums with a piece of charcoal.

That's when I found the crack.

* * *

Let me back up. A proper inspection goes like this. You approach from the south path, because the north path hasn't existed since a rockslide took it before my grandmother's time, and you stop at the boundary stone to say the words of approach—I won't write them here; they're in the ledger and the ledger stays home—and you wait for the feeling of being permitted. That feeling is subtle. It's less a voice than a sense of the mountain deciding not to mind you. Twenty years of practice and I still can't describe it better than that.

Then you walk the perimeter. You check the anchor-stones, twelve of them, sunk in a ring around the Gate at intervals my grandmother measured in paces because she didn't trust anyone else's measurements, including her own, which tells you something about the women who raised me. Each anchor gets a chalk mark, a tap with the knuckle, and a listen. A healthy anchor sounds like tapping a hearthstone. A failing one sounds hollow.

All twelve anchors sounded like hearthstones. Good start.

Then the Gate itself. You check the frame, the lintel, the threshold-slab. You check the script—not read it, never read it aloud, but look at it, every glyph, confirming none has faded or shifted. My grandmother's notes say the script is the lock. Not a description of the lock. The lock itself. Words that were spoken so hard they became stone, which is either beautiful or terrifying depending on how much tea you've had.

And then—and this is the part I've always liked best—you put your palm flat against the Gate and hold it there for one hundred heartbeats, counting.

It should be cold. Mountain-cold, deep-cold, the kind of cold that makes your palm ache and your rings feel like they're shrinking. Every inspection of my tenure, every one, the Gate has been exactly that cold, and I have stood there with my hand on it counting heartbeats like a fool talking to a wall, and gone home.

On the fourth of Thawmonth, the centennial check, the Ember Gate was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a stone step is warm after someone has just stood on it. Warm the way a bed is warm when you're certain you left it empty. I held my hand there through all hundred heartbeats and told myself it was the sun, except it was overcast, except the warmth was coming from inside the stone, except I have spent two decades learning this rock the way you learn a face, and I knew—knew the way you know a lie in a friend's mouth—that it had never been warm before.

I pulled my hand back. The place where I'd touched it stayed faintly pale against the black, like a bruise in reverse, and then faded.

"Well," I said, to no one, because there was no one. "That's not good."

* * *

I did the rest of the inspection properly, because I am my grandmother's granddaughter and we do things properly even when the world is ending, especially then. And it was on the second pass over the Gate's face, checking the script glyph by glyph, that I found the cracks.

Hairline. Three of them, branching off the same glyph near the Gate's center—a character I can't pronounce and have privately named the Knot, because that's what it looks like, a rope tied by someone in a hurry. They were thin as spider silk. If I hadn't been an inch away with the low sun—what there was of it—catching the surface sideways, I'd have missed them entirely. I measured them against a straw from my pack. Two fingers long, maybe less. I marked their ends in chalk, dated the mark, and sat down on the threshold-slab to think.

Here is what I knew, sitting on a door between worlds with my boots dangling over the seam of everything:

One. The Gate was sealed a hundred years ago by seven keepers, of whom my grandmother was the last living, until she wasn't. The sealing cost them—"cost" is doing a lot of work in that sentence; the ledger is vague in a way that reads less like discretion and more like grief—and since then the Gate had held. Perfectly. Boringly. Beautifully nothing.

Two. Hairline cracks in a lock are not damage. They are a message. Something on the other side of a door does not knock politely unless it wants you to know it's there.

Three. I could tell no one, because "no one" was the entire list of people who would believe me, and also the entire list of people who knew the Gate existed. Being Keeper of the Ember Gate means being the only member of your own congregation. It means writing reports nobody will read, in a code only the dead can parse, about a door nobody remembers, guarding against gods everybody agrees are stories.

I'd made peace with that years ago. Mostly. On the good days I called it devotion. On the bad days I called it what it was, which is that I had inherited a vigil the way other people inherit debts, and I paid it alone, and the loneliness of it had worn grooves in me as deep as any the weather had cut in the mountain.

But sitting there, looking at those three silk-thin lines in a century of perfect stone, I felt something new underneath the loneliness. It took me a while to name it, because I'm not in the practice of naming things that frighten me.

Dread. That was the word. Dread, small and bright, like a coal dropped in snow.

* * *

I camped that night in the keeper's hut below the ridge rather than walk down in the dark, which I told myself was prudence and knew was cowardice. I built a fire, ate smoked fish and hard bread, and got out the ledger.

You should understand the ledger. It's not a diary. Grandmother Yarrow—everyone called her that, including me, including, I suspect, my mother—kept it as a working document: dates, measurements, formulas, observations, all in her cramped hand that slanted uphill like it was trying to escape the page. Interspersed are her marginalia, which are another thing entirely. Things like *the third anchor sings flat in wet weather — note for whoever follows.* And once, only once, pressed so hard the charcoal nearly tore the page: *If it ever warms, do not wait. Do not be brave. Be fast.*

I'd read that line a hundred times over twenty years and filed it under old-woman superstition, right next to her instructions for warding off the evil eye with iron nails and her firm belief that crows gossiped about her. Grandmother Yarrow was the most sensible person I ever met and also believed six impossible things before breakfast, and I loved her for both, and I trusted the sensible half exclusively.

Now I sat in the firelight with the ledger open on my knees and read the line again, and the sensible half of her looked back at me across a hundred years and said, well?

I turned pages. Back, back, back, past my mother's neater entries, past the founding of my own tenure—*Year One. All quiet. Anchors sound. Gate cold. Formula spoken without incident,* which is the most accurate summary of my life anyone has ever written—to the early pages, to her hand young and fierce, to the entries from the sealing itself.

Those pages are different. The handwriting shakes. The formulas are written out complete, every word, the only place in the ledger where they appear whole, and between them are gaps—lines scratched out so thoroughly the paper thinned. Whatever happened up there a hundred years ago, she wrote it down and then couldn't bear to have written it.

But at the bottom of the final page of the sealing, beneath a date and a formula and a list of seven names, four of which have lines drawn through them, there is one unscratched sentence:

*The covenant holds so long as it is kept. Gates are only where keepers stand.*

I'd always taken that as poetry. Grandmother Yarrow was not a poetic woman, so I'd assumed it was a metaphor for vigilance: the real seal isn't the stone, it's the attention. Fine. Sensible. I'd built twenty years on it.

Reading it now, with my palm still remembering warmth, it sounded less like poetry and more like instructions.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless. I climbed back up to the Gate, because I wanted to see the cracks in daylight and because part of me—the part I like least—hoped I'd imagined the whole thing. Hope is a liar. The cracks were there, exactly where I'd chalked them, and when I laid my hand against the stone it was warm again, warmer than yesterday if anything, and this time, in the silence, with the wind holding its breath, I heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. More the memory of a sound. Like standing in a room where someone has just stopped speaking, and you can feel the shape of the words in the air. Something on the other side of the Gate was very close to the stone, the way a listener presses close to a door, and it was not speaking, and its not-speaking was louder than speech.

I counted my hundred heartbeats. My heart was going too fast for the count to mean anything, but I did it anyway, because ritual is what you hold onto when the ground moves.

Then I packed up my chalk and my straw and my charcoal, said the words of departure—which the mountain received with what I swear was relief—and walked down off the mountain carrying the worst news in Thornfell, addressed to no one.

* * *

Home is a cottage above Lowmarsh with a crooked chimney and a garden that produces more weeds than anything else, though the weeds are vigorous and I respect that. I banked the fire, hung my wet cloak, fed the cat, who regards me as staff, and sat down at the table with the ledger, the lamp, and a pot of tea strong enough to strip varnish.

The report took me two hours, most of which was spent deciding how honest to be.

Here is the problem with honesty, when you're the only one who knows the subject. Write *the Gate is warm and cracked and something is listening through it* and you have either founded a religion or earned yourself a cell, depending on who finds the book. Write *minor thermal anomaly noted, monitoring* and you have lied in the one document that must never contain a lie, because the ledger is the whole of the record, and records are how the dead speak to the living, and I refuse to haunt my successor with comfortable falsehoods.

I split the difference, which is what cowards call it when they're being careful. I recorded the date, the full inspection, the anchors sound, the script intact. Then I drew the cracks in the margin, exact in position and length, and beneath them I wrote: *Three hairline fractures radiating from the central glyph. Surface temperature elevated beyond any prior measurement. Cause unknown. Repeat inspection in one moon, not four.*

And then, because Grandmother Yarrow deserved it and because my hand shook until I let it, I added the last line:

*It is warm. She said if it ever warms, be fast. I am noting it here so that whatever happens, someone will know I saw it, and I did not look away.*

I blotted the page. I closed the ledger. I put it in its box under the floorboard, under the loose stone, under the layer of ordinary life, and I sat there in the lamplight listening to my house tick and settle, telling no one, because there was no one to tell.

That was the plan, anyway. That was the plan for about nine days, during which I checked the Gate twice more and watched the longest crack grow by a finger's width, and the warmth spread from the center toward the frame like blood through cloth, and the not-sound behind the stone got close enough that I started sleeping with the lamp lit.

On the ninth day I came down from the mountain and Lowmarsh was in an uproar, and I pushed through the crowd outside the Tanner's row to find out why, and that is how I learned that the miller's daughter—drowned two days in the river, dead as winter, laid out on her mother's kitchen table—had sat up and breathed.

Her mother was kneeling in the mud of the yard, saying a name over and over. A god's name. One of the old ones, the banished ones, the ones my grandmother helped lock behind a door of spoken stone.

I knew the prayer she was saying. Word for word, cadence for cadence, from a banned book I kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of my grandmother's chest.

The Gate was cracking, and the prayers were walking ahead of it into the world, and everyone in that crowd was looking at the child with wonder on their faces while I stood among them with dread sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal, knowing exactly which door the miracle had come through—and knowing, too, that I was the only soul in Thornfell who could say so, and that saying so would change nothing, save everything, and cost me the only life I had.

I told no one. Yet.
