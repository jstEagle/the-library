## 2. Prayers Answered

The trouble with keeping a secret for twenty years is that you get good at it, and being good at it means nobody ever thinks to ask you anything. I could have stood in the middle of Lowmarsh square and announced that the Ember Gate was cracking like old glaze on a teapot, and the villagers would have nodded politely and asked if I'd be staying for the harvest supper.

So when Tam Broadhurst came pounding up my path on the third morning after my centennial inspection, breathless and hatless and shouting my name in a register usually reserved for barn fires, my first thought was not *the gate*. My first thought was *someone finally noticed me*, followed immediately by *oh no*.

"Noor! Noor Lockridge!" He caught himself on my fence post, which groaned in protest. "It's Wren. It's little Wren Ashby. She went into the mere."

I was already moving before he finished. The Lockridge house sits at the top of the marsh road for a reason—my grandmother chose it so she could see everything coming, weather and wolves and worse—and I had run down it enough times in my life to know every rut by heart. Tam puffed along behind me, telling the story in pieces: Wren had gone out on the ice at dawn, because children are idiots with no respect for the season, and the ice had given, and her brother had screamed until half the village heard.

"They pulled her out," Tam gasped. "Old Marta did the—the pressing, the breathing, all of it, near an hour, Noor. Near an hour. There's nothing left in her. Her mother's carrying her up to the shrine now but there's nothing—" His voice cracked. "They said come quick. They said fetch anyone who knows anything."

What I knew was that drowned children stay dead past four minutes, and that an hour is not a rescue but a vigil, and that vigils end one way. But I ran anyway, because that's what you do, and because some small traitorous part of me—the part that had spent two days staring at hairline cracks in a door between worlds—whispered that lately, the rules had been getting loose.

* * *

The shrine district of Lowmarsh is three buildings pretending to be more: the old chapel of the Nine, stripped bare a century ago; a granary converted to meeting-house use; and the Widow Sarel's cottage, which served as everything else. A crowd had gathered outside it, the particular crowd of people who want desperately to help and have nothing to offer—heads bowed, hands wringing, someone's dog whining at the smell of grief.

Inside, the air was thick with woodsmoke and wet wool. Wren Ashby lay on the trestle table, gray as February, her lips the color of slate. Her mother, Denna Ashby, knelt beside her with the child's cold hand pressed to her own forehead, and around them stood Old Marta and the miller's wife and two others, all of them wearing the expression of people who have run out of things to do and cannot bear to stop doing them.

Denna was praying.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters later. She wasn't saying the sanctioned words—the Crown-approved petitions to the Concord, which are less prayers than polite letters to an empty address. She was speaking low and rhythmic, in a cadence I knew, in a *shape* I knew, and the words themselves were mostly lost under her breath, but the structure rose off her like heat off a stone.

Call by name. Offer breath for breath. Ask the threshold to open.

I stopped in the doorway like a man walking into glass.

Because I knew that rite. Not from rumor, not from the pulpit-histories they teach the magistrate's clerks. I knew it from a book wrapped in oilcloth and buried under the floorboards of my grandmother's house, a book I had read exactly once, at fourteen, by candlelight, with my heart hammering like I was committing a crime—which, technically, I was. *The Ember Hours*, hand-copied, pre-Banishment, full of rites for gods whose names were struck from every ledger in Thornfell. My grandmother had kept it the way she kept everything about the gate: hidden, maintained, unexplained. When I'd asked her about it she'd said only, "You'll know when you need to," which was her answer to nearly everything, and which I had resented for twenty years.

Denna Ashby was reciting the Rite of the Open Door. The third petition in the Ember Hours. Outlawed for a hundred years. Punishable by the loss of tongue, historically, though Vane's predecessors had settled for fines and fire.

And Wren Ashby coughed.

Not a death-rattle. A cough—a real, ugly, living cough, the kind that brings up marsh water and mucus and complaint. The room made a sound I'll never forget, a collective inhale like a wave drawing back, and then Denna was screaming her daughter's name and the child was turning her head and vomiting black mere-water onto the trestle table while Old Marta wept into her apron and the miller's wife started laughing in a way that was halfway to hysteria.

I should have been rejoicing. Part of me was—I'm not made of stone, whatever else I'm made of. A child was breathing who had been dead. That is a miracle in the plain sense of the word, the sense that makes your knees unreliable.

But another part of me was watching Denna's mouth move through the fourth petition, the fifth, the closing words that bind the prayer shut so nothing follows the petitioner home, and thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*She doesn't know the closing words.*

She was leaving the door open.

* * *

They let me examine Wren, because I'm the closest thing Lowmarsh has to someone who knows things, a reputation built entirely on knowing how to boil water properly and reading more than everyone else combined. Her pulse was slow but steady. Her color was wrong—not blue-dead anymore, but not right either, a faint ash-gray undertone beneath the returning pink, like embers seen through a closed stove door.

"Her eyes," Marta murmured, when the girl slept at last in her mother's arms. "Did you see her eyes?"

I had. For one moment, before sleep took her, Wren had looked at me with eyes the color of banked coals—amber shot through with red, the color of the light that leaked through the Ember Gate's cracks when the wind blew from Cindralward. Then she'd blinked, and they were brown again, ordinary brown, a child's eyes, and I told myself I'd imagined it.

Telling myself things is a specialty of mine. Twenty years of practice.

"She's warm," Denna whispered. She hadn't let go of the child since the coughing. Her hands shook. "Noor, she's *warm*. An hour in the water. Tell me how she's warm."

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said aloud in months.

The crowd outside had grown. Word travels fast in a village, faster than shame, faster than sense. By the time I stepped out of the cottage, there must have been sixty people in the lane, and the look on their faces was one I recognized from history books and hated seeing on my neighbors: hope. Raw, unguarded, dangerous hope. The kind that spreads.

"Was it the Nine?" someone called out. "Did the Nine answer?"

"The Nine are gone," said the smith, but gently, the way you correct a child.

"Then who?"

Nobody answered. Everybody looked at everybody. And in that silence I watched something take root that would cost this village dearly: the understanding that somebody, or something, was listening again.

I slipped away down the side lane, because standing in the middle of that crowd felt like standing on the ice Wren had fallen through, and I needed to think, and thinking requires walls.

* * *

Here is what I did that night, and I record it without excuse: I dug up my grandmother's floorboards for the second time in my life.

The Ember Hours sat where they had always sat, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, smelling of earth and camphor. I unwrapped it at my kitchen table with shaking hands and turned to the third petition, and there it was, word for word, cadence for cadence: the Rite of the Open Door. Breath for breath. Name for name. *Ask the threshold to open.*

In the margin, in my grandmother's cramped hand, someone—she herself, presumably—had written a single line beside the rite:

*Never leave the door unclosed. What comes through will not be what you asked for.*

Below that, older ink, faded almost to nothing, a second line in a different hand, finer, slanted, unfamiliar:

*The gate remembers every opening. So do we.*

I sat with that book until the candle burned down to a thumb's width, and I thought about Wren Ashby's coal-colored eyes, and about the warmth in the gate's stone two days ago, and about the hairline crack I had measured and recorded and told no one about, and I assembled the facts into the shape I most wanted them not to make.

The gate was failing. Something on the other side was reaching. And it had reached all the way to a grieving mother in Lowmarsh, because grief is a door too, and Denna Ashby had opened hers with words she didn't understand, and left it standing open behind her.

Wonder curdles slowly, the way milk does. You don't notice the moment it turns. You just notice, afterward, that what you're holding smells wrong.

By morning, the whole village knew about the miracle. By noon, they knew about three smaller ones—a lame knee that bent, a fever that broke, a cow that calved easy after its owner whispered to the dark. By evening, someone had chalked a spiral sign on the granary door, an old sign, a pre-Banishment sign, and nobody washed it off.

And by the following dusk, there were riders on the marsh road. Six of them, in Crown gray, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who have never once needed to hurry.

An inquisition banner snapped at their head, and I stood in my doorway watching them come, the Ember Hours burning a hole through the floorboards beneath my feet, and I understood that the vigil I'd kept alone for twenty years was over.

Everyone was about to learn what I knew.

The only question left was how much of it I could keep them from learning first.
