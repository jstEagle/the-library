## 3. The Man Who Fell Through

Let me tell you what falling through a threshold feels like, since I have recently become the world's foremost authority on the subject.

It feels like being a word in a language that has been outlawed. It feels like the moment between lightning and thunder stretched out into a season. It feels, most precisely and most painfully, like being poured into a vessel three sizes too small — a mortal body, with its ridiculous plumbing and its constant demands and its knees, gods forgive me, its *knees*.

I came through the Ember Gate on the third night of the waning moon, which is to say I was spat through it like a cherry stone, and I landed in a scree field two thousand feet below the pass with every prayer I'd ever answered burning off my skin like morning fog.

The mountains of Thornfell are old and rude and entirely indifferent to divinity. I lay among the stones for what my new body insisted was a day and a half, shivering, starving, watching vultures conduct their patient arithmetic above me. A godling of thresholds, reduced to counting birds. If Ossuar could have seen me then, he would have been pleased. That thought alone got me upright.

Because here is the thing they do not put in the hymns: exile is not quiet. Exile is a hunger with a shape. On the far side of the gate, the eldest of us has been eating his way through the pantheon for a century, and he has learned to reach, and what reaches through a failing gate does not come gently. I was not sent. Let me be clear about that, whoever reads this, if anyone reads this. I *ran*.

And running, as it happens, was the one thing I knew how to do.

* * *

I smelled woodsmoke before I saw the house, and I want you to understand what an event that was. A hundred years in Cindral without smell at all — the god-realm is all light and pressure and the taste of copper — and suddenly there was woodsmoke, and wet wool, and something baking that my borrowed nose identified as bread, and I sat down on a rock and wept like the mortal I was pretending not yet to be.

Then I pulled myself together, because sentiment is a luxury and I had a job.

The house sat in a fold of the mountain where no sensible person would build one: too high for farming, too far from the road for trade, close as a held breath to the pass that led up to the gate. Which meant, of course, that it belonged to the keeper. The Lockridge line. Grandmother Yarrow's blood, the blood that had helped seal my family behind a door and thrown away the key, or so the story went in Cindral, told in whispers, because even whispering the name Lockridge felt like touching a burn.

I staggered down toward the smoke the way a dying man staggers anywhere: honestly, because he has no strength left for theater.

The door opened before I knocked. Of course it did. Keepers know when something crosses their threshold; it is practically the whole of the profession.

Noor Lockridge stood in the doorway with a lamp held high and a face that gave away nothing except, if you watched very closely, everything. Dark-haired, sharp-eyed, somewhere past thirty and wearing those years like armor rather than wounds. They looked at me — burned, bleeding, half-starved, wrapped in a smuggler's coat I'd peeled off a corpse two valleys back — and I watched them perform a calculation behind their eyes. Not *should I help this man*. That question was already answered; I could see it in the set of their mouth. The question was *what will helping cost me, and how do I make him pay as little of it as possible*.

"You fell," Noor said. Not a question.

"From a great height," I agreed, and my voice cracked on the words, because speaking is different when you have lungs. "There was a fire. In the pass."

"There's no fire in the pass."

"There is now." I swayed, artfully. Mostly artfully. Mortal bodies do not distinguish well between performance and collapse, which I was beginning to count as a professional advantage.

Noor looked at me for a long moment. Then they stepped aside, and the warmth of the house rolled over me like a tide, and I crossed their threshold — *their* threshold, into a keeping older than any of my family's names — and felt the wards settle over my skin like a coat of snow.

They were good wards. Old ones. My grandmother's generation of work, maybe older. And threaded through them, faint as a watermark, was something that made my borrowed heart stutter: ember-light. Gate-light. This house was tied to the gate itself, root and branch.

I had found my keeper. Now I only had to survive them.

* * *

Here is what I learned about Noor Lockridge in the first week, lying on a pallet by their hearth and pretending to be more injured than I was, which required less acting than you might think:

They rose before dawn. Always. They checked the house's edges — doors, windows, the cellar hatch, the loose stone by the chimney — in the same order every morning, a circuit so practiced it had worn a path in the floorboards. They counted things. Jars, coins, arrows in the quiver by the door, the days marked in chalk on the lintel. When they caught themselves humming, they stopped immediately, as though music were contraband.

They spoke perhaps forty words a day, and I began to suspect they rationed them the way a miser rations coin.

And once, on the fourth night, thinking me asleep, they took down a ledger bound in grey leather and wrote in it by candlelight for an hour, and their face while they wrote was the loneliest thing I had seen in a hundred years of exile. I know loneliness. I invented several varieties of it. But this was a particular kind — the loneliness of someone standing watch over something enormous, alone, unthanked, and *unbelieved*. You cannot lie to yourself for twenty years. You can only stop talking about it.

I should say, in fairness, that I was lying to them constantly during this period, so we were even. More than even. I was better at it.

"I'm a smuggler," I told them, on the second morning, when they asked who I was. "Small goods. Salt, silk, the occasional relic that the Crown would prefer didn't exist."

"The Crown prefers a great many things don't exist," Noor said, setting down a bowl of broth with the care of someone handling something fragile, and I understood they meant the bowl was the fragile thing, or possibly I was. "Which pass?"

"The high one. Near—" I let my eyes flick upward, toward the gate, and then away, quick as a card sharp. "Near nothing. There's nothing up there."

"No," Noor agreed pleasantly. "There isn't."

And that was the moment I understood I was in the presence of a master. Because they knew. Of course they knew — the wards had told them something crossed the threshold, the gate was singing to them day and night, and here was a burned stranger appearing from exactly the wrong direction with exactly the wrong injuries. Any village priest could have added those sums. Noor Lockridge had been doing this arithmetic their whole life.

They simply chose not to say it aloud. They fed me, bandaged me, asked me nothing further, and went on with their careful, rationed, silent days, and I lay by their fire and marveled at it like a scholar before a text.

Two people, both pretending to be smaller than they were, sharing a hearth and a silence. It was almost domestic. It was almost funny. I am a godling of thresholds; I appreciate irony the way mortals appreciate weather — constantly, and with complaints.

* * *

On the ninth day my body betrayed me, which bodies do, having no loyalty whatsoever.

I had gotten stronger — mortal flesh heals fast when it isn't busy hosting a divine nature, another point in its favor — and I'd taken to walking the perimeter of the house in the evenings, ostensibly for my health, actually mapping the wards. Old habit. You learn a place's defenses or you die of not knowing them. And on the ninth evening I walked to the north fence, where the mountain dropped away toward the pass, and the wind came up from the valley carrying the smell of rain and pine and — underneath it, thin as a hair in milk — the Ember Gate.

It was *singing*. Badly. Off-key, cracked, a song with holes in it, but singing. A hundred years of silence and now, in the last month, a thread of sound. Ossuar's reaching. The gate failing. Everything I had run to warn them about, audible to anyone with ears, and my ears were currently mortal and it sang anyway.

I gripped the fence post. My vision went white at the edges. Somewhere inside this inadequate body, the part of me that was still a god of thresholds heard the gate the way a musician hears a broken string, and grief hit me like a landslide.

When I came back to myself, Noor was standing six feet away with a crossbow.

Not aimed at me. Held low, casual, the way you hold a tool you've used often enough that it's stopped being frightening. Their face was perfectly calm. Their knuckles were white.

"That fence," they said, "is the edge of my land. Beyond it is Crown forest. Beyond *that* is the high pass, which contains nothing."

"Nothing," I said. My voice came out rough. "Right."

"You were listening to it."

I could have lied. I want that recorded. I had lies ready — I always have lies ready, I was born in a court of them — and instead I stood there in the rain starting to fall, with the broken song of my homeland in my ears, and said nothing at all, which is its own confession.

Noor studied me for a long time. Rain dripped off the crossbow's limb.

"You're not a smuggler," they said finally. Not angry. Almost gentle. "Smugglers don't grieve."

"They don't?"

"No. I've known dozens. They grumble, they boast, they count their money. Grief is bad for business." They turned, slinging the crossbow across their back. "Come inside. You'll catch your death, and I've spent nine days on you already."

I followed them back across the yard, and at the door they paused, hand on the latch, and said — to the door, not to me — "Whatever you are, whatever fell you down that mountain: the bread is on the table, and I don't ask questions I don't want answers to. Yet."

*Yet.* The most terrifying word in any language. I have heard it spoken by judges and by gods and never with such quiet promise as by that tired keeper over a loaf of barley bread.

* * *

That night I lay awake cataloguing my errors, which is what passes for prayer among the exiled.

Error one: I had come to find a keeper of Lockridge blood, and I had found one, and they were not the credulous rustic the plan had assumed. Error two: the gate was failing faster than even I had estimated, which meant Ossuar's reaching was further along, which meant my window was shrinking. Error three, and this was the one that kept me staring at the rafters: I had begun to *like* them.

This was not in the plan. Liking people is how gods get ruined. We are supposed to love them at a distance, abstractly, the way a shepherd loves a flock — collectively, conveniently, with an eye to the wool. But Noor Lockridge rationing their words, checking their wards at dawn, stopping their own humming mid-note as though joy needed a permit — that was not a flock. That was a person, alone on a cold mountain, holding shut a door against my entire family, and doing it *well*, and doing it *unseen*, and I lay there feeling the first dangerous stirrings of respect and thought: *this is how it starts. This is exactly how it starts.*

Somewhere beyond the gate, Ossuar was eating our heaven one god at a time. Somewhere south, the Crown's inquisitors were sharpening whatever they sharpen. And here in a stone house under a broken-singing sky, a keeper was sleeping with one ear open, and a godling was lying by their fire learning what mortal fear felt like from the inside — not the fear of death, mortals barely trouble themselves about that, but the fear of *being known*.

We were both liars, Noor and I. Both hiding in plain sight, both pretending to be smaller than we were, both keeping vigil over something the other couldn't see. The difference was that Noor's pretending had cost them twenty years and mine had cost me nine days, and I suspected — correctly, as it would turn out — that before this was over, the accounting would reverse.

Toward dawn I finally slept, and dreamed, for the first time in a century, and the dream was of a door.

In the dream the door stood open, and through it came a smell of woodsmoke and bread, and a voice said, in the tone of someone reading from a ledger: *"You'll tell me eventually. Everyone tells me eventually. It's the yet that gets them."*

I woke to the smell of actual bread, actual woodsmoke, and Noor Lockridge standing in the doorway with a letter in their hand, gone pale as ash.

"There are riders on the south road," they said. "Crown black and silver. Inquisitors." They looked at me, and for the first time I saw the dread under the calm, and recognized it, because I had carried its twin across a mountain. "So. Smuggler. Whatever you're hiding —"

They folded the letter with precise, terrible neatness.

"— I suggest you decide quickly how much of it survives questioning."
