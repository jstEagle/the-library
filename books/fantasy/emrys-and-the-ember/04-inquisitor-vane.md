## 4. Inquisitor Vane

The inquisitors arrived on a Tuesday, which felt rude of them. Tuesdays were market days, and Lowmarsh's whole talent for looking innocent depended on everyone being too busy haggling over eels to notice anything.

I was selling dried mushrooms at my stall when the riders came up the causeway — six of them, grey cloaks, grey horses, and one lead rider on a black mare who sat like she'd been poured into the saddle by someone with strong opinions about posture. The Crown's colors were stitched small and neat on their shoulders: a silver key crossed with a quill. *We open what you'd rather keep shut,* that crest always seemed to say.

My hands did not shake. Twenty years of keeping secrets had taught them better. But something behind my ribs went very quiet and very still, the way the marsh goes still before frost.

"Inquisitors," muttered Bram Tull from the next stall, not even bothering to lower his voice. "What's the Crown want with our eels?"

"Nothing," I said, arranging my mushrooms with great care. "Probably tax business."

It was a lie, and it came out smooth as river mud, and I hated myself a little for how easy it was.

* * *

They set up in the tithe-barn, which was the largest building in Lowmarsh that wasn't a shrine or somebody's house, and by evening the whole village had arranged itself into two camps: those who wanted to be questioned immediately, to prove their innocence through enthusiasm, and those who wanted to be questioned never. My grandmother's old advice covered both camps: *Be boring, Noor. Boring people are invisible, and invisible people are safe.*

She had also written an entire book of prayers to outlawed gods, so her advice on safety should perhaps have been taken with a grain of salt.

I went to the tithe-barn anyway. Not because I wanted to — every step was a negotiation between curiosity and self-preservation, and self-preservation lost, as it usually does — but because if I didn't go, my absence would be the most interesting thing about me. Inquisitors noticed absences. It was practically their trade.

Inside, the barn had been transformed. Candles burned in ranks along trestle tables. A map of the village hung on the far wall, annotated in a small, precise hand. And at the center of it all stood the lead rider, now robed, copying something into a ledger with the kind of patience most people reserve for prayer.

He looked up when I entered. He had a long face, a short beard going grey at the edges, and eyes the color of pond water in winter — pale, clear, and cold enough to see straight down to the bottom of.

"Name?" he said.

"Noor Lockridge."

"Occupation?"

"Forager. Dried goods. Mushrooms mostly." I held up my basket as evidence, which was either very clever or very stupid; I genuinely couldn't tell which.

"Mushrooms," he repeated, and wrote it down, and I watched him write it, and there was nothing in his face at all. That frightened me more than suspicion would have. Suspicion you can work with. Blankness is just a wall with no handholds.

"You're the third Lockridge this village has produced," he said. "Your grandmother. Your mother. You."

"My mother died young."

"I know. I read the parish rolls before we rode out." He set down his pen and gave me his full attention, which was like being looked at by a lighthouse. "You've lived here all your life?"

"All but four years. I was fostered in the hill country after she passed. An aunt."

"Aunt's name?"

"Yeva Lockridge. She's dead too." This was true. Yeva had been dead for eleven years, which made her wonderfully useful. The dead make the best alibis; they can't contradict you, and they don't mind being invented upon. Grandmother Yarrow had taught me that too, though she'd framed it as *the dead are generous that way.*

"And your aunt lived... where, precisely?"

"The high passes. North of the Ember ridge." I said the name lightly, carelessly, the way you mention a landmark nobody cares about. "Sheep country. Very dull. You'd hate it."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An archivist's amusement, maybe, at a subject attempting charm.

"Perhaps," said Inquisitor Vane. "Thank you, Master Lockridge. Stay available."

I stayed available. Which is to say, I walked home at a reasonable pace, let myself into my cottage, bolted the door, and then sat down on the floor with my back against it and breathed for a while.

*Stay available.* In inquisitorial grammar, that meant: *I am not finished with you.*

* * *

Over the next three days, Vane interviewed the entire village, and I watched him do it the way you watch weather come in — helplessly, and with a growing sense of what it would cost.

He was not what I'd expected. I'd expected cruelty, or at least laziness; the Crown's inquisitors had a reputation built mostly on the second and occasionally the first. But Vane was neither. He was *thorough*. He interviewed the midwife twice. He measured the depth of the millpond where little Wren Callow had drowned, and asked the ferryman about currents, and requested the parish burial records going back forty years, and read all of them, every line, in a single night by candlelight.

And here was the thing that unsettled me most, the thing I turned over in bed like a stone with a sharp edge: Vane *believed*. You could see it in how he handled the villagers' fear — gently, almost tenderly, like a man handling something breakable that he intended to keep. When old Marta wept during her interview, he waited without impatience and then said, quietly, "You're afraid of the wrong thing, mistress. Fear the lie, not the truth. Lies are what fester."

He wasn't performing piety. He *had* it, the way some people have bad knees — a condition he carried everywhere, that shaped how he moved through the world. And a pious inquisitor is a hundred times more dangerous than a cruel one, because cruelty can be bought and belief cannot.

"He asked me if I'd ever prayed to the Old Nine," Tam Callow told me at the well, white-faced, wringing his cap. Wren's father. The man whose daughter had come back from the dead. "I said no. Noor, I said no, but I *had*, I prayed to Ashvael when they pulled her out of the water, I prayed like my life depended on it—"

"And she breathed," I said.

"And she *breathed*." His voice cracked on it. "Was that wrong? Tell me it wasn't wrong. She's my girl. She's warm and she eats and she laughs. How can that be wrong?"

"It isn't," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said all week, and it was also, under the current law, a confession. I put my hand on his shoulder. "You loved your daughter. Nobody in a grey cloak gets to make that a crime."

He looked at me with such naked gratitude that I felt it like a bruise. Because I knew things he didn't. I knew why the prayer had worked, or at least I feared I did, and I knew what the gate was doing, and I knew that every hour I spent being helpful neighborly Noor with my mushrooms and my mild opinions was an hour the wall between us grew another course of bricks higher.

Tam thought I was his friend. Tam was right, in the way that mattered to him and wrong in every way that mattered to me. I could not tell him *your daughter's miracle is a crack in the seal my family has bled to hold for a century, and the thing reaching through it remembers my grandmother's name.* So instead I said, "Keep your head down, Tam. Answer his questions short. Don't volunteer anything."

Good advice. Coward's advice. Both at once. Most advice is.

* * *

On the fourth day, Vane came to my cottage.

I heard the mare on the lane before the knock — she had a heavy, deliberate tread, like her rider — and I had time to sweep the banned prayer book's hiding place with my eyes one last time (under the loose hearthstone, beneath a layer of ash I'd laid fresh that morning) and to put the kettle on, because hospitality is armor.

"Master Lockridge." He ducked under my lintel and stood dripping slightly, because it had started to rain, because of course it had; the sky had been reading the same grim script we all had. "May I?"

"You may sit, and you may have tea. Those are the full extent of my permissions."

That got the almost-smile again. He sat. He accepted tea. He wrapped both hands around the cup like a man warming himself at something other than the fire, and for a moment he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with riding.

"Do you know why we're here?" he asked.

"There was a miracle in Lowmarsh. The Crown doesn't care for miracles it didn't authorize."

"That's the cynical version." He sipped. "Here's mine. A child died. Then she didn't. Everything I have sworn to uphold says that resurrection belongs to the Nine-in-Cindral, and everything I have sworn to uphold also says the Nine-in-Cindral were banished for breaking their oaths, and that any power answering prayers in their name now is either a fraud, a demon wearing a beloved name, or—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—something worse. My job is to find out which. And to protect these people from it. Whatever *it* turns out to be."

"And if it turns out to be a genuine mercy? A god keeping faith after a hundred years?"

"Then I will report that," he said, "and the Crown will burn the village anyway, because the law does not distinguish between mercies and plagues. Only between authorized and unauthorized." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and somehow that was worse. "You think I don't know the shape of my own work, Master Lockridge? I have buried children for lesser miracles than yours."

"*My* miracle," I said. Careful. "Wren Callow isn't mine."

"No," Vane agreed. "But you were the first person to reach the millpond. Before her father. Before anyone. The miller's boy saw you running from the direction of the marsh road, and the marsh road comes from the hills, and the hills—" he turned the cup slowly, once, "—come up against the Ember ridge."

The kettle ticked on the fire. Rain worked at the thatch. Somewhere in my chest, twenty years of discipline closed ranks and held the line.

"I walk the marsh road every morning," I said. "Have since I was a girl. Bad knee keeps me to flat ground — ask anyone. I heard Tam shouting and I ran. That's the whole of it. If you're asking whether I raised a drowned child from the water with forbidden rites, Inquisitor, I'd need to be a far more interesting person than I am."

"Are you?" he asked. Simply. Without malice. As if he were asking whether I kept bees.

"I'm a mushroom-seller with a bad knee."

"Hm." He finished his tea and rose, and at the door he paused, rain-light behind him. "Your grandmother testified before the last commission, you know. Sixty years ago. When they drew up the ban."

The floor tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. I kept my face bored. I have never been prouder of my face.

"Did she?" I said. "She never talked much about the old days. Said history was gossip that got ambitious."

"What did she testify?"

"I honestly couldn't say. I was a child, and she's been dead twenty years." Also true. Also useless. Also a brick.

Vane studied me for a long moment, pond-water eyes doing their patient work, and whatever he found there — boredom, dullness, the careful texture of an unremarkable life — he apparently found it sufficient. "Stay available, Master Lockridge."

"Always," I said, and meant: *always watching you*, and he heard only what I'd built him to hear.

* * *

After he left, I didn't move for a long time. The fire settled. The rain thickened. And I sat in my chair and took inventory of the day's lies, the way a merchant takes inventory after a fire:

*I walk the marsh road every morning.* True.
*Bad knee.* True, though it hadn't stopped me from running to the millpond fast enough to matter.
*I'd need to be a far more interesting person.* True in a way that would have been funny if it weren't fatal.
*She never talked much about the old days.* False. She talked constantly. She just made sure only I was listening.

Four lies, three truths, and every false word fitted so smoothly into place that no seam showed. That was the skill, the inheritance I'd never asked for: not the keeping of the gate, but the keeping of the silence around it. Grandmother Yarrow had trained it into me across a thousand kitchen-table lessons disguised as stories. *Lie small, Noor. Lie local. Never lie about anything a ledger could check.*

And it worked. God help me, it worked. Vane had walked out of my cottage satisfied, or near enough, and tomorrow he would interview the ferryman again and I would remain a footnote in his ledger: *Lockridge, N. Forager. Nothing.*

Nothing. That was the prize. That was the whole art of my life, distilled: to be nothing, convincingly, forever.

Except.

Except Tam Callow had looked at me with gratitude, and I'd taken it while withholding the truth that might save his family. Except Marta had wept in front of a man whose job was to weigh her soul, and I'd gone home and hidden my hearthstone deeper. Except the village had begun, in the way villages do, to fold me into itself — *Noor will know, Noor was there, ask Noor* — and every thread they offered me, I caught and used and gave back nothing real.

Every lie was a brick. I'd known that for years. What I was only beginning to feel, sitting alone in the rain-dark with my tea gone cold, was that the wall they built had a door in it, and I had bricked that up too, from my side, a long time ago. The loneliness of the vigil wasn't that no one knew my secret. It was that I'd made sure no one *could*.

Outside, hoofbeats on the lane. I went to the window, casual as anything, heart hammering like a novice's.

Vane stood in the square below, in the rain, talking quietly to the miller's boy. And the boy was pointing.

Not at me. Not at the marsh road.

At the hills. At the high passes north of the Ember ridge, where a burned and starving stranger lay sleeping under my roof, thirty miles away, with blood in his hair that glowed faintly gold whenever the lightning came.
