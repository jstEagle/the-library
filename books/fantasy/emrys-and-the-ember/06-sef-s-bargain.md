## 6. Sef's Bargain

I had been a god for eleven thousand years, and in all that time I had never once been cornered by anyone holding a teapot.

It is important, when cataloguing the humiliations of exile, to be precise about the details.

"Sit down," Noor Lockridge said.

I was already sitting. That was rather the point of the teapot. It sat between us on the table like a small copper hostage, steaming with an aggression I found frankly theatrical. Noor stood across from me with their arms folded and their grandmother's banned prayer book open on the table beside the bread board, which was — I will grant them this — a very effective piece of staging. The book was the accusation. The tea was the courtesy. Between the two of them, I was meant to understand that I could be killed or comforted, and that the choice of which was entirely mine.

Mortals think they are subtle. Mortals are not subtle. Mortals are *wonderful*.

"You've been here nine days," Noor said. "You eat like a man who hasn't seen food in a century, you sleep facing the door, you flinch every time I say the word *gate*, and last night I watched you talk to my hearth."

"I was being polite."

"It's a hearth."

"Exactly. You'd be amazed how few of those get thanked."

Noor did not laugh. This was disappointing, because I had spent nine days calibrating my humor to their particular frequency — dry, suspicious, allergic to charm but secretly starved of it — and I had thought that one landed. Instead they turned the prayer book around so I could read the page it was open to. The ink had faded to the color of old bruises, but I knew the script. Everyone from Cindral knew that script. It was the hand we wrote our oaths in, back when we still believed oaths were things you kept rather than things you broke.

"This rite," Noor said. "The one your friend in Lowmarsh used to pull a drowned girl back into her body. My grandmother copied it out before the ban. She annotated it." Their finger tapped the margin, where a spidery hand had written: *The threshold answers to the threshold-keeper. Ask it kindly.* "You arrived out of the mountains near my gate, burned half to death, with no name you'll give and no story that survives ten minutes of questioning. And you have burn scars," they added, quietly, "in the shape of a doorway."

Ah. Yes. The scars.

In Cindral, before the fall, I had been Sef-of-the-Liminal, keeper of every small crossing — doorsills and footbridges, the moment between sleeping and waking, the breath between one name and another. A minor god. A god of hinges, if you like. When Ossuar's great working tore at the Ember Gate from the far side, and I slipped through the widening crack ahead of the fire, the gate had taken its toll in the oldest currency: it had marked me with itself. Every threshold leaves a signature on what passes through. Mine happened to be written across my chest and shoulders in lines of silvered scar tissue, branching like a map of everywhere I'd ever let anyone in.

I had covered them with a shirt and a lie. Nine days of lies, actually, each one smaller and more exhausting than the last. I had told Noor I was a smuggler. I had told them I'd been caught in a rockslide. I had told them my name was Fen, which was true in the way that a coin clipped in half is still technically money.

"Fen," Noor said now, and I heard the quotation marks around it. "Who are you?"

Here is something no one tells you about being a god among mortals: it is lonely in a way that heaven never prepared you for. In Cindral I had been known by every door in the world. Doors are honest company. They only ever want one thing from you, and it is the same thing you want from them. But flesh is a locked room, and I had been carrying the truth of myself around inside it like a coal in cupped hands, and my hands were tired, and the coal was burning through.

So when Noor asked *who are you*, some traitorous hinge inside me swung open before I could stop it.

"Put the kettle back on," I said. "This takes a while."

* * *

They didn't sit while I talked. They stood by the window with their back half-turned, watching the mountain through the glass as though the gate might be visible from there, which it wasn't, which was the whole trouble with gates: the most important thing in any wall is the part you can't see.

"My name is Sef," I said. "Not Fen. Though Fen is close enough that I answer to it, which is the nature of names — they're thresholds too, and I've never met one I couldn't slip through. I am — I *was* — a god of thresholds. Minor. Very minor. Do not picture thunderbolts. Picture the feeling you get standing in a doorway at dusk, deciding whether to go out or stay in. That feeling has a custodian. Congratulations. You're drinking tea with him."

Noor's shoulders did not relax. But their head turned, a degree or two, toward me. Progress. With Noor Lockridge, progress was measured in degrees, like the opening of a very stubborn door.

"The war of broken oaths — your histories call it the Sundering, ours called it the Reckoning, both sides called it someone else's fault, and everyone was right — ended with the pantheon exiled behind the Ember Gate. Your grandmother helped seal it. I know because I was there. Not fighting. Gods of thresholds don't fight; we just decide who gets through, and in the end we decided almost nobody would. I watched her set the last wardstone. She was crying. So was the god next to her, and he was one of ours, and neither of them stopped working long enough to wipe their faces. That's what sealing a gate between worlds actually looks like. Not glory. Grief with its sleeves rolled up."

"You said *was*," Noor said. "A god of thresholds. Past tense."

"Because I'm standing in your kitchen wearing a borrowed body and eating bread I paid for with coins I found in a ditch. Exile does things to us. We thin out. We go mortal at the edges first." I held up my hand and let them see it — really see it, the way I hadn't let anyone see anything in nine days. The fingers trembled slightly. Godly hands do not tremble. "I crossed the gate eleven days ago because it is failing, Noor. Cracking. Warming. Don't look surprised — you found the cracks yourself on your centennial inspection, and you wrote it in your ledger in pencil instead of ink, and you told no one, because telling someone would make it real, and then you would have to decide what to do, and you have spent twenty years making sure no decision bigger than *which shelf to dust* ever came near you."

That was cruel. I knew it was cruel when I said it. But there is a kind of truth that only cruelty can carry, the way some doors only open if you push hard enough to hurt your shoulder.

Noor went very still. Then, in a voice of terrible mildness: "You read my ledger."

"I read everything. I'm a god of thresholds. Rooms tell me their business." I softened it as best I could. "Your secret is safe. It was always going to be safe with me. That's rather the entire point of what I am."

"Then say the point plainly," Noor said. "You came through a failing gate, alone, burned, knowing the bloodline of the woman who sealed it. You looked for me. Why? What does a god want with a keeper?"

And there it was. The actual door. I had been circling it for nine days, and now I put my hand on the latch.

"Ossuar is opening it," I said.

* * *

I told them all of it, because a bargain built on half-truths is just a trap with better manners.

I told them how the eldest of us, Ossuar-the-Unyielding, god of foundations and endings, had never accepted the exile — had never accepted that *we* broke the covenant first, that we had grown fat on worship and careless with the world we were sworn to keep. I told them how, over a hundred years, the other gods' strength had begun to ebb toward him, slowly, politely, the way water finds a low place, and how none of us had wanted to name what was happening because naming it would mean choosing sides. I told them how the gate had begun to warm from the far side like a stove through a wall, and how prayers were starting to leak through the cracks — small mercies at first, answered by whatever fragment of divinity happened to be pressed against the breach, each miracle costing the land a little gray ruin, because power pulled through a wound carries the wound with it.

"And the drowned girl," Noor said. Their voice had gone flat, which I was learning was how they carried fear. "The blight in the fields around her house. That was—"

"A leak under pressure. Yes. Ossuar isn't answering prayers. He's *spending* them. Every miracle buys him another inch of the gate. He doesn't care what it costs your side. He cares even less what it costs ours."

"And you?" Noor turned fully from the window at last. "Where do you stand in this? Because from where I'm sitting, Sef-of-the-Hinges, you crossed over right when he needed someone on this side. Burned, starving, helpless — arriving precisely at the home of the last keeper of Lockridge blood. If I were a suspicious person—" they paused, and something wry and sad moved through their face, "—and I am, professionally, a suspicious person — I'd say you were either a deserter or a lure."

The word went through me like a splinter finding bone. Because I had asked myself the same question, alone, on the mountain, in the snow, with my new mortal heart hammering like a fist on a locked door. *What if you weren't escaping? What if you were sent?* Ossuar had spoken to me last, before I ran. He had blessed my going. He had said *find the keeper*. I had told myself it was a threat, that he meant to use me to find and break her successor. I had chosen to believe my own flight was mine.

But gods lie to themselves more fluently than they lie to anyone else. It's our mother tongue.

"I don't know," I said.

Noor blinked. I suspect that in twenty years of keeping secrets, no one had ever answered them with *I don't know*, and the honesty of it disarmed them more thoroughly than any cleverness could have.

"That's not a bargain," they said finally. "That's a confession with ambitions."

"It's the only kind of bargain worth making. Listen to me." I stood, and the chair scraped, and for a moment I let the last of my divinity show through the borrowed flesh — just a shimmer, just enough that the lamplight bent toward me and the room felt briefly, unmistakably, *attended*. "I can't reopen the covenant alone. I can't even hold the gate alone. But there are three Ember Shards scattered in your mortal world — the pieces of the original oath-stone, hidden by your grandmother's generation when they sealed the gate, so that someday, if the terms could be honored again, the covenant could be reforged. Reforged properly. Not Ossuar's way, kicking the door down. The old way. Keeper and god, face to face, terms renewed. I know where the first shard sleeps. I need someone the shards will trust. Someone of her blood."

"No."

"You haven't heard the—"

"No," Noor said again, and now I saw it clearly: not refusal. Panic. Twenty years of a vigil no one thanked them for, twenty years of being the only soul in Thornfell who knew the world had a seam running through it, and now a burnt stranger in their kitchen was asking them to walk straight into the fire they'd spent two decades standing guard against. "I keep the gate. That's the duty. I keep it closed, I keep it quiet, I write it down in pencil, and I die someday and pass the ledger to no one, because there is no one. That was the covenant I inherited. *Keep it shut.*"

"Your grandmother's covenant was *keep it honest*," I said. "Shutting it was just the first installment. Read her margins, Noor. She left you more than a prayer book. She left you instructions for the day the gate started warming. This is that day."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, wind came down off the passes and pressed against the shutters, and I felt it the way I feel all crossings — as a question. *Which side of this door do you stand on?*

Noor picked up the prayer book. Closed it. Held it against their chest like a shield, or a child, or a stone they intended to carry a very long way.

"If I say yes," they said, "you'll tell me everything. No more clipped coins. No more *Fen*. Every plan, every danger, every name — including yours, the real one, the one you're afraid of."

"Sef," I said. "My real name is Sef. I gave it to you five minutes ago. The fear was in the giving."

Something in their face shifted — not trust, nothing so generous, but the recognition of a fellow professional. Two people who dealt in thresholds, acknowledging each other across the counter.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go to the shrine alone and probably die, and Ossuar comes through anyway, and your ledger gets a very short final entry."

"Wonderful. You've made refusing you sound like bookkeeping."

"I'm a god of small things, Noor. Small things are what I have."

They looked at me for a long moment. Then they set the prayer book on the table, took the cold teapot, and refilled both cups — which in the language of Noor Lockridge, I was beginning to understand, was practically a signed treaty.

"We leave tomorrow," they said. "Before dawn. There are roads through the mountains that aren't on any Crown map, and if we're lucky, we'll reach the drowned shrine of Vessa before Vane's inquisitors find the same trail we're following."

"Vane," I said. "The inquisitor. How much time do we have?"

Noor drank their tea and looked, for a moment, genuinely apologetic.

"He reached Lowmarsh yesterday," they said. "I lied to his face this morning about ever having been near the mountains. He thanked me for my candor." They set down the cup. "He didn't believe a word of it."
