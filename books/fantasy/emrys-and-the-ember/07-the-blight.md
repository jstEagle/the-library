## 7. The Blight

The barley went gray on a Tuesday, which felt about right. Catastrophe in Lowmarsh has always kept market-day hours.

I was in the village to buy salt and lie to a neighbor when I saw it—old Tam Wicker standing at the edge of his field with his hat in his hands like he'd come to a funeral and couldn't remember whose. The barley stood in rows the way it had all summer, except where it hadn't. A swath of it, maybe twenty paces wide, running from the lane down toward the well, had turned the color of old ash. Not dead—dead I'd have understood. Dead is brown and honest. This was gray, silver-gray, and when the wind moved through it the stalks didn't bend so much as *crumble*, little flakes lifting off them like the field was quietly smoking without any fire.

"Late blight," Tam said when I stopped beside him. He said it the way people say things they don't believe, just to have said something.

"Late blight doesn't start in the middle of a healthy field," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't."

He didn't look at me. He was looking past the field, past the well, to the Wickers' front step, where his granddaughter Briony sat in the sun shelling peas with the concentration of a child who had recently been dead and found the living part of it still novel. Nine days ago she'd been pulled from the millpond blue as slate, and nine days ago her mother had knelt on that bank and prayed—to no one, to anyone, and then, when desperation stripped away caution, by name. To Vessa of the Deep Water, outlawed for a hundred years, patroness of drowning things given back.

And Briony had breathed.

I'd been there. I'd watched it happen. I'd helped carry the girl inside and told myself, on the walk home, that there were explanations. There are always explanations if you hold them close enough to your face. But I had also gone back that night and looked at the ground where Maren Wicker had knelt, because I am my grandmother's granddaughter and I check things, and the grass there had been gray. Just a handprint's worth. I'd told myself it was frost. In midsummer.

Now the whole path from the doorstep to the well was gray, and the field beyond it, and every place in Lowmarsh where someone had gotten down on their knees and asked an outlaw for help.

"How far?" I asked Tam.

"To the Hedleys' turnips. The Fenn place. Half of Merrick's south acre." He put his hat back on, which seemed to require some courage. "The priest says it's a judgment."

"The priest would."

"He says the gods are coming back and they're rotting everything they touch." Tam's jaw worked. "But Briony's not rotted. She's the healthiest child in the parish. So either the priest's wrong, or—" He stopped. People in Lowmarsh have gotten very good at stopping sentences lately, letting the dangerous halves of them die quietly in the throat.

"Or the price isn't paid in flesh," I finished, because apparently I'd forgotten how to stop sentences too.

Tam nodded slowly. "You know things, Lockridge. Your family always knew things." It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. "What do we do?"

*Burn the prayer book. Kneel on your own floors. Pray to nothing, loudly, until the Crown believes you.* What I said was: "Don't eat anything that grew in the gray. And keep your daughter away from deep water anyway, miracle or no."

It wasn't comfort. But comfort was a currency I'd stopped carrying years ago, and Tam took what I gave him and went inside, and I stood in his dying field doing arithmetic I hated.

Every answered prayer left a scar on the land. I could see the shape of it now, laid over Lowmarsh like a map of its own desperation: gray where the drowned girl came back, gray along the row where Hedley's wife's fever broke overnight, gray tracing the fence line where the Fenns' cow had calved safely after three dead years of calves. Small mercies, each one. Each one a coin spent out of soil that hadn't agreed to the exchange.

Someone was answering prayers through my gate. And the gate—the gate I had kept sealed for twenty years, oiled and warded and checked on its centennial schedule like a good little keeper—was taking payment from the wrong account.

My grandmother's ledger had a phrase for this. I'd read it a hundred times without understanding: *the covenant draws on the near side first.* I'd thought it meant something administrative. It meant this. It meant that when power crossed the threshold uninvited, it didn't pay tolls in heaven. It paid in barley fields and well water and the roots of things, here, where the mortals lived, because mortals were the near side, and mortals were what covenants were made *of*.

I had kept the gate. Therefore the wound was mine.

That thought followed me home like a dog I couldn't lose.

* * *

Vane arrived on Thursday with forty riders and a wagon of instruments, which is what happens when a village's sins get large enough to require logistics.

I'd met Crown inquisitors before—twice, both times briefly, both times while lying—and I'd formed a theory that they recruited for two qualities: piety and patience, in that order. Inquisitor Vane disproved half the theory. He was patient the way a plumb line is patient, but the piety was real, and that frightened me more than fanaticism would have. Fanatics can be predicted. Sincere men simply follow their convictions wherever those convictions lead, and their convictions are usually written by other people.

He set up in the shrine district—which is a grand name for three converted cottages and the old well-shrine—and posted notices before noon. By evening the notices were everywhere, nailed to doors and fence posts, and I stood reading one outside the mill with my heart going quiet and cold in my chest.

*By order of the Crown Inquisition: the affliction known as the Gray Blight is declared a plague of supernatural origin. All gatherings for worship are forbidden. All shrines, images, and writings pertaining to the Banished are to be surrendered for burning. Prayer to any banished name is hereby classified as contagion, and carriers will be detained.* And then, in smaller letters, the sentence that did the real work: *Miracles witnessed and not reported constitute concealment of plague.*

Concealment of plague carried a flogging. Second offense, the gallows.

Maren Wicker had prayed aloud on a public riverbank in front of eleven witnesses. I counted the witnesses twice, because I am thorough even while terrified, and two of them were children who'd tell anything to anyone for the excitement of being asked.

"You've read it, then."

Vane's voice behind me. Of course. Inquisitors materialize; it's practically in the job description. I turned and gave him the expression I'd practiced in mirrors for twenty years—mild, rural, faintly worried, a person whose inner life consists mostly of weather.

"I can't make heads or tails of the legal parts," I said. "Is it true? That the gods are back?"

"That depends what you mean by *back*." Vane was younger than I expected—forty, maybe, with a clerk's ink-stained fingers and a widow's black band still on his sleeve, worn thin with age. Grief as a permanent accessory. His eyes did the thing sincere men's eyes do: they looked at you and genuinely wanted you to be innocent. "There is a power operating in this valley. It answers petitions. It leaves residue. The Church calls the source banished; the residue calls itself merciful. Both cannot be fully true, and yet both appear to be." He said it like a man laying out tools. "You're the Lockridge from the mountain holdings. You know these hills."

"Born on them."

"Then you'll know there are old shrines up there. High places. Places the banished names were whispered before the Ban." He watched me with terrible gentleness. "If you hear such whispering again—in a tavern, on a road, anywhere—you'll report it. Not for the Crown's sake. For theirs." He nodded toward the village, toward Tam Wicker's gray field. "Whatever is answering these people does not love them. Love does not leave rot behind. And the sooner they understand that the thing they're praying to is a predator, the fewer of them will kneel."

And there it was, the trap inside the kindness: he was right. Something was reaching through my gate and taking payment from fields, and I couldn't argue with a word of his warning. I could only disagree with his conclusion, silently, professionally, the way you disagree with a colleague.

"I'll report anything I hear," I said, and hated how smoothly it came out. Twenty years of practice, and the lies had gotten so good they didn't even cost me anything anymore. That was the part I noticed, standing there in the dust of the mill road. Not the lying. The ease.

"Thank you." He almost smiled. "Your grandmother would be proud, I think. The Lockridges were loyal to Thornfell when loyalty was expensive."

Something in my chest turned over and lay still. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I read her file." He said it without apology. "Everyone who kept records in those years has a file. Hers ends oddly. Sealed by order of the provincial governor, the year she died." He tilted his head, and for one moment the plumb line swung, testing me. "Strange, the things that get sealed. Gates. Files. Graves." Then he tipped his hat and moved on down the road to ruin someone else's week, and I stood there understanding that I had not been questioned at all. I'd been *filed*. Somewhere in the inquisition's archive there was a page with my family's name on it, and Vane had read it before he ever rode into Lowmarsh, and every friendly word since had been a key trying locks.

* * *

They burned the shrines on Saturday. Three cottages' worth of carvings and icons and one genuinely beautiful statue of Vessa that the miller's grandfather had hidden through the last purge and his father after him, dragged out into the square by neighbors who wouldn't look at the miller's family while they did it. The fire was hot and cheerful and everyone attended, because attendance was safety, and I stood in the crowd with my hands folded and watched a century of careful hiding go up in an afternoon.

Briony Wicker cried. Not for the statue—for her mother, who was taken afterward. Not arrested, exactly. *Detained for observation*, the notice said, which is the Crown's way of arresting someone gently. Vane himself walked Maren to the wagon, and I saw him speak to her, and I saw her answer, and whatever she said made him stop walking for a moment, just a moment, like a man who's stepped on something soft in the dark.

I know what she said. I'd have said the same. *She was dying. My daughter was dying. You would have prayed too.*

The wagons rolled east with four detainees, and the crowd dispersed the way crowds do, quickly and facing away from each other, and I stayed until the square was empty because I'm a fool with a talent for standing in places I shouldn't.

The ashes were still warm. Under them, where the statue's base had stood, the flagstones were gray.

Not scorched. *Gray.* The same silver-gray as the barley, spreading outward from the spot in a neat circle, as if the burning had been a prayer too—as if Vessa had accepted the offering of her own image and paid for it the only way she could, out of the near side, out of us.

I crouched and touched the stone. Warm. Warm like the crack in the gate had been warm, that centennial morning when I'd noted it in the ledger and told no one, because telling someone would make it real and real things require decisions.

The gate was failing. Something kind and careless was leaking through it, granting wishes at compound interest against the soil of Thornfell, and the Crown was calling it a plague and burning the sick, and I was the only person alive who knew where the wound actually was, and I had told no one. I had filed it in a ledger. I had done what keepers do, which is nothing, beautifully, for years, until the nothing comes due.

My grandmother's ledger had another line I'd never understood. I'd assumed it was senility or code. Now it read like instructions:

*A gate is not a wall. A wall keeps things out. A gate only decides what comes through—and if the keeper will not decide, the gate decides alone.*

The gate was deciding alone. Look around. Gray fields. Burned shrines. A mother in a wagon.

I walked home in the dark, up the smuggler's paths, past the boundary stones, to the high hollow where the Ember Gate stood in its collar of old stone, and I put my palm flat against it the way I had ten thousand times, and it was warm. Warmer than Tuesday. Warmer than it had ever been, a heat like a fever building, like something on the far side pressing its forehead against the door.

"Hold," I told it, uselessly, the way you talk to horses in lightning storms. "Hold on."

Deep in the stone, something shifted—a sound below hearing, a pressure change, the sense of a vast attention turning toward me through six inches of rock. And beneath my hand, faint as breath on a winter pane, the warmth arranged itself into a shape.

Not a crack. A pattern. Deliberate. Almost—almost like writing.

Like someone on the other side had finally learned to spell, and the first word they'd chosen to carve through a hundred years of silence was my family's name.
