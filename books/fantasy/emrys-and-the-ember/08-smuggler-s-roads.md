## 8. Smuggler's Roads

### Noor

The first thing you learn on a smuggler's road is that the map is a lie you tell yourself to keep walking.

The second thing you learn is that Sef cannot read a map at all, which I discovered somewhere past the second ridge, when he looked at my grandmother's hand-drawn chart of the Thornfell passes and asked, with genuine scholarly interest, which way was up.

"That's north," I said, tapping the little compass rose.

"Ah." He nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he has decided not to ask a follow-up question because the answer might reveal something about him. "And this squiggle?"

"A river."

"And this angry scribble?"

"Also a river. My grandmother had strong feelings about that one."

We were three days out of Lowmarsh, and I had already learned several things about traveling with a fugitive godling. One: he talked constantly, as though silence were a debt he couldn't afford. Two: he was stronger than he looked but tired faster than he'd admit, mortal flesh being a garment he hadn't yet learned to wear rather than merely occupy. Three: he watched me. Constantly. The way I imagine a cat watches a door it intends to go through eventually.

I didn't mind the watching so much. I'd spent twenty years being invisible; being seen, even by something that probably wanted something from me, was an indulgence I hadn't budgeted for.

"Your grandmother," Sef said, picking his way over scree in boots I'd stolen from a dead smuggler's cache—boots that fit him badly, because the dead smuggler had been a smaller man, or perhaps gods simply run long in the foot—"she drew these herself?"

"She walked them herself. Every line on that page is a night she didn't sleep."

"Why does a keeper need smugglers' roads?"

I folded the map away. "Because keepers are people, and people have enemies, and my grandmother believed in having two exits from every room." I glanced back down the trail. "Including this one. Move."

He moved. He was learning to move when I said move, which was either trust or self-preservation, and on the third day of a hard road those become the same thing.

The riders had picked us up near dusk—not Vane himself, but his outriders, four of them, moving along the valley floor with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the roads belonged to their master. We watched from a shelf of granite while they passed below, torches swinging.

"They're not tracking us," Sef murmured. "They're sweeping."

"They're sweeping toward the shrine. Vane's methodical. He'll have found the old records by now—the Crown kept ledgers too, once." I stood, knees cracking in a way that reminded me I was thirty-eight and had spent twenty years crouching in front of a door instead of walking anywhere. "We take the high route."

"The high route looks like a rumor."

"It is a rumor. That's the point. Come on."

* * *

### Sef

Here is what no one tells you about mortality: it is heavy.

Not the flesh—I had worn flesh before, in the old days, when the covenant was whole and we walked among mortals like guests who were also furniture. But then, the flesh had been a courtesy. Now it was a sentence. It demanded food. It demanded sleep. It grew blisters on my feet that hurt in a manner I can only describe as *insulting*, because pain without meaning felt less like suffering than like being mocked by my own body.

Noor did not complain. This was the most unsettling thing about them.

On the fourth morning we climbed a ridge that I would swear, under oath before any court in Cindral, was vertical. Noor climbed it the way other people climb stairs, breathing through the nose, hands finding holds without looking, pausing only once—to pull me up over a lip of rock by the wrist, hauling my entire divine weight as though I were a sack of turnips.

"You're heavier than you look," they said.

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain blisters. Sit. Eat."

They handed me dried fish and a heel of bread gone hard as doctrine, and sat beside me with their back against the stone, watching the valley below where the riders' torches had made a thin orange thread in the dark. Their face in that light was unreadable, which I was beginning to understand was its natural state. But their hands—their hands were doing something. Turning a small brass compass over and over. A habit, not a purpose.

"You're afraid," I said.

"I'm always afraid. Fear is a tool. You hold it by the handle."

"That's very wise."

"It's my grandmother's. She had a saying for everything. It was exhausting." They turned the compass over again. Then, without looking at me: "Why did you really come through? And don't give me the speech about restoring the covenant. I've heard speeches. I've given speeches. Speeches are what people do instead of answering."

I should have lied. I was built for lying; thresholds are, in their way, the original lie—a thing that says *here ends one world*, which is never quite true. But there was something about the wind on that ridge, and the fish, and the way Noor held fear by the handle, that made lying feel like tracking mud into a clean house.

"The gate opened for me," I said. "Not because I forced it. Because it recognized something. A threshold opens for what belongs on both sides of it, and I—" I stopped. Started again. "I don't belong in Cindral anymore, Noor. I'm a minor god of doorways and crossroads. When the exile came, the great ones went to the center of the realm and the small ones were pushed to the edges, and the edges are cold. For a hundred years I have stood at the far side of your gate listening to the wind, and when I felt it crack, I didn't come through to save the world. I came through because it was open, and I was lonely, and I am ashamed of how small that reason is."

Noor was quiet for a long time. Below us, the torch-thread crawled on.

"My grandmother's ledger," they said finally, "has a hundred years of entries, and every single one is just *gate holding*. Date, weather, gate holding. Twenty years I've added my own lines. Do you know what I thought when I found the cracks?" They laughed, short and dry. "I thought, *finally, something is happening.* Isn't that obscene? The world might be ending and my first feeling was relief."

"No," I said. "That's not obscene. That's just a person."

They looked at me then—really looked, the way you look at a door you've decided to stop pretending isn't there—and something passed between us that I did not have a name for, in any of my languages, mortal or divine.

"We should move," Noor said. "The shrine's a half-day ahead, and if Vane's outriders find it first, we lose the Shard and possibly our freedom."

"Or our lives."

"Freedom first. Lives are negotiable."

* * *

### Noor

The drowned shrine of Vessa sat in a fold of the mountains where the fog pooled like spilled milk, and I will admit that seeing it again after fifteen years put a lump in my throat that I chose to blame on the altitude.

My grandmother had brought me here once, when I was young enough to still ask questions she answered honestly. She'd stood at the broken archway—stone carved with water-motifs, the goddess Vessa's face worn to a suggestion—and said, "Remember this place, Noor. Remember that even shrines drown."

At the time I'd thought it was a lesson about impermanence. Later I understood it was a warning: *the Crown finds holy places. Never trust a sanctuary to stay hidden.*

Sef stopped dead at the archway. I felt it happen beside me—that particular stillness of his, the one that wasn't human stillness at all.

"You feel it," I said.

"The Shard. It's like hearing your own name shouted from underwater." His voice had changed, dropped some of its performance. "It's frightened."

"Stones get frightened?"

"Shards aren't stones. They're pieces of the covenant itself. Pieces of *us*." He stepped through the archway, and the fog parted around him in a way I pretended not to notice. "Whoever hid it here hid it inside a prayer. Listen."

And I did listen, and beneath the drip of water and the mountain wind there was something else—a low tone, almost subsonic, like a hymn hummed by the ground itself.

We found it in the sunken baptistery at the shrine's heart, where the pool had long since drained to black mud. The First Shard sat in the mud like a coal that refused to die: a splinter of something luminous, gold-white, pulsing faintly in rhythm with that buried hum. Around it, the mud was gray. Dead. I'd seen gray like that before—in Lowmarsh, in the fields where the miracles had touched.

The price, written in the dirt.

Sef knelt at the pool's edge and made a sound I hadn't heard from him before. Not awe. Grief. "Vessa," he said softly. "This was her shrine. She loved this valley. She used to say the rain here fell more politely than anywhere else in Thornfell."

"Then let's not linger at her grave." I pulled the cloth wrapping from my pack and crouched at the pool's edge, reaching—

Hoofbeats.

Not distant. Close. Coming up the switchback below the shrine, more than four this time, and moving fast.

Sef's head snapped up. "How—"

"The fog hides the shrine from above, not the road below. Vane's not sweeping anymore. He's hunting." I grabbed the Shard—it burned cold, a cold like a held breath—and wrapped it, and the moment it left the mud the buried hymn cut off like a throat closed by a hand. Somewhere behind us, in the fog, the ruined archway groaned.

"They know we're here," Sef said.

"They know *something's* here. There's a back way out—my grandmother's maps never showed one entrance to anything." I shoved the wrapped Shard into my pack and looked at him, and he looked at me, and I saw him make the same calculation I was making: run separately and one of us might escape, or run together and neither of us would.

"Together," he said, before I could speak.

"I wasn't going to suggest otherwise."

"You were going to suggest it in a complicated way."

"Move, Sef."

* * *

### Sef

We ran.

I want to record, for whatever eternal ledger keeps such accounts, that I ran until my lungs burned and my legs shook and my vision spotted at the edges, and that Noor ran beside me the entire way without once looking back to check whether I'd fallen—which I understood, finally, was not indifference but respect. Checking would have meant doubting. Noor did not doubt. Noor *decided*, and then the decision became the ground under their feet.

We went down a goat track that I believe goats would have declined, through a streambed, along a ledge barely wider than my shoulders with a drop on the left that went down into cloud. Behind us, voices. Once, a horn—two notes, flat and ugly, repeated twice. Signal code. Vane's people were organized. Righteous men usually are; it's the wicked who improvise.

"There." Noor pointed to a fissure in the rockface, nearly invisible, curtained by hanging moss. "Old mine adit. It goes through the ridge. It's tight."

"How tight?"

"You'll manage."

It was tight. It was insultingly tight. At its narrowest point the stone pressed against my chest on both sides, and I had a moment—darkness, the smell of wet rock and older dust, the weight of an entire mountain resting its elbow on my spine—when I understood something about mortals that a hundred years of exile had never taught me.

They live like this. All the time. Pressed on every side by limits, by hunger, by the simple brutal arithmetic of one body and one life, and they *keep going anyway*. Not because they are strong. Because stopping is not in the grammar of what they are. I had spent a century at the edge of Cindral thinking of mortals as brief creatures, candles compared to our constellations. Crouched in the dark heart of a mountain with my heart slamming like a fist on a door, I understood that I had it backwards. A candle that chooses, every second, to keep burning—that is not lesser than a star. It may be greater. Stars have no choice.

I emerged into grey daylight on the far side of the ridge, filthy, bleeding from both palms, gasping. Noor was waiting, leaning against a boulder, eating an apple they had absolutely not had time to acquire honestly.

"You're alive," they said.

"Barely."

"Mm. Most days end that way." They tossed me the apple core's successor—a whole one, mercifully—and looked back the way we'd come, at the wall of stone between us and the riders. "They'll circle the ridge by nightfall. We've bought maybe six hours."

"Six hours to where?"

Noor reached into the pack and, for the first time since the shrine, unwrapped the edge of the cloth covering the First Shard. Its light spilled out across their fingers, gold-white, steady now, like something that had stopped being afraid. Their face in that light was young and old at once.

"North," they said. "There's a pass the maps call the Widow's Teeth. If we can reach it before Vane, we're beyond his writ—barely, technically, in the way that matters to lawyers and smugglers." They rewrapped the Shard and slung the pack. "And there's something else. When I took it, at the pool—I saw something. Felt it. The covenant's original terms, or a piece of them." They hesitated, and I realized they were deciding whether to tell me, and then decided, visibly, deliberately, the way they did everything. "Both sides broke it, Sef. The gods and the mortals. Nobody innocent. Nobody clean."

I thought of the edges of Cindral. Of the cold. Of the great ones hoarding the warm center. Of Ossuar, eldest, whose silences had grown longer every century, though I had told myself it was wisdom.

"Yes," I said quietly. "I believe you."

And I did. That was the terrible part. Standing in the fog with an apple in my blistered hand and a god's grief sitting in my chest like swallowed stone, I believed every word—and some instinct older than the exile whispered that I had just taken the first step down a road I could not walk back up.

Behind us, faint through the stone, the horn sounded again. Two notes. Twice.

But this time, a third.

Noor went still. "That's not a sweep signal," they said. "That's *found*."

"Not us," I said. "We're over the ridge."

Noor's eyes went north, toward the Widow's Teeth, and I watched them do the arithmetic—the refugees on the hidden roads, the shrine-keepers, everyone who had ever owed my grandmother's memory a kindness and paid it in silence.

"No," they breathed. "It's not us. It's the roads. He's found the roads."

They started running north, and this time I did not ask why. I just ran.
