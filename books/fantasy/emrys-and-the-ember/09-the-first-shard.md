## 9. The First Shard

The shrine of Vessa had been drowning for three hundred years, and it was in no hurry to finish the job.

We came down to it through a sinkhole Sef insisted on calling "the front door," which told me everything about how gods thought of doors. Below us, a bowl of green water held the ruins like a hand holds a coin it's deciding whether to spend: broken colonnades rising out of the flood, a bell tower leaning at an angle that suggested it had given up on standing but not on ringing, and somewhere beneath all that still water, the drowned chapel where the First Ember Shard had been sleeping since before my grandmother learned to walk.

"You said a smuggler's route," I said.

"I said a *route*," Sef said. "I never specified what it was smuggling."

"Water. It's smuggling water into a hole."

"And doing a very thorough job of it." He crouched at the lip of the sinkhole, testing the air the way he did—tasting it, almost, though his mouth didn't move. His burns had faded to something like old candle wax, and he'd stopped flinching when I looked at them, which I chose to read as trust and not as him forgetting to perform being hurt. "The water remembers, Noor. That's why Vessa's shard stayed hidden. Everything that comes down here gets remembered at."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. Reassurance is for people selling things." He glanced back at me, and there was that look again—the one I'd been catching on him since the passes, since the night he'd eaten half my salt pork and then sat very still watching me sleep like a man studying a language he intended to speak someday. "You could wait up here. Let me go down alone."

"If you touch that shard without me, I'll have no way of knowing if you're telling me the truth about anything ever again."

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly why I offered."

Which was either honesty or the most sophisticated manipulation I'd ever been subjected to, and after eight days on smuggler's roads with him—eight days of him marveling at blisters like they were small miracles, of him going silent at every roadside shrine, even the gutted ones—I genuinely could not tell anymore. That, I was beginning to understand, was what it felt like to travel with a god. Not awe. Ambiguity.

* * *

The descent took the better part of the morning. The water came up to our waists, then our ribs, cold enough to make my teeth ache in a way I'd feel for a week. Sef moved through it like it was a rumor about him personally, and I tried not to resent that. I failed.

The chapel doors were bronze gone green, and they stood open—not broken, just open, the way a door stands open when someone left in a hurry or was never asked to close it. Inside, the flooded nave stretched away into gloom, pillars marching down into black water, and above the far altar, impossibly, a skylight had survived: a circle of thick old glass through which the noon light fell in one pale column, lighting nothing but water.

"The shard is under the altar," Sef said quietly. "It always is. Shards are promises, and promises get buried under altars so people stop looking at them and start looking at the altar instead."

"You're full of theology today."

"I'm full of nerves today. Theology is what leaks out."

I swam the last stretch. The altar stone was chest-high even submerged, its face carved with figures I recognized from my grandmother's banned prayer book—the kneeling host, the standing guest, the joined hands between them. The covenant scene. Every child in Thornfell used to know it before the Crown scraped it off the walls. Under the altar's lip there was a recess, and in the recess, something that made the water around it shimmer wrong, the way heat shimmers over a road in summer except this was cold and underwater and *wrong*.

I reached in.

The First Shard was smaller than I'd imagined—a splinter of what must once have been something vast, no bigger than my forearm, dark as cooled iron with a seam of ember-orange running down its length like a vein that hadn't gotten the news that its heart had stopped. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything to do with the gate was warm now; warmth was apparently the universe's way of clearing its throat before saying something terrible.

"Noor." Sef's voice came across the water carefully. "When you take it, it will show you things. Memories aren't stored in shards—they're *caught* by them, the way lint catches on wool. Whoever held it last left their truth behind. You don't have to look."

"Whose truth?"

He hesitated. In eight days I'd learned his hesitations the way I'd learned my grandmother's silences, and this one had a shape I didn't like. "Mine," he said. "Or part of mine. I carried it out of Cindral. It's the reason I'm the one who came through and not someone larger."

"Someone larger," I repeated. "Like Ossuar."

"Don't say that name down here."

"Why not?"

"Because names are also doors," Sef said, "and this place has been listening for a long time."

I took the shard.

* * *

The world went out like a pinched wick.

And then I was standing in a field of grass taller than I was, under a sky the color of a bruise healing, and I knew—without being told, the way you know things in dreams—that this was Cindral, and that it had not yet fallen. The grass sang. Not metaphorically. Each blade hummed a different note, and together they made a chord so patient and enormous that I understood suddenly why mortals had once crossed the gate just to stand in fields and weep.

A god stood in the grass. I couldn't see their face—the memory wouldn't give it to me, or the god wouldn't—but I knew the shape of them the way you know a figure in your own doorway by silhouette. They were holding the shard. This shard. And they were speaking to someone off-memory, someone whose answers I heard only as pressure, as weather.

*"The terms are these,"* the god said, and their voice was tired in a way I recognized from mirrors. *"We keep the land. The rains come when the land needs rain, not when we're bored. The harvests rise. The plagues pass over. And in exchange—"*

The other voice answered, and it was human, and it was furious, and it said: *"In exchange you let us see your ledgers. Every miracle accounted for. Every mercy weighed. If you keep the land out of love, fine. But if you keep it the way a landlord keeps tenants, we will know, and we will say so, and you will listen."*

*"You would audit heaven."*

*"We would keep you honest. That's the whole covenant, isn't it? You keep the land. We keep the gods honest. Neither of us rules alone."*

Silence in the singing grass. Then the god laughed—not cruelly, but with the specific bitterness of someone who has just realized they've agreed to be held accountable forever by creatures who live sixty years.

*"Done,"* the god said. *"Gods keep the land. Mortals keep the gods honest."* And they pressed the shard into mortal hands, and the mortal hands were—

My hands. Older than mine, spotted, scarred at the thumb from a lifetime of ledger work. My grandmother's hands. The memory lurched forward, decades falling away like water off a wheel, and now I was in the Ember Gate chamber the night of the sealing, and my grandmother was thirty years younger and holding this same shard, and she was shouting at someone I couldn't see:

*"You broke it first!"* Her voice cracked on the words. *"Don't you dare stand there and tell me mortals failed the covenant. We kept your ledgers for nine generations. We questioned every miracle. And when we asked too many questions, you stopped answering—and then you started answering the ones who'd stopped asking! You taught them that faith means obedience, and then you punished them for obeying badly—"*

The other voice, when it came, was vast and cracked and wet, like a river grieving. A god who wept. I knew that detail from the gate's memory, from the storm-night vision, and hearing the voice now confirmed it: this was the same one. The weeper. The one who had helped seal the door against their own kind.

*"Not all of us,"* the weeping god said. *"Not all of us stopped listening."*

*"No,"* my grandmother said, and her fury collapsed into something worse, something exhausted. *"Just enough of you. Just enough that the rest couldn't hold the line. That's how covenants die—not with traitors, but with bystanders."* She looked down at the shard in her hands. *"So we defer it. We don't break the covenant—we suspend it. The gate stays shut until both sides remember what they owe. And this stays behind as proof of terms, so nobody gets to pretend the contract said something convenient."*

*"And if neither side remembers?"*

My grandmother was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, I heard the exact weariness I'd inherited along with the ledger and the vigil and the loneliness, passed down like a coat that never quite fit anyone.

*"Then a Lockridge keeps the door anyway,"* she said. *"Until somebody does."*

* * *

I came back to myself underwater, choking, Sef hauling me up by the collar with a strength that had no business living in that borrowed body. I coughed lake water onto the altar steps and knelt there shaking while the skylight's pale column wobbled around us.

"You saw it," Sef said. Not a question.

"I saw it." My voice came out scraped. "Your god-friend in the grass. The one who made the deal. That was—"

"Auditor of Mercies, before the fall. Yes." Sef lowered himself onto a half-submerged pew, water sheeting off him, and for once he didn't perform anything. He just looked tired. "The covenant was my idea, if you want the whole confession. Keep the land, be kept honest. I thought accountability would save us from ourselves." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd seen him wear—genuine all the way down. "Instead it gave Ossuar a grievance with paperwork. Do you know what it does to a god, being told *no*? Being *measured*? Most of them endured it for a century or two. Ossuar endured it for four hundred years and then decided the problem wasn't his conduct. It was the auditors."

"So the gods broke the covenant because mortals kept it too well."

"The eldest did. The rest—" He spread his hands. "Bystanders. Your grandmother had it exactly right. That's the part that should frighten you, Noor. She understood us better than we understood ourselves, and she sealed the door anyway, because understanding isn't the same as trusting."

I sat down on the steps beside him, because my legs had opinions and none of them involved standing. The shard lay in my palm, warm, inert, ordinary-looking as a charred stick. Proof of terms, my grandmother had called it. I turned it over.

"I spent twenty years thinking I was guarding a prison," I said. "Keeping the bad things in. And it's not—it was never a prison. It's a paused negotiation. And I've been sitting on one side of it my whole life, keeping half a promise, while the other side—" I stopped. Started again. "Both sides broke it. That's what the memory says. Both sides. There's no innocent party. There's just everyone abandoning their duties and calling it survival."

"Yes," Sef said simply.

"I hate that."

"I know. It's the worst thing about truth. It doesn't assign villains. It assigns *tasks*."

I laughed, or something near it, and the sound went strange in the drowned chapel, echoing off pillars that had heard prayers for centuries and miracles for none. I closed my hand around the shard. Warmth pulsed against my palm, steady as a second heartbeat, and I thought: *one*. One of three. Two more promises buried under two more altars, and somewhere behind the failing gate, something large and patient and four hundred years aggrieved was pushing at the seams.

"We keep moving," I said. "Second shard. Where is it?"

Sef looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face seemed to settle some question of his own. "North," he said. "High. In the ash country, under a glacier that used to be a god's apology."

"Of course it is."

"And Noor—" He paused, and I braced, because his pauses had shapes and this one was new. "What your grandmother said. About bystanders. I came through the gate because I wanted to finish what I started. But I need you to know that when the reckoning comes, I intend to be neither a traitor nor a bystander. Whatever it costs."

I believed him. That was the alarming part. Eight days of ambiguity, and one drowned memory later, I believed a godling in stolen flesh sitting waist-deep in a forgotten church.

"Good," I said, and stood, and my knees complained, and I ignored them. "Then let's go be accountable."

We waded out through the green water toward the light, and behind us the chapel doors stood open on the dark, the way they had for three hundred years—waiting, like everything else in this story, for someone to finally come back and close them properly.

Above the sinkhole rim, when we climbed out into the grey afternoon, the wind had changed. It came down from the north now, and it smelled wrong—smoke where there should be pine, ash where there should be rain. Sef tasted it and went very still.

"What," I said.

"There's fire in Lowmarsh," he said. "Or there will be by tomorrow."

And the shard in my pocket pulsed once, hard, like a heart that had just heard bad news.
