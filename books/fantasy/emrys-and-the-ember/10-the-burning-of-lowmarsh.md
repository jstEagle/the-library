## 10. The Burning of Lowmarsh

I had been in Lowmarsh four days before I understood that the village hated me less than it feared me, and that both were a kind of courtesy. In the capital, people spat when an inquisitor passed. Here they simply went quiet, the way a house goes quiet when the doctor's cart stops at the gate.

"Another one," said Corporal Hesk, from the doorway of the shrine district's eastern granary. He was a good soldier, Hesk—dull as a mallet, which is the highest praise I can give a man whose job is to stand where I point. "Third gray field this week."

The field in question lay beyond the granary's loading yard, a half-acre of barley that had gone the color of old ash. Not burned—grayed. The stalks still stood. They simply no longer agreed to be barley. When I ran my gloved hand along them, they crumbled like bread left out through a long winter, and the dust smelled faintly of candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a church.

Miracle rot, the villagers were calling it, when they called it anything at all. They said it the way you'd say a neighbor's name—familiar, resentful, unwilling to stop saying it.

"Mark it," I told Hesk. "Rope off the well too. If the blight's in the soil, it'll be in the water."

"Yes, sir." He hesitated. "The miller's wife says her boy's cough cleared up Tuesday. After she prayed. At the—" He stopped himself, which was wise. At the shrine. At the outlawed shrine, in the district I was here to purge, under the nose of the Crown's most methodical servant.

"After she prayed," I finished for him. "To whom?"

He looked at his boots. Everyone in Lowmarsh looked at their boots around me. It was not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the look of people who knew something true and hoped I wouldn't make them say it, because saying it would make it real, and real things got people hanged.

"To the Drowned Lady," he said finally. "Sir."

* * *

You should understand something about me, since history will likely get it wrong if it bothers to record me at all. History likes its inquisitors either monsters or martyrs, and I have never managed to be either. I am a man who reads documents. I count things. I believe that the world is governed by laws—grain yields, water tables, the weight of evidence—and that every exception to those laws is a debt someone will eventually be made to pay.

I also believed, once, that prayer worked.

My son Tam was seven when the fever took him, and I prayed. Understand: I did not pray the way a man prays at a funeral, mechanically, hedging his bets with heaven. I prayed the way I do everything. Methodically. I fasted. I memorized the intercessions of Saint Aldous, all nine hours of them, and recited them on schedule for eleven days while my wife sat by the bed holding our son's hand and I stood in the corner being *useful*, because usefulness is the only form of love I have ever been fluent in.

On the twelfth day a traveling friar came through our town, one of the new charismatic sort, the ones who'd sprung up after the Ban like mushrooms after rain, promising that faith could reach even the sealed heavens. He laid hands on Tam. He shouted. The boy convulsed, rallied—we thought, we *thought*—and died three days later, and the friar was gone by then, two towns over, doing it again to somebody else's child.

So when I tell you that answered prayers are the most dangerous thing loose in Thornfell, I am not speaking doctrine. Doctrine is what I give other men. What I know is this: hope is a loan shark, and it always comes to collect, and it does not care that you are seven years old.

The gods returning would have been bad enough. But these were not the gods returning. These were *answers* returning, small mercies falling out of a locked sky like coins from a torn pocket, and every one of them costing more than it gave. A child pulled breathing from the marsh. A fever broken. A lame ox walking. And behind each miracle, a field going gray, a well going strange, a shadow spreading outward like ink through wet cloth.

Someone was paying for these mercies, and it wasn't the ones receiving them.

* * *

The granaries caught fire on the fifth night.

I was awake—I was always awake, in those days; sleep had become a negotiation I kept losing—and so I saw the first light before I heard anything. Orange, low on the horizon, in the wrong place. The shrine district sits east of Lowmarsh proper, down toward the water, and the granaries sit between the shrine and the village like a wall of dry timber that some fool had stacked directly against a lit match.

Which, it turned out, is precisely what had happened.

By the time I reached the eastern yard, the first granary was fully alight, and the heat came off it in waves you could lean into, and the whole district had turned out with buckets and chains and that particular animal silence of people working a fire they already know they've lost. The miller was there. The miller's wife was there, the one whose son's cough had cleared, and she was screaming—not about the fire, I realized. About the shrine. Because the fire had started *there*, in the little whitewashed building where the Drowned Lady's image stood in a niche above the tide line, and the flames had jumped the gap to the granary roof like it was nothing, like a thing crossing a threshold.

A thing crossing a threshold. I noted the phrase even then. I note everything. It is my gift and my affliction.

"Who lit it?" I asked, and a dozen faces turned toward me, firelit, and for a moment nobody spoke, and I understood that the answer was going to be worse than arson.

"It weren't lit," said the miller. His face was black with smoke and wet with sweat. "Inquisitor, I swear on my mother's grave, there was light *in* the shrine. Blue light. Like the bottom of a flame. And then—" He gestured helplessly at the burning roof. "Then it was this."

Blue light. The bottom of a flame. I wrote it down later, standing in the yard while the granary collapsed inward with a sound like a felled tree, and my hand shook writing it, because I recognized it. Every inquisitor recognizes it. It's in the old seizure reports from the war, the ones we're taught to dismiss as superstition: *the presence manifests as cold blue radiance.* The Ban didn't kill the old gods. It just closed the door. And doors, apparently, had developed opinions.

Three hundred tons of winter grain burned that night. Lowmarsh would not see spring without relief shipments, and relief shipments require writs, and writs require explanations, and explanations require someone to blame.

The village needed someone to blame. So did I.

That is the honest arithmetic of what happened next, and I set it down here because no one else will. Grief wants a target the way fire wants air. Give it one and it roars. Deny it and it eats you from the inside, quietly, over years, until one day your wife tells you she's leaving because living with you is like living with a sealed room.

I did not give grief a target that night. I want that recorded. What I did was worse. I gave it a *procedure*.

* * *

At dawn, with the granaries still smoking and the eastern sky the color of dirty wool, I convened the purge.

Not a mob. I want to be clear about that, because mobs are what happen when men abandon order, and whatever my failures, I never abandoned order. I drew up the writ myself, sitting on a barrel in the smoking yard, using the miller's table because my own quarters stank of ash. *By authority of the Crown and the Office of Inquiry, all structures within the shrine district having been consecrated to proscribed worship are hereby declared contaminated foci of miraculous contagion, and shall be rendered uninhabitable for the practice thereof.*

Contaminated foci of miraculous contagion. I have written a great many sentences I regret. That is not one of them. It was accurate. Whatever had come through—or reached through, or leaned through—the barrier between Cindral and Thornfell had touched that shrine, and everything it touched grayed and crumbled, and grain stores were not the only things in Lowmarsh that could burn.

"The families," said Sergeant Ovey, reading over my shoulder. She was newer than Hesk, sharper than Hesk, and she asked questions, which is either a virtue or a vice depending on how long you intend to survive. "The shrine-keepers. There's a widow down there, Marrow or Maren, keeps the Drowned Lady's lamp. And children, some of them. You're not proposing—"

"I am proposing demolition," I said, "of consecrated structures. Persons will be evacuated to the chapel green pending inquiry. This is not Vell's Landing, Sergeant. We are not burning people."

"No, sir." She didn't move. "And the images? The icons? Because the last time the office purged a district, the soldiers took souvenirs, and half of them were dead of dreams inside a month."

"Nothing leaves the district," I said. "Everything burns. Icons, vestments, records, lamps. Ash goes into the marsh." I looked up at her. "You think I enjoy this?"

"I think," said Ovey, with the terrible honesty of junior officers, "that you lost someone, sir. Everyone knows. And I think you're about to make a whole village pay the interest."

If she had been a man I might have struck her. If she had been wrong, I might have thanked her. She was neither, quite, and so I did the thing I do instead of feeling, which is to issue orders.

"Form the cordon," I said. "Begin at the tide line. Move west."

* * *

Here is what I remember of the purge itself, in the order my memory insists on giving it, which is not chronological, because memory is a coward and rearranges things to spare itself.

I remember the widow Maren standing in the door of the shrine with the lamp still lit in her hands, refusing to leave, and the blue light—that same blue, the bottom-of-the-flame blue—reflected in her eyes, and her saying, not to me, to the air, to whoever was listening: *"You promised you'd hear us now. You promised."*

I remember thinking: yes. Someone promised. Someone always promises. And the promises are always true right up until the moment they cost you everything, and then they turn out to have been true in a way you didn't agree to.

I remember a child's shoe in the mud outside the shrine, small, buckled, abandoned in the evacuation, and stopping dead in front of it for what felt like a full minute while my cordon waited, because Tam had shoes like that once, and I had kept them. I kept them for nineteen years. My wife found them when she was packing to leave and said nothing, which was the cruelest kindness available to her.

I remember the shrine burning blue at its heart and orange at its edges, both fires at once, the god-fire and the ordinary fire, and the ordinary fire winning, because that is the whole of my creed in a single image: the ordinary fire always wins, and the only mercy is to be the one who controls the burning rather than the one burned.

I remember Hesk coming to me at midday, gray-faced, reporting that the gray blight had stopped spreading. Stopped. Dead. The fields beyond the cordon were clean, the wells ran clear, and the miller's wife's son—her healthy, miracle-cured son—had been evacuated with the rest and showed no sign of contamination whatsoever.

The purge had worked. That was the horror of it. The procedure functioned. Burn the foci, scatter the faithful, quarantine the touched, and the contagion halts. I had done the correct thing, by every measure I trust, and the correct thing had produced a village of homeless believers camped on a chapel green in the ash-fall, staring at me with eyes that held no hatred at all, which was infinitely worse than hatred, because hatred would have meant they still thought I could be argued with.

They looked at me the way you look at weather.

* * *

Ovey found me at dusk, sitting in the ruins of the eastern granary with my ledger open and nothing written in it. The grain had burned down to a black glass that cracked underfoot like lake ice.

"You should eat, sir."

"I should do a great many things." I turned the ledger toward her. "Tell me what you see."

She looked. Blank pages. "Nothing, sir."

"Precisely. Four days of investigation, a documented miracle, a documented blight, a fire of supernatural origin, and a successful containment operation, and I cannot write a single line of it. Because every explanation ends at the same impossible sentence." I tapped the page. "*Something answered them.* The Ban holds, Sergeant. The gate is sealed. It has been sealed for a century, sealed by wardens of the Lockridge line, sealed so thoroughly that the Crown forgot the wardens existed. And yet something on the other side is passing notes through the lock."

"You think it's the gate, then. Not the shrines."

"I think the shrines are where the letters get delivered. The gate is where they're posted." I closed the ledger. "There's a warden in these mountains. There has always been a warden. Find me the records—land grants, tithes, anything with the name Lockridge on it older than fifty years. Quietly."

"And the refugees?"

"What about them?"

"Chapel green's overflowing. Winter's coming. Some of them are saying—" She glanced back toward the village, lowered her voice. "Some of them are saying the Drowned Lady appeared to the widow Maren in the fire. Said she was sorry. Said she couldn't hold the door much longer."

The wind moved through the burnt granary, and the black glass sang under it, a thin high note like a struck wire.

"Hold the door," I repeated.

"Yes, sir."

I had spent twenty years telling myself that the heavens were empty and the world was law, and both beliefs had been built on the same foundation: that the door was shut. Take away the shut door and everything else I believed became a matter of *when*, not *whether*. When the answers stopped being small mercies. When the thing on the other side stopped sending notes and started sending itself.

"Double the watch," I said. "And Sergeant—if any stranger passes through asking about the mountains, the passes, or the name Lockridge, I want to know within the hour."

She frowned. "Expecting someone, sir?"

"No," I said. "But doors that open for prayers open for other things too. And something out there has been very busy teaching this village to knock."

Far off in the hills, thunder rolled, though the sky was clear. Or perhaps it wasn't thunder. I have learned, at terrible expense, to distinguish the sounds a world makes when it is merely being a world from the sounds it makes when something is pushing on it from underneath.

That sound was the second kind.

I opened my ledger, and beneath four days of blank pages, I wrote the first line of the report that would follow me for the rest of my life:

*The gate is failing. Find the keeper.*
