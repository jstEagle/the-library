## 11. Refugees on the Ridge

The first sign of trouble was smoke in the wrong direction.

I'd spent twenty years learning the moods of that horizon the way other people learn the faces of their children. Smoke from Lowmarsh rose thin and domestic at dawn—bread ovens, forge fires, the ordinary exhalations of a village going about its business. This was different. This was a fat black column leaning east on the wind, and beneath it, faint as a bad memory, the smell of burning grain.

Granaries. Someone was burning granaries.

I stood on the ridge above the Ember Gate with a ledger under my arm and a cup of tea going cold in my hand, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing for weeks now: Vane had arrived in Lowmarsh in autumn. The blight had spread through the shrine district by first frost. And now, with winter closing its fist around the passes, the Crown's inquisitors were burning food.

You don't burn grain unless you've decided the people who eat it are the problem.

"Right," I said to the tea, because saying it to anyone else would have required an audience, and my audience for two decades had been a gate, a ledger, and a godling who was currently asleep in my cottage with his boots on my good chair. "Right."

* * *

They started coming up the smuggler's roads at dusk.

I should explain the roads, since I built them—or rather, my grandmother built them, and I maintained them, which is a distinction that mattered a great deal to her and very little to anyone else. The Lockridge routes ran through Thornfell like veins through a leaf: goat paths widened just enough for a loaded mule, cairns stacked in patterns that looked like erosion to anyone who didn't know the code, way-stations tucked into hollows and old quarry cuts. They existed so that goods could move without the Crown's tax collectors developing opinions. Brandy, mostly. Salt. Occasionally people, when people needed moving quietly.

My grandmother had kept the roads for the same reason she'd kept the gate: because someone had to, and because telling anyone would have meant trusting anyone, and Yarrows did not trust. We hid things. It was practically our family crest—a lock, a keyhole, and a motto nobody could read because we'd never told anyone what language it was in.

So when I crested the ridge above the first way-station and saw firelight where there should have been darkness, my first instinct—the instinct twenty years of solitude had polished into something almost religious—was *they've found the roads.*

My second instinct arrived about half a heartbeat later, carried on a sound I'll hear until I die: a child crying. Not one child. Several. The particular ragged, exhausted crying of children who have been walking too long and have passed beyond comfort into pure animal misery.

I came down into the hollow with my hand on my knife and found thirty-odd people crammed into a space built for six mules and a dishonest amount of brandy. Lowmarsh faces. I knew some of them. The miller's widow, Tam Reeve, whose left arm had been crushed by his own cart wheel and bound in splints made from a broken shovel handle. Old Marta Cade, who sold eggs and had once accused me of witchcraft over the price of butter, now sitting gray-faced against the wall with her grandson asleep in her lap.

A woman stepped forward—a stranger to me, tall, soot-streaked, holding a billhook like she knew how to use it. Her eyes went from my face to my knife and back.

"We saw your cairns," she said. "The marks on them. A man in Lowmarsh used to run brandy over these paths, before all this. He told us if it ever went wrong, follow the double-stack stones." She swallowed. "Please. The inquisitors burned the shrine quarter. Then they burned the granaries. They said—" Her voice caught and steadied itself with visible effort. "They said anyone who'd prayed to the old gods was carrying the blight in their blood. That mercy to the infected was treason."

Behind her, thirty faces turned toward me. In the firelight they looked like something out of a painting of a famine: hollow-cheeked, ash-gray, waiting.

And here is what I want you to understand about the next ten seconds, because I have relived them often enough to have opinions. Every part of me that had survived twenty years of keeping secrets screamed the same sermon. *These people are followed. These people are frightened, and frightened people talk. Every mouth you feed is a mouth that can be persuaded, tortured, or simply careless. The gate is four miles north. The mission is everything. You are the keeper. Keep.*

It was a good sermon. I'd preached it to myself for two decades and never once been tempted to disagree.

But the child crying in Marta Cade's lap had stopped crying, finally, because she'd fallen asleep mid-sob, and her small fist was closed around her grandmother's shawl like the shawl was the last true thing in the world. And Tam Reeve was looking at his splinted arm with the expression of a man doing sums he already knew would come out badly. And the woman with the billhook hadn't lowered it, because she was protecting these people the only way she had left, and I recognized that posture. I'd been standing in it for twenty years, facing a different direction.

"Put down the billhook," I said. "There's a spring behind the station and barley in the third cache. You can stay tonight."

The billhook didn't come down. "Tonight?"

"I need to check the upper paths," I said, which was true, and also a coward's phrasing, and we both knew it. "If riders come—and they may—you scatter along the west fork, double-stack cairns, all the way to the river. Don't stop. Don't look back."

"And after tonight?" the woman asked.

I looked at her. I looked past her, at all of them. Somewhere in that crowd was every neighbor I'd ever shortchanged with a smooth answer, every person I'd lied to about the mountains, about the storms, about why no one was allowed up the high trails in autumn. Twenty years of bricks, and here they all were, standing in front of the wall I'd built out of them.

"After tonight," I heard myself say, "we talk."

* * *

Sef was awake when I got home, sitting in my chair with his boots—of course—on my table, reading my grandmother's prayer book upside down, which he claimed was how it was meant to be read and which I had stopped arguing about sometime in the second month.

"You're limping," he said.

"I'm walking decisively."

"You're limping *decisively.*" He closed the book. He'd gotten better at reading me; it was one of the many things about him I found inconvenient. "How many?"

"Thirty-one. Maybe more coming. The whole shrine quarter of Lowmarsh is ash, Sef. Vane burned the granaries." I pulled off my boots and immediately regretted having opinions about his. "He's calling it containment. The blight follows answered prayers, so he's going to starve the prayers out of them. Starve the *prayers* out of—gods, listen to me, I sound like him."

"You sound like someone who hasn't slept." Sef unfolded himself from the chair. For a fugitive godling in stolen boots, he managed a remarkable amount of gravity. "Noor. Say the thing you're circling."

"The thing I'm circling," I said, "is that there are thirty-one people in my way-station, there will be a hundred by week's end, and the gate is four miles from the nearest of them. Four miles, Sef. One bad day. One traitor. One refugee with frostbite and a loose tongue and an inquisitor's offer of warm bread." I sat down heavily. "Every plan I ever made assumed the gate stayed secret. The secret is dead. I'm just deciding whether to attend the funeral or pretend it's still breathing."

He was quiet for a moment. Sef's silences had texture; I'd learned to read those too, another inconvenience.

"You could move them," he offered. "Push them downriver, off your roads, let them be the Crown's problem."

"Yes."

"You're not going to."

"No."

"Because?"

I laughed, and it came out wrong, somewhere between a laugh and something else. "Because Marta Cade once called me a witch over butter prices and today she sat in the cold holding her grandson so he could sleep, and she never once asked what was in it for her. Because Tam Reeve's arm is broken and he walked eleven miles anyway. Because I have spent twenty years being the invisible woman on the ridge, keeping a vigil nobody knows matters, and I told myself it was duty, but Sef—it was cowardice wearing duty's coat. I could have had neighbors. I chose a ledger." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "And now the world is ending, and it turns out I'd rather be betrayed by people I know than safe among secrets."

When I took my hands away, Sef was watching me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Reverence, maybe. Or pity. With gods it's often hard to tell the difference.

"There's a version of this," he said carefully, "where you tell them. All of it. The gate, the covenant, the shards. Where you stop being the smuggler's ghost on the ridge and become—" he gestured vaguely, "—the point."

"They'll panic. Or worse, they'll help. Do you know what happens when frightened people help? They form committees."

"Yes. Terrifying. And yet." He leaned forward. "Ossuar is coming, Noor. Not maybe. Coming. When he comes, every soul on this ridge will be standing between him and everything they love whether they know it or not. The only question is whether they'll know which door they're guarding."

I thought about my grandmother, sealing the gate beside a weeping god, and burying the promise so deep that her own granddaughter grew up thinking the family business was brandy. Three generations of silence, and where had it gotten us? A cracked gate, a rising god, and a village full of ash.

"Tomorrow," I said. "At dawn. Ring the bell at the middle station—the big one, the one I told everyone was for rockslides." I looked at my hands, keeper's hands, scarred and unremarkable. "Time to find out what a community weighs."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and cold, and they gathered.

Not all of them—some slept, some tended fires, some were simply too far gone—but most. Thirty-one became forty overnight; three families had come up the west fork in the dark, following the double-stack stones, and word traveled the way it does among the desperate, fast and sideways. They stood in the trampled snow of the middle station's yard, breath steaming, faces turned toward the little stone platform where I'd rung the bell, and I stood on it feeling like a fraud in my own story.

I had rehearsed speeches on the walk over. I'd abandoned all of them by the second sentence.

"My name is Noor Lockridge," I said. "Some of you know me as the brandy-runner. Some of you know me as the woman who won't meet your eye at market. Both of those people were lies I built to hide a third one, and I'm done hiding, because hiding is what got us here."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Four miles north of here there is a gate. Not a wall-gate, not a border post. The Ember Gate—the last door between this world and the one where the old gods were banished, a hundred years ago. My grandmother helped seal it. My family has kept it ever since, alone, in secret, because we believed the secret was the only thing keeping both worlds safe." I held up my hands, palms out, the way you do when you have nothing left to offer but the truth. "The seal is failing. Something on the other side is pushing. The miracles in Lowmarsh, the blight, the answered prayers—all of it comes through that gate, and all of it is my fault, because I kept it, and I kept it badly, and I kept it *alone*, because alone was easier."

Tam Reeve spoke first, of all people, his splinted arm cradled like a sleeping cat. "The inquisitor said the old gods sent the blight."

"The inquisitor says a lot of things. Some of them are even true. The oldest of the gods has been devouring the others to force the gate open—that much is real. But the blight isn't malice. It's leakage. It's what happens when a door that was built to be held shut starts to come apart." I drew a breath. "There's a way to fix it. Reforge the covenant. It will cost more than I can describe, and I'll ask it of no one. But I'm asking you to know. To know what's on this ridge, and what's coming for it, and to choose—with open eyes—whether you stand here or walk down to the river and take your chances with the Crown."

I expected fear. I expected anger—I'd earned some, lying to these people for years while they paid taxes to protect a gate they'd never heard of.

What I got was Marta Cade, pushing forward through the crowd with her grandson on her hip, looking up at me with the same flinty expression she'd once aimed at me over butter.

"Took you long enough," she said.

Someone laughed—a wet, exhausted laugh—and then someone else, and then it moved through the crowd like warmth through cold hands. Not joy. Nothing so simple. But something adjacent to it, something that lives next door to grief and shares a wall.

The woman with the billhook—her name was Corren, I learned later—stepped forward and planted the billhook in the snow like a standard. "We were dead in Lowmarsh," she said. "You gave us roads. Now you give us the truth. What do you need?"

What did I need. I looked out at them—forty-some souls, hungry, hunted, armed with farm tools and fury—and felt something crack open in my chest that had been sealed longer than the gate.

"I need watchers on every pass," I said. "I need the caches opened and the stores shared fairly. I need runners who know the cairn-codes. And I need—" my voice caught, and I let it, "—I need to not be the only one anymore. That's the truth. That's the whole ugly prayer of it."

Marta Cade shifted her grandson to her other hip. "Then start giving orders, Keeper. Winter's coming, and so is whatever else you said."

And that was how, at the worst possible moment, in the worst possible weather, with the worst possible odds, I finally got what I'd wanted for twenty years.

Belonging. Chosen at last, at the end of the world.

We worked until dark. By the time the fires were banked and the watch rotations set, my voice was gone and my ledger had a new section, written in a hand that shook only a little: names. Forty-three of them. People, not assets. The first page of a book I apparently hadn't known I was writing.

I was still awake at midnight, when the runner came down from the north pass at a dead sprint, snow flying, shouting my name across the frozen yard.

"Noor! Noor Lockridge!" She skidded to a stop in front of me, chest heaving, and pointed back the way she'd come, toward the high dark line of the ridge. "Lights. On the mountain road. Torches—a column of them, a mile long, maybe more."

My blood went to ice water. "Inquisitors?"

She shook her head, and her face, in the firelight, was the face of someone who had seen something that rearranged the furniture of the world.

"No," she said. "They're not soldiers. They're pilgrims. Hundreds of them. And they're singing."
