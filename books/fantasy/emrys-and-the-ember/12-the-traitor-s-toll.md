## 12. The Traitor's Toll

I have been a god for eleven hundred years, and I have been mortal for six weeks, and I can tell you which one is harder.

As a god, I never had to decide anything. Decisions were made above me—by Ossuar, by the Concord of Nine, by the slow grinding weight of heaven's etiquette. I was Sef of the Doorways, keeper of thresholds, opener of small gates and closer of smaller ones. My opinions were solicited exactly once in a millennium, and that was about hinges. Mortality, it turns out, is nothing but decisions. What to eat. When to sleep. Whether to tell the woman who saved your life that you are the very thing she has spent her whole life keeping out.

And now: whether to stop a widow from betraying us all.

She was called Maren Ostrey, though at the time I knew her only as the woman with the two children who slept pressed together like bookends. She had come up the hidden roads with the third wave of Lowmarsh refugees, after Vane burned the shrine district, after the granaries went up like festival lanterns. Her husband had kept a shrine to Vessa before shrines became arrests. She wore his amulet under her collar and touched it when she thought no one was looking, which is how I learned to read her, because gods notice devotion the way moths notice lamps.

Noor had given her a place by the fire. Noor gave everyone a place by the fire; it was infuriating, watching them spend their secrecy like a drunkard spends coin, handing out shelter to strangers on roads that existed precisely because no one knew them. But Noor had made their choice on the ridge, loudly and irreversibly, and so we were forty-one souls in the high passes instead of one keeper and one fugitive godling, and every one of those souls was a door left open in winter.

Maren Ostrey left the camp on the ninth night.

I want to be fair to her. Fairness is a new habit of mine—I acquired it around the same time as hunger—and I find it fits poorly but I keep wearing it. She did not leave because she was wicked. She left because an inquisitor's rider had found her at the treeline three days earlier, alone, filling the water skins, and had not arrested her. He had simply talked to her. Asked after her health. Mentioned, in the gentle way of men carrying warrants, that her children were young to be sleeping in the cold, and that the Crown maintained excellent orphanages, warm ones, and that warm things could be arranged for mothers who were helpful.

He let her go. That was the trap entire. A rope you feel is just a rope; a rope you don't feel is a leash.

I followed her down through the pines because I had seen her touch the amulet too often and look south too long, and because being a god of thresholds means I know what it looks like when someone is working up the courage to cross one. She moved badly, mortal-badly, catching her hem and swearing in whispers, and I moved worse, because my borrowed body still resented hills, and between the two of us we made enough noise to wake every owl on the mountain. Neither of us noticed. We were both listening for other things.

She met the rider at the old charcoal burner's clearing, where the stumps stood pale as teeth in the moonlight. He had dismounted. He had even brought bread—a small loaf, wrapped in cloth, the kind of detail Vane's people were trained to provide. Kindness as instrument. I had watched Vane himself do it once, in Lowmarsh, pressing coins into a grieving man's palm while his clerks wrote down everything the man said. The Crown's cruelty was never cruel in the moment. It was patient. It waited until you came to it.

"Mistress Ostrey." The rider's voice carried in the cold. "You've thought about our conversation."

"I want my children warm." Her voice was flat as slate. "That's all. I'm not selling anyone. I'm buying them."

"Of course." He unwrapped the bread and held it out, and I watched her hands shake as she took it, and I understood something then that I have spent eleven centuries failing to understand about mortals: that shame does not prevent betrayal. It attends it. She hated herself thoroughly, completely, with the whole of her heart, and she did it anyway, because her children were cold, and there is no theology on any side of any gate that survives contact with children who are cold.

"The road," the rider said. "The one your host uses. Where does it come out?"

"You know where it comes out. You've known since Lowmarsh." Bitterness sharpened her. Good, I thought, absurdly. Be angry. Anger will cost you less than pleading. "You want the gate. Everyone wants the gate."

"The gate," he agreed pleasantly. "Or the person who keeps it. Either would do."

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about myself, so that what follows makes sense: I was not, among the Nine-and-Their-Leavings, known for mercy. I was known for efficiency. When Cindral fell into its long quarrel and the oaths broke like dropped crockery, I voted—for the only second time in my existence—to seal the gate hard and let the mortals learn to pray to weather. Let them forget us, I said. Forgetting is a mercy. Memory is the wound that never closes.

Ossuar had looked at me with those eyes like banked coals and said, gently, the way you speak to something small: *Little Sef. Doors exist to be opened.*

I believed him for a thousand years. I crossed a failing gate on his word, burned half to cinder in the passage, crawled down a mountain on his errand, and told myself every mile that I was the vanguard of restoration, the herald of the covenant renewed. It took me six weeks of mortality—six weeks of Noor's bad tea and careful silences—to understand that I had been sent through a keyhole as a hook through a fish's mouth. Chapter fifteen of my own story, if you like, though I didn't know it yet that night in the clearing. That night I still believed in the cause. I believed in it the way Maren Ostrey believed in orphanages: because believing hurt less than the alternative.

So when I stepped out from the treeline, I stepped out as a believer. As a godling with a mission and a keeper to protect and every justification heaven ever printed.

"Sef." Maren's breath went out of her. She had guessed, of course. They all half-guessed; I was too strange, too watchful, too unscarred for a smuggler. "Sef, please—"

"Don't." I raised a hand, and I confess I let a little of the old fire show through my skin, just at the fingertips, just enough. It felt obscene, using divinity as a threat against a woman holding bread. "You're going to give him the road."

"I'm going to give him nothing but what he already has," she said, and her chin came up, and I saw her husband's shrine-keeping stubbornness in it, dead man's courage living briefly in his widow. "He'll take the children either way if he wants them. This way they're fed while he decides."

"That's not—" The rider began, and stopped, because I turned to look at him, and whatever he saw made him reconsider the sentence. Smart man. Vane recruited smart men; it was his tragedy and his virtue both.

"Here is what happens now," I said, and I had the whole speech ready, the one I'd composed on the walk down, all fire and thunder. *You will forget this road. You will forget this camp. You will carry back a lie so seamless that Vane himself will sign it.* I had rehearsed it. I had given it rhetorical flourishes. I am, or was, a god of doorways, and a doorway's oldest trick is showing you a different room than the one you're standing in.

Instead I heard myself say: "Why?"

The rider blinked. So did Maren. So, honestly, did I.

"Why sell the road," I said, "when you could sell me? You know what I am—or near enough. One godling, delivered alive to the Inquisition. That buys more than bread, mistress. That buys pardons. Land. Names cleared. Your husband's shrine re-consecrated by the Crown itself, with his name spoken kindly in the record." I took another step into the moonlight. "I'm worth ten gates. And unlike the gate, I'll follow you home."

It was, I want it noted, a genuine offer. That is the part I still cannot explain cleanly. Some cold arithmetic in me had done the sum—my capture versus forty lives—and found the trade acceptable, and found, moreover, that offering myself would spare Noor the horror of Maren's choice and spare Maren the horror of her own. Two birds, one stone, and the stone was me. Very tidy. Very divine. Ossuar would have approved of the economy of it.

"No," Maren said.

The rider shifted. "Mistress—"

"He doesn't understand," she said, and her voice cracked finally, the slate splitting. "You don't understand, any of you shining things. I watched my husband die for keeping a shrine. They broke his hands first, so he couldn't make the signs, and then they asked him questions, and he answered them, because broken hands break everything eventually. And I swore on his grave that my children would never kneel to anyone—not the Crown, not the gods, not *anything*—that they'd grow up stupid and safe and free." She was crying now, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like the tears were an insult. "And here you are, offering to be sold, like it's simple, like it's *kind*, and maybe it is, maybe you mean it—but if I hand one holy thing to the fires to save my own, then everything he died for was true, wasn't it? That we're all just goods. That's what they believe. That's what *you'd* be proving."

The clearing was very quiet. Somewhere downslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"And the road?" I asked softly. "The gate? Forty people asleep up that hill, who opened their fires to you?"

"I haven't told him anything yet." Her jaw set. "Have I, sir?"

The rider said nothing, which was its own confession. Vane's men were trained to wait for the goods before naming the price.

"So here's my toll," Maren Ostrey said, and she looked at me with red-rimmed eyes and the ghost of her husband's stubbornness, "and you'll pay it or you'll stop me, godling, and either way I'll live with it, because that's what the living do. You let me walk back up that hill with my bread and my children, and I never say one word to anyone about what I saw tonight. Not the road, not the camp, not you. You buy my silence with your trust. That's the price. Trust from a traitor—that's the most expensive thing there is, and it's the only thing I can't steal."

* * *

I have refused temptations before. Eleven hundred years offers practice. But this was not temptation; this was the opposite, a temptation *inverted*—every instinct I had, divine and newly mortal alike, screamed to bind her memory, to put fear in her like a splint in a broken limb, to do *anything* rather than walk away from a known leak and trust it to heal.

Because that is the corrupting logic, and I want to write it plainly so that whoever reads this ledger understands what was actually at stake in that clearing: necessity always dresses as mercy. Bind her mind, and call it protection. Take her children hostage to her silence, and call it insurance. Give myself up, and call it sacrifice. Every road led somewhere defensible. That is how you know you are lost—when all directions have good reasons.

Noor would have understood instantly. Noor, who lied to inquisitors with a smooth face and hated themselves for the skill; Noor, who counted every lie like bricks in a wall between them and their neighbors. Noor would have seen Maren Ostrey and thought: *there but for a grandmother's oath go I.*

But Noor wasn't there. That was the point of the test, if tests have points, and I was beginning to suspect that mortality was nothing *but* tests with no examiner and no passing grade, only consequences.

"Go back up the hill," I said.

Maren stared at me.

"Take your bread. Wake your children if they've noticed you gone—mothers always think children sleep deeper than they do; mine certainly didn't. Go back, and tomorrow, tell Noor Lockridge everything. Everything you saw tonight, everything you almost did. Tell them yourself, before the telling can be used against you." I held her gaze. "That's my toll. Not your silence. Your honesty. Silence is just a locked room, mistress, and locked rooms fester. Tell Noor, and let them decide what I would have decided, except they'll decide it better, because they're better at being people than I am. I'm new."

"And if Noor turns me out?" Her voice was small. "Into the snow, with the children?"

"Then I'll argue with them," I said. "I'm told I'm good at it. I argued with heaven once. Well—once and a half."

She laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and cracked, and it was also the first honest thing exchanged in that clearing all night. Then she looked at the rider, and something passed over her face—the last of the shame, perhaps, or the first of the resolve—and she said, "You can tell the Inquisitor the road died in the snow. Landslides. Ask your maps; the passes eat roads every winter." And she walked past me without touching me, which was its own kind of courtesy, and went up the hill toward her children, and I stood in the moonlight and shook, afterward, the way bodies do when they have held something a long time and finally set it down.

The rider hadn't moved. He was watching me with the particular stillness of a man rewriting his report in his head.

"You're real," he said. "The godling. Vane thinks you're a story the keeper tells."

"Vane is right about most things," I said. "Tell him that. Tell him the story eats well and sleeps poorly and has decided, provisionally, to be merciful, and see what he makes of it." I paused. "And tell him—" and here I nearly stopped, because the next part was dangerous, was possibly the most dangerous sentence I had ever spoken, more dangerous than any I'd uttered in Cindral's courts. "Tell him the gate remembers him. Tell him the covenant sees the difference between a man who burns shrines and a man who feeds widows first and asks questions second. Tell him the difference is noted."

He rode away fast. Men always ride away fast from sentences like that.

* * *

Noor was waiting at the camp's edge when I came back up, arms crossed against the cold, wearing the expression they reserved for me—the one that meant *I know you did something, and I know you'll tell me, and I already regret it.*

"Maren woke her children," Noor said. "Sat them by the fire. Fed them the bread." A beat. "Inquisitorial bread, Sef. I know the wrapping."

"She bought it with truth," I said. "Mostly hers." And I sat down on the cold stone beside them, mortal-knees complaining, and told them all of it—the rider, the clearing, the offer I'd made of myself, the toll I'd named instead. All of it, because I had learned that much from Maren Ostrey: locked rooms fester.

Noor listened without moving. When I finished, they were quiet for a long moment, watching the fire where Maren sat with her children, and then they said, in a voice carefully sanded flat:

"You let her go. You caught a traitor with the evidence in her hands, and you let her go, and your price was that she confess to *me*."

"Yes."

"Do you know what Vane will do with 'the road died in a landslide'?" Noor's voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "He won't believe it. He'll send sappers anyway. He'll map every pass on this mountain on the chance that one of them is ours, and when he finds it—and he will, Sef, he's methodical, that's the whole horror of him—we won't get a warning next time. Mercy was the expensive option. Mercy was the option that costs us the gate."

"I know."

"You *know*." They laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "Eleven hundred years old and you've been mortal six weeks and you've already learned the worst lesson mortality teaches—that kindness is a debt someone else pays."

"That's not the lesson I learned."

"No? What was it, then?"

I looked at the fire, at the widow and her children, at Noor's face lit orange and exhausted and furious and, underneath all of it, afraid. "That I used to think doors were simple," I said. "Open, shut, locked. That's all I was, for a thousand years—a mechanism. And tonight I stood in a clearing and every possible action was a door closing on someone, and I chose the one that closes on *us*, and I don't know if that's wisdom or stupidity or just the first honest decision I've ever made, but my hands are shaking, Noor, and gods' hands aren't supposed to shake, and I think—" I stopped. Started again. "I think shaking might be the point. I think that might be what mercy costs. Not the giving. The *after*."

Noor was silent. The fire popped. Down in the valley, far off, a light moved along a road that shouldn't have had lights on it—too steady, too deliberate, a lantern swinging in the rhythm of a patrol, or a scout, or a man with fresh orders and fresh maps.

Noor saw it too. I watched them see it. And I watched the fury drain out of their face and leave behind the thing I had first glimpsed on the ridge, the thing they kept trying not to become: a leader doing arithmetic with people.

"Landslide," Noor said quietly. "He won't believe landslide." They stood, and rolled their shoulders like someone taking up a weight. "Wake the widow. Gently. If Vane's riders are already walking our roads tonight, then we've got until dawn to move forty-one people off a mountain he's about to own—and Sef—" they glanced back at me, firelight and shadow, "—when we reach the gate, there's something I have to show you. Something it showed me in the storm. I think it's why Ossuar wants it open. And I think you're not going to like whose idea the covenant really was."

Far below, the light went out.

That frightened me more than if it had kept moving.
