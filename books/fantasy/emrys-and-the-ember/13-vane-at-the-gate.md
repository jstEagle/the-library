## 13. Vane at the Gate

I had been an inquisitor for nineteen years, and in that time I had learned that the Crown's maps lie by omission. The mountains above Lowmarsh were drawn as a blank—white space, a cartographer's shrug—and I had always assumed the blankness meant nothing was there. It took three days of hard climbing with two sappers, a mule named Providence who despised me personally, and a guide who kept crossing himself whenever we passed a cairn, to learn that the blankness meant something *was* there. Something someone had paid good money to leave undrawn.

The guide's name was Pell. He had taken my coin with the enthusiasm of a man taking a dare, and he had spent the entire ascent trying to talk me out of spending it.

"There's nothing up there but weather," he said, for the fourth time.

"Then the weather won't mind a visit."

"It's not weather I'm worried about." He glanced back down the trail, where Lowmarsh was a smudge of smoke in the valley, half of it still smoking from the granary fires. "It's what people say about the Lockridge land."

"And what do they say?"

"That nobody goes up. That nobody ever has." He wiped his mouth. "My grandfather said his grandfather drove supply carts for a woman who went up once a year with a ledger under her arm and came down looking like she'd argued with God."

"And did she win?"

Pell didn't laugh. That was the thing about these border folk; they had no talent for laughter that wasn't also a prayer. "Nobody asks her," he said.

*Her.* The grandmother. The one whose name appeared in four separate heresy trials across sixty years, each time as a witness, never as the accused—a trick of bureaucratic survival so elegant I almost admired it. The Lockridges had sat at the edge of every investigation into the old faith like a stone sits at the edge of a millpond, disturbing nothing, reflecting everything.

I intended to disturb them considerably.

* * *

We found the gate at dusk on the third day, and I will confess here, in the privacy of my own skull, that my first thought was not righteous. My first thought was: *it's beautiful*, and my second thought was: *God forgive me.*

It stood in a notch between two peaks like a door set into nothing—a frame of black stone veined with seams of dull red light, tall enough that a cathedral could have fit through it with room left over for its ambitions. No wall around it. No building. Just the door, standing alone in the wind, glowing faintly like a banked fire, as though the mountain had grown around it out of embarrassment.

Behind me, Sapper Corren made a sound I had previously only heard from men seeing their first execution.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Corren, you are a professional. Nothing is impossible. Some things are merely expensive." I dismounted—I had abandoned Providence at the tree line, the mule having made her theological objections clear—and walked toward it.

The heat reached me ten paces out. Not the dry heat of a forge but something older, wetter, like standing near a wound. The red veins in the stone pulsed, slow, patient, and I realized with a lurch that they were pulsing in time with my own heart, which is the sort of discovery that makes a man reconsider his diet, his piety, and the fundamental architecture of reality, in roughly that order.

Corren and the second sapper, a silent Wexford woman named Brase, began unpacking the charges without being told. Good sappers are like good priests: they know the liturgy before you finish the invocation. Powder, fuse, drill-irons, the long augers for seating charges deep in stone. Enough to bring down the arch, collapse the notch, and bury whatever was on the other side under forty feet of mountain. The Crown's engineers had assured me it would work. The Crown's engineers had never seen the door. I did not say this to them. A commander who shares his doubts is like a man who shares his bread on a long march—generous, and soon starving.

I unrolled the writ on a flat stone and weighted its corners. *By order of the Crown of Thornfell, all thresholds to the god-realm of Cindral are declared instruments of plague and sedition, to be closed by any means necessary.* I had drafted the language myself. It had seemed very sufficient at my desk.

Then the keeper came out of the rocks, and the day stopped being administrative.

* * *

I had imagined the keeper of the Ember Gate a hundred ways on the climb: an ancient crone, a warrior-priest, some gaunt ascetic with frost in their beard. What I got was a person of middling height in a patched coat, carrying a lantern they didn't need—the gate provided more light than any lantern—and wearing the expression of a neighbor come to complain about my fence.

"Noor Lockridge," they said. Not a question. They knew why I was here the way you know rain is coming—not through evidence, but through the ache in old bones.

"Inquisitor Edmun Vane." I inclined my head. Courtesy costs nothing and buys time. "You're trespassing on Crown business."

"This is Lockridge land. Has been since before there was a Crown." They set the lantern down, which struck me as either very confident or very tired, and I have since learned it was both. "And that—" they nodded at the gate "—isn't Crown business. It's older business."

"The writ says otherwise." I tapped the parchment. "Signed, sealed, witnessed. All thresholds to Cindral are to be destroyed."

"Mm." Noor looked at the writ the way a physician looks at a folk remedy. "May I ask you a question, Inquisitor? Genuinely. Not a lawyer's question."

"You may ask."

"When your men set those charges—do you know what's on the other side?"

"Cindral," I said. "The god-realm. The source of the blight, the false miracles, the—"

"Do you know," they repeated, gently, "what's on the other side. Have you seen it. Touched it. Heard anything through it besides your own fears echoing back."

I opened my mouth to deliver the answer I had prepared, which was the answer the Church had prepared, which was the answer the Crown had prepared, and found—to my considerable irritation—that Noor Lockridge was watching me with the patience of a schoolteacher waiting for a pupil to hear his own nonsense aloud.

"No," I said.

"No," they agreed. "Neither do I. That's rather the point of a door. You don't know who's knocking until you open it. And you don't get to un-open it after."

"We're not opening it. We're collapsing it."

"Yes." Noor looked past me at Corren and Brase, who had gone very still over their powder kegs, listening the way workmen listen when the conversation turns to whether their labor is murder. "You're going to pour fire into a crack between worlds and hope the crack doesn't notice. And if it does notice—if the thing on the other side that's been pushing at this door for twenty years feels your little explosion as an invitation—" They spread their hands. "Well. Then you'll have done exactly what it wanted, and you'll have done it with the Crown's seal on it, so no one can even blame you properly."

For a moment, neither of us spoke. The wind came through the notch and it smelled, absurdly, of woodsmoke and my mother's kitchen, which I chose to attribute to altitude.

"Step aside, Keeper Lockridge," I said.

"No."

It was said without drama. That was the unsettling part. I have faced fanatics who screamed scripture while I chained them, and martyrs who smiled at the stake, and I have procedures for all of it. I did not have a procedure for a tired person in a bad coat saying *no* as though declining a second helping of soup.

"I am authorized to use force," I said.

"I know. I've read your statutes. Chapter nine, enforcement of writs; chapter eleven, obstruction." They folded their arms. "I've also read the ones you haven't. There's an oath in the old law, Inquisitor—older than your Crown—that says a threshold must have a keeper, and a keeper stands in the doorway. Not beside it. Not behind it. *In* it. So." They stepped forward, past me, past the writ flapping on its stone, and planted themselves directly beneath the arch, in the red light, close enough that the glow gilded the gray in their hair. "Here I stand."

* * *

I want to record, because no one else will, that I gave them every chance.

I explained the blight—the gray fields outside Lowmarsh, the crops that crumbled to ash where miracles had touched. Noor listened and said, quietly, "I know. I've walked those fields. I also know the child who breathes because of them. Which sin would you have me weigh heavier?"

I explained the granary fires, the dead, the shrine district burned by frightened men with writs of their own. At that, something moved behind Noor's eyes—grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes—but they did not move from the doorway.

I explained Ossuar. Yes, I knew the name by then; the widow's confession had given us fragments, and the archives at the capital had given us the rest. The eldest god, devouring his siblings' strength to force the gate. The godling Sef, sent through as a lure. I laid it out like a prosecutor, expecting triumph, and instead Noor nodded along like a colleague.

"All true," they said. "Which is why the gate stays shut. Sealed properly. By covenant, not by rubble. Your explosion doesn't stop Ossuar, Inquisitor—it just breaks the only lock between him and everyone you've sworn to protect."

"Your grandmother sealed it," I said, and watched them flinch. "A hundred years ago. With a god's help, according to the records I burned myself, so don't bother denying them. One keeper, one sealing. You're telling me it can't be done again."

"I'm telling you it can be done again," Noor said, "and that it will cost more than powder and fuse. But that's not your road. Your road ends with a hole in the mountain and a very official report, and when the ash starts falling on Lowmarsh—and it will fall, Inquisitor, I promise you it will fall—you'll write another report, and another, right up until there's no one left to file them."

"Then give me an alternative with the Crown's authority behind it."

"No." And then, softer, almost kind, which was worse: "Give me *your* authority. The other kind. The kind you had before the Crown issued it."

I did not answer that. I want it noted that I did not answer, and that the silence lasted longer than professional courtesy required, and that during that silence I looked at the door—at the slow pulse of it, at the way the light bent around Noor Lockridge as though the gate had already decided whose side it was on—and I felt, for the first time in nineteen years of service, the specific vertigo of a man who has discovered his ladder is leaning against the wrong wall.

"Corren," I said. "Set the charges."

* * *

They worked fast, my sappers. Augers biting into stone, charges seated, fuse run out to a hundred paces. Brase worked with her jaw set; Corren wouldn't look at the keeper at all. Noor stood in the doorway through all of it and watched, and did not plead, and did not curse, and somehow that was louder than either.

Pell the guide had retreated to the mules and was praying loudly and badly.

At fifty paces, I raised my hand for the signal, and that was when the gate convulsed.

There is no better word. The whole arch shuddered like a struck bell, the red veins flaring white-hot, and a sound came through—not from the gate, *through* it, from the far side of the world—a sound like a vast intake of breath, or hunger, or both. The temperature dropped so fast my breath frosted mid-air. Corren fell against his powder kegs and caught himself with a soldier's reflexes and a boy's whimper.

Noor staggered but stayed upright, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe, and I saw their lips moving—words too old for the liturgy I knew—and I saw the light bend inward around them, gathering, like water finding a drain.

"What's happening?" Brase shouted.

"The gate's failing," Noor called back, and their voice cracked down the middle. "It's failing *now*. Light those fuses and you won't be closing a door—you'll be tearing a hole in a dam."

Every instinct I had—the Crown's instincts, the Church's instincts, nineteen years of them—said proceed. The writ was signed. The orders were clear. A wavering moment is precisely when weak men abandon their duty.

But I had lost a son to a false prayer. I knew what it sounded like when something holy lied.

And I looked at Noor Lockridge—this stubborn, exhausted, unfashionable person standing in a doorway between worlds, holding it shut with nothing but their own body and an oath no government recognized—and I thought: *that is also a kind of law. Older than mine. Kept longer than mine.*

"Hold," I said.

Corren's knife hovered over the fuse. "Inquisitor—"

"*Hold*, Corporal. That is a direct order."

The gate convulsed again, harder, and this time the light split along one of the black stone seams, and something small and bright came loose from the heart of the arch—a sliver of white fire, spinning slow, drifting down through the freezing air like a leaf made of dawn.

Noor caught it.

I saw their face when they did. I have interrogated saints and sinners for two decades and I have never seen a face do what theirs did in that moment: grief and recognition and terror arriving all at once, like three messengers bringing the same letter.

Because I saw it too. For one heartbeat, before Noor's fingers closed around it, the shard showed me—showed *me*, a man with powder dust on his cuffs and a writ in his pocket—a memory that was not mine. A younger woman with the keeper's same gray eyes, kneeling before this door beside a god who was weeping, and the woman saying words I could not hear, and the god answering with a nod that carried the weight of a broken world.

Both of them grieving. Both of them keeping the bargain anyway.

The vision cut off. The shard went dark in Noor's fist. The gate steadied—not healed, never healed, but steadied, its pulse slowing to something almost like sleep.

Silence, except for Pell, still praying, and the wind, and my own heart conducting its insubordinate rhythm against the stone.

Noor turned to look at me. Their hand was burned where they'd caught the shard; they didn't seem to have noticed. When they spoke, their voice was hoarse.

"You saw it."

It was not a question, and lying to it would have been like lying to the weather.

"I saw it," I said.

"And?"

And. As if the fate of two realms might turn on a single syllable from a tired inquisitor on a mountaintop. I looked at my sappers, at the seated charges, at the fuse coiled like a sleeping snake. I looked at the writ, my own careful language, already curling in the cold. I looked at the door.

"The charges stay," I said finally. "Seated. Unlit. If your covenant fails, Keeper Lockridge—if that door opens one inch beyond what your oath allows—my men and I will finish the job the Crown sent us for. That is the only concession I have to give."

Noor studied me for a long moment, and then—God help me—they *smiled*, faintly, wearily, the smile of someone who has just won an argument they'd have given anything to lose.

"A locked door with a key you're willing to use," they said. "That's more sense than most gods ever managed. It'll do, Inquisitor. For tonight, it'll do."

They turned back to the gate, the dark shard clenched in their burned hand, and began speaking to it in the old tongue, low and steady, like a person talking a feverish child down to sleep.

And behind us, down the black throat of the pass, I heard hoofbeats—many, fast, riding hard against the dark—and I knew, with the certainty of nineteen years' experience, that whatever was coming up that road had not been sent by me.
