## 14. The Second Shard

There is a particular quality to a standoff that no one warns you about, which is that it is mostly standing. Hours of it. The romance of two wills locked across a threshold lasts perhaps the first quarter hour; after that it is cold feet, a full bladder, and the slow realization that Inquisitor Vane has brought sappers who are very good at drilling and very bad at conversation.

The Ember Gate stood between us like a wall of black glass shot through with veins of dying fire. Behind me, the mountain pass. Behind him, forty inquisitors, three sapper crews, and enough scripture scrolls to paper a cathedral. Between us, twenty paces of frost-bitten stone and a hundred years of law.

"Keeper Lockridge." Vane's voice carried in the thin air without effort. He had not raised it once all day. He didn't need to. "I have given you until sundown."

"You've given me until sundown twice already," I said. "I'm beginning to think you enjoy the view."

He did not smile. I hadn't expected him to. But one of his younger inquisitors made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth, and Vane turned his head a fraction, and the young man became intensely interested in his own boots.

It was the fourth day since the refugees had come over the ridge, the third since the widow's betrayal had put this place on Vane's maps, and the second since Sef had stood in front of me with ash still in his hair and said, *Ossuar knows where the door is now. He was always going to know. You have days, not weeks.* Days, not weeks, and here I was spending them watching a man read legal pronouncements at a rock.

Except it wasn't a rock. That was the whole problem. It had never been a rock.

The gate had been getting warmer. I could feel it through my boots, through the soles and the bones of my feet, a pulse like a heart remembering what hearts were for. Every hour the cracks in its surface glowed a little longer after each flare. Every hour the hairline seams spread by a finger's width. My grandmother's ledger said the gate should hold for a century more. My grandmother's ledger, I was coming to understand, had been written by an optimist with a chisel and a prayer.

"The Crown's writ is clear," Vane said. He unrolled another scroll — he had dozens, and I suspected he'd memorized them all and brought them anyway out of principle. "The structure before you is declared an instrument of proscribed worship. It will be collapsed. Its foundations salted. Any who resist are subject to—"

"Yes, yes," I said. "Subject to the full weight of the law. Inquisitor, do you know what's on the other side of this door?"

"I know what came *through* it." Something moved behind his eyes when he said it. Not hatred. Grief wearing hatred's coat, the way grief does when it can't afford its own clothes. "I buried eleven people in Lowmarsh. I read the rites myself because there weren't enough priests left standing to do it."

"And I knew most of their names," I said quietly. "Marta Hollis. The Tern twins. Old Bram who cheated at cards and thought nobody noticed." His jaw tightened. Good. I wanted him to hear the names. Names were the only weapon I had left that didn't require me to move from this spot. "You want to collapse the door because things came through it. But the things that came through it were fleeing something worse, and if you bring down the wall today, the worse thing arrives tomorrow with nothing at all in its way."

"You expect me to take the word of a smuggler and a fugitive godling."

"No," I said. "I expect you to take the word of your own eyes. You're a methodical man, Vane. It's your best quality and your worst one. So be methodical. Your sappers have been drilling test holes since dawn. Ask them what they found."

He hesitated. It was small — a flicker, a breath held half a beat too long — but I saw it, and I understood then that his sappers had already told him. Stone that healed. Drill bits that came up warm. A foundation that went down further than any foundation had any right to go, into rock that predated the mountains around it.

"The Crown does not act on rumor," he said.

"The Crown acts on whatever you put in your reports," I said, "and we both know you write honest ones. It's why they sent you instead of someone pleasant."

That landed harder than I intended. I watched it land. And I hated myself a little for the aim of it, because Vane was not a stupid man and not a cruel one, and cruelty would have been easier to stand against. He was a man who had lost a son to a false prayer and had decided, in the way grieving men decide things, that the world could be made safe if only the right doors were closed. I could have loved him, in another life. In this one I had to stop him.

"Sundown," he said again, and turned away, and the standoff resumed its true form: everyone standing in the cold, waiting for the world to make the decision for them.

The world obliged an hour early.

* * *

It began as a sound. Not a crack — I'd heard cracks, small ones, for months, the gate ticking like a cooling stove. This was deeper. A groan, the kind a ship makes, the kind a house makes, the kind a living thing makes when something inside it breaks and rearranges.

Then the light changed.

Every vein of fire in the gate's surface flared at once, white-gold, blinding, and the warmth under my feet became heat, and the heat became a pulling, like the whole doorway had become a lung drawing breath. The sappers scattered. Horses screamed somewhere down the pass. Vane turned back toward me with his hand on his sword and his face lit gold, and for one unguarded moment he looked exactly like what he was: a man watching his certainties catch fire.

"It's opening," he shouted. "Lockridge, it's *opening*—"

"No," I said, because I could feel it, and feeling it meant knowing it, and knowing it meant I had maybe thirty seconds to do the maddest thing of my life. "It's *offering*."

I ran at the gate.

I heard Vane shout my name — my actual name, not Keeper, not smuggler, *Noor*, though he had no right to it and I had never given it — and I heard boots behind me, and none of it mattered, because the gate had opened for me the way it had opened for my grandmother, the way it opens only ever for keepers, a seam of darkness splitting down the center of the light, and I stepped into it.

Inside was not inside. Inside was the space between, the hinge-place, where the gate kept everything it remembered. It was warm as a held hand and vast as a cathedral and it smelled, absurdly, of my grandmother's kitchen — rosemary and woodsmoke — because the gate keeps what its keepers give it, and she had stood here often, and she had been hungry, and she had thought about soup.

And there, floating in the gold-dark, turning slowly like a leaf in still water, was the Second Shard.

It was smaller than my fist. A splinter of the original covenant-stone, the piece the old gods had broken and scattered rather than let either side hold the whole truth alone. It burned without heat. It looked at me — I know how that sounds, but there is no other word; it *looked*, the way a dog looks, the way a judge looks — and it weighed me.

*I know,* I told it. *I'm not much. Take me anyway.*

I reached out and took it, and the gate showed me everything.

* * *

Memory is a country with no roads. You don't walk through it; you fall through it, from moment to moment, and the falling is the price.

I fell into a field of barley under a sky with two moons. I fell into a hall of woven branches where gods and mortals sat at the same long table, arguing about rain. I fell into the war — oh, the war, the broken oaths, the fields that burned for a generation, the god who drowned a city because a king lied to him, the mortal host that broke a goddess's shrine because her priest had grown rich on tithes while children starved. Both sides. Always both sides. There were no innocents in the falling, only the abandoned and the abandoners, trading places century by century.

And then I fell into the sealing, and I stopped falling, because this part I needed to see whole.

My grandmother, younger than I had ever known her, hair still dark, kneeling at the base of the unfinished gate. Beside her a god — tall, gray-robed, his face running with light the way a face runs with tears. Vessa, I thought. The drowned lady of the first shrine. She pressed her palm to the gate-stone and spoke the terms aloud, and my grandmother repeated them, mortal voice and divine voice braided together:

*The gods shall keep the land. The mortals shall keep the gods honest. When either side fails, the covenant shall sleep — not die, sleep — until a keeper wakes it.*

*Until a keeper wakes it.*

Not destroyed. Deferred. All those years of vigil, all those ledger entries in my grandmother's careful hand, and she had known — she must have known — that the gate was not a tomb but a promise with the date torn off. She had sealed it and swept up and made supper and never told a soul, because some burdens you carry so others don't have to, and she had passed the carrying to me the way you pass anything down: silently, completely, with love.

The vision released me. I was on my knees on the frost-bitten stone, the Second Shard clenched in my fist, and the gate had gone quiet around me — not calm, I understood suddenly, but *listening*.

Vane was ten feet away. Sword drawn. Alone — his inquisitors hung back at the edge of the light, unwilling, and who could blame them, the air still shimmered with what I'd seen.

"Drop it," he said. His voice wasn't steady anymore. "Whatever it is. Drop it and step away."

"Inquisitor." I got my feet under me. My legs shook. My hand wouldn't open. "Come here."

"I will not—"

"Come *here*." And something in my voice must have carried, because he came, warily, sword still up, close enough that I could see the frost in his eyebrows and the exhaustion underneath his discipline. "Hold out your hand."

"This is a trick."

"Everything is a trick to you. Hold out your hand anyway. You're methodical. Consider it evidence."

A muscle worked in his jaw. Then — and I will wonder about this for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be — he sheathed his sword and held out his hand, palm up, like a man asking for alms.

I pressed the Shard into it.

The memory took him the way it had taken me. I watched it happen: the rigid shoulders softening, the breath going out of him, his knees giving until he knelt in the frost without seeming to notice. I couldn't see what he saw. But I saw his face, and his face was the whole story.

He saw the war, I think. He saw the oaths break. And then he saw something specific, something meant for him, because the covenant-shards are not subtle — they find the wound and they press on it. Maybe he saw his son. Maybe he saw the false prayer, the desperate mother, the god who might have answered and didn't, or answered and charged too much. Whatever it was, Inquisitor Aldous Vane, terror of the shrine districts, author of the purge orders, knelt at the foot of the proscribed gate and wept like a man at a graveside, openly, without shame, for a long time.

I stood over him and did not gloat. Gloating seemed beside the point. The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, his inquisitors waited for orders that didn't come.

When he finally looked up, his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear.

"My son," he said. "The prayer that failed. It didn't fail." His voice cracked on the words. "It was *answered*. Someone paid for it. That's what the blight is. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "I have burned shrines for this. I have ruined families. I signed the order for Lowmarsh."

"Yes," I said, because lying to him now would have been its own kind of blasphemy. "You did."

He bowed his head. And here is the thing about fragile pauses: they are not peace. They are the held breath between blows, the instant where a man decides which version of himself survives the night. I watched Vane decide. I watched the inquisitor and the father fight for the same ground behind his eyes, and I understood that whichever won, nothing would ever be simple again.

"The writ says collapse the gate," he said at last, slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. "The writ assumes the gate is the disease."

"And?"

"And I am a servant of the Crown, Keeper Lockridge, and I am also a man who has just been shown—" his hand closed briefly over the Shard, still in his grip, glowing faintly against his palm, "—shown that the Crown has been prosecuting the victims of a crime while the criminal gathers at the border." He looked up at the gate, at the spreading golden seams, at the darkness gathering behind them like a storm behind glass. "What is on the other side? Truly. No parables. No keeper's riddles."

"Something old," I said. "Something hungry. It ate its own pantheon to get strong enough to push this door open, and it sent a friend of mine through as bait, and it is coming, Inquisitor, whether this gate stands or falls, because gates were never the point. Keepers were. And I need every soul in this pass — yours included — ready for what comes next."

Behind him, the gate flared again — longer this time, brighter, the pulse quickening, a heartbeat climbing toward a sprint. And beneath the gold, deep in the dark at the center, something moved. Not a crack. Not a convulsion.

A shape. Vast and patient, pressing its face against the far side of the world, the way a child presses a face against a window.

Vane saw it too. I watched the color leave his face, and I watched him make his choice — or the first installment of it, anyway — when he turned and called out, in a voice hoarse from weeping and absolutely steady:

"Belay the sappers. Strike the camp. Everyone—" he swallowed, "—everyone listens to the keeper now."

The gate pulsed. Once. Twice.

And far off beyond the ridge, in the direction of Lowmarsh, the sky began, very softly, to snow ash.
