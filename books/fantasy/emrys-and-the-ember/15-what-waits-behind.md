## 15. What Waits Behind

There is a particular humiliation in being a god who has learned to knock.

I had spent a century on the far side of the Ember Gate learning it, in fact. In Cindral, doors are not things you open; they are things that consent to be opened, and most of them never do. So when I finally pressed my palm against the crack in the gate's heart—Noor asleep twenty paces behind me, wrapped in their grandfather's coat and dreaming of ledgers, I'd wager—I did it politely. The way you'd knock at the house of someone who once loved you and has since become a stranger.

The gate remembered me. That was the first surprise. The second was that it let me in.

* * *

Crossing was nothing like falling. Falling, I have some expertise in; I wrote the book, or at least the bruise. Crossing was more like being read. The gate took hold of what I am—small, scorched, mostly stubborn—and turned me over like a page, checking for marginalia, for edits, for signs of tampering. It found plenty. It let me through anyway, which should have been my first warning. Gates that are failing do not discriminate. They are like grief that way: they take everything offered and ask if there's more.

Then Cindral.

You want to know what exile looks like from the inside? Imagine your home after a fire that never quite finished. The ember-light of my realm still burned, but low, guttering, the way a hearth burns when everyone has stopped feeding it because everyone has stopped believing anyone will come home to sit beside it. The mountains of Cindral were the same mountains Noor's passes echoed, but wrong—sharper, hungrier, as though the land itself had been chewing on its own bones for a hundred years out of boredom.

And the gods were waiting for me.

I felt them before I saw them, the way you feel a crowd's attention before you find its center. Vessa of the drowned shrine, her voice arriving as water sounds. The twins of the threshold-roads, whose names I will not write here because they sold mine cheaply enough already. Old Marric of the harvest, who used to be tall as autumn and now seemed to lean on his own scythe. They gathered in the ruin of the First Court—a plaza of black glass where the covenant had been sworn, back when swearing meant something—and they looked at me with expressions I could not read, which frightened me more than hostility would have. Hostility I understand. I've inspired it professionally.

"Sef," said Vessa, and her voice broke on the single syllable like a wave on a jetty. "Little door-god. You actually went."

"You actually came," I corrected. "There's a difference. One of us kept faith."

Marric laughed. It was not a kind sound. Nothing about that place was kind anymore, and I want to be fair to them—to all of them—because fairness is the last luxury I intend to afford my own kind before I finish deciding how much of my heart to keep. But fairness cuts both ways, and I was standing in the middle of the blade.

"Kept faith," Marric repeated. He turned to the others. "He says it like a coin he earned honestly."

"I walked out of a sealed gate," I said. "Half-burned. Into a mortal body that treats hunger as a personality trait. I found the Lockridge keeper. I've spent weeks hauling coal and lying to inquisitors and sleeping on a floor harder than my prospects. If that isn't faith, old man, show me your version."

"Oh, we have a version," said one of the twins—the dark one, the one whose name means *the hinge*. "We've had a version for a century. You're standing in it."

* * *

They took me deeper into the Court, past the shattered oath-stones, past the places where the old pantheon's power had pooled and dried like spilled wine. And that was when I began to understand—not all at once, understanding rarely grants that mercy, but in pieces, the way a wall comes down: a crack, then a groan, then everything at once on top of you.

The gods of Cindral were starving.

Not metaphorically. Not in the poetic sense in which exiles are always said to starve for home. I mean that divinity is a fuel, and fuel runs out, and a hundred years behind a sealed gate with no worshippers, no offerings, no prayers crossing the threshold—except lately, except the small mercies leaking through the cracks like light under a door—we had been burning ourselves. Each god dimming a little more each decade. Vessa's tides gone tidal-pool small. Marric's harvests reduced to the memory of grain. The twins flickering between presence and absence so often they'd begun to argue about which of them was the ghost.

And Ossuar—

Ossuar sat at the heart of the First Court on a throne that had not existed when I was banished, and that was the detail my mind kept snagging on, later, in the dark hours. Thrones don't grow. Thrones are built. Someone had built this one, and I knew of only one material sufficient to the task.

The eldest god had been beautiful once, in the way winter is beautiful: vast, necessary, indifferent. Now he was vast in the other direction. He had consumed his own divinity down to the wick and then, when the wick failed, he had begun consuming theirs. I could see it in them, now that I knew to look—the hollowed places, the dimmed crowns, the way Vessa stood slightly behind Marric as though sheltering from him. A hundred years of slow cannibalism, dressed up as shared sacrifice. *We pool our strength,* they would have told each other. *We give to the eldest so he can break the gate.* And each gift had been a little less voluntary than the last.

"Brother," Ossuar said, and his voice was the sound a mountain makes settling, patient and inevitable. "You brought me a keeper."

"I brought myself," I said. "Noor is mine to protect, not yours to spend."

"Mine to spend?" Something moved beneath his surface, something I refused to look at directly, the way you refuse to look at the sun. "Child. Everything here is mine to spend. I am the eldest. I held the sky while the rest of you learned to walk under it. When the mortals broke faith—when *their* keepers drove us through that gate and salted the threshold behind us—who do you think carried us? Who do you think has carried us every year since, growing thinner, growing quieter, so that you small ones would have another season, another decade, another chance?"

It was a good speech. I want to acknowledge that. There was truth in it, real truth, the most dangerous variety. He *had* carried us. I remembered the crossing, the terrible weight of him at the rear of our retreat, holding the gate's closing jaws open with his shoulders while the rest of us scrambled through. I had been a child-god then, a minor spirit of doorways and crossroads, and I had looked back once and seen Ossuar bleeding light into the hinge of the world, and I had thought: *there is devotion.*

I had not thought: *there is also appetite, and they wear the same face.*

"The gate is failing," I said. "On its own. Time and neglect. We don't need—" I gestured at the throne, at the hollowed gods around it, at the whole architecture of quiet horror I was only beginning to map. "We don't need whatever this is. We need the Shards, the covenant reforged, a keeper willing to stand between. Noor is that keeper. We can do this *cleanly.*"

"Cleanly." Vessa made the word sound like a fish she'd been handed. "Sef. Sweet threshold-child. Ask yourself why the gate is failing."

"Time—"

"Ask yourself why it fails *now*. Why the cracks spread in this generation. Why the mercies leak through at precisely the pace needed to draw a keeper's attention." Her voice was very gentle, which from a goddess of drowning is its own kind of threat. "Why do you think you found the cracks so easy to slip through, little door-god? Who do you think widened them?"

The Court went quiet. Even the wind in the burnt mountains seemed to lean in.

"You didn't fail," said the hinge-twin softly. "You were *sent*."

* * *

I want to tell you I raged. I want to report that I denounced them, that I called down what little remained of my divinity and made the black glass sing. What I actually did was stand very still and feel the ground reorganize itself beneath my feet, which is, I suppose, the occupational hazard of being a god of thresholds: sooner or later, every threshold turns on you.

"Sent," I repeated.

"A lure," said Ossuar, without apology, without cruelty even—or with a cruelty so old and so administrative it had transcended cruelty into mere procedure. "A keeper of Lockridge blood sealed the gate. Their blood knows the lock. Only a Lockridge can unmake what a Lockridge made—or remake it, which amounts to the same thing for my purposes. But keepers are cautious animals. They do not come to strange gods. Strange gods must come to them, wearing a face worth trusting." His attention settled on me with the weight of a landslide deciding. "You were always the likable one, Sef. Small enough to pity. Brave enough to admire. Damaged enough to be believed."

Weeks. Weeks of coal-hauling and floor-sleeping and watching Noor count the gate's cracks by lamplight, cataloguing their loneliness in a ledger no one would ever read, and every moment of it—the trust, the wary almost-friendship, the night they gave me their grandmother's coat because I was shivering—every moment had been *assigned*. I had thought I was deserting heaven for a cause. I had been heaven's errand boy all along, and the errand was bait.

"Noor will die," I said. My voice sounded far away, mortal-thin. "When you come through. Whatever you're planning—you've eaten half a pantheon to force a gate, and gates don't open gently. The keeper stands between. That's the whole of the office. They'll stand between *you*, and you'll go through them like weather."

"Yes," said Ossuar.

Just that. *Yes.* No justification, no ornament. I had braced for sophistry and received arithmetic instead, and somehow that was worse. Somewhere behind me, Vessa turned away. Marric studied his scythe. The guilty are always studying their tools.

"There were other ways," I said.

"There were slower ways. Slowness is death, brother. We are decades from guttering out entirely. Another century and there will be nothing left to rescue, only a memory of gods that mortals will misquote." He rose from the throne, and the Court's ember-light bent toward him like iron filings. "You've done well. Better than well—the keeper trusts you, I'm told. Trusts you *genuinely*, which is a delicacy I hadn't dared hope for. Go back. Keep them tender. When the third Shard is taken and the gate stands fully open, step aside, and I will remember you kindly."

"And if I don't step aside?"

The pause lasted exactly long enough to be an answer.

"Then you are a very small god," Ossuar said, "standing in a very large doorway."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about betrayal by your own heaven: it doesn't shatter you. Shattering implies you were whole going in. What it does is quieter—it takes the story you've been telling yourself and reads it aloud in someone else's voice, until every noble word sounds borrowed, because it was. My exile, my fall, my weeks of ash and honesty among mortals: none of it rebellion. All of it commission. I had been so proud of choosing Noor's side, and the choice had been chosen *for* me before I ever fell through the sky.

But.

I am a god of thresholds. Small, yes. Liable to be overlooked, certainly. But thresholds have one property that thrones do not, and Ossuar, in all his vast arithmetic, had failed to account for it:

A threshold decides what passes.

I walked back to the crack in the gate with the Court's eyes on my back and Ossuar's patience stretched behind me like a snare, and I thought about Noor asleep in the cold with a ledger full of secrets and a spine full of duty. I thought about the widow's children, and Vane's dead son, and Lowmarsh's gray fields. I thought about a grandmother who had wept alongside a god while sealing a door, both of them knowing the price and paying it anyway.

Ossuar wanted a lure returned to the line, twitching, drawing the keeper in.

Fine.

I would return. I would carry the message he wanted carried, in the manner I chose, timed as I chose. Let the eldest god learn what the small ones learn first and the great ones never learn at all: that the difference between a door and a trap is *consent*, and I had just withdrawn mine.

The gate took me back the way it had taken me in—reading me, page by page. But this time, as it turned me over, I felt it pause at the newest chapter, the one written tonight, and I swear on what's left of my name that the old stone warmed, briefly, like a hand squeezing back.

Behind me, in the dying light of Cindral, something vast began to move toward the threshold.

Ahead of me, in the mortal dark, Noor stirred in their sleep and murmured a name.

It wasn't mine. It was their grandmother's. And I understood, with the clarity that only arrives after everything else has broken, that I had perhaps one night—one thin, mortal night—to decide whether I arrived at that gate as Ossuar's herald, or as the second-to-last honest thing in two worlds.

I started walking.
