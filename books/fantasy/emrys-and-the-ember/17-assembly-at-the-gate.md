## 17. Assembly at the Gate

I had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and it took me a single morning to undo all of it.

The refugees had come up the smuggler's roads in ones and twos at first—a widow with her children, a shrine-keeper who'd lost his shrine to Vane's purge, a boy of maybe fourteen carrying nothing but a rooster under one arm and an expression of profound grievance on his face. Then they'd come in dozens. By the time I stood on the ridge above the Ember Gate and looked down at what my hidden passes had become—a ragged procession of ash-gray faces, blistered feet, and stubborn, unreasonable hope—I understood that secrecy was a luxury that had quietly died somewhere behind me on the road.

You can't hide forty people in a mountain. Believe me. I did the arithmetic.

So there was only one thing left to do, and it was the thing I had spent two decades avoiding: tell them everything.

* * *

We gathered at dusk in the hollow before the gate, where the old flagstones still held the day's warmth like a banked fire. The Ember Gate rose above us, and even those who had never seen it went quiet when they did. It's hard not to. A hundred feet of black stone veined with ember-light, humming faintly, like a struck bell that never quite finishes ringing. The cracks in it had widened since my last inspection—hairline fractures now run through with a glow that pulsed, slow and arrhythmic, like something breathing behind a wall.

Marta Ashdown stood nearest to me, arms crossed, her children clustered at her skirts. Yes, *that* Marta—the widow who'd sold us to Vane and then, in a fit of penance I still didn't entirely trust, brought back his route maps. She'd earned her place here twice over, once by betrayal and once by repair, and I hadn't decided which counted more. Sef stood at my left shoulder, wearing his mortal shape like an ill-fitting coat, watching the crowd with eyes that held too much light for comfort.

And off to the side, alone, hands bound loosely in front of him by his own request—inquisitor's rope, inquisitor's knots, though he could have slipped them in a heartbeat and we all knew it—stood Inquisitor Edran Vane.

He'd come to burn this gate down three weeks ago. Now he sat on a stone like a penitent, his sappers' charges confiscated, his scripture rolled up and tucked under his arm like a man carrying a grudge against his own book. The memory in the Second Shard had done something to him. Grief recognizes grief; it doesn't forgive, but it sits down beside you and waits.

"Before I start," I said, and my voice cracked on the first word because apparently twenty years of solitary practice does not prepare you for public speaking, "I want to say that most of what you're about to hear sounds like madness. I know. I've been living with it for months, and some mornings I still check whether I've gone mad or just honest."

A few nervous laughs. The boy clutched his rooster tighter. Somewhere in the crowd, someone whispered a prayer—not to any outlawed god, just the shapeless mutter people make when they're frightened—and the gate flickered in answer, and the whispering stopped.

Right. No point easing in.

"My name is Noor Lockridge," I said. "My grandmother helped seal this gate a hundred years ago. I have been its keeper since I was nineteen years old. And everything wrong with the world right now—the blight, the answered prayers, the miracles with teeth—it comes from this gate failing. From my gate failing."

That landed the way I'd hoped it wouldn't. Faces closed. Marta's jaw tightened. A woman near the back—Tamsin, the miller's daughter, whose fields had grayed after her mother's desperate prayer pulled her brother from the river—said, loudly enough to be heard, "You *knew*?"

"I knew the gate was cracking. I didn't know what was coming through until it came." I held up a hand before the murmur could become a mob. "Which is why you're all here instead of dead in your beds. So let me give you the whole ugly truth, and then you can decide what to do with me afterward."

* * *

I told them about Ossuar.

It's strange, saying a god's name out loud to a crowd. The air changes. The gate behind me flared so bright that shadows leapt and scattered, and several people made signs against evil that their grandmothers had taught them in secret, the old signs, the ones the Crown banned and the Crown never managed to kill.

"The eldest god," I said. "Older than the covenant, older than the war of broken oaths. While the other exiled gods waited behind the gate—hungry, yes, angry, yes, but *waiting*—Ossuar has been devouring them. Their power, their names, maybe their selves. He's been eating heaven to buy one door open." I glanced at Sef. This part was his to confirm, and he nodded, once, grim. "He sent Sef through as a lure. A hook baited with a friendly face, to find the keeper's bloodline and draw me close enough to use."

"And you brought him anyway," Tamsin said flatly, looking at Sef.

"He told me himself," I said. "That's rather the point. Ossuar's lure came here, looked at what he'd been sent to do, and refused. He's been paying for that refusal ever since—every day in mortal flesh costs him, and he keeps paying." I turned to face the crowd fully. "I'm not asking you to trust him. Trust is earned, and he hasn't earned yours yet. But I am telling you that the enemy's own weapon chose our side, and I find that worth more than a little."

Vane spoke from his stone, quiet and precise as always. "For what it is worth—which I grant may be nothing—I have read every account of the exiles the Church possesses. None describe a defection. The theology of the age assumed gods were incapable of it." His mouth twisted. "The theology of the age appears to have been written by men who confused power with virtue. I include myself."

Coming from Vane, that was practically a love sonnet.

"So here is where we stand," I said. "Ossuar is coming. Not metaphorically. The gate has days, perhaps less. When it breaks, he comes through with everything he's swallowed, and Thornfell becomes the first course of a very long meal. The Crown can't stop him. Vane's inquisition can't burn him—he *is* fire, or close enough. There is exactly one thing that can."

I took out the shards.

Three of them now—or two and a memory of where the third slept. The First Shard from the drowned shrine of Vessa, cool as river stone. The Second torn from the gate's own heart during the convulsion that nearly killed me, warm as a living thing. They sat in my palm and threw light upward across my face, and I watched forty-odd exhausted people look at them the way starving people look at bread.

"The covenant," I said. "The original terms. The gods keep the land; mortals keep the gods honest. That was the bargain, and both sides broke it—the gods grew proud, the mortals grew careless, and everyone decided the other side would do the remembering. The shards hold the terms. Reforged at the gate, they can bind Ossuar. Not destroy him—you don't destroy a force like that, you *bind* it, you give it terms it cannot break. But reforging requires a keeper. A living keeper, standing at the threshold, willing to be the binding."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, mixed together.

"What does 'willing to be the binding' mean?" Marta asked. Direct as a hammer, Marta. One of the many reasons I hadn't forgiven her and couldn't dismiss her either.

"It means," I said, and here was the sentence I'd rehearsed forty times on the walk down and still couldn't say smoothly, "that whoever stands at the gate's heart when the shards fuse stops being entirely mortal. The threshold needs a consciousness to hold it. A will. Someone who chooses, moment to moment, forever, whom to let through and whom to turn away. You don't die. You just—" I searched for the word and hated every candidate. "—stop belonging. To either side. Ever again."

"You," said the boy with the rooster, with the devastating simplicity of the young.

"Me," I agreed. "Probably. Unless—"

"Noor." Sef's voice, low. Warning and plea in equal measure.

"—unless someone else volunteers," I finished, and immediately felt like a fraud for saying it, because I had no intention of letting anyone else do it, and everyone present probably knew it, and honesty demanded I admit that too. "Look. I'll be plain, since I've apparently become a person who gives speeches. I don't want your help with this part. I've kept this gate for twenty years because nobody else knew it existed, and I told myself that was loneliness, but it was also—convenience. Invisibility is comfortable. Nobody asks the invisible to sacrifice anything." I looked down at the shards glowing in my hand like two small accusations. "But I called this assembly because you deserve the choice, and because—" my voice did something embarrassing, "—because I stopped wanting to be invisible somewhere on the ridge, and I'm told that's called leadership, and leadership means asking even when you dread the answer."

* * *

They stepped forward.

Not all at once—that would have been theater, and these were farmers and millers and shrine-keepers, people who distrusted theater the way they distrusted late frosts. It happened the way work happens. Tamsin came first, arms folded, chin up. "My mother prayed to a banned god and it cost our fields. If there's a way to pray that doesn't cost, I want to be standing near it when it's found." Then the shrine-keeper, Old Hew, who'd tended a purged altar and had nothing left to tend but this. Then Marta, which surprised me, and shouldn't have—people who've betrayed something once understand better than anyone what it costs to lose it twice.

"I owe a debt," she said simply, not meeting my eyes. "Debts get paid."

Then more. The boy with the rooster. A pair of brothers who'd carried their grandmother up the pass on a door torn from its hinges. Vane, last, rising stiffly from his stone, ropes still on his wrists, saying only: "I came to end this gate. Let me help keep it instead. It seems the least a righteous man can do when he learns he was wrong about everything." And then, drier: "Also, my inquisitors will follow me. Most of them. The pious ones are the persuadable ones—they're used to believing difficult things."

Sef didn't volunteer. He just looked at me, and the light in his eyes did the thing it did when he forgot to hide it, and he said, "You know where I'll be. I was a door once, before the exile. I remember how it's done." Which was, from a godling, roughly equivalent to weeping.

I stood there with the shards in my hand and forty people arranged around me in the ember-light, and I thought: *Grandmother, you absolute wretch, you never told me it would feel like this.* Not terror. Something worse. Something like being seen.

"All right," I said, when I could speak steadily. "Here's what happens next. We rest tonight—truly rest, eat everything Hew's been hoarding, sleep in shifts. At dawn we make for the gate's heart, and I take the Third Shard out of whatever's left of Ossuar's grip, and then we reforge the covenant while the world's biggest grudge tries to eat us. Questions?"

The rooster crowed. Of course it did. Dawn was hours away, but roosters have never respected narrative tension.

"No questions, then," I said. "Good. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we save the world, and I promise you—" I looked at the cracks crawling with light across the gate's black face, pulsing faster now, like a heart climbing toward panic, "—it won't wait politely for us to be ready."

Behind us, deep in the stone, something vast shifted its weight, and the first true crack sounded like the sky clearing its throat.
