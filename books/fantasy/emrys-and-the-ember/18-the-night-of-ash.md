## 18. The Night of Ash

### Noor

I had always imagined the end of the world would be louder.

There was noise, certainly—the gate had begun to sing three days before Ossuar came, a low note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and by the night itself the note had climbed into something you felt in your teeth. But the true horror was how quiet everything else became. The wind stopped. The refugees in the passes stopped talking. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath, the way a household does when the drunk father comes home.

I stood at the gate with the ledger under my arm, because apparently my instinct at the apocalypse was paperwork.

"Tell me again why you brought that," Sef said. He stood beside me in his borrowed coat, mortal-fleshed and mortal-pale, and he kept flexing his hands like a man rehearsing for a fight he'd already lost in his head.

"Habit."

"You are about to face the eldest god of Cindral, the Devourer of Names, and your instinct is *bookkeeping*."

"My grandmother started this ledger," I said. "One hundred years of Lockridges writing down what the gate did and hoping someone would read it someday. If I die tonight, someone's going to find it, and they're going to know we didn't run." I looked at him. "That matters more than you'd think. To people like us."

"People like us," he repeated softly. Godling and keeper, both pretending to be smaller than they were. He almost smiled. Then the note in the air broke, like a string snapping, and neither of us smiled at anything again.

The cracks in the gate—hairline, a century old, mine to watch and mine to fail—began to glow from the inside. Not ember-orange. White. The white of a bone held up to fire. And through them came the ash.

It didn't fall so much as arrive, sifting through the cracks as if the gate were an hourglass turned wrong-side-out. It smelled of burnt libraries. Of every prayer ever whispered into a pillow and never answered. I had spent twenty years imagining what would come through that gate if it failed, and I had imagined monsters, armies, fire. I had not imagined grief with weather attached.

"Noor." Sef's voice had changed—deeper, resonant, the voice he'd been born with rather than the one he'd worn for me. "When it opens—and it will open—you cannot look at him directly. Ossuar eats names. You meet his eyes, he starts learning yours, and once he has all of it—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. I'd seen what the blight did to wheat. I could extrapolate to wheat-shaped people.

"And you?" I said. "What happens to you?"

"I'm a god of thresholds," Sef said. "Doors love me. Doors are the one thing in creation that will always take my side." He rolled his shoulders. "I'm going to go stand in front of the biggest door there is, and I'm going to be very rude to what comes through it, and that will buy you time. Don't waste it."

"Sef—"

"Noor." For the first time since I'd known him, he said my name like it was worth something. Like it was a coin he was handing back. "You gathered the shards. You found the terms. Whatever happens next happens *because* someone stood here and refused. That was always going to be you. I just get to be the loud part."

The gate screamed.

Not sang—screamed, a sound like metal being unmade, and the white light split down the center seam, and the night beyond it wasn't night at all but a vast slow turning darkness with something at the middle of it that was *paying attention*.

Sef stepped forward into the light. "Hold my place," he said, and walked through the threshold of worlds like a man stepping out to argue with a landlord.

### Marta

They told us to hold the passes, and I want it written somewhere, by someone, that we held them.

I am Marta Ostrow, widow of nothing worth mentioning, mother of two children who slept that night in the deep cut below Windle Pass because there was nowhere else. I am not brave. I want that written too. When Noor Lockridge stood up in the firelight at the ridge camp and told us the truth—that the gate existed, that it had always existed, that our village had burned because a god was clawing through it—I had wanted to hit them. All those years of smuggler roads above our heads. All those years of secrets while our children prayed to outlawed gods because the legal ones weren't listening.

But nobody else knew the passes. That was the whole of it. The Lockridge roads wound through the mountains like veins through a hand, and Noor knew every one, and now the refugees of Lowmarsh—four hundred of us, maybe five—were the only army between the ash and the lowlands.

So we held the passes. With what? I'll tell you with what. Lantern oil. Sheep tracks we'd herded flocks along since childhood. Grandmother Ostrow's recipe for caltrops, which is just iron scraps in a fire and a prayer, and yes, I know the irony of praying that night, everyone prayed that night, even the ones who spat at the word *god*, because the ash was coming down the north slope like a tide of grey snow and it did not stop for anything living.

The first wave came at moonrise. Not soldiers—there were no soldiers, just the ash itself, moving with intent, and where it touched the pines they went grey and crumbled like the wheat in Lowmarsh. Old Tam Braddock stood on the high shelf with a torch and a barrel of oil and he lit the treeline himself, burned the whole slope rather than let the ash cross it, and the heat drove the stuff back like a hand pushed into water.

"They can't stand fire?" someone asked.

"It's not that they can't stand it," said Tam, coming down black-faced and shaking. "It's that it *hurts* them to be remembered. Fire remembers wood. Ash forgets everything. That's all it is, that's all it wants—to make everything forget itself." He sat down hard on a rock. "Don't let it touch you. Whatever you do. Hold on to your own name."

My daughter woke in the cut below, hearing the fires, and climbed up to me with her blanket dragging. She looked at the burning slope, at the grey tide pressing against the orange line, and she asked me the question every child asks eventually and no parent is ever ready for.

"Mama, who's winning?"

And because I had decided, somewhere between Lowmarsh burning and this cold ridge, that I would never lie to my children again the way this country had lied to all of us, I said: "Nobody yet. But we're still here, and that counts."

She nodded, solemn as a magistrate, and went back down to her brother, and I picked up another armful of iron scraps for the fire, and that was the battle of Windle Pass. No glory. Just people refusing to be forgotten, one hour at a time.

### Vane

I had spent eleven years believing that faith was a wall, and walls keep things out.

I record this now because it is the last honest thing I expect to write. Inquisitor Vane, keeper of the Crown's mandate over Thornfell, architect of the purge of Lowmarsh, author of forty-one death warrants signed in genuine sorrow. I believed the gods' return was a plague. I believed my son died because some exiled thing answered a desperate father's prayer with a miracle priced beyond a farmer's means. I built my life into an instrument for closing doors.

Then I stood at the Ember Gate and watched a godling walk into hell to buy a village time, and watched farmers burn their own mountain to hold a line, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives too late, that I had been guarding the wrong side of the wall my entire career.

"Inquisitor." My lieutenant, Corren, at my shoulder, ash sifting onto both of us like grey snow. Behind us, my riders—thirty men who had come to collapse the gate with sappers and scripture and had instead spent six hours hauling oil and evacuating refugees alongside the very smugglers they'd been hunting. "The northern company reports the pass holds. Barely. They ask—" He hesitated. Corren does not hesitate. "They ask what we're fighting *for*, sir. Officially. For the report."

I looked at the gate. It hung open now, a seam of white light in the mountainside, and through it I could see movement—vast, slow, wrong. Somewhere in that light a young man made of borrowed flesh was shouting insults at the eldest of the old gods, and I could hear it, faintly, echoing off the stone. He was mocking Ossuar's poetry. The god of endings wrote poetry, apparently, and the godling was heckling it.

"For the report," I said, "write this: the Crown's mandate presumed the enemy lay beyond the gate. The mandate was in error. The enemy lies beyond the gate, and also within us, and also—" I stopped. Corren waited. "Also in every mercy we refused to believe in. Write that the inquisition stands with the keepers. All of them. Effective immediately."

"Sir, the Crown will—"

"The Crown," I said, "is a day's ride away, and the end of the world is here *now*. Write it."

He wrote it. I watched him write it by lantern light while ash fell on the page, and then I took up a torch myself, because I had spent eleven years believing faith was a wall, and I intended to spend whatever remained of this night learning what it actually was.

It was, I discovered, a door held open by people who should have run.

### Sef

Let me tell you what Ossuar looks like, since no one else who saw him clearly survived to describe it, and since describing things is, apparently, what I do now that I am mostly mortal and entirely furious.

He does not look like anything. That is the horror of him. He is a shape the way a hole in a page is a shape—he is defined by what is missing around him. Where he stood in the realm-that-was-Cindral, there was simply an absence wearing a suggestion of robes, a suggestion of a crown, a suggestion of a face that my siblings had once loved enough to follow into exile. Around him drifted what he had eaten. I recognized Aunt Vessal's laughter, hanging in the dark like a dead bird. I recognized the green of the orchard-god's spring, chewed grey. He had consumed our heaven slowly, patiently, over a hundred years, and called it *gathering strength to return*, and they had sent me through the gate as his lure, and I had known, hadn't I, somewhere in the marrow of me, that my mission was bait.

"Little hinge," Ossuar said. His voice was the sound a name makes when it is forgotten. "You were meant to open the way. Instead you bring me a keeper, a soldier, and a mob of farmers with torches. Is this what hope looks like now? It's smaller than I remember."

"You remember nothing," I said. "That's the entire problem. You ate your own memory years ago; you've just been too proud to notice. You don't want to return, you want to *consume the returning*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I am going to stand here and explain it to you at length because that is my function tonight."

I am a god of thresholds. I had never fought anything in my existence; doors don't fight, doors *endure*. So I endured. I planted myself in the narrowest point of the passage between realms and I became, for the first time in my long small life, fully what I was made to be: the thing that decides what passes.

Ossuar pushed. I use the word *pushed* the way you'd use it for a flood.

The first blow took my left arm—not wounded it, *unmade* it, unwrote it, and I felt the stories I was made of drain out through the absence like water from a cracked cup. The second blow took my memory of my mother's voice, which I had been saving. The third blow I caught, barely, on the edge of myself, and I heard my own substance ring like a struck bell, and far away, on the mortal side, every door in Thornfell rang with me, and the refugees in the passes heard their cabin doors hum, and I thought: *good, let them know I'm still here, let them know the door holds.*

"How long can you last, little hinge?" Ossuar asked, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than cruelty. "You are spending yourself. Every moment you stand, you become less. I can wait. I have eaten eternity; I know its flavor."

"That's the thing," I said, and my voice was thinner now, fraying at the edges, and I laughed because laughing was the last weapon I had that he couldn't eat. "You've got it backwards. I'm not spending myself. I'm *investing*. Every second I stand here, a girl named Marta's kids stay asleep. Every second, an inquisitor keeps holding a torch for the right side. Every second, Noor Lockridge gets one moment closer to doing the thing they were born to do, which, I'll warn you now, involves terms and conditions you are absolutely going to hate."

I spread what remained of myself across the threshold, thin as gold leaf, bright as a held breath.

"You want through this door," I said. "Over my unwritten body."

And Ossuar, eldest of the exiled, devourer of heaven, leaned his absence close and began, patiently, to eat.

### Noor

From the mortal side, we could see none of it—all we saw was the light flickering, guttering, like a lamp in wind, and Sef's voice coming through the seam in fragments, arguing, joking, and then, horribly, not joking.

"He's fading," I said. My hands were shaking. In my satchel, wrapped in my grandmother's shawl, the three Ember Shards beat against each other like trapped birds. First Shard from the drowned shrine of Vessa. Second Shard torn from the gate's own heart while Vane watched. Third Shard—

The Third Shard was still behind the light. Still in Ossuar's grip. I had known this since the assembly, and I had said it aloud, and volunteers had stepped forward anyway, and now the volunteer who had bought us every hour of this night was coming apart on the other side of the door, and the final piece of the covenant was in the fist of the thing killing him.

"Noor!" Vane, ash-grey, torch in hand, looking less like an inquisitor every minute and more like a man. "The passes are holding. We have minutes, perhaps. What do you need?"

What did I need. What a question. I needed my grandmother, who had sealed this gate with a weeping god and never told me why. I needed a hundred years of ledger entries to mean something. I needed to not be afraid, and I was so afraid that fear had gone past shaking and come out the other side into a kind of terrible calm, the calm of a person standing at the exact center of their own life.

"I need the gate open wider," I said. "And I need someone to hold it open while I go through."

Vane looked at the seam of white light. At the flickering. At me. Eleven years of closing doors sat on his face and lost.

"Then we'd best hurry," he said, and raised his torch, and shouted for his riders, and the men who had come with sappers and scripture to destroy the oldest door in the world began instead to pry it wide.

The Shards beat against my ribs like a heart learning to beat. Beyond the light, Sef's voice frayed to a whisper, and then to a hum, and then to the sound every door in Thornfell was making—a bell-note, stubborn, diminishing, *holding*.

I stepped to the threshold with my grandmother's ledger under one arm and my whole short mortal life in the other hand, and I walked through.
