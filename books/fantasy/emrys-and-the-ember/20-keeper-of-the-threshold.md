## 20. Keeper of the Threshold

The gate's heart was not what I expected. I had spent twenty years imagining it as a chamber, a room, some secret interior with walls and a floor and maybe a chair, because everything in my experience had walls and floors and usually chairs. Instead there was light that behaved like water and heat that behaved like memory, and Ossuar at the center of it all, vast and burning and *hungry*, holding the Third Shard in a fist the size of the granary he'd burned.

He was not what I expected either. The old books called him the Devourer, the End of Covenants, the God Who Ate His Brothers' Names. What stood before me was an old man made of ash and embers, bent under his own hunger, and when he looked at me his eyes were the loneliest things I had ever seen.

"You came," he said. His voice was a landslide with patience in it. "The little keeper. Granddaughter of Maren Lockridge, who sealed my door and wept while she did it."

"She told me you were a monster," I said. My voice sounded very small in all that light. "She lied to me about a lot of things. It runs in the family."

"Did she tell you why?" He opened his fist—not all the way, just enough—and the Third Shard pulsed against his palm like a heart trying to escape a chest. "Did she tell you what it was like on this side? A hundred years of watching your own name fade from every mouth that ever spoke it? A hundred years of prayers arriving like letters to a burned house? We were not banished, girl. We were *forgotten*, politely, by treaty, and forgetting is the slowest way to kill a god."

Behind me—far behind me, back through the collapsing throat of the gate—I could hear the night going on without me. Vane shouting orders in that voice that had learned gentleness from grief. Sef screaming my name in a register no mortal throat should manage. The refugees holding the passes because I had asked them to, which was either the best or worst thing I had ever done.

I did not look back. Looking back is for people who intend to return.

"I know what forgetting does," I said. "I kept a gate nobody knew existed for twenty years. I wrote in a ledger no one will ever read. I lied to my neighbors until lying was just how my mouth worked." I took a step toward him, and the light parted around my feet like I mattered, which was new. "So here's what I've learned about being forgotten, Ossuar. You can burn the world down demanding to be remembered. Or you can find one person who'll remember on purpose."

"Your grandmother found such a person," he said, and something flickered across the ash of his face. "It was not enough."

"No," I agreed. "Because she built a wall instead of a door. Walls don't need keepers. They need guards. And guards get tired, and gates get cracked, and hungry things slip through the cracks." Another step. The heat should have been cooking me alive; instead it felt like standing too close to a hearth after twenty years of cold. "You're not here because you're evil. You're here because the covenant broke and nobody came to fix it. So I'm coming to fix it."

His laugh shook loose cinders from the dark above us. "With what? You hold two shards and a prayer book. I hold the third and the strength of every god I consumed to reach this door."

"With terms," I said.

And I took out the First Shard—the drowned shrine's shard, Vessa's shard, heavy as guilt in my satchel—and the Second, torn bleeding-light from the gate's own heart three days ago while Vane watched and wavered—and I held them up between us like a merchant displaying wares, which, in a sense, I was.

"The original covenant," I said. "I read it in the First Shard. I have it mostly memorized now, and the parts I don't remember, I'm improvising, so let's both pretend I'm authoritative." I cleared my throat. In the face of a devourer god, I cleared my throat. Somewhere my grandmother was either proud or spinning fast enough to power a mill. "*The gods shall keep the land, and the land shall keep the gods honest. Mortals shall pray truly or not at all. No god shall cross uninvited. No mortal shall forget willingly.*"

"That covenant bound us into exile," Ossuar rumbled.

"That covenant was *broken by everyone*," I snapped, and the words came out hotter than I intended, hot as the shards in my hands. "The gods stopped keeping the land. The mortals stopped keeping you honest—they stopped praying honestly, they prayed for rain and got floods, they prayed for sons and got soldiers. Both sides walked away and then both sides blamed each other, and my grandmother papered over the whole rotten thing with a locked door." I held up the shards. "A locked door isn't a covenant. A covenant needs someone standing in it. Someone who belongs to both sides enough to be trusted by neither. Someone willing to stand there *forever*."

Understanding moved through him slowly, the way dawn moves through a valley. "No," he said. "You are not suggesting—"

"The covenant requires a living keeper," I said. "I read that part too. In your hand, actually. The Third Shard has been showing me things since I walked in. Did you know it does that? It's been screaming its terms at you for a century and you haven't listened once, which tells me the listening was always the hard part." I looked up at his burning, grieving, terrible face. "So here are my terms, Ossuar, Devourer, God Who Ate His Brothers' Names. You give me the Third Shard. You give back what you took—all of it, every god you consumed, poured back into the exile-realm so they can stand again. In exchange, the covenant is reforged tonight, with me as its living term. Not a wall. A threshold. And a threshold has rules: no god crosses uninvited, but no god is forgotten either. Prayers arrive. Names are spoken. Lowmarsh learns to pray honestly again, starting with an apology, which I will draft personally because apparently I'm the one doing everything tonight."

"And what do you receive," he said softly, "in this bargain?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the price. That's the whole price. A keeper stands between and belongs to neither side. I stop being fully mortal tonight. I also never become a god. I become a door. Doors don't get to walk through themselves."

For a long moment there was only the sound of the gate dying around us, stone groaning like a ship in weather, and the two shards in my hands singing their thin bright note, and Ossuar looking at me with those lonely eyes.

"You would spend yourself," he said, "on people who never knew your name."

"They know it now," I said. "Vane read it off the warrant. Very official. There was a seal and everything."

Something happened to his face then that I think, on a mortal, would have been called weeping. On a god made of ash it was more like a hillside deciding, after centuries, to finally rest.

"My brothers feared this day," he said. "They feared a keeper would come who understood the terms better than we did." He opened his fist.

The Third Shard rose from his palm and hung in the air between us, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, which is saying something, because I grew up next to mountains. It was small and crooked and it hummed a note I recognized from my grandmother's humming, from her banned prayer book, from every lullaby she sang me about doors and thresholds and standing watch.

I reached for it.

Here is what nobody tells you about becoming a threshold: it doesn't hurt. That's the horrifying part. It feels like *coming home*. The three shards met in my hands and knew me instantly, the way a key knows its lock, the way a word knows its sentence, and they went into me—not through the skin, nothing so dramatic—they went into me the way morning goes into a room. Light everywhere at once. Every crack in my body filled with ember-glow. I could feel the gate around me, feel its failing stones knitting themselves into my bones, feel the whole long scar between worlds settling into my spine like it had been waiting twenty years for a tenant.

I could feel both realms. That was the gift and it was also, I understood immediately, the punishment. Thornfell on one side: rain on the passes, refugees breathing in the dark, Vane's riders, the gray fields slowly remembering green. Cindral on the other: the exiled pantheon reassembling themselves out of what Ossuar returned, blinking, furious, *remembered*. And me in the middle, the hinge, the seam, the door.

Ossuar knelt. A mountain kneeling. When he spoke, his voice had lost the hunger, or rather the hunger had finally been fed the one thing it actually wanted.

"What am I, now?"

"Terms," I said. My voice came out doubled, mine and something older underneath, and I decided I'd worry about that later. "You're the first clause. 'No god shall cross uninvited.' You're the reason the rest of them behave. Congratulations. It's a promotion."

He laughed—a real laugh, ash and warmth—and stepped backward into Cindral, and the gate closed behind him the way a book closes, gently, having said what it came to say.

* * *

Afterward there was quiet, and then there was Sef.

He came through the settling light at a dead run and stopped short three paces away, staring, because I suppose I glowed now, or shimmered, or otherwise failed to look like the smuggler-pretending-mortal he'd shared tea with. His face did several things in quick succession: relief, awe, horror, and then a very specific expression I recognized from mirrors, the one that means *you absolute idiot, you magnificent absolute idiot.*

"You could have told me," he said. "Before. I would have—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "No. I wouldn't have let you. That's why you didn't tell me."

"See, this is why you're going to make a good voice-of-the-threshold," I said. "Quick study."

"Don't joke. Not yet. Give me a minute where I don't joke." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. When he lowered them, he was crying, which gods apparently still do, even minor ones, even exiled ones, especially the ones who fell through mountains to warn a stranger. "You're stuck. You understand that. You can't leave the gate. You can't—you'll feel every prayer, every crossing, forever, and no one can take it from you, and no one can share it, and—"

"Sef." I reached out and touched his cheek, and my hand was warm and glowing and still, unmistakably, mine. "Twenty years I kept a gate alone and told no one. Now I'm the gate, and everyone knows, and you're staying. Do the arithmetic. This is the richest I have ever been."

He laughed wetly and caught my hand and held it like something that might be taken away, which, to be fair, everything in his life had been.

Behind him, at the edge of the light, Vane stood with his scripture closed and his sappers dismissed and his face full of a man watching his entire cosmology get refounded in real time. He didn't kneel. I respected that. He took off his hat, though, which from Vane was practically canonization.

"Keeper," he said. Formal. Careful. "The Crown will want a report."

"Tell them the truth," I said. "No one will believe it. It's the safest kind of report."

He nodded once, turned, and began walking his men down the mountain, and if his shoulders shook a little in the dark, that was between him and whatever god he prayed to now—one of the remembered ones, probably, or maybe just the idea of terms kept.

Sef sat down beside me at the center of the gate, which was now the center of me, and pulled out the tea things from his pack, because he was Sef, and some rituals survive the end of worlds.

"So," he said, pouring. "Door. Does the door take sugar?"

"One lump," I said, and drank, and felt the prayer of a child in Lowmarsh arrive against my skin like a moth against a window—small, warm, asking for nothing but morning—and I opened for it, just a crack, just enough.

Outside, the ash was settling. Inside, the ledger in my mind turned to a fresh page, and I wrote the first line of it in light:

*Centennial check complete. Gate sound. Keeper present.*

It was the truest thing I had ever written, and I was the only one who would ever read it, and for the first time in my life, that felt less like loneliness than like a job well done—though the dawn coming up over Thornfell was already bringing pilgrims up the pass, and the first of them carried a question I was going to have to learn to answer.
