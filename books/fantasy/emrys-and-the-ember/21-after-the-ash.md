## 21. After the Ash

**Vane**

The report took me three days to write, and I burned two drafts before I settled on the truth, which is a thing I had not done in eleven years of service.

The first draft was the official one. *Inquisitor Vane reports the resolution of the Cindral incident.* I got as far as describing the ash storm before my hand stopped. You cannot write *the resolution* of a thing like that. There was no resolution. There was a woman standing in a doorway between worlds, and then there wasn't a woman anymore—there was a door.

I sat in what remained of the Lowmarsh garrison house with its roof half gone and the rain coming through in gray threads, and I wrote the second draft honestly, and it was worse. *At approximately the fourth hour of the storm, the keeper Noor Lockridge spoke words in the old tongue and bound three fragments of divine substance into her own body. She is now the gate. The gate is now her. Ossuar the Eldest is sealed by covenant terms rather than force, which means, as far as I can determine, that he agreed to be sealed, because the terms were just, and gods keep oaths even when mortals do not.*

No one will believe it. That is not false modesty; it is professional assessment. I have spent my career making people believe impossible things—the guilt of the innocent, the necessity of cruelty—and I know exactly how much weight a report can carry. This one carries none. It reads like the fever dream of a man who lost his son and his faith in the same year and finally went under.

I sent it anyway. Unabridged. With my seal and my name and no request for clarification, because I did not want one. Let some clerk in the capital read it and decide I am mad. Madness, I have discovered, is a comfortable country this time of year.

What I did not put in the report: that when the gate flared white and Noor's voice came out of it—not her mouth, because she no longer had a mouth, only a shape of light and grain and doorframe—I heard her say my name. Not accusingly. The way you say the name of someone you have decided to trust at the last possible moment, when trusting them is the only move left on the board.

And I answered. Gods forgive me—or rather, since forgiveness appears to be an administrative function now, let the record show—I said *yes*. I don't know what I was agreeing to. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything. But when the ash settled and the passes were full of living people who should have died, and the eldest god was folded back behind a threshold made of a stubborn woman's bones, I understood that I had been keeping a covenant too, all these years. A bad one. The Crown's. And covenants, I now knew, could be rewritten by anyone willing to pay for the ink.

So: the third thing I wrote, after the madness and the honesty, was short. One line. I read it aloud to my remaining inquisitors in the ruined chapel where we'd sheltered, all nine of them, soot-faced and hollow-eyed and waiting for orders that would either redeem us or finish damning us.

*"By authority vested in the Office of Inquiry, the mandate over the Thornfell mountains and the settlements thereof is dissolved, effective immediately."*

Brother Callow, who once flogged a shrine-keeper for owning a banned prayer book, asked me quietly if that meant we were going home.

"It means," I said, "that there is nothing here to investigate. The crime was committed by everyone, including us, and the sentence has been served by one person, who volunteered. Go home. Or stay. But stop calling yourselves inquisitors."

Callow looked at me the way men look at a captain who has just ordered them to lay down arms in front of the enemy. Half of them thought it was wisdom. Half thought it was surrender. Both halves were right.

I stayed. Someone has to help rebuild the granaries, and I am, whatever else is true, a methodical man. Methodical men make good masons. We measure twice and cut once and we do not pray while we work, but lately, I confess, I have been standing near the gate in the evenings, in the place where the light pools like lamplight through a doorway, and I have been talking to it.

Not praying. I want to be clear about that. Praying implies asking. I have nothing to ask for. My son is dead and will remain dead, and no god worth the name would undo that even if it could, because the undoing of such things is how Ossuar happened in the first place—a heaven so hungry to matter that it began eating itself to reach us.

I just talk. I tell the door about the granary wall, how we set the stones. I tell it about the widow Maren, who came back with stolen maps and a face like an open wound, and how Noor—how *the gate*—forgave her, or rather how the gate's friends forgave her on its behalf, since a door cannot speak except in weather. I tell it about Sef, who has appointed himself translator between the villagers and their new god-door, and who is, I admit freely, better at mercy than I ever was.

And sometimes, when the wind comes down off the mountain in the hour before dark, the light in the doorway leans toward me. Just slightly. Like a listener.

I choose to believe that means something. I have earned the right to one belief. It cost enough.

**Sef**

Here is what nobody tells you about being a god in a village: you become useful.

I was a god of thresholds. Minor. The kind of divinity that gets remembered as a superstition about knocking on wood. For a thousand years in Cindral I watched the great ones scheme and burn, and when the exile came I went willingly, because I had already learned that heaven was a room full of large creatures and no exits. Then I fell through a crack in the world into the path of a keeper who fed me soup and suspected me the entire time, and I learned that suspicion, done properly, is a form of care.

Now I live in Lowmarsh, in a room above the bakery that smells permanently of scorched honey, and every seventh day I stand in the square and teach people to pray.

You heard me. Teach them to *pray*. Not the way they used to—kneeling, begging, offering up their dead and their debts to anything that might be listening. That prayer is what fed Ossuar. Every desperate plea is a coin, and coins pile up, and something vast and patient eventually comes to count them. No, what I teach is smaller and harder.

"Say what you're grateful for," I tell them. "Then say what you're sorry for. Then say what you need. In that order. And then—" and here I hold up a finger, because this is the part they always want to skip—"then you *listen*, and you accept that the answer might be *no*."

Old Wenna, the baker's mother, asked me last week why the order matters. I told her the truth: because gratitude first reminds you that you've been given things; because sorrow next reminds you that you owe things; and need comes third because need is a child, and children must learn to wait their turn.

"And if the answer's no?" she said.

"Then the answer's no," I said, "and you'll still be standing here tomorrow, and so will I, and that is not nothing."

She looked at me a long moment with her flour-white hands folded, and then she said, "You talk like someone who's been told no by very large things."

"I talk," I said, "like someone whose family told him no for a thousand years."

They are learning. Slowly, badly, with backsliding and arguments in the square about whether the gate counts as a shrine or a neighbor. (It is both. I have ruled on this. Nobody obeys me, which is, I am told, how mortal communities work, and I find I prefer it to worship.)

Maren comes sometimes. The widow. The traitor. She stands at the edge of the gatherings with her children, and she does not pray aloud, and no one sends her away, and I think that silence is the hardest prayer any of them say. Last week she brought bread to the gate and left it at the threshold, and the light leaned toward it the way it leans toward Vane in the evenings, and her youngest daughter laughed, and Maren cried, and I stood between them—between the grief and the laughter, the way I was built to do—and I let none of it pass through me unacknowledged.

That is the work now. Not miracles. Miracles are how this all started, and look where they got us: a gray blight, a burned village, a god eaten from inside by his own hunger to be needed again. No. The work is thresholds. Doors that open slowly. Words said in the right order. A community that has decided, against all evidence and instruction, to be kind on purpose.

Some nights I climb the ridge alone and sit with the gate until dawn. I talk to it in our old language, the one from before the war of broken oaths, and I tell it everything: the bread, the arguments, the granary rising stone by stone under a former inquisitor's exacting eye. And the light listens, and sometimes it flickers in patterns I have decided—arrogantly, perhaps—to interpret as laughter.

Once, near morning, the whole doorway brightened, and through it, faint as breath on glass, I felt the others. My kin. Sealed and sleeping behind a keeper's heart, dreaming their small dreams, kept honest at last by terms they could not break and a keeper who would not leave.

"Hold on," I told them. "Someone good is holding the door."

And then, because I am a god of thresholds and therefore contractually incapable of leaving well enough alone, I added:

"She says you can visit. One at a time. Mind your manners."

The light flickered. I choose to call it laughter.

Below me, Lowmarsh woke—smoke from honest chimneys, hammers on the granary wall, a child running an errand through the wet grass. Small. Broken and mending. Mine, in the way that a stray dog decides a street is its.

I went down to open the bakery.
