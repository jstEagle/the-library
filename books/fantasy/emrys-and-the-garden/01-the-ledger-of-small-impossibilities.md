## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle by the time Elodie Delacroix reached the bottom of the day's entries, and she was glad of it. Glad because the wax guttering meant the work was nearly done, and the work being nearly done meant she could stop pretending she wanted to be anywhere else.

She sat in the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things, third floor, north window, where the light was honest even when nothing else was. Around her rose the shelves: ledgers upon ledgers upon ledgers, two hundred years of them, bound in leather that had been dyed every color a dye-maker could charge for. Red for wishes granted. Blue for wishes refused. Grey for the ones nobody could prove had happened at all. Elodie's own ledger — tonight's ledger — was green, because green was for things still in motion.

*Entry four hundred and eleven,* she wrote. *One bottled echo, medium fidelity. Seller: a widow from Harrow's Bend who could no longer bear to hear her late husband laughing at his own jokes. Purchased at fair value: three years of good sleep, certified non-transferable.*

She dipped her pen. The ink was guild-made, ground with a pinch of forgetting so that whatever was written could not be unwritten. That was the first rule. *Record everything.* Not most things. Not the convenient things. Everything that crossed the threshold got an entry, a price, and a page, or the whole edifice came down like a house of cards in a draft.

The echo sat on her desk in its bottle, a squat green thing stoppered with wax, and even now, even muffled, it laughed. A low, warm, male laugh, the kind that starts in the belly. Every few minutes it would go off, and the bottle would fog faintly from the inside, and Elodie would look up from her writing and watch it the way other people watched fireplaces.

Someone, someday, would buy it. Someone who missed a laugh like that. The guild would trade fairly — second rule, *trade fairly*, stamped inside every cover — and the widow would walk away lighter, and some stranger would carry home a dead man's joy in glass, and everyone would be exactly as happy as they had paid to be.

Elodie wrote the entry, sealed the page with the guild's sigil — a door, slightly ajar — and set the bottle on the transfer shelf with the other goods awaiting claim.

Her hand lingered on it a moment longer than procedure required.

Then she took it back off.

*Entry four hundred and twelve,* she wrote instead, in the margin, small enough that an auditor would call it untidy. *Echo remains bottled. Widow's grief noted as ongoing. No buyer yet. No intervention made.*

Third rule. *Never intervene.* It was the rule written largest, over the doors, carved into the lintel in letters you could read from the street if you knew to look up: **THE GUILD RECORDS. THE GUILD TRADES. THE GUILD DOES NOT INTERVENE.** Corvane said it was the only reason the guild had survived two hundred years while every do-gooder society in history had ended up burned, exiled, or turned into a religion. You want to help people? Help them on your own time. In these rooms, everything is inventory.

Elodie had recited the rules since she was nine years old, when Guildmaster Corvane had found her drawing maps in the mud behind a fish stall and decided that a child who drew what wasn't there was either a liability or an asset, and worth finding out which. Fourteen years later she knew which. She was an asset. She was the best cartographer the guild had ever employed, because she was the only cartographer the guild had ever employed who understood that a map is just a ledger with pictures.

And she was lonely in a way she had never once written down.

*

At half past eleven, the night porter brought up the shadows.

He carried them in a strongbox lined with black felt, and he set it on her desk without a word, because the porters were hired for their silence and paid double for keeping it. Elodie unlocked the box. Inside, coiled like sleeping snakes, were forty-one shadows, each one tagged with a lead seal.

*Entry four hundred and thirteen,* she wrote. *Debt received in kind: forty-one shadows, tendered against an outstanding balance owed by Master Fenwick Odo, lamplighter, whose debts to this guild total eleven years of borrowed luck. Shadows verified: seven large (adult), thirty-four small (children). Condition: excellent. Note: Mr. Odo's children will be noticeably difficult to find after sundown until such time as the debt is renegotiated.*

It was a fair trade. That was the ugly genius of the place. Nobody had cheated anybody. Odo had borrowed luck — the guild's luck, drawn from the deep vaults, the luck that kept ships afloat and fevers mild — and when he couldn't pay in coin, the contract allowed payment in kind, and shadows were legal tender, and there was nothing in any rule book against taking them from children, because children's shadows were the purest currency there was.

Elodie counted them twice, matched the seals against the manifest, and logged each one individually, because the first rule did not have exceptions for tedium.

Shadow number nineteen belonged, according to the tag, to a girl named Wren Odo, age six.

Elodie held it up to the candlelight. It stretched long and thin across the wall behind her desk, a six-year-old shape with a crooked little ponytail and one arm raised, forever, mid-wave.

She put it back in the box. She locked the box. She wrote the entry, all of it, complete and accurate and true.

Then she sat for a long time doing nothing at all, which was the one activity the guild had never figured out how to tax.

*

Here is what Elodie knew about herself, in the way you know the layout of a room you've never left: she was very good at her work, and her work was recording other people's stories, and somewhere in two hundred years of ledgers there was not a single entry that belonged to her.

She had checked. Once, years ago, on a slow night much like this one, she had pulled the index of persons and run her finger down the D's. There were Delacroixes aplenty — a Delacroix who traded his reflection for a commission in the army, a Delacroix who sold the guild the secret of her own name and spent the rest of her life pleasantly unbothered by strangers. But no entry for Elodie Delacroix, cartographer. She appeared in the margins of other people's pages — *map drawn by E.D., fee paid in full* — the way a chair appears in the record of a room. Furniture. Apparatus. Part of the mechanism by which everyone else's story got told.

She had tried, once, to write her own entry. She'd been maybe sixteen. She'd sat right here, at this desk, with a fresh page and the good pen, and she had written: *Elodie Delacroix, born—*

And stopped.

Because the truth was she didn't know. Corvane had found her behind the fish stall and that was the beginning of the record, and before that there was nothing but a mud map and a hunger, and when she tried to reach further back her memory went smooth as the blank side of a chart. Everyone assumed she was an orphan of the border wars, same as half the city. Elodie had privately suspected something stranger, but suspecting was not knowing, and the guild dealt only in the known.

So she had written, instead: *Elodie Delacroix, arrived. Employed. Competent.*

And then she had closed the ledger, because even at sixteen she had understood that those three words were not a story. They were a shipping label.

*

The clock in the stairwell struck midnight, and Elodie blew out the stub of candle out of habit rather than need, because the counting room didn't go dark at midnight. It never went fully dark. The shelves gave off a faint luminescence, the accumulated glow of two centuries of recorded wonders — bottled echoes and jarred last words and pressed flowers from gardens that had been wished out of existence — all of it shedding a pale, patient light, like snow that had learned to hold still.

She gathered her things. Coat, keys, the ring of pens she wore at her hip like a duelist wore swords. At the door she paused, as she always paused, and looked back at the great ledger open on the desk, tonight's green pages fanned under the shelf-light.

Four hundred and thirteen entries. A widow's laughter. A lamplighter's children, shadowless after dark. A hundred smaller trades she'd logged between: a man who sold Tuesday, a woman who bought back a mistake at triple rate, a pair of twins who traded one childhood between them and split the interest.

Every one of them had walked out of the guild with something. Even the widow, even the lamplighter, even the twins — they had stories now, stories with prices attached, stories the guild would keep safe forever whether they wanted them kept or not. Their lives were *in the books*. They existed, officially, on paper, in ink that could not be unwritten.

Elodie Delacroix existed in margins.

She closed the ledger. Locked the drawer. Hung the key on its hook, third from the left, where it had hung every night for fourteen years and would hang every night for the rest of her life, presumably, because that was what the record showed and the record did not lie.

She was halfway down the unlit stairs when the bells began.

This was wrong twice over. First, the guild's bells did not ring at night — they rang at openings and closings and trades, and it was neither. Second, and worse, Elodie had catalogued the bells herself, entry ninety-two of the master registry, and she knew their voices the way she knew her own hands: the low bronze *opening bell*, the bright silver *trade bell*, the cracked old iron bell that hadn't sounded in sixty years and was listed, in her own handwriting, as *dormant; purpose unknown; do not ring.*

All three were ringing at once. And beneath them, a fourth voice she had no entry for at all — a sound less like a bell than like a word being spoken very slowly, very far away, in a language older than the stones the tower stood on.

Elodie stood on the stairs in the dark, her hand flat against the cold wall, and listened to her institution call out into the rainless night like a shopkeeper greeting a customer it had been waiting two hundred years for.

The front doors, she realized, were already unbolted.

She had locked them herself at dusk.
