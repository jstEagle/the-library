## 3. Emrys

The world was too loud.

That was the first thing Emrys understood, or thought he understood, which might have been the same thing. The rain made a sound like a thousand small mouths whispering at once, and the cobbles under his bare feet were cold in a way that felt less like temperature and more like an opinion the stones were having about him. Above the street, lamps burned behind glass, and each flame had a voice—a crackle, a hiss, a low mutter—and all of them were talking, and none of them were talking to him.

He stood in the doorway of a building that smelled of paper and dust and something older underneath, something like the inside of a tree, and he pressed his back against the door because pressing against things made them slightly quieter. Not much. But slightly.

*Deliver it,* said the knowing, which was not a voice but a weight behind his ribs, warm as a stone left too long in the sun. *Deliver it to the one who draws what isn't there.*

The map was against his chest, folded into eighths, wrapped in oilcloth that had gone soft with handling. He had carried it for—how long? He tried to count and the counting came apart like wet thread. There had been roads. There had been a forest where the trees grew in rows too straight to be natural, and a river that flowed uphill for exactly nine steps before remembering itself, and a city of bells where everyone spoke in rhymes, and a winter that lasted only as long as he kept walking through it, ending the moment he stopped, beginning again when he moved on. He remembered these things the way you remember a story someone told you about someone else's life.

What he did not remember was anything before the road.

When he reached for it—the before—it slipped. Not like forgetting. Forgetting was soft-edged, a thing fading. This was different. This was like reaching into a drawer that had been emptied so thoroughly that even the shape of what had been there was gone, and the drawer closed on his fingers with a click he could feel in his teeth.

There had been sunlight. That much survived. Half-remembered sunlight, gold and thick as honey, falling through leaves onto someone's upturned face. The face wasn't his. Or maybe it was. Or maybe it was both, the way a word repeated too many times becomes two words and then no word at all.

*Emrys.*

His name. He had that. It was the one thing the road hadn't taken, the single coin sewn into the hem of him. When the noise got too loud he would say it to himself, silently, the way you touch a talisman: *Emrys, Emrys, Emrys.* It didn't mean anything he knew of. It was just his. A hook in the dark of the world, and he hung from it.

The rain, he noticed now, was wrong.

It fell around him, silver threads catching lamplight, soaking his hair, running down his face—and yet the street beyond the doorway's reach was dry. Dust sat undisturbed between the cobbles. A cat picked its way along a wall with the particular arrogance of a creature whose world is behaving correctly, and not one drop touched it. The rain fell only here, only on him, a column of weather with a diameter of exactly one boy.

He held out his hand and watched water pool in his palm. It tasted of green things. Of cut stems and turned earth and the moment just after a storm when everything breathes. It tasted, though he had no word for this yet, of a garden.

Somewhere above him, a bell rang.

It was a small sound at first, a single bronze note trembling out of the dark like a fish rising to the surface, and then another bell answered it, and then another, and soon the whole night was ringing—not the measured peal of hours or alarms but something arrhythmic and urgent, bells talking over each other, bells arguing, bells the way a crowd sounds when it has just seen something impossible happen in the market square.

Emrys pressed harder against the door.

They're ringing for me, he thought, and did not know how he knew, and knew it absolutely. No one traded for me. No one sent for me. I walked here out of the rain that hasn't fallen yet, and the bells are ringing because the guild does not know what I am, and things the guild does not know must be rung for, the way you ring a bell when a stranger enters a house at midnight, except the stranger is me, I am the stranger, I have been the stranger for longer than I can count—

The door opened behind him.

He fell backward half a step and caught himself, and looked up, and the noise of the world went quiet.

Not all at once. It went quiet the way a storm goes quiet—layer by layer, the rain first, then the wind, then the far-off grumble—until the only sound left was his own heart, which had stopped its frightened drumming and settled into something slower and deeper, a knock knock knock against his ribs, patient, certain, like a fist on a door that has finally been answered.

A woman stood in the doorway holding a lamp.

She was tall and thin and ink-stained to the elbows, with grey threading her dark hair back from a face that was all sharp planes and tired eyes, and she looked at him the way—no. No, that wasn't right. She looked at him the way no one had ever looked at him, because there had been no one, there was only the road, and yet the looking itself was familiar, was *known*, was a thing his body recognized before his mind caught up, the way a compass needle recognizes north without understanding magnetism.

She was the one who draws what isn't there.

He knew it the way he knew his name. Not learned. Remembered. As if the knowledge had been folded into him alongside the map, tucked into the same crease, and now the crease opened and there it was: *her*. Elodie. The name arrived in his head unbidden, complete, wearing its own certainty like a coat.

"You're bleeding," the woman said.

Emrys looked down. His feet, on the wet stone, had left prints that were pink at the edges. He hadn't felt it. The road took a lot of things but it had never managed to take his feet entirely, and they had paid for every mile in skin.

"I—" he began, and the language failed him. What came out was a string of syllables in the old tongue, vowels like river-stones, consonants like branches breaking, and he watched the words land on her face and mean nothing, and something in his chest cracked along a fault line he hadn't known was there.

But she didn't step back. That was the thing. That was the thing he would carry with him through everything that came after, the thing he would return to in the dark hours like a hand returning to a lamp: she didn't step back. She tilted her head, the way you tilt your head at a lock you intend to open, and she said, slowly, in that same voice that meant nothing and somehow meant safety:

"Come inside before the rain decides to fall on the rest of us."

And though he understood perhaps one word in ten, he understood her completely.

* * *

Inside was warmth and lamplight and shelves rising into shadow, thousands upon thousands of books and boxes and jars, and the smell of paper so dense it was almost a flavor. Emrys stood dripping on a floor of dark wood while the woman fetched a towel and knelt—knelt, a grown person kneeling to a child soaked in impossible rain—and began, matter-of-factly, to dry his hair.

Her hands were careful. That undid him more than the kindness would have. Kindness he might have distrusted; he had met kindness on the road, once, from a ferryman who fed him and then asked three questions too many about the bundle at his chest. But carefulness—carefulness was attention, and attention was a form of seeing, and being seen after so long unseen was like sunlight on frostbitten skin: agony and relief in the same burning second.

Half-remembered sunlight. Her lamp was the same color. He kept almost saying so.

She talked as she worked, a low stream of words he couldn't follow, and he let it wash over him because the sound of a voice speaking gently was itself a kind of shelter. Occasionally she would pause, study his face, try a different word, gesture. *Eat?* she mimed at one point, and he nodded so hard his neck hurt, because now that she'd said it he discovered he was starving in a way that felt historical, like the hunger of several winters stacked end to end.

She fed him bread and honey and something salty from a jar, sitting across the table from him with her chin on her laced fingers, watching him eat with the expression of someone reading a very difficult text. And between bites, Emrys became aware of a pressure in his chest, growing, insistent, the weight behind his ribs leaning forward like a dog straining at a leash.

*Now,* said the knowing. *Give it to her now.*

But his hands wouldn't move. Because giving it away meant the errand was done, and if the errand was done, then what was he for? The map was the only reason the road had ever let him walk it. The map was the shape of his purpose, and a boy without a purpose on a road like that one simply... stopped. He had seen it happen, or dreamed he'd seen it, or feared it enough that the fear wore memory's clothing: travelers who finished their journeys and sat down wherever they stood and became part of the landscape, moss on their shoulders, patient as stones.

If he gave her the map, the road would be over.

If he gave her the map, he could stop walking.

Both of these thoughts arrived together, and he realized, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he wanted the second one more than he had ever wanted anything, and that wanting it terrified him more than the bells, more than the rain, more than the empty drawer where his before should have been.

The woman was watching his hands, which had risen without his permission to press flat against his chest, over the hidden map.

"It's for me," she said quietly. Not a question. "Isn't it."

He stared at her. She had said it in her own tongue, the tongue of bread and honey and bells, and he should have understood nothing, and instead the meaning rose up through the words like water through chalk. *For me. Isn't it.*

Or maybe she hadn't spoken his language at all. Maybe some part of him was simply done waiting.

Slowly, watching her face the whole time, ready to snatch it back, ready to run, ready to become moss and stone and a story the road tells other travelers—slowly, Emrys unwrapped the oilcloth and unfolded the map on the table between them.

It was larger than it should have been. It always was. It showed a city he didn't recognize, drawn in lines of brownish-green ink that seemed, in the lamplight, to be still drying. Rivers, walls, streets, a palace at the center—and threaded through the palace, unmistakable even to eyes that had never read a map, something growing. Lines like roots. Lines like paths through a garden.

The woman went very still.

Then she did the thing that confirmed everything, that sealed it, that made the trust in his chest go from a candle-flame to a bonfire: she pulled a pen from behind her ear, leaned over the map, and drew—one small mark, a circle, a cartographer's notation—in the margin.

And the mark stayed.

Emrys let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the forest of straight trees. Because he had seen what happened to marks on this map. He had watched, on a hundred dawns, his own finger-tracings fade like breath off glass, the map shrugging off every attempt to fix it, keeping only what it chose to keep. But her line remained, settling into the paper as if the paper had been waiting for exactly that hand.

The map accepted her.

Of course it did, said the knowing, smug now, warm, like a banked fire. She draws what isn't there. It has been walking toward her longer than you can count. You both have.

The woman looked up from the map and studied him again, and this time her gaze was different—sharper, sadder, full of a question she didn't yet have words for either. Outside, the guild's bells had fallen silent, all their arguments spent. The rain had stopped, though the ground beneath the window remained dry as bone.

Emrys touched his own chest, where the map had been, and found the absence strange and light, like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had forgotten its weight. Then, because he had one thing left that was truly his, he pressed his palm flat against his sternum and said, clearly, in the old tongue:

"Emrys."

Her eyes widened. She pointed at him, and said a word in hers—a question shaped like a name.

He nodded.

And she smiled, tired and crooked and real, and something in the room shifted, some ledger somewhere opening to a fresh page, and Emrys felt—for the first time in longer than he could count—that he had arrived somewhere, and that the somewhere had been expecting him.

Then the woman's smile faded. Her gaze had dropped past his shoulder, to the dark window, and her whole body had gone rigid as a plucked string.

Outside, in the dry street, footfalls. Many of them. Moving toward the door.
