## 4. The Cartographer's Oath

Corvane arrived before dawn, which meant he had never gone to bed.

I heard him on the stairs—the particular tread of a man who believes the building belongs to him because he keeps its keys—and I had just enough time to close the ledger and move my body between it and the door of the map room. Not that moving my body between anything and Corvane had ever worked. He was the kind of man who simply went through things, politely, like weather.

He filled the doorway in his grey guild coat, buttons polished, eyes already cataloguing. Behind him came two clerks I didn't know by name, which was itself unusual; Corvane's clerks were usually drawn from the senior ranks, men and women who had logged a hundred impossibilities without flinching. These two looked flinchable.

"The boy," Corvane said. "Where."

"Sleeping." I kept my voice level. "In the reading alcove. Under three blankets and a copy of *Tides of the Southern Reach*, which he cannot read but seems to find comforting."

"And the map?"

"On me." I did not touch my coat. He noticed that I didn't touch it. Corvane noticed everything; it was the one thing about him I'd ever admired.

"You were seen bringing him broth at the second bell," he said. "And again at the fourth. You've fed him twice, Elodie. We do not feed anomalies."

"We feed guests."

"He is not a guest. Guests arrive by trade." He stepped past me into the map room, and his gaze went to the long table where I'd spent the night pinning down the boy's folded map with brass weights—and failing. Even now, pinned under six weights, its corners breathed. Slowly. Like something asleep.

Corvane looked at it for a long moment. Then he said, quietly, with something almost like reverence: "It's redrawing itself."

"It redraws when unobserved. I've watched it for six hours. It hasn't moved once while I was looking."

"That's not possible."

"No," I agreed. "But here we are. Shall I log it as impossible?"

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, or maybe envy. Corvane had been Guildmaster for nineteen years and in all that time he had never once encountered an impossibility he couldn't file. I used to think that was discipline. Standing there, watching him stare at a map that refused to hold still, I understood for the first time that it might be fear.

"Confiscate it," he said to the taller clerk. "Sealed inventory. Category Nine."

Category Nine. Unclassifiable. In the guild's taxonomy, Category Nine items were not studied, not traded, not returned. They were locked in the deep vault beneath the counting house and forgotten on purpose. There were four Category Nine items in the vault's history. No one knew what happened to them. That was rather the point of Category Nine.

"No," I said.

The word came out of me before I had decided to say it. I felt it leave like a bird startled off a wire.

Corvane turned. The clerks turned. Somewhere below us, the guild's bells rang the hour, and I realized with distant clarity that this was the first time in eleven years of service I had contradicted him aloud.

"I beg your pardon?" he said, very softly.

"You can't confiscate it. Not yet." My heart was going like a hammer, but my voice held, and I clung to that. "The Oath of Arrival. Article One. Every arrival at this guild, by whatever door, by whatever means, must be fully mapped before it may be claimed, classified, or traded. You wrote the amendment yourself, Guildmaster, after the Hollowfield incident. 'Mapping precedes ownership.' Your words. They're carved over the front door."

"The Oath applies to arrivals we intend to trade."

"It applies to arrivals," I said. "All of them. That's what makes it an oath and not a policy."

The tall clerk had stopped halfway to the table, weights in hand, looking between us like a man watching two storms decide about each other. Corvane let the silence stretch. He was good at silence. He had taught me most of what I knew about waiting.

"The boy is an anomaly," he said at last. "Anomalies are logged, sealed, and assessed. That is also the law."

"Then log him," I said. "Log him thoroughly. Log every tooth in his head if you want. But mapping comes first, and mapping takes time, and until the mapping is complete, the map stays with the cartographer assigned to it." I put my hand flat on my own chest, over the coat pocket where the folded thing slept and shifted. "That's me. I'm the only cartographer in this building who has observed it redraw. Jurisdiction follows observation. Third Law of Practice."

"There is no Third Law of Practice."

"There will be by noon. I'm drafting it."

For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—I thought Corvane might laugh. Something moved behind his eyes that could have been laughter's ghost, or its opposite. Then it was gone, filed away wherever he kept such things.

"Eleven years," he said. "Eleven years, and you've never once asked for anything. Never contested an assessment. Never taken a day you weren't owed." He straightened his cuffs. "I confess I wondered what it would take."

"You're wondering now?"

"I'm wondering what the boy showed you." His voice dropped, and for the first time since he'd entered, it wasn't cold. It was careful. "Elodie. Listen to me as your Guildmaster and not as your superior, because right now those are different men. That map is older than the guild. Older than the kingdom, if the binding is what I think it is. Things like that don't wander to our door by accident. Someone sent it. And whoever sends a thing like that does not want it mapped. They want it *used*. If you keep it, you are not protecting it. You are standing where it lands."

"I know where I'm standing."

"Do you?" He looked at me a moment longer, and whatever he saw there made him exhale through his nose. "Forty-eight hours," he said. "You have forty-eight hours to complete the mapping. Then it goes to the vault, and the boy goes into general inventory. Non-negotiable. And Elodie—" he paused at the door, not turning, "—there is a buyer. There is always a buyer. When there is one for this, remember that I told you first."

He left. The clerks left. The bells finished their hour, and the map under its brass weights went on breathing, slow and patient, like it had all the time in the world and intended to use mine.

* * *

Emrys woke when the light came through the reading-alcove window, which meant he woke at the exact moment the sun cleared the guildhouse roof, which meant he had woken at that precise minute every morning since he arrived, as though he were keeping an appointment with it.

He sat up clutching *Tides of the Southern Reach* and looked at me with those eyes that were too old for his face—not old the way tired men are old, but old the way rivers are old, worn smooth by something that had passed through him a long time ago.

"Morning," I said.

He tilted his head. He did that when I spoke—tilted his head like a bird working out whether a sound was danger or dinner. Then he patted the blanket beside him. An invitation. As if I were the guest.

I sat. I had brought paper, ink, and the stub of charcoal I used for field sketches, and I laid them out between us on the blanket like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"We're going to try something," I said. "I know you don't understand me. Humor me anyway."

I drew a cup. A simple cup, three lines and a curve. Then I pointed at the actual cup on the shelf above the alcove, then back at the drawing, then said, slowly: "Cup."

Nothing. He watched my finger travel from drawing to object and back with total attention, the way he watched everything, and I thought: nothing, this is nothing, he's a child found in impossible rain speaking a dead language, what did I expect—

"Cup," he said.

Not quite. It came out rounded at the edges, softened, like a word carried a long way through water. But it was the word. My chest did something unprofessional.

"Yes," I said, perhaps too loudly. "Cup. Again?"

"Cup." Better that time. He touched the drawing with one finger, tracing the curve of it, and then—he did this thing I would see a thousand times after and never stop finding strange—he drew a second cup beside mine. Same shape. But he added steam. Three small curls rising off the top.

"Hot," he said. Or a word that meant hot, or meant warm, or meant *this one is full of something good*. He looked at me expectantly.

"Steam," I said. "Cup. Steam. Hot cup."

"Hot cup," he repeated, satisfied, and I swear the boy smiled like he'd just paid off a debt.

We worked through the morning. Bread. Door. Rain—he already had a word for rain, his own, a soft falling sound like *shhh-ell*, and when I gave him ours he kept both, testing them against each other, clearly deciding they described slightly different rains. Window. Light. I drew the guildhouse cat and he laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and named it something in his own language, a word with a purr built into the middle of it, and I wrote it down phonetically in the margin because some things belong in ledgers even if no category exists for them.

He learned fast. That was the part that unsettled me, later, when I let myself think about it. Not bright-child fast. Fast like dry ground taking water. By midday he had thirty words. By late afternoon, sixty, and he had begun doing the thing where he took the words apart and put them together in orders I hadn't taught, correctly, as though grammar were something he'd merely misplaced rather than never known.

Once, he paused over my sketchbook page—filled with quick drawings of the day's vocabulary—and tapped the empty space at the bottom. Then he looked up at me and said, carefully, in his new borrowed words:

"More. Story."

"Story?"

He nodded, urgent now, and pointed at the drawings one by one—cup, bread, window, cat—as though assembling evidence. Then he pressed both hands flat over his own chest.

"Story," he said again. "Me. More story."

And I understood, finally, what he was asking. Not vocabulary. He was asking me to write him down. To give him, in ink, a self that someone else could read. He had arrived with nothing—not even a language—and he had looked at my sketchbook full of small ordinary things and understood exactly what it was: proof that things existed. That they could be fixed to a page and kept.

"All right," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "Your story. Let's start at the beginning."

I drew a door. Then rain falling on the other side of it. Then a small figure in the rain, holding something folded.

He went very still.

I hadn't meant to draw that part. My hand had done it on its own, the way hands do when they've been thinking harder than their owner. The little figure stood in my clumsy rain-lines, and the folded thing in its arms was unmistakable, and Emrys looked at it for a long time.

Then he reached out and touched the figure's face in the drawing, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. And he said a word in his own language—just one, low, half to himself—and though I couldn't translate it, I knew its shape. It was the same word he'd murmured the first night, half-drowned at my doorstep, before he collapsed. The word he'd said like a name.

Like *his* name, maybe. Or like the name of whoever had sent him walking through a rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Tomorrow," I said softly, closing the sketchbook. "We'll do tomorrow tomorrow."

* * *

I should have slept. I had been awake for the better part of two days, and Corvane's forty-eight hours were already burning down, and somewhere in the city a buyer existed who wanted a foundling badly enough that the Guildmaster had warned me about it unprompted—which, from Corvane, was practically a letter of resignation on my behalf.

Instead I sat alone in the map room with the boy's map pinned under its six brass weights, and I unrolled my oath-scroll on the table beside it, and I read the words I had sworn at nineteen years old, kneeling on cold stone, certain I was joining the most honest institution in the world.

*Mapping precedes ownership.*

*Record truly, trade fairly, intervene never.*

I had recited it a thousand times since without hearing it. Intervene never. Never intervene. Eleven years of logging other people's miracles in a fair hand and shipping them off to buyers, and calling the not-helping *neutrality*, and calling the neutrality *honor*.

Every entry in every ledger I'd ever kept was someone else's story. The bottled echo. The debt paid in shadows. The woman who traded her reflection for safe passage and came back years later asking if we had change. None of them were mine. I had told myself that was the price of the work—that a cartographer draws the coastlines but doesn't live on any of them.

A boy had walked out of an impossible rain to my door, and the first thing he'd asked me for, in a language he didn't have yet, was a story of his own.

I looked at the map. It lay still under the weights, obedient, pretending.

"Don't bother," I told it. "I know what you do when I blink."

I pulled a fresh sheet toward me, dipped my pen, and began—at the bottom of the official mapping record, in the space reserved for the cartographer's certification—to draft the Third Law of Practice. *Jurisdiction follows observation.* It was nonsense, legally. I had invented it that morning out of spite and desperation.

But oaths have to start somewhere. Someone writes the first line.

I was still writing when I heard it: footsteps on the stairs again, unhurried, unfamiliar. Not Corvane's tread. Lighter. Softer. The steps of someone who had never needed to believe the building belonged to him, because he knew it did.

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three raps. Patient.

Through the map room's high window, far off across the rooftops, I could see the road that ran from the capital—and on it, small with distance, a single grey rider coming toward the guild at an easy canter, carrying something sealed and official and wrong.

Forty-six hours left on Corvane's clock.

Someone had decided not to wait.
