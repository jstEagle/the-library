## 6. Lessons in Borrowed Words

The words came to Emrys the way rain comes to dry ground—not all at once, but sinking, sinking, until the whole of him was dark with it.

He had not known, before Elodie, that a mouth could be a door. In the long walking, his mouth had only been for breathing and for the old language, the one that lived in him like water lives in stone—there whether you asked for it or not. But Elodie sat across from him each morning in the guild's back room with paper and charcoal, and she would draw a thing—a cup, a window, her own hand—and say its name, and the name would go into him and *take root*.

*Cup.* He could feel it growing somewhere beneath his ribs.

*Window.* And there.

By the fourth day he had forty words. By the seventh he had so many that at night they pressed against the inside of his skull like seedlings under snow, and he would lie on the narrow guild bed and say them silently to himself, one by one, the way other children might count sheep. *Lamp. Blanket. Floor. Tomorrow.* That last one he loved best. *Tomorrow* was a word that only made sense if you believed you would still be here to see it.

He believed it here. He did not know why. He only knew that Elodie's room smelled of ink and cold tea and something green pressed flat between the pages of her books, and that when she said *Emrys*, she said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable.

"You're learning too fast," she said on the eighth morning, frowning at the slate where he'd written, in wobbly but unmistakable letters: **THE RAIN IS NOT REAL YET BUT IT IS COMING.**

She stared at it a long time. "I never taught you *coming*."

"It taught itself," he said, and heard his own voice do the strange new thing, the bending of borrowed sounds into shapes that fit. "Words are like—" he searched, found nothing, drew instead: a root, branching, drinking from a dark line that meant water. "Like this. I drink them."

Elodie looked at the drawing for longer than a drawing deserved. Then she wrote something in the margin of her own notebook, small, and turned the page away from him before he could read it.

He was learning to read her silences too. That was a language no one had offered to teach him, and yet he drank it as well.

* * *

The lessons happened in the mornings because the mornings were safe. The guild woke slowly around them—boots on stairs, the clink of the ledger room, someone whistling three notes and always forgetting the fourth—and none of it touched Emrys. Corvane had ordered him logged, catalogued, confined to these rooms until "classified," a word Elodie said with her jaw tight. But mornings belonged to the two of them and the charcoal and the growing garden of words.

It was in the afternoons that the fragments came.

They came without warning, the way weather comes. One moment he would be practicing *spoon* and *spilled*; the next, the word would open in him like a door left unlatched, and behind it: sunlight through leaves, the smell of turned earth, a voice saying *careful now, careful, the roots go deeper than you think*—

"Emrys?" Elodie's hand on his shoulder. "You went somewhere."

"A garden." His mouth said it before his mind agreed to. "There was a garden. Someone promised something in it. I can't—" He pressed his palms against his eyes. "It's like holding water. It runs out through my fingers."

"What kind of promise?"

"I don't know the shape of it. Only the weight." He tried to show her with his hands: heavy, held out flat, both arms. "A big promise. A king-sized promise."

Elodie went very still. "A king?"

And there it was—the fragment surfacing, glinting, gone. "There's a king," Emrys said slowly, feeling along the sentence like a man feeling along a wall in the dark. "There's a king and there's a crown and the crown isn't—" He stopped. Something in him flinched away from the next part, some animal wisdom older than any of his borrowed words. "The crown is wrong. The crown is *green*."

"Green," Elodie repeated. She wrote it down. Her pen scratched like a small nervous animal.

"Crown of green," Emrys said, and then, unbidden, in the old language, three syllables that made Elodie's head come up sharply, though he didn't know why, and neither, truly, did he. They had simply risen in him, the way bones rise to the surface of eroding ground.

"What does that mean?" she asked. "Those words."

"I don't know. They're not mine. They're just *in* me." He looked down at his hands, which were a child's hands, ink-stained now, charcoal under the nails. "Everything in me is either very old or very new. There's almost nothing that's just mine."

Elodie put down her pen. For a moment she looked like she wanted to say something large and true, and he watched her decide not to, and file it away in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes.

"We'll find what's yours," she said finally. "That's what maps are for."

* * *

But the palace—

The palace was different. The palace was the locked door inside him, and everyone kept trying to knock on it.

It started small. A clerk passing in the corridor mentioned the capital, and Emrys dropped the slate. Not fumbled—dropped, like his fingers had been called home by something else. The slate cracked clean in two, and he stood looking at the halves and did not know how to explain that for one heartbeat he had seen a throne room full of flowering vines and smiling faces with nothing behind the smiles, and that the seeing had felt like drowning in warm water.

Then a trader came to the guild's door with goods from the eastern road, and he mentioned the king's tax season, just talk, just wind, and Emrys had to lock himself in the washroom and breathe until his ribs stopped trying to escape his chest.

Elodie found him there, sitting on the cold floor with his knees up.

"The palace," she said gently. No—she wouldn't have said it gently; she would have said it plainly, because plainness was her kindness. "What happens when you hear about the palace?"

"Something knows it," Emrys whispered. "Not me. Something in me. And it's *afraid*, and it's not afraid of the palace, it's afraid *for* it, or—" He shook his head hard. "Or afraid of what's inside it wearing it like a coat."

"Wearing it."

"The way I wear these words." He looked up at her. "Borrowed. You gave me *cup* and *window* and *tomorrow*. What if somebody gave the palace to something? What if the palace is wearing a crown the way I'm wearing speech?"

Elodie crouched down to his level, which she did more often now, as if she'd stopped remembering he was an anomaly and started remembering he was a boy. "Emrys. Listen to me. Whatever is in you—it came to *you*. It walked to my door. Things that are worn don't walk."

"This thing walks," he said. "This thing has been walking longer than I've been anything."

She didn't argue. That was one of the things he was learning about her: she did not waste words arguing with truths she couldn't disprove. She just wrote things down, and carried them, and got a certain look—like now—of a woman standing at the edge of a map with no edges drawn yet.

"Tomorrow," she said, standing, offering him her hand. "We'll work on tomorrow."

And he took her hand, and let himself be pulled up, and thought: *tomorrow, tomorrow,* the word going round and round like a coin in a pocket. As long as she kept saying it, he would keep believing it.

* * *

That night he dreamed in the old language again, and for once the dream gave him something whole.

A garden. Walled, warm, the light coming through pear trees in coins of gold. A boy—no. A young man, tall, sleeves rolled, dirt to the elbows, laughing at something. And Emrys stood at the garden's edge knowing, with the terrible certainty of dreams, that the laughing man was a king, or would be, or had forgotten he already was, and that the two of them had made a promise here, in this dirt, under these pears.

*Whatever happens,* the young man had said—or would say, or had always been saying, the tense broken, the grammar of it ruined by time—*whatever happens, remember the garden. Not the crown. The garden.*

Emrys woke with tears on his face and the taste of pear blossoms in his mouth, and lay in the dark saying the only words he trusted:

"Elodie. Elodie. Elodie."

Not because she could hear him. Because her name was the newest root in him, and the deepest, and when everything else in his chest was either stolen or ancient, hers was the one word that felt like it belonged to the life he was living now, in this narrow bed, in this guild of impossible things, learning to speak a language that might be the only weapon anyone had left.

In the morning, he decided, he would tell her about the garden. All of it. Even the parts that frightened him.

He fell asleep before he could notice the sound of boots on the stairs below—Corvane's measured tread, and beside it, lighter, stranger, the tread of someone who did not belong to the guild at all, and who had arrived, as the worst things always did, without knocking.
