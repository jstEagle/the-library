## 7. The Guild's Third Law

Corvane summoned me at dawn, which was the first wrong thing.

The Guildmaster did not summon people at dawn. Dawn was for the ledgers—for the quiet arithmetic of the night's trades, ink drying on entries that would never be read aloud. Corvane himself had taught me that rhythm twenty years ago, when my hands were too small to hold a quill properly and he'd said, *Elodie, the guild does not sleep, but it pretends to. Learn the pretense or you'll never survive here.* I had learned it. I had lived inside it so long that its walls felt like my own bones.

So when a junior clerk appeared at my door with grey eyes and a greyer voice, saying only, "The Guildmaster will see you now," I knew before I reached the stairs that something in the ledger had gone out of balance.

Emrys was still asleep in the map room.

I had argued for that arrangement myself—*he cannot be left unattended, Guildmaster, he speaks a language that predates our records, someone must watch him*—and Corvane had granted it with a nod so smooth it should have warned me. Corvane never granted anything without already knowing what it cost. I had told myself his ease meant acceptance. It meant patience.

The stairwell smelled of tallow and old paper, as always. The bells of the guild hung silent in their tower, the same bells that had rung for Emrys though no trade had been struck for him. I passed the Hall of Recorded Wonders on my way up—the bottled echo from Tuesday still sat on its velvet cushion, labeled in my own hand: *One echo, unsourced, answers questions asked in grief.* Beside it, the jar of shadows paid against a miller's debt, faintly restless even in daylight, pressing at the glass like moths. My cataloging. My competence. Twenty years of entries in other people's stories.

Corvane's office occupied the top floor, beneath the bell tower, where he could hear everything the guild said about itself. He was standing at the window when I entered, hands folded behind his back, watching the city wake below us. He did not turn around.

"Elodie." My name, spoken the way he spoke all names—precisely, without warmth, as if filing them. "Sit."

I sat. The chair across from his desk was hard oak, deliberately. Everything in this room was deliberate. That was what I had loved about it, once: a place where nothing was accidental, where every impossible thing was given a number and a shelf and a price. Order against chaos. I had believed that with my whole mapped heart.

"You've been teaching the boy to draw," Corvane said.

"I've been mapping him. As the oath requires. All arrivals are mapped before they're claimed—"

"The oath applies to arrivals." Now he turned, and his face was the face I knew best in the world after my own: long, dry, patient as a ledger line. "Tell me, Elodie. What is an arrival?"

I hesitated. This was a catechism; we had both memorized it decades ago. "A person or object entering the guild through trade, discovery, or accident, recorded within one bell's toll of entry."

"And what is an anomaly?"

"An arrival that resists classification."

"Good." He came around the desk and stood over me, and there was something in his posture I had never seen before—not anger, not disappointment. Carefulness. As if I were a bottled echo that might answer questions asked in grief. "And what is the third law?"

My mouth went dry. There were two laws. Every clerk learned two laws. *Record everything. Trade fairly.* They were carved above the door in letters deep enough to hold rainwater. I had walked under them ten thousand times.

"There is no third law," I said.

"Every law has a third clause," Corvane said quietly. "You simply were never senior enough to hear it. Congratulations. As of last night, you are." He folded his hands. "An anomaly that cannot be classified must be traded or destroyed. Those are the options. There has never been a third option. There is no shelf for a thing that refuses to be shelved."

The words landed in me like stones dropped into still water, and the ripples went outward and outward, touching everything.

"Destroyed," I repeated. The word tasted like ash and copper. "He's a child."

"He is an unclassified entity wearing the shape of a child. He arrived through no door, paid no toll, rang bells that were not rung for him. His map redraws itself in violation of at least four principles of cartography that you, of all people, should be able to recite." Corvane's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "You of all people, Elodie. You taught the juniors that sentiment is a clerical error."

"Sentiment is not—" I stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower. "Guildmaster. The boy knows things. He speaks a language older than any kingdom in our records. The map shows the capital. It shows the palace. If we destroy him, we destroy the only witness to whatever is happening behind those walls."

Something flickered across Corvane's face then—there and gone, quick as a page turning—and I would spend the rest of that day trying to decide whether it had been guilt or recognition.

"Yes," he said. "About that."

* * *

He opened the drawer of his desk and took out a document.

I want to record exactly what happened in my body when I saw it, because later—when everything burned—I needed to remember that the betrayal did not arrive all at once. It arrived in stages, the way a flood arrives: first the sound, then the smell, then the water at your ankles, and by the time you understand, you are already drowning.

The document was a writ. Heavy paper, sealed in green wax, and the wax had been stamped with the royal seal of King Aldous Vey. But the seal was wrong—or rather, it was right in a way that made my cartographer's eye stutter. Vines grew across it. Not printed vines, not engraved ones. Grown. Tiny leaves unfurling from the wax itself, green and alive, and when Corvane set the writ on the desk between us, I swear one of the leaves turned, slowly, toward the window light.

"This arrived eleven days ago," Corvane said.

Eleven days. I counted backward without wanting to. Eleven days before a soaked boy knocked on our door speaking a dead language, carrying a map that remembered.

"Before him," I said. My voice sounded far away, like someone else's entry in someone else's ledger. "It came before he did."

"It did."

"What does it say?"

Corvane slid it across the desk. I read it twice because the first time, the words refused to arrange themselves into meaning:

*To the Keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. The Crown seeks a foundling, age approximately eight years, who shall come to your door bearing a self-amending chart. The Crown offers forty thousand crowns, the deed to any holding within the realm, and the Crown's enduring gratitude. Deliver the foundling unharmed and unspoken-with. The matter concerns the health of the realm.*

Unspoken-with. Do not speak with him. Do not let him tell you anything. Because whatever he tells you might make it harder to hand him over.

"They described him," I whispered. "They described the map. Corvane—they knew. The palace knew he was coming before he ever reached our door." I looked up. "What else have you not told me?"

"Elodie—"

"No." I stood, and my chair scraped back loud in the quiet room. "No. You let me take him in. You let me feed him, teach him, sit with him while he cried in a language nobody understands—you let me do all of that while a royal writ offering forty thousand crowns sat in this drawer. How long? How long have you known what he is?"

"That," said Corvane, "is precisely the question I have spent two hundred years being unable to answer."

Two hundred years.

The number hung in the air between us, absurd, impossible, and yet—this was the Guild of Impossible Things. We had a shelf for impossible numbers. We had a taxonomy. And underneath my shock, the old clerk in me reached for the classification reflexively, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"Explain," I said.

Corvane walked to the window again. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, wearier, stripped of the Guildmaster and down to something older underneath.

"When I was young, the guild was different. Smaller. Meaner, some said, but I say more honest. We kept three laws then, not two. Record everything. Trade fairly. And the third—" he touched the windowsill, absently, the way a man touches a scar— "the third was written after the Famine Years. After the Verdant Writ."

The word *Verdant* went through me like cold water. I had never heard it before. I was certain I had never heard it before. And yet somewhere behind my eyes, a page I had never cataloged seemed to turn.

"The king wished to end the famine," Corvane said. "Kings wish, Elodie. It is nearly all they do. But Aldous Vey wished through channels no one had used in centuries, and something answered. Something that grants wishes the way a fire grants warmth—by consuming everything that burns. It ended the famine. And it began eating memories. First the countryside's. Then the court's. Then the king's own, year by year, crown by crown, until the man on the throne was mostly garden." He turned to look at me. "The guild could not fight it. We tried. We lost nine keepers in a single winter, and their names are in a ledger you will never see. So we did what the guild does. We made a trade."

"What kind of trade?"

"We took what the Writ had stolen—the king's childhood, his earliest years, the part of him the garden hadn't finished digesting—and we gave it a shape. A vessel. A fragment sent beyond time, beyond the Writ's reach, to grow whole and return one day as a key." Corvane's hands, always so steady, closed briefly into fists. "We called the project many names in the old ledgers. The last one was Emrys."

The room tilted. Or I did. I gripped the arm of the chair.

"He isn't a messenger," I said. "He isn't an anomaly. He's a piece of the king."

"He is a failsafe that was lost," Corvane said. "For two hundred years, Elodie. The guild that made him fell apart and rebuilt itself and forgot why. The third law survived as habit without memory—we destroyed or traded what we couldn't classify because once, long ago, classifying wrongly was how the Writ got its hooks into us. And then eleven days ago a writ arrives from a palace grown over with living green, describing a boy who hasn't arrived yet, and I understood that the debt had come due." He met my eyes. "I did not tell you because I hoped I was wrong. I hoped the boy was only a boy. I have watched you these past weeks, and I know what you will do with the truth, and I am an old man who did not want to watch it."

"But you're telling me now."

"Because the buyer's emissary arrives today."

* * *

The word *today* moved through me like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

"Today," I repeated.

"At noon. In the receiving hall. Forty thousand crowns, Elodie. Deeds. Gratitude." He said the words flatly, a clerk reading an inventory. "The council voted last night. Four to one. The guild cannot refuse the Crown—it never has. And the boy cannot be classified, and the third law is the third law, and I am sorry, truly, but the law is the only thing that has kept this institution alive through twenty centuries of impossible things. If we bend it for one child, we bend it for everything, and then we are not a guild. We are merely people with a large house."

"Then be people," I said. "Gods, Corvane. Be people with a large house."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion, as if I'd struck him—and I realized with a strange, distant clarity that I had never once raised my voice in this room in twenty years. I had been the perfect clerk. The quiet competence. The woman whose loneliness fit neatly between ledger lines.

"You knew him," I said, softer now. "Didn't you? Two hundred years ago. You were there when they made him."

Corvane did not answer. He did not need to. His silence was an entry in itself, and I had spent my whole life learning to read silences.

"I need until noon," I said.

"You have until noon," he said. "And Elodie—" I paused at the door. "Whatever you're considering. Consider what happens to him if you fail. The Writ doesn't kill what it takes. It keeps. It grows things in the dark for centuries. Is that mercy?"

I didn't answer. I went down the stairs two at a time, past the Hall of Recorded Wonders, past the bottled echo and the restless shadows, past twenty years of my own careful handwriting, and every label on every shelf looked different to me now. Not order. Cages. A museum of stolen things, each one priced and filed so no one would ever have to ask what it wanted.

* * *

Emrys was awake when I reached the map room.

He was sitting cross-legged on the floor with the chalk I'd given him, drawing on the flagstones—drawings I now recognized as his language, those looping root-like characters that predated every script in our archives. The shifting map lay beside him, and as I watched, it trembled at its folds and redrew a river that had not been there a moment before.

He looked up at me, and his face did the thing it always did—that fierce, inexplicable trust, arriving whole, as if he had been walking toward me longer than he could count. He said something in his old tongue, soft, questioning. Then, in the borrowed words I'd taught him, carefully, like a child carrying water:

"You are sad. Why are you sad?"

Because in four hours men will come to buy you. Because the institution I gave my life to made you and lost you and sold you before you ever drew breath. Because every story in this building is someone else's, and now yours is about to become one of them, filed and priced and delivered unspoken-with.

"I'm fine," I said, and knelt beside him, and picked up a piece of chalk.

Outside, the guild bells began to ring noon—early, wrong, rung by human hands for the first time in living memory. Hooves sounded in the courtyard below. Grey cloaks. A voice asking politely to be received.

Emrys went rigid. The chalk snapped in his small fingers, and his eyes went wide and dark with a terror older than kingdoms, and I understood, finally and completely, that I had already chosen. I had chosen weeks ago, at a midnight door, in the rain that hadn't fallen yet.

I just hadn't known it until now.

"Emrys," I said, taking his hand. "How fast can you learn the word *run*?"
