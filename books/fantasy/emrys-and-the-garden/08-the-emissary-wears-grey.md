## 8. The Emissary Wears Grey

The word for *hide* was one of the first words Elodie taught me.

She drew it in her notebook — a small stick figure crouched behind a box, a second figure with a question mark over its head. She said it twice: *hide. Hide.* And because I was learning to be a boy made of borrowed words, I took the word and kept it, the way roots take water, the way I took everything she gave me.

I did not know yet that I would need it so soon.

* * *

It began with the bells.

The Guild of Impossible Things had bells for everything. Bells for arrivals, bells for trades, bells for debts paid and debts defaulted. Elodie had taught me their language in the same week she taught me mine. The morning bell was two notes, low and patient. The trade bell was three, quick as a heartbeat. The bell that rang when something arrived that no ledger could hold was a single long tone that seemed to come from under the floor rather than above it.

On the morning the emissary came, none of those bells rang.

Instead there was a fourth sound — one I had never heard, one Elodie's face said she had never heard either. It came down from the tower like a held breath being released slowly, a note that bent in the middle, that wavered the way light wavers through water. Corvane appeared at the top of the stairs before the sound finished dying, which told me he knew what it meant, and that what it meant was old.

"Grey is coming," he said. He did not look at me. That was its own kind of warning. "Elodie. The foundling stays out of sight. Whatever you hear, whatever you're asked — the boy was never here."

"He was logged," Elodie said. "You logged him yourself. Anomaly seven-one-four."

"Then anomaly seven-one-four was never here." Corvane's voice was flat as an unmarked map. "Grey does not ask questions he doesn't already know the answers to. Grey asks questions to see what you do while answering them." His eyes finally moved to me, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before — not the cold arithmetic I'd learned to expect, but something older and wearier, almost like grief wearing a clerk's face. "Boy. Do you understand hiding?"

I nodded.

"Do you understand that if you are found, you will be taken, and no law of this guild will stop it, because the person asking will have brought laws of his own?"

I nodded again. It was not entirely true. I understood the shape of the words. I did not yet understand their weight. Words were still new to me; they sat on my tongue like coins from a country I'd never visited.

"Good," Corvane said, and left, and his leaving was quick in the way of men who have decided not to say a larger thing.

* * *

Elodie took me down.

Not out — down. Beneath the guild there were cellars where the impossible things slept between trades, and beneath the cellars there was a place that wasn't on any floor plan, a crawlspace behind a wall of shelved ledgers where the dust lay in patterns too regular to be accidental. Someone had hidden here before. Someone had hidden here often, and for a long time, and had scratched small marks into the brick at knee height — tally marks, hundreds of them, counting days or hours or something else entirely.

I put my fingers against the marks. They felt warm.

"Emrys." Elodie crouched in front of me, and her hands were steady but her eyes were doing the thing they did when she was translating fear into competence. "Listen to me. This man — Grey — he works for the palace. For the king."

The word *king* went through me like cold water through cloth.

I had said it myself, once, in my broken first words to her. *A king. A crown of green. A promise.* But hearing it from her mouth, in her careful teacher's voice, was different. Hearing it from her made it real in the way that things drawn on maps are real even if you never go there.

"You mustn't make a sound," she said. "No matter what you hear. No matter—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "No matter what you remember. Can you do that?"

I didn't answer fast enough, and she saw it.

"Emrys."

"I can try," I said, in my new voice, my stolen-and-returned voice, and she kissed the top of my head the way I think mothers do, though I wouldn't swear to it, and then she closed the shelf behind me and the world became a slit of candlelight and the smell of old paper.

* * *

He came at noon, and the guild grew quiet around him the way a forest goes quiet around a wolf.

I heard him before I saw him, because the shelf slats had gaps, because dust carries sound, because some part of me had been listening for this voice since before I had ears. Footsteps first — measured, unhurried, the steps of someone who has never once needed to hurry because everything arrives to meet him. Then the door. Then voices, low, Corvane's and another, and the other voice was—

The other voice was *warm.*

That was the horror of it. I had expected cold. I had built, in the dark behind the shelves, a picture of the palace's man: grey robes, grey voice, a face like a closed ledger. And instead the voice that came down the corridor was the warmest sound I had ever heard. It was low and it was kind and it moved the way honey moves, slow and golden, and every word it touched turned gentle.

"*Master Corvane. You honor the crown.*"

"*The crown honors itself,*" Corvane said, dry as August. "*What does it want with mine?*"

A soft laugh. "Always the same. Two hundred years, and you greet every visitor like a debt collector." A pause, footsteps, the creak of a chair accepting weight. "I've come about a boy."

My heart stopped. I mean that almost literally — I felt it hesitate in my chest like a bird deciding whether to keep flying.

"There are many boys," Corvane said.

"There is one boy," the warm voice said, "who walked out of nowhere carrying a map that redraws itself. There is one boy who speaks a dead language. There is one boy whose arrival rang the Foundling Bell — a bell, Master Corvane, that has not sounded since the reign of Aldous the Fifth." Another pause, longer, and in it I could hear the smile, the way you can hear rain coming before it falls. "Shall I go on? I have a great deal more. I have been given a great deal of time."

"You have been given nothing," Corvane said. "And neither have we. The guild's records are sealed. Its arrivals are its own. If a boy passed through, he passed through, and where he went is no business of the crown."

"The crown," the emissary said mildly, "disagrees."

Behind the wall, in the dark, I pressed both hands over my mouth.

Because here is what I have not yet told you, what I did not know until that moment, what rose up in me like floodwater breaking a door: *I knew that voice.*

Not the way you know a voice you've heard recently. Not the way you know the voice of your teacher after three weeks of lessons. I knew it the way the ground knows rain. I knew it from somewhere deeper than memory, from somewhere memory is *made*. That voice had sung to me. That voice had sung to me in a garden, in sunlight, in a time I could not reach but that reached for me now with both hands — lullabies in the old language, the language older than kingdoms, the language I had arrived speaking, songs about a green crown and a sleeping year and a promise made under a tree whose name I had forgotten but whose shadow I could feel, right now, falling across me across two hundred years.

He sang to me. The man in grey sang me to sleep.

And I remembered — fragments, burning, unbearable — strong arms lifting me. A laugh like the front door of summer opening. A hand much larger than mine closing around my whole fist. And always the voice, the warm voice, saying words I suddenly understood without translation, words that had been waiting inside me all this time like seeds in frozen ground:

*Sleep, little sprout. Grow slowly. The garden will wait.*

I made a sound. I don't know how loud. A small broken thing escaped through my fingers, and I clamped down on it, and I froze, listening, listening—

"—Did you hear that?" the emissary said.

"I heard the cellar settling," Corvane said. "This building is older than your king. It groans."

"Mm." Paper rustled. "Old buildings hold so much. Old buildings remember everything done in them. That's rather the problem with old things, isn't it, Master Corvane. They remember." The warmth in the voice had not gone away. That was the worst part. The warmth stayed, spread itself like butter over the threat underneath. "Well. I am a patient servant of a patient master. I'll leave an offer. Consider it — courtesy. The crown does not forget its friends, nor forgive its obstacles, but it does *courtesy* beautifully."

Something heavy set down on wood. A seal clicked against a surface.

"When you're ready to talk about the boy," the warm voice said, rising, moving toward the door, and oh, oh, it was closer now, it was passing along the very wall I hid behind, close enough that I could hear the fabric of his robes whisper against stone, close enough that I could smell him — and he smelled of roses, of cut stems and green sap, of a garden — "you know how to reach the palace. Everyone knows how to reach the palace. These days, Master Corvane, the palace reaches back."

The door opened. Rain-sound came in — real rain, the first I'd heard since arriving, though Elodie had told me the storm that soaked me that first night had fallen three counties east and a week ago. Time ran strangely near me. I was learning that about myself, and hating it.

"One more thing," the emissary said from the threshold, and his voice dropped its warmth all at once, like a mask lifted, and what was underneath was worse than any coldness — it was *tender*, it was grieving, it was the voice of a man looking at a grave. "Tell whoever is keeping him — tell them the king misses his son."

The door closed.

* * *

I don't know how long I stayed behind the wall.

Long enough for the candlelight in the gap to shift color. Long enough for the tally marks at my knees to seem less like scratches and more like a message, hundreds of days of someone hiding exactly where I hid, from exactly what I hid from, and I thought with a strange calm: *the guild has done this before. The guild has hidden boys from the palace before. Or one boy. Over and over. Like a tide.*

When Elodie opened the shelf, I was crying without any of the noise of crying, tears just falling out of me like they'd been scheduled, and she gathered me up and I let her, and we sat on the cellar floor among the sleeping impossibilities while the guild overhead resumed its ordinary bells.

"He sang to me," I said into her shoulder.

Her body went still.

"The man in grey. He sang to me. In the garden. Before—" My new vocabulary failed me. I had words for trade and debt and anomaly and oath, words Corvane used like knives, but I did not have words for whatever was happening inside my chest. "Elodie. He isn't hunting me. Hunters are cold. He *loves* me. He loves me and he's going to take me anyway, and I don't—"

"Slowly." She held my face in her hands. "Breathe. Tell me again. From the beginning."

So I told her. The lullabies. The arms. The garden. The words in the old language that had lived in me all along, untranslated, waiting. As I spoke, her expression changed by degrees, the way dusk changes — not all at once, but steadily, toward dark.

"The royal seal," she said at last. "The writ he left. Corvane will want it locked away. Which means I need to see it first." She stood, pulling me up with her, and there was iron in her voice now, the iron I'd begun to recognize as the true metal underneath her gentleness. "Stay close to me. Don't touch anything. And Emrys—"

"Yes?"

"If you remember anything else — anything at all — you tell me. Not Corvane. Me." Her thumb brushed my cheek, wiping a tear I hadn't noticed. "Whatever you are, whatever they made you to be, your memories belong to *you*. That's the one law I believe in."

We climbed the stairs together.

* * *

The writ lay on the council table where the emissary had left it, and even Corvane stood back from it as though it were a sleeping animal.

It was a scroll, sealed with the royal crest — a tower beneath a sun — but the wax had not been allowed to simply be wax. Moss grew from it. Green threads crept out from under the seal and down the parchment's edge, fine as veins, alive, and as I leaned closer I saw that the moss was *moving*, very slowly, growing even as we watched, the way frost grows across a window.

"It's been sealed a long time," Elodie whispered.

"No," Corvane said. His voice had gone quiet in the way of a man reading his own death warrant. "Sealed things don't grow. This seal was fresh this morning. The moss came *with* it. Through it." He looked up, and for the first time since I'd known him, Guildmaster Corvane looked afraid. "Two hundred years I've kept this guild neutral. Two hundred years, and the palace sends us a writ grown over with living green, and asks after a boy, and calls him—"

"His son," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"That's what the emissary said," I went on, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, because the words were arriving from somewhere deeper than me, rising like water finding its level. "He said the king misses his son. But that isn't right. That isn't what he meant." I stepped toward the table. The moss on the seal stirred, faintly, turning toward me the way flowers turn toward light. "The king doesn't miss a son. The king *is* missing. Something took the son-shaped part of him, the childhood part, the part that sang in gardens and believed in promises—" My hand rose without my permission. "And sent it walking. For two hundred years. Until it found the one who draws what isn't there."

My fingers touched the seal.

The moss bloomed open like a small green eye, and the writ unrolled itself down the table, and the words on it were in my language — the old language, the first language, the one Elodie had spent weeks teaching me to leave behind — and I read them aloud before anyone could stop me, and the reading of them felt like a door opening in my chest:

*To the fragment of our king, wherever he has wandered: come home. The garden grows hungry. The garden grows toward you. Come home, little sprout, before it learns to walk.*

The candles guttered. Somewhere far above, the Foundling Bell rang once, unpulled, unmanned — ringing for me, again, though I had never been traded for.

Corvane's face had gone the color of old paper. Elodie's hand found mine and gripped it hard.

"We leave tonight," she said.

And Corvane, keeper of the third law, breaker of nothing, did not argue.
