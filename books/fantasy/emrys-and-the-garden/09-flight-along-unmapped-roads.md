## 9. Flight Along Unmapped Roads

We left through the window.

Not the door—the door was being watched, and besides, doors are for people who intend to come back. I had spent eleven years entering and exiting the Guild of Impossible Things like a tide, punctual and harmless, and I had never once considered that leaving might be an act. It was. My hands shook so badly on the sill that Emrys had to steady me, which was absurd—a boy of perhaps ten years holding up a grown woman—but his grip was certain as a root, and I let it hold.

Below us, the alley ran grey with pre-dawn. Above us, the guild's bell began to ring.

It was not the hour bell. It was not the trade bell or the anomaly bell. It was the third bell, the one I had heard rung exactly twice in my career, both times for things that had to be unmade rather than recorded. The sound went through my chest like a hook through cloth.

"They're waking the hunters," I said. "Emrys. We have to go *now*."

He didn't ask what hunters were. That was the thing about him that still unsettled me, weeks into our flight from every rule I owned—he understood urgency in his bones, in whatever old language his bones spoke. He simply climbed down after me, barefoot on wet brick, and waited at the bottom with his map clutched to his chest like a bird he'd caught.

The rain that hadn't fallen yet was starting to fall now. I could feel it coming the way you feel a sneeze—pressure behind the world. Good. Rain would blur scent, blur sound, blur the neat little records of a night watchman who would swear later that no one had passed. I had logged enough evasions in my time to know how they worked from the inside. I had just never imagined I'd be the entry.

"Elodie." He said my name carefully, the way he said all his new words, tasting them. "Where?"

"The capital," I said, and hated how the word sounded—like a sentence being pronounced. "Where the palace is. Where your garden is."

His face did something complicated. Fear and longing fighting over the same ground. But he nodded, and he took my hand without being asked, and we ran.

* * *

Here is what they don't teach apprentices about cartography, because most cartographers never need it: a road is not a thing. A road is an agreement. It is a place where enough feet have agreed to walk that the earth gives up resisting. Remove the agreement, and there is no road—only country, indifferent and continuous.

But an oath-sworn cartographer of the Guild of Impossible Things carries something else. She carries ink that remembers her hand. And ink that remembers can *insist*.

I had drawn escape routes as an exercise, years ago, the way you practice scales. Little theoretical roads across blank vellum, annotated with the guild's stamp: HYPOTHETICAL. NOT FOR USE. Corvane had smiled at them and called them charming. What he never understood—what I was only understanding now, at a dead run through streets going silver with rain—was that nothing drawn by an oath-sworn hand is ever entirely hypothetical. The roads existed. They existed the way a held breath exists: coiled, patient, waiting for permission.

Permission was a drop of blood on the page and a word in the old cartographer's grammar, and I gave both at the mouth of Tanner's Row, crouched over my satchel with Emrys shielding the paper from the rain with his whole small body.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"A road," I said. "For about as long as I can keep drawing it."

I stood, and the road rose out of the mud like a whale surfacing.

That is the only way I can describe it. One moment there was the ordinary filth of Tanner's Row, and the next there was a lane running straight as a ruled line between the warehouses—cobblestones still glistening wet from my ink, hedgerows on either side that had never been planted, growing even as I watched, their leaves the exact green of fresh verdigris. It smelled like rain on hot slate. It smelled, faintly and impossibly, like my childhood.

"Oh," said Emrys, in his own tongue first, then translating himself with visible effort: "Oh, it's *awake*."

"Yes," I said. "And it will stay awake exactly as long as I keep drawing. Which means, Emrys, that we are about to find out whether I can draw and run at the same time."

I could. Barely. I walked backwards down the impossible lane with the vellum braced against my forearm, pen moving, extending the road stroke by stroke ahead of us while the world behind us closed like water behind a swimmer. Where the ink ended, the road ended; where the road ended, Tanner's Row resumed, innocent and empty. Twice I glanced back and saw the lane dissolving into ordinary mud mere yards behind Emrys's heels, and twice my hand wanted to shake, and did not, because a cartographer's hand does not shake while the line is live. That law I kept. It was, I realized with a wild laugh that startled a rook off a chimney, the only one left.

Because I had broken the rest. All of them, in one night.

*Record everything.* I had stolen an anomaly out of the guild's custody instead of logging him.

*Trade fairly.* There was no fair trade that covered a child. The emissary's fortune sat in Corvane's strongroom now, I had no doubt, weighed against a boy, and found sufficient.

*Never intervene.*

My pen dug hard enough to spatter. Never intervene. Eleven years I had written other people's stories into ledgers and called it service. A bottled echo, catalogued and shelved. A debt paid in shadows, witnessed and stamped. Every grief and wonder in the city passing through my hands, and my hands only ever *held* things—they never once *helped*. Neutrality, Corvane called it. I had called it that too. Walking backwards down a road made of my own insistence, with a stolen child's hand in mine, I finally called it what it was: complicity with excellent handwriting.

And the terrible thing—the thing I could not stop feeling, that bloomed in my chest warmer than guilt, hotter than fear—was that it felt like *waking up*.

"Elodie," Emrys said. "You're smiling."

"I'm committing crimes."

"You're smiling."

"Both things can be true." I extended the line another fifty yards. The hedgerows leaned over us, and I noticed—with the part of the mind that never stops taking inventory, even mid-flight—that their leaves were unfurling in patterns. Spirals. And within the spirals, if I let my eyes unfocus: letters. His letters. The dead language, growing along my invented road like ivy along a wall.

The map was leaking into my work. Or my work was listening to him.

I decided not to think about that yet. There is a skill every ledger-keeper learns, and it is not bookkeeping. It is the discipline of the deferred entry: *noted, to be resolved later.* I noted the hedges. Later.

* * *

We traveled three days that way, more or less. Time goes strange when the road under you exists only by continuous assertion. We slept in dells that appeared when I drew them and were gone by morning—we'd wake in open fields, our blanket of hedge-shadow withdrawn like a tide. We ate bread bought from a farmwife whose lane I briefly extended to her doorstep; she charged us fairly and remarked that she'd always wondered where that side path went, and I didn't correct her, because neither of us knew.

Emrys learned as we went. Not just words—I was feeding him those steadily, pointing and naming, watching him absorb vocabulary the way dry ground absorbs the first rain—but *travel*. He had walked two hundred years to reach my door, by whatever measure, but he had never walked anywhere *with* someone. He kept checking, every hour or so, that I was still beside him. Not anxious. Just confirming, the way you touch a pocket to confirm a coin.

"You could go faster without me," I told him once, on a ridge I'd drawn above a valley of fog. "Your legs are younger."

He looked at me with genuine puzzlement, the deep kind, the kind that has to translate itself twice before it can speak.

"The walking isn't the point," he said.

I had no entry for that. I wrote it in my head anyway, in the margin where I was beginning to keep a different kind of ledger.

At night he studied the map by firelight—my fire, drawn and burned and gone—and I studied him studying it. The capital sat at its center now, rendered in obsessive detail: the palace with its towers, and threading through the towers, through the walls themselves, the garden. Green veins in the stone. Paths that spelled words I couldn't read but he could; sometimes his lips moved over them silently, like a man praying in his sleep.

"It's remembering," he said on the second night, unprompted. "Maps aren't supposed to remember. You told me. Maps say what is."

"This one says what was," I said. "Or what wants to be again." I turned the vellum toward the light. "Emrys. When we get there. What happens to you?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire settled twice.

"I don't know," he said finally, and it was the first lie I'd ever caught him in, and we both knew it, and neither of us said anything more.

* * *

On the third day, the road began to fight me.

I felt it first as friction—the pen dragging, the ink browning at the nib. Then the hedgerows started producing things I hadn't drawn: a rusted gate, a milestone with numbers worn to ghosts. The road was aging faster than I drew it, burning through its own existence like a candle lit at both ends. An oath-sworn road lasts as long as the drawer's certainty, the old texts say, and somewhere in the last day my certainty had developed cracks, because I had begun to imagine the end of the journey, and imagining the end is the first step toward doubting the middle.

Also—and this I admitted only to myself, in the strict privacy of my own skull—I was tired. Drawing a world continuously for three days takes something out of you that sleep doesn't put back. My margins were blurring. Twice I'd had to redraw a bend I'd already drawn, and the second time the road had forked for half a heartbeat, both versions real, before I chose.

"We're close," Emrys said. He wasn't looking at the road. He was looking at the sky, southward, where clouds piled over something vast and pale. "I can feel it. Like a tooth that wants to fall out."

"Charming."

"That's what it feels like." He touched his own jaw, demonstrating. "The whole city. Something loose in it."

I extended the line over a river I declined to look at too closely—it held itself obediently beneath my bridge for exactly as long as we crossed, and I heard it rush back in behind us like a held breath released—and then we came up over a last drawn hill, and there it was.

The capital. Vast, pale, crowned in haze. From this distance it looked peaceful. From this distance you couldn't see the green.

But Emrys could. He went very still beside me, and his hand found mine again, and his grip was not steady this time.

"There's a song in it," he whispered. "Under everything. It's been singing the whole time. I thought it was a dream I kept having." His voice cracked down the middle, boy and something older than boy. "Elodie, it's singing *his* name."

"Whose name?"

He looked up at me, and his eyes had gone the green of my hedgerows, the green of fresh verdigris, the green of the paths on the map.

"The king's," he said. "It's singing the king's name. And Elodie—" his small fingers tightened around mine, cold as ink, "—it knows yours too. I don't know how. But the song has your name in it now."

Behind us, far back along the way we'd come, I heard a sound I had hoped not to hear for days yet: a bell. Not the guild's third bell. Something older, carried on the wind from the city itself, ringing through the haze.

And on the road behind us—on *my* road, the one I was still drawing, the one only I could stand on—footsteps.
