## 10. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The road ended where the map said it would, which was the first strange thing.

Emrys had learned that roads were supposed to go on being roads after you stopped looking at them. Elodie had explained this on the second night of their flight, drawing in the dirt with a stick: *a drawn road lasts as long as the ink holds its shape. When we sleep, I redraw it. Don't walk past the edge of the line.* He had asked what happened to people who walked past the edge, and she had looked at him for a long moment and said, "They arrive somewhere honest," and he still didn't know what that meant, but he liked the way she said it, like it was a door she'd left open on purpose.

Now the map's last line curled into a valley full of smoke, and beyond the smoke was a village with no name on any page.

"It's called—" Elodie stopped. Frowned. Touched her temple the way she did when a word slipped sideways out of reach. "It's called..." She pulled the map from her coat and held it flat against her knee. The parchment shivered, the way it always did when it thought no one was watching, and letters surfaced along the valley's edge like fish rising to feed: **Marrowmere**. Then they sank again.

"Marrowmere," Emrys read aloud. The word fit his mouth strangely, like a shoe worn by someone else's foot. "Why does it hide?"

"Because something ate it," Elodie said quietly. "Come on. We need bread, and I'm nearly out of ink."

* * *

The village smelled wrong before it looked wrong.

Emrys had been learning smells the way he learned everything—fast, greedy, roots taking water. Woodsmoke meant hearths. Bread meant morning. Wet wool meant sheep and patience. Marrowmere smelled like all of these at once, but underneath them ran a thin gray thread, a smell like paper left too long in the sun, like a book whose words had faded but whose pages were still warm. He knew that smell. It lived at the edges of his own memory, wherever he tried to look directly at his beginning.

"The Writ," he said.

Elodie glanced down at him. She had been doing that more lately—glancing, checking, the way you check a candle in a drafty room. "You can smell it?"

"It smells like almost." He struggled. Words were still new enough that some of them only worked if he pushed. "Like remembering that you forgot."

She didn't answer, but her hand found his shoulder and stayed there, and they walked into Marrowmere together.

The houses leaned close over the lane, roofs nearly touching, and the people moved through the gaps between them with a smoothness that took Emrys three doors to understand. They weren't stumbling. They weren't lost. They were *efficient*. A woman carried water from the well without looking at the path. A man split wood in steady strokes, eyes fixed on nothing. Everyone knew exactly what their hands were for.

Nobody knew anything else.

A boy about Emrys's age sat on a step, turning a wooden top over and over. "Hello," Emrys tried.

"I'm the cooper's son," the boy said. Not *I'm Tam*, not *I'm Joss*. Just the trade, passed down like a name-shaped hole filled with work. "Do you want to see the tops? I make good ones. That's mine, that is. Being good at tops."

"What's your name?"

The boy's hands went still around the top. For one heartbeat his face opened—something behind his eyes leaning forward, listening, reaching—and then it closed again, gentle as a door in wind. "I make good ones," he repeated, and smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing Emrys had ever seen.

He felt it then, under his ribs, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. The gray thread-smell thickened. Somewhere north and far away, past hills he couldn't see, a king sat in a garden wearing a crown that drank, and the drinking had spread this far, seeping outward like water through cloth, taking names first because names were the smallest things to carry and the hardest to live without.

Elodie had gone ahead to the baker's. Emrys could tell by the smell—real bread, honest bread, bread that hadn't forgotten itself even if the baker had.

* * *

The bakery was warm, and the baker was a mountain of a woman with flour to her elbows and a face like a question she'd stopped asking.

"You'll be wanting loaves," she said. "That's what people want, coming in here. I've got the dark rye and the white with the crust like glass, and I've got the honey rolls on account of it being Thursday." She paused. Her brow creased. "Is it Thursday? It's the day I do honey rolls. So it must be."

"It's Thursday," Elodie said smoothly, counting coins. Emrys knew that voice. It was the voice she used on innkeepers and guild hunters and anyone who might look too closely at a woman traveling alone with a boy who spoke old languages in his sleep. "And we'll take six of the honey rolls, because my son has been walking since dawn and eats like winter."

*My son.* Emrys's chest did something complicated. He was getting used to the lie and not used to liking how much he liked it.

The baker wrapped the rolls, and her hands moved with total certainty, and that was the thing that kept snagging in Emrys like a burr in wool. She kneaded without thinking. She shaped without measuring. Every loaf came out identical, perfect, *right*—because the bread remembered even if she didn't. The knowledge had sunk below the place where the hunger reached. But ask her her mother's name, or the color of her wedding dress, or why she'd chosen flour over fish, and there would be nothing there. A clean room. A swept floor.

"How long," Elodie asked, casual as weather, "has it been hard to find things? Names, I mean. Little things."

The baker laughed, short and easy. "Oh, everyone's the same round here. You get to a certain age and the names go slippery. My husband's mother—" She stopped. Stood very still. "I had a husband's mother," she said slowly, like reading it off a list. "I don't recall her face. I recall her kitchen. I recall the smell of her stew." She looked down at her floured hands as if they belonged to someone else. "Funny. You don't miss what you can't find. You just keep working."

"That's not age," Emrys said.

Both women turned. He hadn't meant to speak. The words had come up from somewhere below language, from the place where the map lived in him, the place that was not quite his.

The baker studied him with those question-mark eyes. "You're a strange little lad. Where's your folk from?"

"He's from far north," Elodie said, "and he talks nonsense when he's tired. Thank you for the rolls."

But the baker wasn't finished looking at him. Something in her face had shifted—the door behind her eyes had cracked open again, just a finger's width, and through it Emrys saw a vast dark room full of furniture covered in sheets. "Say that again," she said. "What you said. About the names."

Emrys looked at Elodie. Elodie gave him the smallest nod, the kind she gave when she was letting him choose something and would stand in front of whatever he chose.

"It's not age," he said again. "Something is eating them. Far away. It started with a wish." The old words rose in him unbidden, syllables older than kingdoms, and he let a few of them through, soft, half-sung: *velen atha, velen atha, soth il marrow...*

He didn't know what the song was. He had never learned it. It simply was in him, the way blood is in you, and it came out the way breath comes out when you're asleep.

The sheets blew off the furniture.

The baker sat down. Not gracefully—she dropped, onto the flour sack by the oven, and her great hands pressed against her mouth, and above them her eyes went wide and wet and *full*. "Maretta," she whispered. "Her name was Maretta. She burned the honey rolls every single Thursday and blamed the oven, and my husband—Tomas, his name is Tomas, he proposed to me right there by the well because he was too shy to do it indoors—" She was laughing now, and crying, and laughing, flour dust rising around her like snow going the wrong way. "I have a *sister*. I have a sister in Lowford and she owes me eleven coppers and I never once remembered to collect—"

"Marrowmere," Emrys said, understanding arriving all at once, cold and bright. "The village. It's called Marrowmere. The map told me."

The baker gripped his arm with fingers like bread paddles. "Boy. Boy, what did you just *do*?"

* * *

By sundown, half the village was in the square outside the bakery, and Emrys understood that he had made a mistake, or a miracle, and could no longer tell the difference.

Word traveled fast in a place where everyone had suddenly remembered what they'd lost. They came in twos and threes, then dozens, faces lit by lanterns and by something brighter than lanterns. The cooper's son—Joss, his name was Joss, Joss Barrow—came running with his wooden top clutched to his chest and shouted "I'm Joss!" at Emrys from ten feet away, twice, because once didn't feel like enough. An old man wept openly by the well, saying *Edith, Edith, Edith* like a prayer, like a debt repaid. People were telling each other their own names in the street, trading them back and forth like coins, hoarding them, saying them just to feel the weight.

"They'll come at moonrise," Elodie murmured beside him. She'd found the corner of the bakery wall that let her watch the whole square, and she stood there with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the way that meant she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "When the singing starts. Word will travel faster than we can."

"Singing?"

"Emrys." She crouched to his level, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for truths. "You sang four lines of a dead language and gave forty people back their lives. Whatever sits on that throne in the capital will feel it the way you'd feel a splinter. And whatever serves it—" she didn't say *the emissary*, didn't say *grey*, but the unsaid words filled the space between them like smoke— "will feel it too. We have until moonrise. Maybe less."

"Then I should sing more," Emrys said. "While we're here. There are more of them. There's a whole village, Elodie, and they're all walking around with empty rooms inside—"

"And every note you sing draws a line on a map straight to us." She caught herself; he watched her catch herself, watched her soften by force. "I didn't survive two hundred years of guild rules to lose you to a crowd that wants to touch your sleeves. We eat, we sleep two hours, we're gone before the moon clears the ridge."

"But—"

"*Emrys.*"

He shut his mouth. But the square was still singing—not him, them, the villagers, snatches of songs they'd recovered, someone playing a fiddle badly and joyfully—and every scrap of melody tugged at him like current tugs at a swimmer. Because he finally understood what he was. Not a messenger. Not a warning. Elodie had said it that morning, half to herself, staring at the map: *you're not the letter, love, you're the antidote.* And an antidote that stays in its bottle helps no one.

The baker—Bess, her name was Bess, Bess Hallow—brought them stew and wouldn't hear of payment. "You gave me back my Tomas," she said, gripping Emrys's shoulder. "Eleven years I've been calling him 'husband' in my head like he was a trade. Eleven years." Her voice cracked and reformed. "Whatever you're running from, lad, you run through my kitchen anytime."

After she left, Emrys ate in silence and listened to the square.

"Elodie," he said finally. "When I sang. I didn't choose the words. They chose themselves. Like the map." He turned his bowl in his hands. "What if that's what I'm *for*? Not delivering something. Giving it back. What if every person I heal is one more root holding me to the world?" He thought of the notebook in his pack, the one they'd begun filling on the road—*his* story, in *her* hand, the first thing that had ever belonged to him. "What if I'm not dissolving when I give memories back? What if I'm *arriving*?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that the fiddle music filled the pause.

"When you talk like that," she said at last, "you sound like the map." She reached over and ruffled his hair, rough and quick, the way she did when affection embarrassed her. "Eat your stew. Moonrise soon."

* * *

They left at the edge of dark, and that was when Emrys broke his promise without meaning to.

It happened at the well. The old man—Edith's widower—was still there, sitting on the well's stone lip in the blue dusk, whispering his wife's name over and over like a man checking a coin isn't counterfeit. And when Emrys passed, the man looked up, and his face was the cooper's son's face had been: open, reaching, a door held ajar.

"Just one more," the man said. "Just one. I have her name but not her voice. Forty years and I can't find her voice, lad. Please."

Emrys stopped. He heard Elodie, three steps ahead, stop too. He knew what she'd say. He said it for her, gently: "We can't stay."

"Then don't stay." The old man's hands trembled. "One line. One note. However you did it. Please, boy. I'm asking plain."

And Emrys looked back at Elodie, and Elodie looked at the ridge where the moon was climbing, and neither of them said no, and that was the whole decision, made in the space between heartbeats.

He sang.

Not the old words this time—or not only them. He sang the lullaby fragment he'd given Elodie on the third night, the one that lived in him like a coal that wouldn't cool, and he wove the dead syllables through it, *velen atha, soth il*, and he put Edith's name inside the melody the way you'd slip a letter under a door.

The old man's face broke open like bread.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, there she is. There she *is*." And he began to laugh and weep in the same breath, and down the lane windows lit one by one as people woke to voices they'd forgotten they loved.

Emrys stood in the middle of the recovering village with his heart hammering, feeling it happen—feeling each returned memory settle into him too, not taken but *echoed*, the way a bell keeps the shape of every sound it's ever made. Joss Barrow's delight. Bess Hallow's Tomas. Edith's voice, low and off-key and beloved. They were threading into him, anchoring him, dozens of small bright ropes thrown across the dark. He was not disappearing. He was becoming a place people lived.

Then the bells started.

Not the village bells. These came from everywhere and nowhere, deep and wrong, a sound like a giant ledger closing, and Emrys knew them the way he knew the gray thread-smell. The guild's bells. They had rung for him once before, on a midnight when rain fell upward, though he had never been traded for.

Elodie was already moving, grabbing his hand, pulling him toward the lane's dark end. "Hunters," she breathed. "That's not palace grey—that's *guild* iron. They rang the finding-note." Her grip tightened. "Emrys, listen to me. If they ring it three times—"

The second peal rolled over the rooftops.

"—then it's not hunters." Her face in the moonlight had gone pale and sharp as paper. "Three times means the Guildmaster himself has come. Three times means Corvane is here."

The third bell rang.
