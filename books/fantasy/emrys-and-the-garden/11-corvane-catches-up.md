## 11. Corvane Catches Up

The road ended where I drew it, and for three days that was enough.

We traveled the way cartographers travel when they are running: by ink. I would sketch a lane onto a sheet of vellum—just a suggestion of one, a line with intention behind it—and Emrys and I would walk it while it existed. Behind us the roads unmade themselves like breath fading off glass. The guild's hunters were good, but they hunted on maps that had been drawn years ago, and I was drawing new ones every morning out of nothing but stubbornness and a tin of cheap pigment.

Emrys had taken to naming things as we went. It was part of his lessons, though he'd outgrown my lessons weeks ago; now he learned words faster than I could teach them and spent the surplus on the world.

"That's a *harrow*," he said, pointing at a rusted thing in a fallow field.

"It is."

"Harrow." He said it twice, tasting it. Then, because he always did this: "In my language there is no word for harrow. We borrowed the shape of the word for *plough* and bent it. Words bend easier than iron."

"Everything bends easier than iron," I said. "That's why we make laws out of iron instead."

He looked at me sidelong, the way he did when he suspected I was teaching him something without admitting it. "Is that what you're doing?"

"Bookkeeping," I said. "Keep walking."

But he'd caught me, and we both knew it. Three days of flight had done something to me that twenty years of ledgers hadn't: they'd made me honest. Every rule I broke felt less like a crack in myself and more like a window opening in a house I'd been told had no windows. I kept waiting for the guilt to arrive. It kept not arriving. That frightened me more than the hunters did.

The hunters, meanwhile, were closing.

I knew it the way I knew weather—by pressure, by the behavior of small things. On the second night, the crows left a stretch of forest all at once, in silence, which crows do not do unless something worse than crows is coming. On the third morning, we found a waymarker stone with a guild sigil freshly chalked onto it, and beneath the sigil, a number: **three**. Three hunters within a day's walk. They were leaving me messages the way I used to leave corrections on junior mappers' drafts. *Your line drifts east, Delacroix. We see you.*

By dusk we reached the ruins of an old tollhouse where two dead roads crossed, and I made a decision I hated: we stopped. Emrys needed sleep—he'd been dreaming badly, waking with dirt under his fingernails and green stains on his palms that washed away and came back—and I needed to redraw our route, and running on a blank page is how runners die.

"We rest here," I said. "One night."

"One night," Emrys agreed, and then, quietly, watching me unroll the map: "They're close, aren't they. The ones who follow."

"Yes."

"You're not afraid."

"I'm terrified. It's different from being afraid. Fear makes you run. Terror makes you think." I pinned the map's corner with a stone. "Running is over. Thinking starts now."

He considered that with the grave attention he gave everything, as if the whole world were a lesson he intended to pass. "Then think well, Elodie. I have only just learned your name properly. I don't want to need a new one."

I laughed despite everything—the first real laugh since the guild door—and he smiled at having caused it, pleased as a boy who has made a bell ring.

That was the last easy hour we got.

* * *

They came at moonrise, and they came politely, which is the guild's way. No arrows, no nets. A lantern kindled at each end of the tollhouse ruin, and between them, figures in grey oilcloth, standing with the patience of furniture.

Three hunters. And then a fourth lantern, carried slower, and Corvane stepped into the ruin.

Guildmaster Corvane is not a large man. He has never needed to be; he occupies space the way a signature occupies a page. His coat was dry despite the river mist. His hands were folded. He looked at me across the broken tollgate with an expression I had seen him give misfiled ledgers: not anger. Disappointment with a footnote.

"Elodie."

"Guildmaster."

"You've cost us eleven days and four contracts. The Meridian Cartographers withdrew their standing order. Do you know how long it took to build that relationship?" He sighed, genuinely pained. "Forty years."

"Send them an invoice."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "There she is. I wondered where you'd gone." He glanced past me to where Emrys stood half-hidden in the doorway shadow, watchful, coiled. "And there he is. The foundling. Hello, boy. You've grown into your grammar, I hear."

Emrys said nothing. But I saw his hand move, small and deliberate, until his fingers touched my sleeve. Not clinging. Anchoring. *I am here, and so are you.*

"He doesn't go back with you," I said.

"No," Corvane agreed mildly. "He doesn't."

I had prepared arguments. I had prepared, frankly, for violence—I'd mapped three escape lines out of the tollhouse before sundown, each annotated in my head with wind direction and footing. None of my preparations covered *no*.

"Then why are you here?"

"To tell you the truth." He said it simply, and that simplicity was more alarming than any threat. "You've earned it, I think. You've certainly paid for it." He looked around the ruin, chose a fallen lintel, and sat down on it like a tired clerk at the end of a long audit. "Sit, Elodie. This is a long entry, and it deserves a steady hand to record it."

"I don't have my ledger."

"You never stopped keeping it. It's in your head. It's always been in your head—that's why I hired you." He nodded at the ground beside him. "Sit."

I didn't sit. But I didn't run either, and Emrys stayed where he was, and Corvane took that for consent, which perhaps it was.

"Do you know," he began, "what the Guild of Impossible Things was, before it was a guild?"

"A trading house. Records, custody, fair exchange—"

"A *ward*. Originally." He said the word carefully, laying it down like something fragile. "Two hundred years ago, there was no guild. There was a circle of cartographers and record-keepers who understood something the crown did not: that wishes have weight, and weight must be carried by someone. When King Aldous Vey stood in his garden during the famine winter and wished—with the Verdant Writ coiled around his crown—for the hunger to end, someone should have asked him the bookkeeper's question."

"Which is?"

"*Paid by whom?*" Corvane's voice didn't rise. It never rose. "The Writ ended the famine, Elodie. Grain on every table by spring. And it paid itself out of the kingdom's memory—first the small things, then the names, then the histories. By the time anyone noticed, the noticing itself had been eaten. Whole villages forgot they had ever been hungry, which meant they could not remember what had saved them, which meant they could not remember what was still feeding." He paused. "Except the Writ wasn't finished. A wish granted is a debt recorded. And when the kingdom ran out of memories worth eating, the Writ turned to the only account left open. The king's own."

Emrys made a sound. Small. I looked at him and his face had gone the color of paper.

"The circle couldn't undo the wish," Corvane went on. "Wishes can't be undone, only *answered*. So they did the only thing bookkeepers do with an unpayable debt. They set aside a reserve." He unfolded his hands at last, and I saw that they were older than I remembered, older than they had any right to be. "They took the childhood the Writ had already stolen from Aldous—the years it had eaten first, because a king's own past is the tenderest meat—and they did not let it be consumed. They held it. Shaped it. Gave it legs and a voice and a task, and hid it beyond time, beyond mapping, in a place even we could not draw."

The ruin was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"They made a failsafe," I said slowly. "Against the Writ."

"We made a *boy*, Elodie. Let's use accurate language; you taught me that." Corvane's eyes went to Emrys, and for one unguarded moment there was something in them I had never seen in forty years of knowing him. Grief, maybe. Or its ledger-entry: regret. "A fragment of Aldous Vey's stolen childhood, sent back as a warning. He was meant to reach the palace while the king still had a self left to wake. He was meant to say: *this is what you lost, and here is what it cost.*" He spread his hands. "He was lost instead. Misdelivered. Two hundred years in the dark between pages. Until he knocked on your door."

"And the guild knew." My voice came out flat. "When he arrived. The bells rang for him. You knew what he was."

"We knew what he was *for*. Not whether he still was. Things change in two hundred years, Elodie. Reserves deplete. We needed to study him before we spent him." He said *spent* without flinching, and I hated him for it, and I hated more that some cold clerical part of me understood exactly what he meant. "And then you burned your copy of his intake record and walked out the door with him, and the question became academic."

"The emissary," I said. "The palace offered a fortune for him. You were going to sell him to the thing that ate him."

"We were going to *trade* him," Corvane corrected, "which is not the same—" He stopped himself. Closed his eyes briefly. "No. You're right. It is the same. That's rather the point of what I've come to tell you."

I stared at him. "You came to confess."

"I came to *balance*." For the first time, the Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things looked tired. "Elodie, I have served the third law for sixty years. Anomalies that cannot be classified must be traded or destroyed. I wrote half of it. And in sixty years I have told myself it was neutrality. That we kept our hands clean by keeping records instead of taking sides." He looked down at his folded hands. "But neutrality between a debt and the debtor isn't neutrality. It's collection. We weren't standing apart from the Writ all these years. We were its *clerk*. Recording what it ate. Filing its appetite under *completed transactions*."

The words landed somewhere in me and rang like a struck bell. Because I had felt it for years without naming it—every entry in every ledger, someone else's story, none of them mine, and I had called that professionalism. We all had. A whole institution of people mistaking complicity for discipline.

"So the law was wrong."

"The law was *incomplete*," Corvane said. "It accounted for everything except the possibility that the guild might owe something. And we do. We owe him." He nodded at Emrys. "Two hundred years of interest."

Emrys spoke for the first time. His common speech was better now than it had any right to be, but he chose his words like a man crossing ice.

"You made me. From him. From the king." His hand was still near my sleeve. "So when I look at Elodie and feel that I have been walking toward her longer than I can count—"

"That's the mapping instinct," Corvane said gently. "The circle put it in you. A compass toward the one who draws what isn't there. She was always going to find you, or you her. The failsafe required a cartographer. It required *her*, specifically—we just lost track of which generation you'd arrive in." Something almost wry crossed his face. "You were delivered late, boy. By two hundred years and one very stubborn woman."

"Then take me to the palace," Emrys said. "Finish the delivery."

"No," I said, and Corvane said, "Yes," in the same instant, and we looked at each other.

"Not with the guild's plan," I said. "Whatever the plan is. Trade, leverage, containment—you don't get to spend him, Corvane. Not even to fix your own books."

Corvane regarded me for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and produced something I recognized with a lurch: a ledger. Not mine. Older—leather gone soft as cloth, corners rounded by centuries of handling. He set it on the broken tollgate between us.

"The original intake," he said. "The circle's own record of what they made and why. Everything I've told you is in here, in their hands, in their ink. I'm giving it to you. Consider it the guild settling a debt." He stood, and the hunters at the ends of the ruin shifted, and then, at some signal I missed, stood down. "The palace knows where he is now—the emissary reported before you ever left the city. You have perhaps four days before the Writ sends something worse than polite men with lanterns. Go north. There's a village on the old salt road where the forgetting has already arrived; you'll want to see it, both of you, before you reach the walls. It will show you what you're walking into."

"Why help us?" I asked. "After everything."

Corvane paused at the edge of the lantern light. When he answered, he didn't turn around.

"Because I checked the books, Elodie. All of them, all the way back. And there is one entry the guild has never once balanced in two hundred years." The mist took his outline. "The failure. Ours. Go make it right."

Then the lanterns went out, all four at once, the way candles go when a door closes somewhere far away, and the ruin belonged to the moon again.

* * *

For a long time neither of us moved.

Then Emrys picked up the old ledger from the tollgate. He held it the way he held everything new—carefully, as if it might be a word he could break. He opened it to the first page, and even by moonlight I could see the writing was in no alphabet I knew, and I have known most of them.

"Read it to me," he said. "Tomorrow. On the road." He closed it and handed it to me, and his eyes when they met mine were steady and terribly young. "Tonight I want to sleep. And Elodie—" He hesitated, and for a moment he was only a boy standing in a ruin, holding on to the hem of the only person who had ever chosen him. "Was it true? What he said? That you were always going to find me?"

I thought about lying to soften it. I had spent my whole life recording truths and withholding my own; it seemed a poor night to start a new habit.

"Yes," I said. "Apparently I've been waiting for you for two hundred years. Which means I'm owed considerable back pay."

He laughed—a startled, bright sound in the dark—and something in my chest that had been clenched since the guild door finally, cautiously, let go.

We slept in shifts. In the morning I drew us a road north, toward the salt road, toward the village that had forgotten its name, toward the walls of a palace strangled in bloom. And all day as we walked, Emrys hummed something under his breath—an old tune in a dead language, soft as roots growing—and I found I was no longer afraid of what waited ahead.

Only of what it would cost to get there.
