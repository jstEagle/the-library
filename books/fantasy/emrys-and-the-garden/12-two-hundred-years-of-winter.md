## 12. Two Hundred Years of Winter

The fire in the ruined mill spoke a language Emrys almost understood.

Not words—words were still new to him, still slippery, each one a small animal he had to catch and hold and learn the shape of before it bit him. But fire was older than words. Fire said *warmth* and *danger* and *stay close* all at once, and it did not need Elodie's patient drawings in the dirt to make itself known.

He sat as near to it as he dared, his knees pulled up to his chest, watching Corvane across the flames.

The Guildmaster looked different out here. In the guild hall he had been all sharp angles and colder rules, a man made of ledgers. But three days of hard travel had worn him down the way rain wears stone. His coat was muddy to the knee. His hands, which Emrys had only ever seen holding quills or pointing in accusation, now held a tin cup of tea he kept forgetting to drink.

Elodie sat beside Emrys with her back against the mill's broken wall, her map spread across her lap even though she wasn't looking at it. She never really stopped looking at it, Emrys had noticed. It lay there like a sleeping animal she didn't trust.

"You asked me once," Corvane said, "why the bells rang for you."

Emrys nodded. He knew that word now. *Nodded.* He knew a hundred words, then five hundred, then more than he could count, because they came to him the way water comes to dry ground—fast, greedy, filling every crack. Elodie said it wasn't natural. Elodie said most children took years. Elodie had gone very quiet the third time he'd used a word she'd only taught him that morning, and had written something in her notebook and underlined it twice.

"You were never traded for," Corvane went on. "Nothing arrives at the guild without a price paid somewhere. That is the first law. And yet the bells rang for you, which means the ledger recognized a debt already settled." He turned the cup in his hands. "Two hundred years ago, someone paid for you in advance."

"Who?" The word came easily. It was one of his favorites. *Who* opened doors.

Corvane looked at the fire for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he looked at Elodie.

"You should know this too," he said. "You're going to hate me for keeping it."

"I already hate you for the hunters," Elodie said. Her voice was flat but her hand found Emrys's shoulder and stayed there, warm and certain. "This can't be worse."

"It can." Corvane set down the untouched tea. "It's much worse. Because I'm going to tell you that everything we've done—the law of neutrality, the confiscation order, the emissary from the palace—we did it not because we don't know what the boy is." He paused. "We did it because we do."

* * *

Emrys listened the way he listened to everything now: completely, hungrily, letting the words pour into him and trusting that they would settle into some kind of shape.

Corvane told it like this:

Two hundred years ago, there had been a kingdom called Vey, and a young king named Aldous, crowned at nineteen when his father died of a fever that also took half the court. Aldous had been, by every account the guild preserved, a good king. Not a great one—not yet—but good. He laughed easily. He remembered the names of kitchen girls and stable boys. He walked the walls of his capital in the evenings because he liked to see the lamps come on.

And in his fourth year, the winter came that did not end.

Three harvests failed. The rivers froze so deep that even breaking the ice accomplished nothing; the water beneath had gone silent and gray. People ate bark. People ate their seed grain and cursed themselves in spring. People in the outer provinces simply stopped, quietly, in the snow, and the snow covered them, and by midwinter no one had the strength left to bury anyone else.

"Aldous was twenty-three years old," Corvane said. "And he did what kings do when they run out of ordinary answers. He went looking for extraordinary ones."

"The Writ," Elodie said. Not a question.

"The Verdant Writ." Corvane's mouth twisted around the name like it tasted of rot. "You've seen what grows through the palace on your map. Imagine it smaller, then. A living document—a contract that grew on parchment like moss on a stone. It appeared in the royal vaults the same winter the famine began. No seller. No provenance. The guild has never learned where it came from, and believe me, we have spent two centuries trying." He leaned forward, and the firelight made his face into something older than it was. "The Writ grants wishes, Elodie. Truly grants them. That's what no one understands about it. It isn't a trick. When Aldous pressed his seal to it and wished for the famine to end, the famine *ended*. The ice broke overnight. The rivers ran. By summer the fields were greener than any farmer could remember."

"But," Emrys said.

Corvane closed his eyes. "But the Writ takes payment. And its price is memory. Every wish granted costs the wisher something they remember—and if the wisher cannot pay enough, it takes from everyone the wish touches." He opened his eyes and looked directly at Emrys. "Aldous wished to end a famine that touched an entire kingdom. So the Writ reached into an entire kingdom and began to eat."

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the dead trees along the riverbank, and Emrys thought of the village they'd passed through—the one where the baker had smiled at him with empty eyes and called him *little sir* because she couldn't call him anything else.

"That village," he said. "The people who forgot their names."

"The hunger spreads outward from the palace," Corvane said. "Slowly. The Writ feeds at the center first—it took the court within a decade, then the city, then the roads, and now it reaches the far provinces. Two hundred years, and it has eaten nearly everything. The kingdom you traveled through, Elodie—the one on your maps, the one with the capital and the king and the history books—" He shook his head. "Most of it is invention. Patchwork. Stories people tell each other because the true stories are gone. They remember that there was a famine. They don't remember why it ended. They remember there is a king. Most of them would struggle to say anything true about him at all."

"And Aldous himself?" Elodie's hand tightened on Emrys's shoulder.

"At first, the king paid his own way," Corvane said. "That's the tragedy of it. He was strong-willed. Each time the Writ came due, he fed it pieces of himself—deliberately, choosing what to lose. He gave up his mother's face first. Then his father's voice. Then the name of the tutor who taught him letters. He rationed his own life, memory by memory, buying his kingdom time." Corvane's voice dropped. "But the Writ is patient, and it is hungry, and eventually it reached the memories he could not bear to lose. And those are the ones it wanted most."

"Which ones?" Emrys asked, though some part of him, some part older than his few weeks of language, already knew.

"His childhood," Corvane said.

The fire seemed to lean closer.

"A king's childhood is the root of him," Corvane said. "Everything he believes about the world grew from it. The Writ understood this before we did—that if you eat a man's childhood, you don't just take his past. You take the boy who might have become someone else. You leave only the shape of a man, hollow, wearing a crown." He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the capital none of them could see in the dark. "Aldous still sits his throne. He still signs decrees. The court smiles at him and he smiles back, and neither of them remembers why any of it matters. The garden grows through the palace now because the Writ no longer bothers to hide. Why should it? There is almost nothing left to eat."

* * *

Emrys discovered he was crying.

It surprised him. He hadn't known, until that moment, that he could—that his body held salt water ready for exactly this purpose, that grief had a physical shape, wet and hot and embarrassing. He wiped his face with his sleeve and found Elodie watching him with an expression he couldn't read yet, though he was learning her expressions faster than her words.

"Why am I crying," he asked. "I never knew him."

"Didn't you?" Corvane said softly.

And then he told them the rest.

"The guild watched the famine end and understood immediately what had been traded for it. We tried to intervene—this was before the law of neutrality, before we learned our lesson about meddling in the affairs of crowns. We sent agents to steal the Writ. Three of them. None came back; the Writ grew through them the way it grows through the palace walls, and what walked out wearing their faces was not them. After that, the guild did what the guild has always done when it cannot fight. We prepared."

"Prepared how?" Elodie asked.

"We couldn't destroy the Writ. We couldn't steal it. But we could study it, and studying it taught us one thing of value: the Writ does not digest what it eats. It *stores*. Everything Aldous lost—all two hundred years of a kingdom's remembering—is still inside it, intact, waiting. Memory cannot be destroyed by that thing. Only held." Corvane turned fully toward Emrys, and his voice changed, became quieter, became almost gentle, which frightened Emrys more than shouting ever had. "Including the childhood. Including the boy."

"What did you do?" Elodie whispered.

"We took the king's stolen childhood out of the Writ's stores," Corvane said. "One theft for another. We could not give it back to Aldous—the Writ guards its hoard too well, and returns nothing willingly. But we could do something else with it. We could grow it." He spread his hands. "Memory wants to be lived, Elodie. Given flesh and time and rain, a stolen childhood will try to become a child again. We gave it a body. We gave it a name. And we hid it beyond time, in a place between moments, where the Writ could never reach it and the world could never find it—until the day came when it needed to be found."

The fire cracked. Emrys felt Elodie's hand go still on his shoulder.

"No," she said.

"Yes," Corvane said. "You've been walking toward the palace since the moment you were made, boy. You are not a messenger carrying a warning. You *are* the warning. You are the last unspoiled piece of Aldous Vey—the childhood the Writ swallowed and we stole back. Every memory that king lost before the age of thirteen, every summer and every lullaby and every scraped knee—you're carrying all of it. You've been carrying it your whole existence. You just didn't know it was yours."

* * *

Emrys stood up.

He didn't decide to stand; his legs simply did it, carrying him away from the fire, away from Corvane's voice, to the broken window of the mill where the night sky hung over the black trees. He gripped the cold stone of the sill and breathed, and the breath came out white in the dark.

*A childhood that was never lived.*

He tried to reach for it—to feel it, the way Corvane described, the summers and the lullabies—but there was nothing there. Of course there was nothing there. That was the whole horror of it. He was a room built to hold treasure, and the treasure had been taken out and hidden in the walls, and now the room walked around asking strangers who it was.

Except—

No. Not nothing. He searched harder, the way Elodie had taught him to search for words, patiently, turning things over. And deep underneath the noise of himself, underneath the fear and the confusion and the terrible new vocabulary of his short loud life, there were... fragments. Not memories. Shadows of memories. The feeling of sunlight through leaves without any leaves attached. The sensation of being sung to without a voice or a song. A warmth that had a name once, a name for a person, and the name was gone but the warmth remained, stubborn, refusing to dissolve.

He had been walking toward Elodie, he realized, because she drew what wasn't there. And he was made entirely of what wasn't there.

"Emrys." Elodie's voice behind him. Close. She had followed him to the window, and now she stood beside him, not touching him, giving him the space he apparently needed without knowing he needed it. "Look at me."

He looked at her.

"Corvane says you were made as a failsafe. A tool. Something the guild planted and waited two hundred years to harvest." Her jaw was set in the way he'd learned meant she was angry, though not at him—never, he was fairly sure, at him. "But I've watched you for weeks now. I've taught you. I've heard you sing that baker's name back to her. Whatever they made you from, you are not what they made you *for*. Do you understand? A failsafe doesn't choose to comfort a stranger. A tool doesn't learn five hundred words because it likes the sound of them."

"But it's true," Emrys said. His voice came out small. "What he said. I'm the king's childhood. I'm—" He pressed his hand flat against his own chest, as if he might feel the missing years through his ribs. "I'm a hole shaped like a boy."

"You're a boy shaped like a boy," Elodie said fiercely. "I have the map that proves it."

Despite everything, despite the tears still drying on his face, Emrys laughed. It burst out of him sideways, unexpected, and Elodie's stern expression cracked into something warmer.

Behind them, Corvane had risen too. He stood by the fire with his shoulders bowed, and when he spoke, all the Guildmaster had gone out of his voice, leaving only an old tired man.

"There's more," he said. "The part I truly dread telling you. The reason the guild hid you beyond time instead of using you straightaway." He met Emrys's eyes across the dark room. "There is a way to return the childhood to the king. To wake him, to starve the Writ, to end two hundred years of winter. But the door it requires opens only for a true name, freely given. And your true name, boy—" He stopped. Started again. "Your true name is *his*."

The fire burned low. Outside, the wind picked up, moving through the trees like something searching.

"And giving it," Corvane finished quietly, "may mean there is no *you* left afterward to want anything at all."

Emrys looked at Elodie.

Elodie looked at Emrys.

Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing yet to say—but her hand found his in the dark, and held on, and did not let go.
