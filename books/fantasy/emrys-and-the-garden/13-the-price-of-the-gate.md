## 13. The Price of the Gate

The map stopped moving on the third night outside the capital.

I noticed it the way you notice a clock that has gone silent in a room where it has ticked your whole life. For weeks the thing had been a living document—coastlines breathing, roads unspooling behind us like thread from a spool, the palace at its center drawn and redrawn until I'd stopped trying to fix its position and started trying to read its *intent*. Then, somewhere past the last village with a name anyone could remember, the ink settled.

Every line firmed. Every path resolved. And at the heart of it, where the palace should have been, the map drew something else: not a building but a door.

A single door, standing free in a courtyard of green, framed in stone older than any masonry I had ever catalogued. Around its lintel, in Emrys's dead language, ran a sentence I had copied into my notebook so many times I could write it blindfolded.

*It opens for a true name, freely given.*

I sat up in the dark of the abandoned mill we'd sheltered in, the map across my knees, and felt the particular cold that cartographers know—the cold of a map telling you something you don't want to hear. Maps are honest in a way people are not. They cannot flatter. They can only show you the terrain between where you are and where you must go, and this terrain had one road on it, and the road ended at a door, and the door wanted what doors like that always want.

Everything.

"Elodie?"

Emrys's voice from the loft above. He slept badly now; he'd slept badly since Corvane told us what he was. Two hundred years of stolen childhood had a weight to it, and the boy carried it the way children carry things too big for them—badly, stubbornly, refusing to put it down.

"Go back to sleep," I said.

"You're reading it again." Bare feet on the ladder rungs. He came down into the moonlight from the broken roof, small and grave, his hair silvered at the temples lately in a way I refused to examine too closely. The king's years were leaking into him as we drew closer. Or perhaps he was leaking into the king. The direction of the flow was the question I could not make myself ask out loud.

"I'm reading it again," I admitted.

He sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and looked at the door the map had drawn. "I know that door," he said quietly. "I walked through it once. Before."

"Before you were sent away."

"Before I was made," he corrected, gently, because he was always gentler with the truth than I was. "Corvane said it. I'm not a boy who was hidden. I'm a boy who was *built*. From what the Writ took first." He traced the lintel with one finger, not touching the ink. "This is the garden gate. The old one. Before the palace grew over it."

"And the words around it?"

"I know them," he said. "But I can't say them. It's strange—I know what they mean the way you know a smell. It means *come home*. It means *the door knows you*." He frowned. "It means more than that. There's a word in the middle I've never been able to find in any language you've taught me. I keep thinking tomorrow I'll know it. I keep being wrong."

I turned the map toward him. "Show me which word."

He did. His finger stopped on a character like a seed split open, a root reaching downward, and something in my chest went very still, because I had seen that character before—not on the map, but in the guild's oldest ledger, the one no one was permitted to open without the Guildmaster's seal. I had seen it once, years ago, when Corvane had left the vault unlocked and my curiosity had been younger and better behaved than it was now.

It was the character used in the old tongue for a true name. Not a name given by parents. Not a name earned or traded or recorded. The name a thing is before anything is written about it. The guild had a whole taxonomy of names—we dealt in them constantly, bought and sold identities like flour—and every classification began with the same warning, inked in red:

*A true name is not property. A true name is the thing itself.*

"Emrys," I said. "When you speak that word—at the door—what do you think happens?"

He considered it the way he considered everything now, with a patience that broke my heart, because patience was not a child's trait. Patience was something the centuries had pressed into him like a flower in a book.

"The door opens," he said. "And then I go home. That's what it's for, isn't it? That's why they made me. I'm the key that remembers it's a key."

"That's not what I asked."

He looked at me then, and in the moonlight his eyes were the exact grey-green of new leaves, and I understood that he already knew, and had known for some time, and had been waiting for me to say it first because saying it first was my job and not his. He was kind that way. He was kind in all the ways that cost him most.

"Ask me properly," he said.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat by the dead miller's hearth with my notebook and did what I do best, which is take an unbearable truth and cut it into pieces small enough to hold.

Here is what the pieces looked like.

Piece one: the Verdant Writ is bound to King Aldous Vey by wish and consumption. It granted his famine-wish by eating the kingdom's memories, and it sustains itself by eating him—his past, his selfhood, his name. Corvane had said it plainly by the fire two nights ago: *a crown of living green does not sit on a head, girl. It roots there.*

Piece two: the Writ cannot be cut away while the king consents to it, even unconsciously. A wish freely made holds until it is freely revoked. But Aldous no longer has enough self left to revoke anything. You cannot unsay a promise if the mouth that made it has been eaten.

Piece three—which was the piece I kept turning over until my fingers bled, metaphorically speaking: the guild had built Emrys from the part of Aldous the Writ took *first*. The childhood. The original material. Which meant that somewhere inside that small solemn body was the king's beginning, the seed-self, the name-before-names. The Writ had eaten forward through Aldous's life, year by year, decade by decade—but it had never been able to eat backward past the point where the guild had made its cut. Emrys was the one piece of Aldous the Writ had never tasted.

Which meant Emrys's true name and Aldous's true name were not similar.

They were identical.

There was only one name. There had only ever been one name. The guild had not created a boy; it had amputated a man's origin and taught the amputation to walk.

And here was the arithmetic I could not escape: if Emrys spoke that name at the door—freely given, as the lintel demanded—it would not merely open a gate. A true name spoken freely by its owner is the strongest assertion of selfhood that exists. It would reach into the throne room and find the Writ mid-meal and declare, in the oldest grammar there is, *this self is claimed.* The binding would begin to tear. The king would begin to wake.

And the name would be spent doing it. Because a name is not a word you keep after you use it. A name is the thing itself, and the thing itself would be pouring through that door into a dying man who needed all of it—

—and the boy standing at the threshold would be left holding nothing.

I wrote it out in my notebook in my cleanest hand, because ugly truths deserve good penmanship, and then I read it back and hated every word of it.

*To free Aldous Vey, Emrys must give his name. To give his name is to give himself. The door will open. The king will rise. The boy will not.*

I closed the notebook. Outside, the capital glowed on the horizon like a coal wrapped in moss, and I thought about the guild's first law, the one I had recited every morning for eleven years: *record everything.* I had recorded ten thousand impossibilities and never once been one of them. Now I was. Now I stood on the wrong side of the ledger, and the entry being written was me.

* * *

Emrys found me at dawn. Of course he did. He always knew where I was; I had stopped finding it uncanny and started finding it unbearable, the way he oriented to me like a compass needle, like I was the one fixed point in a life that had been stolen and rebuilt and stolen again.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"No."

"You figured it out." He sat down across the ashes from me. "Two days ago, actually. Maybe three. You've had the look since the village."

"What look?"

"The look of someone carrying a map she doesn't want me to see." He smiled a little. "You taught me that maps are honest. You didn't teach me that mapmakers aren't."

The dawn came up grey through the broken roof. Somewhere a bird sang three notes and stopped, as if it had forgotten the rest—a small thing, a nothing, and still it tightened my throat, because forgetting was spreading outward from that glowing city like frost across a window, and every forgotten birdsong was a brick in the wall we were walking toward.

"Tell me what you concluded," Emrys said. "All of it. Don't arrange it kindly. I've had two hundred years of other people arranging things kindly for me. Corvane hid me. The guild lost me. Someone decided, century after century, what I could bear to know." His voice stayed level, but his hands, in his lap, had curled into fists no bigger than plums. "You promised me truth. In the notebook. Every page. So tell me the page you're on right now."

So I did. I laid it out the way I lay out a survey: the name, the identity of names, the door, the expenditure. I told him that speaking his true name would begin the unbinding, and that the unbinding would feed on the very thing he offered, and that what remained afterward might be a waking king and no boy at all—or worse, a boy-shaped emptiness, a coat with no one in it. I did not soften it. He watched me the entire time with those leaf-colored eyes and did not look away once, and when I finished he was quiet for a long moment.

"Good," he said finally.

"*Good?*"

"It's a real answer." He nodded slowly. "Do you know how rare those are? Everyone else gave me riddles. Prophecies. *You will know when the time comes.* You gave me a price tag." He looked at the map, lying between us with its patient door. "Is there another way in? Be honest."

"There are ways into the palace," I said. "Servants' corridors, the river gates, the paths the map draws. We could get inside. We could probably even reach the throne room. But the Writ's defenses aren't walls—they're invitations. It got Aldous by offering him exactly what he most wanted. Anyone who comes at it with force or trickery gets the same treatment. An offer tailored to their hunger. And no one refuses their own hunger, Emrys. I've watched it work on a village. I've felt it working on me." I flexed my right hand, which had been aching since the village where nobody remembered their names—an ache shaped like a hand that wasn't mine, a cartographer's calluses I'd never earned. "The only thing the Writ can't offer is what it's already eaten. The only person alive who owns a piece of Aldous that the Writ never swallowed is you. The door doesn't want violence. It wants restitution. Something taken, returned freely."

"Then it's simple," Emrys said.

"It is not *simple*—"

"It's simple," he repeated, with that terrible patience. "One boy against a kingdom's memories. Even I can do that sum. What I want to know—" and here his voice did something I had never heard it do, cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in a wall I'd believed load-bearing— "is whether you'll let me. Because you have that face. The face you wore when Corvane ordered me logged as an anomaly. The face that means you're planning to be noble on my behalf without consulting me."

"Emrys—"

"I'm not finished." He leaned forward. "I was made for this. Do you understand what that means to someone like me? I have no birthday. No mother. No first word, no scraped knee, no winter I remember learning to hate. Corvane says I was built as a failsafe. Fine. Then this is the purpose I was built *for*, and everything else—all of this, the roads, the lessons, the notebook—" his fist came down gently on it, "—was the life I got to live in the margins while I waited. Most people never get a purpose at all. They spend their whole lives searching for one. Mine has been sitting in a throne room for two hundred years with my name on it."

"Your name," I said, "is exactly the problem."

"Then it's fitting," he said, "that giving it away is the answer."

I got up. I couldn't sit still for it anymore; the argument was a pressure behind my ribs and if I didn't move I was going to say something unforgivable, something like *you're a child* or *you're mine to protect*, both of which were lies, both of which were the guild talking, the old law wearing my voice. Never intervene. Never spend yourself. Record the story; don't become it.

I had burned that law on a roadside nine days ago. Apparently it had left ghosts.

"Elodie." He had followed me to the doorway, where the morning fog was burning off the millpond. "Why did you really leave the guild? Corvane gave you a choice. Hand me over, keep your ledgers, your rank, your life. You threw it away for a boy you'd known a fortnight. Why?"

"Because it was true," I said. "What you are. What happened to the king. The guild knew and filed it away and called the filing neutrality, and I couldn't—" My voice caught. I mastered it. Cartographers master things; it's the whole profession. "I couldn't be the drawer of a map that lied by omission. Not anymore."

"Exactly." He stepped closer. "Truth. You chose truth over safety. Over the institution. Over everything you'd built your life on." He held out his hand, palm up, and in it he had placed the notebook—our notebook, filled front to back with our journey in my handwriting and his careful, rootlike script. "So choose it again. Tell me the truth you figured out at dawn, the one under the one you told me. The one you're still not saying."

The pond steamed. The bird tried its three notes again and got four this time.

"If you speak your name," I said, "you may not survive as yourself. And I—" The word rose in me like floodwater, eleven years of loneliness behind it, every ledger entry that was someone else's story and none of them mine, every night of competent silence, every impossible thing catalogued and none of them kept. "I have never had anything that was mine. Not a history, not a family, not a story. And then you knocked on my door, and for the first time in my life I was inside a story instead of writing other people's, and I am not—"

I stopped. Breathed.

"I am not ready to be alone in one again."

Emrys looked at me for a long moment. Then he did the cruelest, kindest thing he had ever done: he took my hand, the aching one, the one with the phantom calluses, and pressed the notebook back into it.

"Then write faster," he said softly. "Because I'm going to say the name, Elodie. Tomorrow, at that door. And whatever's left of me when the echo clears—I want it to be able to read about us. All of it. Even tonight. *Especially* tonight." He turned toward the capital, toward the glow under the moss-green haze, and the light caught the silver at his temples. "Start the page now. Put today's date on it. And write this at the top, exactly as I say it."

His shoulders squared. When he spoke, his voice was steady, and underneath the steadiness I heard it—the buried cadence of the old language, the rhythm of a king's childhood rising through a boy's throat like water through a root.

"*On the last day,*" he dictated, "*I gave my name freely, and it was mine to give, because I spent one perfect season learning what it was worth.*"

I wrote it down. My hand shook. The ink held.

Behind us, on my knees where I'd left it, the map stirred once in the windless mill—and drew, in fresh ink, a second figure standing beside the first at the garden door.
