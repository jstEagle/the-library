## 14. What I Would Keep

The night before the gate, Emrys could not sleep.

He lay on the narrow bed in the safehouse above the tannery, listening to the city breathe. Tomorrow they would go to the palace. Tomorrow he would stand in front of a door that opened only for a true name, and Elodie had told him what that name was—*his* name, the king's name, the word that would unmake him. He understood it the way you understand a stone falling: not as a choice but as a fact with weight.

But sleep would not come, and so he got up.

Elodie was still awake too. He knew it without looking—the way he always knew now, some thread between them that had grown since the village, since the singing, since she had held his hand through the memory-floods and never once flinched. She sat at the little table by the window with a candle, drawing. Of course she was drawing. She drew when she was afraid; he had learned that about her the way he had learned *door*, and *bread*, and *stay*.

"I can't sleep," he said. His common speech was good now, good enough to surprise even him sometimes. Words came to him like water finding a crack.

"Neither can I." She didn't look up from the page. "I'm mapping tomorrow's route for the fourth time. It keeps coming out different."

"It's not the route that's wrong."

She stopped. Set down her pen. Looked at him across the candlelight, and he saw that she already knew what he had come to say, because Elodie always knew first and hated knowing first.

"Sit down," she said quietly.

He sat.

* * *

"I want you to write something," Emrys said. "Not a map."

"A map is safer than words."

"No." He shook his head. "Maps change. You told me. Yours changed every time you slept. But a story—" He struggled. The old language rose up in him, the one older than kingdoms, full of roots and green things, and he pushed it down because this needed to be said in her tongue, plainly, so there could be no mistaking it. "A story stays where you put it. Even if everything else changes."

Elodie turned her chair toward him fully. In the candlelight her face looked carved and tired and very dear.

"What do you want me to write?"

"Everything." His voice cracked on it, and he pressed on before it could fail him entirely. "Everything about us. From the door. From the rain that hadn't fallen yet. The bottled echo, the bells ringing for me though I was never traded for—" he smiled a little at her expression— "yes, I remember that night, I remember all of it, that's the problem. That's exactly the problem, Elodie. Tomorrow I say my true name and the Writ tears loose and two hundred years of Aldous come back into me like a flood into a dry riverbed. And I don't know what's left after that. Corvane doesn't know. Nobody knows. Maybe nothing. Maybe just the king, whole again, and no boy at all."

"So you want—"

"A record. So that some version of me might remember. Or so that *you* might remember for me. Or so that nobody has to remember, and it's written down instead, which is better than both, because writing doesn't grieve." He swallowed. "You taught me that. You said ledgers are how we refuse to let things vanish. You said it angrily, the night we ran, but you said it."

"I said a lot of things the night we ran."

"You meant them anyway."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her satchel—not the great cartographer's satchel with its instruments and its rolled vellum, but the small inner pocket she kept against her ribs—and drew out a notebook. Small, cheaply bound, the kind a merchant's clerk might carry. Its pages were blank.

"I bought this in the village," she said. "The one that forgot its name. I don't know why. I stood in the baker's shop while you sang his memories back to him, and I bought a notebook, and I couldn't tell you why, and I've been carrying it ever since feeling like a fool."

"You're not a fool."

"I know." A ghost of a smile. "I know why now. Sit closer. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly, and properly means together. It's your story. I'm only the hand."

* * *

They started at the beginning, or as near to it as either of them could find.

"The door," Elodie said, pen moving. "Midnight. Rain falling upward off the cobbles, which I have never recorded before or since. And a boy standing in it, soaked, holding a folded map that moved when I blinked."

"I wasn't soaked," Emrys said. "I was *rained*. There's a difference. The rain came out of me. I think—I think I'd been walking through weather that hadn't happened yet, and it clung."

She wrote it down exactly as he said it. *He was rained.* He watched the words appear under her pen and felt something loosen in his chest, some knot he'd carried since the guild door.

"Tell me about the walking," she said. "Before. Before me."

And here he had to stop, because the before was mostly noise. He closed his eyes and let himself sink into it, the way he'd learned to, gently, like wading into cold water.

"There was a road," he said slowly. "There was always a road. Sometimes it was white chalk on grey stone, sometimes it was just the feeling of being pointed somewhere. I knew three things. I knew I had to keep walking. I knew the map would show me the way when no one was watching it. And I knew I was going to find 'the one who draws what isn't there.'" He opened his eyes. "That was you. I didn't know your face. I didn't know your name. But I knew you the way you know a word you've never spoken—you feel the shape of it in your mouth. When I saw you through the window, cataloguing that echo, I thought: *oh*. Not *there she is*. Just *oh*. Like something finishing."

Elodie's pen had gone still. There was wet light on her lashes that she pretended was the candle.

"*Oh,*" she repeated, and wrote that down too. She wrote: *He recognized me instantly. He says it felt like something finishing.*

"It did," Emrys said. "It still does. Write that."

"I'm writing everything. That's the deal. Even the parts that make me cry on the page, apparently."

"Especially those. Ink dries faster than tears."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then had to put the pen down and press the heel of her hand against her eyes for a moment. When she picked it up again, her voice was steady.

"Lessons. Tell me about the lessons."

"You drew pictures. A sun. A house. A man and a boy eating bread." He grinned despite everything. "Your houses were terrible. All leaning. Like they were drunk."

"My houses were *stylized*."

"They were drunk, Elodie. I learned the word for bread before I learned the word for house because I refused to learn the word for house until you admitted the roof was crooked."

"Noted for the record," she said solemnly, writing. *My houses are terrible. He is insufferable. I would not trade him for anything, which the Guild would tell me is a violation of fair trade.* Then she crossed it out—or tried to. He caught her wrist.

"Leave it."

"It's not—"

"Leave it," he said again, gentler. "It's true. The book is for truth. You taught me that too. Everything true goes in, even the parts that cost."

Her breath went out of her slowly. She uncrossed it.

* * *

They worked until the candle burned low and she had to light another, and then a third. The notebook filled. The flight along roads that existed only while drawn—she made him describe the exhilaration of it, the way the world felt *unlocked*, and he described it shyly, blushing, saying it was the first time he'd ever seen her look young. The village with no names. The baker who wept over his own daughter's face like a man given back his own hands. The firelight and Corvane's terrible history, two hundred years of winter, a famine ended and a kingdom eaten as the price.

"And the emissary," Elodie said. "The one in grey."

Emrys went still. Even now, months later, that voice could reach into him and pull.

"He sang to me," Emrys said finally. "When I was—when I was small, when I was the part of Aldous that laughed. That voice sang lullabies in a garden. And then it grew old and hard and it came hunting me politely, with a writ sealed in moss." He looked at his hands. "Write that the past hunts politely. That's important. People think the past comes like a storm. It doesn't. It knocks. It brings gifts. It waits to be invited in."

She wrote it. *The past hunts politely.*

Then, quietly: "Emrys. Why are we really doing this? Not for some version of you. Tell me the true reason. The book is for truth, remember."

He had known this question was coming. He had been walking toward it longer than he could count, the way he'd walked toward her.

"Because tomorrow I choose," he said. "Corvane thinks I'll hesitate at the gate. He thinks part of me will refuse—that the boy will cling to being a boy. And maybe he's right. Maybe when I open my mouth, what comes out will be screaming instead of naming. So I need to decide tonight, while it's quiet, and I need the decision written down where I can't lie about it later." He met her eyes. "I choose the truth. I choose the king. I choose to say the name even if it unmakes me. Because I was made from stolen things, Elodie—from a childhood that was taken and never lived. And I would rather be spent than hoarded. That's what the Writ never understood. That's what the guild never understood. Things kept are things that rot. Things given—" he touched the notebook— "things given become stories, and stories don't die."

Elodie set down the pen.

For a long moment she didn't move at all, and Emrys watched her and understood that he had just done something to her—that the lesson had traveled backward, teacher becoming student becoming teacher. She had spent her whole life recording other people's stories behind a wall of neutrality, and here was a boy made of borrowed years telling her that the wall itself was the rot.

"You're teaching me the choice I have to make," she said at last. "Aren't you. At the gate. Whatever happens to you."

"I'm not teaching you anything," Emrys said. "I'm just telling the truth. What you do with it is yours."

She picked up the pen. Her hand was shaking, but the letters came out clean.

*He chooses truth over survival,* she wrote. *And in choosing it, he teaches me that survival was never the point.*

* * *

Near dawn, the notebook was nearly full. Emrys took it in both hands—his first possession, the first thing in his long strange life that belonged to him and no one else—and turned the pages slowly, reading his own life in her careful hand.

"There's room for one more," Elodie said. "Last page. Say something. Anything. Your words, not mine."

Emrys thought. Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, and somewhere below, the city was waking toward the day they would walk to the palace, toward the garden in the walls, toward the door and the name and whatever came after.

He said: "*If someone reads this and I am gone—remember that I was happy. Not at the end. All the way through. Even the frightened parts. Especially the walking. I was walking toward her the whole time, and I arrived.*"

Elodie wrote it without a single correction.

Then she closed the notebook and pressed it back into his hands, folding his fingers over the cover.

"Keep it," she said. "It's yours. First possession, first story. And tomorrow—" her voice caught, steadied— "tomorrow, whatever version of you walks out of that throne room, this goes with him. Or it stays with me, and I read it aloud, and I don't stop reading until every word has found a home. Do you understand? Nothing in this book gets to vanish. I'll burn every ledger in the kingdom before I let that happen."

Emrys looked at her—at the woman who had drawn him drunk little houses and named him with borrowed words and loved him in ink—and nodded.

Down in the street, a bell began to ring.

Not the guild's bell. Not any bell he knew. It came from the direction of the palace, deep and slow and green-sounding, and as it rang, every vine on every wall outside the window turned its flowers toward the sound at once, like faces turning toward a name being called.

Elodie stood. "It knows we're coming," she whispered.

Emrys slid the notebook inside his shirt, against his heart, and reached for his coat.

"Then let's not keep it waiting."
