## 15. The Garden in the Walls

The map stopped redrawing itself the night before we reached the capital.

I noticed it at dawn, when I unrolled it on the floor of a hay barn outside Vey's Reach and found every line fixed, every path inked solid as if carved into stone. For weeks the thing had been a living document—roads sliding like rivers, the palace garden blooming and wilting across its surface in slow cycles. Now it was still.

"It's finished," Emrys said. He'd learned to speak so quickly that sometimes I forgot he'd once communicated entirely in gestures and that old, old language of his. Sometimes he forgot too, and slipped back into it mid-sentence, words falling away from him like leaves off a branch. "It's not drawing anymore. It's *showing*."

"Those aren't different things."

"They are." He crouched over the map, his finger hovering above the palace without touching it, the way you hover near a sleeping animal. "A drawing is someone deciding what to put down. This is just—" he searched for the word, found it, winced at it— "*remembering*. Out loud."

We had three days' walk to the city walls, and for those three days the map lay quiet in my satchel like a thing that had said everything it intended to say. I kept checking on it anyway. Old habit. A cartographer trusts her instruments or she trusts nothing, and I had spent eleven years trusting mine.

What I hadn't spent eleven years doing was breaking into palaces.

* * *

Vey's Reach was worse than I remembered, and I had never actually been there—which is its own kind of sentence to write. The guild's archives held maps of the capital going back four hundred years, and I had copied half of them as an apprentice, tracing the same streets until I knew them better than my own face. So walking those streets felt like meeting a stranger who insists you're old friends.

The city was beautiful. That was the first wrongness. Famine had ended here two centuries ago; the Writ had seen to that, granting Aldous's wish by eating the kingdom's memories one by one, and a well-fed city shows its feeding. Market stalls overflowed. Fountains ran clear. Every window box spilled flowers so lush they looked painted.

And every person we passed smiled.

Not the ordinary smiles of ordinary people—not the quick grimace of courtesy or the real thing that starts in the eyes. These were smiles held at a fixed width, like doors propped open with wedges. A fishmonger arranging silver-bellied catch. A guard at the outer wall, halberd planted, chin lifted toward nothing. A woman shaking out a rug from a second-floor window, her arms moving in perfect rhythm while her smile never wavered and her eyes never moved at all.

"They're not unhappy," Emrys whispered. We stood in the shadow of a grain merchant's awning, watching the street flow past us like something poured rather than walked. "That's the terrible part. Look at them. They're *content*."

"I know."

"You don't. You can't." His hand found my sleeve—the gesture was becoming his, the way certain phrases were becoming mine. "Contentment without anything underneath it. It's like—" He struggled. He was getting better at metaphor; I'd taught him most of them myself, which meant I was partly responsible for sentences like what came next. "It's like a room where someone took all the furniture out and hung paintings of furniture instead."

The village we'd passed through a week ago had been emptier than this—people who'd lost their names entirely, keeping only their trades, a baker who couldn't tell you his mother's face but could describe proofing dough in his sleep. Here the hunger had gone deeper and gentler. Here people still had their names. They'd just stopped needing them.

"The palace," I said. "Focus. The map showed us the way in."

It had shown us more than a way in. Over the last week of travel, as the lines settled and hardened, the hidden paths had emerged one by one: a drainage culvert beneath the east wall that no guard watched because no guard remembered it existed; a seam in the palace's foundation where two eras of construction met badly; a stair that appeared on no survey because it had been bricked over during Aldous's grandfather's reign—and then, apparently, unbricked by roots. The map didn't show the palace as it was. It showed the palace as the garden had made it, and the garden had been patient for two hundred years, working its way through mortar and memory alike.

Emrys carried the map now. I'd handed it to him outside the city and hadn't asked for it back. Some part of me understood—I think I understood even then—that the thing wasn't guiding me anywhere. It was guiding itself home, and we were luggage it had grown fond of.

* * *

The culvert smelled of green.

That sounds like nothing. It isn't nothing. Culverts smell of stone and standing water and the particular sourness of whatever cities wash away. This one smelled of a greenhouse in high summer—loam and sap and pollen thick enough to taste. Our boots splashed through an inch of clear water, and where each drop fell, I swear the moss on the walls brightened.

"Elodie." Emrys had stopped. Ahead of us, the culvert bent left toward the foundation seam, exactly as mapped. But the moss—it covered everything, ankle-high, knee-high in places, and it was growing *toward* us. Not fast. Growing the way plants grow, which is to say imperceptibly and yet unmistakably, fronds curling in our direction like faces turning.

"It knows we're here," he said.

"Plants don't know things."

"This isn't a plant." He said it flatly, without drama, and that flatness frightened me more than any trembling would have. "You've read the ledgers. The old ones, the sealed ones Corvane let you copy before we ran. What did they call it?"

"A living instrument," I said. "A writ that enforces itself. Text that became root." I had copied those pages by candlelight in Corvane's study, two hundred years of guild observation distilled into careful, neutral language: *the Verdant Writ continues to expand its territory at a rate consistent with the king's remaining memory reserves.* Neutral language. The guild's specialty. Reading it, you'd think we were describing weather.

"And what does text do," Emrys said, "when the thing it's written about walks into the room?"

I didn't answer. The moss answered for me, a slow ripple running along the culvert wall ahead of us, outward from our position, like a word being passed down a line of whispering children.

We went on. There was nowhere else to go, and besides—the thought arrived cold and unwelcome—we were expected. Whatever lived in this green had been expecting us since before either of us was born.

* * *

The foundation seam was exactly where the map promised: a gap wide enough for a thin woman and a thin boy, walled on both sides with brick older than the dynasty, threaded through with roots so thick they'd become structural. Emrys went first. I followed, and halfway through, when the gap narrowed and I had to turn sideways and exhale everything from my lungs, I felt them—the roots—shift. Adjusting around me. Making room.

Polite. Everything in this place was polite.

We came up through a collapsed section of cellar floor into a servant's corridor, and that was where the vines began in earnest.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was my trade and it's all I have left of myself some days. The corridors of Vey's Reach were not ruined. They were not decayed. They were *occupied*. Flowering vines ran along every wall in patterns too regular to be wild growth and too organic to be architecture—wainscoting of living wood, cornices of woven stem, and everywhere, everywhere, blossoms. White ones, mostly, five-petaled, faintly luminous, opening as we passed and closing behind us like eyes deciding we weren't worth remembering.

And the courtiers.

The first one sat in an alcove that should have held a statue—a young man in silk gone soft with age, hands folded in his lap, smiling at the middle distance. As we crept past, his lips moved.

"—and the harvest came in gold, and no child went hungry, and the king said *let it always be so*—"

He wasn't talking to us. He was reciting. I realized, with a lurch, that he was reciting the wish. The original wish, the famine-ending wish, worn smooth as a river stone from two hundred years of repetition. Further down the corridor, another courtier, an old woman in a gown stiff with dried perfume, murmured the same words at a different point in the sentence, their voices overlapping like rounds of a song.

"They're the memory," Emrys breathed. "The first memory it ate. It didn't digest it. It *planted* it."

Every alcove held a figure. Every figure recited. And every one of them, as we passed, turned their head—not quickly, not with any intent I could name, but with the slow inevitability of sunflowers tracking light—to watch us go. Smiling. Always smiling. Their eyes were the worst part: not vacant, exactly. Vacant implies absence. Their eyes were *full*, full of something green and growing, full of contentment with nothing underneath.

One of them, a girl no older than sixteen, reached out as Emrys passed and touched his hair. Gently. Almost lovingly.

"*There* you are," she said, in his language—the dead language, the old tongue, the one no ledger recorded until he arrived speaking it. Then she folded her hands again and resumed her recitation, and did not look at us again.

Emrys didn't speak for a long time after that. Neither did I. Some silences are too heavy to carry words.

* * *

The throne room doors were oak, twice a man's height, banded in bronze gone green—and I mean green-green, verdigris and living moss in equal measure, metal and plant fused into a single material that might have been beautiful if beauty hadn't stopped being the right word somewhere back down the corridor.

They opened before we touched them.

No groan of hinges. No ceremony. They simply swung inward, smooth as a shopkeeper's greeting, and warm air rolled out over us carrying the scent of ten thousand blossoms, and beyond the doors—

Beyond the doors was not a throne room.

I had studied the surveys. The throne room of Vey's Reach was a vaulted hall eighty paces long, floored in checkered marble, built to hold a thousand petitioners beneath a ceiling painted with the dynasty's founding. What lay beyond those doors was a garden. No—*the* Garden. The one the map had been remembering all along, rendered at last in full scale: paths of white gravel winding between beds of flowers in colors I had no names for, hedges sculpted into shapes that resolved, the longer you looked, into figures—courtiers, farmers, children—all frozen mid-celebration. An orchard heavy with fruit grew where the petitioners' benches should have been. A stream crossed the hall where the aisle to the throne should have run, clear water chuckling over stones, and flowering vines had swallowed the walls entire, climbed the vaulted ceiling, and bloomed there in cascades, so that petals fell through the air like slow snow.

At the far end, where the throne should have been, sat the king.

He was not what I expected, though I couldn't have said what I expected. Not a skeleton wrapped in bark. Not a monster. He was a man perhaps fifty years old, though the records said he should be past two hundred—preserved the way things are preserved in amber, whole and unchanged and utterly unable to move. He wore robes that had become a garden themselves, fabric and foliage indistinguishable. His hands rested on the arms of a chair that was less a throne than the heart of a great tree, grown around and through and over the original seat until the two were one object with two histories.

And his crown was alive.

I had read the phrase a dozen times in the sealed ledgers—*a crown replaced by a living thing*—and had imagined something parasitic, something gripping. This was worse. The crown was a wreath of branches growing directly from his skull, rooted in him, leafed in tiny perfect leaves, and threaded through with small white flowers that pulsed with light in a rhythm I recognized only after several seconds of horrified counting.

It matched his heartbeat.

"He's still in there," Emrys whispered. He had moved forward without my noticing, drawn down the gravel path as surely as water finds its level. "Elodie—he's *in there*. I can feel him. Like hearing someone call from very far away, under the ground."

"Emrys." My voice came out strange. Thin. "Wait."

But he was already walking, and the garden was already responding—flowers turning toward him, the stream's murmur shifting pitch, the petal-snow thickening—and I understood, standing there on the threshold with my cartographer's soul screaming that I was looking at territory no map had ever survived, that this was the trap. Not ahead of us. Not waiting. *This*. The beauty itself. Two hundred years ago a starving kingdom had wished for plenty, and the Writ had granted it, and this was plenty: abundance without end, contentment without depth, a room where all the furniture had been taken out and replaced with paintings of furniture, and the paintings were lovely, God help me, the paintings were the loveliest thing I had ever seen, and every instinct I owned wanted to lie down among the flowers and stop wanting anything ever again.

The king's eyes opened.

They were green. Of course they were green. And they were not empty—they were the opposite of empty, flooded, drowned, a man looking out from inside his own overgrown mind like someone waving from a window of a house being swallowed by ivy. They found Emrys walking toward him down the white path, and something moved across the king's face, something that fought its way up through two centuries of green like a shoot through pavement:

Recognition. And grief. And hope, vast and hopeless, the hope of a drowning man who has forgotten what air is but remembers, remembers, that he once breathed.

The flowers on the crown flared bright.

And the whole garden—the vines, the hedges, the sculpted celebrants, the thousand thousand blossoms—turned toward us at once, and the king's mouth opened, and what spoke through it was not his voice. It was many voices braided together, the courtiers in their alcoves, the city in its streets, the whole fed and smiling kingdom speaking as one, gentle as a lullaby:

"*Welcome home,*" said the Verdant Writ, in Emrys's own dead language. "*We have kept your place.*"
