## 16. Audience with the Verdant King

The garden had a heartbeat.

Emrys felt it before he understood it—a slow pulse under his bare feet, rising up through the roots that had eaten the marble floor of the throne room and turned it into loam. Each beat came a little slower than the last, like something enormous breathing in its sleep, or pretending to.

*Don't be afraid,* Elodie had said at the doors. But she was afraid too. He could see it in the way she held her satchel against her chest, the notebook inside it pressed flat as if she could keep their whole story safe by holding it tight enough. Fear didn't make him distrust her. It made him trust her more. She was afraid and she had come anyway, and that was what people did when something mattered.

The king sat on a throne that was no longer a throne.

It had been wood once—dark oak, maybe, carved with lions or lilies, some heraldry from before—but green had taken it the way water takes paper. Vines wrapped the arms and legs of the chair and climbed the king's body with the same patient tenderness. They wound around his ankles, his wrists, his throat, and they bloomed where they touched him: small white flowers opening along his forearms like a second skin, a skin that grew instead of aged.

His crown was alive.

That was the thing Emrys's eyes kept returning to, the thing that made his stomach turn over and over. It wasn't metal wearing leaves. It was a wreath of branches that had grown into the shape of a crown, twined through hair gone white as frost, and the branches were still growing—he could see it, the slow creak and curl of new shoots, buds swelling and opening and falling as petals all around the king's shoulders in a snowfall that smelled like spring.

The king's eyes were open.

They were green. Not the green of irises but the green of leaves held up to the sun, light coming *through* them, and they were fixed on the far wall without seeing anything—or seeing everything, all at once, every year at the same time.

"Aldous," Elodie whispered beside him. Not a question. A name, spoken the way you'd speak a word into a lock.

The garden's heartbeat stuttered.

*He can hear that,* Emrys realized. *Under everything, he can still hear his own name.*

He took a step forward. The vines on the floor shifted—not grabbing, not yet, just turning toward him the way flowers turn toward light—and he felt them reading him, tasting the air around him, and he understood suddenly why the emissary's voice had made his blood sing backwards. These things knew him. The Writ had made him. Somewhere in its long green memory, he was a page it had written and then lost.

"Hello," Emrys said.

His voice sounded very small in all that growing. The white petals kept falling. The king's leaf-green eyes did not move.

"I brought you something," Emrys said, because it was true, though he hadn't known until this moment that it was. His hand went to his chest, to the place under his ribs where the notebook's words lived even when the notebook itself was back in Elodie's satchel. *You ate bread in a village that forgot its own name, and you gave the baker hers back.* "I brought you yourself."

The heartbeat stumbled again. Faster now. The vines along the walls shivered, and somewhere high above, in the flowering dark of the ceiling, something groaned—the sound of stone being gently, patiently pushed apart by roots that had been pushing for two hundred years and saw no reason to stop.

And then the king moved.

It was small, at first. A finger uncurling from the arm of the vine-wrapped throne. Then his head turned, degree by degree, bark cracking softly at his neck like ice on a pond in thaw, and those lit-green eyes slid down from the far wall and found Emrys standing in the middle of the garden that had been a kingdom.

The king smiled.

It was a terrible smile and a beautiful one, and Emrys felt both of those things at once, felt them split him down the middle. Because it was the smile of a man waking from a dream he doesn't want to leave, blinking into a room he half-remembers. And it was the smile of someone looking at—

"Oh," the king said. His voice was dry leaves and deep water. "Oh, there you are."

Emrys's whole body went cold.

"There you are," Aldous said again, warmer, and he reached out one hand—vines parting for it, blossoms brushing his wrist like ladies-in-waiting—and Emrys knew, with a certainty that arrived like a blow, exactly who the king thought he was looking at.

Not a messenger. Not a fragment. Not a failsafe forged by frightened cartographers two hundred years ago.

A son.

"You're late," the king said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through into something wetter. "Your mother said you'd be late. She was always right about—" He stopped. Blinked. The green light in his eyes flickered like a candle in wind. "She was... there was a garden. We made a promise in a garden."

*We made a promise in a garden.* The words Emrys had carried since the beginning, since the first night in the rain that hadn't fallen yet, since the map that drew itself. He had thought they were his memory. He understood now, standing in the source of them, that they had never been his at all. They were the king's. Everything he was had been scooped out of this man like flesh from a fruit, and the rind had been sent walking through the rain to find its way home.

"I'm not—" Emrys started, and couldn't finish. Because some traitor part of him wanted to be. Wanted to step into those open arms and be the son, wanted the belonging that poured off the king like heat off a hearth. Two hundred years old and less than a year alive, and here was the only person in the world who might look at him and see family.

Elodie's hand found his shoulder. Light. Steady. *I'm here*, the hand said. *You're not alone in this.*

He breathed. He stepped closer anyway—but closer to the truth, not the arms.

"My name is Emrys," he said.

The garden flinched.

Every flower on every vine closed at once, a thousand small fists, and the heartbeat under the floor went silent for three full seconds, and in that silence Emrys heard something else underneath: a rustling, a whispering, the sound of countless pages turning in a book no one was holding.

Then the king's spine straightened, and the voice that came out of him was not the king's voice.

* * *

It was still coming from Aldous's mouth. That was the horror of it—that the Writ didn't wear a body of its own, that it spoke through the man it had hollowed, using his lips, his tongue, his ruined gentle voice, so that every word it said was also a violation. The king's face stayed soft. The king's eyes stayed kind. The words came out of him like water through a broken pipe.

*Cartographer.*

The word rolled across the garden and settled over Elodie like a net. Emrys felt her go still beside him.

*You have traveled far. You have broken your laws for love of this small thing. I know what that costs. I have been an institution myself.* The king's head tilted, courteous, almost amused. *I would not see you punished for it. I would see you rewarded.*

"We're not trading," Elodie said. Her voice was level. Emrys heard the effort underneath it, the iron she was lifting with every syllable. "Whatever you're offering—"

*I am offering you nothing you must take blindly.* The Writ let the silence stretch. Petals drifted down between them. *I am offering you the only thing you have ever wanted. And I know what that is, because I have read you, Elodie Delacroix, the way you read your maps.*

Emrys looked at her. She had gone pale in the green light, and her hand had come up unconsciously to her collarbone, to the hollow of her throat, and he remembered—suddenly, completely—the night by the fire in the village of forgotten names, when she had told him why she kept ledgers for other people's stories. *Every entry is someone else's life. Mine starts the day I walked through the guild door with empty hands and a letter of introduction. Before that, nothing. No town. No family name that fits anywhere. I'm a map with the middle torn out.*

*You came to us with nothing,* the Writ said through the king's smiling mouth. *No history. No origin. You have spent your whole life drawing other people's worlds because yours is a blank page, and blank pages are a kind of hunger, aren't they? You know exactly what it is to reach for a memory that isn't there.*

"Stop," Elodie whispered.

*I can give it back.* The vines along the walls stirred, and among the white flowers, others opened—pale gold, shaped like pages, like folded letters. *Somewhere in my keeping is the girl you were before the guild. The road you came down. The voice that taught you to draw. I hold two hundred years of what this kingdom surrendered, and you would be amazed, cartographer, at what people gave up without knowing they were giving it. Perhaps some of it is yours. Perhaps all of it is. Walk out that door with the boy and never look back, and tomorrow you will wake up knowing your mother's face.*

Emrys watched Elodie's face do something he had never seen it do.

It broke.

Not all the way. Just a crack, right through the composure she wore like armor, and through the crack he saw it—the want, vast and starving, the want of a woman who had catalogued ten thousand lives and never once been able to find her own. Her eyes went bright. Her breath came ragged. For one long moment she was not a cartographer or a rebel or the woman who had burned her ledgers in her mind already; she was just a girl with a torn-out middle, being offered the missing piece.

And then she looked at him.

That was all. She looked at Emrys, standing in the falling petals with the king's stolen childhood in his chest, and whatever she saw in his face—whatever he couldn't hide, the fear and the fierce hope and the terrible knowledge that he was asking her, just by existing, to choose him over herself—it steadied her. He watched the crack close. Not healed. Sealed. The way you seal a letter you've decided to send.

"No," Elodie said.

*Think—*

"I've thought." Her voice rang off the flowering walls. "I've thought about it every night since I met him. Do you know what I found? I found that I don't want a past if buying it costs me this. If the price is walking away while he dissolves—if the price is becoming someone who could do that—then whoever I was before isn't worth meeting. She'd disgust me." She lifted her chin. "Keep my history. Or give it to me free, right now, and watch me burn it in front of you."

The garden went very quiet.

The Writ studied her through the king's eyes, and Emrys felt the attention of it like weather, like pressure before a storm, ancient and vegetal and utterly without mercy. When it spoke again, the kindness was gone from the king's voice, and what remained was worse: patience.

*You would refuse a self to keep a fragment. Sentiment. The guild taught you better than that.*

"The guild taught me nothing worth keeping," Elodie said. "That's rather the point."

*Then we are at an impasse.* The king's head turned, slowly, back to Emrys, and the warmth flooded into the ruined voice again, and that was the cruelest trick of all—the Writ putting tenderness back on like a mask it knew fit. *But you, little page. You I do not need to bargain with. You are mine. Every root of you. You were cut from him and sent to me like a letter, and letters return to their sender.*

"I'm not a letter," Emrys said. His voice shook. He made it keep going. "I'm a person. Elodie wrote me down. I have a notebook. It has my whole story in it, and none of it is yours."

*All of it is mine,* the Writ said, gentle as rot. *But we needn't quarrel. Come here, child. Come and be whole. You have been walking toward this door longer than you can count—I felt your footsteps in my roots for two hundred years. Don't you want to stop walking? Don't you want to rest?*

And oh, he did. That was the trap inside the trap. The vines looked soft. The king's open arms looked like the end of every cold night on unmapped roads. Rest, the garden whispered in the language older than kingdoms, the language Emrys had spoken before he could speak anything else, and his feet took one step without his permission, and another—

"Emrys." Elodie's voice, sharp as a drawn line. "Look at the baker."

He stopped.

The baker. In the village with no names, the woman who had wept over her own hands because she couldn't remember what they were for—and how Emrys had sung, badly, the song he didn't know he knew, and how her face had come back to her like sunrise. She had remembered because of him. Not because of the Writ. The Writ only ever took. What he had given her, he had given freely.

*Memory taken is theft,* he thought, and the thought felt like a blade finding its hilt. *Memory given is a gift. That's the difference. That's the whole difference.*

"That's not rest," Emrys said aloud. He planted his feet in the living loam. "What you're offering isn't rest. It's being eaten slowly enough that it feels like sleep. I've seen your work. The village. The courtiers in the halls with their vacant smiles. You don't keep anyone. You digest them."

For the first time, the garden's patience cracked.

The heartbeat slammed back—fast now, furious, a drum instead of a breath—and every vine in the throne room stood up off the walls at once, and the white flowers all turned toward Emrys like a thousand eyes, and the king rose out of his throne with bark splitting along his arms, crowned in living green, terrible as spring itself.

*You do not know what I am,* the Writ said, and the whole garden shook with it, petals raining, roots groaning. *I am the wish that saved them. I am the famine's end. I am the only reason there was a kingdom left to map.*

"And you ate it to do it," Elodie said. She had moved—Emrys saw it sideways, her hand sliding into the satchel, closing around something. "You ended the hunger by eating everything else. And now there's a boy standing in your garden with your king's childhood in his chest, and he knows your name, Writ. He knows what you are."

*Names,* the Writ hissed through the king's beautiful ruined mouth, *are how this ends, little cartographer. So let us speak plainly of names. Let us speak of the one name that opens the heart of this place. Let us see whether either of you has the courage—*

But Emrys wasn't listening anymore.

Because the king—Aldous, the man himself, buried two hundred years deep under the green—had surfaced. One flicker, one gasp of the drowned. His leaf-light eyes cleared for a single instant, found Emrys's face, and the expression on them was not confusion and not mistaken love but recognition, sharp and agonized, the look of a man seeing his own childhood walk toward him across a battlefield.

*"Run,"* the real Aldous mouthed, silent, desperate. *"Before it learns—"*
