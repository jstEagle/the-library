## 18. Emrys, Unbound

The garden goes quiet when I open my mouth.

Not the quiet of listening. The quiet of a held breath. Even the vines stop their slow green crawling along the throne room walls, and the roses—the impossible, memory-eating roses—turn their faces toward me as if I am the sun they have been waiting two hundred years to find.

Elodie stands beside me. Her hand finds mine. Her fingers are ink-stained and trembling, and I hold on because she taught me that holding on is a thing people are allowed to do.

"You don't have to," she says.

But that is the whole point. That is the only point there has ever been. The door opened for a true name freely given, and everything in my life that was ever done to me was done without my consent—I was made without asking, hidden without asking, sent walking through centuries of rain without anyone once wondering what *I* wanted. This is the first thing that will be mine. My choice. My voice. My name.

Except it isn't my name at all. That's what makes it true.

I look at the king on his throne of woven roots. Aldous Vey. He is half-awake inside the green, his eyes the color of leaves held up to light, and somewhere deep beneath the Writ's flowering grip he is still in there—still the boy who made a promise in a garden, still the man who wished too large, still the hollowed shape of every memory the crown has eaten. When he looks at me, something moves behind his eyes like a fish under ice.

"Emrys," he says, or the Writ says through him, sweet as rot. "Little messenger. You came so far. Put down your burden. Let us give you rest."

"I know what you are," I say, and my voice does not shake, which surprises me. "You eat remembering. You make people trade their lives for wishes. You took a kingdom's name and called it mercy."

"We gave them spring," the Writ says. "We gave them full bellies and empty winters and no grief at all. What is grief but memory with teeth?"

"And what is love?" Elodie says quietly. "Memory with wings."

I squeeze her hand once. Then I let go.

I step forward into the aisle the vines have left for me, and the light comes through the broken ceiling in long gold columns, and I understand suddenly why the map kept redrawing itself: it was never showing me where to go. It was showing me who I was. Every path led here. Every version of the capital, every shifting line of ink, all of it spiraling inward toward this throne, this king, this moment where a fragment walks home to the whole.

"I was made from what you lost," I say to Aldous. "Your childhood. Your summers. The sound of your mother's voice. They took it all when you made your wish, and they folded it small, and they hid it beyond time, and somehow—" my throat tightens— "somehow it learned to want things. It learned to walk. It learned to love a woman who draws maps."

Behind me, Elodie makes a small sound. I do not turn around. If I turn around I will lose my courage, and I have carried this courage across two hundred years of unmade roads. I will not drop it now.

"The guild made me a warning," I say. "But warnings are just truths that arrived early. So here is the truth, spoken freely, given gladly."

I breathe in. The garden breathes with me—all those roses, all those roots, the whole hungry green cathedral of it—and I feel the Writ lean toward me the way a starving animal leans toward warmth.

And I speak my name.

"Aldous Vey."

* * *

It begins as a tearing.

Not of flesh. Of *green*. The Writ screams—a sound like a forest falling in reverse, like ten thousand roots ripped up at once—and the crown of living branches convulses on the king's brow, thorns lengthening, leaves browning at the edges as if autumn has finally been allowed into this endless stolen spring.

Then the flood comes.

Two hundred years pour into me.

I thought I knew what memory was. I thought it was the fragments I carried—the half-remembered sunlight, the lullaby in the emissary's voice, the promise made under a tree whose name I didn't have words for. I was wrong. Those were crumbs. This is the loaf entire, breaking open, and I am drowning in it, and I am *grateful*.

I am six years old, falling out of an apple tree and laughing before I hit the ground because my father is watching and fathers catch you or they don't and either way the falling is yours.

I am nine, learning letters by candlelight, the wax pooling like tiny moons, and my tutor's patience, and the smell of new parchment—which is, I realize with a jolt, the same smell as Elodie's ledger room, and I understand now why her door felt like coming home.

I am twelve, standing in the palace garden making a promise to a gardener's daughter that I will keep even if it kills me, and it nearly does, though not the way I meant.

I am seventeen, crowned too young, the weight of the circlet cold and then warm and then simply *there*, part of me, the way a stone becomes part of a riverbed.

I am twenty-five, and the famine has come, and the fields are gray, and the people are dying in the streets outside my windows, and I would give anything—*anything*—

And I did. That is the terrible arithmetic of it. I stood in this very room and I wished, and the Writ unfurled like a green dawn, and the rains came back and the wheat stood tall and not one more child starved—but the price was paid in names. In birthdays. In the village baker's hands forgetting bread. In mothers looking at their children and feeling only a polite, distant fondness, like acquaintances met at a fair. I ate my kingdom to save it, and then the Writ turned its hunger on me, course by course, year by year, until all that remained of Aldous Vey was a boy's worth of summer, folded small and hidden beyond time.

Hidden in me.

I am all of it now. The boy and the king. The wisher and the wish. Two hundred years of stolen living crash through this small body like a river through a broken dam, and I feel myself stretching, thinning, *unbinding*—because a vessel built for one childhood cannot hold a whole life twice-lived.

My hands are glowing.

I look down and see light moving under my skin like sun through water, and where it pools brightest, I can see the throne room wall behind me. I am becoming transparent. I am becoming the thing I always secretly feared I was: not a boy at all, just a message, and messages end when they are delivered.

"Emrys!" Elodie's voice, cracking straight down the middle. She is running toward me, ledger pages still scattered around her feet where she burned them, ash in her hair, and her face—her face is the worst thing in the world, because it is the face of someone watching the exact future happen that she warned me about, that we both chose anyway.

"It's all right," I tell her, and my voice comes out layered now, a boy's voice with a king's echo underneath it, harmonizing. "Elodie. Look."

She looks. At the throne.

Aldous Vey is waking.

* * *

It happens slowly, then all at once, the way spring comes.

The Writ tears free of him in ribbons—great lengths of flowering vine peeling off his shoulders, his arms, his chest, shrieking that green falling-forest scream, each strand wilting as it separates because Elodie's burned truths are still working in the soil, still poisoning the lie at the root of everything. The false crown crumbles. The last rose closes its petals like a fist relaxing.

And the king draws a breath.

It is the loudest sound I have ever heard, that breath, louder than the Writ's dying, louder than the bells that rang for me at the guild door. Because it is the first breath Aldous Vey has taken as himself in two hundred years, and every lungful of air since the wish belonged to something else.

His eyes clear. Leaf-green fading to a brown like tilled earth, like ordinary human ground where ordinary human things can grow. He looks at his own hands—old hands, far older than they should be, hands that aged while the rest of him slept in green—and he turns them over, and he weeps.

"Emrys," Elodie says again, and now she is beside me, and I realize I have fallen to my knees, and the light is pouring off me in slow golden sheets.

"He's awake," I say. "Look. He's awake, and he remembers, and it worked, it actually—"

The sentence won't finish. There isn't enough of me left to finish sentences. I can see the floor through my own chest now, the mosaic tiles, a pattern of interlocking circles that some dead craftsman laid down four hundred years ago and no one has ever properly appreciated, and I think: *someone should appreciate that. Someone should write it down.*

That's when I laugh.

Because of course. Of course this is how it ends—not in darkness, not in erasure, but in light, in *belonging*. All my life—my strange, borrowed, secondhand life—I wanted one thing I could never name until Elodie taught me the word for it. Not a name. Not a history. A place. A spot on someone's map where the lines converge and say: *you were expected here. You were always coming here.*

I walked longer than I can count. Through rain that hadn't fallen yet. Through doors that existed only while drawn. And every step of it was toward this: a moment where I am the most important person in the world, where two hundred years of a kingdom's grief pass through me and come out clean, where a king wakes because a boy chose truth over survival.

I finally belong somewhere.

Even if only for a moment.

The moment is enough. No—that isn't honest, and honesty is the whole law now, the law Elodie wrote in fire. The moment is not enough. I want more. I want the notebook filled with our journey, I want the lessons in borrowed words, I want to learn what apples taste like with a tongue that's mine instead of a memory of a tongue. I want decades. I want to grow up.

But wanting is not having, and having this—one bright, blazing, chosen instant—is more than I was ever promised.

"Elodie," I say, and the light is so thick now that speaking feels like pushing words through honey. "The book. Don't let it—don't let the story stop. Even if I do."

"Emrys—"

"Promise. Freely given. Like a name."

Her hands close around mine, and hers are solid and warm and real, and mine are barely there, and still she holds on, because she taught me that holding on is a thing people are allowed to do, and I think maybe I taught her too.

"I promise," she says.

The king rises from the throne.

He crosses the garden on unsteady legs, a man learning to walk inside his own body again, and he kneels in front of me—Aldous Vey, King of a kingdom that forgot itself, kneeling in the ashes of his own wish—and he takes my glowing face in his old hands.

"I know you," he whispers. "I don't know how, but I know you. You're—" and his voice breaks, because the memories are settling into him now, returning through whatever thread still connects us, and he sees it, he sees what I am. "You're my summer. You're the years they took. Oh, child. Oh, *child*, what did you pay for me?"

"Everything," I say happily. "It was worth it."

The light takes my legs first. Then my hands, still held in Elodie's—she can't feel them anymore, I can see her feeling nothing, and the grief on her face is a country I want to map, every valley and ridge of it, so no one ever forgets what it cost her to keep her word to me.

"Don't be sad," I tell her. "I'm not ending. I'm—I'm being *delivered*. That's different. Corvane told me once, the night by the fire. A message isn't gone when it arrives. It just stops being separate from the one who receives it."

"That's not good enough," Elodie says fiercely, tears cutting tracks through the ash on her cheeks. "You're not a message. You're a *person*. You're my student. You're—"

"I know." And I smile, and I feel the smile more than anything else now, the last solid thing in a body made of morning. "Say it back to me sometime. In the book. Write that he smiled. Write that he wasn't afraid."

The king holds me as I come apart. That matters. I dissolve into light in someone's arms, in a garden going honestly green for the first time in two centuries, with the smell of rain beginning somewhere far above the broken ceiling—real rain this time, rain that has already fallen, rain that belongs to now.

And in the last instant, as the boundaries of me go soft as watercolor, I feel something impossible.

I feel *held*. Not by the king's arms. By something wider. Threads of warmth reaching toward me from impossibly far away—from a village where a baker suddenly remembers bread, from a guild hall where bells are ringing, from a hundred small moments I touched on the road without knowing I was planting myself in them.

Something is anchoring me.

Something is *refusing* to let me go.

The light should scatter. Light doesn't gather; everyone knows that. But mine is gathering, drawing inward, knitting itself around those far-off threads of remembered kindness, taking a shape—

The king gasps. Elodie's head snaps up.

Where a boy made of light knelt in the ruins of the Writ, something else is forming—smaller than a boy, stranger than a boy, a figure woven from glow and root and every memory anyone ever gave back to me, flickering like a candle deciding whether to become a flame.

And from beyond the shattered doors of the throne room, running through corridors strangled free of vine, come voices I know. The baker. The weaver. The whole nameless village, carrying their returned names like lanterns, calling out into the ruined garden:

*"Emrys! EMRYS!"*

They remember me.

And I am beginning to suspect that might be enough.
