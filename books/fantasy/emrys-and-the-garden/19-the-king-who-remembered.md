## 19. The King Who Remembered

The name left Emrys's mouth like a bell being struck, and the world rang with it.

I felt it in my teeth first. Then in the floor of the garden, which shuddered as though something vast had turned over in its sleep. The Verdant Writ tore free of King Aldous Vey with a sound I will never be able to write down—not a scream, not a tearing, but something like a word being unspoken after two hundred years of being said.

Green light poured out of the boy. It came off him in sheets, in rivers, in threads finer than anything my finest pen could have drawn, and every thread was a year. Two hundred years of stolen childhood, flooding back to the man on the throne. I watched Aldous Vey age backward and forward at once—watched his face fill in like a map being inked, hollows becoming cheeks, fog becoming eyes.

And Emrys began to fade.

"No," I said. It was not a plan or a plea. It was just the only word left in me. "No, no—you hold on, you *hold*—"

He was smiling. That's what broke me. He knelt where he'd stood, still in that ridiculous borrowed coat I'd bought him in the village that forgot its name, and he was smiling up at the light coming off his own hands like a boy watching snow fall for the first time.

"It doesn't hurt," he said. His voice was going thin, going distant, a voice heard through water. "Elodie. It doesn't hurt. I remember everything."

"You're not allowed," I told him, and my voice cracked down the middle. "You're not allowed to go. We made a deal, you and I. You were going to see spring. You were going to learn what rain tastes like when it isn't falling yet. You promised."

"I know." He lifted one hand toward me and the fingers were translucent now, lit from inside. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. But Elodie—the king is waking up. Look. Look at him. It worked."

It had. Behind him, King Aldous Vey rose from the throne of woven green like a man surfacing from deep water. The living crown sloughed off his brow, leaf by leaf, and each leaf fell as a seed. The Writ itself—that beautiful, hungry thing that had eaten a kingdom's memories and then begun eating its king—crumbled from the throne in long ribbons, and the ribbons curled, and the curls contracted, and everywhere they touched the ground they became seeds. Thousands of them. Small, dark, ordinary-looking seeds scattered across the garden floor like the aftermath of some enormous, generous storm.

Aldous Vey stood. He swayed. He looked at his own hands as though confirming they belonged to him, and then he looked at Emrys, and the sound the king made was not a kingly sound at all. It was raw. It was the sound of a man recognizing his own face in a stranger.

"You," the king said. He crossed the garden in three strides and caught Emrys before the boy could tip over, lowering him down among the fallen seeds with a tenderness that no court would ever have believed of him. "I know you. Gods help me, I know you. You were—" His voice failed. He tried again. "There was a garden. Before all of this. There was a boy who was supposed to get to grow up in it."

"That was me," Emrys said. "Sort of. Mostly." He laughed, and the laugh flickered like a candle in wind. "I never got to. So they sent me back instead."

The king bowed his head over the fading boy, this stranger who was also himself, and I understood that there are griefs so strange that no language has been built to hold them. Aldous Vey was mourning a childhood he couldn't remember losing, held in the arms of the child who had lost it for him.

I should have been kneeling beside them. Instead I did the only thing I knew how to do.

I reached into my coat and pulled out the notebook.

*

It was small. Cheap paper, string-bound, bought from the same village baker whose name Emrys had sung back to him. We had filled it together on the eve of the assault, sitting by firelight while Corvane kept watch: every road we'd drawn into existence, every lesson, every joke, every fear. His handwriting had started as careful copies of mine and ended as something entirely his own—slanted, eager, letters leaning forward like they couldn't wait to become words.

His first possession. His first story.

The light coming off him was dimming now, gathering low around his chest, and his eyes were having trouble finding me. But when he saw the notebook, they sharpened.

"You kept it," he whispered.

"Of course I kept it." I sat down in the seeds, not caring about the state of my coat, not caring about anything except getting close enough. "Emrys, listen to me. The Writ took memories by taking them. Stole them, ate them, made them into wishes. That was its whole trick. But this—" I pressed the notebook into his fading hands, and wonder of wonders, his fingers closed around it, "—this was given. Freely. Nobody can take back a gift. Whatever happens to you, this happened. This is yours. And I am going to read you every single page if it's the last thing I do."

"Elodie," the king started, gently, "perhaps if we—"

"Your Majesty," I said, without looking at him, "with respect, you have been asleep for two hundred years. I have been reading aloud since I was six years old. Let me work."

To my lasting surprise, the King of the Realm laughed—a cracked, rusty, astonished laugh—and settled back to listen.

So I opened the notebook, and I gave the boy back his story.

*

"*Entry the first,*" I read. "*In the handwriting of E. Delacroix, cartographer, formerly of the Guild of Impossible Things, currently a criminal.*"

Emrys huffed. Even fading, even dissolving, he managed to look offended on my behalf. "You're not a criminal."

"I broke every law the guild has. I fled with contraband. I lied to Guildmaster Corvane's face, which, I'll have you know, is very difficult; his eyebrows are trained to detect it." I turned the page. "*Entry the second. Today a boy arrived at my door speaking a language older than kingdoms. He was soaked in rain that hadn't fallen yet. He carried a map that moved when I wasn't watching. He looked at me like he'd been walking toward me longer than he could count.*"

"I remember that door," Emrys murmured. "It was blue."

"It was grey."

"It was blue. You wanted it to be blue. I could tell."

I laughed, and the laugh came out wet, and I didn't care. "*Entry the third. He learned his first common word today: 'map.' He pronounced it 'map-uh' and then looked so pleased with himself that I couldn't bear to correct him.*"

"I still say it like that sometimes," he said. "Inside. Where nobody hears."

"*Entry the ninth. He was afraid of the palace today. Not the guards, not the emissary—just the idea of it. He drew it instead of saying it: a house with too many teeth. I told him houses don't have teeth. He looked at me with such pity. He was right, of course. This one did.*"

The light around his chest pulsed, steadied. I kept reading.

I read him the night we fled along roads that existed only because I drew them, the ink still wet under our boots. I read him the village that forgot its name, and the baker, Tomas, who wept when his daughter's face came back to him mid-song. I read him the terrible supper we'd shared with Corvane by firelight, two hundred years of winter laid out plain, and how Emrys had listened without flinching and then asked, quietly, whether the king had liked honey cakes, because he remembered liking them and didn't know why.

"He did," Aldous said softly behind me. "He does. With the burnt edges. My cook used to burn them on purpose because I—" He stopped. Pressed his hand to his mouth. "Because I liked them that way. I remember now. I remember *all of it*, and it's—" His voice shook. "It's unbearable. All those years, gone, and here they are anyway, in a child."

"In a boy," Emrys corrected, with fading dignity. "Nearly a man. Ask me any curse word. I know dozens. Elodie taught me."

"I taught you *scholarly* terms of frustration."

"You wrote them in the notebook. Page thirty-one. Read page thirty-one, Elodie."

I refused, with great professionalism, to read page thirty-one, and both of them laughed—the dying boy and the waking king—and I thought: *let this be the memory. Not the light going out. This. The three of us laughing over a stolen notebook in a garden of seeds.*

But the light kept thinning. I could see the throne room doors through it now, and the vines beyond them, and the vacant-eyed courtiers beginning to stir and murmur as the Writ's grip loosened across the palace. Time was spending itself whether I read fast or slow.

So I read faster.

*

"*Entry the forty-fourth,*" I read, and my voice was steady, mostly, "*he asked me today what I would keep, if I could keep only one thing. I said I'd keep my hands, because a cartographer without her hands is just a woman with opinions. He said that was cheating. He said what he would keep was the day I taught him the word 'home,' because before that, home was just a place you slept, and after, it was a place that knew your name.*"

I stopped.

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere above us, through the broken ceiling, real sky—ordinary sky, unmapped sky—was turning the color of late afternoon.

"Elodie." His voice was almost gone now, more memory than sound. "Read the last page. I wrote it while you were sleeping. You don't know it yet."

My hands were not steady anymore. I turned to the final page, and there it was, in his leaning-forward letters:

*If someone is reading this to me, then it worked, or it didn't, and either way I was here. I was really here. Tell Elodie she didn't lose a boy. She found one, and found ones get to choose, and I chose her. Put that in your next ledger. First entry. Nothing traded, nothing taken, everything told.*

I read it aloud. Of course I read it aloud. My voice broke on the second sentence and I read it anyway, all of it, because he was right—it was a gift, and gifts cannot be taken back, and truth spoken freely is the one currency the Writ never learned to steal.

When I finished, the light around him had gathered into something small and bright and heartbreakingly calm, resting against the king's chest like a lantern about to gutter.

"Thank you," Emrys said. To the king. To me. To the garden, maybe, or the seeds, or the sky. "I got to be somebody. For a little while, I got to be *somebody*. That's more than most fragments get."

"Emrys—" I reached for him.

And the light went out.

*

For a moment there was nothing in the king's arms but an empty coat and a cheap notebook, and the silence of the garden was the worst sound I have ever heard.

Then the seeds moved.

Every seed the Writ had crumbled into turned at once toward the place where the boy had been, the way iron filings turn toward a lodestone, the way travelers turn toward a fire. They quivered. They sank into the soil. And from the spot where Emrys had faded, a single shoot rose—green, insistent, impossibly fast—unfurling leaves, climbing the king's sleeve, reaching toward my hand.

Not the Writ. I knew the Writ's touch, its hunger, its beautiful strangling grace. This was something else. Something that grew *toward* people instead of around them.

"What is it?" Aldous breathed. "What's happening?"

"I don't know." And I, who had built my life on knowing, on recording, on mapping every impossible thing that crossed my path—I said it and meant it and found it didn't frighten me at all. "I don't know, and I think that might be the point."

The vine bloomed. One flower, white, ordinary, smelling of rain that had actually fallen. And beneath it, where the soil had swallowed the seeds, I saw movement—shapes rising in the doorway of the ruined hall, dusty, dazed, calling out names as they remembered them.

Tomas the baker came first, running, his daughter's hand in his, drawn across half the city by something none of them could explain. Then others. The villagers. Strangers. Dozens of them, crowning the broken threshold, all looking not at the king, not at me, but at the small white flower and the vine that held it.

Tomas met my eyes, and he was crying, and he said the thing that I will carry longer than any entry in any ledger:

"We remember him," he said. "All of us. He sang my daughter's name. He walked our roads. Whatever he was—we *remember* him. And things that are remembered aren't finished."

The flower turned, very slightly, toward the sound of his voice. Toward all their voices. Growing toward everyone who carried a piece of him, the way he had grown toward me since the night he knocked on a grey door I would have sworn was blue.

I knelt in the soil among the seeds, holding a notebook that had survived its author, and I understood that the story was not over. Stories that are given freely never end. They just change hands.

I opened the notebook to the first blank page, took out my pen, and began to write:

*Entry the last. Or possibly the first. A boy dissolved into light today, and the light did not leave. It planted itself.*

Behind me, the flower opened another petal—and leaned, unmistakably, toward the sound of my pen.
