## 20. Where the Garden Ends

I am coming apart, and it does not hurt.

That is the strangest part. I had braced for pain the way you brace for a fall—shoulders up, breath held—but there is no falling. There is only a loosening, like fingers uncurling after holding something too long. The light that was flooding into me when I spoke my name has not stopped. It pours through me and takes pieces with it on the way out, and each piece goes somewhere, and I can feel every going.

The king's memories are in me now. Two hundred years of them. A boy's summer with a dog named Bristle. A first snow. A mother's hands braiding hair by a window. A famine, and a wish spoken in desperation, and the terrible green silence after. They are not mine and they are not his anymore; they are passing through me like water through a channel, going back to their owner, and as they go they carry away bits of the channel.

My hands are the first to thin. I watch it happen with a strange detachment—my fingers going translucent at the edges, the garden's roses visible through them, pink ghosts blooming in pink blooms. Elodie is shouting something. The king is holding me. Both of their faces are very close and very frightened, and I want to tell them: don't be frightened, this is what I was made for, this is the whole of the errand. But my mouth is also becoming an idea rather than a fact, so instead I smile, because smiling is simpler than speech and I have always been better at meaning things than saying them.

*This is all right,* I try to say. *This is all right.*

The garden hears me. I think the garden has been hearing me all along.

* * *

Dissolving, it turns out, is not like dying. It is like being read aloud.

Each memory that leaves me takes a piece of me with it, but before it goes, it plays. I feel myself becoming the boy who found a fox den behind the palace stables. I feel myself becoming the young prince who lied to his tutor and felt awful about it for a week. I feel the wish—the wish itself, hot and desperate in a starving throat—and I understand, finally, why Aldous made it, and I forgive him for it, which I suspect he has never managed to do for himself. Forgiveness travels with the memory, back to its source. I am delivering more than I ever knew I carried.

And underneath all of it, threaded through everything, there is Elodie.

Her ledger-hand drawing letters in firelight. Her voice teaching me the word *window*, the word *bread*, the word *friend*. The night she drew me a map of her own face so I would know what laughter looked like before I could make one. Those memories are not the king's. They have nowhere to return to. So they stay, and they anchor me, and I begin to understand that this is how I will not vanish entirely—not by holding on, but by being held.

Then the garden gate opens, and the village comes through it.

* * *

They arrive the way weather arrives—all at once, from everywhere, without any single decision having been made. The baker first, still in her flour-dusted apron, running down the overgrown paths in shoes meant for ovens, not gardens. Behind her the cooper and his wife, the ferryman, the schoolteacher with her ink-stained cuffs, old Tam with his bad knee moving faster than it has moved in twenty years. Forty of them. Sixty. Everyone whose name came back to them when I sang in that nameless village, everyone who woke in the night knowing exactly who they were and, somehow, knowing where they were needed.

Elodie sees them first. I feel her turn—I am in the king's arms, growing lighter by the moment, and she has not let go of my hand even though holding it must be like gripping smoke—and her face does something complicated, grief and hope fighting for the same ground.

"They followed the singing," she says. "Emrys—they followed the singing."

I did not know I was still singing. But I suppose I am. The song I gave the baker, the song that returned her mother's kitchen to her—it never stopped. It has been going out of me this whole time, along with everything else, riding the light like seeds ride wind.

The baker reaches us and drops to her knees in the soil, and she takes my thinning hand in both of hers, and her grip is warm and solid and utterly unafraid.

"You gave me back my name," she says. "Maren. My name is Maren, and my mother taught me to knead dough in a kitchen that smelled of cardamom, and I had forgotten, and you gave it back." She presses my hand against her cheek. "I don't know how. I don't care how. But nobody returns a gift like that and walks away while it's still being unwrapped."

"We remember you," says the ferryman, kneeling on my other side. "All of us. Every one of us remembers the boy who sang."

"That's not—" I try to say, because it isn't true, they barely saw me, I was a stranger in their square for half an hour—but then the memories start arriving, and I stop arguing.

Because they do remember me. Not the way I remembered them, not the deep stolen years of it, but truly. The baker remembers the exact pitch of my voice and how the light bent strangely around me. The schoolteacher remembers that I stood with my feet planted like someone who had been walking a very long time and had finally decided to stop. The cooper's small daughter remembers that I smiled at her, and that it was the best thing that happened to her that week, and she has told the story four times since we left the village. They remember me the way people remember anyone: imperfectly, affectionately, in fragments, in stories.

And those fragments are landing in me like roots finding soil.

* * *

Here is what I understood alone, dissolving in a dead king's garden: that I was made from someone else's childhood, and that everything I called mine was borrowed, and that when the borrowing ended there would be nothing left. That was the arithmetic of it. I had done the sums on the road, lying awake under stars, and the answer was always zero.

But here is what I understand now, with sixty villagers kneeling in the roses and holding on to whatever parts of me they can reach: identity was never a thing you hold alone. It is a thing that is woven. The king's memories made my thread, yes—but Elodie's lessons wove the warp, and Maren's gratitude wove the weft, and the cooper's daughter's story about the smile, and the ferryman's memory of my tired feet, and Tam's gruff declaration that "anyone who sings like that owes me a rematch at draughts"—every strand they hand me now becomes part of the cloth.

I am not vanishing. I am being *made*. For the second time, but properly this time—not carved out of one stolen life, but gathered from many given ones.

It feels like this: imagine you have been a single note, held for two hundred years in a dark room. And then the room opens, and an orchestra sits down around you, and begins to play, and you discover you were never meant to be a note at all. You were meant to be music.

The light stops taking. Or rather—it keeps flowing through me, but nothing leaves anymore, because everything that passes through catches on the weave and stays. The king's memories finish their journey home; I feel him shudder beneath me as two centuries settle back into their rightful place, and his arms tighten around me, and he makes a sound I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe: the sound of a man getting back his own soul and finding it heavier than he remembered and weeping with relief at the weight.

But I remain. Changed. Thinner in some places, I think—when I look at my hands they are solid again but not quite the same hands, the way a river is the same river. And fuller in others, full of sixty strangers' kindnesses and one cartographer's stubborn love and a dead garden slowly remembering how to be alive.

"Emrys." Elodie's face is above mine, wet-eyed, searching. "Emrys, look at me. Are you—are you still—"

"I'm here," I say, and my voice works, and it is my voice, and it is also a little bit everyone's. "I'm still here. I don't know exactly what I am. But I'm here."

She laughs, or sobs, or both. "That," she says, "is the most honest thing anyone has said in this guild-adjacent disaster of a kingdom."

The king lifts his head. His eyes are clear for the first time—clearly, terribly clear, the eyes of a man seeing the ruin of his reign and the miracle in his arms at once. He looks at me the way you look at a mirror that has been covered for decades.

"You're the summer," he says hoarsely. "You're the dog, and the snow, and the— you're everything I paid away." His voice breaks. "What do I call you?"

And that is the question, isn't it. Not *who are you*—that I am learning, stitch by stitch. But *what do we call you*, because names matter in this kingdom, names open doors and bind kings and end famines, and I cannot be Aldous, and I am no longer only Emrys-the-messenger, the fragment, the failsafe.

Maren answers before I can. She is still holding my hand, flour and all, and she says it simply, the way she'd announce bread coming out of the oven:

"He's ours. We'll call him ours until he tells us different."

* * *

We stay in the garden while the sun goes down.

The Writ is dead—or mostly dead; Elodie keeps eyeing a patch of vine near the eastern wall with deep suspicion, and I catch her muttering about ledgers and loose ends, which I have learned means she intends to handle it personally and thoroughly. What remains of it crumbles quietly into seed husks, black and small, and the king orders—his first order as a waking man, delivered in a voice still rough from disuse—that none be burned and none be planted, but kept, in a sealed box, in a room no one visits, so that the kingdom remembers it once wished wrongly and may learn to wish better.

The courtiers wake slowly, blinking in the rose-light, their vacant smiles fading into confusion and then, in most cases, tears. Some of them have been standing in these corridors for years. Some sit down right where they are and refuse to move until someone brings them tea. The palace, it turns out, does not know what to do with a king who remembers, and the king does not much care; he sits on the steps of his living throne with a boy who is not quite a boy beside him, and asks questions, and listens, and weeps twice more before full dark.

At some point Elodie appears at my elbow with her notebook—the small one, the one we filled together on the eve of the assault, my first possession, my first story. She has been reading from it to the villagers, I realize. Telling them the journey. Giving them the whole of me, not just the fragments they caught.

"There's a blank page left," she says, sitting down beside me. "One. I've been saving it."

"For what?"

"For whatever you are now." She holds out the pencil. "I could write it for you. Or you could write it yourself. First entry in your own hand."

I take the pencil. My new-old fingers close around it, steady enough. I think about what to write. I think about the note and the orchestra, about thread and cloth, about a baker's warm grip and a king's returning soul and a woman who burned her ledgers because some truths are worth more than neutrality.

In careful letters, in the language Elodie taught me, I write:

*Emrys. Grown from a king's childhood, rooted in a village's memory, tended by a cartographer. Still becoming.*

Elodie reads it, and reads it again, and then closes the notebook gently, like closing a door on something sleeping.

"Well," she says, and her voice is thick. "That's a start."

Beyond the garden walls, somewhere in the dark city, a bell begins to ring—not the guild's bells, not yet, but soon, soon—and Elodie tilts her head toward the sound with an expression I have learned to read very well indeed. It is the expression of a woman who has just realized the next chapter of the story has already started without her.

"The Guild," she says. "Corvane will have felt the Writ break. Half the hunters in the capital will be converging on this garden by morning, and the other half will be asking awkward questions about a certain missing cartographer." She stands, brushing rose petals from her coat, and offers me her hand. "Come on, then. If we're going to explain what you are, we'd better decide quickly—and I intend to do it with evidence."

She pulls me to my feet. The garden holds me. The village holds me. The story holds me.

And for the first time in two hundred years, something in this kingdom begins—quietly, petal by petal—to grow.
