## 22. Emrys and the Garden

Spring came to the capital the way forgiveness comes—slowly, then all at once, then so thoroughly you could not remember when it had been absent.

Elodie noticed it first in the gutters. For two hundred years, the city's rainwater had run green, tinted by pollen from the Verdant Writ's endless flowering. Now the gutters ran clear, and children splashed in them without their mothers calling them back, and a fishmonger on Copper Lane swore he'd found his grandmother's name again—not written anywhere, just *there*, settled into him like a stone at the bottom of a well.

The palace garden grew ordinary roses.

That was how Elodie thought of it, though she knew *ordinary* was doing quiet work in that sentence. The roses were red and white and one stubborn yellow variety that refused to climb the trellis properly no matter what Emrys did to encourage it. They bloomed and wilted and dropped petals on the gravel paths like any garden in any duchy in the kingdom. They required watering. They attracted aphids. They did not grant wishes, consume memories, or spell out warnings in dead languages along their borders.

They were, in other words, exactly what they appeared to be.

And every morning, before the courtiers woke and the bells rang the hour of bread, a young man with eyes the color of late-afternoon light walked those paths with a watering can, learning how to be someone.

* * *

Emrys had learned that being someone was harder than being a fragment.

A fragment had simple work. A fragment carried a map, delivered a warning, dissolved into light at the appointed hour. A fragment had an ending built into its bones, and endings, he was discovering, were restful things. You walked toward them. You arrived. You were done.

Being someone had no such architecture.

Some mornings he woke and did not know which memories were his. He would lie in the narrow bed the king had given him—the bed Aldous Vey had insisted on, in the east wing, where the morning sun came through windows older than the Writ—and he would hold up each memory like a coin and try to read its face. *Learning to whistle: whose?* The taste of river water and the terror of falling from a rope swing: whose? The sound of a woman singing in a language with no living speakers: his, certainly, but *his* how? His as the boy who had never lived? His as the king who had lived too much? His as whatever new thing had coalesced in the garden two springs ago, rooted in the returned memories of a village full of bakers and weavers and one very stubborn farrier?

He had asked Elodie about it once, in the early weeks, when the question still felt like drowning.

"You're asking the wrong question," she had said. She was sketching the roses—she sketched everything now; her hands seemed unable to stop since the ledgers burned—and she didn't look up. "You keep asking *whose* memory is this. Ask instead: what do I do with it? Whose you are is just the sum of what you choose."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"Then you choose again tomorrow." She had looked up then, and smiled the small dry smile he had learned to read better than any map. "I've been choosing wrong for thirty years, Emrys. It's not fatal. Usually."

He was learning. Slowly, clumsily, the way the yellow rose was learning the trellis.

He learned that he liked rain but not storms—that the first was memory and the second was instinct, and both belonged to him regardless of origin. He learned that he could not abide the smell of lilies, and when he finally traced the aversion to its root he found a funeral, two hundred years old, for a queen whose name the Writ had eaten and whom no one mourned because no one remembered her. He wept for her in the potting shed, alone, and afterward planted lilies anyway, because someone should tend the grave even if the grave had no name.

He learned that Elodie hummed when she drew. That Corvane, visiting from the rebuilt guildhall, pretended to disapprove of everything and approved of nearly all of it. That King Aldous Vey came to the garden most evenings and did not speak, and that the silence between the king and the boy who had been his childhood was not comfortable yet—but was honest, and honesty, Emrys was finding, could ripen into comfort given enough seasons.

And he learned the names of everyone who remembered him. He kept them in the little notebook—his notebook, the first thing he had ever owned, its cover soft now with handling. The baker Marta, who left loaves at the garden gate every third day without a note. The farrier Doss, who had taught him to hold a hammer. The weaver's daughter Sef, who was teaching him dice games he kept losing. Forty-one names now. The village had grown. People came from farther and farther away, having heard—no one could say how, since Elodie's book was unfinished and unprinted—that there was a garden where lost things came back.

He watered the roses. He tied back the yellow climber, gently, knowing it would escape again by week's end. He said good morning to the bees.

It was, he decided, enough. Not the whole of him—he suspected the whole of him was still being woven, thread by thread, by everyone who remembered—but enough for today. Today was the only day anyone ever actually got.

* * *

Elodie wrote in the mornings and the evenings and, increasingly, in the strange golden hours between, when the light through the greenhouse glass made the pages glow as though the book itself were deciding what it wanted to be.

She had tried three times to give it a proper title. *The Ledger of Emrys.* Rejected—it wasn't a ledger; nothing in it balanced. *An Account of the Verdant Writ.* Rejected—the Writ was one chapter, and honestly the least interesting one. *Cartography of Impossible Things.* Rejected with particular violence, because she had spent thirty years believing impossible things should be catalogued rather than understood, and she was done writing books that flattered her old mistakes.

For now the title page read only: *for whoever needs it.*

The book was unlike anything she had made. Guild training sat in her hands like scar tissue—she reached for precision and had to consciously set it down. A ledger recorded what happened. This book had to record what things *meant*, and meaning, she discovered, was slippery as the shifting map had been: look away and it changed. She wrote a scene of Emrys singing to the baker, restoring her name, and the first draft made it sound triumphant. It hadn't been, entirely. It had been terrifying. A child had opened his mouth and undone something ancient, and none of them had known what else might come undone with it. She rewrote it four times until the fear and the hope sat together on the page like two people sharing a bench.

Corvane had read the first hundred pages. He'd come to the garden with the manuscript under his arm and his expression arranged into official gravity, and Elodie had braced for the guildmaster's verdict.

"The guild could never have produced this," he said.

"I know."

"That's what I mean as praise." He set it down on the potting bench, next to the twine and the clay pots. "We recorded. We traded. We told ourselves neutrality was virtue." He paused, and for once the old man's face showed its age—all of it, every year of the two centuries he had overseen the failsafe and the failure both. "You burned the ledgers, Delacroix. I have spent two years rebuilding the guild around that fire. New law, first law: *nothing is traded that cannot be told truthfully.* Do you know how many entries fail that test?"

"How many?"

"Most of them." He almost smiled. "The guild has become mostly an apology bureau. People come to tell truths they've owed for years. We witness. We write them down. We don't trade them." He picked up his hat. "Your book will be the only honest history of the Writ. Write it slowly. Write it true."

So she did.

She wrote about the famine and the wish and the price, plainly, without the guild's careful hedges. *The famine ended,* she wrote, in the chapter that had cost her the most. *The kingdom was the price. This is not a paradox. This is arithmetic, and someone must be willing to say the sum aloud.*

She wrote about the emissary in grey, who had turned out to be a man named Halloway who had sung lullabies to a stolen child and spent two hundred years serving the thing that stole him. Halloway had come to the garden once, in autumn, and stood at the gate for a long time, and Emrys had brought him a rose without a word, and Halloway had taken it and left, and Elodie still did not know what to make of it, so she wrote exactly that: *I do not know what to make of it. Some entries refuse to close. Perhaps that is what honesty looks like.*

She wrote about the gate that opened for a true name freely given, and about the moment Emrys spoke it, and she wrote around the name itself—not hiding it, but honoring it, letting the blank space stand where the boy had chosen to spend himself. Some readers would call that evasion. Elodie called it cartography: you did not draw the sacred places. You drew the roads leading to them, and trusted the traveler.

And she wrote about herself—which was the hardest part, the part no ledger had ever held. She wrote about thirty years of entries that were everyone else's stories. About loneliness dressed up as competence. About the night a knock came that was older than kingdoms, and how she had opened the door, and how opening doors, it turned out, was the whole of the work.

* * *

On the first truly warm day of spring, the king held court in the garden.

This was Aldous's own invention, and the court had hated it and then loved it and now could not imagine otherwise. Instead of the throne room—still scarred, still half-strangled in dead vine-roots that masons worked patiently to clear—the king received petitioners among the roses, standing, no crown on his head. He had melted the old regalia down, all of it, and commissioned from the metal a bell that hung now in the guildhall tower, rung whenever someone came to tell a truth they'd been carrying too long.

Elodie watched from the edge of the garden, manuscript on her knee, as a farmer presented his case—a boundary dispute, mundane as bread—and the king listened with the particular attention of a man who knew precisely what it cost to lose yourself and had sworn, quietly, daily, never to do it again.

"He's good at it," Emrys said, appearing beside her with dirt on his hands and the notebook tucked under one arm. "Listening. Better than he used to be. He told me he used to perform attention. Now he just pays it."

"Do you remember him performing it?" she asked. "From inside?"

"Sometimes." Emrys watched the king laugh at something the farmer's daughter said. "It's strange, having memories of someone from the inside. Like remembering a house you lived in that also lived in you." He shrugged. "But we're working on it. We take walks. He tells me which memories are worth keeping and I tell him which ones hurt and we argue about whether the rope swing was near the mill or past it."

"Who wins?"

"He does. It was past the mill. But I get to keep being annoyed about it, and he says that's fair, since he got to keep the actual childhood."

Elodie laughed, and the farmer's daughter looked over and waved, and for a while they simply stood in the warm air while the bees did their ancient, unhurried work.

Later, when the court had dispersed and the shadows of the trellis stretched long across the gravel, Elodie spread her pages on the potting bench to read the day's work aloud—because that was their ritual now, hers and Emrys's, established the night she had read a dying boy back to himself and never stopped. He settled onto the upturned crate that served as his seat and listened with his whole body, the way he did everything.

She read him the new chapter: spring in the capital, the garden growing ordinary roses, a bell rung for truths, a farmer's boundary dispute. Small things. Human-scale things. The kind of entry no ledger would ever have accepted, because nothing in it could be weighed or traded or priced.

When she finished, Emrys was quiet for a long moment.

"It doesn't end," he said finally. Not a complaint. An observation, made with a cartographer's eye.

"No," Elodie agreed. "It doesn't."

"Ledgers end. Accounts balance or they don't, and then you close the book." He turned the notebook over in his hands—his notebook, forty-one names and counting, the first story he had ever owned. "This just... keeps going."

"That's what makes it a story instead of a record." She gathered her pages, tapping them square against the bench. "Records are about what happened. Stories are about what it meant. And meaning—" she smiled, "—meaning doesn't balance. It grows."

Emrys looked out over the garden then—at the roses, red and white and one gloriously disobedient yellow; at the king walking the far path alone, at peace with his own footsteps; at the gate where tomorrow Marta would leave a loaf and Sef would arrive with dice and some new pilgrim would come seeking a name they'd lost—and something in his face settled, the way soil settles after rain, into the shape it intended to keep.

"Read me the last line again," he said. "The one you just wrote."

Elodie found the page. She read it aloud, into the gold-and-green evening, planting it where it could grow:

*"The famine ended. The kingdom paid. The boy chose. And in a garden where a wish had eaten a world, a woman who once recorded other people's stories finally wrote down her own—and found, to her astonishment, that it was not hers alone. It never had been. It belonged to everyone who remembered, and everyone who remembered kept adding to it, and the book, like the garden, like the boy, like the truth itself, went on growing."*

She closed the manuscript.

Somewhere across the city, faint and clear, the bell in the guildhall tower began to ring—someone, somewhere, coming at last to tell the truth they'd been carrying. Emrys tilted his head toward the sound, listening, and Elodie saw him smile, and she took up her pen.

There was room on the next page. There always would be.
