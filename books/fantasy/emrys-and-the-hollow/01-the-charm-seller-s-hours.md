## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

The bell above the door of Dunmore's Charms & Curios had a crack in it, so instead of ringing it made a sound like a small bird being mildly inconvenienced. Rosalind Dunmore had never fixed it. A cracked bell told him things a whole one wouldn't — whether the customer was in a hurry (the bird-sound came fast), whether they were browsing (slow, apologetic), or whether they were trouble (it didn't ring at all, because trouble held the door).

Tonight the bird was inconvenienced at half past nine, which meant someone wanted something badly enough to knock on a charm-shop door after closing.

"We're shut," Rosalind called from behind the counter, not looking up from his ledger. He was doing sums that would not balance, mostly because he kept undercharging old Mrs. Tallow for her arthritis charms and overcharging nobody at all, which was no way to run a business and exactly how he ran his.

The door opened anyway. The bell made its complaint. And a young woman stepped inside with rain on her shoulders and a look on her face that Rosalind knew the way you know your own handwriting — the look of someone who has been promised by every sign in the city that magic is real, and has come to collect.

"Sign says *open*," she said.

"The sign says *open most days*. It's a very honest sign. Look closer."

She looked closer. She did not leave. She was perhaps twenty, with the kind of tiredness around her eyes that doesn't come from one bad night but from a season of them, and her hands were wrapped around something in her pocket like it might escape.

Rosalind set down his pen. "You want a luck charm. Or a love charm. Or a charm against nightmares, which I'll tell you now is my best seller and also my biggest lie, because sleep is a country I've never successfully charmed anyone into."

"I want the real thing."

Ah. There it was. He'd known it was coming since the bell — since before the bell, honestly, since the sky had gone strange last week and the criers had started saying words like *border* and *collapse* in voices carefully sanded smooth of feeling.

"Madam," said Rosalind, spreading his hands in the manner of a man presenting an empty tray, "you are standing in a shop full of dried lavender, river stones, and optimism. The love charms are rose petals and good intentions. The luck charms are copper and confidence. The nightmare charms—" he tapped the little blue sachets hanging by the window, "—are chamomile, and I stand by them, but only because chamomile works and I'm honest about why."

"You're lying," the girl said. Not accusingly. Almost hopefully. As if she wanted to be proven wrong, wanted him to drop the act, wanted the world to be the shape she needed it to be tonight.

And here was the thing about ten years of hiding in plain sight: it taught you to lie with your whole body. Your shoulders learned it. Your breathing learned it. Rosalind Dunmore could hold a lie the way other men held a note in church — steady, sustained, until the congregation looked away.

"Sit down," he said instead, because her knees were doing something untrustworthy. "I'll make tea. It's chamomile too. Everything in this shop is chamomile if you hit it hard enough."

* * *

Her name was Sera, and her brother was a fisherman, and three nights ago he had walked into the water at Grayford and not come out — except that he *had* come out, an hour later, walking wrong, talking wrong, saying there was a hole in the dark out past the buoys and the hole had said his name. The river men were calling it fever. The wardens were calling it nothing at all, and confiscating anyone who asked twice.

"The stories say there used to be a man in Corvale who did true workings," Sera said, holding the tea without drinking it. "Before the ban. That some of them stayed on. Hidden. That if you found the right shop—"

"The right shop sells chamomile."

"Please." Her voice cracked on it, and the crack went through Rosalind like a hairline fracture through good porcelain — invisible, structural, permanent. "I don't need much. I just need to know he's still in there somewhere. My brother. Behind whatever took him. I need someone to *look*, and everyone with eyes is either dead or arrested or pretending they were born blind."

Rosalind looked at his tea. In its surface, faint and trembling, he could see the lamp overhead — and beneath the lamp, if he let his focus slip the way he never let it slip in company, the fainter light of the wards he'd have to check tonight. His nightly rounds. His second shift. The job that did not exist, performed by a man who did not exist, for a Gate that officially did not exist either. Corvale was a city built on three layers of pretending, and Rosalind was the only maintenance staff.

He could look. He had the gift; he'd had it since before he had a name worth keeping. One touch of true sight and he could tell her whether her brother's soul was still moored to his body, whether there was anything left to call home to.

He could also, with one careless working, light up the city's detection wards like a festival lantern, and be unmade by morning, and leave the Hollow Gate — the actual, load-bearing, world-edge Gate — untended in the dark.

"I can't help you," he said.

Sera set down the cup. Her hands had gone very still, the way hands do when they're deciding whether to become fists. "You won't."

"There's no difference that matters."

"There's every difference," she said, standing, "and I hope someday somebody makes you feel it." She put two coppers on the counter — payment for nothing, dignity preserved — and paused at the door. "They say the stars are going out past the border. Did you know that? My brother said it first. Before the water." The bell complained her out into the rain.

Rosalind sat alone in his shop for a long time after that, listening to the rain and the tick of the cooling stove, and did not move until the coppers on the counter seemed to accuse him less than the empty chair across from it did.

Then he closed the ledger, banked the lamp, and got ready for work.

* * *

The honest part of Corvale slept by eleven. The dishonest part woke around midnight, which suited Rosalind fine, because the dishonest parts of any city are where a man can walk without being seen. He took the back way off Tanner's Row, through the gap between the tannery and the wall where the ivy had eaten a doorway-shaped hole decades ago, and dropped into the old drainage tunnels that smelled of river and rust and, faintly, of deep places that had no business smelling of anything in a city this far inland.

He had done these rounds every night for ten years. Three thousand-odd nights of checking the anchor-points: the iron nail driven into the tunnel wall that hummed when touched; the mirror-sized slab of black glass behind a false brick in the aqueduct, warm as a sleeping animal; the well shaft in the abandoned chapel district where you leaned over the edge and listened, and prayed — in the secular, embarrassed way of modern men — that you heard nothing back.

Ten years ago there had been six anchors. Now there were four. Nobody had decommissioned the other two. They had simply stopped existing, along with the streets above them, along with the people who'd lived on those streets, along with — Rosalind suspected, though he'd stopped letting himself finish the thought — the memory of them in anyone's head but his.

He touched the nail. It hummed, thin and reedy, a tuning fork a half-step flat. Weaker than last month. Weaker than last week.

"That's not encouraging," he told it. The nail did not reply, which was normal, and somehow felt pointed tonight.

At the black glass he pressed his palm flat and closed his eyes and let himself, briefly, be what he actually was: not a charm-seller, not a coward, but a keeper — the last of a line of keepers, if a line can be said to continue when it consists of one frightened man with a shop full of chamomile. Through the glass he felt the far side of the country, the border range where the great wards stood in their ancient rows like teeth. Like teeth, he thought, is exactly right, because tonight they felt loose.

He counted them the way you count your own heartbeat, by feel rather than number. Forty-one. Forty-one, forty—

Forty.

He opened his eyes. Counted again. Forty.

A ward did not fail quietly. A ward failing was supposed to be a cathedral-bell event, a tearing of the sky, a thing that woke wardens in their beds three countries away. This one hadn't failed. It had simply *stopped being there*, the way Sera's brother had stopped being in the water, the way villages stopped being on maps. No sound. No shudder. Just one fewer tooth in the jaw of the world, and the dark pressing in a little closer to fill the space.

Rosalind stood in the dripping tunnel with his hand on warm black glass and understood, with the calm that arrives precisely when panic would be most useful, that the decade was over. Whatever this was — slow rot, or hunger, or something patient finally done waiting — it had begun while he sold rose petals to lovers and lied kindly to grieving sisters. It had begun on his watch, and his watch was the only one there was.

He finished the rounds. He always finished the rounds. At the well shaft he leaned over the edge as he had three thousand times before, and listened.

For ten years: nothing. Water sounds. Drip, echo, stone.

Tonight, from very far below — below the water, below the stone, below, he suspected, below — something breathed. Not wind. Breath. The long, tidal, unhurried breath of a thing that had been asleep and was now, without hurry, without malice, waking up. And underneath the breath, so faint he could pretend afterward that he'd imagined it, a sound like a voice trying out a word it hadn't spoken in centuries.

It sounded almost like his name.

Rosalind straightened, wiped his palms on his coat, and began the walk home through the dishonest half of the city, moving quickly, telling himself the usual lies in the usual order: *I have time. It's slow. I've handled worse.* He was halfway up Tanner's Row, passing his own darkened shop window with its honest sign and its hanging sachets of chamomile, when he noticed the sky.

Over Corvale, the clouds had broken. The stars were out — most of them. The familiar constellations, the ones sailors steered by and children wished on, burned as they always had.

But along the northern horizon, low over the border hills, there was a stretch of sky where stars should have been and weren't. Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Absent* — a clean black absence in the pattern, starless in the specific shape of something enormous taking a bite.

Rosalind Dunmore stood alone in the street, invisible by choice for ten years, and watched the dark spread by a single star's width while he stood there.

"Well," he said quietly, to no one, to the night, to the thing breathing under the world. "That's going to be a problem."

Behind him, in the locked and shuttered shop, the cracked bell gave one soft, unbidden, bird-like complaint — as if something had just come through the door that wasn't a customer at all.
