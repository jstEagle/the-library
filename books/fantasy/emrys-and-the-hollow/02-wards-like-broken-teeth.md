## 2. Wards Like Broken Teeth

Rosalind Dunmore had a rule about midnight rounds: never bring anything you couldn't drop and run from.

It was a rule he'd made for himself ten years ago, on the first night, when his hands had still shaken and his pockets had still held the ashes of everything he'd owned. He'd broken it exactly once since—carrying a teacup, of all things, a chipped blue thing he'd found in a ditch that reminded him of nothing at all and therefore of everything—and he'd spent three weeks afterward jumping at shadows, convinced the cup had marked him.

Tonight he carried nothing but his keys, his chalk, and a small tin of mending-salve that smelled of pine and old pennies. He locked the shop behind him with three locks, only one of which was actually a lock. The other two were charms of his own devising: one that made the door forget it was interesting, and one that gave a mild, apologetic jolt to anyone who touched the handle without honest purpose. He'd calibrated the jolt carefully over the years. Enough to discourage. Not enough to make the Warden's Office curious about why a charm-seller's door bit people.

The streets of Corvale at midnight were his favorite version of the city. By day, Corvale was all noise and negotiation—fishwives haggling, carters swearing, the criers shouting the news as though volume could improve it. But at midnight the city exhaled. Lamplighters had done their rounds hours ago; now the lamps burned low and orange, pooling on cobblestones like spilled honey. A cat regarded him from a windowsill with the particular disdain cats reserved for men who walked with purpose after dark.

"Evening, Duchess," Rosalind said. The cat looked away. Ten years of this exchange, and she'd never once acknowledged him. He respected that enormously.

His rounds took him east along Tanner's Row, where the tanneries slept off their stink, then up through the Weft—the weavers' quarter, all narrow lanes and leaning houses that gossiped against each other's shoulders—and finally out through the Salt Gate, where the city's boundary stones began.

There were forty-one boundary stones between Corvale and the open country. Most people thought they were just stones. Old surveyor's markers, maybe, or relics of some forgotten king's vanity. Rosalind knew better. Each one held a thread of the border wards—the great invisible lattice that ran the length of the country's edge, woven by hands long dead and maintained, these last ten years, by hands very much alive and extremely tired.

His hands, specifically.

He knelt at the first stone and pressed his palm flat against its mossy crown. The ward-thread answered him the way an old dog answers its name: a slow, familiar warmth, a pulse of recognition. *Still here,* it said. *Still holding.*

"Good lad," Rosalind murmured, and drew a thin line of chalk along the stone's base—a maintenance sigil, harmless-looking, the kind of mark a superstitious man might leave for luck. Then he moved to the second stone, and the third, working his way along the dark road with the unhurried rhythm of a decade's practice.

This was the work no one paid him for. By day he sold charms in his little shop on Candlemaker's Lane—love-luck knots that did nothing but look pretty, traveler's tokens that kept feet dry through honest physics, birth-blessings carved from river wood because river wood was cheap and mothers liked the grain. He charged fair prices and told gentle lies: *this one brings sweet dreams, this one keeps arguments small.* None of it was magic. That was the point. In a country where magic was outlawed—where the Wardens came with their iron pokers and their ledgers and their terrible, tidy efficiency—a charm-seller who sold actual charms would last about as long as snow in summer.

So Rosalind sold nothing, and mended everything, and told himself every night that invisibility was a kind of heroism too, though most mornings the telling rang a little hollow.

The seventh stone was cold.

Not cool. Not night-chilled. *Cold*, the way a hearth is cold the morning after the fire dies—cold with absence, cold with something *gone*. Rosalind pressed his palm harder against it and felt the ward-thread beneath like a snapped harp string, still humming faintly with its last note.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, no."

He moved faster. Eighth stone: intact, warm, confused by his urgency. Ninth: intact. Tenth: intact. Eleventh—

Cold. Thread gone. And this time there was something else, something that made the hair on his arms stand up and take notice: a smell. Faint, wrong, like the air after lightning but *underneath* somehow, like lightning's shadow. The Hollow-breath. He hadn't smelled it in ten years, and his body remembered it the way a body remembers falling.

He ran.

* * *

The stones came quicker after that, and so did the failures. Thirteenth, cold. Fourteenth, cold. Fifteenth—he skidded to his knees beside it, palms flat, pouring what little he could spare into the thread, trying to knot the fraying ends back together the way you'd mend a net. For a moment it caught. He felt the ward take hold again, fragile as spun sugar, and he allowed himself one breath of relief before the thread dissolved between his fingers like smoke deciding it had better places to be.

"I'm sorry," he told the stone. It seemed important to say it aloud. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry."

He'd been saying that a lot lately, he realized. To stones. To customers he turned away. To a chipped blue teacup on a shelf at home that he still couldn't bring himself to throw away. An entire decade of apologies, delivered quietly, to things that couldn't hear.

The sixteenth stone stood at the crest of Gallows Hill—not named for executions, whatever the tourists said, but for an ancient gallows-oak whose roots the stone nestled against like a child against a sleeping parent. From here, on clear nights, you could see the whole curve of the world: the dark fields rolling south, the distant smudge of the Thornwood, and above it all the sky, generous and glittering, more stars than any city deserved.

Rosalind crested the hill, chest burning, and looked up to get his bearings by the Shepherd's Chain—the bright arc of stars that hung over the southern border like a promise.

The Shepherd's Chain was going out.

Not dimming. Not clouding over. Going *out*, star by star, in sequence, west to east, like candles being snuffed by a careful hand. Alcor first, then Mizar, then the little trio the farmers called the Three Sisters, each one winking from brilliance to blackness with a finality that made his stomach drop through the hill and keep dropping. There was no cloud. There was no smoke. The stars were simply *leaving*, and the sky behind them wasn't empty the way night is empty—it was empty the way a room is empty after the furniture's been carried out. Absent. Unfurnished. Wrong.

"No," Rosalind whispered. "No, no, no. You're supposed to have years. I *built* you years."

But even as he said it, he knew. He'd known for months, if he was honest—if honesty counted for anything at three in the morning on a hilltop while the sky dismantled itself. The threads had been thinning all autumn. The mending-salve had needed doubling, then tripling. Last week, the fourth stone had hummed a note he didn't recognize, low and hungry, and he'd told himself it was his imagination, because the alternative was admitting that the decade of hiding was over, and he had gotten *so good* at hiding. He'd made an art of it. He'd made a *life* of it.

The stars went out overhead like snuffed candles, and Rosalind Dunmore stood alone on Gallows Hill and felt ten years of careful, patient, painstaking invisibility crumble around him like wet paper.

The Hollow Gate was four miles south. He ran anyway.

* * *

The road south passed through country that remembered being loved. Orchards, mostly—old ones, gnarled and half-wild, planted by someone's grandmother's grandmother—and stone walls that wandered the hillsides with the cheerful aimlessness of things built by hand rather than plan. In daylight it was pretty. At night, with half the sky gone dark, it was a landscape holding its breath.

Rosalind ran with the particular economy of a man who has done this before and hated it then too. His lungs complained. His knees filed formal grievances. Somewhere around the second mile, a fox fell into step beside him for a hundred yards, trotting along the wall-top, watching him with bright professional interest, before concluding he was not worth the trouble and peeling away into the dark.

"Wise," Rosalind wheezed at it. "Very wise. Follow your instincts."

The trouble with running toward the thing you dread, he reflected, is that it gives you plenty of time to think. And thinking, for Rosalind, meant counting. How many stones had failed? Sixteen of forty-one on this stretch alone, and this was the *near* stretch. The far border, the deep-country line along the Thornwood's edge—that would be worse, or already gone. How long since the last full survey? Nine days. Nine days ago everything had been thin but holding, and now the sky was being put out like a lamp. Whatever was happening at the Gate had stopped being gradual. Gradual had ended. They were in the steep part of the curve now, the part where the healers go quiet and start holding your hand.

And underneath the counting, the other thought, the one he'd been stepping around for ten years the way you step around a loose floorboard:

*I sealed it once. I know what it costs. And it's awake, and it's hungry, and this time I'm not sure sealing is the answer.*

He pushed the thought down. There'd be time for philosophy later, assuming there was a later, which was looking increasingly negotiable.

The orchards ended. The land opened into moorland, tussocky and black, and ahead—visible now even without stars, because it made its own absence of light—the hollow.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing nobody understood about the Hollow, the thing the old stories got wrong with their fire and their screaming. It was just a place where the land dipped, gently, naturally, the way land does—a shallow bowl of moor perhaps half a mile across, ringed with grass that grew a little too green and flowers that bloomed a little out of season. Sheep avoided it. Birds flew around it. Children raised nearby were taught, without ever being told why, not to play there.

At its center stood the Gate.

To look at, it was almost nothing: two standing stones and a lintel, weathered grey, lichened, unremarkable—the kind of ruin farmers lean fences against. Rosalind had spent ten years maintaining it and had never once been able to make it look like anything other than a doorway to a sheep field. The wards lived underneath, in the soil and the stone and the space between, a weaving so dense that the air inside the doorway itself bent slightly, the way light bends over a hot road.

Rosalind slowed to a walk at the lip of the hollow, because running into it felt like running into a mouth.

The silence hit him first. Moorland at night should whisper—wind, grass, the small busy sounds of things eating other things. Here there was nothing. The quiet had texture. It pressed against his ears like deep water.

Then he heard it. Or felt it. The distinction had never mattered much with the Gate.

It was humming.

Low, vast, patient—a note below hearing that arrived instead through his teeth, his breastbone, the soles of his boots. Ten years ago, the Gate's hum had been the sound of a held breath, a drawn bowstring, a wound bound tight. This was different. This was the sound of something waking up and finding itself *hungry*. It rose and fell in slow swells, and each swell left him feeling slightly smaller, slightly less solid, as though the hum were tasting the edges of him and finding them—so far—uninteresting.

"Not tonight," Rosalind said aloud, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the only kind of brave he'd ever been any good at. "I know you're awake. I know you can hear me. We had an arrangement."

The hum swelled. The grass at the hollow's rim shivered though there was no wind.

He descended. Step by step, the green growing lusher underfoot, the flowers thicker—vetch and yarrow blooming together in November, drunk on something that wasn't sun. Halfway down, he noticed the dew on the grass was rising. Not evaporating. *Rising*, beading upward off the blades in tiny silver spheres that drifted a handspan above the ground and hung there, trembling, catching light that had no source.

The Gate stood at the center, and the space between its stones was wrong.

Ten years ago, the doorway had shown the moor beyond—a shimmer, a bend, but the moor nonetheless. Now the space inside the frame was simply *dark*. Not shadowed. Dark the way the sky was dark where the stars had gone out. Furnished-with-nothing dark. And as Rosalind approached, the darkness leaned toward him, gently, the way a sleeping animal leans toward warmth.

He stopped six feet away. Close enough to feel it. Close enough for the hum to climb into his ribs and set up residence.

"I mended you," he said softly. "For ten years. Every week. Do you know how many nights? Four hundred and—" he did the arithmetic automatically, uselessly, "—five hundred and twenty. Give or take. I gave you everything I had left, and you *held*, and I got to be nobody in a little shop, and that was the bargain. That was the whole bargain."

The dark in the doorway rippled. And in the ripple—for half a heartbeat—he saw faces. Not clearly. Impressions. A woman laughing with her head tipped back. A boy running through wheat. Hands, many hands, reaching not to grab but the way hands reach in dreams, open, wanting. Wanting *in*, or wanting *out*, or wanting only to be seen—it was impossible to tell, and the not-telling was the worst part.

Rosalind's knees nearly went. He locked them. He had learned, ten years ago, standing in this exact spot, that grief and terror feel identical in the body, and that the only way through is to pick one and call it duty.

"You stay shut," he told the Gate, and his voice cracked on the second word, ruining the speech entirely. "You hear me? Whatever's changed, whatever's pulling at you—you stay *shut*."

The hum deepened. The dew-spheres rose higher. And far to the north, back the way he'd come, a light bloomed over Corvale—not firelight, not lamplight, but a cold white flare that climbed the underside of the clouds like a searchlight sweeping for something specific.

Something specific like him.

Rosalind stood alone in the hungry dark with the Gate humming its waking song, and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives only at the absolute worst moments of a life, that the decade of hiding was over—and that somewhere behind him, in a city that was about to become a very dangerous place to own his face, the hunt had already begun.
