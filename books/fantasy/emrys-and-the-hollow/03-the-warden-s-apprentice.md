## 3. The Warden's Apprentice

Ivet Kess had been a Warden's apprentice for six years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things she was certain of: that the Wardens' oath was the truest thing in Corvale, that her mentor Serah Vane was the finest woman alive, and that magic was a rot you cut out before it spread.

She had believed all three things the way you believe in the sunrise—without effort, without examination, without ever once standing outside on a cold morning to ask whether the sun might simply be a very large lamp someone else was holding.

The morning it all came apart began, as most terrible mornings do, with porridge.

"You're eating like a soldier," Serah said, watching Ivet shovel her breakfast at the garrison table. "Slow down. The purge won't start without you."

Ivet swallowed. "Is it starting today? Truly?"

Serah was fifty-three and built like a door, gray-streaked hair cropped short, hands scarred from twenty years of confiscating things that bit back. She had taught Ivet everything: how to detect a charm's residue by smell (burnt sugar and river water, usually), how to break a warding circle without losing your eyebrows, how to be kind to the people you arrested because kindness cost nothing and fear cost everyone.

"The orders came at dawn," Serah said. "Registered mages first. Names on the ledger, houses on the list." She turned her cup slowly on the table, around and around, which she only did when something was bothering her. "It'll be orderly. It'll be lawful. That's what matters."

"Right," said Ivet, who had spent the last two nights dreaming of orderly, lawful things and waking up with her heart hammering anyway. "Orderly."

Outside the garrison windows, Corvale was going about its business. Bakers' chimneys smoking, cart wheels rattling over cobbles, the fishmongers shouting their prices in the market square as though the stars hadn't gone out four nights ago, as though the sky were still full of anything but black.

* * *

The first house on the list belonged to Maren Ostler, who lived two streets from Ivet's own lodging and sold pickled vegetables at the Saturday market, and who had once given Ivet a free jar of onions because Ivet looked "like a girl who needs feeding."

Maren was also, according to the ledger, a registered hedge-witch of the third circle, which meant she could coax a bruise to fade faster and keep milk from souring in summer. Ivet knew this the way everyone in the neighborhood knew it, the way everyone knows which neighbor talks to bees or reads tea leaves—the way you know a thing and file it under *harmless* and never think about it again.

Except now the country had thought about it again, all at once, and filed it under *treason*.

Serah knocked first. She always knocked first. That was one of the rules: *We are not thieves, we are not cowards, we announce ourselves.* Maren opened the door in her apron, took one look at the Warden gray, and went white as flour.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, Serah. It's you."

"I'm sorry, Maren." And she did sound sorry. That was the worst part, later—Ivet would think about this for weeks, months, years maybe—that Serah sounded genuinely, warmly sorry, and did it anyway. "You know how this goes. The books, please. All of them."

"They're just—" Maren's hands twisted in her apron. "They're recipes, mostly. My mother's. Remedies. There's nothing in them about gates or stars or any of that."

"I know," Serah said gently. "That's not what they're for anymore. You understand. Everything registered gets collected. Everyone gets processed."

*Processed.* Ivet wrote the word down in her head and examined it. It was an ugly little word, smooth as a pebble, designed not to mean anything. Processed like grain. Like ore. Like meat.

They went inside. Maren's house smelled of dill and vinegar and woodsmoke, and there was a cat on the windowsill who watched the whole proceeding with the flat disapproval of cats everywhere. The spellbooks were in a cedar chest under the bed—six of them, leather-bound, corners worn soft from handling. Ivet carried them out to the cart herself, because Serah told her to, and because carrying them felt marginally better than watching someone else carry them.

Then Serah built the fire right there in the street, in an iron basin brought for the purpose, because the new orders said burnings were to be public. *Visibility deters,* the order read. Ivet stood holding the second armful of books while the first curled and blackened, and the pages lifted on the heat and tore away upward, flapping like small desperate birds trying to reach a sky that no longer had stars in it.

A crowd gathered. Crowds always gather for fires; it's the oldest law there is. Nobody spoke. An old man near the front crossed himself in the old way, left to right, which hadn't been done in Corvale since Ivet's grandmother's day. A boy of maybe eight asked his mother loudly what was burning and was shushed so hard he nearly fell over.

And Maren Ostler stood in her doorway with her arms wrapped around herself, watching her mother's handwriting go up in smoke, and she did not cry, and somehow that was worse than crying.

"There," Serah said quietly, when the last book was ash. "It's done, Maren. You'll come to the garrison tomorrow for processing, and after that—" She stopped. Even she couldn't finish the sentence, because nobody had actually explained what happened *after*, only that there would be an after, and that it would be orderly.

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, in a voice like a closing door:

"You used to eat supper at my table, Serah Vane. When your husband died. Three winters running."

"I remember," Serah said.

"Yes," said Maren. "Well."

She went inside and shut the door, and the cat stayed on the windowsill, and Ivet stood in the street holding an empty basket that smelled of cedar and smoke, and felt something inside her chest shift—not break, nothing so clean as breaking, but *shift*, the way a foundation shifts, so that every door in the house hangs crooked afterward.

* * *

That afternoon they processed eleven more names. By the seventh, Ivet had stopped tasting her lunch. By the eleventh—a nervous young clerk named Tam Wicker whose crime, as far as anyone could determine, was owning a book about weather charms that he'd bought as a joke—she had stopped speaking entirely, and Serah noticed.

"You're quiet," Serah said, as they walked back through streets gone strange and early-dark, because without stars the evenings came on like a lid closing.

"It's a lot of books," Ivet said. "A lot of burning."

"It's necessary."

"Is it?" The question escaped before Ivet could catch it, small and sharp as a splinter. She kept walking. "The stars went out. Something happened at the borders. But Maren Ostler keeps milk sweet. Tam Wicker thinks thunder-charms are funny. How does burning their books bring the stars back?"

Serah stopped walking. For a moment Ivet thought she'd finally done it—crossed the line, earned the rebuke—and part of her braced for it, and another part, a newer part she didn't recognize, almost wanted it. Wanted the argument. Wanted Serah to say something that would make the shifting foundation settle back into place.

But Serah just looked up at the starless sky for a long moment, and when she spoke, her voice was lower than usual.

"When I was your age," she said, "the Wardens weren't hunters. We were engineers. We maintained the border wards, we trained registered mages in safe practice, we kept the peace between the gifted and everyone else. It wasn't perfect, but it was a *system*. Then the wards failed, and the stars went out, and the Council needed someone to blame before the panic ate the country. So now we're hunters." She started walking again. "The system is broken, Ivet. We're what's left of it. Do the work in front of you. Don't look past it. That's how you survive."

"That's how you survive," Ivet repeated. "Not how anyone else does."

Serah didn't answer. In the dark, Ivet couldn't see her face, but she saw her mentor's hand rise briefly toward her own chest—an old gesture, half a prayer, half a check that the heart was still where she'd left it—and fall again.

* * *

Ivet did not sleep that night. She lay in her narrow cot in the apprentices' dormitory listening to the building settle and thinking about Maren's cat, which was absurd, but the cat's face kept arriving in her mind with its expression of flat, unimpeachable judgment, as if to say: *Well? Are you going to do something, or just lie there?*

Around midnight she got up. This was against approximately nine regulations, and she catalogued each one as she broke it, which was a habit of hers—knowing exactly which rules she was breaking made her feel less like a criminal and more like a scholar of crime, which was somehow more comfortable.

The records room was on the ground floor, guarded by a lock older than the garrison and a guard younger than Ivet, both of whom yielded without much fuss. The ledger lived in an iron cabinet marked **REGISTERED PERSONS — CORVALE DISTRICT**, and Ivet had fetched it a hundred times for Serah without ever reading past the columns she needed.

Tonight she read all of it.

Names, addresses, circles of registration, notes. Page after page after page. A candlemaker who charmed his wicks to burn brighter. A midwife, flagged twice, *cooperative*. A ferryman. A schoolteacher. Whole families, some of them, registered across three generations like a curse passed down in reverse.

And then, near the middle, a name that stopped her breath:

**DUNMORE, ROSALIND.** Charm-seller, Lantern Row. Registered 11 years prior. Circle: indeterminate. Note, in faded ink: *Assessment incomplete. Subject cooperative but evasive. Recommend continued observation.*

There was something else, too—a marginal mark, a small symbol Ivet didn't recognize, drawn beside his name in different ink, older ink. She stared at it. It looked almost like a keyhole. Or an eye.

*Rosalind Dunmore.* She knew the shop. Everyone on Lantern Row knew the shop—the little place with the painted sign, selling luck-knots and traveler's charms and bottles of "genuine moonlight" that were, everyone agreed, definitely just oil and glitter. She'd bought a charm from him once, a cheap brass thing against nightmares, and it had worked, which she'd assumed was coincidence, and then she'd assumed was placebo, and then she'd stopped assuming because assuming was uncomfortable.

He was on the list. He'd be processed. His books burned, his shop confiscated, whatever indeterminate circle he belonged to fed into the machine that had no *after*.

Ivet stood in the cold records room holding the ledger, and the shifting thing in her chest shifted again, further, and this time she heard it clearly for what it was: not doubt. Doubt was soft. This was harder. This was the sound of a decision being born.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: the Wardens had a list of every mage in the city, and the Hollow—or whatever had eaten the stars—was out there taking whole towns off the map, and the Council's answer was to feed the list into the fire one name at a time and call it order. Either the Wardens were fools, or they were something worse, and Ivet had loved them too long and too well to let them be fools without checking.

She copied Rosalind Dunmore's page. Then, because her hands seemed to have opinions of their own now, she copied the next page, and the next, working fast, the pen scratching in the dark like a small animal. When dawn grayed the window she had forty names in her notebook and ink on her fingers and no plan whatsoever beyond the first step, which was: *not this.*

She put the ledger back. She closed the cabinet. On her way out she paused at the records-room door and allowed herself one moment—one long, honest moment—of grief for the girl who had walked in here a hundred times believing the cabinet held a list of dangerous people, instead of a list of neighbors.

Then she went upstairs, packed a bag, and left a note on Serah's desk that said only: *Gone to do the work in front of me. —I.*

It was cruel, maybe. She'd decided she could live with cruel.

* * *

Lantern Row at dawn was the one street in Corvale that refused to believe anything was wrong. The shops were shuttered but the lanterns—real ones, oil and wick, defiantly ordinary—still hung lit over every door, because the Lantern Row guild charter, written two hundred years ago, required it, and the guild had voted unanimously that a charter was a charter regardless of astronomical circumstances.

The charm shop sat between a cobbler and a tea merchant, its painted sign swinging gently: **R. DUNMORE, CHARMSELLER — HONEST GOODS, FAIR PRICES**. The honesty of the goods was much disputed. The fairness of the prices was not.

Ivet stood across the street for a quarter hour rehearsing openings. *Good morning, I've come to warn you* — too abrupt. *You don't know me, but I have your death warrant in my pocket* — accurate but alarming. She was on her fourth draft, something involving the word "hypothetically," when the shop door opened and a man stepped out to sweep his step, broom in hand, humming.

He was younger than she expected, or perhaps just worn in a way that made age unreadable—dark-haired, narrow-faced, with the particular stillness of someone who has spent years practicing not being noticed. He looked up, saw a stranger in Warden gray staring at him from across the street, and went very carefully, completely still.

Ivet crossed the road. Her rehearsed openings abandoned her entirely.

"Rosalind Dunmore?" she said.

His eyes flicked—to her badge, to her face, to the bag on her shoulder, back to her badge. When he spoke, his voice was pleasant and mild and utterly without warmth, a shopkeeper's voice deployed as armor. "Welcome to honest goods and fair prices. If you're here about the recall notice, I regret to inform you the moonlight is non-refundable."

"I copied your page out of the registry," Ivet said. "Yours and thirty-nine others. The Wardens start processing the eastern district tomorrow. You're on the list. They're going to burn your books and take you somewhere, and nobody will tell me what happens after the somewhere, and I've decided I don't want to find out on your behalf." She pulled the notebook from her bag and held it out. "So. Hypothetically. If a person wanted to not be on a list—"

Rosalind Dunmore looked at the notebook for a long moment. Then he looked at her—at the Warden gray, at the badge, at the ink stains on her fingers—and something moved behind his eyes, quick and tired and old.

"You stole a ledger," he said, "from the people who trained you, to warn a stranger who sells fake magic."

"It's not entirely fake," Ivet said. "The nightmare charm worked."

For the first time, something in his face cracked—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might live, if a person still kept one. He set down the broom.

"Come inside," he said. "And walk quickly. From where I'm standing, apprentice, it looks like the whole sky is about to fall on both of us."
