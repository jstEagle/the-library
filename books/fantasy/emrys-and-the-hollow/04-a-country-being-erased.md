## 4. A Country Being Erased

The crier came to Corvale on a Tuesday, which Rosalind Dunmore had always considered the most dishonest day of the week.

He was in the shop, ostensibly sorting a delivery of river-glass that would become luck-charms by Friday and had been, in truth, sitting at his counter for the better part of an hour doing nothing at all, when the man's voice came rolling down Tallow Street like a barrel dropped from a great height.

"Hear ye! By decree of the Provisional Council of Corvale: the border settlements of Marrowfen, Little Ashby, and the ferry town of Grayford no longer exist. Any person claiming kinship with, memory of, or correspondence from these places is advised that such claims are erroneous and possibly seditious. The Crown thanks you for your understanding."

Rosalind's hands went still over the river-glass.

Little Ashby.

He knew Little Ashby. He had bought honey there every autumn from a woman named Sera Quill who kept bees that hummed in harmony, actual harmony, three notes apart, which was either magic or miracle and nobody in Little Ashby had ever cared to find out which. He had kissed someone under her eaves one October night so long ago it felt like a story he'd heard rather than a thing he'd done.

And now a man in a council coat was standing in the street announcing, with the flat confidence of all official announcements, that she had never existed at all.

The shop bell chimed. Mrs. Halloway from the chandlery leaned in, breathless with the particular excitement of people who have news but not yet anyone to give it to.

"Did you hear?" she said. "Grayford's gone. Not burned, not flooded—*gone*. My cousin's husband drove a cart out there last week to buy tallow and swears the road just... ends. Fields. Nothing but fields, and a stile in the middle of nowhere leading over a fence to more nothing." She lowered her voice, delighted and horrified in equal measure. "And the strange part is the fields are *good* fields. Plowed and everything. As if they'd always been fields."

"Perhaps they were," Rosalind said, and was proud of how steady his voice came out.

"Perhaps they—" Mrs. Halloway squinted at him. "You're not going soft in the head too, are you? Half the street's gone queer this morning. Old Tam Vessy stood in the square weeping because he says he has a sister in Grayford, except nobody can remember him ever having a sister, and by afternoon even *he'd* stopped being sure. That's the part that turns my stomach, Rosalind. It's not that the towns are gone. It's that the forgetting spreads."

She bought a charm against bad dreams—one of his honest ones, mostly glass and suggestion—and left him alone with the river-glass and the terrible arithmetic of what she'd said.

Because Rosalind understood. Of course he understood. He had spent ten years pretending not to understand, which is a different and much more exhausting discipline than ignorance.

The Hollow did not eat land. Land was only where it kept its teeth. What the Hollow ate was *memory*—the remembering itself, the thread between a name and a face, between a place and its people. When the wards along the border had shattered four nights ago, singing their dying note into the dark while he ran through the streets like a madman whom nobody saw, the Hollow had begun to feed. And it fed the way fire feeds: outward, greedily, taking first what was nearest and thinnest. Border towns. Border roads. Border people, whose names lived furthest from the capital's attention and so burned fastest.

Sera Quill and her harmonizing bees. The ferryman at Grayford who'd once told Rosalind his fortune for free and gotten every line of it wrong except the last. Gone—not dead, which would at least leave graves, but *unwritten*, the way a word is unwritten when the page burns.

And the Council, in its wisdom, had decided the best response was to agree with the monster. To declare the erasure official. To make forgetting a matter of public record.

Rosalind looked down at the river-glass in his hands and realized he was gripping it hard enough to cut. A bead of blood welled along his thumb, absurdly bright, and he watched it fall onto the counter and thought: *this is real, this hurts, I am still here.* It seemed suddenly important to verify these things.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty shop, in the tone of a man agreeing to something unpleasant. "Right."

He flipped the sign to CLOSED, which he had not done during daylight hours in ten years, and went upstairs to take out the maps.

* * *

His rooms above the shop were small and deliberately unremarkable, furnished in the style of a man who wanted to be forgotten by his own furniture. But beneath the loose floorboard under the bed—the third floorboard, the one that complained loudest when stepped on, camouflage by annoyance—there was a box, and in the box there were maps.

Not Odile Vane's maps; he didn't know her work yet, wouldn't for some weeks. These were his own, drawn in the years before the sealing, when he had still believed that knowing a thing precisely was the same as being able to hold it. Maps of the borderlands. Maps of the wards, each one marked with the day he'd renewed it, a decade of small secret maintenance written in fading ink. And on the newest sheet, the one he'd started four nights ago with shaking hands, a map of the erasure itself: little hollow circles spreading inward from the border like rings in dark water.

Marrowfen. Little Ashby. Grayford. Three circles now where there had been none a week ago.

He spread them across the bed and knelt over them, and the light through the window went from afternoon to evening without his noticing, and somewhere in there he began, quietly and without drama, to despair.

It wasn't the speed of it. Well—it was partly the speed of it. Ten years of careful warding, and the Hollow had undone the outer ring in four nights. But it was more than speed. It was the *method*. The Hollow wasn't breaking through the country's defenses. It was eating the country's belief in itself, and the country was helping. Every crier's proclamation, every map redrawn in some ministry office, every neighbor who shrugged and said *well, I never heard of Grayford myself*—each one was a door held open. You could not ward against a people volunteering to forget.

A knock at the shop door below, sharp and double-rapped.

Rosalind froze. Closed signs invited curiosity, not customers. He slid the maps back into their box, pressed the complaining floorboard home, and went downstairs with a candle, telling himself it was Mrs. Halloway having forgotten her change, or a drunk, or—

The knock came again. Then a voice, low and clipped, pitched to carry through wood without carrying down the street:

"I can see your candle moving behind the shutters, Mr. Dunmore. Open the door or I'll conclude you're hiding something, which will be correct either way."

Rosalind opened the door.

The young woman on his step was soaked to the bone, though it hadn't rained since morning—she'd come through the reed-flats, then, or the canal district, wading where a sensible person would walk. She wore a Warden's coat with the insignia torn off, leaving a paler rectangle of cloth where the badge had lived, and under the coat she carried a ledger the size of a paving stone, clutched the way other people clutched infants or weapons. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, and both emotions appeared to be aimed at him personally.

"You're Rosalind Dunmore," she said. "Charm-seller. Registered mage, class three, minor workings, renewal overdue by—" she consulted a memory rather than a page, "—four years, eleven months, and some weeks. You've been lying on your census forms about the scope of your gift since before I finished training."

"That's a great deal of accusation to deliver on an empty stomach," Rosalind said. "Would you like tea first, or shall we go straight to the part where you arrest me?"

"I'm not arresting you." She pushed past him into the shop, dripping, and set the ledger on his counter with a thump that made the river-glass jump. "My name is Ivet Kess. My mentor was High Warden Solmere until six hours ago, when I stopped calling him that. And tonight I learned that everything I spent five years believing is either a lie or worse than a lie, and you—" she jabbed a finger at him, hand steady despite everything, "—you are going to tell me what the Hollow Gate actually is, because I read the old reports tonight, the sealed ones, and every single one of them is signed off by a *Dunmore*, and I don't believe in coincidences that expensive."

For a long moment Rosalind looked at her—at the torn coat, the stolen ledger, the fury that was so obviously grief wearing armor—and felt the past rise in him like water in a flooded cellar. Ten years he had kept this shop, sold his honest charms, renewed his wards by night, and told himself that invisibility was safety. And here was proof arriving on his doorstep, dripping canal water: the past did not stay buried. It waited. It got patient. And then it knocked twice, sharply, and demanded tea.

"The kettle's in the back," he said finally. "You'd better sit down, Apprentice Kess. This is going to take longer than one cup."

"Don't call me apprentice." But she sat, pulling the ledger toward her like a shield, and when he brought the tea she wrapped both hands around the cup and stared into it as if the leaves might arrange themselves into a country that still made sense.

"Start with the wards," she said. "Four nights ago. Everyone says it was an attack. Foreign sorcery, maybe. The Council's saying—"

"The Council is saying whatever keeps them from having to say the true thing," Rosalind said. "There was no attack. There was a failure. The wards along the border were old—older than the ban, older than the Wardens—and they were never meant to hold forever. They were meant to be *renewed*. Quietly, constantly, by people who understood what they were for." He turned his cup a slow half-circle on its saucer. "People like me."

"And what were they for?"

"Keeping something asleep." He met her eyes. "Or keeping something wounded quiet—I'll come to that, and you won't like it any better than I do. The point, Kess, is that the wards broke, and the thing they held woke up hungry, and what it eats isn't flesh. It's remembering. Marrowfen, Little Ashby, Grayford—they're not destroyed. They're *forgotten*, and the forgetting is spreading, and every proclamation your former master's Council issues is setting another place on the table."

Ivet had gone very pale. "That's insane."

"Yes," Rosalind agreed pleasantly. "Welcome to the last two weeks of my life."

She set down the cup. He watched her decide—watched the war move through her face, loyalty and evidence grappling in broad daylight—and then she pulled the ledger around and opened it to a page near the back, and turned it to face him, and his own name looked up at him from the ink.

*Rosalind Dunmore. Corvale. See annex: GATE.*

"There are forty names in this annex," Ivet said quietly. "Forty mages the Wardens have known about for years—known, and left alone, and registered under seal. Everyone else gets burned out of their homes. These forty get *visited*. Do you know why, Mr. Dunmore? Because Solmere's orders say the Gate must be maintained, and maintained by the gifted, and the gifted must therefore be catalogued like livestock." Her voice cracked, just barely, on the last word. "I thought I was stealing a list of victims tonight. Turns out I stole a list of *tools*."

The candle guttered. Outside, far off toward the border, the night sky was missing stars—he could see it through the shutter gap, that wrongness, that absence shaped like absence—and Rosalind thought of Sera Quill's bees humming their three-note chord in a village that no longer existed, and felt the full weight of the decade settle onto his shoulders like wet wool.

"Kess," he said. "Before I tell you anything else, you should know that the last person who asked me these questions paid a very high price for the answers. And you should know that I intend to answer anyway, because I am apparently constitutionally incapable of learning."

"What happened to them?"

"He walked into the dark," Rosalind said, "and I let him, and that is the shortest version of the longest mistake of my life."

Ivet looked at him for a long moment. Then she closed the ledger, squared it on the counter like a soldier dressing ranks, and said:

"Then start talking, and talk fast. Because according to the patrol rotations in here, Solmere's men sweep Tallow Street tomorrow at dawn. Whatever you are, Mr. Dunmore—charm-seller, lawbreaker, gate-keeper—you've got one night to be it out loud."

Above the shop, faint through the floorboards, the hidden maps lay in their box, three dark circles spreading inward like rings in water.

The past, Rosalind thought, pouring the tea, had excellent timing and terrible manners.

He began to talk.
