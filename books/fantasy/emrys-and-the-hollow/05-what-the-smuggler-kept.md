## 5. What the Smuggler Kept

Bram Ferrow had a rule about mornings: he didn't have them. Mornings were for people with honest work and clean consciences, and he had neither, so he slept through the gray part of the day on principle and let the river do his waking for him.

The river woke him that morning by banging on his hull.

He came up the ladder from the *Kestrel's* hold with a boat hook in one hand and half his wits still asleep in the other, ready to explain to whatever drunk fool was hammering on his deck at dawn exactly how the Ferrow family had come to own the only stretch of the Vell that still ran all the way to the sea.

The drunk fool turned out to be Rosalind Dunmore.

Bram stopped with one boot on the deck and the other still on the ladder rung, which meant he was standing in the least dignified possible position when the man he'd spent ten years not thinking about looked up from under a hood gone soft with road dust and said, "Hello, Bram."

"Absolutely not," Bram said.

"I haven't asked for anything yet."

"You were about to. You've got your asking face on. I've seen it. It's the face you made before you talked old Master Corvel into letting you sit his examinations early." He climbed the rest of the way up because standing on a ladder like a heron was worse than facing this. "The answer is no."

"The answer," Rosalind said, "is that I've brought passengers."

And there they were, stepping out of the shadow of the warehouse pilings: a young woman with a wardens' coat cut down to look like nothing and a stolen ledger hugged to her chest like a baby; and a boy—no, a child, maybe nine or ten, hollow-eyed in the way refugee children got hollow-eyed, except this one kept turning his head toward the water as if listening to it.

Bram looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked back with those gray eyes that had always been able to make Bram feel like a man being weighed against something better.

"No," Bram said again, mostly to hear how weak it sounded.

* * *

They sat in the *Kestrel's* cabin, which was built for two men and currently held four and a half, counting the ledger, which took up more room than its size suggested. The girl—Ivet, she said, former apprentice, emphasis on *former*, delivered with the flatness of someone who'd rehearsed the word until it stopped bleeding—sat with her back to the wall where she could see everyone. The child, Pip, fell asleep sitting up within four minutes of sitting down, mid-sentence, like a puppet with cut strings.

Rosalind caught him before his head hit the table and laid him gently on the bench, and Bram watched him do it and hated how careful his hands still were.

"So," Bram said, once they'd moved up on deck out of earshot of sleeping children and suspicious ex-wardens. "Ten years. Not a letter. Not a whisper down the river, and rivers talk, Rosalind, rivers never shut up. And now you show up at dawn with a fugitive and a kid who listens to water, during the worst month this country has had since the war, and you want passage downstream."

"I want passage to Grayford."

"Grayford's past the third lock. The third lock's been sealed since the wards went down. Nobody's running cargo past the second lock because nobody knows what's past the second lock anymore." He heard his own voice climbing and made himself stop. "Why Grayford?"

"Because things are being erased, Bram. Towns. Roads. People's memories of them. And I think I know why, and I think I know how to stop it, and both of those things are my fault."

There it was. Ten years ago, Rosalind had stood on this same deck and said some version of *I can't tell you*, and then he'd been gone by morning, leaving behind a cold bed, an unpaid debt at the Chandlery, and a man who'd spent a decade telling himself he was well rid.

"You always did have a talent for making your faults sound noble," Bram said.

"It isn't noble. It's just true."

"Everything with you is true and terrible and none of it is ever simple enough to be useful." Bram gripped the rail. Below them the Vell ran brown and slow, fat with spring melt, and somewhere under that brown water were lanterns—he'd seen them last night, coming upriver, drowned lanterns burning under the black water off Sallow Bend, whole streets' worth, glowing down there like a sunken festival. He hadn't told anyone. He wasn't sure what he'd say. *Excuse me, the river has lights in it now.*

"Where did you go?" he asked, quieter. "That night. Just tell me that much. Not the reasons. Not the mission. Where."

Rosalind was silent long enough that a heron went flapping past, offended by the quiet.

"Away," he said finally. "Somewhere that doesn't exist anymore. That's not poetry, Bram, that's literal. There was a village. I grew up there. You couldn't have found it if you tried, and lately—" his jaw worked— "lately even I have trouble finding it. In my head. I remember the name of the street I lived on and the memory slides off like water off oilcloth."

Bram stared at him. He wanted to call it madness, but he'd rowed past Sallow Bend last night and seen a lamplighter's round burning forty feet underwater, so he was in no position to call anything mad.

"And you couldn't have told me that? Ten years ago? 'Going away, might be erased, save my seat'?" His voice cracked on the last word and he hated that too. "I thought you'd died. For the first year I thought it every day. Then I decided you'd chosen to die, which was worse, and then I decided you'd never loved me at all, which was easiest, and I built a very comfortable life on top of that particular grave, thank you very much, and here you are digging it up at dawn."

"I know."

"Don't say *I know*. Don't be wise at me. I'm angry. Let me finish being angry before you start being right."

Rosalind, infuriatingly, smiled a little. "You always let me finish being right eventually."

"That was ten years ago. I've gotten stubborn since." But the anger was going out of him the way it always did around this man, draining through some crack in the hull he'd never found to patch. He looked out at the water instead. "There are lights under the river," he said. "Off Sallow Bend. Lanterns. Burning underwater. Whole rows of them, like someone dropped a street."

Rosalind went very still. "Sallow Bend," he repeated. "There was a ferry landing at Sallow Bend."

"There still is. Sort of. Half of it's missing. Not broken—missing. Like the carpenter got bored halfway through."

"Sallow Bend had a lamplighter named Odette Marrin." Rosalind said it slowly, like a man reading names off a stone. "She used to race the dark home every evening. If she lit the last lamp before full dark she'd ring her pole bell twice, and the whole bend would ring back."

Bram felt the hair rise on his arms. "I heard bells," he said. "Last night. Under the water. Two rings."

Neither of them said anything for a while. Pip, below decks, murmured in his sleep—a low, tuneless humming, almost like the river itself.

"All right," Bram said at last, because what else was there. "Grayford. Past the third lock. Which means passing the Warden station at Tarnholt, which means bribing a man I've already bribed twice this season, which means he'll either raise the price or report us, and I genuinely don't know which."

"Thank you, Bram."

"Don't thank me. Thanking implies forgiveness, and I haven't decided." He jabbed a finger at Rosalind's chest. "But understand the terms. You're paying crew rates. You're hauling rope. And when we get wherever we're going and you decide to vanish again, you'll leave a note. A note, Rosalind. With words on it. Addressed to me."

Something moved across Rosalind's face—guilt and grief and something warmer underneath—and he nodded. "A note."

"Good." Bram turned to the rigging, because there was always rigging, and because his hands needed work that wasn't shaking. "Now get below and sleep. You look like something the river refused."

* * *

They cast off at dusk, because Bram ran the *Kestrel* on smuggler's hours: hidden by day, working by dark, tied up in reeds when the moon was bright. The girl Ivet proved useful inside an hour—she read water like a warden reads faces, spotted the revenue skiff tucked in the willows above the second lock before Bram did, and said nothing about it, just touched his shoulder and pointed. He revised his opinion of her from *liability* to *possibly worth feeding*.

Pip stood at the bow the whole first watch, holding the rail, humming.

It wasn't an annoying hum. That was the strange thing. It was low and steady and it matched the engine-thrum of the current so exactly that after a while Bram stopped hearing it as separate from the river, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Only when the boy paused—to drink, to sleep, to stare at something none of them could see—did the silence feel wrong, like a missing tooth.

"What's he humming?" Bram asked Rosalind, low, at the tiller.

"I don't know yet." Rosalind's face in the dark was hard to read. "I know it matters. I don't know how."

"You're a font of certainty tonight."

"I'm a font of guilt tonight. Certainty comes later, if we live."

Bram snorted despite himself. Ten years, and the man could still land a joke like a fly-fisherman lands a trout—lightly, precisely, when you least expected it. It wasn't fair. Nothing about Rosalind Dunmore had ever been fair.

They passed Sallow Bend at midnight.

Bram felt it before he saw it—the humming at the bow changed pitch, rose, quickened, and Pip pressed both hands flat against the rail and leaned out over the black water like a plant toward light. Then the lanterns came into view beneath them, down in the deep, dozens of them, glowing gold-green through the murk, strung along a street that had no business existing under a river. A lamplighter's round. And faintly, muffled by fathoms, two bell-rings.

Nobody spoke. Ivet came forward and put a hand on Pip's shoulder, and the boy reached up and gripped her fingers without looking away from the water.

"They're not drowning," Rosalind said softly. He was leaning over the rail beside the boy, and his reflection floated among the drowned lamps, gray-eyed, ghost-pale. "Or—they weren't. Whatever's down there, it kept the lighting of them. Kept the best part of the day." His voice caught. "Odette Marrin raced the dark home every night of her life. Something down there remembers that."

"Is that what happens?" Bram asked. His throat was tight. "When places get eaten. The good parts sink?"

"I don't know." Rosalind straightened. "But I intend to find out. That's what Grayford's for. There's a mapmaker there—Verrin, old man Verrin—who charts places that don't exist. If anyone's drawn what's happening to us, it's him."

Below them, the lanterns slid astern, one by one, going out—not snuffed, Bram noticed, but *passed*, each lamp dimming as they left it behind, like windows going dark down a street you'd grown up on.

Pip hummed them a farewell. Two bell-rings, in hummed notes.

Bram worked the tiller and said nothing, because if he opened his mouth he was going to say something unforgivably soft, like *I missed you*, or worse, *don't ever leave this boat*, and he had a reputation to maintain among absolutely no one.

* * *

They made the second lock by grey morning and paid the lockkeeper triple to look elsewhere, which he did with the professional serenity of a man who had seen everything twice. Beyond the lock the river changed. Bram had run these waters since he was twelve, and he knew every bend the way you know the lines of your own hand, and the bends were wrong. A curve that should have swung east swung north. An island he could have sworn had a heronry on it was bare gravel, new-made, as if the river had only just thought of it.

"Is it doing this?" Ivet asked quietly, beside him. She'd barely slept. She kept touching the ledger at her hip like a woman checking a wound. "The river. Changing."

"The river's fine," Bram said. "It's the country around it that's forgetting where it put things." He spat over the side, an old habit that suddenly felt like defiance. "My gran used to say the Vell was older than any kingdom and would outlast them all. Gran also said never trust a man with two coats, so her record's mixed."

That got a huff out of Ivet—the first sound she'd made that wasn't tactical. Progress.

At midday they passed the mouth of the Aldwater, and that was where they found the boat.

It came drifting out of the tributary, a small river skiff, oars shipped, riding low and steady. Empty. Except it wasn't empty—there was a coat folded neatly on the stern thwart, and on top of the coat, weighted down with a river stone, a lantern. Unlit. Clean glass. Someone had trimmed the wick with care.

Bram brought the *Kestrel* alongside without being told to. Some instincts predated thought.

"Should we take it?" Pip whispered. He'd appeared at the rail, awake, solemn.

"It's not ours," Bram started—

"It's a message," Rosalind said. He was looking at the lantern the way he'd looked at the drowned lights. "A lit lantern means *someone's home*. An unlit one, sent adrift..." He swallowed. "Someone's saying their light's gone out. Or asking us to carry it."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Pip reached across the gap between the boats, lifted the lantern aboard, and set it down on the *Kestrel's* deck with the gravity of a coronation.

"We'll light it," the boy said. Not a question. "Tonight. When it gets dark. Somebody should."

Bram looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the lantern. Somewhere south, past the dead third lock, past Tarnholt and its bribable sergeant, Grayford waited with its impossible maps, and between here and there lay a country busy forgetting itself, one village, one lamplighter, one folded coat at a time.

"Well," Bram said, and pushed off from the empty skiff, and turned the *Kestrel's* nose downstream toward everything he'd sworn off and everyone he'd never stopped keeping a spare berth for. "Somebody should."
