## 6. The Child Who Hears It

The refugee camp at Marsh Hollow smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular despair of people who had been told their homes no longer existed and had decided, collectively, not to believe it.

Ivet stood at the edge of it with her hood up and her stolen ledger burning a hole against her ribs, and she thought: *this is what the end of a country looks like when it's trying to be polite about it.*

There must have been three hundred souls camped in the flood-meadow where the River Vane bent lazy around a stand of willows. Tents made of sailcloth, of blankets, of one memorable structure that appeared to be an overturned ferry with a door cut into its hull. Cookfires in long lines. Children—gods, so many children—running between the tents with the fearless energy of the young, who had not yet learned that the sky was supposed to have stars in it.

"You're staring," Bram said beside her. He had his riverman's slouch on, hands in his pockets, projecting *I am nobody, I have always been nobody, please do not rob me, I have nothing worth taking.*

"I'm assessing," Ivet said.

"You're staring *sadly.* There's a difference. Sad-staring gets us noticed."

Rosalind, on Ivet's other side, said nothing. He had said very little since Grayford Bridge, since the confession, since Bram had walked off into the dark and then, two days later, reappeared at their fire without a word, as if he'd merely stepped out to check on his boat. Ivet had decided not to ask. Some reconciliations were like wounds; poking them was how you lost the finger.

"We need food," Rosalind said finally. "And news. And a place to sleep that isn't a ditch."

"And we need to not be recognized," Ivet added. "Half the camp will have seen Warden proclamations. My face is on some of them."

"Your face is on *posters?*" Bram looked delighted. "What do they have you doing? Menacing?"

"It's a very official menace. They got my nose wrong, for the record."

"Your nose is your best feature," Bram said. "Tragic. Come on, sad-staring lady. Let's go be nobody."

* * *

The camp had organized itself with the grim competence of people who had done this before. There was a soup line, a complaints line, and a line that appeared to exist purely for arguing about the other two lines. A woman with a ledger of her own—hand-inked, fat with names—directed newcomers to space with the weary authority of a harbor master.

"Names?" she said when they reached her.

Bram gave his without hesitation. Ivet gave the name she'd been using on the road—Maren, a weaver's daughter from a town that had conveniently been erased, and thus could not contradict her. Then it was Rosalind's turn, and Ivet watched him do the thing she had watched him do twice now: the small pause, the half-second where a man weighed his own name in his hand like a coin he wasn't sure was still legal tender.

"Roz," he said. "Just Roz."

The woman wrote it down. "Roz. Weaver too?"

"Charm-seller," Rosalind said, and Ivet's heart performed a small horrified gymnastic routine, but the woman only snorted.

"Half the camp's a charm-seller this week. Tent forty, past the ferry-house. Soup's at dusk. Don't fight anyone over the good spoons, we only have four."

They got their soup—it was barley and river-fish and something green that might have been a weed or might have been a vegetable, and it was, in Ivet's professional opinion as someone who had eaten Warden garrison food for six years, a masterpiece—and found a place on a log near the biggest cookfire.

That was where Ivet first heard the humming.

It came from somewhere behind the ferry-house, threading through the camp noise like a silver wire through burlap. At first she took it for a lullaby, some old border tune. Then the hair on her arms stood up, because she *knew* the tune. Not with her ears. With the place behind her sternum where the wards used to live, back when she'd been allowed to have them.

It was the Hollow's song. The resonance the border wards were tuned to. She had felt it a hundred times through the warden-stones at Corvale, a low patient pressure, like something enormous breathing in a room next door.

Someone here was humming it. Perfectly. Note for note.

"Bram," she said quietly. "Roz. Listen."

They listened. Rosalind's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. Bram's face went through several expressions in rapid succession, settling on *deeply suspicious.*

"That's a child's voice," Bram said.

"That's the Hollow," Ivet said. "In a child's voice."

They found the source behind the ferry-house, in the trampled grass between the hull and the willow line: a small figure, maybe eight years old, sitting cross-legged with their back against a root, humming to a row of snails arranged in a careful line before them. The child was thin, filthy, wearing a coat that had clearly belonged to someone twice their size, and humming the song of the thing that was eating the country, with the serene absent-mindedness of someone humming a tune heard from a neighbor's window.

The snails, Ivet noted, were all facing the child. Attentively. That was probably nothing.

"Hello," Rosalind said, very gently, the way you'd speak to a bird you didn't want to startle off a branch.

The child looked up. Their eyes were the color of pond water and entirely unbothered. "Hello. This is Snail. This is also Snail. This one's Snail Two."

"Excellent names," Bram said. "Very distinct."

"You're humming," Ivet said, and then winced, because that was not the gentle opening line she'd planned.

The child shrugged. "It's the under-song. It's always going. Most people can't hear it 'cause their ears are full of themselves." They resumed humming. The snails swayed slightly, which—Ivet checked Bram's face to confirm she wasn't imagining it—yes. The snails were swaying. In time.

Rosalind crouched down slowly, bringing himself to the child's level, and Ivet watched him listen the way she'd seen him listen to wards: with his whole body, eyes half-shut, head tilted. When he opened his eyes again they were wet.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Pip."

"Where are your parents, Pip?"

"Gone." Said the way children say it—flat, a door closed, a room not entered. "They went to sleep in Marrowfield and the town went to sleep too and I woke up on the road. A lady gave me bread. Then the lady was gone but the bread wasn't, so that was all right."

Ivet felt something in her chest crack along a seam she'd thought was load-bearing. Marrowfield. It was in her ledger. She didn't say so.

"Pip," Rosalind said. "The song you're humming. Does it ever tell you things?"

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "It's not a *telling* song. It's a *missing* song. Like when you lose your mitten and you keep patting your pocket." The child patted their pocket to demonstrate. "It's patting its pocket. All the time. For everything it lost." Pip's pond-water eyes went distant. "It lost so much. That's why it's so loud lately."

The cookfire cracked. Somewhere in the camp, someone laughed at a joke. The ordinary world went on being ordinary, and Ivet stood in the grass and understood, with the clarity of cold water, that this child had been sitting here comforting the apocalypse with snails.

"We should take the child," Bram said, very quietly, from behind them.

"We just met the child," Ivet whispered back.

"We just met the child who is *harmonizing with the world-eater.* I don't want to imagine the alternative custody arrangement. The Wardens will want this one, Ivet. You know what 'relocate' means now."

She did know. She'd read the internal orders in the ledger. *Relocate* was a word with a trapdoor in it.

Rosalind was still crouched, still level with Pip. "Would you like to come with us, Pip? We're traveling. We have a boat."

"Is the boat nice?"

"It leaks a little," Bram said, "but it leaks with *dignity.*"

Pip looked at the snails. This was clearly a negotiation of state. "Snails come."

"Snails come," Rosalind agreed solemnly.

Pip stood up, gathered the snails into a tin with practiced tenderness, and took Rosalind's hand as if they'd been doing it for years. Found family, Ivet thought, watching the small fingers close around the large ones. It doesn't ask permission. It just happens to you, like weather.

She should have known it was too easy.

* * *

The horns sounded an hour after full dark.

Ivet was asleep against the ferry-house wall with the ledger under her coat, and the sound went through her like a blade through canvas—two short notes and a long one. Warden muster. She was on her feet before she was fully awake, six years of training doing the thinking for her.

"What is it, what is it, is it breakfast?" Pip asked, sitting up in the nest of blankets they'd made between the three adults, snail tin clutched to their chest.

"Soldiers," Ivet said. "Stay behind me."

They came through the camp's main lane with lanterns held high and faces hidden behind warden-masks, a dozen of them, and behind them a crier with a scroll, and the crier's voice was the polished, reasonable voice of the proclamations, the voice that made atrocity sound like administration.

"By order of the High Warden's Office: all persons harboring the gifted are commanded to surrender them for relocation and care. Gifted children are a public danger and a public sorrow. Cooperation will be rewarded. Concealment will be prosecuted."

*Public sorrow.* Ivet's hands curled into fists. They'd gotten better at the language, she'd give them that. Solmere's people could make a cattle-raid sound like a charity drive.

The soldiers began going tent to tent. There was a list. Of course there was a list—there was always a list, and Ivet had the horrible thought that somewhere in the camp a child had been reported, some frightened or pious or merely tired soul had said *the little one hums strange, the little one knew about the flood before it came,* and now the machinery was here, lanterns and masks and a trapdoor word.

"They're coming this way," Bram breathed. He'd woken silently, which was a smuggler's gift, and he had a knife out and was looking at the willow line the way other men looked at a lover. "Back of the camp. The reeds. There's a cut-through to the oxbow—my boat's moored two bends down."

"You said the boat leaks with dignity," Ivet whispered.

"It'll leak with urgency. Go."

They went. It did not go smoothly, because nothing involving children and night and soldiers goes smoothly. A lantern swept the willow line as they crossed the open ground behind the ferry-house, and Ivet pulled Pip down into the shadow of the hull, and Pip—bless the strange, terrible, wonderful child—did not cry out, did not whimper, only pressed the snail tin to their chest and hummed, very softly, and Ivet felt the hum settle over them like a blanket, like a hush, like the sound a house makes around you when it loves you.

The lantern passed on.

Then the reeds. Tall as a man, dry as whispers, and the ground under them going soft and treacherous toward the water. Bram led, Rosalind carried Pip when the mud got deep, and Ivet walked backward the whole way with her hand on her knife, watching the lanterns bob and multiply behind them like a procession of malignant fireflies.

They were fifty feet from the water when the shout went up.

"Contact! Willows, north side!"

"Run," Bram said, with feeling.

They ran. The reeds whipped Ivet's face. The mud sucked at her boots and gave back with a sound like rude applause. Pip, on Rosalind's back, had stopped humming and started laughing—a bright, wild, entirely inappropriate laugh—and Ivet would remember that sound for the rest of her life, she suspected: the sound of a child finding joy in the exact middle of the worst night of the year.

The boat was where Bram had left it, which was the single piece of good fortune the night offered, and they piled in, and Bram cut the line, and the current took them.

Not fast enough. Lanterns along the bank, running figures, a voice bellowing something about the oxbow and the weir. An arrow hissed past and buried itself in the gunwale with a *thock* that Ivet felt in her teeth.

"Can this boat go *faster*?" she demanded.

"It's a boat, not a rumor!"

"Then make it a rumor!"

Bram swore with the fluency of a man who had grown up in smuggler taverns, threw his whole weight on the pole, and the boat leapt—well, *lurched*, but in a manner that could generously be called leaping—around the bend, and the bank, and the lanterns, and then there was only the river, black and broad and mercifully empty, and the reeds sliding past, and Pip's laughter finally winding down into hiccups.

They drifted a mile before anyone spoke.

Ivet sat in the stern with the ledger against her knees and watched the soldiers' lights shrink to nothing behind them, and felt the strangest thing rising in her chest. It took her a moment to identify it, because it had been so long since she'd felt it that it arrived unlabeled, like a package from an old friend.

It was *belonging.* Ragged, terrified, mud-soaked belonging. A disgraced apprentice, a heartbroken smuggler, a man with no name he'd admit to, and a child with a tin of snails, fleeing together down a dark river—and it was the first time in six years she hadn't felt alone inside the law or outside it.

Pip had fallen asleep against Rosalind's side, mid-sentence, mid-hiccup. Rosalind sat perfectly still so as not to wake them, looking down at the sleeping child with an expression Ivet had no name for either, except that it looked like a man being handed back something he'd paid too much for.

"They hummed the wards calm," Ivet said quietly. "In the willows. You felt it."

"I felt it." Rosalind's voice was rough. "Ivet—the Hollow's song, in a child's mouth. That's not a gift. That's a *door.*"

"Then we'd better be the ones holding the key."

Bram, at the pole, snorted. "Look at you two. One night of running from soldiers and you're already finishing each other's ominous sentences."

"Jealous?" Ivet said.

"Deeply. I used to be the ominous one." But he said it softly, and there was no barb in it, and when Rosalind glanced up at him across the sleeping child there was a look between them that Ivet politely pretended not to see.

The river carried them on through the dark. And it was only later, much later, when they'd tied up in the cutbank and taken the first watch, that Ivet remembered the ledger against her knees—and the entry she'd read at Grayford, the one she'd been carrying around like a stone in her boot, telling herself she'd imagined its meaning.

*Names fed to the Gate. Quarterly. Authorized: Office of the High Warden.*

She had thought it was a metaphor. Sitting in the dark with a sleeping child's humming still caught in her ears, Ivet Kess understood that it was not a metaphor at all.

She opened the ledger by the light of no star at all, and began, at last, to count the dead.
