## 7. Ledger of the Condemned

The ledger had been sitting in Ivet's pack for three days like a stone she kept forgetting to throw away.

She told herself she hadn't opened it yet because there was no time. There was always no time. Pip needed feeding, Bram needed arguing with, Rosalind needed watching in that particular way one watches a person who has decided to be brave about something terrible. The boat had put them ashore at a place called Millbrook that was, according to every map Ivet had ever memorized in her six years of warden training, a wheat field.

The wheat field had a well. The well had clean water and a bucket on a rope, which meant someone remembered it, even if the country didn't.

So there was time. She just hadn't opened it.

Now, on the third night, with Pip asleep against Odile's rolled charts and Bram on watch at the edge of the firelight, Ivet finally untied the oilcloth and set the ledger on her knees.

It was heavier than it should have been. She'd carried ledgers before—hundreds of them, in her apprenticeship. Rosters of registered mages, quarterly reports, incident filings. This one was bound in grey leather gone soft at the corners, stamped with the Wardens' sun-and-bar sigil, and it had been locked in High Warden Solmere's own cabinet, behind a ward keyed to his thumbprint alone.

She had stolen it in the same hour she'd stolen herself out of the Wardenhouse. It had seemed, at the time, like a secondary concern. Evidence. Leverage. A thing to wave at someone important later, when she'd worked out who the important someone ought to be.

Her fingers hesitated over the clasp.

"You going to read it or propose marriage to it?" Bram asked from the dark, without turning around.

"I'm deciding whether I want to know."

"Never once stopped you before." He shifted, and the firelight caught the scar along his jaw. "You're the sort who reads the last page first. I've watched you do it."

"That's called being efficient."

"It's called being unable to stand not knowing, which is a different animal wearing the same coat."

Ivet snorted despite herself, and undid the clasp.

* * *

The first hundred pages were exactly what she expected. Names. Dates of registration. Districts. Notes on manifestation—the age a gift first showed, its shape, its strength, graded on the old five-rung scale she'd been taught to administer herself. She knew this format the way she knew her own handwriting. She'd filled in columns like these. She'd believed, with the whole earnest weight of her nineteen years, that registration was protection. That the list existed so the Wardens could *shield* mages from panic, from mobs, from the old bad days.

She found Rosalind Dunmore on page forty-one. Registered at fourteen. Manifestation: charmwork, minor. Strength: second rung.

Ivet stared at that entry for a long moment. *Second rung.* She had met the man. She had watched him hold back the Hollow's attention with nothing but a bent iron nail and a whispered word while the border wards came apart like wet paper. Second rung was what you gave to farmwives who could coax milk from going sour.

Someone had falsified his grade. Someone had looked at Rosalind Dunmore and written down *harmless*, deliberately, and filed him away where no one would look twice.

Which meant someone had known what he really was. And chosen to leave him alone.

Her mouth had gone dry. She turned the page.

The next section began without ceremony, no new heading, as if the clerk had simply run out of one kind of horror and started another in the same hand, same ink, same afternoon. But the columns changed. Where the first pages recorded names given freely by mages presenting themselves for registration, these pages recorded names *taken*. Arrests. Detentions. Transfers.

And beside each transfer, in a column that had no business existing in any honest ledger, a single word:

*Fed.*

Ivet read the word eleven times before it stopped looking like a mistake.

*Fed.*

Maren Ostler, hedge-healer, transferred to the capital, fed, autumn before last. Dovan Reeve, storm-caller, fed. The Tallow twins, illusionists, aged nine and nine, fed. She knew some of these names. Not personally—but she knew their files. She'd been the apprentice who fetched them. She'd carried Maren Ostler's paperwork down the corridor to Solmere's office and stood at attention while he thanked her and said the woman would be *processed humanely*.

Processed. Fed. The same thing, said in two languages—one for the corridor, one for the cabinet.

Her hands were shaking now. She made herself keep reading, because stopping felt like letting them win, and because some part of her—the part that had burned bright and stupid with loyalty since she was twelve years old—was still looking for the explanation. The context. The paragraph that would make it all right.

She found it on the next leaf, in Solmere's own handwriting, cramped and precise, the hand of a man who underlined things he considered self-evident:

*"The Gate does not sleep. It listens. It is quieted by offering. A name given willingly buys a season; a name taken buys less, but suffices. The purge is not a response to the crisis. The purge IS the response. We have known the terms for sixty years. Let the Council bleat about law; the ledger is the law that matters."*

Sixty years.

Ivet closed the ledger. Opened it again, because closing it felt like drowning. Read it again.

Sixty years. Her whole order. Her whole life. Every lesson about duty and vigilance and the mortal danger of unregistered magic—and underneath it all, the Wardens hadn't been fighting sorcery at all. They'd been running a farm. A slow, patient, institutional farm, planting mages into the dark one season at a time, keeping the Hollow fat and quiet and *secret*, because a secret was power, and Solmere sat at the top of the secret like a spider at the center of a very tidy web.

And the stars had gone out anyway. The wards had broken anyway. Because whatever the Hollow wanted, it had never been full. It had only ever been *patient*, and patience was the one thing an institution could outlast anyone in.

Except it hadn't outlasted this.

"Ivet." Bram's voice, closer now. "Your face has gone a color I don't love."

She couldn't answer. She held the ledger out instead, open to Solmere's note, and watched him read it by firelight. Watched his expression do something complicated—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. Like a man who'd suspected the floor was rotten and finally heard it creak.

"The Tallow twins," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Nine years old. I carried their file. I remember thinking how neat the clerk's handwriting was."

Bram said nothing, which was, from Bram, a kindness.

"I thought we were protecting people." She heard how young she sounded and hated it. "My whole—everything I was, everything I trained for, I thought the list was a shield. You write your name down so someone stands between you and the dark. That's what they tell you. That's what I *believed*—"

"And it turns out the list was a menu," Bram said quietly.

The words landed like a dropped stone. Ivet looked at him. He wasn't being cruel. He was being accurate, which from Bram was worse and better at once.

"A menu," she repeated.

"Every smuggler learns to ask who profits from a rule," he said. "Wardens' rules especially. I used to wonder why the patrols never actually caught anyone who mattered. Now I know. They weren't hunting. They were *harvesting*. Slowly. Politely. With excellent handwriting."

Ivet laughed—a short, awful sound with nothing in it—and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Six years. Six years of drills and oaths and polishing her mentor's boots, of believing she was one of the good ones, of feeling sorry for the frightened mages she processed because they didn't understand that the system was *for* them.

They'd understood fine. They'd understood better than she had.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Somewhere past the deepest part of the night she gave up pretending and walked to the edge of the camp, ledger under her arm, and found Rosalind awake too, sitting on the dry lip of the old well with his coat around his shoulders, looking up at the starless sky like a man reading a letter he already knew by heart.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked.

"I sleep about four hours a decade," he said. "Sit down. The stones are warmer than they look."

She sat. For a while neither of them spoke. The Hollow's silence pressed at the edges of the dark—not sound exactly, more the absence of sound where sound should be, like a tooth socket where the night used to have teeth.

"I read it," she said finally. "All of it. Or enough."

Rosalind nodded slowly. He didn't say *I'm sorry*, which she appreciated. Everyone else would have said *I'm sorry*, and she'd have had to decide whether to hit them.

"Sixty years," she said. "Did you know?"

"I suspected. Knowing and suspecting are neighbors, and I tried very hard not to visit either." He turned the bent iron nail over in his fingers—an old habit, she'd noticed, the way other men worried coins. "Ten years ago, when I sealed the Gate, I assumed the Wardens were fools who'd let it decay through neglect. Neglect I could forgive. It's practically a virtue compared to—" He gestured at the ledger. "That."

"They fed people to it." Her voice cracked on *people*. "To keep it quiet. To keep it *secret*. So they could keep being important." She hugged her knees. "Do you know what the worst part is? It's not even that they lied. It's that they were *orderly* about it. Columns. Grades. Cross-references. Evil with a filing system."

"Most evil is," Rosalind said. "Chaos is honest. Order can lie to itself for generations and call it diligence." He glanced sideways at her. "What will you do?"

The question sat between them. She turned it over, examining it the way she'd once examined evidence, and found—to her mild surprise—that the answer had already assembled itself somewhere below her conscious mind, the way answers did when you'd actually made them days ago and merely hadn't admitted it yet.

"Solmere," she said. "He wrote that note in his own hand. He signed the transfers. He built the harvest. Whatever happens to the Gate, whatever we find at the end of this road—he doesn't get to walk away from it dressed as a protector. The country's about to learn what the Hollow is. Fine. Let it also learn what the Wardens were." She looked down at the ledger in her lap. "Every name in here deserves to be more than a line item. Including the ones that are just lines now."

"That's justice," Rosalind said carefully. "Outside the law."

"The law was the menu, Rosalind."

"Yes," he said softly. "I know. I just wanted to hear you say it. People say it differently when they choose it than when it's forced on them. Chosen sounds steadier."

She almost smiled. "You always talk like a book."

"I am a book. Several, actually. Poorly reviewed." He rose, joints cracking, and offered her a hand up. "Get some sleep if you can. Tomorrow we start toward the capital, and I suspect the road will be educational in all the worst ways."

She took his hand. Then, before she could think better of it: "Page forty-one. You're listed as second rung. Charmwork, minor."

A flicker crossed his face—guilt, amusement, something older than both. "A kind clerk," he said, "a very long time ago."

"No," Ivet said. "A scared one. Or a smart one. Or both." She tucked the ledger under her arm like a shield, or a weapon—she hadn't decided which yet, and suspected she wouldn't until the moment she needed it to be one. "Whoever they were, they lied in ink to keep you alive. That's the first decent thing this book's ever done."

She walked back toward the dying fire, and behind her she heard Rosalind murmur something to the well—thanks, maybe, or an apology, or just the small maintenance-work of a man who could not stop tending things—and she realized that for the first time since the wards broke, since the stars went out, since she'd run from everything she'd been,

she wasn't afraid of the dark.

She was angry at it. Anger, she was discovering, was a much better thing to carry. It had handles.

Tomorrow, the capital. Tomorrow, Solmere.

Tonight, she slept holding the ledger like it owed her something—which, she reflected as she drifted off, it did. It owed her every name inside it. And she intended to collect.
