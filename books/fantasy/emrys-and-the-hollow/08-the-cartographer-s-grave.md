## 8. The Cartographer's Grave

Bram Ferrow had spent his whole life learning which silences meant trouble. The silence of a river before a weir. The silence of a customs officer deciding whether to search your hold. And now this: the silence of a village that was supposed to be there and wasn't.

"Vellin's Ford," Ivet said, checking the ledger against the road sign—or what remained of a road sign, a post with half a word still legible, *VELL—*, like a man interrupted mid-sentence. "Population four hundred. Two mills. A famous mapmaker."

"A famous mapmaker," Rosalind repeated quietly.

Bram grunted. In his experience, when a place was described in the past tense by someone who'd never seen it, that place was already gone. He'd watched it happen all the way down the river: towns thinned to sketches of themselves, then sketches thinned to rumors, then rumors thinned to nothing at all except a strange smooth feeling in your memory, like a word on the tip of your tongue that you eventually stopped reaching for.

The ford itself was still there. That was the cruel part. The water ran shallow and bright over the stones, exactly as a ford should, but there were no mills, no bridge, no roofs. Just meadow going soft and green where a village had been, as if the land had healed over the wound so neatly you might almost believe nothing had ever been cut.

Almost. Except for the well. It stood alone in the middle of the grass, stone-lipped and patient, with no house around it to explain why anyone would have dug it there.

Pip stood beside the well and looked down into it for a long time.

"Don't," Bram said.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're thinking about climbing in."

"I'm *looking*," Pip said, with the dignity of a child who had been caught thinking about climbing in. "There's a bucket chain. Somebody would've needed water every day. You don't dig a well for nobody."

Bram looked at the child—at the small stubborn set of those shoulders—and felt something twist. Three weeks ago Pip had been a stranger humming a song that made Rosalind go white as bone-meal. Now the kid was lecturing him on the archaeology of wells like a scholar twice their age.

"You're right," he said. "Somebody dug it for somebody." He turned away before his voice could do anything embarrassing. "Come on. If there's a mapmaker's cottage, it'll be uphill. Mapmakers always live uphill. Something about liking the view and looking down on everyone."

Rosalind almost smiled. It was a thin thing, his smile these days, worn at the edges like an old coin, but Bram had decided—privately, stubbornly, against considerable evidence—that he was going to keep collecting them anyway.

* * *

The cottage was there, which surprised Bram more than its absence would have. Perhaps the Hollow ate villages whole and left oddments behind, the way a gull leaves the bones of a fish. Or perhaps—though he didn't say this aloud—the mapmaker had simply refused, in whatever way a man can refuse, to be forgotten.

The door wasn't locked. Inside, dust lay thick over everything except one table near the window, which was clean, and on which sat an inkwell, three pens, and a sheet of paper covered in fresh handwriting.

Ivet's hand went to her knife. "Someone's here."

"Or was," Rosalind said. "Recently."

The paper was a letter, unfinished, the ink not yet browned:

*To whoever finds this — if my hand has failed before my charts are done, look to the churchyard. Everything Auguste Vellin knew is buried with him, except the part of it that isn't, because I have it, and I am harder to kill than he was.*

Below that, the handwriting stopped mid-word.

"Well," said Bram. "That's either a warning or an invitation."

"With people like this it's usually both," said Ivet.

They found the churchyard behind the cottage, up a slope of long grass gone to seed. There was only one grave. That was wrong too—a village of four hundred didn't leave a single grave—but the stone was new-cut and the earth over it was dark and settled, months old at least.

**AUGUSTE VELLIN**, it read. **CHARTMAKER OF PLACES THAT NO LONGER EXIST. HE DREW WHAT HE LOVED UNTIL THE DRAWING WAS ALL THAT WAS LEFT.**

And sitting on the flat slab of the tomb, legs crossed, mending a pen nib with a small sharp knife, was a woman of perhaps thirty, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the gray-green of old copper, watching them with the unhurried assessment of someone who had already decided they weren't dangerous enough to be interesting.

"You took your time," she said.

"We didn't know we were coming," Bram said.

"That's generally how it works with the ones who find me." She went back to her nib. "You're the charm-seller from Corvale. Rosalind Dunmore. The Wardens have your name in red ink now, which in my father's ledgers meant *important*. The tall one is ex-Wardens—I can tell by how she stands, like she's expecting to be arrested by her own spine. The river man is Ferrow; you've the shoulders of someone who's hauled rope for twenty years and the face of someone who regrets it. And the small one—" she looked at Pip, and something in her expression shifted, softened, sharpened all at once— "the small one hums."

Pip stopped humming immediately.

"It's all right," the woman said. "My father used to say the Hollow had a song too. He said everything true does." She set down the nib and stood, brushing off her skirts. "Odile Vellin. You want a map. Everyone who climbs this hill wants a map. Sit down, all of you, and tell me which places you need to exist."

* * *

They sat in the cottage while Odile worked, and Bram decided within the first quarter hour that he liked her enormously and trusted her not at all, which was, he reflected, roughly how most people felt about him.

She unrolled charts across the table—dozens of them, drawn in fine confident lines, and every one of them impossible. Villages that criers had declared erased. Roads through country that official maps showed as blank cream paper. A whole nation's worth of places that existed nowhere except in ink and memory, rendered with such loving precision that Bram could smell woodsmoke off a chimney she'd drawn no bigger than a grain of rice.

"My father started it as a joke," Odile said, dipping her pen. "He'd chart a town the government struck from the rolls, just to spite them. Then the striking got worse. Then the towns started actually going." Her pen didn't waver. "He discovered something the Wardens would burn him for saying. The erasure isn't complete. Not ever. Something always remembers—a well, a grave, a song, a daughter. The Hollow takes the world, but it can't take what people carry. So he charted what people carried. And the strange thing—" she glanced up, and for the first time she looked tired, deep-down tired, the tiredness of years— "the strange thing is that where his charts are good enough, the places stay. Fainter than they were. But they stay."

"That's why your cottage is still here," Rosalind said softly. "You drew it."

"I drew it every morning for two years. Every beam, every crack in the plaster, every weed in the garden path. Father called it *anchoring*. Said a thing loved precisely enough becomes difficult to erase." She smiled without much humor. "Then last winter the Hollow came up the valley anyway, and he went out to meet it. He said somebody ought to look at it properly, since he was the only one with the training. He sat on the churchyard wall with his sketchbook and he drew it while it came." Her pen paused, just once, above the paper. "His last chart is of the Hollow itself. It's the only one I can't finish. I've tried forty times. My hand won't do it. I think—" she resumed drawing, briskly, as though the pause had never happened— "I think you have to understand a thing to draw it, and I refuse to understand it."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, wind moved through the grass where Vellin's Ford used to be, making a sound like a crowd remembering something.

It was Pip who broke the quiet, in the way of children, which is to say sideways and devastatingly. "Did it hurt him? When it took him?"

"No," Odile said. "That's the worst part. He wrote that it felt like being *included*." She looked at Pip a moment too long, then back at her work. "Now. Where do you need to go?"

"The Hollow's heart," Rosalind said. "The center of it. Whatever's left of the Gate."

Odile set down her pen.

For a long moment she studied Rosalind—not the way she'd studied the rest of them, cataloguing and dismissing, but slowly, carefully, the way she might study a coastline she intended to chart.

"You're the one who sealed it," she said. "Ten years ago. Before the ban, before any of this. My father corresponded with a circle of warders back then—people who kept the old border-wards alive after the Crown abandoned them. One of them signed his letters with a charm-seller's mark instead of a name, because names were already becoming dangerous." She tapped the corner of one chart, and Bram saw, stamped faintly in wax, a little device: a key crossed with a sprig of rowan. "Is that yours?"

Rosalind went very still. "Where did he get that?"

"He traded for it. Letters for maps. Your friend here—" a nod at Ivet— "would call it sedition. My father called it keeping accounts." She rolled out a fresh sheet of heavy paper, weighted its corners, and began, without further ceremony, to draw.

What emerged under her pen made Bram's chest hurt. It was Corvale, or the bones of it—the river, the bridges, the ring of the old city walls—but beyond the walls the country opened out, and at the center of the page, maybe six days' hard travel northeast, she drew a shape that had no right to be on any map: a slow spiral, tightening inward, coil upon coil, like a whirlpool drawn in ink, like a rose drawn by someone who hated roses.

"The Hollow's center," she said. "Every chart my father ever made agrees on it, and he made nine hundred. All the erased places arrange themselves around it. Villages, roads, rivers, stars—it's not eating randomly. It's spiraling home." She touched the innermost coil. "Here. Past the capital. Past Solmere's walls and his processions and his harvest-fields. The heart of the Hollow sits under the ruins of Aldermere."

Bram heard Rosalind stop breathing.

He'd heard a lot of sounds out of Rosalind Dunmore over the years—laughter once, long ago; grief often; lately a careful, rationed calm like a man spending his last coins. But this was new. This was the sound of a door being kicked open inside a man who'd braced it shut for a decade.

"Aldermere," Rosalind said. His voice came out sanded flat. "That's not possible. Aldermere was—"

"Erased ten years ago," Odile finished gently. "First of all of them. The price, my father's letters said, of the sealing. A whole village, paid into the dark so the Gate would close." She met Rosalind's eyes. "Your village. I'd wager my father's grave on it."

The silence stretched. Ivet looked between them, quick-eyed, adding things up. Pip reached over without looking and put one small hand on Rosalind's sleeve, the way you'd steady a lantern in wind.

"So the road runs home," Rosalind said at last, very quietly. "Of course it does. It always did. I just kept drawing the map wrong."

"Not wrong," Odile said. "Unfinished. There's a difference. My father used to say a map is just a promise that a place will still be there when you arrive." She began ruling the margins, swift and sure. "I'll come with you."

Bram blinked. "You'll—no offense, miss, but you're a mapmaker, not a—"

"A mapmaker whose entire life's work is keeping erased places alive by remembering them out loud?" Odile raised an eyebrow. "Ferrow. We are walking into the one country where that makes me the most heavily armed person you know." She blew across the wet ink. "Besides. Someone has to finish my father's last chart. And I've begun to suspect that the only way to understand the Hollow enough to draw it is to walk up to its front door and look it in the face."

"She's coming," Pip said, in the tone of a verdict.

"I hadn't agreed to be outvoted by a child," Bram said.

"Yes, you had," said Ivet. "You do it constantly. It's honestly your best quality."

* * *

They left at first light, five of them now, walking north along a road that Odile had redrawn overnight—because the original road, she explained cheerfully, had been eaten two springs ago, and the only way they'd cross the Marle hills was by telling the hills they were still there.

It worked. That was the mad part. It worked.

They walked in a loose knot and they talked—about the mill at Vellin's Ford, which Ivet invented plausible details for and Pip improved shamelessly; about a festival in a town none of them had ever seen, which Odile described from her father's notes until Bram could swear he remembered the smell of the frying cakes; and beneath their feet, mile after mile, the road held firm, pale and real, where every official map showed nothing but blank cream paper.

Bram walked at the rear, as he always did, keeping watch on the country behind them. And it was from the rear, in the last gray hour before dawn, that he saw it: high above the eastern hills, where no star should have been able to survive, a single point of light falling—slowly, gently, trailing silver like a lamp lowered on a string—down toward the dark fields ahead.

"Pip," he said. "Kid. You'll want to see this."

Ahead of them, small and quick, the child was already running toward the light, jam jar in hand.
