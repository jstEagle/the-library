## 9. Stars in a Bottle

Pip had decided, somewhere around the second week of running away from everything, that the world was mostly made of waiting.

Waiting for Bram to stop arguing with the river. Waiting for Ivet to stop sharpening things that were already sharp. Waiting for Rosalind to come back from wherever he went at night with his coat collar up and his face doing that thing where it looked like a door someone had locked from the inside.

Pip was very good at waiting. Pip had been an expert at it since before they could remember why. But even experts got bored, and boredom was how Pip found themselves crouched in the reeds at the edge of a drowned orchard at midnight, holding a jam jar, watching the sky fall.

Well. Watching one small piece of it fall.

It came down slow, slower than anything that big and bright had any right to move, hissing like a kettle as it dropped through the dark where stars used to be. The sky had been empty for weeks now — everyone said so, in the low frightened voices people used for saying true things — but this little bit of light hadn't gotten the message. It tumbled down past the black water, past the dead apple trees standing knee-deep in the flood, and landed in the mud not ten paces from Pip's boots with a sound like a spoon dropped in porridge.

Pip looked over their shoulder. The campfire was a smudge of orange behind the willows. Bram snored like a landslide. Nobody was coming.

"Right," Pip whispered, because important things always needed saying out loud, even when there was nobody to hear. "Don't be a monster. Be a *present*."

They crept forward. The mud sucked at their boots. The fallen thing lay in a little crater of steam, glowing gold-white, about the size of a plum — no, smaller than a plum, more like a crabapple, or one of those hard green sweets that Odile kept in her pocket and pretended she didn't have. It wasn't hot. Pip knew this because they did the sensible thing and poked it with a stick first, and the stick didn't catch fire or turn into a frog or anything, so then it was allowed.

Up close, the star-fragment hummed. Not a scary hum. A *friendly* hum, the kind of hum you did when you were working on something and it was going well. Pip knew that hum personally. They'd been told off for it in three different kitchens.

"Oh," Pip breathed. "You're like me."

The fragment pulsed, once, like a wink.

Pip unscrewed the jam jar — raspberry, mostly licked clean, because priorities — and scooped the little star inside with both hands. It weighed nothing at all and everything at once, which made no sense, but Pip had stopped expecting sense around the time the village of Marrow's Bend had walked out of every map and memory like a guest leaving early. They screwed the lid on tight, punched a few holes in it with a nail (the star didn't seem to need air, but it seemed polite), and sat back on their heels in the mud to admire their work.

A star. In a jar. In their lap.

"I'm keeping you," Pip informed it. "I hope that's all right."

The star glowed a little brighter, which Pip chose to take as yes.

* * *

The trouble with secrets is that they glow. Literally, if your secret is a star.

Pip managed to hide it until breakfast, which by Pip's standards was practically forever. They'd wrapped the jar in their spare shirt and tucked it under their arm like a loaf of bread nobody wanted, and they were sitting on the overturned rowboat eating burnt porridge and radiating innocence in every direction when Odile set down her tin cup and said, without looking up from her charts:

"Pip. What's in the shirt?"

"Nothing."

"You're humming."

"I always hum."

"Not that tune." Odile's pencil stopped moving. Her eyes came up, gray and sharp as a compass needle. "That's not a tune anyone taught you. That's the one you do without knowing. And it's changed key."

Pip looked at Rosalind for help. Rosalind, who could usually be counted on to rescue people from Odile's questions with a joke and a second helping of porridge, was staring at the bundle under Pip's arm with an expression Pip had only seen once before — the night the border wards broke, when he'd stood in the road with his hands half-raised toward a sky going dark, like a man trying to catch rain.

"Pip," Rosalind said quietly. "What have you got?"

And Pip, who had never in their short and much-relocated life been good at lying to people who fed them, unwrapped the shirt.

The jar caught the morning light and threw it back tenfold. Gold spilled across the rowboat, across the porridge, across four stunned faces, and inside the glass the little star turned slowly, lazily, like a cat deciding whether to stay.

Bram choked on his tea. "Is that — did you *catch* a star? You can't just *catch* a star, that's not — there are rules—"

"There's a hole in the lid," Pip offered helpfully. "For politeness."

"That's not — that's not how *any* of this works—" Bram ran both hands through his hair, which did nothing to organize it. "Stars don't fall anymore! That's the whole problem! The sky's been empty since the wards went, we've all been walking around under a ceiling with the candles blown out, and you're telling me one fell into the *reeds* and let you *pick it up*?"

"It didn't let me," Pip said. "It followed me. I think." They frowned, remembering. "It was humming my song first. Then I was humming its song. It's hard to say who started."

Rosalind hadn't moved. He was leaning forward now, slowly, the way you'd approach a bird you didn't want to frighten, and his face had gone strange and hungry and afraid all at once. "May I?" he asked Pip — asked, not took, which was one of the thousand small reasons Pip had decided to follow him out of the refugee camp and hadn't regretted it yet.

Pip handed him the jar.

The moment Rosalind's fingers closed around the glass, the star flared.

Not dangerously. More like recognition — the way a dog lights up when its person comes home after a long day. Light poured between Rosalind's fingers, and inside it Pip saw shapes: rooftops, a river, a bridge with lanterns strung along it, a market square full of people who moved like memories move, too smooth and too bright—

"What is that," Ivet whispered. She was on her feet, hand nowhere near her sword, which from Ivet was the same as screaming. "What am I seeing?"

"The world," Rosalind said. His voice had gone rough. "As it was. Before."

* * *

They carried the jar to the top of the levee, where the light wouldn't wake the other refugees, and they sat in a row like birds on a wire: Bram, Ivet, Odile, Rosalind, and Pip in the middle with the star balanced on their knees, because it was Pip's star and everyone understood this without discussion.

"Everyone touch the glass," Odile said. "All at once. If it's showing each of us something different, I want to know what."

"How d'you know it shows anything at all?" Bram grumbled. But he put his big scarred hand on the jar anyway, and Ivet put hers next to his, careful not to touch him, and Odile laid two fingers delicate as a moth, and Rosalind cupped the whole bottom of it in both palms like he was warming them at a fire.

Pip pressed their whole face against the side, because subtlety was for grown-ups.

The world inside the jar opened like a book.

There was a city — no, a *country*, Pip understood somehow, the way you understand things in dreams — rolling away in every direction under a sky absolutely *stuffed* with stars, thousands of them, tens of thousands, more stars than Pip had ever imagined existed, wheeling slow and silver over fields and forests and towns with smoke rising straight and contented from their chimneys. The Hollow Gate stood far off on the horizon, and it was — Pip squinted — it was *small*. Just a door in a hillside, mossy and ordinary, with wildflowers growing around the frame. Nothing humming. Nothing hungry. Just a door, shut the way doors are shut when there's nothing frightening behind them.

"Look," Odile breathed. "The coast road. My father charted that — it's *there*, all of it's there, the lighthouse at Corren's Teeth, the salt marshes—" Her voice cracked in the middle and she pretended it hadn't.

"There's Marrow's Bend," Ivet said softly. "On the border. Whole and lit. Every house." She swallowed. "My grandmother was born near there. I used to think the stories about the lantern festival were exaggerated."

"They weren't," said a voice, and everyone jumped, because it was Bram, and Bram never talked about before-things. He was staring into the jar with an expression Pip couldn't read at all, soft and wrecked at once. "There. The bridge with the blue lanterns. That's Grayford. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "I used to run cargo under that bridge. There was someone who used to wait on the bank with a lamp so I could find the mooring in the dark."

Nobody asked. Everybody understood.

Then Rosalind made a sound.

It was a small sound, barely a breath, but Pip was sitting right beside him and heard it plain, and turned, and saw his face gone white as milk. He wasn't looking at the country. He was looking at one small part of it — the hillside with the Gate, the wildflowers, the door — and at the figures standing before it.

There were seven of them. Seven people in traveling clothes, worn and muddy, arranged in a half-circle before the little mossy door, their hands raised together, light streaming from their palms into the wood. Sealing it. The moment of sealing, frozen mid-miracle inside the jar like a fly in amber.

"Seven keepers," Odile murmured. "So the old stories were true. Seven sealed the Gate after the Sundering—"

"No," Rosalind said.

His voice came out wrong. Flat and thin, like paper.

"No, that's not — those aren't the old stories' keepers. Look at the clothes. Look at the—" He lifted the jar closer to his face, and Pip watched him look, and watched whatever he saw go through him like a spear. "That's ten years ago. That's *us*. That's the night we—"

He stopped. His thumb was shaking against the glass.

Pip looked. Really looked, harder than they'd ever looked at anything, and the star — kind thing, generous thing — leaned into Pip's gaze and showed them clear.

Seven figures. And the one at the center, the one whose hands burned brightest, the one the others leaned toward like plants toward sun —

Was Rosalind.

Younger. Hair longer, tied back with a red cord. Face open in a way Pip had never seen it open, laughing even in the middle of the working, alive in a way that hurt to look at. And beside him, holding his left hand as the light poured through them both, a woman with gray in her braid and Bram's stubborn jaw, and beyond her five others whose faces blurred whenever Pip tried to fix on them, as if the jar itself couldn't bear to show what had become of them.

"Bram," Rosalind whispered. "Bram, don't—"

But Bram was already looking. Pip felt him go still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"That's Lira," Bram said. Very quietly. Very evenly. "That's my sister. On that hill. Holding your hand."

The fire crackled somewhere below them. The river muttered. Inside the jar, seven keepers held their impossible pose, and the little mossy door drank their light, and somewhere in the frozen instant a young man with a red cord in his hair was smiling like a person who believed the worst was over.

"You *knew* them," Bram said. "You knew all of them. You sealed it with them. Ten years ago. And you stood in my boat three weeks ago and told me you were nobody, a charm-seller, a man with no past worth the telling—"

"Bram—"

"*Who are the other four?*" Bram's voice cracked like ice. "Where are they *now*, Rosalind? Where's my sister *now*?"

Rosalind closed his eyes. And Pip, watching him, saw the answer arrive on his face before he said a word — saw it land and settle and become permanent, the way scars arrive.

"The Gate took its price," he said. "From all of us. From everything we—" His breath hitched. "Bram, I have to tell you something. All of you. About that night. About what sealing it *cost*."

He opened his eyes and looked at the jar, at the seven bright ghosts, at the woman with the red-corded young man's hand in hers.

"And I think," Rosalind said, "you'd best hear it before the Wardens find us. Because they've been following that light since sundown."

Ivet's head snapped up. Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the creak of oars and the jingle of chain.

Lots of chain.
