## 10. The Confession at Grayford Bridge

The bridge at Grayford was older than the country that had forgotten it, and it showed. Its stones were furred with moss the color of old coins, its parapets chipped by centuries of cart wheels and floods and one memorable incident involving a bull that Pip had demanded to hear about twice. Below it, the Grayford ran shallow and quick over pale stones, and above it—Rosalind checked, as he had checked every night for two weeks—the sky held no stars at all.

They made camp in the shadow of the bridge's eastern arch, where an old tollhouse leaned against the stonework like a drunk against a friend. Odile spread her father's charts on the tollhouse floor and weighted the corners with river pebbles. Ivet took first watch on the parapet, her warden's coat turned inside out so the gray lining faced the world instead of the silver. Bram built a fire small enough to hide behind a cupped hand and cooked a fish he had caught with a line, a hook, and an amount of profanity that Pip found educational.

"Say the one about the eel again," Pip said, mouth full.

"I've said it four times."

"The part where it goes up your sleeve."

Bram pointed the fish-turning stick at him like a duelist. "The eel went up my sleeve because your predecessor in this boat"—a glance at Rosalind, not entirely unkind—"panicked and dropped the net."

"I did not panic," Rosalind said. "I executed a strategic withdrawal from the net."

"You screamed like a kettle."

"A dignified kettle."

Pip laughed so hard he nearly fell into the fire, and Odile caught him by the collar without looking up from her charts, the way mothers and cartographers do, both professions requiring constant correction of things that drift off course.

Rosalind sat a little apart, his back to the tollhouse wall, and let the sound of them wash over him. This was the trouble with found families, he thought. They were warm. Warmth made you want to stay, and staying meant they would eventually learn what you were, and then the warmth would go out of them all at once, like the stars had gone out of the sky, and you would be left standing in the dark holding nothing but the truth.

He had been carrying the truth for ten years. It weighed exactly as much as a village.

* * *

It was Ivet who heard them first—a habit she hadn't managed to break, listening for boots the way other people listened for birdsong.

"Lanterns," she said quietly, from the parapet. "Four. Maybe five. Coming up the west road, walking easy." She dropped down inside the arch. "Wardens walk easy when they think they've already got you."

The fire was dead in three heartbeats; Bram smothered it with sand he kept for the purpose, and the smell of extinguished fish hung briefly in the dark like an apology. Odile rolled her charts with practiced speed. Pip pressed close to Rosalind's side, and Rosalind felt the child's hum start low in his chest—that faint, tuneless note he made without knowing it, the Hollow's song leaking through him like water through a cracked jug.

"Don't," Rosalind murmured. "Not now, little star."

"I can't help it." Pip's voice was very small. "It's excited."

That was worse than lanterns. That was infinitely worse than lanterns.

The Wardens came onto the bridge from the west, five of them, their silver-trimmed coats catching the last gray light of a moonless sky. At their center walked a woman Rosalind recognized even before the lantern swung: Warden-Captain Hesk, broad and patient as a headstone, who had once signed the paperwork that declared Rosalind Dunmore dead. She had been kind about it, in the way of people who are kind while doing terrible things efficiently.

She stopped at the middle of the bridge and did not draw her weapon. She didn't need to. Four wardens flanking her drew theirs.

"Rosalind Dunmore," she called across the water. Her voice carried the way voices carry at night over rivers, flat and clear. "Or whatever name you're using this decade. The ledger has you. Come out, and the child walks free. That's the offer. It won't improve."

Rosalind closed his eyes.

Behind him, in the dark under the arch, he heard Ivet's breath change—the sharp inhale of someone hearing their own order read aloud. He heard Bram shift, placing himself, without any discussion, between the bridge and Pip. He heard Odile whisper something that might have been a prayer or might have been coordinates.

And he understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the worst moments, that there was no way through this. Not with tricks, not with charms, not with the small dishonest magics of a charm-seller's life. Hesk had found them because the Hollow wanted them found, or because the country's hunger for scapegoats was a current stronger than any river, and either way the arithmetic was done.

There was only one thing left in his hands. It was heavy, and he had carried it too long, and it was time to set it down.

"Stay here," he told them softly. "All of you. Whatever happens."

Then he stepped out of the arch's shadow and walked toward the bridge, and the lantern light found him like a spotlight finding a man who has finally stopped running.

* * *

"Captain Hesk." He stopped at the near end of the bridge, hands loose at his sides, palms out—an old gesture, half surrender, half street-performer's flourish. Ten years ago it had been the second thing. Tonight it was only the first. "You're looking well. Prisons agree with you."

"Rosalind." Hesk's face didn't move, but something behind it did—a flicker, quickly banked. "Ten years, and you still can't be serious for a full sentence."

"One of us should be consistent." He glanced past her at the wardens. Young faces, mostly. Nervous. One of them couldn't have been more than nineteen, gripping his pike like it might bolt. "Five of you for one charm-seller. I'm flattered."

"We're not here for a charm-seller." Hesk's lantern rose slightly, throwing light past him, into the arch. "We're here for the Gate-keeper. The one who sealed it. The one whose signature is in our oldest books, written in a hand we've spent a decade trying to match." She lowered the lantern. "It matches, Rosalind. It always matched. We were waiting to see if you'd surface on your own."

The words landed in the silence like stones into deep water.

Behind him, under the arch, nobody moved. But he could feel them listening—Ivet rigid, Bram utterly still, Odile's breath gone careful—and he understood that the confession he had been rehearsing for ten years, in the privacy of his shop after midnight, in the pauses between customers, in every lonely hour of his chosen invisibility, was about to happen without his permission, dragged out of him by a stranger's lantern.

No. He would not have it that way. If they were going to learn what he was, they would learn it from him, in his own words, while he still had the courage to say them.

"Hesk," he said, "give me a moment. Not to run—you know I won't. To tell them the truth. They've earned it, and I've owed it long enough that interest has accrued."

Hesk considered. She was, in her stone way, a fair woman; fairness was simply another instrument of the law, and she played all her instruments well. "One moment," she said. "Then everyone comes with me. The child included. Solmere's orders."

The name went through Rosalind like cold water through cloth. *Solmere.* High Warden Solmere, whose harvest Ivet had described with shaking hands in the flooded chapel, whose column of chained mages they had struck on the remembered road, whose ambition was a wound wearing a uniform.

"Solmere's orders," Rosalind repeated. "Of course they are."

He turned around.

Under the arch, the four of them stood in the last of the dark, and he looked at each of them in turn, because a man paying a debt should look at the people he owes.

Ivet, arms crossed, jaw set, watching him with the expression of an apprentice who has already suspected the lesson and hates being right. Odile, one hand resting on her rolled charts as if they might need defending. Pip, wide-eyed, humming that thin thread of a note, his small hand finding the hem of Rosalind's coat and holding on.

And Bram. Bram Ferrow, smuggler, liar, keeper of drowned lanterns and older griefs, standing between the child and the bridge as if his body were a lock and love were the key that had rusted inside it ten years ago.

"Ten years ago," Rosalind said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved, "I sealed the Hollow Gate."

Nobody spoke. The river ran underneath them, quick and indifferent.

"There were six of us. Keepers—we weren't wardens, we were something older, something the wardens' order grew out of and forgot. The Gate was failing. It had been failing for years, and we knew it, and we hid that knowledge because the truth was unbearable." He swallowed. "A seal that size doesn't run on nothing. It runs on sacrifice. Not blood—not the cheap kind. It runs on *taking away*. You give it something whole, something loved, something real, and it consumes the fact of that thing so completely that even memory can't find the hole where it used to be."

Ivet's arms uncrossed slowly. "Rosalind—"

"The price was named," he went on, because if he stopped now he would never start again. "By lot. By the old rite. And the lot fell to me—to my home. Ashenmere. Forty-one souls. My mother's kitchen. The mill. The willow on the green where—" His voice caught, and he let it, because hiding the catch was how he'd gotten here. "Where children carved their names. Every soul in it, erased. Not killed. *Erased.* Unwritten from the world and from every mind that ever held it. There is no grave because there is no place. There is no mourning because there is no memory. I am the only person alive who remembers Ashenmere existed, and some days I'm not certain I trust myself."

The silence under the arch was total. Even the river seemed to hush.

"Why are you the only one?" Odile asked gently. "You said six keepers."

"Because the seal needs a living anchor. Someone who remembers, so the Gate knows what it ate. Someone who stays near it. Someone who—" He laughed, short and awful. "Someone who spends ten years selling lucky charms in a city full of people who'd unmake him if they knew, checking the wards every midnight, alone, because that was the sentence and I served it. Because duty is a door that only locks from the inside."

"That's not—" Pip's voice, high and fierce. "That's not fair."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing true is."

But he wasn't looking at Pip. He was looking at Bram, and Bram was looking at him, and between them lay a thing that had been lying between them since the day Rosalind vanished from the river landing without a word, without a note, without so much as a returned letter.

Because Bram had grown up in Ashenmere too. Bram's sister had carved her name in that willow.

"Bram," Rosalind said. "Mara. The lot—it fell on the whole village, but the anchor needed a name at its center, a name the Gate could hold onto, and the rite chose—" He stopped. Started again. "I didn't choose her. I want you to know that. I have spent ten years wanting you to know that, and I chose the coward's way of telling you, which was never telling you at all. The rite chose Mara Ferrow as the seal's keystone. And I let it stand. I stood in that circle and I spoke the words and I let it stand, because the alternative was letting the Hollow out, and I told myself that duty—" His breath shuddered. "Duty having won. Duty having won too long ago, and too completely, and it cost me everything including the right to explain myself to you, which is the least of what it cost *her*, and I am sorry. I am sorry in every language I know and a few I invented for the purpose."

Bram did not move.

The lantern light from the bridge reached the edge of the arch and stopped there, as if even it understood this was none of its business.

"Mara used to say," Bram said at last, and his voice was the flat, careful voice of a man handling something with broken edges, "that you'd talk your way out of anything. That you could charm the hinges off a door." He looked at Rosalind, and his eyes were dry, which was somehow far worse than tears. "She was right. Look at you. Ten years, and you're still talking."

"Bram—"

"You let me grieve her." Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "For ten years. You watched me pour lanterns off the stern for her every year on her birthday—drowned lanterns, Rosalind, do you know why I do that? Because there was no body. No grave. Nothing to put flowers on. So I sent light down into the water because it was the only direction left to send anything." His jaw worked. "And you stood there. On the boat. Holding the line. Letting me mourn my sister while you kept her bones in your pocket like a coin you were saving."

"I was keeping the Gate shut," Rosalind said. "Every hour of every day. If I had opened my mouth—"

"You'd have had company!" Bram's voice cracked open at last, loud enough that the youngest warden on the bridge flinched. "You'd have had *me*. You absolute, self-flagellating fool—you thought the choice was carry it alone or drop it? Some of us would have helped you carry it! Some of us *loved* you!"

The word rang under the arch and faded into the sound of the river.

Rosalind stood in it. He had imagined this moment a thousand times, and in none of those imaginings had Bram's anger hurt less, and in none of them had it been this bearable, because being hated by Bram Ferrow for the truth was a warmth compared to being loved by him for a lie.

"I know," Rosalind said quietly. "That was the cruelest part. That I knew."

From the bridge, Hesk's voice, not unkind: "Time, Rosalind."

He nodded. He turned back to his family—Ivet and Odile and Pip, who were looking at him the way people look at a landscape after an earthquake, trying to see what still stands.

"Ivet. The ledger. Burn it or use it, but don't let Solmere harvest a single name from it. Odile—keep drawing. Places only exist if somebody insists. Pip—" He crouched, and took the boy's small face in his hands. "Little star. When it hums, you listen. But remember: you are not its song. You're the one hearing it. That's different. That's everything."

Pip threw his arms around Rosalind's neck and held on with the desperate strength of small children, and Rosalind held him back and did not let himself memorize it, because memorizing was how the Gate worked, and he refused to give it anything else tonight.

He straightened. He stepped out from under the arch, into the lantern light, and walked toward the bridge and the waiting wardens.

"Rosalind." Hesk met him at the bridge's foot, and behind her the young wardens shifted, uncertain. "You understand I have orders. Solmere wants the Gate-keeper alive. What happens after that isn't mine to—"

"I understand," Rosalind said. "Better than you do, I'm afraid."

He offered his wrists. Hesk hesitated—ten years of professional courtesy flickering across her stone face—and then bound them with warden's cord, which bit with the particular magic of instruments made to hold people who could otherwise leave.

They were halfway across the bridge when the sound came from behind them.

Not shouting. Not Bram's voice at all. A single, clear, rising note—Pip's hum, breaking free of his chest and climbing into the air like a bird released, and beneath the arch the darkness *moved*, the shadows folding inward around the tollhouse, around the family, around the space where Rosalind had been, and Hesk spun with her lantern raised and shouted an order that dissolved into the hum.

Through the cord's bite and the wardens' sudden chaos, Rosalind saw it: the dark under Grayford Bridge thickening, gathering, opening—not the Hollow itself, not yet, but its breath, its attention, drawn by a child's song to the exact spot where a confession had finally been spoken aloud.

And in the middle of that gathering dark, Bram Ferrow picked up his pack, took one last look at the bridge—at Rosalind, bound and lit and helpless—and turned, and walked into the black alone.

"Bram!" Rosalind pulled against the cord until his wrists bled. "*Bram!*"

Bram didn't turn around. The dark closed behind him like water over a sunk lantern, and the last thing Rosalind saw of him was the set of his shoulders: not fleeing, Rosalind realized. Walking toward something. The way a man walks when he has finally decided where his sister's grave is, and intends to visit it.

The hum faded. The dark settled. Hesk's grip tightened on his arm, and the bridge carried them onward, west, toward the capital, toward Solmere, toward whatever waited at the end of a road paved with names—

—and under the arch, small and fierce and unafraid, Pip began to follow.
