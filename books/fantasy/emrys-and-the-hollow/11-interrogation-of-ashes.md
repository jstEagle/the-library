## 11. Interrogation of Ashes

They took her irons off in the antechamber, which was the first thing Ivet Kess understood to be wrong.

She had been a Warden's apprentice for six years. She knew procedure the way other people knew their own hands. Prisoners of any consequence were brought before a magistrate with their bindings intact, catalogued, and witnessed by two officers of rank. There was no antechamber in the Corvale Wardenhouse. There was a corridor, a duty desk, and cells that smelled of lye and old cabbage.

This room had carpet.

It was gray, fine-woven, and it swallowed the sound of her boots, and Ivet stood on it with her wrists newly bare and thought: *this is not an interrogation. This is an audience.*

The room itself was wrong too. The Wardenhouse was built of honest stone, but this chamber's walls were paneled in dark wood she didn't recognize, wood that seemed to drink the lamplight rather than hold it. There were no windows. There was a single door behind her, now closed, and a long table at the far end where a man sat alone with a cup of tea.

High Warden Solmere did not look up when she entered. He poured a second cup from a small porcelain pot, set it on the table across from him, and waited, as though she were a guest who had merely arrived late to an appointment she herself had requested.

"Sit," he said.

Ivet did not sit. "Where are my companions?"

"Sit," Solmere said again, mildly, "and I will tell you something true. You've had very little truth lately, I think. Your mentor gave you procedure instead. Procedure is a kind of lie we tell in bulk."

That landed harder than it should have. Master Ellery's face rose unbidden — the bonfire in the neighbor's yard, spellbooks curling like autumn leaves, his expression neither cruel nor kind, just *procedural*. She sat down. Not because he'd earned it. Because her legs wanted a decision made for them.

The tea was blackleaf, unsweetened. Her favorite. Nobody should have known that.

"You ran," Solmere said. "You stole a ledger of the condemned, you consorted with a registered mage, and you crossed three districts in the company of a smuggler and two unregistered gifts." He said it all without accusation, the way one might read a shopping list. "I want to be clear that none of that is why you're here."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because you were the best of them." He looked up then, and his eyes were pale gray, the color of ash after the fire has gone through. "Because when I read your file — and I have read every file, Apprentice Kess, every one — yours was the only one that made me pause. You flagged irregularities in the Third District census that no one asked you to flag. You filed a formal objection to the burning of a nine-year-old's primer. You lost the objection." A thin smile. "You kept filing them."

"I filed four."

"Five. The fifth was intercepted. By me." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I want you to understand that I am not your enemy, Ivet. I am the only person in this building who has never lied to you. So let me ask you plainly: what do you think the purge is?"

Ivet's mouth was dry. "Panic," she said. "The wards fell. The stars went out. People need someone to blame, so they blame the gifted. It's happened before. It'll pass, or it won't, but it isn't—"

"It isn't what?"

"*Necessary.*"

Solmere nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something for him. "Yes. That's what I believed too, once. Sit there a moment."

He rose and went to the paneled wall, and Ivet watched him press his palm flat against the dark wood, and the wood — *opened*, folding inward like a page being turned — and cold air came out of the space beyond, air that smelled of river silt and something older underneath, something like a cellar that had never seen light.

"Come and look," Solmere said. "You may keep your distance. Nothing in there can touch you through me."

Every instinct she had left said stay in the chair. She went anyway, because she had spent six years learning that fear was information, and the information right now was that she would rather know than be safe.

Beyond the folded wall was a gallery. Long, low-ceilinged, lit by lamps that burned without flame. And along both walls, in niches cut into the stone, stood jars.

Not jars. Vessels — some glass, some clay, some things she had no name for, veined with a dull silver like frost inside a windowpane. Each one held a faint light. Each light was different: one gold and steady, one flickering green, one the deep bruised violet she had only ever seen in the ledgers' margin-notes beside the word *dangerous*.

"What are they?" she whispered.

"Names," said Solmere. "Or near enough. Every mage taken in the purge passes through this room before the records say they were 'relocated.' Their gift is drawn out — gently, I assure you; the process is painless, which is more than most of them deserve — and stored. The body goes on to whatever story we write about it. Fever. Flight. Drowning." He walked the row slowly, hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring his own orchard. "Do you know what the Hollow eats, Ivet? Everyone assumes it eats places. Towns. Roads. Stars." He stopped before a vessel of swirling white light and rested two fingers on the glass, almost tenderly. "It eats memory. And a gift is nothing but memory — the memory magic has of the world, lodged in a person. Feed it enough gifts and it quiets. It sleeps. It stays *sealed*."

The floor seemed to tilt. "The Wardens have been feeding it mages."

"The Wardens have been feeding it mages since before your grandmother was born," Solmere said. "There were eleven vessels in this gallery when I inherited it. There are four hundred and six now. The purge isn't panic, child. The purge is a *harvest*, and it is the single reason this country still exists on the maps." He turned to face her fully. "And when the new moon comes, I will open the Gate myself, pour everything in this room into the wound at once, and end the hunger forever. No more hiding. No more ledgers. No more burnings." His voice softened into something almost gentle. "No more of you having to choose between your conscience and your oath. I'm going to burn the whole rotten system down and build something quiet in its place. Someone has to rule what remains. It may as well be someone who understands the cost."

"You're going to rule," Ivet said. Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking through her. "Over a country you've hollowed out."

"Over a country I've *saved*."

"They're people." She gestured at the gallery, at the four hundred and six small lights, and her hand was shaking and she let it shake. "They're not fuel. They had names. That woman—" she pointed at the white light, "—that's not a *vessel*, that's Maren Ostwick, she sold bread on Tallow Street, I interviewed her daughter last spring over an unlicensed charm, she was *kind to me*—"

"Maren Ostwick's gift was killing her," Solmere said, unmoved. "It would have eaten her alive within the year. I gave her peace and purpose. You may thank me at the harvest moon."

Something in Ivet went very quiet then. Not calm — quiet the way a house goes quiet just before the roof beam cracks. Six years of oath. Six years of believing the burnings were grief misapplied, that the order was harsh but honest, that somewhere above her the machinery was clumsy rather than hungry. Master Ellery's face at the bonfire. The ledger in her stolen satchel, four hundred names in her own neat apprentice's hand, each one a line item.

She had thought she was defecting from cruelty.

She had been defecting from *accounting*.

"I see," she said.

"Do you?" Solmere studied her, head tilted. "I don't think you do yet. But you will. That's why you're here, Ivet. I'm not punishing you. I'm offering you the gallery. When I walk through the Gate, someone must remain who knows what was paid. Someone with a conscience, even — *especially* — a conscience. Rule with me. Keep the records honestly, for once in this order's history." He extended his hand. "You were the best of them. Be the best of what comes next."

Ivet looked at his open hand for a long moment.

Then she looked past it, at the vessels, and did the thing she had trained herself never to do in six years of warden discipline: she reached out and touched one.

The glass was warm. The light inside flared at her contact — the gold one, third niche from the end — and something moved in it, quick and eager, like a bird pressing against a window. And through her fingertips, faint as a voice through a wall, came a sensation she had no vocabulary for: *recognition*. The gift in the jar knew what she was. Had always known. Was glad of her, the way a dog is glad.

Her eyes stung. "It likes me," she said stupidly.

"Gifts recognize their own," Solmere said. "Take your hand off it. That one's scheduled."

*Scheduled.* She pulled her hand back as if burned. The gold light dimmed, dimmed, dimmed — and settled into a slow pulse, patient, waiting, like a heart deciding to keep beating anyway.

Ivet turned back to Solmere, and found that the shaking had stopped.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"You asked me what I think the purge is. Here's what I think *you* are. You're a man who found a machine that eats people and called it mercy because it made his ledgers balance." She stepped back from him, toward the chair, toward the door, buying distance with words because words were the only weapon left in the room. "My mentor taught me procedure. You're right — it was a lie in bulk. But you taught me something better tonight, High Warden. You taught me exactly what happens when someone decides the cost is theirs alone to weigh. You don't get my conscience. It's spoken for."

Solmere sighed, and it was the sigh of a teacher whose best student has failed an easy exam. "I was afraid you'd say that."

He snapped his fingers.

The wall unfolded. Two wardens stepped through — not the corridor guards she'd known, but big, quiet men in undyed wool, the kind of men who existed in no roster she'd ever seen. They took her arms with professional gentleness, which was somehow worse than violence.

"Cell her below," Solmere said, turning back to his tea. "Not the main blocks. And Ivet—" he glanced over his shoulder, ash-gray eyes almost kind, "—don't take it personally. In a fortnight none of us will remember our names anyway. I'm doing you the courtesy of meaning it kindly while kindness is still on offer."

They marched her down. Past the gallery, past the folded wall, down stairs that grew damper and older until the paneling gave way to raw stone and the lamps gave way to nothing at all, guided by the wardens' grip alone. They put her in a cell with a straw pallet and a bucket and a door of iron-barred oak, and locked it, and their footsteps faded upward, and the dark came in around her like water.

* * *

She did not cry. She wanted to note that, for the record, wherever the record was kept now. She sat on the pallet with her back against the stone and she did not cry, though her throat worked at it for a long time, and what finally came out instead — quietly, involuntarily, into the black — was laughter. Small, cracked, disbelieving laughter, the kind that escapes a person when the alternative is worse.

Four hundred and six lights. *Scheduled.*

Maren Ostwick, who had pressed a heel of bread into Ivet's hands at the end of that interview and said, *you look underfed, dear, don't tell anyone I did that.*

Scheduled.

She pressed her palms against her eyes until the darkness went red. Think. Think like a warden. What do you have?

She had a cell. She had a body that was tired and bruised and fed, annoyingly, with decent stew — Solmere wanted her healthy, wanted her *convertible*. She had six years of training. And she had—

She went still.

She had touched the gold light. It had recognized her. *Gifts recognize their own.*

Which meant—

Which meant the thing she had spent six years enforcing against, the thing she had helped drag neighbors in for, the thing she had filed five objections about because some part of her had always, always known — which meant it lived in her too. Small, maybe. Dormant, maybe. But the jar had *known* her, and jars do not make mistakes.

Ivet Kess sat in the dark of a Wardenhouse cell and began, very carefully, to laugh again — and this time it wasn't despair. It was the laugh of a woman reading her own arrest warrant and finding her name spelled correctly.

"All right," she whispered to the dark. "All right, then."

Now. What did she have?

She had a pallet. Straw, bound with twine. She had a bucket, tin. She had a belt — they'd left her belt, another sign this was an audience and not an imprisonment, another courtesy she intended to repay with interest. She had the hairpin holding her braid together, because apparently nobody had thought a warden's apprentice dangerous enough to search properly, and the sheer institutional arrogance of that nearly made her laugh a third time.

Lockpick, shim, garrote, and — if the stories Bram told were true and half of them were — a tool for persuading stubborn locks with violence. She laid her small inventory out on the straw and got to work on the door lock with the pin, and it was slow, and it was humiliating, and twice the pin bent and she had to straighten it against the floor stones with her teeth.

But here was the thing about six years of warden training: she knew these locks. She had *installed* half of them during the summer refits, third district rotation, the year she'd been praised for her thoroughness. She knew the lower cells used the Corvale pattern, lever tumblers, left-handed, prone to sticking in damp weather.

And the lower cells were damp tonight. She could feel it in the stones. Could feel it the way she suddenly realized she could feel a great many things — the water in the walls, the iron in the door, the slow cold attention of something vast turning over beneath the city like a sleeper shifting under blankets.

The Hollow. Underneath her. All along, underneath everything, and the Wardens had built their house directly on top of the wound and called it a fortress.

*Gifts recognize their own,* she thought, and closed her eyes, and did the thing she had watched Rosalind do a dozen times without understanding: she stopped gripping, and listened.

The lock was iron. Iron was ore once. Ore had been stone, and stone had been sea-floor, and the sea-floor remembered being soft. She didn't ask the lock to open. She reminded it that it had ever been anything other than a lock — and felt, distinct as a key turning, the levers lift.

The door swung inward with a creak that sounded, in the silence, like a shout.

Ivet stood in the doorway of her own cell, trembling, her hands full of nothing but warmth, and understood that there was no going back to any life she had known. Not the order. Not the law. Not the girl who filed objections and believed the machinery above her was merely clumsy.

Fine. Institutions had failed her. Institutions could fail without her.

People, though. People were another matter. A charm-seller with guilty eyes. A smuggler who loved loudly and forgave slowly. A child who hummed songs that frightened soldiers. Those were worth faith. Those had *always* been worth faith.

She crept up the stairs, past the gallery — and stopped. One breath. Two. Then she slipped inside, went straight to the third niche from the end, and lifted the gold vessel into her arms. It pulsed against her chest like a second heartbeat, eager, trusting, *hers* somehow, though she couldn't have said how.

"Hold on, Maren," she whispered. "I'm getting you out of here. All of you, if I can. But you first."

The gallery door opened onto the corridor. Above her, the Wardenhouse slept, four hundred and five lights waiting for the new moon. Somewhere beyond the walls, the city lay starless under the Hollow's hunger, and somewhere south of the city, on a flooded road she could not yet imagine, three people were probably worrying about her — or worse, blaming themselves, which was the same thing wearing different clothes.

Ivet shifted the vessel into the crook of one arm, drew the hairpin with her free hand, and started for the doors.

She had a name to answer for, a master's betrayal to outlive, and a family — she let herself think the word, finally, *family* — waiting for her to come home.

The first guard rounded the corner before she was ready.

He saw her. She saw him. And the gold light in her arms flared bright as dawn.
