## 12. The Market of Unmade Things

Bram Ferrow had smuggled a great many things down the Graywater in his day: untaxed brandy, fugitive wardens' ledgers, one memorable season of illegal peacocks. He had never before smuggled his own temper, and it was proving the hardest cargo yet.

It sat in his chest like ballast he couldn't jettison. *You sealed the Gate and let them erase my sister's whole world to pay for it.* That was what he'd shouted at Rosalind on the bridge at Grayford, and then he'd walked into the dark alone, exactly as dramatic as he'd always sworn never to be.

Three days later, here he was, standing in a flooded quarry outside the drowned town of Marrow's Bend, haggling with a woman wearing a candlestick as a hat pin.

"Welcome to the Market," she said, "of Unmade Things."

* * *

He'd found it the way everyone found it: by getting lost on purpose. The market moved, the smugglers said—drifted from quarry to cellar to dry riverbed like a fish that had learned to walk. You didn't go to it. You stopped knowing where you were, and it took pity on you.

Bram knew the trick from river work. Sail into fog without a destination and sometimes the fog gave you one. So he'd walked out of the refugee camp at dusk, past the sentries who pretended not to see him, and let his feet forget themselves. By full dark he was descending stone steps that hadn't been there an hour ago, following lantern-light and the smell of woodsmoke and old paper, into a cavern where several hundred people were trading in ghosts.

The stalls were made of salvaged doors laid across trestles. The goods upon them defied sense: jars of rain that had fallen on villages that no longer fell anywhere; a child's shoe, single, polished with care; bundles of letters addressed to houses that had never been built, or had been built and then un-built, along with their street, their town, and the memory of both in every living head but these.

That was the trade, Bram understood slowly. When the Hollow ate a place, it ate the remembering too. But some things resisted. Objects soaked in love, maybe. Or grief. They surfaced afterward—washed up on riverbanks, turned up in plowed fields, sold by farmers who couldn't say why they kept a key that fit no lock they knew—and the market gathered them, and people came to buy back pieces of lives they could no longer name.

Everyone here had the same look. Not weeping. Past weeping. It was the look of people doing arithmetic with what remained.

"You're staring," said the candlestick-hat woman. She was small, sixty-odd, with forearms like a dockworker and eyes that missed nothing. "First time?"

"That obvious?"

"You've got the face. Everyone gets the face." She stuck out a hand. "Maren Quill. I run the salvage end."

"Bram Ferrow." Her grip could have cracked walnuts. "I'm told you can find things. Things from places that—" He stumbled over it. Ten years a smuggler and he couldn't finish a sentence.

"Places that aren't anymore?" Maren said briskly. "Aye. Can't promise. But show me what you've got to trade and tell me what you're after, and we'll see what the market says."

"I don't want anything for myself."

"Nobody does. That's how I know you're lying." She jerked her chin toward a stall deeper in. "Come on. Walk first. Looking's free. Buying costs whatever the thing thinks you owe."

"The thing *thinks*—"

"Everything here remembers being loved, love. Loved things have opinions about price." She set off through the crowd, and Bram followed, because what else was there.

* * *

The market had its own weather. Lanterns hung on wires strung between stalactites, and beneath them the crowd murmured in the low, careful voices of people in a sickroom. A man at one stall was buying back a wedding ring—a plain gold band, nothing special—and the stallkeeper was asking him not money but a story.

"Tell me her name," the stallkeeper said. "That's the coin. Tell me her name and one true thing about her, loud enough for the ring to hear."

"Serah," the man said, and his voice cracked like river ice. "Serah. She laughed at her own jokes before she got to the end of them. Every time. Even the bad ones. *Especially* the bad ones."

The ring, sitting on its square of velvet, seemed to brighten. Bram would have sworn it brightened. The stallkeeper wrapped it in cloth and pressed it into the man's hands, and the man walked away holding it against his chest like a bird with a broken wing.

Bram stood very still.

Ten years ago, Rosalind Dunmore had kissed him on a bridge not unlike Grayford Bridge and said, *There's something I have to do, and if it works you'll never know I did it.* Then he'd vanished so completely that Bram had spent two years telling himself the man was dead, because dead was easier than chosen-elsewhere. And now Bram knew: Rosalind had gone off to seal a wound in the world, and the price of thread had been an entire village—Harrowmere, Bram's mother's birthplace, where his cousin Sera had raised goats and laughed at her own jokes—

No. That wasn't right. Serah had been Harrowmere. His cousin. Goats. He was almost sure. The details slid like wet fish. That was the horror of it; even the grief came to him secondhand, pre-erased, and he had to take it on faith.

But the ring-man's grief was whole and present and loud, and hearing it, Bram felt his own anger shift—not lessen, exactly, but turn over, like a boat righting.

* * *

"Here." Maren stopped at a stall stacked with hardware. Hinges, latches, lamp brackets, a drawer pull shaped like an acorn. Ordinary iron, except that each piece sat in its own little pool of attention, the way valuable things do. "Salvage from the eastern erasures. Harrowmere way."

The word went through him like a hook.

"How much for—" He couldn't say it. He pointed instead, at a doorknob near the back. Brass, gone green at the base, with a scratch across it shaped like a crescent moon.

The stallkeeper, a gaunt fellow with ink-stained fingers, looked at the knob, then at Bram, and frowned. "Odd. This one's been marked twice. Once by whoever loved it first, once by—" He tilted his head. "Once by someone passing through, three days back. Tall fellow. Sad hands. Asked me to hold it."

Bram's heart performed a maneuver no heart should attempt. "Sad hands."

"You know the type. Hands that apologize." The stallkeeper turned the knob over. "Said someone might come looking. Said if they did, tell them—" He squinted, retrieving it. "Tell them *the latch still lifts.*"

The quarry seemed to tilt. Bram gripped the trestle.

Rosalind. Three days ago. Which meant that while Bram had been stomping around the refugee camp rehearsing grievances, Rosalind had come here—to this market, this exact stall—and left a message inside a piece of Harrowmere, like a man leaving flowers on a grave he expected to be cursed at.

"What did he trade for the holding-fee?" Bram managed.

"That's the strange part." The stallkeeper leaned in. "Nothing. He offered, mind. Had coin, had stories, had a knife I'd have taken. But the knob wouldn't price him. Just sat there glowing faint when he touched it, which they don't do for strangers. So I held it gratis, and he wrote his message, and he left walking like a man carrying a piano."

Maren made a sound beside him. Bram turned. She was studying him with her dockworker eyes, and there was something almost gentle in them now.

"Ferrow," she said. "Whoever the sad-hands fellow is—he's the reason you came."

"He's the reason I came to shout at somebody," Bram said. "Shouting's off. I don't know what's on."

"Mm." Maren picked up the doorknob, weighed it in her palm like a judge weighing a verdict, and handed it to him. "Then buy it. Not for him. For you. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"And the price?"

She nodded at the stallkeeper. "Ask it proper."

Bram cleared his throat. Felt ridiculous. Did it anyway, because the whole night had been ridiculous and one more absurdity wouldn't drown him.

"What do I owe you?" he asked the doorknob.

The market noise seemed to dip. The stallkeeper's eyebrows rose. Maren went quiet. And the knob—warm suddenly, warmer than iron-and-brass had any business being—gave up its answer, not in words but in knowing, the way you know a tune's next note:

*A door opened for you once. Open one back.*

"That's it?" Bram said aloud. "That's the whole price?"

"They're usually shorter than you fear and harder than you think," Maren said. "Pay it somewhere it counts."

He paid the stallkeeper in honest coin anyway—the man protested, Bram insisted, old smuggler's pride—and then he stood there holding a green-splotched brass doorknob from a village that had never existed, from a house that had never existed, that his cousin Sera had maybe painted yellow, maybe, he thought, he chose to think, because someone ought to remember the color of the damn house even if the color itself was gone.

And under the anger, under ten years of hurt, something shifted its weight and settled. Not forgiveness. Forgiveness was a country he hadn't entered yet. But this was the border. This was the checkpoint. Forgiveness beginning as an act of will—which meant deciding, here, now, holding a dead house's front door in his hand, that Rosalind Dunmore had carried enough. That the piano-walk was punishment enough. That somebody in this miserable erasing world had better start opening doors, and it might as well be a smuggler, since smugglers were professionally good at it.

"Ferrow," Maren said. "Your face is doing something complicated."

"It does that."

"Good. Complicated faces are how this market stays in business." She steered him toward the steps. "Now get out. We close before dawn, and the stairs only go up for people who've finished their grieving, and you look half-finished at best."

"Half-finished," Bram repeated. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me all year."

* * *

The stairs did go up. They went up into a plowed field under a starless sky, and behind him the quarry mouth was just a quarry mouth, dark and ordinary, though he could hear, faintly, the murmur of several hundred people trading in ghosts.

He walked until the camp fires showed, then stopped on a rise and took out the doorknob and turned it over in the light of the moon-that-wasn't.

*The latch still lifts.*

Three days ago. Which meant Rosalind had come here straight from Grayford Bridge, straight from Bram shouting at him and walking away—had come here, to the wreckage of the place he'd paid to save the world, and left a message for the man who hated him, in case the man who hated him ever came looking.

That was either guilt or love, and Bram had known Rosalind long enough to know it was both, and that the ratio was the whole tragedy.

He also noticed, turning the knob, the crescent-moon scratch, and remembered—suddenly, wholly, a gift the Hollow hadn't managed to steal—being nine years old, visiting Harrowmere, watching Cousin Sera chase a goat across her own threshold while little Rosalind-of-all-people—no, not Rosalind, a neighbor boy, some kid—no. Actually. Yes. A skinny dark-haired boy who visited every summer, who'd scratched a moon into her doorknob with a nail because he'd read somewhere that marks kept doors loyal. Sera had scolded him. The boy had apologized with his sad hands. And Bram, fourteen and already hopeless, had watched him apologize and thought, *oh no.*

The memory arrived whole, unasked-for, the first piece of Harrowmere the Hollow had ever given back. Bram sat down hard in the cold dirt and laughed until it turned into the other thing, and let it, because there was nobody to see, and because a man can only smuggle so much.

When he was done, he stood, pocketed the knob, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Grayford.

If the market existed—if erased places left salvage, if salvage remembered love—then the erasure wasn't total. Things leaked. And if things leaked, then records leaked, and if records leaked, then somewhere in that quarry, in Maren Quill's salvage ledgers, there might be proof of what Harrowmere's unmaking had actually bought. Rosalind had claimed thousands lived because the Gate closed when it did. Bram had called it a liar's arithmetic.

Time to check the sums.

He found Maren's stall again by letting his feet forget themselves a second time—easier now, the market apparently liking him—and asked, blunt as a barge pole, whether she kept accounts of where salvage surfaced and when.

"Every piece," she said. "Why?"

"Because I want to see the dates around the eastern erasures. Ten years back. All of them."

Maren looked at him a long moment, then hauled a ledger off a shelf, blew dust off it, and turned pages with a dockworker's efficiency. "Harrowmere went first. You'll want the flood years after. Here." She spun the book toward him. "Read the column on the right. Salvage volume. Every erased place sheds some. Except."

Except. The column after Harrowmere's date dropped to nearly nothing. Village after village, town after town, all along the eastern reach—places that should have washed up spoons and shingles and wedding rings for decades—and the market had received barely a trickle. As if the erasure had been cut short. As if a wound, mid-devouring, had been cinched shut.

"Hollow got interrupted," Maren said quietly, reading his face. "We used to argue about it. Old-timers swore the eastern erasure just... stopped, one night, all at once, like a tide going out backwards. Never knew why." She tapped the ledger. "Whatever your sad-hands friend did, Ferrow, he did it *early*. Everything east of the Graywater that's still on anybody's tongue—that's still anywhere at all—it exists because he ran out the clock."

Bram stared at the numbers until they blurred.

Thousands. Rosalind had said thousands, and Bram had wanted to hit him for saying it, and here it was in a stranger's handwriting: a column of almost-nothing where a decade of ghosts should have been.

He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"Maren. If a man wanted to send word upriver—Corvale way, or wherever a certain sad-handed fool is hiding—how fast could a smuggler move it?"

Her grin was all teeth. "Depends how much he's willing to owe me."

* * *

By gray dawn Bram stood at the camp's edge with a doorknob in one pocket and a message in another, watching the river run south toward Corvale, toward Ivet Kess and Pip and Odile and the rest of his improbable, load-bearing family.

The message read: *Found the knob. Checked your sums. You're still an idiot who walks away from bridges. Come to the flooded chapel at Tarn Mill—we'll be there by the new moon. Bring your sad hands. — B.*

He gave it to a eel-fisher heading downstream, paid double, and watched the boat shrink into the mist.

Then he touched the knob through his coat, once, the way you touch a charm, and started walking north, because forgiveness, he was learning, wasn't a feeling you waited for. It was cargo. And cargo didn't move itself.
