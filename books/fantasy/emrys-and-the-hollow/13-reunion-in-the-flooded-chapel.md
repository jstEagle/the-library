## 13. Reunion in the Flooded Chapel

The chapel had drowned a hundred years before Rosalind was born, when the river Ferrow's people called the Liss decided it preferred the low ground to the high and moved accordingly. Its bell tower still stood above the waterline like a man wading out to his chest, and its roof had long since gone to herons and moss, but the nave itself lay just beneath the surface—arches and pillars standing in six feet of green-gold water, light coming down through the broken ceiling in slow, wobbling coins.

Rosalind sat on the altar, which was the only dry-ish place left, his boots off and his feet dangling over the flood, watching fish investigate the hem of his coat.

It was, he reflected, not the worst place he'd ever hidden. There was something honest about a flooded chapel. The god it had been built for was long gone—no one remembered which one—and what remained was architecture doing its best: stone still holding up stone, arches still arching, even with no congregation left to admire them. He found that he envied it. Ten years of hiding, and he had never once managed to be so useful while so forgotten.

Pip sat beside him on the altar, legs swinging, humming.

Rosalind had stopped flinching at the humming. That was new. For weeks the sound had crawled up his spine like cold water—the Hollow's song wearing a child's voice—but somewhere on the road he had begun to hear it differently. Pip didn't hum it the way the Hollow meant it. Pip hummed it the way you might whistle a sad song your grandmother used to sing, without knowing the words, keeping it company.

"You're staring at me," Pip said, not looking up from the jar in their lap. The star-fragment inside glowed faintly, like a firefly deciding whether to bother.

"I'm watching the water," Rosalind said. "You happen to be between me and the water."

"Liar." Pip said it comfortably, the way other children said *pass the bread*. "Ivet's been gone two days. You keep looking at the door like it owes you money."

"The door doesn't owe me money."

"Everything owes somebody money. Bram says so."

"Bram," Rosalind said, "has been saying that since before you were born, and it has never once been true."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "So it's a lie he keeps telling because he likes how it sounds."

Rosalind opened his mouth, closed it again. Out of the mouths of orphans. "Yes," he admitted. "I expect it is."

"Like when Bram says he's not worried about Ivet."

Rosalind looked at him. Pip looked back, guileless as an egg.

"He's been pacing the bell tower for two days," Pip said. "He says he's checking the sightlines. You can't check sightlines in a chapel, there aren't any, it's all walls and water. He's worried about Ivet."

"Yes," Rosalind said quietly. "I know."

* * *

Bram came down from the tower at dusk, and if his face said *sightlines*, everything else said *two days of pretending otherwise*. He dropped onto the altar steps across from Rosalind, produced a flask, drank, offered it, and watched Rosalind refuse it with the expression of a man who had catalogued every refusal and filed none of them away.

"Water's rising," Bram said. "Half a finger since morning. River's restless."

"Rivers usually are, this time of year."

"This river isn't. This river's got opinions." He turned the flask in his hands. "Odile says the old roads south of here have started going soft. Not erased—soft. Like bread left in milk. Places flickering. She charted a bridge yesterday that was there at dawn and a memory by noon."

Rosalind felt the words land in his chest, heavy and familiar. "That's the Gate breathing harder. When it opens fully, everything loose goes first. Roads. Names." He didn't say *people*. He didn't need to; they both heard it anyway.

Bram nodded slowly. Then, in the tone of a man approaching a fence he wasn't sure would hold his weight: "She'll come back. Ivet. She's too stubborn to die and too angry to get lost."

"I know."

"You don't look like you know. You look like a man rehearsing a eulogy and hating the draft."

"That," Rosalind said, "is simply my face. It has been my face for ten years. You used to find it charming."

Something flickered across Bram's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "I used to find a lot of things charming." He stood, stretched until his back cracked like green wood, and climbed back toward the tower without another word. But at the top of the stairs he paused, silhouetted against the last grey light, and said, not turning around:

"If she comes back tonight, there's stew. If she doesn't, there's still stew, and we eat it anyway, because moping on an empty stomach is a luxury for people with better chapels."

It was, Rosalind understood, the closest thing to comfort Bram Ferrow had offered anyone in ten years. He took it the way you take a hand extended across a wide gap—carefully, gratefully, without commenting on the gap.

* * *

Ivet came back in the dark, and she came back loud.

Not intentionally. But the chapel's acoustics had been designed to carry a choir to the heavens, and they served equally well to carry a soaked, furious ex-apprentice wading through the nave, swearing at the water in the fluent, inventive manner of someone who had spent years among soldiers.

"Left," she was saying to no one, "left at the mill, the ledger *said* left, and the mill is a *field*, the mill is a field where a mill has never been—" Her lantern light swung around the flooded arches, found the altar, found them. She stopped. Water dripped off her elbows. Her hair had given up all pretense of order.

"You're back," Rosalind said, with tremendous dignity, "and you've brought the entire river with you."

"The river can have the mill," Ivet said. "The mill can go to the river. I don't care anymore." She waded forward, hauling a satchel over her head to keep it dry, and clambered up onto the altar platform, leaving wet boot prints that steamed faintly in the star-jar's glow. Up close she looked wrung out and lit from within, like a lamp burning its last inch of oil at maximum brightness.

"They're moving them," she said, without preamble. "Solmere's harvest. I got close enough to the capital to read the notices, and then closer than that, and Rosalind—it's not a purge. It never was. It's a *procession*. Every registered mage in the city, every gifted child they've scooped out of the camps—they're chaining them into columns and walking them east. Toward the Hollow. Toward the Gate."

The word landed in the chapel and rang.

"When?" Bram asked, from the stairs. His voice had gone flat and hard, smuggler-flat, the voice he used when the cargo was dangerous and the price was higher than gold.

"The new moon. Eleven nights." Ivet pulled papers from the satchel—copies, real ones, ink still sharp. "Solmere's own seal. He means to open the Gate *fully*, feed it the mages all at once, and walk through whatever's left standing. He thinks he can rule what's on the other side. He thinks the Hollow will *thank* him." Her laugh came out wrong, jagged. "There's a whole doctrine written out. *The Warden's Covenant.* We were never guarding the country from magic. We were the Hollow's shepherds. Feeding time, twice a century, dressed up as law."

Pip had gone very still, the jar held against their chest. "It's hungry now," Pip said softly. "It's hungrier than it's ever been. It's counting."

Everyone looked at the child. Nobody asked what it was counting. Nobody wanted to know badly enough to make Pip say it.

"Eleven nights," Rosalind repeated. He found his hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, some old sealed room of himself had begun, very quietly, to shake. "Then we don't go around Solmere's column. We go ahead of it. To the heart. Before the moon turns."

"That's four days' hard travel through country that's forgetting itself," Bram said. "Through terrain Odile has to hold together with stories and chalk."

"Yes."

"And when we get there? What exactly is the plan, Rosalind? Because the last time you stood at that Gate, the plan cost a village."

The silence after that had teeth. Ivet glanced between them. Pip hugged the jar.

Rosalind met Bram's eyes across the dark water. "This time," he said, "the plan is to listen first. And to pay whatever it asks out of my own account, and no one else's."

Bram held the look for a long moment. Whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy some private auditor of his, because he nodded once, curtly, and said, "Stew's on," and went to fetch it, which in the current currency of their company was roughly equivalent to an embrace.

* * *

They ate on the altar platform, cross-legged in a circle around the pot, steam rising into the ruined vaulting. Ivet told the rest of it between mouthfuls—how she'd walked into the capital behind a fish cart, how she'd stood in the Wardenhall's great archive and read the ledgers of the condemned, name after name after name, and understood at last that her whole life had been bookkeeping for a wound.

"There was a boy," she said, staring into the pot. "Tomas Ellery. Sixteen. Registered at twelve for lighting candles without matches. They took him Tuesday." Her jaw worked. "I trained under a woman who taught me the wards were mercy. Containment was mercy. I believed her."

"Do you still?" Pip asked.

"No," Ivet said. Then, more honestly: "I don't know what I believe. Except that I'm done believing things that require other people to be chained up so I can sleep at night."

Rosalind reached over and, before he could think better of it, put his hand briefly on her shoulder. She stiffened—then, deliberately, made herself not pull away. Progress, in their particular grammar, was measured in inches.

It was later, when the pot was empty and Pip had finally gone asleep curled against Ivet's side like a cat claiming a warm chair, that Bram drew Rosalind aside to the chapel's western window, where the moonless dark pressed against the glass like a face.

He held out something small.

A doorknob. Brass, worn smooth, its base still bearing a curl of old paint the color of buttercups.

Rosalind knew it before his mind caught up with his hands. Knew the weight of it, the little dent near the keyhole where Nana Sef had struck it with a poker in '41 and declared the dent *character*. Knew the house it had belonged to, in the village that did not exist, on the lane that had never been a lane.

"Where—" His voice failed. He tried again. "Where did you find this?"

"Market of Unmade Things. Under the capital, down where the river smell hides the rest of the smells." Bram's voice was carefully level, a man walking on ice he'd tested. "Salvage from erased places. Somebody's been dredging them up for years. Selling memories by the pound." He turned the doorknob in the starlight-less dark. "I went there to be angry. Figured I'd earned it. Ten years of anger, saved up, compounding."

"And?"

"And I found out what your sealing bought." He said it grudgingly, the way a man admits a debt he'd rather not owe. "The market keeper had records. Dates. Your village went into the Gate, and three towns downstream stayed *out* of it. Four thousand people who don't know they're alive because of you. Four thousand people who got to keep their mills and their lanes and their stupid dented doorknobs." He pressed the brass into Rosalind's palm and closed Rosalind's fingers over it, rough and warm. "I'm still angry. I expect I'll be angry at my funeral. But I'm done making you carry it alone."

Rosalind looked down at the doorknob. Something in his chest that had been clenched for ten years unclenched by perhaps a tenth of its width—a small thing, a hinge beginning to move.

"Bram—"

"Don't." Bram held up a hand. "No speeches. No apologies. If you apologize I'll have to forgive you properly and then I'll lose my edge, and I *am* my edge, it's basically all I've got left besides the boat." He jerked his chin toward the sleeping forms by the altar. "Besides. You've got eleven days to get yourself killed at the end of the world. Save the speeches for the eulogy. I'll write it. I'll make you sound taller."

Despite everything—despite the drowned chapel and the hungry dark and the child counting in its sleep—Rosalind laughed. It came out rusty, half-sob-shaped, but it was a laugh, and Bram's mouth twitched at the sound of it like a man hearing a song he'd sworn he'd forgotten.

* * *

Near midnight, Rosalind stood alone at the western window with the doorknob in his pocket and the star-jar glowing on the sill beside him, and looked east, toward the capital, toward the Gate, toward the wound in the world that had eaten his name once already and would ask for it again.

In the jar, the fragment of fallen star showed him—if he tilted it right, if he let his eyes unfocus—a street he had walked as a boy, a door the color of buttercup paint, a hand reaching for a handle worn smooth by generations. The world as it was. The world as it could be again, maybe, if they were fast enough and brave enough and lucky enough, and if the Hollow could be persuaded to want something other than silence.

Behind him, the chapel creaked and settled, stone holding up stone over black water. Pip murmured in sleep, half a bar of the Hollow's song, then—this was new—changed the tune mid-hum, bending it into something smaller and sweeter, a lullaby with no origin that anyone knew. As if the child were teaching the song a kinder version of itself.

Rosalind smiled at that, and touched the doorknob through his coat, and made his vow to the dark, quietly, the way you promise things you intend to keep:

*Eleven nights. We're coming. And this time we're not bringing chains—we're bringing witnesses.*

Far to the east, past the soft roads and the flickering fields, something vast and grieving turned over in its sleep—and, for the first time in a hundred years, seemed to listen.
