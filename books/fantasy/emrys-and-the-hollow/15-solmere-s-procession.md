## 15. Solmere's Procession

Ivet had learned, in her years as a warden's apprentice, that the best hiding places were the ones nobody wanted. Nobody wanted a drainage culvert beneath the old toll road. Nobody wanted the bramble-choked hollow behind it where the water went to die. And so that was where the five of them crouched on the morning the world ended for the second time—Ivet counted it as the second; Rosalind insisted the first had been ten years ago and she was welcome to her own opinion.

"Your elbow is in my ribs," Bram whispered.

"Your everything is in my everything," Ivet whispered back. "This hollow was not designed for four adults and a child."

"It wasn't designed at all," Odile murmured. "It's a wound in the hillside. Everything is, lately." She said it without self-pity, the way she said most things, her bandaged arm cradled against her chest like something borrowed from a stranger. The wound from the bridge had closed, but the healers among the freed mages said the memory of the stone she'd held would ache in her forever. Odile seemed almost pleased by this. *A cartographer should carry her maps somewhere,* she'd said.

Pip sat in the middle of all of them, cross-legged, absolutely still, which was how they knew something was wrong. Pip was never still. Pip was a creature assembled entirely of elbows and questions and the urgent need to show you a beetle. Stillness in Pip was like silence in a river: a sign that something upstream had gone badly wrong.

The humming started low, barely there, a thread of sound more felt than heard. Ivet felt it in her teeth first, then in the scar tissue along her palms where the warden's manacles had bitten her during her escape from Solmere's tower.

"Pip," Rosalind said softly. "What do you hear?"

Pip's eyes were fixed on the road above them, on the gray ribbon of the toll road winding east toward the capital. "It's happy," Pip said, and the word came out wrong, flattened, like a coin someone had stepped on. "It doesn't get to be happy. It's usually just sad. But today it's—" Pip searched for it, small hands opening and closing. "*Hungry-happy.* Like when you've been waiting for supper for so long you stop being hungry and start being excited instead."

Ivet's stomach dropped through the mud beneath her.

*Supper.*

* * *

They heard the column before they saw it—not hooves, not boots, but chains. Thousands of links dragging over packed earth, a sound like a river made of iron, and underneath it the shuffling rhythm of feet that had stopped believing in destinations.

Ivet rose just high enough to part the brambles with two fingers.

They came around the bend below the old tollhouse: a procession half a mile long if it was an inch. Wardens in their gray-and-silver rode flank, lances upright, banners snapping—the crossed keys of the Order, the sigil Ivet had once embroidered onto her own sleeve with such fierce pride that her mother had joked she'd bleed thread before blood. Behind the riders walked the mages.

Chained at wrist and throat, roped together in files of twenty, they shuffled east with their heads down. Ivet counted by training before she could stop herself. Sixty files. She did the arithmetic and wished she hadn't. Over a thousand.

She knew some of them. That was the thing nobody warned you about betrayal—that it would have *faces*. Third file, a woman with white streaks in her dark hair: Maren Ostwick, who had registered her gift honestly at sixteen because she believed the law was a promise. Ninth file, an old man walking with a stick despite his bound hands: Corvin Hale, who had once sold Ivet roasted chestnuts outside the wardenhall and warmed her fingers when she'd forgotten her gloves, and whose gift, per the ledger, was making bread rise. *Making bread rise.* A thousand people, and among them a man whose crime was loaves.

And at the head of the column, riding a white courser too fine for the mud, High Warden Solmere himself.

He looked exactly as he had in the tower: composed, silver-templed, handsome in the way a well-made lock is handsome. He did not look at the mages he was driving. He looked ahead, toward the capital, toward the Hollow beyond it, with the expression of a man reviewing a delivery he expected to arrive intact.

"He's taking them to the Gate," Ivet breathed. "Not to the prisons. To the harvest. He's going to feed them in all at once."

Below her, very quietly, Bram said a word that would have gotten him fined three coppers in any respectable tavern, and then, "How many wardens?"

"Two hundred mounted," Ivet said. Her voice came out flat and professional, the voice of the apprentice she had been, and she hated it. "Maybe fifty on foot with the rear files. They're not expecting trouble this close to the capital. Why are we counting?"

Nobody answered, which was itself an answer.

"We slip past," Odile said. It wasn't cruelty in her voice; it was arithmetic, the same cold sum Ivet had just done. "We're five. One of us is wounded, one of us is nine years old, and our entire strategy for the last month has been *don't be seen*. We go north through the fells tonight and reach the Gate before the new moon. That is the mission."

"I know what the mission is," Ivet said.

"You're doing the thing with your jaw," Bram observed.

"I don't have a thing with my jaw."

"You have a whole architecture of things with your jaw. Right now it's the one you used to do before telling Kessring he'd misfiled a report."

Ivet realized her teeth were clenched hard enough to ache and made herself let go. Below them, the column dragged on. Somewhere in the middle of it, Corvin Hale stumbled, and the mage behind him caught his arm, and the warden nearest struck both of them across the shoulders with a lance-butt, casually, the way a farmer swats flies.

Pip's humming climbed.

It had been building the whole time, Ivet realized—a thin rising note, frantic now, and when she looked down at the child she saw tears sliding silently down Pip's face while Pip's mouth kept humming anyway, helpless, a tuning fork for something enormous.

"It's excited," Pip whispered. "It can smell them coming. All those people with the light in them. It's been starving for ten years and it thinks—" Pip's breath hitched. "It thinks it's finally getting fed."

Rosalind put his hand on Pip's head. His face, when Ivet glanced at it, was terrible and calm, the face of a man reading a sentence he'd already served once. "Hush, little star," he murmured. "You don't have to sing its song. Sing ours."

And Pip—this was the thing Ivet would remember afterward, in the nights before the battle, in the years after—Pip stopped. Stopped mid-note, wrestled the Hollow's melody out of their own throat, and began instead a crooked little market-day tune, the fishmongers' song from Corvale, the one about the eels, which had no business being beautiful but became, hummed by a terrified child in a ditch, the most defiant sound Ivet had ever heard.

Bram exhaled slowly. "Right," he said. "So. We're going to do something stupid."

"We are *not*—" Odile began.

"We're absolutely going to do something stupid," Ivet said. "I'm sorry, Odile. I've been doing the arithmetic and the arithmetic is wrong."

"The arithmetic," Odile said, with the patience of a woman addressing a flood, "is that we cannot fight two hundred and fifty wardens."

"No," Ivet agreed. "We can't. But we're not fighting two hundred and fifty wardens. Look at the column again. Really look."

Odile looked. So did Rosalind, and she watched him see it a heartbeat after she had: the way the chained files moved, not as a line but as a rope under strain. A thousand people who had nothing left to lose except each other. A thousand people who only needed one reason.

"A spark," Rosalind said quietly. "You want to be the spark."

"I want to be the *diversion*," Ivet said. "Sparks are romantic. Diversions survive. I spent six years learning how warden columns think, and I'm telling you: Solmere is running a supply convoy, not a battle formation. He's proud. He believes the country is already his. Pride is a door, and I know exactly which hinges squeak."

* * *

They laid it out in the culvert's dark with Odile's charcoal stub scratching on the back of one of her father's charts, because paper was scarce and maps were what they had.

"Bram takes the tollhouse," Ivet said, tapping it. "It's dry rot and sentiment. You've told me yourself you could burn it by sneezing at it. When the rear guard reaches the bend, it goes up. Fire draws every eye south."

"And me?" Bram asked, though his grin said he already knew.

"You're the smuggler. While they're looking at the fire, you cut the drovers' pens loose up the slope—those mules will bolt straight through the rear files, and no amount of training makes a warden hold formation against forty panicked mules. I've seen it. It's the funniest thing in the world and it works every time."

"Genuinely," Bram said, "this is why I loved you."

"*Was*," Ivet said, and enjoyed the flicker of his wince. "Odile holds the road. Not with stone—we haven't got the strength for remembered bridges anymore—but with fog off the millpond. Your father charted that pond; you know its moods better than the miller does. Give me ten minutes of weather."

"Ten minutes," Odile said. "After that the fog belongs to itself."

"Ten minutes is a lifetime. Rosalind—" Ivet turned to him last, because his piece was the piece she'd argued with herself about all night. "You take Pip and go north. Now. Before any of it starts."

"No," Rosalind said.

"Yes."

"Child," Rosalind said, and there was warmth in it, real warmth, and steel under the warmth, "you are brave and I am fond of you and the answer is no. If your diversion works, those people run *somewhere*, and somewhere needs to exist. Someone has to be waiting in the fells with water and a map and a plan for a thousand frightened strangers. That's not the errand boy's job, Ivet. That's the mission. You said it yourself—we reach the Gate before the new moon. Well. Now we reach it with company."

Ivet opened her mouth. Closed it. He had, infuriatingly, out-flanked her with her own words.

"He's right," Odile said, sounding surprised. "That's the annoying part. He's right."

"I'll take care of him," Pip said solemnly, gripping Rosalind's sleeve. Then, after a moment, with the gravity only children and cats can manage: "He'll take care of me too. That's how it works."

Rosalind crouched and pressed his forehead briefly to Pip's curls, and then took Ivet's hand—her scarred, manacle-marked hand—and held it a moment longer than necessary. "Warden Kess," he said, using the name she'd abandoned like a coat by the roadside, wearing it back into her like a blessing. "Make it loud."

* * *

The column reached the bend below the tollhouse as the sun cleared the fells, gilding the dust, gilding the chains, gilding the bowed heads of a thousand people being delivered to their deaths.

Ivet lay in the wet grass at the road's edge with a warden's lance she'd taken off a sentry two nights ago, and watched Solmere's white horse come even with the tollhouse, and thought about Corvin Hale's chestnuts, and Maren Ostwick's honest signature, and every report she had ever filed that made the machine run smoother, and she found—to her surprise—that she was not afraid. What she felt was larger and colder than fear. It was the feeling of a scale settling true after years of sitting crooked.

Bram's fire went up on cue, roaring out of the tollhouse like a dragon with a grievance, and every head on the road turned south.

Then the mules came down the hill, forty of them, shrieking their opinions, and the rear files broke like a dropped plate.

And Ivet Kess stood up out of the grass in front of a thousand chained mages, in her stolen warden's coat with the crossed keys torn off, and shouted in the carrying parade-ground voice of the Order itself:

"Wardens! Your High Warden is delivering you to the thing that ate the stars! Drop your lances or answer for it!"

For one heartbeat, nothing. Dust, fire, screaming mules, Solmere wheeling his white horse around with his face going from confusion to fury.

Then, third file from the front, Maren Ostwick raised her chained hands and *pulled*, and the chain-link between her file and the next parted with a sound like a bell, and a thousand people remembered in the same instant that they were not cargo.

The road erupted. Fog rolled off the millpond thick as wool. Somewhere in it, Bram was laughing. Somewhere in it, wardens were choosing—she could hear them choosing, lance-butts falling on stone, boots running *toward* the chains instead of away.

Solmere's voice cut through it all, cold and clear as a blade drawn: "Kess. I should have unmade you in the tower."

"Yeah," Ivet called back, wading toward him through the smoke, lance leveled, heart hammering with something that was absolutely not fear. "Everyone says that."

Behind her, the freed mages were turning east—not toward the capital, not toward the Gate, but north, toward the fells, toward the water and the maps and the crooked little fishmongers' song waiting for them in the hills.

Toward the door that somebody, soon, would have to answer.
