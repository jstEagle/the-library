## 16. The Night of Small Rebellions

Bram Ferrow had smuggled a great many things down the Vell in his day: untaxed brandy, fugitive printers, one extremely indignant peacock belonging to a magistrate's wife. He had never smuggled hope, and he was beginning to suspect it was the heaviest cargo there was.

It sat in the boat with them now—forty-odd pounds of it in the shape of Odile's rolled maps, Ivet's stolen warden baton, Pip clutching the jam jar with both hands like it might fly off to join its brothers in the sky. And it sat in the people lining the riverbank, too, because word traveled fast when you told it right.

"Say it again," Bram said, poling through the reeds. "The part where you tell me this isn't suicide."

"It's not suicide," Rosalind said mildly, from the bow. He'd been saying that all evening with the serene confidence of a man who had once sealed a hole in the world and considered that a Tuesday. "It's a rescue."

"Of thirty chained mages being marched to their deaths by two hundred armed Wardens."

"Rescue," Rosalind confirmed.

"Right." Bram shoved the pole into the mud and pushed. "Just so we're clear on terminology before we die."

Pip giggled from between them—a small, startled sound, like the child had surprised themselves by remembering how. It did something to Bram's chest that he chose not to examine. There would be time for examining later, if later existed.

The column was due at Grayford Bridge an hour past midnight. Solmere's harvest procession moved slow and ceremonial, torches lit, drums beating, because cruelty performed in public was cruelty meant to teach. Which meant, Ivet had pointed out with the flat calm she'd learned from the inside of the order, that everyone watching already hated what they saw. They just needed permission to say so.

"Permission," Bram had said, "or a reason."

"Both," Ivet said. "Wardens break crowds the way you break weather. You give them nothing to gather around. So we give them something."

What they gave them, as it turned out, was a bridge.

* * *

Grayford Bridge had been erased three weeks ago—not collapsed, *erased*, along with the village of Grayford and the mill and the widow Ansel's famous plum orchard. The river ran through empty air now, or rather it didn't run at all; it pooled against nothing and slid around an absence, which was the most unsettling thing Bram had ever watched water do. The procession would have to ford downstream at the shallows, adding hours to their march.

Unless, of course, someone remembered the bridge back into existence.

Odile stood on the near bank with her charts spread across a plank table, her father's compass in one hand and chalk dust to her elbows. She looked, Bram thought, like a general who happened to be nineteen and had ink on her chin.

"I can hold it for maybe twenty minutes," she said. "Longer if people help. Memory is load-bearing, but it needs weight distributed." She tapped the map. "Everyone who crosses has to be thinking about it. Not wishing. *Remembering.* The stones were gray. The rail was oak, split on the north side. There were seventeen arches—no. Sixteen. Sixteen, I'm sure of it, Papa counted twice."

"And if people stop remembering?" Bram asked.

"Then anyone standing on it stops standing anywhere." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and somehow that was worse. "So don't stop."

Rosalind put his hand briefly on her shoulder. "You don't have to be on the bridge yourself."

"Yes, I do." Her jaw set in a way that made her look suddenly, startlingly, like her father. "I've been drawing places other people lost my whole life. Tonight I get to build one back. Let me have it."

Bram knew better than to argue with that tone. He'd been married to a version of it once, in all but name, and it had never once yielded.

* * *

The bridge came back at moonrise, and it came back the way a sneeze comes—suddenly, entirely, and with a strange sense of relief.

One moment the river pooled against nothing. The next, gray stone arched out over the black water, sixteen arches of it, oak rails split on the north side exactly as promised, and the whole company made a sound like a held breath let go. Pip laughed outright and had to be hushed. Even Ivet, who trusted nothing, touched the rail with two fingers as though checking whether forgiveness could be felt.

"That's the strangest thing I've ever seen," Bram said, "and I once watched a man sell his own shadow."

"You'll see stranger tonight," Rosalind said. "Get to your positions."

They had spent the afternoon turning smugglers' tricks into siegecraft. Bram's river folk knew every trick of misdirection on the water: lantern-rigs that made one boat look like six, oil-slicks poured to send torchlight skittering wrong, decoy fires on the far bank built by cousins who asked no questions because family didn't. Ivet drilled the plan in warden cadence, crisp and numbered, until even Bram's least reliable cousin could recite it drunk. And Rosalind—

Rosalind walked the bank talking to people. That was all. Farmers, ferrymen, refugees from towns that no longer appeared on any map. He spoke low, and he listened more than he talked, and wherever he passed, people stopped looking frightened and started looking angry instead, which is a much more useful thing to look.

"He does that," Ivet murmured, appearing at Bram's elbow with her hood up and her old warden badge turned inward against her chest. "Ten years ago he did it too. Half the reason the Gate held so long was that ordinary people kept small secrets for him and never knew why."

"Half," Bram said. "What was the other half?"

Ivet was quiet a moment. "Guilt, probably. It burns hot."

Bram grunted. He knew about guilt's fuel value. He also knew—I'd cooled some, walking out of the Market of Unmade Things with a doorknob from a village that officially had never existed, cool enough to stand next to Rosalind again, not yet cool enough to say so out loud. Some things you showed by holding a bridge together instead.

* * *

The column arrived late, drums dragging, torches guttering in a wind that seemed to come from nowhere in particular—which Bram chose to read as the Hollow breathing in its sleep, and tried very hard not to think about further.

Thirty mages shuffled in chains between ranks of white-cloaked Wardens. Old men. A girl younger than Odile. A woman carrying a toddler who wasn't hers but wouldn't be put down. Solmere rode at the rear under a canopy, distant and immaculate, a man attending his own coronation early.

They reached the place where Grayford Bridge should not have been, and stopped dead.

For one long moment nobody moved. Then a Warden sergeant raised his lantern, and the light fell on honest gray stone, worn hollow in the middle by two hundred years of cartwheels and feet, and a murmur went down the column like fire through dry grass. People *knew* this bridge. People had crossed it. The erasure had taken the village, but it hadn't quite taken the crossing from everyone's bones, and seeing it standing there—impossible, solid, *remembered*—did something to the crowd of conscripted porters and camp followers trudging behind the soldiers that no speech could have done.

A woman near the front began to cry. A man beside her said, loudly, "That's Grayford Bridge. My gran sold eels off it." And then, louder, to no one and everyone: "They told us there was never anything here."

"There was never anything here," a Warden repeated automatically, the trained line, and it landed wrong—landed like an insult—and Bram, crouched in the reeds with his hand on the sluice-gate rope, felt the night tip over.

"Now," he said.

The lantern-rigs went up: six boats becoming forty on the dark water, torchlight multiplied and scattered, and the Wardens' formation broke toward a threat that didn't exist while the real threat walked up the bank wearing mud and determination. Ivet went first, baton reversed, taking the rear guard's legs out with a textbook sweep and then, breaking textbook entirely, hauling the man behind a boulder and knocking him gently unconscious instead of worse. "Old habits," she muttered, when Bram caught up. "Shut up."

"Didn't say a word."

"Your face said several."

The chains were the problem, and the chains were Rosalind's. He moved down the line of prisoners with his sleeves pushed back, and where his hands touched iron, it simply forgot how to be locked—the wards on the manacles unraveling like knitting with the end pulled. Ten years of hiding, Bram thought, and the man still worked magic like a carpenter works wood: unshowy, exact, sure.

"Go," Rosalind told each freed mage, pointing them toward the boats, toward the decoy fires, toward cousins with oars. "Don't run—walk fast, stay together, don't look back at the lights."

Halfway down the line, the toddler's woman grabbed his wrist. "Why?" she demanded. "Who are you?"

Bram, passing with a freed boy slung over his shoulder, heard Rosalind's answer and thought it was the truest thing he'd said in ten years:

"Nobody. Go on. Nobody's the best thing to be, tonight."

* * *

It was going well. That was the trap of it. Fifteen mages free, boats loading, the crowd of porters refusing—openly, loudly, gloriously refusing—to help the Wardens reform their lines. A fishwife stood toe to toe with a sergeant, hands on hips, informing him that her husband was on that chain-line and if the sergeant wanted him back the sergeant could come and try. Two conscripted drovers simply sat down in the road. Courage, Bram realized, watching it spread like spilled lamp-oil, was the one cargo that shipped itself once you got the first barrel open.

Then the horns sounded from the rear, and Solmere's canopy folded away, and the High Warden himself rode forward into the torchlight with six black-cloaked wardens who moved wrong—too smooth, too quiet, their eyes gone flat and pale as river ice. Hollow-touched. Bram had heard rumors of them from Ivet and hadn't wanted to believe them.

"Break the bridge," Solmere said, in a voice pitched to carry, mild as a man ordering wine. "Whatever holds it up—break *that*."

The black-cloaks turned, all six together, toward the near bank. Toward Odile.

She stood at her chart-table in full view, chalk in hand, holding sixteen arches of stone above black water through sheer stubborn memory, and she saw them coming, and she did not run. Bram was already moving—he'd been moving since the horns—but the bank was long and the black-cloaks were fast, and he understood with horrible clarity that he would not arrive in time.

Odile planted her feet, gripped her father's compass, and *pushed*.

The bridge answered her. Stone thickened. Rails rose. Arches deepened, groaning, the whole span flexing like a living thing, because she was feeding it everything—every detail her father had ever taught her, every measurement, every story, the widow Ansel's plums dropping ripe on the roadway, the mill wheel turning, the bell in the chapel tower ringing the hour that no longer existed anywhere except in her. The bridge became so vividly, furiously *real* that the black-cloaks staggered, drowning in a place that had been deleted, and two of them broke and fled from pure ontological terror.

But she was spending herself to do it. Bram could see that from thirty yards—see her swaying, chalk-white, nose bleeding freely onto the charts, her voice running in a cracked whisper through an endless inventory: *gray stone, oak rail, sixteen arches, sixteen, sixteen—*

He hit the first black-cloak from the side with a boat-hook and no finesse whatsoever. The second he took low and dirty, smuggler-fashion, all elbows and river-mud. The third got an arm around his throat from behind, and the world narrowed to pressure and stars that weren't the missing kind—

—and then Ivet was there, baton cracking precisely, warden-trained and warden-furious, and the arm went slack. She stood over the fallen cloak, breathing hard, and looked at Bram with an expression he'd never seen on her: grief and pride in equal measure, badly mixed, like paint stirred wrong.

"Go," she said. "She's fading. Get her off the table."

He reached Odile as the last of the prisoners cleared the span. Her whisper had thinned to almost nothing. The bridge was going translucent at the far end, arches dissolving upward like breath off cold glass, and the last freed mages were sprinting the final yards with the river showing through the stones beneath their boots.

"Papa counted twice," Odile whispered, blood on her chin, her eyes fixed on nothing. "Sixteen. Tell him I kept count."

"You'll tell him yourself," Bram said, and swept her up off her feet, charts and compass and all, and ran.

Behind them the bridge sighed out of existence, gently, almost politely, and the Hollow's silence closed over the space where it had been. But the boats were pushing off—full boats, heavy with fifteen saved lives and one bleeding mapmaker and a crowd of ordinary citizens who had seen chained neighbors and chosen, publicly and irreversibly, to choose otherwise.

* * *

They made the far bank by grey dawn, and Bram counted heads twice, the way his mother had taught him to count barrels: once for the number, once for the truth of it.

Fifteen freed mages, scattered among sympathetic farmsteads by routes only his cousins knew. One High Warden left standing on the wrong side of a river that now remembered how to be uncrossable, furious, and—according to a breathless fishwife who'd stayed to watch—one step closer to whatever terrible ceremony the new moon required.

And Odile, laid in the stern of Bram's own boat, wrapped in every coat they owned, conscious but grey-lipped, her hands trembling too hard to hold chalk.

Pip knelt beside her, jam jar in hand, and looked up at Bram with those too-old eyes. "She gave all her remembering away," the child said. "There's none left in her for herself. I've seen that before. In the Hollow." Pip's small fingers curled tighter around the jar, inside which a fragment of fallen star glowed like a captive morning. "I know how to fill somebody back up. But it costs, and—" the voice dropped, scared and steady at once, "—and I've seen what's at the bottom of it. What the Hollow really is. And I think you all need to hear it before the new moon, because it changes everything."

Bram looked at the child, then at Odile's fluttering eyelids, then downriver toward the capital, where a madman was counting days to a harvest.

"Aye," he said quietly. "Tell us."
