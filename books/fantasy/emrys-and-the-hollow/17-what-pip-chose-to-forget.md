## 17. What Pip Chose to Forget

Pip had learned that grown-ups lied in a very particular way when something bad was happening. They got quiet-loud. Their voices went soft, but their feet went fast, and their hands did things like press bandages and shake and pretend not to shake all at the same time.

That was how it was now, in the ruined mill at the edge of the drowned fields, where Odile lay on a pallet of smugglers' canvas with her side wrapped in so much linen she looked like a parcel nobody wanted to claim.

"It's nothing," Odile said, which was lie number one. "A scratch," which was lie number two. "I've had worse from a compass," which was probably true but wasn't about the wound at all.

Bram knelt beside her with his sleeves rolled up, doing the thing he did where he looked calm by refusing to blink. Ivet stood at the broken window, watching the dark for Warden lanterns. And Rosalind sat on the floor with his back against the wall, holding the jam jar in both hands like it was a bird with a broken wing.

Inside the jar, the star-fragment glowed its small stubborn glow. The light that remembered everything.

Pip knew what was in it. Pip had seen it first, after all—had caught the falling piece of star out of the black sky like catching a snowflake, except this one had hummed against his palms and shown him the world as it used to be. Villages with smoke rising from chimneys. Roads full of carts. People laughing in market squares that no longer existed anywhere except inside that jar.

Everyone else had looked into it and seen wonders. Rosalind had looked into it and gone gray as ash, because he'd seen his own face among the people who sealed the Gate ten years ago. But Pip had looked into it longest, and Pip had noticed something the others hadn't.

The light was sad.

Not broken-sad. Not dying-sad. Sad like a dog left at the side of a road, watching the cart get smaller. Sad like a song that stops halfway through and never gets to finish.

"Odile needs more than linen," Ivet said quietly, without turning from the window. "You all know it. I know you know it."

"I know it," Odile said cheerfully, which was lie number three, delivered with the same bright confidence she used for charting impossible roads. Her face was the color of old candle wax, and there was a shine on her forehead that wasn't sweat exactly, or wasn't only sweat. "What I need is for everyone to stop looming. Looming is terrible for the constitution."

Bram made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't gotten stuck on the way out.

Pip crept closer to Rosalind and the jar. Nobody told him to go away. That was one of the good things about this strange collection of people—they let him be near important things, which the matron at the camp had never done, which soldiers certainly never did.

"Rosalind." Pip said it softly, the way you'd say a word you weren't sure you were allowed to keep.

Rosalind looked up. He had tired eyes, kind tired eyes, the kind that always seemed to be apologizing for something even when he was handing you bread. "Yes, sprout?"

"What happens if we open the jar?"

The room went still in the way rooms do when everyone has been thinking the forbidden thing and now it's sitting on the table wearing a hat.

"We don't know," Rosalind said honestly. Pip liked that about him too. Grown-ups who said *I don't know* were rarer than stars these days, and twice as bright.

"The light remembers things," Pip said. "Remembering fixes things. Odile's map works because she remembers places out loud and they come back. So maybe—" He shrugged, because he was eight and shrugging was half his vocabulary. "Maybe remembering can fix Odile."

"That's not how wounds work," Bram said roughly. "Believe me. I've tried pouring memory on plenty of hurts."

"You've tried pouring *drink* on them," Ivet said. "Different thing."

"It's adjacent."

But Rosalind was looking at Pip with an expression Pip couldn't read, something moving behind his face like fish under ice. "Pip. When you look into the light. What do you see?"

Pip hugged his knees. This was the hard part, the part he'd been carrying around for days like a stone in his pocket, too heavy to show anyone and too warm to put down.

"I hear it," he admitted. "The Hollow. I always hear it, you know that. It hums. But since I caught the star, the humming has words in it. Sort of. Not words like talking. Words like crying is words, if crying could talk."

"And what is it saying?" Rosalind asked, very gently, very carefully, the way you'd ask someone what a nightmare was about.

Pip thought about it. Really thought, the way Odile had taught him, which was: don't grab the first answer, let it float up on its own like a cork.

"It's saying *nobody came*," Pip said. "Over and over. Everything it ate—the villages, the roads, the people—it kept. It keeps everything, that's the horrible thing, it doesn't destroy anything, it just takes it away where no one can visit. And it's been waiting and waiting for somebody to come see what it took. Because taking things isn't the point. Taking things is just what happens when nobody comes."

Nobody spoke. The star-fragment pulsed in its jar, slow, like breathing.

"That's why it's eating faster now," Rosalind said, mostly to himself. "It's not hunger. It's loneliness. Ten years ago we shut the door on it and called it sealing, and it's been alone in the dark with everything it ever swallowed ever since." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, just enough that Pip heard it and pretended not to, because some things you pretend not to hear as a gift.

"So here's my thinking," Pip said, standing up, because standing up made arguments better, everyone knew that. "The light remembers. Odile needs remembering. If I give the light to her—"

"If you *what*?" Bram said.

"—if I give it freely, like a present, not like using it, because presents work different, everybody knows that too—then maybe the remembering goes where it's needed. Into her. Into the hurt. And maybe the light knows how to fix people because the light is made of everything that used to be alive and well and having dinner."

"Pip," Ivet said, crouching down to his level. She had warden eyes, sharp and steady, and right now they were scared, which meant something, because Pip had seen her fight three soldiers and her eyes hadn't been scared once. "If you give away the fragment, we lose the only map to before. We lose what it shows us. We might need it."

"But you'll still have Odile," Pip said. "And Odile *is* the map. She said so herself. Her whole head is full of everywhere." He turned to the pallet. "Isn't it?"

Odile was looking at him with an expression Pip had never seen on her before—soft and undone, like someone had handed her something she didn't have a chart for. "Sprout," she said, and her voice came out crooked, "you can't spend your wonder on me. Wonder's the rarest coin there is."

"I'm not spending it," Pip said. "I'm *sharing* it. Sharing's different. Sharing makes more of it. That's how it worked with the biscuit Bram gave me, I shared it with the gull and then there were eleven gulls."

"That's not—" Bram started.

"There were *eleven*, Bram."

There was a silence, and in the silence Pip felt the thing he felt sometimes, the humming under everything, the Hollow's breath against the back of the world. Except tonight it wasn't frightening. Tonight it sounded like someone leaning against a door on the other side, listening to voices through the wood, wanting desperately to be let in and knowing they wouldn't be.

Pip knew that sound. He'd made it himself, outside the orphanage door, the night before the soldiers came.

He held out his hand to Rosalind. "Please. The jar."

"Pip—" Rosalind began.

"You always say found family means you don't get to decide alone," Pip said. "Well. I decided. I'm the one who caught it. It's mine. Catching something makes it yours, that's the oldest law there is, older than wardens, older than kings." He lifted his chin. "And I'm giving it to her."

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed—a small, wet, surprised laugh—and pressed the jar into Pip's hands.

"When did you become the bravest person in this company?" he asked.

"Probably the day you all found me in the reeds," Pip said. "Bravery's contagious. You said so."

*

They gathered close, because some things shouldn't be witnessed from far away.

Odile propped herself up against Bram's arm. Pip climbed onto the edge of the pallet, jar in his lap, and the star-fragment glowed up at him through the glass, sad and patient, like it had been waiting for exactly this.

"Hello," Pip told it. "I'm going to open the lid now. Don't be scared. We're not losing anything. We're just putting things where they belong."

He unscrewed the lid.

The light rose out of the jar like breath in winter—a ribbon of silver-white, smelling faintly of rain and woodsmoke and, strangely, of fresh bread. It hung above Odile for a moment, turning, considering, the way a bird considers a hand.

Then Pip cupped his palms beneath it and *pushed*—not with his arms, with whatever lived behind his sternum, the place where the humming came from. *Here,* he told it, the way you tell a lost animal *here, this way, safe.* *She's hurt. Remember her better.*

The light poured down.

It went into Odile like water into dry ground. For a heartbeat nothing happened, and Pip's heart dropped somewhere near his boots. Then Odile gasped—an enormous, tearing gasp, like a woman surfacing from deep water—and her eyes flew open, and they were *full of light*, literally full, silver flooding the blue, and she said, in a voice that was hers and also somehow everyone's:

"Oh. Oh, I remember *all of it*—"

And Pip fell in after her.

That was the only way he could describe it later, though he never described it to anyone but Rosalind, much later, on a night when neither of them could sleep. He fell into the light the way you fall into a pond, and instead of cold water there was—

Everything.

He stood in a village square at harvest time, lanterns strung between houses, fiddles playing, and he knew—knew in his bones—that this was Dunmore's Hollow, the village that no longer existed, and the woman laughing by the well had a name, Maren, Maren who grew beans that climbed higher than the eaves, and the boy chasing a hoop was Tam, Tam who was afraid of geese, and every single person had a name, had a face, had a favorite meal and a grudge and a song they hummed wrong on purpose—

And underneath it all, beneath the village, beneath the world, the Humming.

Pip turned toward it, because of course he did; he was the child who hears it, and hearing it was the whole of who he was.

He expected a monster. Everyone expected a monster. Soldiers drew pictures of it on walls, all teeth and shadow, and wardens preached about it like a fire, and even Bram talked about it the way you talk about weather that kills you.

But the Hollow's heart, when Pip finally saw it—not with his eyes, with the part of him that heard—was not teeth. Was not shadow.

It was a great slow gathering of everything, the way a nest is a gathering of twigs. Every erased thing lay there, held carefully, held *gently*: villages folded like letters never sent, roads coiled like sleeping snakes, songs stored whole with all their verses, and people—people everywhere, thousands upon thousands, not dead, not destroyed, just *kept*, laid out in the dark like precious things in a box too deep to climb out of.

And over all of it, the sorrow. It rolled off the heart of the Hollow in waves, and each wave was a name, and each name was a question: *Do you remember? Does anyone remember? Were we ever real?*

Pip understood, suddenly and completely, the way children understand big true things in one clean swallow: the Hollow wasn't eating the country.

The Hollow was *keeping* it. Keeping it the only way it could, because it was itself made of forgotten things, and forgotten things hoard, and hoarding feels like love from the inside and looks like hunger from the outside.

It wasn't a monster. It was a wound that had learned to hold itself closed so nothing else could fall out, and it had been holding on so long, so terribly long, that holding on was all it remembered how to do.

*Nobody came,* it sang, and the song shook the dark. *Nobody came. Nobody came.*

"I came," Pip said.

His voice was small in the vastness, eight years old and reedy, and it didn't matter even slightly. The whole dark heart of the Hollow turned toward him like a sunflower toward morning.

"I came," he said again, louder. "I hear you. I hear all of it. Maren and her beans. Tam and the geese. The fiddle tune that goes da-da-DAH and then pretends it didn't. I hear you. You're not a monster. You're just sad. Being sad isn't the same as being bad."

The sorrow-waves stopped.

For one long moment the entire accumulated grief of an erased country hung absolutely still, listening to a small boy in a ruined mill, and Pip felt what it felt, which was the strangest feeling of his whole life: it felt like being hugged by something that had forgotten hugs existed and was remembering very fast.

*You hear us,* the Hollow said, in the voice of every name at once. *You truly hear us?*

"Every bit," Pip said firmly. "And I'm going to tell the others. They're good at hearing, once you give them a chance. Well. Bram takes a while. But he gets there."

Something that was almost laughter moved through the dark, vast and rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years.

*Keep the door open, little listener,* the Hollow sang, softer now. *Tell them. Tell them we are not the end of the story. Tell them we are the part everyone skipped.*

"I will," Pip promised. "I'm very good at telling things. Ask anyone. Actually don't ask Bram, he says I embellish."

*Embellish,* repeated the Hollow, tasting the word, delighted, keeping it forever the way it kept everything.

And then Pip was rising, rising, out of the silver light and back into his own body, back into the mill, back into the smell of linen and lamp oil and Bram's terrified face an inch from his own.

"—breathing, he's breathing, Pip, *Pip*—"

"I'm here," Pip said. His voice came out scratchy. "I'm fine. Did it work? Is Odile—"

He turned.

Odile was sitting up. Sitting *up*, color in her cheeks, the wound at her side visible now through the unwrapped linen—and closed. Not scarred. Closed, smooth, as though the light had remembered her whole, the way she used to be before the bridge of stone broke under her.

She was staring at her own hands, and tears were running down her face, and she was laughing at the same time, which Pip privately considered the best sound in the world.

"All of it," she whispered. "Pip, I have *all of it*. Every place my father ever charted. Every place he never got to. I could draw the whole country from memory now—every road, every ford, every—" She stopped. Looked at Pip. Looked at him properly, the way you look at something miraculous. "What did you give me?"

"A present," Pip said, and yawned so hugely his jaw cracked. All of a sudden he was tired the way you're tired after crying, empty and light at once. "Also, um. Important news. Everybody sit down. Especially you, Rosalind. You're going to want to sit down."

Rosalind, who had gone very pale and very still, sat down slowly on the floor without taking his eyes off Pip. "What is it, sprout?"

Pip looked around at all of them—Bram with his fists still clenched, Ivet with her hand on her knife out of pure habit, Odile glowing faintly at the edges, Rosalind with his tired kind eyes wide open now.

"It's not a monster," Pip said. "The Hollow. It never was. It's everything the country threw away, and it kept it all, and it's been waiting ten years for somebody to come and *visit*." He took a breath. "And it wants us to come. Not to fight it. To *witness* it. It said—" he screwed up his face, reaching for the exact words, because exact words mattered, "—it said we're not the end of the story. We're the part everyone skipped."

Silence. The kind of silence that has a floor falling out of it.

Then Rosalind made a sound Pip had never heard from him before, half laugh and half sob, and pressed both hands over his mouth, and Ivet said, very quietly, "The Gate. It was never a prison. It's a *door*, and it's been knocking—"

"No," Pip corrected, with the patience of the very young explaining things to the very tired. "We've been knocking. From the inside. All of us. Everybody the country forgot—we've all been knocking this whole time." He crawled over to Rosalind and leaned against his shoulder, because his bones had decided they were done for the day. "Also the Hollow thinks 'embellish' is a funny word. I promised we'd come. Before the new moon. You have to help me keep it, okay? A promise to the whole forgotten country is a lot of promise for one eight-year-old."

Rosalind's arm came around him, careful and warm and trembling slightly.

"We'll keep it together," Rosalind said. "All of us. That's rather the point of us, I think."

From the window, Ivet suddenly went rigid. "Lights," she said. "On the river road. Lanterns, dozens of them, moving toward the capital." She turned, and her face in the lamplight was grim and lit with purpose all at once. "That's not a patrol. That's a procession. Solmere's bringing his prisoners to the Gate early."

Pip felt the humming shift under the world—quickening, eager, frightened all at once, like a heart before a door opens or breaks.

"Well then," said Bram, already reaching for his coat. "Best not keep a grieving country waiting."
