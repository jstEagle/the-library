## 18. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The road to the Hollow's heart did not exist, and so they walked it anyway.

That was Odile's doing. She had spread her father's last unfinished chart across a flat stone at the edge of the remembered country, weighted its corners with four river pebbles and one jam jar containing a star, and said, in the matter-of-fact voice she used for everything: "It's here. It just needs saying."

So they said it. All day, in shifts, while the land beneath their boots flickered like a candle in wind — Pip humming the Hollow's song under his breath, Ivet reciting warden regulations she'd once memorized to enforce and now recited because they were full of place-names, Bram telling filthy stories about river towns that had been eaten whole, stories so detailed and so unrepeatable that Rosalind was fairly sure two of the bridges they crossed were held up entirely by innuendo.

And now, at dusk of the third day, the road simply stopped pretending.

Rosalind felt it before he saw it — a change in the air, the way you feel a door open in a house you thought empty. The grass thinned. The stones went pale. And ahead, where Odile's map promised a threshold, the world ended not with a wall or a cliff but with a *seam*, a line of darkness too clean to be shadow, running from horizon to horizon like a stitch someone had forgotten to tie off.

Beyond it: nothing. Not blackness, exactly. Blackness would have been something. This was the absence of having-been, and Rosalind's eyes kept sliding off it the way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

"Well," said Bram, behind him. "Lovely spot. Terrible for property values."

"Bram." Ivet's voice was gentle, which meant she was frightened.

"I'm coping," Bram said. "I cope by talking. You've all had ten days to learn this about me."

Pip tugged Rosalind's sleeve. The boy had stopped humming. In ten days he had hummed through meals, through sleep, through Ivet's attempt to teach him cards; the silence was louder than any scream. "It knows we're here," Pip whispered. "It's been waiting so long it forgot it was waiting. Like when you hold your breath and then remember you have lungs."

Rosalind crouched to the boy's level. Up close, Pip's eyes held flecks of the star-fragment still — a gift freely given left traces, Odile had said, the way spilled honey leaves a floor sweet. "What does it feel like? Now. Truly."

Pip considered this with the terrible seriousness of eight-year-olds. "Like the moment after you break something," he said, "before anyone finds out. But forever."

* * *

They made camp fifty paces back from the seam, because even Odile admitted there was no sense standing closer than necessary to the end of the world. Bram built a fire out of driftwood from a river that no longer existed, which burned with a faint smell of lavender, which nobody commented on, because commenting would have required stopping.

Ivet took first watch, naturally. She always took first watch, and second if you let her, as though sleep were a debt she hadn't yet finished paying. Rosalind sat beside her at the fire's edge and watched her sharpen a knife she did not need to sharpen.

"Say it," she said, without looking up.

"Say what?"

"Whatever speech you've been drafting since Grayford Bridge. You get a look. The look of a man rehearsing an apology to a room that isn't listening." She tested the blade against her thumb, nodded. "Out with it. I'd rather hear the bad version now than the polished one later."

Rosalind laughed despite himself — a small, cracked sound. "There's no speech. There's only..." He turned his hands over, palms up, the old scars from the sealing still faintly silver in the firelight. "Ten years ago I stood at that seam with six other mages, and we closed it. We called it sealing. A gate implies a door, Ivet. Doors open both ways. What we built was a knot. And the price—" His throat closed around the village name. He had not spoken it aloud in a decade; speaking it felt like digging up a grave with his bare hands. "The price was taken from me. From us. And I told myself it was worth it. I told myself that every single morning for ten years, and some mornings I almost believed it."

"And now?"

"Now I know what it wants." He looked toward the seam, where the dark did not so much sit as *wait*. "Not conquest. Not hunger, the way the Wardens taught it. It wants to be witnessed. Everything it took, it keeps. Everything the country threw away — the villages, the names, the people — it holds them like a hoarder holds letters from the dead. It is the most lonely thing in creation, and we made it that way, and then we outlawed the only people who could hear it crying."

Ivet was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had the flat steadiness she used for hard truths, aimed at herself as often as others. "My order knew. Solmere knew. They fed it names to keep it quiet instead of feeding it what it actually needed." Her jaw tightened. "I spent five years learning to hunt sorcerers for an institution that was running the largest sorcery in the country."

"Yes."

"I'm going to be angry about that for a very long time."

"You should be."

She sheathed the knife. "Good. Then we understand each other. Go sleep, Dunmore. Tomorrow you're going to do something terrifying and noble and probably stupid, and I want you rested enough that when I call it stupid, you can't blame exhaustion."

* * *

He did not sleep. He walked.

He told himself it was to check the seam, which was a lie so transparent that even the seam seemed embarrassed by it. He walked because his feet remembered the way his heart refused to speak, and because somewhere in the last ten days he had stopped being able to stay still near the thing he had spent ten years fleeing.

The others let him go. That was the miracle of them, he thought — Bram with his back against a stone, watching Rosalind pass with eyes that said *come back*, Ivet pretending to sleep, Pip actually asleep with the jam jar cradled like a stuffed animal. They let him walk toward the end of the world alone because they trusted him to walk back, and trust, Rosalind was discovering, was a far heavier thing to carry than duty had ever been.

The seam stood waiting.

Up close it made a sound. Not the breathing Pip described — that came in dreams, in the space between heartbeats. This was quieter: a hum below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the scar tissue of his palms. It sounded, absurdly, like a choir warming up in an empty cathedral. Like something practicing being heard again after years of being told to hush.

Rosalind raised his hand. Stopped an inch from the dark.

Here was the truth he had run from, laid out plain as a confession on a cold morning: *it cannot be sealed again.*

He had known it since the stars went out. Sealing had worked once because seven mages had given everything — names, homes, futures — to twist the wound shut, and the wound had accepted the payment the way a creditor accepts a final installment: completely, and without gratitude. There was nothing left in him to pay a second time. There was nothing left in *anyone*. Solmere's harvest, his chained columns of mages, his arithmetic of souls — it wouldn't seal anything. It would only feed the knot until it burst.

No. The Gate could not be shut. It could only be *answered*.

And answering meant opening.

Rosalind pressed his palm flat against the seam, and the dark was neither cold nor warm but *attentive*, the way a sickroom goes attentive when the fever breaks. Through his hand came images, or memories, or invitations — he could not tell which, and suspected the distinction was one living people invented to make themselves brave:

A kitchen with blue tiles, and bread rising under a cloth. His mother's laugh, except it wasn't his mother, it was everyone's mother, all of them at once, the accumulated motherhood of a hundred erased villages. A wedding ring turning in dust. A dog waiting at a gate for an owner who had been subtracted from the world mid-whistle. A boy of nineteen standing in a circle of light with six friends, shouting words of closing, and the look on his face as the price was named — young, terrified, certain he was saving everyone, and the specific horror of learning afterward that *everyone* included no one he loved—

"That's me," Rosalind whispered. "You kept him."

*We keep everything,* said the Hollow, in no voice at all, in the voice of bread and dogs and mothers and dust. *That is all we are. A room where the thrown-away waits to be found.*

Terror and longing rose in him together, braided past untangling, and Rosalind understood finally why he had never been able to explain his fear to Bram, to Ivet, to anyone. They feared the Hollow might destroy them. He feared it might *give him back*. Every erased face, every unsaid name, his village whole again and his younger self standing in it, innocent, pre-sealing, pre-everything — and what would remain of the man who had traded them away? What right did a ghost of a sacrifice have to stand in the doorway of its own refund?

He wanted it. Gods help him, he wanted it the way the drowning want air, and wanting it felt like betrayal, and refusing it felt like betrayal, and between those two betrayals stood a door.

"Rosalind."

Bram's voice, behind him. Close. Of course close — Bram Ferrow had never in his life let Rosalind walk into danger more than thirty seconds unaccompanied, whatever he claimed about anger.

"Don't come nearer," Rosalind said. His hand was still on the seam. He could not seem to take it away. "It shows you things."

"It showed me drowned lanterns on the river, ten days ago. Showed me a wedding I wasn't invited to." Bram stopped an arm's length away, arms folded, jaw set against the dark. "I know what it shows. Question is what it wants."

"To be answered." Rosalind laughed, wet-eyed. "Ten years I built a prison and called it mercy. It's not a prison, Bram. It's a locked room full of grief, and we threw away the key and posted wardens outside to arrest anyone who asked about the noise."

Bram was quiet. Then, in the tone he used for the truest things, the tone he usually buried under jokes: "So open it."

"Not like this. Not torn open by Solmere's harvest, not forced — that's how you get another wound. It has to be answered. Witnessed. Mourned." Rosalind finally drew his hand back and looked at it, at the silver scars, at the man's hand they belonged to. "Someone has to go through and give it that. Face to face. Name for name."

"Name for name," Bram repeated slowly. "Meaning—"

"The bargain runs both ways. It gave up holding my village hostage when I sealed it. If I answer it fully — if I go through and witness everything, mourn everything, release everything — it will ask a price in kind." Rosalind met Bram's eyes, and found that saying it aloud was easier than he'd feared, the way amputation is easier than the weeks before it. "It will ask for my name. The one I sealed it with. Rosalind Dunmore stays on that side of the door."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind them, faintly, Pip had begun to hum in his sleep — and the Hollow's hum bent toward the boy's like a plant toward light.

"No," Bram said.

"Bram—"

"No. Listen to me, you insufferable, self-sacrificing, *vanishing* man. Ten years ago you decided alone what your life was worth and paid with mine along with yours, and I have been angry about it every day since, and I will be angry about it every day after, and I have *earned* a vote." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "You don't get to disappear again. Whatever you give it, you give it with us standing there. That's the terms. Ours, not yours."

Rosalind looked at him — at this stubborn, furious, load-bearing man — and felt something in his chest unlock that had rusted shut a decade ago.

"All right," he said. "Together, then."

And that was when the horizon lit.

Not dawn — the wrong direction, the wrong color, a bruised gold crawling up the southern sky where the capital lay. The ground shivered. Pip's humming spiked into something frantic, and Ivet was suddenly beside them with her knife drawn, staring south.

"That's not a fire," she said. "That's a procession. Torches — hundreds. He's coming." Her voice went flat and cold. "Solmere. With his harvest. He means to force the Gate tonight."

Rosalind turned back to the seam. It had begun, very softly, to tremble — and beneath the choir-sound, for the first time, he heard fear.

The new moon was rising, and the door was about to be broken by men who had never once asked it what it wanted.
