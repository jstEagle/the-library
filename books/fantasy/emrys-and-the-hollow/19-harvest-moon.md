## 19. Harvest Moon

The new moon rose like a held breath, and Solmere's army came up the valley beneath it with torches lit, as if darkness were something to be punished rather than endured.

Ivet saw them first from the ridge above the threshold—a column of silver and black winding through the dead fields, a mile of it, maybe more. Chained mages staggered in the middle ranks, their wrists bound in iron that drank the light around them. At the column's head rode a palanquin she knew too well, lacquered and gold-leafed, carried on the shoulders of twelve wardens who had once been her colleagues.

"He brought the whole harvest," she said. Her voice came out flat. She'd been practicing flat for three days now, ever since Grayford Bridge, and mostly it worked.

"Of course he did," Bram said beside her. He was checking the straps on his crossbow with the unhurried care of a man who had decided that if this was his last night, he would at least die with well-maintained equipment. "Man spends ten years feeding the thing names one at a time. Why not open the pantry all at once?"

Below them, the Hollow Gate stood in its wound of earth—not a gate at all anymore, really. It was a seam in the world, a place where the grass grew gray and the air tasted faintly of every goodbye anyone had ever said badly. Rosalind had spent the afternoon walking its edge, touching nothing, murmuring to it in a language Ivet didn't know. When she'd asked him what he was saying, he'd smiled with an expression she could only call homesick.

"Introductions," he'd said.

Now he stood at the seam's edge with his sleeves rolled up and his hands empty, which frightened Ivet more than any weapon would have.

* * *

Bram counted torches because counting kept his hands steady. Four hundred soldiers, give or take. Sixty wardens. One High Warden in a chair being carried uphill by other people, which told Bram everything he needed to know about Solmere's relationship with effort.

"Odile." He didn't look away from the column. "How long can you hold?"

Odile sat cross-legged on a flat stone with her father's charts spread around her like a card reader's table, her ink-stained fingers pinning down corners against a wind that shouldn't have existed. The battlefield—the whole valley—existed right now because she was remembering it out loud, a low continuous murmur: *the millstone by the ford, the alder row along the western fence, the shepherd's path, the second stile.* Every few minutes a piece of the landscape flickered like a lamp running dry, and her voice stitched it back.

"As long as I can talk," she said. Then, honestly, because Odile was always honest: "My voice is going to give out before theirs does."

"Then we make it quick."

"You've never made anything quick in your life, Bram Ferrow."

He grinned despite himself. "No. But I'm holding the line tonight, apparently. Lines are quick."

Pip sat pressed against Odile's side, humming. The child had been humming since sundown, and the tune had changed—it wasn't the Hollow's slow grieving song anymore but something faster, brighter, wrong the way a smile is wrong on the wrong face. Pip looked up at Bram with eyes gone wide and dark.

"It's excited," Pip said. "It thinks they're bringing it presents."

Bram's stomach turned over. He thought of the chained mages in the middle ranks, sixty or more, each one a person who'd been arrested for the crime of existing wrongly. Each one a meal.

"Right," he said. "Well. Let's go ruin its dinner."

* * *

They met the vanguard at the old ford, where Odile's remembered millstone stood guard over water that ran backward if you watched it too long.

Bram had planned it the way smugglers plan things: cheaply, dishonestly, and with maximum inconvenience to the opposition. The smugglers who'd answered his summons—forty-odd river rats from every bend of the Corvale waterway, men and women he'd cheated and been cheated by for twenty years—had spent the evening stringing lines across the shallows and sinking every boat, barrel, and floating thing they owned just upstream.

When the vanguard hit the ford, the river gave back everything it had been holding.

It wasn't a flood, exactly. It was an ambush of debris—barrels, planks, a drowned lantern or two still burning under the black water, surfacing suddenly like accusations. Horses went down. Torchlines broke. And into that chaos came Ivet Kess, warden-trained, moving through the smoke and shouting commands in the formal cadence of the Order, and half the vanguard obeyed her before their brains caught up with their ears.

"Bram!" she yelled, driving her pike-butt into a soldier's chest plate. "Left flank! They're cutting the mages loose from the rear—they're going to drive them toward the Gate!"

"I see it!"

"Don't die!"

"That's the plan! Generally!"

* * *

Solmere watched the ford dissolve into farce from his palanquin and felt, for one unguarded moment, something close to delight.

He had expected resistance. He had hoped for it, in fact—resistance meant survivors worth interrogating, meant names, meant more material. What he had not expected was the small figure standing on the millstone with a stolen warden's pike planted before her like a boundary marker.

"Apprentice Kess," he called, when the fighting thinned enough for voices to carry. His wardens formed a crescent behind him, and the chained mages were driven forward into the space between, stumbling, iron-cuffed, human shields arranged in neat rows. "You were the finest student I ever failed to corrupt. Come home. There will be no punishment. There will be a promotion."

Ivet walked toward him across the mud, and Bram, watching from the flank with his heart in his throat, saw that she moved exactly like Rosalind did when he approached the Gate—like someone approaching something they loved and intended to survive anyway.

"You fed them to it," she said. "One at a time. For years. I did your paperwork."

"Yes." Solmere said it the way a man admits to pruning a garden. "And the country slept soundly, and the stars stayed up, and no one thanked me. Do you know what I learned in those years, Kess? That fear is a currency, and I am the mint. Tonight I stop making coins and start owning the bank. The Hollow will open for me, and what remains of this country will be ruled by the only man who understands it."

"It doesn't want to be ruled." Ivet's voice cracked, and she let it. "It wants to be *mourned*. You can't even mourn your own—"

She never finished. Solmere lifted one hand, and the iron cuffs on the chained mages flared white, and they screamed, all of them at once, and drove themselves forward at her like puppets whose strings had been pulled by grief.

* * *

Rosalind felt every one of those screams land in the Gate behind him.

He had been trying, all evening, to do the hardest thing he had ever attempted: nothing. To stand at the threshold and wait, trusting that the family he'd assembled out of guilt and luck and love could buy him the time he needed. But the Gate was drinking the pain of sixty captive mages, growing fat and bright and awake, and the seam in the world was widening by inches, and Pip's frantic humming was rising toward a pitch that made teeth ache.

"They're using them as a key," Pip said, appearing at his elbow, having crossed a battlefield the way only children and cats can. "It hurts them and the door likes it. That's the trick. That's the whole trick."

"I know, little one."

"Are you scared?"

Rosalind considered lying, decided Pip deserved better, and said, "Terrified."

"Good." Pip took his hand, small and grimy and certain. "Odile says scared means you're paying attention."

Down the slope, Ivet was fighting the people she'd trained beside, weeping without slowing, and Bram's smugglers were falling back by inches, and Odile's voice was fraying thread by thread—the millstone flickered, steadied, flickered—and the new moon reached the top of the sky, a coin of perfect absence, and Solmere raised both arms and began the harvest in earnest.

Sixty mages' magic tore free of them all at once.

The light of it was beautiful. That was the worst part. It rose off the chained rows like heat off summer stone, silver and green and gold, every color a person could be, streaming toward the Gate in a river of everything stolen—and the seam opened like an eye.

Rosalind stepped forward into the light.

"No," he said—not loudly, but the valley heard it, because Odile was still talking, still remembering, and her memory carried his voice the way a bell carries a strike. "This is not yours. None of it was ever yours."

Solmere turned, and for the first time in ten years, the High Warden of Corvale looked afraid.

* * *

Ivet saw it happen in pieces, the way disasters are always seen.

She saw Rosalind walk into the river of stolen light and not burn. She saw the light bend around him, recognize him, hesitate—she would swear to her dying day that it *hesitated*, like a dog hearing an old name. She saw Solmere shout an order that no one obeyed, because everyone on that field, warden and smuggler and chained mage alike, had stopped to watch a charm-seller in rolled-up sleeves stand between the country and its hunger.

She saw Solmere draw the warden's blade—the black one, the one reserved for unmaking—and start down from the palanquin himself.

And she found, rising in her chest where fear should have been, something calm and cold and entirely her own.

"Master," she said, and set herself in his path. "You're under arrest."

He actually laughed. "By whose authority?"

"Mine." She leveled her pike. "Turns out I'm the last honest warden left. Somebody has to do the paperwork."

What followed was not a duel in any ballad's sense. Ballads want footwork and quips. This was two people who had trained together for six years trying to hurt each other with total competence, blades ringing in the strange gray light, Solmere's power lashing out in threads of compulsion—Ivet's knees buckling, her own hand turning traitor against her—and Ivet answering with the one lesson she'd learned since leaving the Order: magic was not a crime. Magic was a *voice*. She stopped suppressing hers for the first time in her adult life and spoke.

The pike blazed. The compulsion shattered. Solmere staggered, and Ivet struck the black blade from his hand, and it spun away into the grass and lay there like a snake with its head cut off.

"You were the best of us," Solmere said, on his knees in the mud, genuinely puzzled, which was somehow the most damning thing about him. "Why?"

"Because someone has to witness it," Ivet said. "All of it. Even you."

Behind them, the Gate stood open, and Rosalind Dunmore stood in the doorway of the world, and the new moon hung overhead like a blank page waiting to be written on—and the Hollow, vast and grieving and finally, fully awake, began to speak.

* * *

Bram heard it from the ford, where he was kneeling beside a smuggler named Tansy who had taken a blade meant for him and was insisting, with her characteristic stubbornness, that it was merely a flesh wound and also that he owed her eleven crowns.

The voice didn't come through the ears. It came through the memory, through the soles of the feet, through the place in the chest where lost things live. It sounded like every village that wasn't there anymore. It sounded like his mother's kitchen. It sounded, God help him, like Rosalind, ten years younger, saying *goodbye* badly.

"Hold the line," Bram whispered—to Tansy, to himself, to the forty smugglers still standing, to the whole ragged idea of them. "Whatever happens. Hold the line."

He picked up his crossbow, turned toward the light, and started uphill—because the man he loved was standing in a doorway between worlds, and Bram Ferrow had walked away from him once already, and there was not enough erasure left in the universe to make him do it twice.
