## 21. Dawn With Stars In It

Ivet Kess had spent her whole life learning what the sky was supposed to look like. Warden training included astronomy—not for wonder, but for navigation and, more practically, for spotting the telltale shimmer of unauthorized scrying. She could name every constellation in the Corvale almanac. She had drawn them from memory a hundred times in the margins of her ledgers, back when drawing them felt like duty.

So when the first star came back, she knew exactly which one it was.

"The Ferryman's Eye," she whispered, because saying it out loud made it real, and because Pip was asleep against Bram's shoulder and someone in this ruined company ought to keep records.

They were camped on the threshold of the Hollow—the great scar in the world where the Gate stood open now, not as a wound but as a doorway, its edges glowing like the rim of a lamp seen through fog. Rosalind had walked through it an hour ago. Or a year ago. Time behaved strangely near an open door between what was and what had been refused.

The star hung low over the eastern hills, trembling, as if it weren't sure it was welcome.

Then a second star. A third. And then the whole sky tore open like a curtain drawn back after a decade of dust, and the stars came pouring home.

Pip woke up screaming with joy.

Ivet would remember that sound for the rest of her life—this small, hoarse child who had learned to hum the Hollow's song before they'd ever met her, standing on a battlefield littered with Solmere's broken column, arms flung up at a sky full of light, laughing so hard she fell over backward into Bram's lap. Bram caught her without looking away from the sky himself. His jaw was working. Ivet pretended not to notice that he was crying, and he pretended not to notice that she noticed, and that was how they managed each other: carefully, kindly, without making anyone say anything.

"Look," Odile breathed. She was propped against a cart wheel, her bandaged side rising and falling too fast, but her eyes were bright as the stars she'd spent her life charting from memory. "Look at the—there. There! The Shepherd's Crook. The Nine Sisters. I drew them all wrong, I drew them from my father's notes, I never thought I'd—" Her voice cracked. She pressed her chart roll against her chest like a shield. "I'm going to have to redraw everything."

"You're going to have to *rest* everything," Bram said. "Doctor's orders. I've decided I'm a doctor."

"You held a bridge together with your bare hands six hours ago."

"And my hands are fine. My hands are heroes." He flexed them theatrically. They were shaking. Nobody called him on it.

But Ivet wasn't looking at the sky anymore. She was looking at the ground.

Because the erased places were coming back too.

It started at the horizon—a shimmer along the eastern ridgeline, like heat off summer stone, except it was cold morning and the shimmer moved *toward* them. Then the ridge itself seemed to breathe, and where there had been empty grassland there was suddenly a road. An actual road, rutted and honest, running east toward hills that hadn't existed at dawn. A farmhouse assembled itself out of nothing, wall by wall, chimney last, smoke already curling from it as though the fire inside had never gone out. A woman stepped out of the farmhouse door, saw the sky, dropped her water bucket, and sat down hard in the dirt.

"They're surfacing," Ivet said. "Like divers. Coming up for air."

She said it quietly, but Pip heard. The child scrambled up and ran three steps toward the returning land before Bram snagged her collar.

"We don't know what's out there yet," he said gently.

"It's *Grayford*," Pip said. "I can hear it. It sounds like bread."

And it did—Ivet could hear it too, faintly, under the wind. Not words. More like the memory of a smell, or the feeling of a room you loved. Grayford Bridge, where Rosalind had confessed, where he'd told them the truth about the village he'd traded away: Hollowmere, and everyone in it. If the erasure was reversing—

"Hollowmere," Ivet said aloud, and hated herself for the hope in it, because hope was a knife you handed to other people and asked them to hold.

Bram went very still. "You think—"

"I don't know. I don't know what the bargain does. I don't know if—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "He gave his *name*, Bram. That was the price. We heard him say it. Whatever comes back, we have to be ready for the fact that he might not be in it."

* * *

They waited.

There was nothing else to do. Odile slept in fits; Bram foraged the returning countryside, which had the disorienting generosity of places that remembered being inhabited—orchards heavy with fruit nobody had picked, wells full and sweet. Ivet walked the threshold of the Gate and kept watch, because keeping watch was the only prayer she knew.

The Gate itself had changed. She'd expected—she didn't know what. Roaring dark. Teeth. Instead it stood quiet, a tall archway of pale stone that hadn't been pale stone yesterday, and through it she could see... not the Hollow. Something softer. Light like late afternoon in a place she'd never been but missed anyway. Occasionally something moved in there, slow and vast and sorrowful, like a whale passing beneath a boat.

Once, she thought she heard singing. Many voices. She didn't go closer. Some doors you knock on; some doors knock on you, and you wait.

Near midday, the land to the south shivered.

Ivet felt it in her teeth before she saw it—a rolling tremor, gentle as a cat stretching. Then, across a mile of meadow that had been empty scrubland since before Ivet was born, a village surfaced.

She knew it instantly, though she'd never seen it. She knew it from Rosalind's face every time he'd almost spoken of it. Thatched roofs. A crooked bell tower. A mill by a stream that glittered like spilled coins. An orchard. A green with a maypole, absurdly, wonderfully intact.

Hollowmere.

The company gathered at the edge of the rise without anyone calling them together. Even Odile came, leaning on Bram, gray-faced and refusing to admit it. Pip held Ivet's hand so tightly her fingers went numb, and Ivet let her.

People were moving in the village. Figures in the lanes. A man carrying buckets. Children chasing a dog.

"They're alive," Bram said. His voice was wrecked. "They're—ten years, Ivet, ten years, how are they—"

"I don't know." Her warden's mind offered answers she didn't want: time runs differently behind a sealed door; the erased aren't dead, only unfiled. She pushed them away. "Does it matter?"

"Yes," he said. "Yes, it matters, it matters *enormously*, and also no, not even slightly." He laughed, wet and cracked. "There's the miller. That's Aldous the miller, I used to run his flour downriver, he used to cheat me on weight and I let him because his wife made plum brandy—"

"Bram." Ivet caught his arm. "Wait."

Because down the lane, past the well, past the maypole, a figure had stopped walking. A slight figure in a travel-stained coat, dark hair gone longer than it had been a week ago, standing very still among people who were embracing and shouting and falling to their knees in the grass.

The figure turned. Looked up the hill. Straight at them.

Ivet's heart did something complicated and painful.

"He can't see us," she said. "Can he? From here?"

"He's looking right at us," Pip whispered.

They went down. None of them decided to; their legs simply agreed on it. The walk took forever and no time at all. The villagers watched them come with the wary warmth of people who half-remembered being saved and couldn't say by whom. Someone pressed bread into Pip's hands. Someone else started crying when they saw Odile's charts under her arm and said, "You're the mapmaker's girl—you're the one who kept drawing us"—and Odile, who had held a bridge of remembered stone against an army, burst into tears at the word *us*.

And then they reached him.

Rosalind Dunmore stood in the middle of his own resurrected village, and Ivet saw it immediately, the way you see a missing stair in a house you know by heart.

He was looking at them with polite, open attention. The particular attention of a stranger meeting new people. Kind. Curious. Slightly puzzled.

"Hello," he said. His voice was the same voice. That was the cruelest part. "I'm sorry—are you travelers? We don't get many, since—" He frowned, reaching for something. "Since. Hmm. Since..."

"Aldous!" someone shouted from the mill. "The wheel's turning! The wheel's turning again!"

The moment passed through him like weather. He smiled, apologetic, and turned toward the shout, and Ivet understood.

The Hollow had taken his name. Not just the word—everything the word carried. Ten years in Corvale. The shop. The midnight rounds. The sealing of the Gate. The bargain. The confession at Grayford Bridge. All of it filed away in the dark, paid out like coin, spent to buy back a country. What stood in the lane was the shape of him with the story scooped out.

"Bram," Ivet said quietly. "Don't."

But Bram was already stepping forward, and Ivet realized she'd misread him. He wasn't reaching for Rosalind. He was reaching past him—for the space where the story should have been—and what he said was:

"Do you like plum brandy?"

Rosalind blinked. Turned back. Considered this strange question from a large, rough-looking riverman with red-rimmed eyes. "I—don't know," he said honestly. "Should I?"

"There's a woman here," Bram said, steady as a keel, "Marta Aldousdatter, who makes the best plum brandy in three counties, and cheats smugglers on weight, and I have it on excellent authority that anyone who tastes it is never quite the same afterward. So. Do you want to find out?"

Something crossed Rosalind's face. Not recognition—nothing so kind. But a flicker, the way a sleeping man frowns at a sound. "...That sounds like something I should want," he said slowly. "I feel like I've lost a bet I don't remember making."

"You have," Ivet said, stepping up beside Bram. "With us. And we intend to collect."

He looked at her then—at the stolen ledger still strapped under her coat, at the warden's posture she couldn't fully unlearn—and something else flickered. Wariness, maybe. Or its opposite.

"You talk like a lawbook," he said.

"I *was* a lawbook," Ivet said. "I'm being rewritten."

Pip tugged his sleeve. He crouched automatically, easily, the way you do for children whether or not you remember why. Pip studied his face with the terrible frankness of the young, then looked up at Ivet.

"He's sad," Pip announced. "Underneath. He doesn't know why. It's the same song. It's quieter, but it's the same song."

"Pip," Bram warned.

"No, it's good!" Pip insisted. "Songs don't stop being songs when you forget the words. You just have to hum them till somebody remembers."

Silence, then. The kind of silence that has weight and texture. Around them Hollowmere went on resurrecting—laughter from the orchard, the millwheel knocking out its old rhythm, a bell in the crooked tower ringing noon for the first time in ten years, though no one could say who pulled the rope.

Rosalind straightened up from crouching and looked at the four of them lined up in his lane like a jury, or a family, or both.

"I don't know any of you," he said. Not unkindly. Carefully, the way you handle something that might break. "Do I?"

"No," Ivet said.

"Yes," Bram said, at the same moment.

They looked at each other. Bram shrugged. "Both things are true. That's sort of our whole thing."

Rosalind's mouth twitched. Just barely. The ghost of a smile belonging to a man none of them had ever actually met—the man before the hiding, before the Gate, before the cost. Maybe he'd never existed. Maybe he was surfacing too, somewhere far below, swimming upward through the dark toward air.

"Well," he said. "I appear to have nowhere else to be, and you appear to know where the brandy lives. It seems irresponsible of me to refuse."

"That," said Odile, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, "is the most sensible thing anyone's said all year."

* * *

They stayed in Hollowmere three days, because the village fed them and wouldn't hear otherwise, and because Odile needed rest and would accept it from no one but an entire community of the previously erased, who took turns bringing her soup and arguing about which landmarks she'd drawn wrong.

On the second evening, Ivet found Rosalind at the millstream, alone, skipping stones badly.

"Your form's terrible," she said, sitting beside him.

"Is it? I keep feeling like I used to be better at things." He tossed another stone. It plunked. "Everything feels like that. Like there's a me-shaped hole in my head and the edges of it ache. Does that make sense?"

"Perfect sense."

"You knew me. Before." It wasn't quite a question.

"Yes."

"Was I..." He turned the next stone over in his fingers. "Was I good?"

Ivet thought about charm shops and midnight rounds. About a decade of chosen invisibility. About a man who'd traded his own name to give a country back its dead. About the ledger in her coat, full of names, hers among them once, and the order she'd betrayed, and the people she'd chosen instead.

"You were the best person I ever arrested for," she said. "That's not much of an answer. Ask me again in a year."

He laughed—startled, rusty, real. "A year. You're planning on a year."

"I'm planning on several." She pulled the ledger out and set it between them on the bank. "This is yours, actually. Part of it. Your page is in here. Registered mage, Corvale district, charges pending." She tore the page out, folded it into a paper boat, and set it on the stream. They watched it wobble downstream toward the millwheel. "Consider the charges dropped. New administration. Very lenient. Terrible at paperwork."

"What happens now?" he asked. "To any of us? The Gate's open. The stars are back. Somebody has to—"

"Somebody will," Ivet said. "Not tonight. Tonight there's brandy, and Marta cheats everyone on weight, and tomorrow—" She paused, listening. Under the millwheel's knock, under the evening birdsong, she could almost hear it: the song from beyond the door, patient and vast and grieving, waiting to be witnessed properly. Waiting for keepers. "Tomorrow we start figuring out how to tend an open door. All of us. It's going to take years, and none of us knows what we're doing, and I expect we'll argue constantly."

"That sounds awful," Rosalind said.

"It's the best thing I've ever heard," Ivet said, and meant it.

Downstream, the paper boat caught the current and sailed—nameless, unburdened, going wherever the water decided. Above them the first stars were coming out, all of them home, all of them accounted for.

Except, Pip would insist later, one. One small star, low over the Gate, that hadn't come back from anywhere at all—but had, somehow, been born there. New. Hanging above the doorway like a lamp left lit for someone expected.

They'd need a name for it, eventually. Odile was already sharpening her pencils.
