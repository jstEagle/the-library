## 22. The Keeper of an Open Door

The bell above the shop door had been Pip's idea. Not a charm-bell, she'd insisted—those were for warning off spirits and bad debts—but an ordinary brass bell bought from the market with ordinary coins, so that when it rang, it meant only one thing: someone had come in.

Rosalind was restocking the shelf of honest charms when it rang on the first morning of autumn, a full year to the day since the stars came back.

"Morning," said the customer, a farmer's wife from two villages over, which was itself a small miracle, because a year ago there had been no village two over. It had come back in June, whole and smelling faintly of woodsmoke, its churchyard full of names nobody remembered giving but everybody agreed to keep.

"Morning," Rosalind said. "What can I do for you?"

"My cow won't calve. Old Marta used to say you sold things that worked."

Rosalind set down his rag. "Marta was generous. I sell knots and braided thread and little bags of dried lavender. If your cow is troubled, what she needs is a steady hand and warm water, and I can tell you where to find both."

The woman looked at him the way people had looked at him all year—with that particular Corvale squint that meant *I know what they say about you, and I haven't decided whether to believe it.* "They say you opened the Hollow Gate."

"They say a lot of things."

"They say you gave away your name to do it."

"That part's true." He smiled, because it was easier now than it had been in the spring, when saying it aloud still felt like pressing on a bruise. "But I'm told names grow back if enough people insist on calling you something."

"And what do they call you?"

"Rosalind. Mostly. Pip calls me Ros when she wants honey."

The woman bought a knot of red thread—for luck, not magic, he made that distinction clear—and left with her cow problem unsolved by sorcery and, he suspected, soon solved by patience. The bell rang behind her. Rosalind stood a moment in the quiet shop, in the smell of lavender and beeswax and old paper, and let himself feel the thing he still wasn't entirely used to feeling:

He was not hiding.

For ten years, every customer who walked through that door had been a risk to be managed, a face to be forgotten before it could remember him. Now the door stood open from dawn until dusk, and the only thing he had to hide was the fact that he'd burned the morning's bread again, and Pip would mock him for it without mercy.

* * *

Pip arrived at midmorning, as she did most days, by way of the roof, because doors were for other people. She came through the back window feet-first, landed in a crouch among the drying herbs, and held up a jam jar with the triumph of a general presenting a captured banner.

"Look what I found."

Inside the jar, a star-fragment no bigger than a thumbnail drifted lazily against the glass, shedding light like slow honey. Not one of the old ones—the old fragments had gone dim after the Gate was answered, their borrowed memories finally laid to rest. This was new. A piece of the sky that fell in the night, bright with whatever the stars remembered *now*.

"You're supposed to bring those to the Gate," Rosalind said.

"I'm bringing it to the Gate. Eventually. It wanted to see the shop first." Pip turned the jar, watching the fragment tumble. She was taller this year, all elbows and sharp questions, her hair cropped short for practicality and kept short, she claimed, because it annoyed the Wardens' new recruitment officers, who preferred children who looked obedient. "It's got a funny feeling in it. Not sad like the old ones. More like—" she hunted for the word, "—like the first day of summer holidays."

"The stars have summer holidays?"

"Everything does. You just have to listen." She set the jar carefully on the counter, next to the ledger Odile had given her for exactly this purpose, and wrote the date in her careful, backwards-leaning hand. Then, because she was twelve and could not help herself: "Bram says you cried at the harvest festival."

"Bram says many things."

"He says you cried into the cider."

"It was a long year," Rosalind said with dignity, "and the cider was very good."

* * *

The Gate stood three days' walk east of Corvale, where the border towns had once thinned into fields and the fields into rumor. It did not look like much, which was the point. Ten years ago it had been a wound in the world—a seam of hungry dark that ate memory and starlight and, eventually, villages entire. Now it was a doorway: a frame of pale stone that Odile swore matched no architecture of any living country, standing free in a meadow of grass that grew green all winter.

Around it, someone had built a garden.

That had been Bram's doing, mostly, though he denied it with the vigor of a man denying sentiment. Terraces of vegetables climbed the hillside below the Gate. Beehives hummed along the southern wall. There was a well, a smokehouse, a long table under an arbor heavy with grapes, because Bram Ferrow held the unshakable conviction that no sacred duty should be performed hungry.

They arrived at the Gate in the golden hour, all five of them, which had become their habit on the first day of each season. Ivet came up from the wardens' station at the road's end, out of uniform, though she still wore the warden's ring on a cord around her neck—turned inward, so the sigil faced her heart instead of the world. Odile arrived last, as always, walking with her father's rolled charts under one arm and a cane she used mostly as a pointer, having decided that gravity was a suggestion and pain a nuisance to be negotiated with rather than obeyed.

"Well?" Ivet said, when they'd all gathered at the threshold. "Anything?"

Rosalind put his hand flat against the stone of the doorframe.

This was the work now. Not sealing—never sealing again—but listening. The Gate was awake and open a crack, the way a window stands open in a house where someone is always home. Through it, faintly, came the sound it had always made, the sound Pip had heard even as an orphan in the camps: breathing. Slow. Vast. But no longer hungry. A year ago, Rosalind had stepped through that door and offered the thing beyond the only gift it had ever been given—not a prison, not a sacrifice, but witness. *You were everything we threw away,* he had told it. *Every place we erased, every name we let fall out of the world. We see you. We mourn you. Go, if you can; stay, if you must; but be seen.*

And the Hollow—which was not a monster but a grief, the accumulated sorrow of a country that had spent centuries pretending its dead did not exist—had accepted.

Mostly. Grief, Rosalind had learned, was not a door you closed. It was a door you tended.

"She's restless today," he reported. "Something on the far side. Not angry. Expectant."

"Expectant how?" Ivet asked, in the tone of a woman who had learned never to dismiss a child's or a novelist's instincts about the Gate.

"Like the night before a festival."

Odile unrolled a chart across the garden table and weighted its corners with stones. Her maps had changed since the world came back. Where she had once charted places that existed only in memory, she now charted the borders between what returned and what hadn't—because not everything had. Some villages had surfaced empty, their houses whole and their hearths cold. Some had come back wrong by a street or a season. And past the far edge of her newest map, drawn in ink mixed with starlight, lay a region marked simply: *Not yet.*

"There," Odile said, tapping it. "That's where the expectant feeling lives, if feelings live anywhere. Three hundred miles of country that hasn't come back. My father charted its edge eleven times before he died. It moves."

"It breathes," Pip corrected. "In and out. When it breathes in, the edge moves closer. When it breathes out—" she shrugged, "—it lets go a little."

"Then we'll want to talk to it while it's exhaling," Bram said, coming around the side of the smokehouse with a tray of bread and cheese and the expression of a man who had opinions about conducting diplomacy unfed. He set the tray down, glanced at Rosalind, and added, gruffly, "There's honey. For the growing girl who tattles on me."

"I stand by my testimony," Pip said, already eating.

* * *

They ate as the sun went down, and the talk turned—as it did every season—to the question that had split the country's councils for a year and showed no sign of healing: what was to be done about magic?

The law banning it had died with High Warden Solmere, unmade by the erasure he had tried to wield, leaving behind an order in disgrace and a country with no rules about the gift except the oldest one: *some people have it.* The provisional council in Corvale had spent twelve months arguing. Petitions came weekly. Half the country wanted the Wardens rebuilt, stricter than before. Half wanted every law burned and the registers published as an apology. And a growing middle—led, to everyone's astonishment, by farmers and fishwives and the sort of people who had never cared about politics until politics came for their neighbors—wanted something in between: schools, maybe. Licenses, possibly. A country that trusted itself enough to look at its own strange children without flinching.

"They want me to testify at the assembly next month," Ivet said, turning her ring on its cord. "About the harvest. About what Solmere did. About what the order knew."

"Will you?"

"I don't know." She said it plainly, which was how Ivet said everything now; the girl who had fled with a stolen ledger had grown into a woman who could admit uncertainty without it feeling like defeat. "Every time I start to write my statement, I end up writing about you instead. About the shop. About how easy it would have been to find you any time in ten years, if anyone had actually looked. The law didn't fail because it was too weak. It failed because it was built to make hiding the sensible choice." She looked up. "How do you testify to that without indicting everyone who ever obeyed? Including me?"

"You don't," Rosalind said. "You just tell it true, and let them do the arithmetic."

"That's terrible advice."

"It's the only kind I give. Ask Bram."

"Oh, his advice is worse," Bram said comfortably. "His advice is always some version of *open the door and see what happens.*"

"It worked once."

"It got you eaten by a hole in the world."

"For a little while," Rosalind conceded. "And I came back."

Bram's hand found his under the table, brief and rough and certain, and neither of them made anything of it, because they had spent a year learning that the truest things between them needed no announcement. That was the shape love had taken after everything: not the burning thing of ten years ago, nor the raw wound of Grayford Bridge, but something quieter and load-bearing, like the beam of a house that holds the roof without asking for thanks.

* * *

Full dark came, and it was time.

They stood together at the threshold, as they did every season, and Rosalind pressed both hands to the stone and spoke the words he had spoken a thousand times now—not a spell, nothing so grand, just the litany of witness that Odile had helped him write and Pip kept correcting:

"We see you. We remember what was lost. We are sorry. We are here."

The Gate breathed.

And through the crack of it, as always, came the answering—not words, never words, but a pressure of presence, vast and sorrowful and, this year, unmistakably gentler. The erased dead did not return through it; that bargain had been settled honestly, and Rosalind did not regret it even on the hard nights. What came through instead was smaller and stranger: sometimes a scent, woodsmoke or cut hay from a village no map remembered. Sometimes a sound, half a lullaby in a language the country had let die. Once, memorably, a single perfect apple, which Pip had eaten despite universal objection and declared *fine, a bit sad-tasting.*

Tonight, the Gate exhaled, and the air through the crack smelled of rain on hot stone, and somewhere far inside, something that might have been a voice or might have been wind said—or seemed to say—a single word that all five of them heard differently and all five of them, later, described the same way:

*Soon.*

Pip's jar, sitting on the garden table, flared bright as a struck match. The new star-fragment spun once, twice, and settled, glowing like the first day of summer holidays.

"It's counting down," Pip whispered. She wasn't afraid. She had stopped being afraid of the Hollow a year ago, in the way of children who look directly at large sad things and refuse to look away. "Rosalind. It's counting down to something."

"To what?" Ivet asked.

Nobody answered. Odile was already reaching for her charts, her eyes gone distant, mapping in her head. Bram had gone still in the way he went still before a river went bad. And Rosalind stood with his hands on the stone of the open door, feeling through his palms the slow enormous breath of the thing beyond, and thought of a country arguing itself awake in candlelit assembly halls, of laws not yet written, of a name he had given away and gotten back one caller at a time—and understood, with the calm of a man who has already survived his own worst ending, that the door he kept was not finished with him yet.

Whatever was coming, it would not wait for the assembly to finish its debates.

"Well," Rosalind said, turning back to his family in the starlight, "I suppose we'd better get the garden in order."
