## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

Market day in the Valley of Hollow smelled of wet wool and frying onions, and Kestrel Ishikawa moved through it the way water moves through cloth — present everywhere, noticed nowhere.

He had been working the crowd since the bells rang terce from the chapel of Saint Aldhelm's-Remnant, which was what the valley called its one church now, though no one living remembered who Aldhelm had been or what remained of him. The stalls along Cooper's Row leaned under bolts of gray flannel and pyramids of late apples, and the vendors called out prices in voices worn smooth as river stones, and above it all hung the sky — a flat, pale ceiling of cloud that never quite rained and never quite cleared, as if even the weather had agreed to some long, unspoken compromise.

Kestrel wore his compromise too: a coat of undyed wool, patched at the elbows by his own hand because paying a tailor meant being remembered by a tailor, and being remembered was the one expense he could not afford. His hair was brown. His eyes were brown. He had spent years making himself the color of nothing, and he was very good at it.

The soldier came through the north gate at half past noon.

Kestrel saw him before he saw anything else about him — saw the way the man walked, heel-first, weight settled low, the walk of someone who had learned that the ground could betray him. A soldier's walk. The valley got perhaps six travelers a month, peddlers mostly, pilgrims going to nowhere in particular because the roads beyond the passes had gone strange in the last few years, and soldiers were rare enough to be interesting. This one was thirty maybe, sunburned to the color of old brick, with a sword wrapped in sacking on his back like something he was ashamed of. He stopped at an apple stall and counted out coins twice, lips moving, as if arithmetic were a language he'd once known and was relearning.

Kestrel drifted closer, bought an apple he didn't want, and let the crowd carry him into the soldier's shadow.

*Reading* a person was the easy part. Anyone with patience could do it — watch where the eyes went, count the blinks, notice how the hands behaved when they weren't being told what to do. The soldier's hands kept rising toward his temple and then falling away, again and again, like a man swatting at flies only he could see. His gaze snagged on the brazier outside the pie-seller's stall, where a scrap of fat had caught flame, and stayed there three seconds too long. When a cart rattled past loaded with charcoal, black as burnt bone, the soldier went gray around the mouth.

Fire, then. Fire and something worse than fire.

Kestrel finished his apple, dropped the core into the gutter, and began the work.

It took four exchanges over two hours, because good work was slow work. A question about the road north, answered with a grunt. A shared complaint about the price of oats. A moment at the well where Kestrel, all apology, jostled the man's elbow and steadied him with a grip on the forearm — skin to skin, three heartbeats, enough to taste the surface of him. What came back was smoke and screaming and a name, *Marta*, repeated like a prayer said wrong, and beneath all of it a city burning in a way cities did not burn anymore, towers coming down in slow orange silence while people ran below like ants whose hill had been kicked.

Kestrel stepped back, murmured another apology, and lost himself in the crowd with his heart beating quick and shallow.

There it was, then. A memory worth money. Not the whole of it — he never took the whole of anything; that was butchery, and besides, a hollowed man died or went mad, and either way the magistrates asked questions the valley had no patience for. No, you took a slice. You took the thing that woke them at night, the thing behind their eyes when they stared at walls, and you left the rest: the name, the face, the dull daily grief that a body could carry the way it carried a bad knee. People healed around the absence. That was the mercy of it, and Kestrel had told himself it was a mercy so many times over the years that the words had worn smooth as the vendors' voices, smooth as everything in this valley where nothing sharp was permitted to stay sharp.

He took the slice at dusk, in the alley behind the tannery where the smell covered everything and no one lingered.

The soldier sat on an overturned crate, drinking cheap wine from a clay cup, and Kestrel sat down beside him as if they were old friends, and maybe for the length of the work they were. Skin to wrist this time, a firm grip, the old gesture of comradeship. Kestrel closed his eyes and reached in and found the burning city waiting exactly where he'd left it, still roaring silently, still raining ash, and he lifted it out the way you lifted a hot stone from a fire — quickly, cleanly, with the whole of your attention.

The soldier shuddered once. Then he sighed, long and deep, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying, and looked at the wine in his cup with mild surprise, as if he couldn't quite recall why he'd been drinking.

"Bad day?" Kestrel asked.

"Don't know," the soldier said slowly. "Feels like I've had a lot of them." He frowned at nothing. "Was I somewhere? Before here?"

"Passing through," Kestrel said gently. "Most people are."

The soldier nodded and drank, and whatever had lived behind his eyes was now folded small inside Kestrel's chest, a coal wrapped in wet cloth, heavy and warm and not his own.

* * *

The widow was waiting for him at the shrine of the Nameless Saints, which stood at the crook of the millrace where the water went quiet and dark. It wasn't much of a shrine — three stones stacked by hands unknown, older than the church, older probably than the town — but people came to it anyway, because the Valley of Hollow had a hunger for holy things the way a starving man has a hunger for the smell of bread. Three hundred years since the gods had spoken here. Three hundred years of prayers going up like sparks from a doused fire, rising and fading and landing nowhere. The valley folk prayed anyway. They couldn't seem to help it.

Maren Collier stood among the stacked stones with her hands folded, and when she heard Kestrel's boots on the millstone she turned, and her face did the thing faces always did when they saw him clearly for the first time — a flicker of recognition without content, the look of someone meeting a person they felt they ought to know.

"You're the thief," she said.

"I'm a trader," Kestrel said. "Thief is such an ugly word for honest commerce."

"They say you can take a thing out of a person's head."

"They say a lot of things. Half of them are true and the other half are cheaper." He came to stand beside her at the stones. Below, the millrace turned black and patient. "You're Maren Collier. Your husband was Tam, a cooper. He drowned in the spring flood four years ago, and they never found the body, and every year when the snowmelt comes up the river you go down to the banks and watch the water as if it owes you something."

Her breath caught. "How—"

"Your sister told me. You should be careful what you tell your sister, Mrs. Collier. She worries about you, and worried people talk." He watched her hands tighten on each other. "She thinks you're dying of it. Slowly. She might be right."

"I don't want to forget Tam." The words came out fast and fierce, and then wavered. "That's not — I don't want to forget *him*. I want to forget the rest. The four years of the rest. Waking up and reaching across the bed. Hearing the rain start and thinking he'll have brought the barrels in, and then remembering there are no barrels and there is no bringing and there is no him, except there *is* him, he's just — under the water, somewhere, and I can't—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I dream about the flood. Every night. The same dream. The water coming up the lane and me standing in the doorway and I can't move and I know — I *know* in the dream — that if I move fast enough I can save him, and I never move fast enough. Four years. Every night the same dream, and every morning I wake up and he's dead all over again."

Kestrel said nothing. This was the part of the trade he hated, or told himself he hated, the part where he listened, because listening made the taking feel like surgery instead of theft, and he needed it to feel like surgery. He needed that very badly, some mornings more than others.

"I can take the dream," he said quietly. "Not the love. Not the man. Just the drowning — the flood, the water, the four years of waking up to it. You'd keep Tam the way you keep a photograph keeps a person. Whole, and still, and kind. You'd remember marrying him. The good mornings. You'd remember that you loved him and that he died, the fact of it, clean as a line in a ledger. But the water—" He shook his head. "The water would be gone."

"And the price?"

"The usual. Coin, if you have it. Work, if you don't. Or a memory of equal weight, given freely, which most people find they prefer." He smiled the smile he used for this part, the reasonable smile, the merchant's smile. "Grief weighs a great deal, Mrs. Collier. Most people are surprised what their sorrow fetches on the open market."

She laughed — a cracked, startled sound, half sob. "You're terrible."

"I'm the best in the valley," Kestrel said. "Which is less impressive than it sounds, since I'm also the only one."

She paid in coin, most of her savings, and neither of them remarked on that. They stood together at the dark water while he worked, his fingers resting light on the back of her wrist, and he went down into her the way he went down into everyone — carefully, hating himself a little, telling himself the little hate was proof he was still a decent man.

Her grief was exactly as she'd described it: a flood-dream laid down in layers, four years thick, each night adding sediment. He gathered it the way he gathered everything, folding it inward, compressing, until the whole drowning of Tam Collier fit in the palm of his mind like a stone. And then, because he was a professional, because he always did this last part whether he admitted it or not, he paused at the edge of her leaving and looked — just looked — at what remained.

And that was when he felt the other memory.

It wasn't hers. He knew her grief now the way he knew his own hands, and this was not woven anywhere into it. This was lodged deeper, stranger, sitting in her like a splinter sits in wood — foreign matter, old matter. Curiosity was supposed to be a vice he'd outgrown, but he was already turning toward it before he could stop himself, brushing against it lightly, the way you'd touch a knot in the dark to learn its shape.

A market square. Not this market — bigger, louder, hung with banners the colors of which he somehow could not hold in his mind, that slid away from attention like fish. And in the middle of the square, a face.

He had no word for what he saw. It was a face the way the sea is a puddle — technically accurate, catastrophically insufficient. It was vast and it was near and it was looking directly at Maren Collier, at some younger Maren, and its attention had weight, physical weight, the pressure of a hand on the crown of the head. And the face was —

*flicker*

— gone. Sliding away. He grasped for it and caught only an impression: gold, perhaps, or the idea of gold. An expression that might have been sorrow, or hunger, or recognition. And underneath, faint as a watermark, a single syllable of a name that his mind refused to carry even the distance of a heartbeat.

Kestrel pulled back so fast he nearly staggered.

Mrs. Collier blinked at him, serene, empty of her flood at last. "Is it done?" she asked, and touched her own cheek wonderingly. "I feel — lighter. Is that strange? I feel like I've set down a basket I forgot I was carrying." Her eyes went soft and distant. "Tam. My husband. He died in the flood, didn't he. Four years ago." She said it the way you'd read a line from a ledger, clean and factual, and then her face crumpled and reformed, crumpled and reformed, grief arriving without its teeth. "Oh. Oh, it doesn't hurt. Why doesn't it hurt?" She was crying and laughing at once. "Bless you. Bless you, whoever you are."

"Nobody," Kestrel said automatically. "I'm nobody."

She pressed coins into his hand, thanked him again, and went off along the millrace toward the lamplit lanes, walking lighter than she had come, and Kestrel stood alone at the shrine of the Nameless Saints with a stolen grief in one pocket and a stolen god's-face burning a hole through the other, and the dark water turned and turned below him and went nowhere.

* * *

His rooms above the chandler's shop were cold, as always, and he did not light a fire, as always. Fires invited neighbors to borrow coals, and borrowed coals became borrowed evenings, and evenings became friendships, and friendships were debts with better manners. He ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window, watching the market square empty itself below, lanterns going out one by one until only the chapel lamp remained, a single yellow eye in the dark.

The soldier's memory sat in him like a swallowed coal.

This was the part no client ever thought to ask about, the part Odile never put on the ledger: the goods stayed with the merchant. Memories didn't keep. They were not coins, to sit inert in a box; they were live things, and they wanted. The widow's flood-dream was already seeping into the floor of his sleep, he could feel it, the water already climbing the lane of his own dreams, and tomorrow he would decant both memories into glass — the vials were Odile's, fine work, wax-sealed, labeled in her cramped hand — and sell them on to whoever wanted to *feel* something, because that was the other market, the one nobody spoke of above a whisper. The valley was so full of people who felt nothing that a bottled grief sold for three times what an erasure did. There were men in Rooksbridge who had drunk a dozen strangers' sorrows trying to remember what it was to be afraid of losing something.

Kestrel had never drunk. He told himself it was discipline. Some nights, alone in the cold room, he suspected it was fear — that if he ever tasted one, really tasted it, he wouldn't be able to tell where it ended and he began, because there was so little of him to begin with.

He held the soldier's burning city up in his mind, examining it before storage, the way a jeweler examines a stone.

Towers falling in orange silence. Ash falling like gray snow. People running. Marta, whoever Marta was, her hand torn out of someone's grip. All of it ordinary horror, the sort of thing men did to each other in the world beyond the passes, the world the valley had voted, in its bloodless way, to stop having opinions about—

The face came back.

It rose out of the burning city the way a leviathan rises under a boat — the water didn't change, and yet suddenly there was something enormous beneath you, and the enormity had been there all along. The face filled the sky above the burning streets, and the firelight bent *around* it rather than onto it, and it was looking down at the running people with that expression he had seen in the widow's head, sorrow or hunger or recognition, and this time it did not slide away. This time it looked at him.

At *him*. Through the soldier's eyes, across three hundred silent years, out of a memory that had no business containing anything larger than a man's grief — it looked at Kestrel Ishikawa, alone in his cold room above the chandler's shop in a valley where magic had not entered since his grandmother was young, and its mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the distance, he understood it perfectly, the way you understand a blow.

One word.

*Kestrel.*

The vial slipped from his fingers. He caught it against his chest, heart slamming, and stood in the dark breathing like a man surfaced from deep water, and the burning city was only a burning city again, only goods, only stock, only a soldier's worst night folded small and ready for sale.

But frost was creeping up the inside of the window glass, feathering outward from the center in delicate white fronds, in a valley where it was not yet winter, in a room with no fire, in a place where magic could not follow.

Kestrel looked at the frost for a long time.

Then he did what he always did with things that frightened him: he sealed it in wax, labeled it, and put it in the drawer with the rest — telling himself, in the smooth worn voice he used for mercies, that he would deal with it in the morning.

Outside, far off toward the river, someone was praying.
