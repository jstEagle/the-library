## 2. Prayers in a Godless Place

The dream began with water.

Not the river Kestrel knew — the slow, brown, patient thing that wound through the bottom of the Valley of Hollow, good for trout and laundry and nothing else — but a river the color of green glass, running fast and bright, loud with the sound of its own going. He stood on its bank in the dream and felt, absurdly, homesick for a place he had never seen.

A child was kneeling in the reeds.

She was small, six years old perhaps, with her hair braided tight and her hands pressed together in the way children pray — fingers interlaced so hard the knuckles went white, as if the prayer might escape between them if she didn't hold it. She wasn't praying to the sky. She was praying *down*, into the water, the way you'd speak to someone standing just below the surface.

"Please," she said. "Please, Grandfather River. It's been winter too long. Mama says the fish have gone down deep where even you can't find them, and Papa says the seeds won't wake, and my brother coughs at night and Mama doesn't sing anymore. Please bring the spring. Please. I'll give anything. I'll give you my best stone, the blue one with the hole in it. I'll give you my name if you want it, I don't use it much anyway."

Kestrel stood behind her in the dream and understood, with the flat professional clarity he brought to all transactions, that this was not his memory. It was not a memory at all. It was a *prayer* — live, raw, addressed — and it was passing through him the way water passes through a culvert, and somewhere on the far side of him something was listening.

He tried to speak. In dreams you always try to speak, and in dreams your voice is always a stone in your mouth. The child didn't turn. The green river ran. And beneath the running water, deep down where the light went brown and then black, Kestrel saw — or almost saw, or dreamed he saw — something enormous shift its weight. An eye, maybe. Or the place where an eye had been for three hundred years, and the slow grinding attention of a thing waking up.

*Hollow*, he thought. *This is the Valley of Hollow. Nothing answers here. Nothing has answered for three hundred years. That is the whole of the law and the whole of the peace.*

The child's prayer went through him like an arrow through a leather target, and on the far side, something caught it.

He woke with his heart going like a trapped bird and his hands so cold he couldn't feel the blanket.

* * *

The frost had spread.

It was no longer one window. It had climbed the second pane, and the third, and it had found the water jug by the bed and frozen it solid from the inside out, the ice burst the clay in a small clean crack, and it had crept across the floorboards in a fan-shaped bloom that ended precisely, unnervingly, at the threshold of his bedroom door. Beyond the door: the ordinary dark of his house, the smell of last night's lamp oil, the tick of the stove iron cooling. Inside the door: winter.

Kestrel sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, in the gray before dawn, and looked at it the way he looked at everything — as inventory. One cracked jug. One bloom of frost, fan-shaped, deliberate. One dream that did not belong to him, filed under a category that did not exist until this morning.

In the Valley of Hollow, magic could not follow. That was not a saying; it was a fact of geology, of weather, of law. Witches who came over the pass found their charms gone dull as river stones by the second milestone. Gods — the few that still troubled themselves to look — saw only clouded glass. His grandmother had built the wards that did it, or so the story went, and the valley had been the safest place in the world for three hundred years precisely because it was the emptiest. No miracles. No curses. No answers. People came here to forget, and the valley's great mercy was that nothing here would ever speak back.

And yet a child had prayed through him in the night, and the frost had answered in the shape of a fan, and the water jug had died of it.

Kestrel got dressed, boiled water he no longer needed to boil, and did what he always did with things that frightened him. He took a stub of wax, softened it over the stove, pressed it onto a sliver of slate, and scratched into it with his awl: *Frost, indoor, unexplained. Dream — prayer — child — river. First instance.* Then he opened the long drawer under the window where he kept the things he could not sell and could not throw away, and he laid the slate inside among its brothers — *the soldier's god-faced memory, sealed and labeled; the coin that was warm on the wrong side; the letter in a hand identical to his own* — and he shut the drawer on all of it.

Telling himself, in the smooth worn voice he used for mercies, that he would deal with it in the morning.

The morning came, and with it, knocking at his door, Odile.

* * *

She arrived the way she always did, which was to say as if she had been standing outside it for some time and had merely decided the moment had ripened. Odile was his fence — the woman who moved what he lifted out of the valley and moved coin and goods back in, who never asked where a memory came from and never, in eleven years of partnership, had asked him a personal question. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a barrel stave, with iron-gray hair cut short as a man's and hands that looked like they had been carved for gripping. She carried a basket of winter pears she did not offer to share.

"You look terrible," she said, stepping past him into the house without invitation, because the threshold of Kestrel's house had never once slowed her.

"I slept badly."

"Mm." She set the basket on his table, looked at the cracked jug, and looked at the fan-shaped stain on the floorboards where the frost had melted and left the wood pale and silvered, as though the grain itself had been bleached. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she took off her coat, sat down, and said, "Tell me the dream."

Kestrel hesitated. He was a man who made his living knowing what things were worth and to whom, and eleven years of Odile had taught him that she always knew more than she said — but also that she had never once used what she knew against him, and never once asked for more than her cut. That was worth more than most marriages.

So he told her. The green river. The child in the reeds. The prayer, and the blue stone with the hole in it, and *I'll give you my name, I don't use it much anyway*. The shifting weight beneath the water. The waking cold.

Odile listened without moving. When he finished she peeled a pear with a small knife, in one long unbroken spiral, and did not eat it.

"You know what a prayer is," she said at last. It wasn't a question, but he waited, because with Odile the waiting was the conversation. "Most people think it's asking. It isn't. A prayer is a *door*. You open it, and you say a name through it, and whatever is on the other side gets to decide what comes back through." She laid the pear spiral on the table between them, pale and perfect. "Your valley has no doors. That was the whole point of it. Your grandmother —"

"I know what my grandmother did."

"Do you?" Odile looked at him then, and her eyes were the flat gray of the sky before snow. "You know she built the wards. Everyone knows that. The saint of Hollow, the woman who shut the door on the gods and gave the world one safe room. Do you know *why*? Do you know what a ward that size is *made* of?"

Kestrel said nothing.

"Neither do I," Odile said. "That's the honest answer, and I'll thank you to remember I gave it to you. But I know what I know about doors." She tapped the table with one carved finger. "A thing answered that child. In a place with no doors, a thing answered. And here is the part you need to hear, Kestrel, so I'll say it plain and you can be angry at me later. Things that answer prayers do not do it for free. That's not cruelty. That's *nature*. The rain doesn't fall because you're thirsty. But a god — a real one, an old one — a god that hears a child beg for spring and reaches through a sealed valley to give it, that god is spending something. And it will come to collect. From the child, or from the valley, or—" she looked, without hurry, at the silvered floorboards, "—from whatever it used as a door."

"I'm not a door," Kestrel said. "I'm a thief. There's a difference. Doors let things in on purpose."

"Thieves leave locks broken behind them," Odile said. "You've been picking at the world's pockets for eleven years. Did you think the world wasn't picking back?"

He wanted to be angry at that, and found, to his discomfort, that he mostly wanted to sit down. He sat. The stove ticked. Outside, the ordinary sounds of the valley on an ordinary morning — a cart, a dog, someone hammering at the smithy — went on being ordinary with a stubbornness that felt, today, like a performance.

"The frost," he said. "It stopped at the door. Exactly at the door. Like it was —"

"Contained," Odile said. "Or *kept*. There's a difference between those words too, and I don't like the second one." She finally ate a piece of the pear, chewing slowly. "I'm going to ask you something now, and I want the truth, because I sell truth in both directions and I know what it costs. Last night, when the prayer went through you. Did it feel like passing through you? Or did it feel like it was *looking for something in you*?"

Kestrel opened his mouth to say *passing through*, because that was the true answer, and then he was silent, because it wasn't. It had gone through him the way water goes through a culvert — but a culvert is a pipe, and what he had felt in the dream had been a *shape*. The prayer had bent around something in him. It had poured through the valley of his sleeping mind and found, at the bottom of it, a basin that fit it exactly, a hollow worn smooth as if a thousand prayers had already passed that way and left their mark.

"It fit," he said quietly. "Like a key in a lock that was already cut."

Odile closed her eyes for a moment, the way people do when a long-feared bill arrives and they find they can pay it, and hate that they can.

"Then listen to me," she said, leaning forward, and for the first time in eleven years her voice had something in it that was not commerce. "You will not pray. Not joking, not drunk, not desperate, not even in anger, and especially not in church, because the valley's dead shrines are dead the way a well is dry — the shaft is still there. You will not go near the river at night. You will sell me what you lift, you'll take your cut, and you will not go looking into that drawer. Some merchandise is cursed not because of what it is but because of who's looking for it. Do you understand me?"

"Odile." He almost laughed. "You're talking like the gods are *shopping*."

"I'm talking," Odile said, rising, taking up her coat and her basket and her knife, "like a woman who once watched a god collect a debt. It was before your time. It was before the valley, even, if you can believe I was ever anywhere else." At the door she stopped, and did not turn around, and said the thing she had come to say in the flat voice of a merchant quoting a hard price:

"Prayers aren't letters, Kestrel. They're debts with the debtor's name left blank. And the thing under that river has just been handed a slip of paper with *your* shape on it."

She let herself out. The latch fell. The house was very quiet, and the silvered fan on the floorboards caught the morning light and threw it, faintly, upward — so that for a moment the whole room seemed to be lit from below, from under the floor, from under the earth, from somewhere deep and patient and green.

Kestrel looked at the drawer under the window for a long time.

He did not open it. But he stood over it with his hand flat on the wood, feeling — or imagining, he told himself, *imagining* — a faint, rhythmic warmth beneath his palm, like something on the far side breathing. Like something on the far side *knocking*.

Outside, far off toward the river, faint and high and unmistakable, a child was laughing.
