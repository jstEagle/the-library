## 3. The Woman Who Remembered Him Wrong

The stranger came into Hollow on a Tuesday, which Kestrel would remember later because Tuesdays were the days he did his honest work, and there was nothing honest about what happened to her.

He saw her first from the window of Odile's back room, where he went on market mornings to count his take and drink tea that tasted of nothing in particular. The Valley of Hollow had exactly one street worth naming — Wren Street, cobbled and crooked, running from the millpond to the border stones — and the woman was walking up it the wrong way, against the flow of carts and gossip, as if she were swimming upstream through the ordinary day.

She was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, dressed for travel in a coat that had been good once and then been mended so many times it had become something else entirely — a coat made of decisions, each patch a place where she'd chosen to keep going rather than stop. Her boots were wrong for the road. Too fine. The kind of boots a person wore in a city where roads were swept by someone else.

Kestrel set down his cup.

There were two kinds of people who came to Hollow from outside: those who stumbled in by accident, following bad maps and worse advice, and those who came looking for something. The first kind he ignored. The second kind paid well.

But this woman wasn't looking at the stalls or the shrine-less chapel or the millpond with its flat, obedient water. She was looking at faces. One after another, she'd catch a passerby's eye, hold it a moment too long, and move on — a fisherman, a baker's boy, old Marta with her basket of onions. Searching. And every face she passed gave her back nothing, because that was what Hollow did best. It gave back nothing.

She stopped in front of the chandler's shop, directly below Odile's window, and stood very still. Then, without any warning at all, she turned her head and looked directly up at Kestrel.

Not at the window. At him.

Her expression didn't change — no shock, no recognition, none of the things a face does when it finds what it's been hunting. It simply *settled*, the way a compass needle settles, and Kestrel felt the settling like a hand laid flat against his sternum.

"Downstairs," Odile said behind him. She'd come up without a sound, as she always did, a narrow gray woman who moved through her own house like smoke through a keyhole. "You've got your look on."

"I don't have a look."

"You have several looks. That one means trouble wearing a coat." She peered past him at the street. "Traveler?"

"Lost traveler. She's asking faces questions."

"Hollow doesn't answer them." Odile said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the woman a moment longer than lightness required. "Go do your work, Kestrel. Market day won't thieve itself."

* * *

His stall was three planks and a curtain in the shadow of the wool merchant's awning, and it sold nothing anyone could see. Kestrel sat behind it with a tray of trinkets — brass buttons, a cracked spyglass, ribbons gone sun-faded — and waited for the desperate ones to find him. They always found him. Grief has a nose for its own kind.

Business had been strange since the frost on his windows. Strange in a way he couldn't name. Twice this week he'd reached for a memory he'd taken — the soldier's burning city, still rolled tight in its vial of glass and lead — and felt it *shift*, like something alive turning over in its sleep. He'd told himself it was imagination. He was getting very good at telling himself things.

The woman found him before noon.

She came around the awning without browsing, without pretense, and stood before his little table of junk with her hands folded like a woman attending a funeral. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and long roads, and there was a fine white scar across her left eyebrow, old and clean.

"You're the one who buys memories," she said. Not a question. "Or sells them. I'm told you do both, depending on which way the grief runs."

"I sell souvenirs," Kestrel said. "Buttons. Ribbons."

"Mm." She looked at the tray. At him. "My name is Liria Voss. I've come a very long way, and I'll be plain with you, because plainness is cheaper than the alternative. I knew you when you were small. Before the valley. Before—" She stopped herself, and something crossed her face, quick as a bird's shadow. "You had another name."

The market noise seemed to step back from them, leaving a pocket of quiet around the stall.

"People say that," Kestrel said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later. "People say a lot of things when they want a discount."

"Your name was Emrys."

The word landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He listened for the splash and heard — nothing. No echo. No flicker of recognition, no ache of something almost remembered. Just the word falling and falling into a darkness that received it without comment.

"That's not my name," he said.

"No," Liria agreed softly. "It isn't anymore. Names can be taken, Em— " She caught it again, that same bird-shadow flitting across her face, and this time Kestrel saw what it cost her. A wince. As if the name itself had edges. "As I said. Plainly. I served your family. I held you when you were small enough to fit in a bread pan. You had a sister who bit. You had a mother who sang off-key on purpose to make you laugh. And you had a grandmother who—"

"Who died," Kestrel said. "Two years ago. I buried her myself, out past the millpond, under the rowan."

Something changed in Liria's face then. Not doubt. Worse than doubt. Pity.

"Yes," she said. "I imagine you did."

"What is that supposed to mean?"

"It means," she said, "that I need to talk to you somewhere the walls aren't listening. And I mean that literally, thief. Your valley listens. Have you never wondered why nothing here grows crooked? Why the river runs straight? Why no one dreams?" She leaned closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I've come to bring you home, Emrys. Whatever she told you she was — whatever you buried under that rowan tree — your grandmother is not done with you yet."

Kestrel's hands, resting flat on the tray of worthless treasures, had gone cold.

"I think you should leave," he said.

"I think I should," she said, and smiled, tired and true. "But I won't. I've walked eleven years to say what I have to say, and I'll say it at the border stones if I have to, because I am not permitted to forget it, and you are not permitted not to hear it."

* * *

They walked out together in the late afternoon, and the whole way Kestrel kept telling himself he was only escorting her — a courtesy, a shepherd's kindness, making sure the madwoman got safely to the edge of town. The border stones stood in their usual ring past the last field: nine gray monoliths furred with lichen, older than the town, older than any record, marking the line where the world outside ended and the Valley of Hollow began. Travelers crossed them daily without noticing anything except a vague lightening of mood, a sense of having left a weight behind. That was the wards working. That was the silence doing its job.

Liria stopped just inside the ring and faced him, and the low sun came through her gray hair and made it briefly gold.

"Eleven years ago," she said, "I was sworn to your mother's household. When the valley closed, I was outside it. Do you understand what that means? Everyone inside was sealed in. Everyone outside was sealed *out*. But I carried a token, and tokens pass where flesh cannot, slowly, over years, wearing grooves in the ward like water wearing stone. Eleven years, Emrys. I have spent eleven years walking toward you, and the ward has spent eleven years letting me, a mile at a time, because the token remembers what the valley wants forgotten."

"Stop calling me that."

"Emrys Ishikawa," she said, relentless, gentle, terrible. "Son of Maerin Ishikawa, who sang off-key. Brother of Sef, who bit. Sworn in the temple of the river god before you could walk, an oath-child, marked and promised, and hidden here when the sky broke — hidden by the only person strong enough to hide you, who paid for it with everything, and who has been dead these two years only in the way that suits the story."

"The story is my life," Kestrel said, and hated how loud his voice came out, how young. "I grew up here. I learned letters here. I stole my first memory here, from a peddler who didn't even feel it go. There is no temple. There is no sister. There is no—"

"Token," Liria said.

And she drew it out.

It hung from a cord around her neck, and she lifted it free and held it up between them on her open palm, and the sight of it went through Kestrel like cold iron through warm flesh.

A disc of dark metal, older than any smithing he knew, worked with a crest he had seen ten thousand times and never once questioned: the crest on his grandmother's ring. The crest carved into the lintel of her house. The crest stitched, in faded thread, into the hem of the only blanket he'd ever kept from childhood. Two rivers meeting to form a third. Around the rim, in a script that hurt slightly to look at, ran words he couldn't read and yet somehow knew the shape of in his mouth, the way you know the shape of a word you spoke before you could speak.

An oath-token. He didn't know how he knew the term. He knew it the way he knew the crest — without learning it.

"Where did you get that," he said. It wasn't a question either.

"It was given to me," Liria said, "to give to you. Along with a message, which I was sworn to deliver word for word, and which I will now deliver, and then, gods willing, I can finally rest." She took a breath, and Kestrel saw — absurdly, horribly — that her eyes were wet. "The message is: *The vial with your name on it was never empty. Come and see.*"

The world tilted very slightly, the way a floor tilts when a ship that shouldn't be moving moves.

Because he had seen that vial. Three nights ago, in the dream that wasn't a dream — the locked room beneath his grandmother's house that he had never found while she lived, the shelves of glass and lead, the labels in her handwriting. And one slot, near the end, empty, with a small card bearing a single word in her careful script:

*Kestrel.*

"How do you know about the vials," he whispered.

"I know about everything," Liria said. "That was my office. Rememberer to the House of Ishikawa. I kept what others lost." Her hand closed slowly around the token, and her knuckles whitened. "And now I'm going to give it to you, and I'm going to tell you the last thing, and then—"

She stopped.

Mid-word. Mid-breath. Her mouth stayed open on the shape of the next syllable, and her eyes — her eyes went somewhere else entirely, someplace behind her own face, and Kestrel watched, frozen, as the woman who had walked eleven years to reach him began, silently, to come apart.

It started at the edges. The scar above her eyebrow faded — not healed, *faded*, like ink lifted out of cloth. Then the lines around her eyes smoothed. Then her hands, still curled around the token, grew smooth-knuckled, younger, younger, the calluses of eleven years' road dissolving off her palms, and Kestrel understood with a horror that arrived whole and complete, needing no explanation, that the valley was taking back what she'd carried in. Every memory of him. Every memory of the road. Every year the token had bought her passage with.

The wards weren't keeping her out.

They were *unmaking her arrival*.

"Liria." He grabbed her shoulders. Her eyes refocused on him, and for one terrible instant they were the eyes of a stranger — polite, puzzled, the eyes of a woman seeing a young man at a market stall selling buttons and ribbons. "No. No, stay with me. You walked eleven years. Stay."

"Have we met?" she said pleasantly.

And then she fell.

He caught most of her weight, lowered her onto the grass between the border stones, and knelt there holding her hand while the last of her went. It didn't hurt her — that was the worst part. She drifted, breathing slow, her face settling into peace, growing younger and simpler and more vacant by the minute, until what lay in the grass was a woman of perhaps thirty with no scars and no history, blinking up at the evening sky and saying, in a mild, wondering voice:

"What a lovely valley. Is this Hollow? I've always wanted to see Hollow."

Kestrel could not speak.

The token had fallen from her hand. It lay in the grass between them, dark metal drinking the last of the light, and he picked it up with fingers that shook, and the moment it touched his skin the words around the rim warmed faintly, like a thing recognizing kin.

Around him the nine stones stood in their ring, patient as they had been for three hundred years, and for the first time in his life Kestrel Ishikawa looked at them and saw not the edge of home but the bars of a cage — and saw, too, that the cage had teeth, and that the teeth had just eaten a woman's entire self rather than let one true sentence cross the line.

"Help," said the woman in the grass, sitting up, smiling at him with a stranger's open kindness. "I'm sorry — do I know you? You look like someone I ought to know."

"You don't," Kestrel said hoarsely. "Nobody does. Not anymore."

He got her to her feet and walked her back toward the lights of town, to find Odile, to find answers, to find anything solid to stand on. The token rode in his fist, warm as a living thing.

And all the way down Wren Street, in the deep of his coat pocket, it kept whispering — not in words, not quite, but in the shape of words, the shape of a promise made to a child before the child could walk:

*Come and see. Come and see. The vial with your name on it was never empty.*
