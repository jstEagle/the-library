## 4. Grandmother's Locked Room

The house did not want him there.

Kestrel had known it since childhood, in the way children know things they cannot name — that the rooms of his grandmother's house arranged themselves around him like a held breath, that the hallway to her study was always a little colder than the rest of the house, that the floorboards above the cellar creaked even when nothing walked on them. He had thought it was grief, after she died. Houses grieved too; everyone in Hollow said so. But it had been two years now, and the house had not softened. If anything it had grown more watchful, the way an empty chair grows more present the longer no one sits in it.

He stood at the gate with the stranger's effects in his satchel and let the cold iron latch bite his palm before he lifted it.

*Say it,* he told himself. *Out loud.*

"I'm coming in, Gran."

The words felt foolish on the empty street. The house said nothing back, but the pressure behind his ribs eased by a fraction, the way it always did when he spoke to her as if she could hear — which was absurd, because this was the Valley of Hollow, where the dead stayed dead and the gods stayed silent and nothing listened, nothing ever listened.

Except something had listened. Something had answered a child's prayer through him three nights ago, and left frost on his window like a signature, and Odile had looked at him across her counter with an expression he'd never seen her wear — fear, worn carefully, like borrowed jewelry.

And then a woman had died at the border calling him by a name he'd never heard, carrying a token stamped with his family crest.

He climbed the steps. The door opened before he touched it — no, it didn't, he corrected himself firmly, he had pushed it, the hinges were old, old houses settled — and the smell of the front hall rose up to meet him: dried lavender, lamp oil, and beneath both, faint as a watermark, the mineral scent of the river stones she used to keep in every windowsill.

"You kept those," he murmured, seeing them still lined up in the study window, grey and patient, catching the afternoon light. "You said they were just decoration."

His grandmother had lied about small things all his life. Where she came from. Why she never prayed, not even the polite grace-before-meals that even the least devout in Hollow mumbled out of habit. Why she flinched, only slightly, when travelers described the world beyond the passes — not with longing or curiosity, but with the tight, controlled expression of someone listening for a specific word in a foreign language, afraid of hearing it.

Kestrel hung his coat on the hook that had been his since he was six years old and stood in the hall, turning the oath-token over in his fingers. Silver, worn nearly smooth, the crest sharp enough still to read: a kestrel in flight above a broken circle. His family had been smiths and tinkers for four generations. No one had ever explained the crest. No one had ever needed to. It was simply theirs, the way the crooked chimney was theirs, the way the third stair that sang underfoot was theirs.

Except the woman at the border had carried one just like it, and she had called him *Emrys*, and the sound of it had done something to him he still couldn't describe — not recognition exactly. More like the moment when you press your tongue against a missing tooth and find the socket deeper than you expected.

He went to the study first because it was the obvious place, and obvious places were where thieves started, and he was, whatever else he was, a professional.

* * *

The study resisted him politely.

That was the only way to put it. The desk drawers opened smoothly enough, but everything inside them was arranged to be found: household accounts, seed catalogues, letters from the tinker's guild, a half-finished sock whose mate he'd never located. He searched for an hour, growing more frustrated, feeling increasingly like a burglar in his own inheritance. Under the loose floorboard by the hearth — the classic spot, the spot any child would check — he found a tin of lemon drops gone sugary and hard, and a note in his grandmother's hand: *For the boy, when he finally goes digging. Eat something. You get thin when you think.*

He laughed despite himself, and the laugh cracked into something else halfway through, and he sat on the hearthstone eating stale lemon drops with wet eyes until the light through the windows went amber and long.

"She knew me," he said to the empty room. "She knew I'd come looking."

Which meant the real hiding place wasn't hidden at all. It was hidden from everyone *except* him. She had built the house's defenses around a single assumption: that eventually, whatever happened, Kestrel would stand in this room and start pulling up floorboards, and the house would need to decide whether to let him.

He closed his eyes and did the thing he was best at in the world. He stopped being Kestrel Ishikawa, memory-thief, and became instead the thing he touched — became the house itself, its grain and dust and old mortar, the way he became a soldier's burning city or a widow's wedding day when he lifted a memory. Not taking. Listening. Feeling for the seams.

The house flinched.

It was like holding a bird. A wild thing with a heartbeat going far too fast, pressed against his palms, wanting desperately to be somewhere else. And threaded all through its terror, so familiar it made his throat close, was a texture he knew from a thousand thefts: memory. The house was soaked in it. Not one memory — hundreds, layered like sediment, packed into the walls and floors and the cold space beneath the cellar stairs with a density no natural object should carry.

"Oh," Kestrel said softly. "Oh, Gran. What did you *do*?"

The house shuddered — an actual tremor, dust sifting from the ceiling beams — and somewhere below, behind the wall at the end of the cellar passage, something clicked.

* * *

The cellar door had always been locked. As a child he'd assumed it held preserves and turnips and the ordinary damp wealth of a careful household. His grandmother had kept the key on a ring at her belt, and once, when he was nine, he'd asked what was down there, and she had looked at him for a long moment with an expression he'd spent twenty years misreading as simple sternness.

"Nothing you need," she'd said. "Not yet."

Not yet. Not *never*. He understood now, standing before the door with the key — which he found exactly where a thief would look, in the flour jar, wrapped in oilcloth — that she had been waiting for this day the way the valley waited for spring that never quite came. Everything in this house was a message addressed to him, delayed delivery, postage paid in years.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Lamp oil, match, flame. The cellar breathed out at him: cold stone, river water, and underneath it a smell that raised every hair on his arms — the smell of the border at night, of the place where the valley ended and the world began. Ozone and old snow and something sweeter, something like the air before a storm that never breaks.

The stairs went down further than the footprint of the house allowed. He noticed that distantly, cataloguing it the way he catalogued anomalies in a stolen memory — *impossible geometry, flag for later* — because directly ahead of him, filling the space where turnip shelves should have been, was the wall of vials.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Racked floor to ceiling in neat cedar frames, each vial no longer than his finger, each one glowing with a faint inner light that shifted as he moved, colors sliding like oil on water. Gold and green and a deep bruised violet, a white so bright it hurt, a black that wasn't darkness but depth. Each rack bore a strip of paper labeled in his grandmother's small, precise hand.

He read the nearest label and forgot how to breathe.

*River-of-Laughter, minor god of the millstream at Vell's Crossing. Taken winter of the 298th year of silence. Weeping. Asked twice for return.*

Below it: *The Hearthmother of Aldenbrook. Taken spring, same year. Did not resist. Said: tell my people the fire will keep.*

Below that: *A god of doors, name unlearned. Taken autumn. Fought. Cost me two fingers' worth of years.*

Kestrel reached out and touched the frame, and the memories in the vials *surged toward him*, a thousand voices pressing against glass, and he snatched his hand back as if burned. They weren't inert. They weren't stored. They were *held* — captive, aware, praying in their small glass prisons to the only person who came down here.

To her, for three hundred years' worth of someone's lifetime. And now to him.

"No," he said aloud, and his voice sounded very young in the stone room. "No, no, no."

Because he understood it now, all of it, the whole architecture of his life laid suddenly bare. The valley's peace. The silence of the gods. The reason magic couldn't cross the border, the reason prayers fell flat as dropped stones, the reason Hollow was the safest place in the world to be a man who trafficked in other people's pasts. It wasn't a sanctuary.

It was a vault. And his grandmother had been the vault-keeper, and the treasure was *gods* — their memories, their names, the living substance of them, sealed away in glass by the thousands while the world outside starved for want of them. Every dying shrine beyond the passes, every starving minor god begging at the roadside — hungry because their own selves were locked in a cellar under a tinker's cottage in a valley that existed for no other purpose than to hold them.

The valley's peace was a cage. He had grown up inside the lock.

He walked the racks slowly, reading labels, and the labels got worse the deeper he went. Gods he'd never heard of. Gods with names in languages that made his teeth ache. Some entries were clinical. Some were desperate — *I am sorry, I am sorry, there was no other way* — written and rewritten, crossed out, rewritten again. One vial near the back was labeled simply: *The last prayer I ever answered. Do not open this one, Kestrel. Not first.*

*Not first.* As if there were an order. As if she'd planned his education down here like a schoolmistress planning lessons.

And then he saw the empty slot.

It was at chest height, center rack, the position of honor — the place your eye went first, the place a curator puts the masterpiece. The cedar around it was worn smooth, handled often, though the slot itself had stood vacant for years; dust lay in it undisturbed. And beneath it, on the little brass plate where every other slot bore a god's name, was engraved a single word in his grandmother's hand:

*Kestrel.*

Not Emrys. Kestrel. The name he'd borne all his life, the name she'd given him — except she hadn't given it, had she, because names weren't given, they were *kept*, and this slot had been cut to fit a vial that should have held his memory, his whole self, the original of him, and it was empty. Had always been empty. Or —

Or it had been full once, and she had poured it back into him, and the name on the plate was a promise rather than a record: *this belongs here, and someday it must come home.*

His hand was shaking so badly he had to brace it against the rack. The vials nearest his touch glowed brighter, straining toward him, and he realized with a lurch that they recognized him — that to the captive gods in their glass, he smelled like the vault-keeper's blood, like the lock itself, like the one creature in the world whose hands could open every cage in this room.

"Tell me what to do," he whispered, and hated how it sounded — a child asking the dark for instructions.

The house answered instead. Above him, through the floor, through the foundations, he felt it — a groan in the timbers, a shifting of weight, and then a sound he'd heard only once before, the night after her funeral: the deep, grinding reluctance of the house itself moving, walls settling into new positions, the geometry of the cellar rearranging around him with the slow patience of a thing that had been holding its breath for decades and could hold it no longer.

Sealing him in? Guarding him? He genuinely could not tell, and that frightened him more than either option. The house didn't know either. The house was only doing what it had been made to do, and its maker was two years dead, and the instructions had run out.

In the new quiet, one vial near the empty slot flickered — a soft gold pulse, steady as a heartbeat. Its label read: *For the night he asks why. Begin here.*

Kestrel stood in his grandmother's cellar, in the heart of the lie his whole life had been built on, surrounded by the imprisoned light of a thousand gods, and reached for the vial with his name in the world still caught between two shapes — the thief's son, the vault-keeper's heir, the boy called Emrys by a dying woman at the border.

The glass was warm as a living thing. Inside, something turned over in its sleep and began, unmistakably, to wake.

And far above, at the edge of the valley where the wards thinned to gossamer, something that had been waiting a very long time felt the vault stir, and smiled.
