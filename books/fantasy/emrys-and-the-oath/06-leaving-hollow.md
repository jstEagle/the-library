## 6. Leaving Hollow

Kestrel had spent his whole life learning the exact weight of other people's years — the heft of a soldier's first kill, the lightness of a bride's wedding morning, the dense black gravity of a mother's grief for a drowned son. He knew how memories sat in the palm, how they warmed or chilled, how they could be poured from one vessel to another without spilling a drop of blood.

He had never once considered what it would cost to carry his own across a border.

The morning they left, the Valley of Hollow wore its ordinary face: gray mist pooling in the low fields, woodsmoke standing straight up from chimneys because there was no wind worth mentioning, the market square already filling with carts and argument. Ordinary. That was the cruelty of it. The valley looked exactly as it had every day of his life, and it would go on looking that way after he was gone, indifferent as a closed door.

"You're stalling," Odile said.

She stood at the edge of his yard with a travel pack slung over one shoulder and her silver hair braided back tight, practical as a fence post. She had agreed to guide him out with the enthusiasm of a woman agreeing to attend her own funeral, and she had made him swear three times that he understood what waited beyond the wards. He had sworn. He had lied about understanding.

"I'm not stalling," he said. "I'm saying goodbye to my house."

"Your house tried to bite you last night."

"It did not *bite* me. It locked a door I'd already opened and then sulked." He touched the doorframe as he passed under it, and felt the faint resistance again, like skin flinching. Since he'd found his grandmother's hidden chamber, the house had developed opinions about him. He chose not to think about that. There was a long list of things he had chosen not to think about lately, and it was growing faster than he could manage it.

They walked down through the terraced fields where the barley was already cut and stacked, past the shrine-less well where children had once left offerings before anyone remembered why, past the tannery and the mill and the crooked signpost at the crossroads that pointed to places no one in living memory had gone. Hollow's Edge, the mapmakers called the valley's rim. The locals just called it the Rim, and spoke of it the way sailors speak of the horizon — something you can see but never reach, because reaching it means leaving everything you are.

Because that was the truth of the valley, the truth Kestrel had learned in fragments over the past weeks and now carried whole and cold in his chest: magic could not enter Hollow. Neither could the old gods. And neither, apparently, could certain truths about himself. His grandmother had built the wards out of stolen god-memories, and whatever she had sealed inside them included him — the vial with his name on it, the empty slot, the life he could not remember living.

Outside those wards, the world was waiting to give it all back.

"You can still refuse," Odile said as they climbed the last slope. Her voice was flat, but her hands were not; she kept adjusting the strap of her pack, touching it, letting it go. "Veyran's herald won't wait forever, but he'll wait longer than winter will. You could stay. Board up your grandmother's room. Sell memories until the wards fail on their own, which they will, and sooner than either of us would like."

"And be here when they do."

"Yes," she said simply. "You'd be here when they do. It would be quick, probably."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," said Odile. "It's supposed to make you stop asking stupid questions."

The mist thickened as they climbed, and the sounds of the valley fell away behind them — the mill's creak, a dog barking at nothing, the far bell of the morning market. Kestrel found himself cataloguing them without meaning to, the way a man drowning catalogs the surface of the water. He had robbed this valley for fifteen years. He had sold its travelers their own pasts and its widows their forgetting, and he had told himself it was honest work, clean work, work that hurt no one who wasn't already hurting. Now, walking away from it, he understood that he had never once looked directly at what he was. The valley had been kind enough not to show him.

The border, when they reached it, was invisible.

That was the thing no one told you. Three hundred years of stories, and every child in Hollow imagined the Rim as a wall of light, a shimmering curtain, some visible seam between the blessed silence and the dangerous world. But it was only a hillside. Scrub grass, loose shale, a wind-bent hawthorn growing sideways off the slope. Kestrel had been within twenty paces of this spot a hundred times in his boyhood, daring himself closer each summer, always turning back at some invisible line his body seemed to know even when his mind didn't.

His body knew it now. Ten paces out, his skin began to prickle. Five paces, and the prickling became a hum, deep and subcutaneous, like standing too near a struck bell.

Odile stopped. "Last chance."

"You've said that four times."

"And you've ignored me four times. Consistency is a virtue." She looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been pity, or its close cousin, guilt. "When we cross, don't fight it. Whatever comes into you — don't fight it. Fighting it is how people break."

"What comes into me?"

"Everything," Odile said. "Everything the valley kept out. Including the parts it kept out of *you*."

She stepped through first.

Kestrel watched her cross — one stride, two — and saw nothing happen to her at all. No flash, no shudder. She simply continued walking uphill, and the world continued containing her, and that was all.

Then it was his turn, and it was not all. It was not remotely all.

* * *

Later, he would have no adequate words for it. He was a thief of memories; language was his trade; and still the best he ever managed, telling Sef about it in the weeks after, was: *it was like being born, if being born hurt and remembered.*

The first thing was cold. Not the cold of air — the morning was mild — but the cold of water closing over the head, an entire ocean admitted at once. Magic came back into him the way blood returns to a sleeping limb, agonizing and abundant, and with it came everything the wards had strained against for three centuries. He felt the valley behind him like a held breath finally released. He felt the hillside under his boots become not merely ground but *ground*, alive and attentive, every root and stone dimly aware of his weight. He felt the sky open above him like an eye.

And then the memories came.

They were not gentle. They did not arrive one at a time, politely labeled, the way his stolen goods always had. They arrived as weather — a squall, a flood, a whiteout — and they were not his, and they were his, both at once, and he went down onto his knees in the scrub grass with his hands pressed to his skull.

A temple of white stone, incense rising in braided columns. A woman's voice singing a name — *his* name, another name, a name that fit him better than Kestrel ever had — and small hands gripping his, a girl's hands, laughing, pulling him up temple steps two at a time. A promise spoken over a basin of river water. A face he knew, gods help him, he *knew*, though he had never seen it: the stranger from the border, younger, unbroken, calling him by a childhood name across a crowded courtyard—

"Kestrel." Odile's hand on his shoulder, iron-firm. "Breathe. Don't fight it. Let it move through."

"They're not mine," he gasped.

"Some of them are." She crouched beside him, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Some of them are exactly yours. That's rather the problem."

He knelt in the wet grass and let it move through him, wave after wave, and it did not stop so much as settle, the way sediment settles in shaken water, layering itself into him. When he could stand again, the world had changed.

Not the world — his *seeing* of it. The hillside was the same hillside. But now he could feel the hawthorn tree dreaming its slow wooden dreams. Now he could taste the coming season on the wind, and the taste was wrong, and that was the second thing he understood, standing there dripping with returned divinity:

The world beyond the valley was dying of winter, and it was the wrong time of year for it.

* * *

They walked all day, and the wrongness grew.

It was late summer — it should have been late summer; the valley's harvest had proven it that very morning — but the land beyond the Rim had not received the news. The oak woods they passed through stood half-stripped, leaves browned and falling in midsummer, crunching underfoot like broken crockery. Streams ran thin and glassy at their edges, laced with ice that clicked softly against the stones. In the fields, crops stood blackened in rows, killed by frosts that had come weeks early and stayed like unwelcome guests. Once, crossing a rise, they startled a herd of red deer moving downslope in silent single file, heads low, migrating as if it were November.

"The long winter," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question. He'd heard the phrase from Odile, from the herald, from the whispering vials in his grandmother's chamber. Hearing it and seeing it were different animals entirely.

"Three hundred years building," Odile said. "Now it's finishing. The covenant broke, the gods starved, and the seasons forgot their promises. Veyran calls it the world's natural correction." Her mouth twisted. "Veyran says a great many things."

Kestrel touched a frost-killed wheat stalk as they passed. Under his fingers — under his new, strange, flooded senses — he felt the last of its life gutter like a candle. And beneath it, deeper, he felt something else: a vast slow absence, a shape of emptiness moving through the land the way a hole moves through cloth. Where a god should have been, there was a god-shaped nothing, and the nothing was cold.

He snatched his hand back. "There was a field-god here. Wasn't there."

"There was a field-god everywhere, once. Every furrow, every hearth, every ford and threshold. Small ones. Hungry ones. They lived on the covenant — mortal prayer in exchange for divine keeping." Odile walked on, eyes fixed on the road. "You'll see some of what's left of them soon enough. Try not to feed them anything important."

"Feed them—"

"Don't ask questions I'll answer worse later."

They camped that night in the shell of a waystation inn, a two-story building whose roof had mostly surrendered, its common room open to the stars. Someone had lived here recently — a cold firepit, a bedroll gone gray with frost — and someone had left in a hurry, or been taken, or simply lain down. Kestrel built a fire with hands that wouldn't quite obey him. Every few minutes, another memory surfaced, unbidden, bright as a fish rising: a festival of lanterns on a river; an old man teaching him to read a covenant-script that predated any alphabet he knew; his own hands, younger, pressing an oath-token into a friend's palm while snow fell on a temple roof.

Snow. On a temple roof. In a city he had never visited, in a life he had never lived, and yet the memory sat in him with the terrible specific weight of the real — the smell of the incense, the ache in his knees from kneeling, the sound of his sister's laughter—

"Sef," he said aloud, without deciding to.

Odile looked up sharply from the fire.

"The girl in the memories. My—" He stopped. The word wouldn't come out right. *Sister.* It came out right and it didn't; it was true and it was impossible; he had grown up an only child in a house at the edge of a market town, and he had also grown up with a sister in a city of white temples, and both histories lay in him side by side like two roads diverging, and he did not know which one his feet had actually taken. "I had a sister."

"I know," Odile said.

"You *know*?" The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, ice shifted on the stream. "You've known this whole time? What else do you know, Odile? What else have you been carrying around behind that fence-post face while I—"

"Everything," she said quietly. "I know everything, Kestrel. I helped seal it. I told you that much before we crossed, and I'll tell you the rest when we're somewhere safer than an open roof under a dying sky." She fed a stick to the flames and did not look at him. "But yes. You had a sister. Her name is Sef. And whether she still lives depends very much on how fast we walk."

The words landed in him and kept landing, echo after echo. He gripped his own knees and breathed, the way she'd taught him at the border. *Don't fight it. Let it move through.* But this wasn't magic moving through him. This was grief, arriving ahead of schedule, for a sister he'd mourned without knowing he'd lost her.

Above the broken roof, the stars came out hard and clear, and among them — he saw it clearly now, with senses no one in Hollow had possessed for three hundred years — the constellations were wrong. Not missing, not rearranged. *Dimmed*, as if seen through smoke. As if the lights themselves were starving.

"We leave at first light," Odile said. "There's a village two days east, Hallowmere, if it still stands. There's a shrine there. A river-shrine." She paused, and in the firelight her face was older than it had been in the valley, older by centuries, maybe. "If the river god is still in it, he may know where your sister walks. If he isn't in it anymore — then we'll know something worse."

Kestrel pulled his blanket around his shoulders and lay back against his pack, and the last thing he saw before sleep took him was the frost creeping across the inside of the ruined doorway, feathering outward from the edges of the world like a disease.

In his dreams, a child prayed to a river for spring, and for the first time since the valley, Kestrel heard the prayer *land* — heard it strike something vast and weak and listening, like a coin dropped down a very deep, very dry well.

When he woke at dawn, there was frost on his bedroll shaped, unmistakably, like a handprint. Small. A child's.

It was pointing east.
