## 7. The Road of Small Gods

The first god Kestrel met outside the valley was the size of a teacup.

They had been walking for two days through a country that seemed to have forgotten what season it was meant to be — hedgerows rimed with frost in late harvest, orchards hanging heavy with apples gone black on the branch, and everywhere that low, wrong silence that follows a winter that has come too early and shown no intention of leaving. Odile walked ahead of him with her hood up and her staff striking the frozen ruts in a rhythm she never varied, as if the road were something to be argued with rather than followed. She had spoken perhaps forty words since they crossed the border, and most of them had been *drink water* or *walk faster* or *don't touch that*.

So when the shrine appeared at the crossroads — a squat stone thing no bigger than an oven, its niche crusted with lichen and old candle-fat — Kestrel stopped without meaning to.

Something in it was looking at him.

Not the carved face above the niche, which was worn nearly smooth, all its features weathered into suggestion. Not the offerings mounded at its base: a child's shoe gone gray with rot, a clay cup fused to the stone by ice, a wreath of straw that had been fresh once, recently, and was now stiff as wire. It was the space inside the shrine itself, the small dark hollow where a god should have lived, and it had turned toward him the way a sunflower turns toward light — slowly, hungrily, with the whole of its being.

"Odile," he said quietly.

She didn't stop walking. "Crossroads god. Probably a warden of ways, once. Leave an offering if you're superstitious and keep moving."

"It's looking at me."

That stopped her. She came back three steps, squinted at the shrine the way one squints at a dog of uncertain temper, and then her mouth thinned. "Huh."

"Huh isn't reassuring."

"It shouldn't be able to look at anything." She crouched, unhurried, and studied the little hollow the way she studied everything — like a merchant appraising goods she already intended to undervalue. "Gods need feeding, boy. Prayer, sacrifice, remembrance. The covenant fed them; that was half of what it *was*. A bargain: mortals pour belief into the vessel, gods pour blessing back out. Three hundred years of nothing poured in—" she tapped the shrine's lintel with one knuckle, "—and whatever's left in there is dregs. Sediment. It shouldn't have the strength to notice its own name."

The thing in the hollow shifted. Kestrel felt it the way he felt a pickpocket's fingers near his purse — a faint wrongness against his skin, attention with weight behind it. And then, faint as a voice heard through a wall, through water, through years:

*—please—*

He took a step back. "It spoke."

"It begged," Odile corrected. She rose, brushing frost from her knee. "Different thing entirely. Come on."

But Kestrel didn't come on. He stood in the frozen road with his breath steaming and looked into the little dark hollow, and what he felt there was not fear, not exactly. It was recognition. He had spent his life listening to people describe the things they wanted to forget — the soldier's burning city, the widow's drowned daughter, the merchant's ruined brother — and underneath every story ran the same current, the same raw nerve: *I cannot carry this alone.* He knew what hunger sounded like when it had been alone too long.

He dug in his coat pocket and found the heel of yesterday's bread. He set it in the niche.

The darkness around it seemed to exhale. For one heartbeat the air above the offering shimmered like heat-haze in reverse, like cold given shape, and Kestrel saw — or almost saw, or dreamed he saw for the rest of his life — something small and ragged fold itself around the bread the way a beggar folds around a coin. Then it was only a shrine again, and a crust going stale in a stone niche, and Odile watching him with an expression he couldn't read.

"You fed it," she said.

"It asked."

"That's how it starts." Her voice wasn't mocking. That was somehow worse. "You know what I did before you were born? No — don't answer, you don't, and that's the point. I've seen men feed stray gods before. It goes one of two ways. Either they starve anyway and you've wasted bread, or they don't starve, and then they follow you. And a god that follows you, Kestrel Ishikawa, is a debt that walks."

She turned up the road. After a moment he followed, and neither of them mentioned that the shrine's attention stayed on his back for nearly a mile, warm as a hand between his shoulder blades.

* * *

The village was called Brackenmere, though the sign at its edge had been defaced so long ago that only the *B* remained legible, and Odile said they'd find beds there because it was the last settlement before the fens and even a dying world needed somewhere to sleep. What they found instead was a wake.

Not for a person. For a well.

Kestrel understood this gradually, the way one understands any foreign country — by details first. The village green was crowded, which was strange for a working day. The crowd was silent, which was stranger still. And at the center of the green stood the well itself, an old round thing of fitted stone, its bucket drawn up and set aside with a kind of ceremonial care, and around its lip the villagers had laid rushes and winter branches and small offerings: buttons, a wooden horse with one leg snapped, a hairpin, a doll's shoe to match the one at the crossroads shrine, and Kestrel felt the pattern click into place with a cold he hadn't earned from the weather.

"They're mourning it," he said.

"The well-god, yes." Odile had pulled back her hood. In the flat gray light her face looked older than it ever did indoors, carved into lines of someone who had watched this exact thing happen many times and learned to keep her hands still while it happened. "River gods, well gods, hearth gods, field mothers — the small ones went first when the covenant broke. The great gods could sleep through famine. The small ones *were* the famine. Every prayer unanswered was a meal missed."

An old woman detached herself from the crowd and came toward them, and Kestrel braced for suspicion — strangers on the road in a starving season drew suspicion like wounds drew flies — but her face held none. It held hope, and hope was worse.

"You felt it," she said. It wasn't quite a question. Her eyes were fixed on Kestrel with an intensity that made his skin prickle. "When you came up the road. The others felt it too. Like a lamp carried past a dark window."

"I don't know what you mean."

"She means," said a voice from the crowd, "that we can feel you, stranger. Whatever you are."

They had begun to gather now, not threateningly, but the way the starving gather around a smell of cooking — drifting closer without deciding to, drawn by something below decision. And Kestrel realized, with a lurch, that he could feel *them* too: faint threads of attention, dozens of them, each one a tiny tug against his ribs, like hooks set in shallow water. It was the shrine-thing's gaze multiplied. It was the sensation from the border crossing, the flood of magic and memory, but gentler, distributed, and aimed.

"What am I?" he asked, and hated how young his voice came out.

"Full," said the old woman simply. "You walk like a man carrying a full jug through a land of cracked cups. No offense meant. We haven't seen fullness in three hundred years."

*Three hundred years.* The number again, always the number, stitching itself through everything like a seam. His grandmother's sealed valley. The empty vial with his name. The covenant broken. He looked at the well, at the rushes laid with such tenderness, and asked the question he was afraid of: "Is it dead?"

"Not yet." The old woman's composure cracked, just slightly, just enough to show the thing beneath it. "That's why we're here. It's been dying for a week. Slow. Our grandmothers' grandmothers said when a well-god dies the water goes bitter and the children sicken, and our water's been turning since the frost came early, and we thought — we hoped —" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "We heard there was a man on the road who carried prayers. That prayers reached the old gods through him. Is that true? Are you him?"

Kestrel opened his mouth to say *no*, because saying yes felt like standing up in a lightning storm, and what came out instead — quiet, traitorous, true — was:

"Let me try."

* * *

What he tried was simple, because simple was all he had. He knelt at the well's lip the way he'd knelt at a thousand market stalls, closed his eyes, and did the only thing he knew how to do with the insides of other people: he listened.

And the well listened back.

It was not like the shrine. The shrine had been a beggar's whisper through a wall. This was a hand closing around his wrist from underwater — weak, but a hand, with knuckles and intent. He felt the well-god the way he'd felt the soldier's burning city: as memory given texture. But this memory was vast and slow and *thirsty*, a consciousness that had spent centuries as sediment dreaming of being a river, and it pressed against him with a desperation that had passed through hunger into something more terrible, which was patience.

*Little vessel,* it said, in a voice like water finding cracks in stone. *Little jar. You are so full.*

"I'm not giving you my memories," Kestrel said aloud, and heard the villagers murmur at a man who talked to wells.

*No?* Amusement, thin as ice. *Not even the spare ones? I can taste them from here. Strangers' years. A soldier's smoke. A woman's grief. You carry stolen goods, thief, and I am starving, and starving things do not respect property.*

"I'm trying to help you."

*Are you.* The pressure eased, fractionally, became almost conversational. *Then listen, jar-bearer, and carry this much at least: we are waking. All of us, the dregs and sediments, the scraps the covenant left behind. Your passage north is a lamp through a dark country, and everything half-alive will turn toward it. Some will beg. Some will bless. And some —* the hand around his wrist tightened, not cruelly, but with the impersonal strength of deep water, *— some are so far gone that they will not remember that vessels are not for drinking.*

The pressure released. He gasped, rocked back on his heels, and found the villagers staring, and the old woman crying without any change in her expression, tears simply falling as if her face had given up holding the rest of it.

"Did it speak?" she whispered. "What did it say?"

Kestrel looked at the well. At the rushes. At the doll's shoe. He thought about lying and found he had no lie in him that would fit this place.

"It said it's dying," he said. "And it said I should hurry."

* * *

They made six miles before dusk caught them, and camped in the lee of a fallen boundary stone carved with figures whose faces had been chiseled off — deliberately, thoroughly, by hands that had hated them — and Kestrel sat wrapped in his cloak watching his breath freeze and thinking about fullness.

"You felt it too," he said. "In the village. The way they looked at me."

"I felt it before you did." Odile was cleaning her nails with her knife, unhurried, a woman who had made camp in worse places than this and would again. "At the border. Magic doesn't flood into a man like that and leave him ordinary. You're a lit window in a blackout, boy. Everything moth-winged in this world is going to find you eventually."

"The well-god said some things might try to eat my memories."

"Mm."

"*Mm?*"

She examined her thumbnail. "Small gods go mad the way starving dogs go mad. Not evil. Just past the point where manners survive. There'll be shrines ahead that beg prettier than the crossroads one. And there'll be shrines ahead that don't beg at all." She sheathed the knife and looked at him across the small fire, and firelight did strange generous things to a face like hers, softening it into something almost kind. "Your grandmother stole from gods, Kestrel. Did you think theft was a one-way street? Everything you carry wants to come home. The question you'll be answering for the rest of this road is which of them love you enough to ask, and which of them just take."

He didn't sleep well after that. He dreamed of the well — of a slow blind thing pressing its face against the inside of a jar, patient as water — and woke before dawn with frost on his blanket in the shape, he could almost swear, of a handprint.

* * *

Two days later, the shrine in the birch wood didn't beg.

They smelled it first — or Kestrel did: a sweetness under the cold, honeyed and rotten, like fruit past saving. Odile stopped so abruptly he walked into her staff, which she had leveled across his chest like a barrier rail.

"No," she said.

"No what? We haven't even—"

"There's a shrine thirty paces into those trees. Old one, pre-covenant, probably. And it's *baiting* you. Can't you smell it? That's god-scent, boy. Hunger dressed up as welcome." Her eyes were narrow, scanning the white trunks. "The crossroads thing begged. The well mourned. This one hunts."

"We could go around."

"We could. It's a day out of our way over fen ground, and Veyran's riders are on the roads behind us, so every mile costs. Or—" she hesitated, and Kestrel heard the hesitation and understood it cost her something, "—or you walk in there knowing what it is, because you're your grandmother's blood and apparently can't pass a hungry thing without feeding it."

He went in. He told himself it was to scout the road beyond the wood, which was even mostly true.

The shrine stood in a clearing where the birches grew in a perfect ring, as if planted by ceremony, and it was magnificent — that was the horror of it. Where the crossroads god had been a lichen-crusted oven, this was a temple in miniature, its stone still sharp-edged, its carvings crisp: processions of figures bearing sheaves and jars and infants, all approaching a central form whose face was serene and open-mouthed. Offerings ringed it in their hundreds, ancient and modern side by side — bronze coins green as pondwater beside a plastic-wrapped candy, a wedding ring beside a milk tooth — and every single offering remained untouched. Unconsumed. Arranged.

The sweetness thickened. And the god looked at him.

This time there was no mistaking it for anything else. The serenity of the carved face did not change, but the air above the shrine gathered itself into a shape, tall and robed and gracious, and it smiled with the carved mouth, and its voice arrived inside Kestrel's skull already mid-sentence, as if it had been speaking to him for years and he had only just tuned in:

*—and you came. How good. How very good. We smelled you on the wind days ago, little carrier, little laden boat, and we have waited so patiently, because patience is what distinguishes us from the animals. Do you know what we were? Before the silence? We were Harvest-Father in these hills. We were thanked in this tongue and that and another older than both. Three hundred years, and do you know what we ate?*

The shape drifted closer. The offerings did not move, but Kestrel had the sudden vertiginous certainty that they were not offerings at all. They were table settings.

*Candy wrappers. Milk teeth. The leavings of a faithless age. And now, walking through our wood, comes a boy stuffed with centuries — stolen years, borrowed griefs, a whole pantry of other people's lives — and he stops at our door. Tell me, vessel. Tell me truly. When you open yourself, does it hurt?*

Kestrel's hand had found the knife at his belt without his permission. He knew, distantly, that a knife was useless. He also knew — with the strange clarity of the moment before violence — that the thing was right about one thing: he was full, and it was starving, and the arithmetic of that was very old indeed.

"I'm not food," he said.

*Everything is food,* said the Harvest-Father gently, *to something. Even gods. Especially gods — ask the god-king, who has been eating us for three centuries and calls it stewardship. Ask your grandmother, who packed us into glass. You are not apart from the feast, little boat. You are the feast, walking.*

It lunged — or the world lurched, and Kestrel could never afterward say which — and the clearing tilted, and the serene carved face filled his entire vision, and he felt the first pull at the edges of himself: not pain, but *loss*, a memory sliding loose like a knot coming undone. He grabbed for it blindly and caught — what? A color. A smell of rain on dust. Something small and golden he hadn't known he had until it began to leave—

A staff struck the shrine-stone with a crack like a rifle shot.

The shape shattered. The sweetness curdled into stench. Odile stood over him where he'd fallen — when had he fallen? — with her staff raised and her face twisted into an expression he had never seen on her: fury, yes, but underneath it, unmistakably, fear.

"*Enough!*" she snarled at the clearing, and the word came out doubled, resonant, in a voice that was hers and something older, and every birch in the ring shuddered though there was no wind. "You know me, Harvest-Father. You knew my hands when they were younger and did worse things. Take one more pull at that boy and I will remind you what I remember of your true name, and you will spend the next century as a *doormat*."

Silence. Long, total, the silence of held breath. Then the presence withdrew — not vanishing but receding, folding back down into its beautiful stone like a snake into its hole, and the last thing Kestrel felt from it was not rage but something far more chilling: *amusement*, and beneath the amusement, patience renewed.

Odile hauled him upright by the collar. Her hands were shaking. So, he discovered, were his.

"What did it take?" she demanded. "Check. Now. What's missing?"

He checked, the way he'd taught himself to check his purse in crowds: running through the inventory of himself. The soldier's burning city — there. The widow's grief — there. The child praying for spring — there. His mother's face, his grandmother's kitchen, the weight of his first stolen memory, the sound of Odile's voice saying *walk faster* —

All there. All present. Except.

"A color," he said slowly. "Rain on dust. Something gold. I don't — I don't know what it was. I can't miss it properly because I don't know what it was."

Odile closed her eyes briefly, the way one does at news that confirms a feared diagnosis. "That's how it works," she said quietly. "That's exactly how it works. You don't miss the memory. You miss the hole where it was." She gripped his shoulder, hard, and turned him back toward the road. "Walk. Fast. And Kestrel — from here to the fens, you don't stop at any shrine, any well, any standing stone, any churchyard, anything with a face on it. Nothing begs anymore. They've all smelled you now. Word travels between gods faster than between men."

They walked. The afternoon aged into a bruised violet dusk, and the early winter deepened around them, snow beginning to fall in fat silent flakes that should have been weeks premature. Kestrel kept checking the inventory of himself, compulsively, a man counting coins after a brush with a cutpurse, and the golden absence rode along with him like a splinter.

It was full dark when he finally said it, because he couldn't not say it.

"Odile. The well-god. The crossroads thing. The Harvest-Father. They're all starving because the covenant broke. Because my grandmother helped break it." He watched her back stiffen under her hood. "Every mile of this road, they get hungrier. And I'm the only thing on it that's full."

"Yes."

"So either I figure out how to feed them—" he turned the words over as he said them, tasting their weight, feeling them settle into him with the terrible rightness of a key entering a lock, "—or I'm not a traveler on this road at all. I'm supplies."

Ahead of them, far off through the falling snow, a light burned in a farmhouse window — the first human warmth in hours — and beside the road, half-buried already in white, stood a milestone carved with a worn face, and as they passed, Kestrel would have sworn on anything he had left that its stone eyes followed him, and its stone lips moved.

Not begging. Not hunting.

Counting.
