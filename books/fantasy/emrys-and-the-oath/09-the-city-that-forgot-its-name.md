## 9. The City That Forgot Its Name

They saw Ashfall three days before they reached it, and Kestrel wished they hadn't.

It rose out of the white plain like a wound that had learned to stand — a city built in tiers of black stone, ringed by walls so high their tops vanished into the low gray sky, and above it all, at the crown of the highest tier, a spire of pale glass that caught no light and gave none back. The road to it was straight as a blade, lined on both sides with statues. Kestrel counted them as they walked. Soldiers, scholars, priests, mothers holding children — hundreds of figures, each one rendered in exquisite detail, each one with its face worn smooth.

Not weathered. *Worn.* As if a thousand hands had reached up, year after year, to touch the faces and found nothing there.

"Veyran's ancestors," Odile said, when she noticed him looking. "Or his victims. With him it was never clear which."

"You've been here before."

"I told you I served him." She pulled her scarf higher against the wind. Her breath came out in little ghosts that the cold ate instantly. "I stood in his throne room and swore an oath I'm still paying for. You'll see the throne room eventually, if we're unlucky. But first you'll see the streets, and the streets are worse."

"Worse than a dead god's winter?"

"Worse," Odile said, "because the people here don't know they're cold."

* * *

The gates of Ashfall were open. That was the first wrong thing. A capital in wartime — and every village they'd passed had spoken of war, of raids from the north, of the god-king's hunters riding out — should have barred its gates against strangers. Instead the gates stood wide, flanked by guards in black lacquered armor who did not check papers or ask names. They only looked at your hands.

Kestrel understood why when he saw the first citizen up close.

She was a baker's wife, or had been; she stood at a stall near the gate selling loaves, and around her neck, on a fine silver chain, hung a disc of smoked glass the size of a coin. When a customer asked for six loaves instead of four, she smiled, counted them out perfectly — and then paused, hand hovering over the change, until her fingers found the glass disc and pressed it briefly to her brow. Something flickered behind her eyes, like a lamp relit. Then she made change without hesitation.

"What is that?" Kestrel murmured.

"Memory-token." Odile didn't lower her voice. There was no need. No one here listened to strangers; no one here remembered strangers. "Smoked glass, ash-glass, whatever the street calls it. Each one holds a piece of the wearer's life. Their mother's face. Their wedding day. The name of the street they grew up on. Without it—" She nodded toward a side alley, where a man sat slumped against a wall with a bare throat, no chain, no disc. He wasn't begging. He was staring at his own hands as though they belonged to someone else, which, Kestrel thought, they did. "Without it, they're newborns in adult bodies. Veyran calls them the Unstrung. The city feeds them, houses them, works them. They just don't remember doing any of it."

Kestrel stopped walking.

He had spent his whole life handling memories the way other men handled coins. He knew what a stolen childhood felt like in the palm — warm, restless, heavier than its size. He knew what it did to a person to lose one: the widow in Hollow who'd bought herself empty, the soldier who'd sold his burning city and walked away lighter and somehow smaller. He had told himself, for years, that what he did was mercy. Surgery. The knife was sharp but the cut was clean.

This was not surgery. This was architecture.

Every token he saw — and now that he looked, he saw them everywhere, glinting at throats and wrists and ears, thousands upon thousands of them, a whole city strung with smoked glass — every token was a vial. His grandmother's vials, scaled up from a locked room to a metropolis. The same craft. The same theft. Just systematized. Sanctified. Made *fashionable*.

"They gave them up," he said. It wasn't a question, but Odile answered anyway.

"Some did, at first. Three generations ago, when the tokens were new, there were riots. People burned the mint where the glass was made." She pointed with her chin at a plaza ahead, where a fountain played — the only unfrozen water Kestrel had seen since leaving Hollow — and at its center stood a statue of a man mid-leap, hurling something. "That's supposed to be the ringleader. Veyran had him executed and then had him commemorated, because Veyran understands something important about people, Kestrel. He understands that memory is a burden, and that anyone who has carried one long enough will eventually want to set it down. All he did was offer to hold it for them. *You'll forget your grief. You'll forget your debts. You'll forget the war.* And people wept with gratitude."

"And the ones who didn't give them up?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. Ahead of them, a funeral procession crossed the square — six mourners in gray, a coffin on a bier, and not one of them crying. At the cemetery gate, each mourner touched their token to their lips in turn, and Kestrel realized with a lurch that they were *reading* their grief off the glass. Rehearsing sorrow they could not otherwise access. He watched a woman press her disc to her forehead and begin, obediently, to weep.

"The ones who refused," Odile said softly, "are the Unstrung. Or they're statues on the north road. Or they're nothing at all."

* * *

They took a room at an inn called the Ledger, because Odile said it catered to out-of-town merchants who asked few questions, and because — she added, with a thin smile — it was built over the old mint. "Poetic," she said. "I have a taste for it."

The innkeeper wore three tokens: one at her throat, one braided into her hair, one on a cord around her waist like a chatelaine's keys. She rented them a room without writing anything down. When Kestrel asked how she'd remember the booking, she looked at him with genuine puzzlement.

"Remember?" she said. "Sir, I *keep* accounts. That's different." And she tapped the disc at her throat twice, the way another woman might touch a saint's medal, and turned to the next customer.

Their room was small and clean and had a window overlooking a lane where lamplighters were working — except the lamps were not oil but glass, great globes of smoked crystal, and when the lamplighter touched his own token to each globe in turn, the globe would glow with a soft amber light that Kestrel slowly understood was not fire at all. It was *remembered* light. Summer afternoons, hearth-fires, candlelit dinners — harvested warmth and brightness, stored in glass, burned back to the city a secondhand sunset at a time.

He sat on the bed and put his head in his hands.

"I stole memories," he said to the floor. "For twenty years. I told myself it was small. One traveler at a time. They came to me, mostly. They wanted to forget."

"And now you're standing inside what it looks like when someone doesn't bother with the wanting part." Odile sat across from him, unbinding her boots. Her feet were bleeding; she'd walked the last day on a twisted ankle and said nothing. "Welcome to the end of your rationalizations. It's a large room. There's room enough for both of us."

"It's not the same."

"No. Yours was smaller." She said it without cruelty, which was worse. "But it's the same craft, Kestrel. Your grandmother's vials, Veyran's tokens — do you really think that's coincidence? Where do you think he learned it? Who do you think taught the first token-smiths to draw a life into glass?" She leaned forward. "Your grandmother sealed a valley and hid a handful of gods' memories in a locked room. Veyran sealed an empire and hid millions of lives in jewelry. She was a thief working alone. He is a thief with a mint, an army, and three hundred years of patience. But you of all people should recognize the signature."

Kestrel thought of the hidden chamber beneath his grandmother's house. The rows of labeled vials. The empty slot bearing his own name. He had felt betrayed by her then. Now, walking these streets, he felt something more complicated and more terrible: he felt *at home*. The architecture of Ashfall — the way memory had been made external, portable, tradable, taxable — was the architecture he'd grown up inside, the grammar of his own trade, written at the scale of a civilization.

He recognized it the way a forger recognizes his master's hand.

* * *

They went out at dusk, because Odile said dusk was when the city was most honest, and she was right.

In the blue hour, the tokens came alive. Workers coming off shift would pause in doorways, press glass to skin, and *become* — one moment blank-faced automatons shuffling home, the next laughing, weeping, singing snatches of songs, embracing strangers who were wives, sons, friends. For an hour the city overflowed with recovered humanity. And then, as the light failed completely, the tokens dimmed — Veyran's curfew, enforced not by soldiers but by the glass itself — and the people went quiet again, folding themselves back down into the present tense, carrying their histories home in lockboxes at their throats.

Kestrel watched a young man on a bridge over the frozen canal take out his token and simply hold it, turning it in the last light, not pressing it to his skin. Just holding it. Looking at the life inside the way a starving man looks through a window at a feast.

"He can't afford the hours," Odile said beside him. "Tokens charge. Every retrieval costs something — a coin, a favor, a day's labor. The poor sell their retrieval rights to the rich. There are men in the upper tiers who haven't lived a single hour of their own lives in forty years. They just wear other people's."

"That's not storage," Kestrel said. His voice came out strange. "That's *usury*. He's lending people back to themselves at interest."

"Now you understand why I left." Odile's breath ghosted in the dark. "I helped build this. Not the tokens — the ledgers. The accounting. I told myself it was bookkeeping. Someone has to keep track of what belongs to whom. It took me eleven years to hear what I was actually counting."

Below the bridge, in the shadow under the arch, Kestrel saw movement: the Unstrung man from the alley by the gate, or one like him, huddled with others of his kind around a small fire. There were perhaps a dozen of them, bare-throated, and they were doing something Kestrel had not seen once in the whole city.

They were telling stories.

An old woman among them was speaking, hands moving, and the others were leaning in, and though Kestrel couldn't hear the words, he knew the shape of it — the shape of people passing memory mouth to mouth, the old way, the free way, the way that required no glass and no coin and no king's permission. They had nothing to remember with. So they remembered *together*, badly, inventing, contradicting, correcting, and the story changed a little every night and belonged to all of them and none of them.

Something in Kestrel's chest turned over.

"Sef," he said, without meaning to say it aloud.

"What?"

"Nothing. Someone I have to find." He turned from the railing. "Odile. The covenant-stone. Veyran keeps it in the palace?"

"In the sanctum, under the glass spire. Why—"

"Because those people down there are proof of something." He was surprised by the steadiness of his own voice. "He took everything from them and they're still telling stories under a bridge. Memory wants to be shared, not owned. That's the flaw in his whole design. That's the crack." He looked up at the pale spire, rising black against the darker sky, at the sanctum somewhere beneath it where a shattered stone waited to be reforged — by Veyran's hands, if Veyran had his way, with mortals keeping only what he permitted. "And I think I'm the crack too. He knows it. That's why the gates were open."

Odile went very still. "What do you mean?"

"He let us in, Odile. No checks, no questions. Guards who look at your hands — looking for a vessel, not a weapon." Below them, the storytellers' fire guttered and bloomed. "He's been waiting three hundred years for the Oath to walk into his city. And tomorrow morning, I'm going to make sure he knows exactly what he's caught."

Above them, at the crown of the spire, a single window lit — not with remembered light, but with something colder, whiter, watching.

And far to the north, beyond the walls, beyond the winter, something that had once been a friend turned its smooth-worn face toward Ashfall and began, at last, to come home.
