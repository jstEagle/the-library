## 10. Audience with the God-King

They came for him at dawn, and they were courteous about it.

That was the worst part, Kestrel thought as he was escorted through the palace of Ashfall — not chains, not blades, but six guards in grey lacquered armor who bowed to him at the door of his cell and said, "His Radiance requests the pleasure of your company," as if this were an invitation to a garden party and not the den of the thing that had hunted him across half a dying world. They did not bind his hands. They did not need to. The stranger — Wren, the name surfaced now and then like a drowned body, *Wren, who used to laugh with rice stuck in his teeth* — walked beside him the whole way, hollow-eyed and silent, close enough that Kestrel could feel the absence radiating off him like cold from a stone.

The palace was beautiful in the way a well-made trap is beautiful. Corridors of white marble veined with silver, windows of glass so clear it seemed the winter morning had been let indoors. And everywhere, everywhere, the memory-tokens: citizens passing in the halls with small amulets at their throats or wrists, touching them absently the way a man touches a wound, checking that they are still themselves. Kestrel watched a young woman stop mid-stride, frown at her token, tap it twice against her collarbone, and only then continue on, her face smoothing into blank contentment. He had seen his grandmother's vials. He knew this architecture. Veyran had simply built it large enough to hold a city.

"You're staring," Wren said. His voice was flat as slate. "Don't. It marks you."

"Does it hurt?" Kestrel asked quietly. "Being what he made you?"

Something flickered behind Wren's eyes — deep, deep down, like a fish under winter ice — and was gone. "Nothing hurts," he said. "That's the point."

* * *

Veyran received him in a room without walls.

Not literally — there were walls, high ones, hung with tapestries gone grey with age — but they seemed to recede as Kestrel entered, so that the god-king sat alone in a vast field of shadowed air at the center of which burned a low fire, and around the fire, arranged like relics on an altar, lay pieces of a broken stone.

Kestrel stopped three paces from the fire. Every instinct he owned — the thief's instincts, the survivor's, the vessel's — screamed at him to run. He made his feet stay.

"Kestrel Ishikawa." Veyran's voice was warm. That was the second worst part. Kestrel had expected thunder, had braced for the voice of a landslide, and instead got something like a scholar greeting a colleague across a library table. The god-king rose from his seat — a plain chair, deliberately plain, a man who wanted you to notice he didn't need a throne — and he was not what Kestrel had imagined either. Tall, yes, and carrying his years strangely, the way old trees carry theirs; but his face was kind at rest, lined around the mouth as if he smiled often, with dark eyes that held no madness at all. Only patience. Patience worn smooth as river stone by three centuries of waiting.

"You know my name," Kestrel said.

"I have known your name since before your grandmother was born." Veyran gestured to the seat opposite his own. "Sit. Please. You've crossed the Roof of the World in the wrong season to be offered nothing but standing."

Kestrel sat. The fire between them smelled of cedar and, beneath it, faintly, of ozone — the smell of the border, of magic pressing against a sealed door.

"Do you know what these are?" Veyran asked, laying one long hand near the largest fragment of stone. It was black shot through with veins of gold, and where it had broken, the gold had gone dull and grey, like blood dried in a wound.

"The covenant-stone," Kestrel said. "Sef told me. Odile confirmed it."

"Sef." Veyran said the name gently, tasting it. "Your sister. Yes. She would know — she has spent her life feeding crumbs to a starving god, poor child, out of loyalty to a covenant she never saw whole." He turned the fragment slightly, and the firelight moved along its dead gold veins. "This stone was not built, Kestrel. It was *spoken*. When the world was younger and the gods still walked openly among mortals, the two peoples met here — on this exact floor, though the floor was then a mountain meadow — and each gave an oath. The gods swore their strength to the keeping of the seasons, the harvests, the births and deaths. Mortals swore their remembrance. Memory is food to a god. Did you know that? Not worship — worship is merely memory wearing its Sunday clothes. Every story told about a god, every prayer, every curse, every child's frightened whisper — all of it sustains. And in exchange, the gods poured themselves into the world. Rain. Spring. Mercy in small doses."

"And then it broke."

"Then it broke." For just a moment, something moved under the kindness in Veyran's face — grief, perhaps, or its fossil. "Three hundred years ago, a woman named Maerin Ishikawa gathered up the memories of seventeen gods into vessels of glass and hid them inside a valley she walled away from the world, and she spoke a silence over the whole arrangement so complete that even the gods forgot to miss themselves. The stone shattered. The covenant died. And winter—" he spread his hands, encompassing the tapestries, the city, the frozen fields beyond, "—winter began to learn patience too."

"My grandmother," Kestrel said carefully, "did it to protect someone."

"Oh, she told herself that. They always tell themselves that." Veyran leaned forward, and the firelight caught his eyes, and Kestrel saw — with a lurch — that they were not entirely dark after all. There was gold in them, deep down, the same dead gold as the stone's veins. "But come. I did not summon you across the ice to argue genealogy. I summoned you because you are the last living vessel of the Oath, and I have waited three hundred years for exactly one thing, and you are standing in the middle of it."

* * *

He showed him the rest of the collection first.

It was, Kestrel had to admit, a museum of exquisite cruelty. Down corridors that breathed warm air against the killing cold outside, Veyran walked him past vaults and vitrines: crowns of minor kings whose kingdoms had forgotten them; a bell that rang only when someone lied nearby; the tax ledgers of Ashfall going back nine generations, each citizen's life reduced to columns of neat figures. Mortals lived in this palace the way specimens live in jars.

"Why do they wear the tokens?" Kestrel asked, watching a servant glide past with a bronze disc at her throat. "What do they get?"

"Peace," Veyran said simply. "Ask yourself what your valley was, Kestrel. A place where nothing magical could ever hurt anyone. No curses, no hungry gods, no debts collected in the dark. Your grandmother built a little Ashfall and called it Hollow. I have merely refused to pretend the peace must be small." He paused before a window overlooking the city, roofs furred with snow, smoke rising straight and orderly from a thousand chimneys. "Every soul down there knows exactly as much of their past as is good for them. The widow does not relive her husband's death every night. The soldier does not wake screaming. The debt-collector's victims do not remember the faces of those who ruined them. Is that so different from what you did? You sold forgetting in a market stall. I give it away."

"It's not the same."

"No," Veyran agreed pleasantly. "Yours was cruder. But the principle, Kestrel — the principle is identical, and you know it, and some tired corner of you has always known it. That is why you are here. That is why you crossed the border rather than letting the wards fail and crush you. Because some part of you suspected that the man at the end of the road might not be a monster." He turned from the window, and his smile was rueful. "I will disappoint you eventually. Everyone does. But not today."

* * *

They returned to the fire and the broken stone, and Veyran crouched beside it and lifted the largest fragment as if it weighed nothing, turning it in his hands.

"When the Oath was spoken, it required a bearer," he said. "A mortal who carried the mortal half, as the eldest of the gods carried the divine half. The bearer remembered for everyone. When mortals faltered, the bearer's own memory fed the stone. It was an honor and a horror, and the last bearer was Maerin Ishikawa herself — until she decided the price was too high, and stole the office along with everything else, and hid it in her grandson's blood." He set the fragment down with a click. "You felt it, didn't you? In the valley. Prayers arriving in your sleep like letters addressed to a dead man. Frost on your windows in a place where magic cannot go. The gods have been knocking on you for years, Kestrel. You are a door pretending to be a wall."

Kestrel's mouth was dry. "And you want me to open it."

"I want us to open it. Together." Veyran sat again, and when he spoke, his voice dropped into something almost intimate, almost gentle. "Hear me fully before you refuse. Reforge the Oath — you and I. You hold the mortal half, the true half, the half that remembers. I hold the divine. The gods wake fed and whole. The winter breaks within a season — not the slow, bleeding restoration your sister's ragged priests dream of, but spring, real spring, green to the high passes by midsummer. Mortals keep their memories — all of them, freely, no tokens, no vials — except—"

"Except," Kestrel echoed.

"Except what I permit." Veyran said it without flinching, without apology, and somehow that honesty was worse than any lie. "There must be order, Kestrel. You have seen the world outside your valley. You have seen what mortals do with freedom and famine. Some memories are plagues. Some griefs burn villages. I would curate. Gently. Rarely. But I would hold the shears, because someone must, and I have had three hundred years to learn how heavy they are."

"And the gods? What do they get to keep?"

"Their lives. Their worship. Their places in the world." Veyran's golden-dark eyes held steady. "Under me, no god starves. Under me, no god eats a village's memories to survive the winter — you've seen that too, don't pretend you haven't, on your precious Road of Small Gods. I am not offering tyranny. I am offering *stewardship*. The alternative is what you have already watched happen: gods waking mad and starving, mortals freezing by degrees, and somewhere in the middle, a thief being slowly erased by his own good intentions." He tilted his head. "How much of yourself have you lost already, since you left the valley? Be honest. You count them at night, don't you? The missing hours. The names you can't find."

Kestrel didn't answer. He didn't have to. Veyran saw it land, and had the grace not to smile.

* * *

"There is one more thing," the god-king said, when the silence had stretched long enough to ache. "You should hear it from me rather than from Odile's guilty deathbed confessions or Sef's bitter arithmetic."

"Don't." The word came out of Kestrel before he chose it.

"She loved me once." Veyran's voice went quiet, and the patience in his face cracked — just a hairline, just for a breath — and underneath it Kestrel saw something old and wounded and terribly human, or what had been human once. "Maerin. Before she was your grandmother's grandmother's ghost, before she was the woman who walled away the world — she was my friend. More than my friend. She sat where you are sitting, by this same fire, when it was a campfire on a mountainside, and we planned the reforging together, the two of us. She was going to be the bearer. She was going to stand at the stone and speak the Oath with me, and we were going to end the first winter, the small one, the ordinary one." He looked into the flames. "And then she saw what bearing the Oath had done to the bearer before her — what it cost, what it *ate* — and she could not bear it. Not for herself. For the child she was already carrying. So she took the divine memories, all seventeen gods' worth, and she ran, and she built her cage, and she called it mercy, and she let the world freeze rather than let her blood pay my price."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the high windows with a sound like a long exhale.

"She never spoke of you," Kestrel said at last.

"No. She wouldn't. Guilt makes archivists of us all — we seal what we cannot burn." Veyran looked up, and the kindness was back, seamless, and Kestrel understood suddenly that the crack had been the performance and the kindness was the mask and possibly both were true at once, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "I am not asking you to forgive her, Kestrel. I am asking you to finish what she could not. She chose fear. Choose better. Take my hand at the stone, speak the Oath as it was meant to be spoken, and your sister keeps her god, your friend Wren keeps whatever is left of him, and the world comes back from the dead. Or refuse me, and I will take the memories you carry anyway — I have the means, you know I do, I have had three hundred years to prepare the means — and the Oath will be forged in chains instead of partnership, and you will watch it happen from wherever hollowed men go."

He extended his hand across the fire. Palm open. Patient. Golden-eyed.

"You are a thief," Veyran said softly. "The best I have ever heard of. So weigh it as a thief would: what do you gain, what do you lose, and who pays the difference? I have made my offer fair. Fairer than fair. All I ask is that you sleep on it — in comfort, unguarded, because we both know the guards are a courtesy — and give me your answer at dawn."

Kestrel looked at the hand. At the broken stone gleaming dully beside it. At the fire that smelled of cedar and borders.

He did not take it. He rose, bowed the precise degree courtesy demanded and no more, and let the silent guards walk him back through the beautiful corridors, past the citizens touching their tokens, past Wren walking beside him like a man-shaped absence.

They gave him a room with a fire and a window and a lock on the *outside*, and left him alone with the longest night of the year coming on.

Kestrel sat by the fire and counted what remained of himself, name by name, hour by hour, and somewhere past midnight he realized his fingers had been tracing, over and over on the armrest, a shape he did not remember learning:

the outline of a small glass vial.
