## 11. Memories That Aren't Mine

The palace gates were behind him, and Kestrel ran.

Not the practiced, economical run of a thief — that was a different body, a different life, a man who moved through market crowds like water through reeds. This was the graceless, lung-tearing flight of an animal that had finally understood it was prey. His boots struck the pale flagstones of Ashfall's processional way and the sound came back at him doubled, tripled, from the marble faces of the god-king's avenue, and every echo sounded like pursuit.

Behind him, the palace doors stood open still. Veyran had not ordered them closed. That was the worst of it — Kestrel had looked back once, halfway down the steps, and seen the god-king standing in the doorway with his hands folded, watching him go the way a man watches weather he has already predicted. *Come back when you're ready,* the posture said. *You will be ready.*

Kestrel turned down the first alley and ran harder.

Ashfall at dusk was a city of small lights and smaller silences. He had noticed it on the way in, cataloguing it with a thief's eye: how few people spoke to one another, how they touched the memory-tokens at their throats before answering even simple questions — *your name, your trade, do you have children* — as if checking coin before spending it. A city where everyone was rich in recollection and none of them could afford it. His grandmother's theft, he had thought then. Scaled up. Perfected. He had felt horror, clean and distant, the horror of a craftsman seeing his tools misused.

That distance was gone now.

He ducked through a fish-market shuttered for the evening, over a wall slick with old brine, into a tanner's yard where the smell hit him like a physical blow — and in the middle of the smell, absurdly, impossibly, he smelled woodsmoke and crushed mint, and he was somewhere else entirely.

* * *

A temple. White stone gone gold in the light of a hundred lamps, and the air so thick with incense that breathing felt like drinking. He is small — no, not small, but young, young in a way he has never been young, because the Valley of Hollow does not allow this kind of remembering — and his knees ache from standing too long on stone, and someone is laughing beside him.

A girl. Dark-haired, quick-eyed, her braid coming loose the way braids always did when she couldn't be bothered to fix them. She elbows him, and the elbow says everything: *stand straighter, sing louder, you're embarrassing us both.*

"Kestrel," she whispers, during the pause between hymns, savoring it like stolen honey. "When I'm a priestess, I'm going to make them name a holiday after you. The Feast of the Boy Who Fidgets."

"I don't fidget."

"You are fidgeting *now.*"

He looks down at his own hands — a child's hands, ink-stained, one knuckle scabbed from climbing the temple wall — and the hands are wrong, they are *wrong*, they are not the hands he has worn for thirty years, and the temple begins to tilt —

* * *

— and he was on his knees in the tanner's yard, vomit burning his throat, salt-stink in his nostrils, and the last gold light of the temple lamps still ghosting across his vision like an afterimage burned into the eye.

"No," he said aloud, to no one, to the yard, to himself. "No, that isn't — I never —"

But he had. That was the thing about it. It hadn't come to him like a story or a dream, soft-edged and negotiable. It had come like a *memory*. With weight. With the ache in his childish knees still lodged in his adult legs. With the girl's laugh still caught somewhere behind his sternum, refusing to be swallowed.

He knew that girl.

He didn't know her. He had never seen her in his life. And he knew her the way he knew his own heartbeat, which was to say: completely, and without ever having thought about it once.

*Sef.*

The name arrived in his mouth unbidden, and he clapped a hand over it as if he could push it back in.

* * *

He made it three streets before the next one took him.

This time it happened mid-stride, between one cobblesone and the next, so that he walked straight out of Ashfall and into a river shallows under a sky bruised purple with late summer. Cold water to his ankles. Dragonflies stitching the air. And the same girl — older now, twelve maybe, thirteen — standing hip-deep and furious, holding up a fish by the gills like evidence at a trial.

"You promised," she said.

"I didn't promise anything."

"You *promised.* You put your hand on the stone and everything. That's how promises work where we're from, Kess. Hand on stone. Binding." She dropped the fish back into the water with tremendous dignity. "You said if Mother let me train at the river shrine, you'd come see me take my vows. All of them. Even the boring ones. Especially the boring ones."

"And I meant it."

"You meant it *then.*" She waded toward him, and her face did something complicated — the fury cracking just enough to show the fear underneath, the fear every child of the temple families carried without naming. "Everyone means things then. The gods mean things then. That's what Grandmother says. That's why the Oath matters, Kess. Because everyone breaks, and the stone remembers anyway."

She held out her hand, dripping, imperious.

"So say it again. On the stone."

And he — this other boy, this boy with his own face and a life he had never lived — took her hand, and pressed it flat against the covenant-stone at the river's edge, old and warm as a sleeping animal, and said:

"I'll be there. Whatever happens. Whatever comes. I'll find you, Sef. Hand on stone."

* * *

Kestrel came back to himself against a wall in a guttering lamplit street, both palms pressed flat to the cold masonry as if he'd been holding it up. His breath came in ragged pulls. Passersby gave him the wide berth Ashfall gave all erratic behavior; one woman touched her token and murmured something that was probably a prayer and probably went nowhere.

*Hand on stone.*

His hands were shaking. He looked at them — his real hands, thief's hands, scarred across the palm from a lockpick slipped twenty years ago — and tried to fit them against the boy's hands in the memory, and they matched. Of course they matched. Same bones. Same crooked second finger on the left, the one Odile had once laughed at and called a cardsharp's finger.

The memories weren't visiting him. They weren't haunting him. They were *returning*. Coming home the way water comes home to a dry channel, finding the shape that had been cut for them and waiting, empty, for three hundred years.

Three hundred years.

No. Not three hundred years. That was the valley's arithmetic, the sealed and frozen arithmetic of a place outside time. Out here, in the world where winters deepened and gods starved, the sum came out differently. The boy in the temple would be — he did the calculation and his mind flinched away from it — the girl would be older than Odile. Older than the hills, almost. Sustained by something. Stolen years, banked somewhere, poured into two children to keep them fresh until —

Until what?

Until their grandmother finished hiding them.

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do, fully formed and undeniable, and Kestrel slid down the wall until he was sitting in the street like a drunk, not caring who saw.

The empty vial.

In the locked room beneath his grandmother's house there had been dozens of vials, each labeled in her careful hand — *the storm-god's grief, the hearth-mother's patience, the river-lord's first spring* — each one a stolen piece of a god, hidden in the one place magic could never reach. And one slot, near the center, empty. One label with no vial above it.

*Kestrel Ishikawa.*

He had stood in that room three days ago — or a lifetime ago; time had gone strange since the border — and read his own name and felt only puzzlement. A mistake, he'd thought. An old woman's sentimentality. A placeholder.

It was not a placeholder. It was a *label*. The most accurate one in the room. Every other vial held a god's memory, torn out and bottled. His slot was empty because his memory had never been bottled at all. It had been left exactly where it was — inside him — buried so deep beneath the valley's silence that even he couldn't dig it up. His grandmother hadn't stolen his past.

She had *hidden* it in him. The one vault no thief would ever think to rob.

"Oh," Kestrel said. It came out very small. "Oh, you *cruel* old woman."

* * *

He didn't remember deciding to move, but he was moving — up out of the street, through the tanners' quarter, toward the high terraces where the city thinned and the night wind came down off the mountains smelling of snow that should not exist in autumn. Grief and fury rode him in harness, taking turns with the whip.

Because it wasn't only cruelty. That was the trap of it, the thing that made his eyes burn and his jaw ache. He could see it all now, assembling itself with terrible clarity, the way a fence sees the whole shape of a con only after the mark has paid.

Veyran wanted the Oath reforged under his dominion. Wanted a vessel. Wanted someone mortal enough to hold the covenant's human half and pliable enough to hold it *his* way. And there had been a boy born to a temple family, a boy whose blood marked him for exactly that work, a boy who would have grown up into the god-king's instrument —

— unless someone loved him enough to steal him.

His grandmother had not erased his life. She had *smuggled* it. Carried him across the world into a valley with no magic in it, buried his true self so deep that no god could smell it, no king could find it, and raised him as a petty thief in a market town, teaching him — oh, she must have *laughed* — teaching him to steal memories. Teaching him the family trade as a joke only she understood. *Here, child, learn to lift what people keep in their heads. Practice on strangers. Never once look in the mirror.*

And she had died before she could tell him. Or chosen not to tell him. Or told him in the only language she trusted him to survive hearing — an empty vial and his own name written in her hand, waiting thirty years for him to be desperate enough to break into her locked room and read it.

"You could have *told* me," he said to the night, to her, to the dark windows of Ashfall's upper city. His voice cracked on it. "You could have sat me down. One evening. One cup of tea. *Kestrel, you have a sister.*"

A sister.

The word detonated quietly, and under its shock something else bloomed — something worse than grief, warmer and more dangerous. Hope. Because the memories kept coming, and in every single one, the girl was alive, laughing, furious, *real* — and memories were not proof of anything, he knew that better than any man alive, he had sold a thousand false tomorrows to people who needed them —

— but the soldier's memory in chapter one of his career, the burning city, the flicker of a god's face — that had been real. The prayer of the child begging the river god for spring — that had been real. Everything real reached him. Everything real *collected* on him, Odile had said. Things which answer prayers also collect debts.

If the memories were returning, they were returning for a reason. If the covenant-stone had shown him the boy's face, if Veyran had looked at him across a table with something like recognition and something like hunger, then the boy was not dead. The boy had been *kept*. Somewhere out in the starving winter world, an old woman sustained by stolen years was still alive, still waiting for a brother who had promised, hand on stone, whatever happens, whatever comes —

"I'll find you," Kestrel whispered to the empty street. His breath smoked in the wrong-season cold. "I know I said it. I don't remember saying it, and I'm going to be angry about that for the rest of my life, but I said it, and I keep my promises. It's the only thing I've got left that's mine."

Except it wasn't. None of it was his. His childhood was a forgery. His skills were a disguise. His name was a label on an empty vial. He stood in a foreign city in a dying winter and could not point to a single true thing about himself except the crooked finger and the promise.

And yet.

And yet the promise had survived. Buried under three hundred years of silence and a forged life, sealed behind wards his grandmother had bled to make, and it had *survived*. Whatever else she had stolen from him, she had not managed to steal that. It had waited in him like a seed waits — not alive, exactly, but not dead either, holding its small green secret through the longest winter.

Maybe that was what identity was. Not the memories. Not the name. Maybe it was the thing that kept the promise even when the boy who made it was gone.

He was still standing there, holding onto that thought like a rope over a drop, when he heard the footsteps.

Not the random scatter of Ashfall foot-traffic. Measured. Deliberate. More than one set, spread across the mouth of the alley behind him and, when he turned, another pair at the far end, blocking the way toward the terraces. Figures in the grey livery of the palace, unhurried, their breath smoking in the cold, and at their center a woman whose face stopped Kestrel's heart and restarted it wrong.

He knew her.

He had met her once, dying at the valley border, her memory of him torn out mid-sentence. He had found the oath-token on her body bearing his family crest. And now here she stood, alive and upright and hollow-eyed, wearing the god-king's grey, and when she smiled it was the smile of someone doing it from the outside, a skill remembered rather than felt.

"Hello, Kess," she said gently. "You've been a difficult man to find. Well — no. That's not true, is it? You've been a difficult man to *keep*."

The soldiers began to close.

Kestrel's hand went to his knife out of thirty years of habit, and the habit, at least, was truly his. But his eyes were on the woman, on the beloved stranger's face, and the last thought he had before the alley swallowed him was not of escape.

It was of a river shallows, and a girl holding out a dripping hand, saying *say it again, on the stone* — and somewhere in the starving winter world, if she still lived, his sister had no idea that the brother she mourned had just been found.

By the wrong people.
