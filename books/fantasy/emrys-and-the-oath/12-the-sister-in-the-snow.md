## 12. The Sister in the Snow

The memory-threads did not behave like threads at all.

Kestrel had expected them to be like the silver filaments he'd seen once in a dying god's shrine — delicate, luminous, something you could follow with your eyes. Instead they were more like currents in deep water: invisible to sight, felt against the skin, tugging at him from somewhere north and east whenever he stood still long enough to listen. Odile called them *pulls* and refused to say more about them, which was her way of saying she knew exactly what they were and hated every one of them.

Three days out of the frozen village where the river god had died mid-prayer, the pull grew strong enough that Kestrel stopped needing to sleep to feel it. It hummed in his back teeth. It itched behind his sternum like a word on the tip of the tongue. And threaded through it — faint, but unmistakable now that he knew what to listen for — was a voice.

Not words. Not yet. But a cadence. Someone praying, over and over, in a rhythm as familiar to him as his own heartbeat, though he could not have said why.

"She's close," he said, on the morning of the fourth day, when the pull yanked so hard he stumbled on the ice-glazed road.

Odile looked up from rewrapping her boots in strips of wool and tar. "You don't know that."

"I know it the way you know a name is yours." He flexed his fingers inside his gloves. The cold had gotten into everything since they'd left Hollow; even fire barely seemed to reach him anymore, as if the stolen years in his blood were slowly teaching his body that warmth was someone else's property. "It's her. The sister."

"The sister who would be sixty years old by now," Odile said, not unkindly, which for Odile was a kind of tenderness. "If she's alive at all."

"She's alive." He said it with a certainty that frightened him, because it didn't come from hope. It came from the thread. From the prayer-rhythm that matched his pulse. "And she's not sixty."

* * *

They found the shrine at dusk, where the road climbed out of a birch hollow and the snow lay blue with coming dark.

It was nothing. That was Kestrel's first thought — a disappointment so sharp it felt like grief. A ring of river stones half-buried in drift. A post hung with ribbons gone stiff as wire. A wooden bowl filled with snow that someone had been melting, drop by drop, over coals in a tin brazier, because you could not give a river god ice; you had to give it water, even here, even now, even if it took all day to make a cupful.

And kneeling beside the brazier, feeding it splinters of wood one at a time, was a woman in a priestess's gray.

She was younger than Odile. Younger than Kestrel had braced himself for. Her hair was dark, shot through early with white at the temples, and her hands were cracked and red from cold and work, and there was frost in her eyelashes, and none of that mattered at all, because when she lifted her head at the crunch of their boots —

He knew her face.

Not the way he knew faces in stolen memories, catalogued and secondhand. He knew it the way you know your own reflection: instantly, totally, before thought. The set of her jaw was his jaw. The way her brow furrowed was the way his furrowed in every polished surface he'd ever glanced past. She looked at him across twenty feet of trampled snow, and something in his chest tore open along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"Sef," he said. His voice came out ruined.

The woman went very still. The splinter in her hand dropped into the snow. "That's not possible," she said. "That's not — no one knows that name. No one living knows that name."

"You do," Kestrel said. He took a step forward and the world tilted, the pull in his chest and the recognition in hers meeting somewhere in the air between them. "Sef. It's me."

"Don't." She was on her feet now, backing away, one hand pressed flat over her heart as if holding something in. "Don't come closer. Don't say it again. You can't be —" Her breath came out in ragged plumes. "I buried you. I *buried* you, I carried you myself, I was eleven years old and I carried you out of the temple and the river took —"

"The river didn't take me." The memories were surfacing now, unbidden, the way they always did outside the valley — but these came gently, like things rising in warm water rather than knives. A temple of white stone. A garden with a plum tree. A girl with braids chasing him down a corridor, both of them laughing, both of them maybe eight years old, maybe nine. "The river kept me. Sef — Seffy —"

"*Don't call me that.*" But her voice broke on it, and the hand over her heart pressed harder, and Kestrel saw — really saw, with a thief's eyes that had never once failed him — what was wrong with her.

She was too old.

Not old the way he'd feared, not sixty. But she was not the girl from the garden either. She was perhaps thirty-five, forty, lines at her eyes and that premature white in her hair — and the memories rising in him said she should be nineteen. Twenty at most. The same years that had been stolen from him had been *spent* somewhere, and here was the spending, written on her face.

"You've been burning them," he said quietly. "My years. Ours."

Sef flinched as if struck.

* * *

They sat around the brazier because there was nowhere else to sit, and because the cold beyond its little circle of light had teeth. Odile hung back at the edge of the shrine, arms crossed, watchful, saying nothing — a witness, not a participant. Kestrel understood. This was not her reconciliation to intrude upon. Though he noticed she kept her eyes on Sef's hands, and her weight on the balls of her feet, and he wondered what Odile saw when she looked at a priestess of the dying river god that made her ready to run.

"You were supposed to stay dead," Sef said at last. She had not touched the tea she'd made — real tea, hoarded, brewed in a pot black with use. "That was the whole point. Grandmother sealed the valley with you inside it, asleep, *unremembered*, so that Veyran could never find you. So that no one could ever find you. And instead you walked out. You walked *out*, Kestrel, with your face and your name and that —" her eyes flicked to his chest, to whatever she could see glowing faintly under his coat, the vessel-light he'd stopped being able to hide weeks ago, "— that thing in you, and now every god and every hunter on two continents can smell you."

"I didn't choose to walk out. The wards failed."

"They failed *because of you*. Because the prayers started coming through you." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, brittle as the ribbons on the shrine-post. "Do you know how I found out you were alive? Three hundred years I served the river god. Three hundred years, Kestrel. I was there when the covenant broke. I watched the water go gray. And then one night, a few months ago, my god sat up in his shrine like a man hearing his name called across a crowded room, and he said: *someone is carrying my brother's face.*"

Kestrel's mouth went dry. "Your god is three hundred years old."

"We all are." She said it simply, terribly. "Everyone the covenant touched. Everyone who held a piece of it. When it shattered, time stopped knowing what to do with us. Some of us froze. Some of us starved. Some of us—" she spread her cracked hands, "—burned. The stolen years weren't just hidden in you, brother. They were *rationed*. Grandmother siphoned them out to keep the ones she loved alive. Me. A handful of others. Little drips and drabs, enough to keep our hearts beating while the world forgot us." Her eyes came up, and they were wet and furious and full of love, all three at once, and it was the worst thing Kestrel had ever seen. "Every year of my life since the valley sealed has been paid for out of your account. Out of the life you were never allowed to live."

The brazier popped. Somewhere in the birches, snow slid off a branch with a soft whump.

"So every winter I get older," Sef went on, quieter now, "and every year the drips get smaller, because grandmother's stores are running out, because *you're* running out, because you keep giving the memories back — yes, Odile told me, don't look surprised, we've corresponded longer than you've known her — and when the last of it goes, I go with it. Back to my true age. Which is dust." She wrapped her hands around the untouched teacup, not drinking, just holding the warmth. "So understand what you did, Kestrel, when you crossed that border. You didn't just endanger yourself. You started a clock. My clock. Every mile you walk toward Veyran, I burn faster."

The accusation landed exactly where she meant it to. Kestrel sat with it, let it sink into him like cold water, because he owed it that much. He thought of the empty vial in grandmother's locked room with his own name on it. He thought of the temple, the sister, the promise made before the valley existed — still only fragments, but fragments with weight now, fragments with her face attached.

"I'm sorry," he said. And then, because he was his grandmother's grandson and a thief to the bone and sorry had never once fixed anything: "But I'm not going back."

"I know." To his surprise, there was no anger in it. Something almost like relief. "Grandmother told me you wouldn't. She said —" Sef stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again. "She said you were the stubborn one. She said you got that from her."

"She told you about me? All this time?"

"Every visit. Every decade or so, when I could afford the journey to the border. I'd stand on the outside and she'd stand on the inside and we'd shout across the wards like fishwives." A ghost of a smile, quickly gone. "She couldn't tell me where you were or what you'd become. The seal wouldn't allow it. But she let me see you once. From a distance. Market day, four years ago. You were arguing with a fruit seller about the price of quinces, and you were winning, and you looked —" her voice caught, "— you looked happy. Ordinary. I stood in the snow for three hours watching you buy quinces, and then I walked home and thanked the river god you'd never know any of this, because ignorance was the only gift anyone had ever managed to give you."

Kestrel looked into the coals. "The quinces were overpriced," he said, and Sef laughed despite herself, a short wet sound, and for a moment they were just two people who shared a jawline and a garden and a plum tree, sitting by a fire while the wrong winter pressed in around them.

Then the light under his skin flickered, and the moment passed.

"It's getting worse," Sef said, all business now, leaning forward. "The flickering. Show me."

He pulled open his coat. Beneath his shirt, the vessel-mark — the oath-sigil that had burned itself into his chest the night the first prayer came through him — pulsed with a slow, arrhythmic light, like a lamp low on oil. Sef sucked in a breath through her teeth.

"How long?"

"A month. Maybe more. It's worse when I return memories. Each one takes something."

"Because you're not a thief anymore, you're a *keystone*." She said it the way a physician names a disease. "The covenant-stone shattered, but stone remembers its shape. All those pieces — the god-memories, the stolen years, the oath itself — they've been looking for a place to gather for three centuries. And grandmother, may her bones forgive her, built you into the mold before she poured you into the valley." She reached out, hesitated, then laid two fingers lightly over the sigil. The light steadied under her touch, and Kestrel gasped — not in pain, but in recognition, because her fingers were warm in a way that had nothing to do with the brazier, warm the way the temple had been warm, warm the way home —

"You feel it too," he whispered.

"I've felt it for three hundred years." She withdrew her hand. "It's the covenant, Kestrel. What's left of it. It's been calling its pieces home, and you are carrying most of them, and the reason you're flickering is that you're trying to be a keystone without being an arch. It doesn't work. It was never meant to work. There's only one way to hold the Oath, and grandmother couldn't do it, and Veyran won't do it, because the price is —"

She stopped.

The wind moved through the birches. In the bowl beside the shrine, the melted snow-water trembled, though there was no tremor in the ground.

"What price?" Kestrel asked.

Sef looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide — watched the calculation move behind her eyes, love and fear and three hundred years of careful rationing all weighing each other — and he knew, with the certainty of the threads and the pull and the prayer-rhythm that matched his pulse, that whatever she said next would change the shape of everything.

Before she could speak, the water in the bowl froze solid with a crack like a snapped bone.

Sef was on her feet instantly, staring at the bowl, and her face had gone the color of the snow. "No," she breathed. "No, no — he was stronger today, he drank, I gave him —"

From the shrine stones, from the ribbons, from the frozen ground itself, came a sound Kestrel had heard only once before, in a village where a god had died mid-prayer: a long, thin exhalation, like wind through a reed, like the last breath of something vast.

The river god was dying. Here. Now. And from the look on Sef's face — from the way she fell to her knees in the snow and pressed both hands to the frozen bowl as if she could warm a river with her palms — Kestrel understood that it wasn't dying of the winter.

It was dying because its last strength had been spent keeping *him* alive. Keeping the vessel breathing until the pieces could arrive.

"Kestrel," Sef whispered, and her voice was no longer accusatory at all. It was terrified. "Whatever I was about to tell you — forget the price. We don't have time for the price anymore. They're coming. Tonight. I can feel them on the road — iron and honey, that's how Veyran's hunters smell. Iron and honey."

Far down the valley road, where the dark had pooled thickest, something glinted.
