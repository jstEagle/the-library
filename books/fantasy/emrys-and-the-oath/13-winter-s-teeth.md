## 13. Winter's Teeth

The snow began to fall upward.

Odile noticed it first, on the third day out of the river country, when the wind died and the flakes drifted past her face toward the bruised sky like sparks returning to a fire. She had seen weather do strange things in three hundred years of walking the world's edges, but never this — never a winter so hungry it pulled at the world instead of merely covering it. She said nothing. Kestrel walked ahead with his collar up and his hands buried in his sleeves, and Sef moved beside him with the careful, counting steps of someone who had learned long ago that the ground could not be trusted. Neither of them needed another reason to be afraid.

They were following the old pilgrim road north, the one that threaded between the shrines of the small gods, and the road was dying under their feet. Odile had walked this way as a young woman — truly young, before Veyran had taught her that youth was a currency and she'd learned to stop spending it — and she remembered wells that sang, hedgerows thick with offerings, door-lintels carved with little faces whose eyes followed travelers with something like affection. Now the shrine-niches stood empty or worse than empty: some held gods still, but shrunken, gray as ash pressed into stone, their features worn smooth by neglect. One well they passed still murmured when Sef leaned over it, a thin voice repeating *cold, cold, cold* in a language older than any kingdom, and Sef had wept without seeming to notice she was doing it, and Kestrel had stood very still, listening the way he listened to everything now, as if the world were a memory he was trying to steal back from itself.

Odile watched him and felt the confession rising in her throat like water in a flooded cellar. She had been holding it down for days. She would not be able to hold it much longer.

*Not yet,* she told herself. *Let them have one more warm night.*

The village appeared at dusk, if it could be called appearing — it simply resolved out of the white, a cluster of low houses around a chapel-shrine, smoke rising from chimneys in threads so straight and still they looked painted. The place was called Vell's Crossing, though the sign at its edge had been defaced, the god's name chiseled out by someone who had perhaps hoped that anonymity might protect what devotion no longer could. A woman met them at the first house, arms crossed against the cold, face raw and red.

"Travelers." Not a question. Her eyes went to Sef's priestess marks, then to Kestrel, and lingered there with an expression Odile couldn't read — hunger, maybe, or hope, which in these days amounted to the same thing. "We've room. We always have room now. The ones who left didn't take theirs with them."

"You're kind," Sef said.

"We're cold," the woman said. "Kindness is what's left when the firewood runs short."

Her name was Maren, and she fed them barley soup in a room where every surface had been given over to candles — dozens of them, hundreds, guttering on shelves and sills and the floor itself, wax pooling in the cracks between stones. Odile counted them out of old habit and did not like the number she arrived at. Candles were prayers made visible. This many meant a village that had stopped believing its prayers were being heard and had begun, desperately, to shout.

"The river god," Maren said, when Sef asked. "Vell. He's been here since before the village. He's in the spring under the chapel — the water never froze, not even in the worst winters my grandmother told stories about. But it's freezing now. An inch a night. We can hear it. Like something knocking."

"Like something drowning," Odile said quietly, and Maren looked at her with the flat defiance of people who have decided not to hear bad news.

"He won't die," she said. "Gods don't die."

"No," Odile agreed, because there was no kindness in correcting her, only cruelty. "They don't. They just stop being listened to, and then they starve down to nothing, and then there is a hole in the world shaped like them, and the cold comes in through the hole." She wrapped her hands around the soup bowl. "I've watched it happen. In the north, when I was young. Whole valleys went quiet in a single season. You'd walk through them and feel like you'd gone deaf — not silence exactly, but the absence of something you hadn't known was there until it wasn't."

Maren's jaw tightened. "Then why are the gods letting it happen? If they're real, if they're starving — why don't they *do* something?"

Odile glanced at Kestrel. He was staring into the candle flames, and the light moved across his face in a way that made her think of water over something submerged. He had been quieter each day since Ashfall, quieter even than grief accounted for, and she knew why. She had known since the night by the well. Every mile they traveled, the stolen memories in him stirred like fish beneath ice, surfacing in flashes — a temple bell, a woman's laugh, a promise spoken in a language dead three centuries. He was becoming less himself and more everyone else, and he bore it the way he bore everything, with that thief's stillness that was either courage or emptiness and had never once been both.

"They can't," Kestrel said, without looking up. "That's the whole of it. The covenant broke, and the covenant was how they touched the world. What's left of them is what leaks through the cracks." He turned his head, finally, and looked at Maren. "Your god isn't letting this happen. Your god is the last person in a burning house, calling for help through a window no one remembers is there."

"And you?" Maren said. "The priestess says you're the vessel. Says the memories are coming back to you. Are you the help?"

Kestrel smiled, and it was the market-day smile Odile had seen him wear in the valley, the one that sold comfort while keeping none for himself. "I'm the window," he said. "Maybe. Ask me again when I know what's on the other side of the glass."

* * *

She woke in the black hours before dawn to a sound she had heard only once before, in the north, in the year the valleys went quiet.

It was singing. Thin, cracked, wordless — a voice like a wire drawn tight and struck, coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls themselves. The candles in the common room had all gone out at once, and the dark was the particular dark of a place where something has just left. Odile sat up, heart slamming, and reached for the knife under her pillow out of a habit older than most kingdoms, and then stopped, because she understood what the sound was.

A prayer. A god's prayer. Vell, under the ice of his own spring, calling up through three hundred years of silence to whatever remained of the family that had made him — and there was almost nothing left to call to, and he knew it, and he sang anyway, because that was the last thing a god could do: keep faith alone.

By the time she got her boots on, Kestrel was already in the doorway, and Sef was already running barefoot through the snow toward the chapel, and Odile followed them both into a cold that had teeth now, actual teeth, a cold that bit down on exposed skin and did not let go.

Inside the chapel the air was wrong. It pressed. The candle stubs along the walls burned with flames that bent sideways, all of them, uniformly, toward the spring-well at the altar's heart — as if the fire itself were leaning in to listen. And the well — the water that had never frozen — was sealed under a lid of ice so clear it might have been glass, and beneath the ice, light moved. Faint, blue-white, pulsing slow as a failing heart.

"It's him," Sef whispered. She knelt at the well's edge, palms flat on the ice. "He's — oh, he's so *small*. He was a river, he was a whole watershed, and now—" Her voice broke. Under her hands the light pulsed once, twice, and Odile saw the shape of it then, dim beneath the frozen surface: not a figure, not a face, just a brightness trying to hold the memory of its own outline. "He's been praying. All night. To no one. Because there was no one—"

"There's me," Kestrel said.

He stepped forward, and Odile saw it happen — saw the light beneath the ice turn toward him the way a flower turns toward sun, saw the pulse quicken, strengthen, the blue-white glow climbing the well shaft like dawn climbing a mountain. The god had found a listener. After three centuries, the god had found *him*, and the joy of it was terrible to watch, because joy that bright, in something that frail, was indistinguishable from the last flare of a lamp running dry.

"Vell," Kestrel said softly, kneeling beside Sef. "I hear you. I'm here. Tell me—"

The ice sang.

Not cracked — sang, a high pure note, and the light beneath it flared white, and Odile felt the prayer arrive in her own chest like a hand pressing down: *warm them, warm them, I was supposed to keep them warm, tell them I tried, tell them I kept the spring open every winter, every winter, tell them—*

And then the light went out.

Not gradually. Not fading. It went out the way a candle goes out when pinched — deliberately, completely, mid-word. The song cut off. The pressure lifted from Odile's chest so suddenly she swayed. And the cold came in.

It came in through the hole. She had told Maren the truth about that and had not understood, until this moment, how literally true it was. The cold poured into the space where Vell had been like water into a broken jar, and it did not come from outside. The chapel doors were shut. The windows were shuttered. The cold came from *inside* — rising out of the floor, breathing up through the stones, the absence of the god drawing winter after it the way a wound draws infection. Frost bloomed across the walls in ferning patterns. The candle flames shrank to blue points and died. And in the dark, from the village around them, Odile began to hear screaming.

They ran out into streets where frost was climbing the houses from the foundations up, sheathing timber and thatch in ice that thickened as they watched. People stumbled from doorways wrapped in blankets, breath pluming, eyes wild. An old man beat at his own front door, which had frozen shut from within. A child stood in the middle of the lane, perfectly calm, watching her own breath freeze into crystals that fell rather than rose, glittering down around her small boots like snow falling from her mouth. Maren pushed through the crowd, saw Sef, saw the dead well behind them through the chapel's open door, and Odile watched understanding arrive on the woman's face the way frost arrives — slowly, then all at once.

"No," Maren said. "No, he wouldn't. He *kept* us. Three hundred years, he kept the spring open—"

"He starved," Sef said. Her voice was wrecked, gentle, merciless. "He starved for three hundred years and spent the last of himself on one more winter of keeping your water moving. He died praying for you. That's what a god's death is. It's not punishment. It's just — the end of a very long act of love."

The cold deepened. By full morning, seven villagers were dead — frozen in their beds, in their chairs, one at his loom with the shuttle still in his hand, all of them from the inside out, their bodies stiff with a frost that had started in the heart. The well water froze to the bottom. The trees split with reports like gunfire as their sap expanded. And Odile stood in the chapel among the dead candles and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade being drawn, that she could not wait any longer.

* * *

She told them at noon, in Maren's kitchen, over a fire built from the pews of the dead god's chapel, because there was nothing else left to burn and the dead do not begrudge the living warmth.

She told them all of it. Not the version she had given on the road — the partial confession, the debt to Kestrel's grandmother, the theft of the god-memories. The whole of it. How the covenant-stone had been shattered not in war but in bargain; how Veyran had been mortal once, a king who wanted what kings want, which is forever, and had found in the covenant's architecture a way to take divinity piecemeal, god by god, feeding on worship rerouted through fear. How her grandmother's generation — no, she corrected herself, Kestrel's grandmother's generation, hers too, they had all been young together in the service of the old temples — had chosen sealing over surrender, hiding the divine half of the Oath inside mortal vessels rather than let Veyran digest it. How the vessels had failed, one by one, across three centuries, until one remained.

How the Oath could be reforged. She made herself say it plainly, looking at Kestrel, because he deserved plainness and had rarely gotten it from anyone.

"The stone is a lock with two keys," she said. "The divine half — the memories, the stolen years, everything we're carrying back to it. That part is nearly gathered. But a lock is not a door. Something has to stand in the doorway and hold it open while the two halves fuse. A living hinge. A vessel." She took a breath. "A mortal has to hold the Oath. Take it into themselves, all of it, the whole weight of the covenant — every god's claim, every mortal's obligation, the entire treaty between heaven and earth — and carry it, permanently, in a body, in a soul. That's what an oath-bearer is. That's what your grandmother was training you to be, whether you ever knew it or not."

Kestrel sat very still. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves with a sound like fingers.

"And the cost," he said. His voice was level. Thief's level. "There's a cost. There's always a cost. What is it?"

Odile had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it by the fire, on the road, in the dark hours when the confession climbed her throat. None of the rehearsals survived contact with his eyes.

"The Oath doesn't fit inside a person alongside anything else," she said. "It's not a memory among memories. It's the frame that holds all memory — theirs and ours, the whole architecture of promises. For it to live in you, there can be nothing else in the house." Her voice did not quite steady, but it held. "You would forget everything, Kestrel. Not set aside. Not sleep. Surrender. Your name. Your sister. The valley. Every stolen life you carry, yes — but also your own. The boy who stole a soldier's burning city on market day. The man who crossed the border. All of it, given up, so that the covenant has somewhere clean to live." She spread her hands, empty. "You would save the world and be erased from it. No one would remember you, because the Oath takes the memory of you along with your memory of everything else. You'd stand in the spring sunlight afterward, and Sef would look at you and see a stranger."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the frozen village a beam settled under the weight of ice.

Sef had gone white. "There has to be another way. There's always — Odile, you of all people, you don't get to say *there's no other way*, you helped build this cage—"

"I did." Odile did not flinch from it. "And I have spent three hundred years telling myself the seal was mercy. It wasn't. It was fear wearing mercy's clothes, and the interest on that fear has compounded into this—" she gestured at the frosted window, the dead village, the hole in the world where a river god used to be "—this winter. So no, I don't get to say it. But I'm saying it anyway, because I have read the ritual texts, and I have checked the arithmetic, and there is no other way. Only cheaper lies."

Kestrel looked into the fire for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that they had to lean toward him, three people huddled toward a small flame in a large cold room, which was, Odile thought, a fair image of everything.

"In the valley," he said, "I sold memories. That was the trade. Someone couldn't live with what they carried, and I took it, and gave them back an empty room to start over in. I used to think I understood what I was taking. Grief, mostly. Guilt. Things with shapes." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if weighing something invisible. "But I never took a whole person. I never took the *name*. And I think—" he paused, and for a moment the thief's stillness cracked, and she saw underneath it the thing she had suspected for weeks: not emptiness. Terror. Ordinary, human terror, worn like a coat. "I think I understand the widow now. The one who bought the soldier's burning city. I thought she was buying peace. She was buying a smaller self to survive inside, because the big one was killing her." He closed his hands. "You're asking me to make myself small enough to hold the world."

"I'm asking you to decide," Odile said. "Only that. Decide. Not today. Not tonight. But soon, and knowing fully, because the ritual does not forgive hesitation halfway through, and neither do I forgive myself if you walk into it not understanding what you're paying."

"And if he refuses?" Sef asked, sharp with grief. "If he says no? Then what, Odile? We let the winter finish eating the world?"

"Then we let the winter finish eating the world," Odile said, "and I will help him refuse, and I will carry that too. I've had practice carrying things." She looked at Kestrel, and let him see all of it — the guilt, the debt, the three hundred years of compounding interest. "I will not choose for you. I did that once, with your grandmother, and look what it grew."

Kestrel nodded slowly. Then he stood, and went to the frosted window, and wiped a circle clear with his sleeve, and looked out at the frozen village — at the child in the lane, still standing where she had been at dawn, still breathing her small impossible snowflakes, waiting for a world that would thaw or wouldn't.

"Vell prayed all night," he said, "to no one. And the last thing he asked was for someone to tell the village he tried." He was quiet a moment. "Someone should tell them. And someone should write down what he was, before the name goes — the real name, the river-name. Sef, do you know it?"

"A little of it," Sef whispered.

"Then write down the little. All of it matters now. Every name." He turned back to them, and the thief's smile came, but changed, tempered, like metal that had been through a fire and cooled into something harder. "Because I need to know exactly what a name is before I decide whether to give mine away."

He left the kitchen then, and they heard his boots on the frozen lane, going door to door, telling the living about their god's last night — *he tried, he kept the spring open, he loved you* — and Odile sat by the fire of the dead god's pews and felt the dread in her chest finally take its true shape.

Because there was one thing she had not told him. One line from the ritual texts, one clause she had swallowed, and it sat in her now like swallowed ice.

The vessel must surrender all personal memory — yes. But the texts were specific about *how*: the surrender had to be complete before the fusing, and it could not be taken. It had to be *given*, freely, by one who understood — and the giving began the moment the choice was truly made, not at the stone, not at the solstice, but at once, immediately, a thread unraveling from the moment the weaver consented. Every hour Kestrel spent deciding was an hour he still owned. But the moment he decided —

The moment he decided, he would begin to forget. On the road, in the snow, mid-sentence, mid-embrace. His sister's face first, probably. Names first. They always went first.

Outside, Kestrel knocked on the next door, and Odile bowed her head over the fire, and did not tell him yet.
