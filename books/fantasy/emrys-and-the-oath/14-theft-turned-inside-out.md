## 14. Theft Turned Inside Out

The first god-memory Kestrel returned was small — a wren of a thing, a river-spirit's recollection of the first frost it had ever painted on a millpond, three hundred years ago and more.

He had carried the vials for weeks now, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs like a second heart. Grandmother's handwriting labeled each one in her precise, slanted script: *Nethys of the Ford — joy of running water.* He had expected the giving-back to feel like relief. Instead, when he uncorked the vial at the ruined ford where Nethys still clung to existence like moss to a wet stone, the memory left him the way blood leaves a body — warmly at first, then with a coldness that climbed his arms and settled behind his eyes.

He did not notice it immediately. That was the trick of it. Loss, he had learned long ago in the Valley of Hollow, never announces itself. It simply subtracts, quietly, and lets you discover the subtraction later, when you reach for something and find your hand closing on air.

Nethys drank the memory from his cupped hands like a starving bird. The little god brightened — color flooding back into what had been a gray smear of divinity, scales gleaming along its eel-thin body, eyes going from dull riverstone to living amber. It sang, one high silver note, and the dead ford shivered: ice crystals bloomed across the shallows in patterns no frost had made since before the valley was sealed.

"Thank you," Nethys said, in a voice like water over pebbles. "Oh, thank you, vessel. Thank you."

*Vessel.* The word should have warned him.

He walked back toward the village where Sef waited, and it was only halfway there, stopping to answer Odile's question about how many vials remained, that he found he could not remember the name of the first town he'd ever worked as a thief. He knew he had worked towns. He knew there had been a market, a fence, a first stolen memory sold back to a weeping man who wanted to forget his brother's face. But the name of the place — gone. Not hidden. Gone, the way a tooth is gone from the gum: a smooth absence that the tongue keeps returning to.

He told himself it was tiredness. He was very good at telling himself things.

* * *

By the fourth restitution, Sef had noticed.

They were working north along the river road, following the labels in Grandmother's hand like a scavenger trail — each vial matched to a shrine, a well, a hollow tree where some minor god had once kept house. The winter deepened around them with every mile, wrong and hungry, snow falling upward in the passes some mornings, rivers freezing mid-current so that fish hung suspended like ornaments in glass. The gods they woke were grateful and terrible in equal measure, and Kestrel poured their memories into them one by one, and one by one he felt himself thinning.

It was Sef who caught him at it — literally caught him, arm hooked under his shoulders as he swayed on the steps of a roadside shrine, the empty vial still dangling from his fingers.

"You're white," she said. "Kestrel. Look at me. You're white as the snow."

"I'm fine." The lie came easily; all his lies came easily these days, because lying required a self to do the lying, and his self was becoming a rumor. "It's the cold."

"It is not the cold." She took his face in both hands, and her palms were warm — impossibly warm, warm with the stolen years she'd been fed without her consent, years that had kept her alive and young while three centuries passed outside the valley. Her thumb traced the hollow under his eye. "When did you last sleep? When did you last eat? What did you eat?"

He opened his mouth to answer and discovered, with a lurch of vertigo, that he could not remember. Not the meal — the *taste*. He remembered that food existed, that he liked bread, that honey existed as a concept. But the taste of honey, the gold stickiness of it on the tongue — that was somewhere else now. Poured into a god. Given away.

"Sef." His voice came out strange. Small. "What does honey taste like?"

She went very still. Then she sat him down on the shrine's frozen step, built a fire with hands that shook only slightly, and made him tell her everything — every vial, every giving, every small subtraction he had filed away as tiredness or cold or the road wearing at him.

"The millpond spirit," he said. "And the ford. And the two brothers of the birch grove — those went together, they wouldn't separate. And the old woman of the well, though she wasn't really an old woman, that was just the shape she wore—"

"Stop." Sef pressed her fingers to her temples. "Kestrel, that's seven. Seven memories, each one decades long, centuries long, lived experiences of actual divine beings — and you've been carrying them since you were a *child*. Since Grandmother hid them in you. You weren't storing them, you were *being* them. They've been woven into you so long you can't tell which threads are yours."

"They're not mine," he said. "That's the point. I'm giving them back. That's the whole—"

"That's not how containers work!" She was shouting now, and he realized distantly that she had been crying, that her tears were freezing on her cheeks in tiny bright beads. "You think a wineskin pours out wine and stays exactly as it was? You think a mold comes away unchanged? You have spent your whole life inside those memories, Kestrel. They shaped you. Every one you pour out takes the shape of itself with it!"

Odile crouched by the fire, feeding it sticks, saying nothing. Her silence had a texture he had learned to read: this was the silence of someone who already knew.

"You knew," Kestrel said to her.

Odile didn't look up. "I suspected. I helped your grandmother hide them. I knew she needed a vessel that would hold divine memory without burning out — a mortal born under the covenant, with the Oath's old blood in him. I did not know what the pouring-out would cost. But I feared it would cost something." She met his eyes at last, and her face was older than he had ever seen it, older than her stolen service to Veyran should have allowed. "Everything costs, Kestrel. I told you that the night you dreamed the child's prayer. Things that answer prayers also collect debts. You are answering three hundred years of prayers. The debt is being collected."

* * *

He should have stopped. Any reasonable person would have stopped.

But that evening, as they made camp in the lee of a fallen waystone, a fox came down out of the snow — except it was not a fox, or not only a fox, and it sat at the edge of the firelight with its too-many-jointed legs folded beneath it and looked at Kestrel with eyes like struck flint, and it said, in a voice like dry leaves: *"Please. I have been nothing for so long. I remember being a guardian of crossroads. I remember children leaving bread. Please, if you carry what I think you carry—"*

And Kestrel, who had spent his life selling memories to people desperate enough to pay anything, looked into the hollow dark of a dying god's eyes and recognized the desperation as his own stock-in-trade, and reached for the oilcloth against his ribs.

Sef grabbed his wrist. "No."

"Sef—"

"*No.*" Her grip was stronger than it should have been, strong with borrowed years. "One more day. One day to rest, to eat, to let whatever's left of you knit back together. One day, and then if you must—"

"If I must?" He laughed, and the laugh frightened him, because it sounded thin, like paper tearing. "Sef, look at it. Look at what's left of them. The winter is eating them alive. Every day I wait, another shrine goes dark, another village freezes from the inside. I held the memories. It's mine to give back. That's not heroism, it's just — returning stolen goods. I'm a thief, Sef. It's the one honest thing I know how to be."

"You don't even know if it will work!" She was on her feet now, firelight jumping across her face. "You don't know that reforging the Oath saves anyone. You don't know that giving yourself away piece by piece buys anything but an empty vial with your name on it! Grandmother wrote your name on that slot, Kestrel. Your name. She knew. Some part of her always knew what you'd have to become, and she hid you anyway, and I will not stand here and watch you walk into her fear with open arms!"

The words landed like blows, and worse — like *familiar* blows, and he understood suddenly that she had said versions of them before, long ago, in a temple, in a life he could not reach. He saw it flicker across her own face a heartbeat later: the horror of having argued this argument before, with a brother who had then been someone else entirely.

The fox-thing waited patiently at the edge of the light, and its patience was the most terrible thing Kestrel had ever seen. Gods, he was learning, did not beg twice. Begging was for creatures with time.

"One day," he said.

Sef searched his face for the lie and, finding none — finding, perhaps, something worse than a lie, something true and immovable — let go of his wrist.

* * *

He used the day the way he had once taught himself to use quiet hours between thefts: taking inventory.

He sat apart from the others, in the snow, and made lists in his head. Things he knew about himself. His name — Kestrel Ishikawa, though *Ishikawa* felt like a coat borrowed from a stranger, and lately even *Kestrel* echoed oddly, like a shout in an emptying hall. His trade. His valley. His grandmother's kitchen, the smell of cedar smoke, the locked room he had broken open. Odile's dry voice. Sef's laugh, which he had known for mere weeks and which had somehow become load-bearing, structural, part of the architecture of him.

And underneath the list, gaps. Smooth absences. The taste of honey. The name of his first town. The color of his mother's — no. He had never known his mother. Had he? He reached for the fact and found the fact itself uncertain, a door painted on a wall. Somewhere in the pouring-out, he had lost not just memories but the *certainty* of memories, the scaffolding that told him which losses were wounds and which were simply the shape of an ordinary life.

He was becoming, he realized, exactly what he had sold to others for coin: a person with holes where a history should be. A customer. The thought almost made him laugh again, that thin paper-tearing sound.

At dusk, Sef came and sat beside him without speaking. For a long while they watched the light die over the white country.

"When we were children," she said finally, "in the temple — you used to steal things. Little things. Candied ginger, a copper whistle, once an entire ceremonial knife that you buried in the garden because it scared you. And when Mother caught you, you never denied it. You'd just say, *it wanted to come with me.*" She smiled, and the smile hurt to look at. "I used to think it was an excuse. Now I think it was the truest thing about you. Things want to come with you, Kestrel. Memories, gods, people. You're a vessel. It's what you're *for*. And I hate it, because I know what vessels are for after they've been poured out."

"What?" he asked, though he knew.

"Breaking," she said. "Or keeping. One of the two."

* * *

The fox-god got its memory the next morning, because Kestrel had never in his life broken a promise to a desperate creature, and because — he admitted it now, honestly, in the privacy of his thinning skull — because the giving had begun to feel like the only thing he was still certain of. Each vial emptied was a debt paid. Each debt paid was a step toward the covenant-stone and the shattered Oath and whatever waited for him there. If the road consumed him as he walked it, then the road's price was simply the price, and he had spent his whole life charging other people prices for forgetting. It was fitting, in a grim and symmetrical way, that he should now pay for remembering.

This memory was large — a crossroads-god's long stewardship, generations of travelers blessed and turned aside from harm, centuries of small kindnesses stacked like stones. It poured out of him slowly, and he knelt in the snow and held the vial until his arms ached, and the fox-thing grew and grew, shedding its fox-shape, becoming a tall figure in a ragged cloak whose face was four roads meeting.

And Kestrel gave, and gave, and gave —

— and when it was done, he stood up, and the world tilted, and Sef caught him, and he looked at her face and felt the bottom drop out of his stomach.

Because for one full, endless second, he did not know her.

Her features were there — the dark eyes, the stubborn mouth, the frost-beads in her hair — and he could see that she loved him, that she was terrified for him, that she had crossed three hundred years to stand in this snow holding him up. All of that was legible, written plainly in her face. But the *her* of it, the sisterhood, the temple, the childhood, the weight of shared history that turns a stranger's face into home — that was gone. Poured out. Spent on a crossroads god who thanked him in a voice like four winds and vanished into the morning.

Then it came back — Sef herself flooding in, memory reasserting, the scaffolding groaning back into place — but the second had happened. The second had *been*, and they both knew it.

"Don't," Sef whispered. Her composure had finally cracked clean through, and she held his face the way you hold something you are watching break. "Kestrel, please. Please don't. There are eleven vials left. Eleven. At this rate there won't be enough of you left to speak the Oath at all — you'll reach the stone and there'll be nobody home to offer it. Veyran won't even need to fight you. You'll have erased yourself for free, and he'll take the covenant off your corpse and call it mercy."

"He'll have to catch me first," Kestrel said.

It was meant to be light. It came out flat and strange, and he heard in his own voice how much of him had already gone — how much of the sardonic, careful, hollow-valley thief was simply no longer in residence. What remained was quieter. More deliberate. A vessel, filling with purpose as it emptied of everything else.

Behind them, far down the white road, a horn sounded — long, low, wrong, a note that made the snow shiver sideways. Odile was on her feet instantly, shading her eyes, and Kestrel watched her expression change from vigilance to recognition to something he had never seen on her face before.

Fear.

"Riders," she said. "Black banners. They've found us." She turned, and her eyes went to the eleven vials against Kestrel's ribs, and then to his face, and he understood that she was doing arithmetic — the same arithmetic Sef had done, the same arithmetic he was doing — weighing what remained of him against what was coming down the road.

"How long?" Kestrel asked.

"An hour," Odile said. "Perhaps less. The snow favors them."

Sef's hand found his, gripping hard. Eleven vials. One hour. And somewhere ahead, through the deepening winter, the covenant-stone waited — patient as a grave, hungry as a god — for whatever was left of him to arrive.
