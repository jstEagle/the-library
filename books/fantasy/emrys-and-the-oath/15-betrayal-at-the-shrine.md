## 15. Betrayal at the Shrine

The shrine of the river god stood where the road bent away from the water, a low stone hollow roofed with slates gone green as pond-skin, and it was the first holy place Kestrel had ever entered that did not try to take something from him.

That alone should have warned him.

He had grown used to the small gods by now — their hunger dressed up as hospitality, their blessings that clung like burrs. But the river god's shrine was nearly empty. Whatever lingered there was old and thin and too tired to want anything except what Sef had been giving it for weeks: her voice, her prayers, the little offerings she set out each dawn. A cup of melted snow. A ribbon from her sleeve. Once, memorably, an argument, delivered at length, about whether mercy was a form of pride.

Kestrel had come to love this place in the way you love a room where someone you love has slept.

He sat on the cold step with his back against the pillar and watched Sef work. She had laid out the recovered memories on a cloth of undyed wool — forty-one vials now, glass catching the grey light, each one holding a swirl of color that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. God-memories. The stolen years. His grandmother's theft, carried three hundred miles in a pack on his back, returned one by one to the shrines and wells and standing stones where they belonged.

Each return had cost him. He'd stopped pretending otherwise after the twelfth. Something went out of him with every vial — not memory exactly, not yet, but the *scaffolding* of memory, the beams and joists that held a self upright. He woke some mornings unsure of his own age. He had called Odile by his grandmother's name twice before he caught himself.

Sef noticed. She always noticed. She said nothing, which was its own kind of noticing.

"Forty-two," she said now, lifting a vial to the light. This one held something dark and slow, like ink dropped in oil. "The river god's own. I can feel him leaning toward it already. Like a dog hearing its name." She smiled — the crooked smile, the one that had survived three hundred years of stolen time without losing its shape. "You know what he told me last week? Through the water? He said he'd forgotten the taste of rain. Can you imagine. A river god who forgot rain."

"He should talk to me," Kestrel said. "I'm becoming an expert."

It was meant as a joke. It came out flat, and Sef heard the flatness, because she set the vial down and crossed the shrine and sat beside him on the step, shoulder to shoulder, the way they must have sat as children in a life he could not remember.

"Show me your hands," she said.

"Why?"

"Because you've been hiding them in your sleeves all morning, and because I'm your sister, and because I said so."

He showed her. His hands were steady. That was the strange part — everything else trembled, names and dates and the shape of his own face in still water, but his hands had never been steadier. Thief's hands. They knew how to hold things even when the rest of him was coming apart.

"They're fine," he said.

"Liar." But she said it gently, and she took one of his hands anyway, and turned it over, and traced the lines of it with one finger like she was reading a map of somewhere she'd been long ago. "When we were small — before, I mean, in the true before — you used to steal things and give them back. Did I tell you that? You'd lift a fruit off a cart and then feel so bad about it you'd leave coins. You were the worst thief in the temple district. Everyone loved you for it."

"You've told me."

"I'll keep telling you. Someone has to hold the parts of you that leak." Her finger paused on his palm. "Kestrel. When the last vial is given back —"

"Don't."

"— there may not be much left to give the Oath. You understand that. Odile thinks —"

"Odile thinks a great many things," Kestrel said, "and half of them are apologies."

Sef laughed despite herself, a short bright sound that the thin old god in the stones seemed to lean toward too. And for a moment — just a moment — the winter light through the shrine door went gold instead of grey, and Kestrel felt something vast and weary turn its attention toward them from under the earth, from under the ice, the way a sleeper turns toward a window.

Then the light went grey again, and Sef stiffened.

"What is it?" Kestrel asked.

"Someone's coming up the road." She was already moving, gathering vials into the cloth with hands that had learned speed in centuries he hadn't lived. "Two. No — four. Walking wrong. Too even, like soldiers trying not to march."

Kestrel rose. His thief's hands went, without instruction, to the knife at his belt — a habit so deep it predated his memories, which frightened him more than the footsteps did.

Through the shrine door he saw them come around the bend: four figures in travel-stained grey, hoods back, faces ordinary as bread. Hunters. He'd seen Veyran's hunters before, outside Ashfall, and these had the same quality — the same terrible unremarkableness, as if someone had sanded the personality off them. Behind them, unhurried, came a fifth.

A woman.

She wore a plain brown cloak, and her hair was bound back, and she walked like someone arriving rather than attacking, and Kestrel's whole body went cold before his mind caught up — because he knew her. He had watched her die at the valley border. He had knelt beside her in the snow while the words tore themselves out of her mouth mid-sentence, her eyes going empty as a snuffed lamp, and he had buried her effects — the oath-token, the crest, the proof of a life he'd never been told about — in a drawer he still hadn't opened again.

"Stay behind me," he said to Sef.

"I'm three hundred years old," Sef said. "I stayed behind people when you were learning to walk. I'm not doing it now."

The woman stopped ten paces from the shrine door. The four hunters fanned out with the smooth economy of men who had done this many times, cutting off the sides of the building, though none of them drew steel. They didn't need to. Kestrel understood that with a thief's instinct: they were not here to fight. They were here because the fighting was already over and no one had told him yet.

"You're alive," Kestrel said.

"Yes," the woman said.

"I buried you."

"You buried my effects. What was left of me." She tilted her head, and there was something in the gesture that snagged at him, some familiar angle, like a melody heard through a wall. "Veyran keeps what he catches. Even broken things. Especially broken things."

"Sef," Kestrel said quietly, without turning. "The vials."

"Already moving."

The woman's eyes flicked past him, tracking the motion, and for an instant something moved behind her face — something quick and hungry and *sad* — and then it was smoothed away, sanded down, filed into that ordinary bread-loaf calm.

"Give them to me, Kestrel Ishikawa," she said. "And the Oath will pass you by. Veyran swears it. You can go back to your valley. It's open now, you know. The wards are failing everywhere. You could grow onions. Live small. Die old."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take them," she said, "and I take her too, and you learn what Veyran does with the sister of an oath-bearer. He's wanted a priestess of the river god for a long time. They pray so *loudly*."

Kestrel felt the fear arrive, cold and complete, and he let it sit in him the way he'd let stolen memories sit in him for years — acknowledged, catalogued, not obeyed. He looked at the woman properly then. At the way her weight rested slightly on her left leg. At the small scar on her jaw. At the way her right hand kept curling, very slightly, as if around the ghost of something it used to hold.

"You knew me," he said. "Before. In chapter one of whatever story this is. You're the one who came to the valley looking for me. You died saying my childhood name."

Something happened to her face.

It was not a flinch. It was larger than a flinch — a structural event, like watching a crack run through plaster. For one heartbeat the sanding came off, and underneath it Kestrel saw — *recognized* — a face he had never seen but somehow knew the way you know a room you dreamed once. Younger. Laughing. Sitting across from him at a table he couldn't picture, in a city that had burned or hadn't, saying words he couldn't hear:

*I swear it, Ren. Whatever they make you, whatever they take — I'll find you. I'll find every piece.*

The vision broke. The woman's hand had gone to her temple, pressing hard, and her breath came out ragged, and one of the hunters shifted forward and she waved him off with a sharpness that was almost human.

"Ren," Kestrel said slowly. "Is that what I was called?"

"Don't." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Don't say it like you mean it. You don't mean it. You don't *remember* it. You're reading it off my face like one of your stolen goods, and it isn't yours anymore, and neither am I, so stop *looking* at me like —"

She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again the bread-calm was back, but thinner, worn through at one knee.

"My name was Wren," she said. "Before. We grew up together in the temple district. You stole fruit and left coins and I covered for you with the priests, and when the covenant broke and Veyran came for the oath-bearer's line, your grandmother hid you and I —" her jaw worked — "I stayed. I looked. Three hundred years, Kestrel, I looked for you, and I found you, and I got one sentence out at your border before he pulled me apart and put me back together as *this*." She spread her arms, presenting herself: the grey cloak, the four hunters, the hollowed eyes. "So yes. I knew you. Past tense. Whatever's left of me belongs to the god-king now, and the god-king wants the vials."

"Wren." Sef's voice, from inside the shrine, low and stricken. "Little Wren. You used to braid your hair with river-glass."

"Don't," Wren said again, but her voice shook, and Kestrel saw it then, the whole shape of it, the trap within the trap: Veyran had not sent a weapon. He had sent a wound wearing a weapon's clothes. Every word out of her cost her something; every word cost *them* something; and the god-king, wherever he sat in his palace of tokens and forgetting, was counting both.

"The vials," Wren said, and now the hollow tone crept back in, imposed from outside, a hand closing over her voice like a puppeteer's. "Last offer. Give them up."

"No," Kestrel said.

Wren sighed. It was the saddest sound he had ever heard.

"So be it," she said — and snapped her fingers.

The four hunters moved at once, and they did not move like men. They moved like the space between heartbeats, and Kestrel had time for exactly one thought — *they're not here to fight, they're here because the fighting is over* — before the world went sideways.

Not darkness. Worse. *Absence.* One moment the shrine door was in front of him; the next it simply wasn't, along with the pillar, the step, the bend of road, as if a page had been cut cleanly out of the morning. He staggered, blind, reaching for Sef —

— and his hand closed on cloth, and then on nothing, and then the world snapped back and he was on his knees outside the shrine and the hunters were walking away up the road, unhurried, and between them, bound in grey rope that seemed to drink the light, walked Sef.

She was not struggling. She was looking back at him over her shoulder, and her expression was not fear. It was fury — clean, ancient, three-hundred-years-deep fury — and beneath the fury, aimed at him with terrible precision, one message, mouthed clearly enough to read:

*Run. Don't follow. RUN.*

Then the cut-out piece of the world closed over them like water, and the road was empty, and the wind came down off the mountain smelling of nothing at all.

Kestrel ran anyway. Of course he ran. He ran until the road split and split again and gave him nothing, until his lungs burned with air that tasted of ash and early winter, until he doubled back to the shrine and found it exactly as they'd left it — the cloth, the cup of melted snow, the ribbon from Sef's sleeve — everything except the forty-two vials, and the woman who had waited three hundred years to find him.

Everything except his sister.

He stood in the doorway shaking. Not with grief yet. Grief would come; grief always came; grief was patient as a creditor. Right now it was something colder and more useful: the thief's clarity, the market-day calm, the part of him that counted exits and weighed purses even while the world burned.

They had taken the memories. They had taken Sef. They had left him alive, untouched, unrobbed — and that was the detail that made his blood run thin, because thieves do not leave the purse behind unless the purse itself is what they want.

*Come to Ashfall,* the empty road said, in Veyran's courteous voice. *Bring yourself. Bring the Oath. We have your sister.*

Behind him, in the shrine, the thin old god stirred in his stones. Kestrel felt it — a pressure, a lean, a voice like water over gravel, faint and cracked and unmistakably urgent:

*Boy. Boy, listen. There is a way into the palace that even Veyran has forgotten. My brother keeps it. Come to the river's source, and bring me my name —*

The voice frayed, thinned, guttered like a lamp out of oil.

*— and hurry. Winter is coming down off the mountains like a wolf, and it is following you.*
