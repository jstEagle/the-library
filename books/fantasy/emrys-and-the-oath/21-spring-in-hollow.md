## 21. Spring in Hollow

The road down into the Valley of Hollow was the same road that had led out of it, which was the first strange thing the nameless man noticed about coming home.

He did not think of it as home. He thought of very little as anything in particular these days; his mind was a house with all its furniture carried out, rooms swept bare and waiting, and he had learned — slowly, the way one learns to walk on a leg that has healed crooked — to be at peace with the emptiness. But when the road bent at the shoulder of the mountain and the valley opened below them, green and gold in the late light of a true spring, something in his chest turned over like a key in a lock he could not see.

"There it is," Sef said beside him.

She said it lightly, but her hand found his sleeve, and he understood that for her the moment was heavier than words. She had not seen this place since she was a child. She had been carried out of it — or out of what it had been — before the wards went up, before their grandmother sealed three hundred years of silence over the valley like a lid over a well. She remembered a home here. He remembered nothing, and yet his body remembered the shape of the road: the way it narrowed where the rock overhung, the place where the stream crossed beneath it and you could hear water through the stones under your boots. His feet knew the pace of the descent. He let them lead.

The winter's ruin was everywhere and nowhere. On the high passes they had walked through country still scarred by the long cold — black stands of trees, villages with their roofs caved under snow-weight, shrines newly swept clean and hung with ribbons of every color, because the small gods were awake again and hungry for company. But here in the valley the thaw had come early and thoroughly, as if the land itself were embarrassed by what it had endured and was hurrying to make amends. Meadows ran green to the river. Orchards stood in full white blossom, so heavy with flowers that the branches looked burdened rather than adorned. Bees worked the air in a low, contented hum.

"It shouldn't be this far along," Sef murmured. "Not this far north."

"Maybe it's making up for lost time," he said.

She glanced at him — that glance she gave him often, half tenderness and half arithmetic, weighing how much of him was present in any given sentence. He had stopped minding it. It was, he had come to understand, simply how she loved people: carefully, counting what was there against what should have been.

They came down through the orchards as the sun lowered, and the first person to see them was a boy up a ladder with a picking basket, who shouted something into the blossoms and slid down the ladder's rails and ran ahead of them into town, shouting still. By the time they reached the market square, word had outrun them, and a loose crowd had gathered the way crowds do in small places — not hostile, not exactly welcoming, but hungry for the sight of anything new.

He felt their eyes find him and slide off. That was the way of it now, everywhere. People looked at Sef and saw a priestess of the river god, travel-worn and serious, with frost-colored eyes that made old women touch their charms. People looked at him and saw — nothing much. A tall man with a thief's easy shoulders and an unremarkable face, standing slightly behind someone more interesting. Their gazes touched him and found no purchase, like rain on oiled cloth. Even the boy who had run ahead, breathless and pointing, pointed mostly at Sef.

It did not wound the way he had expected it to. In the first weeks after the reforging, being unremembered had been a kind of vertigo — every stranger's indifference a small proof that he had traded away whatever it was that made people hold onto you. He had lain awake in wayside inns turning the loss over like a stone with no warm side. But somewhere on the road, walking day after day through a world waking from three hundred years of winter, the vertigo had gentled into something else. A freedom, almost. He was no one's debt and no one's grief and no one's ghost. He was only what he did next.

That was the lesson the Oath had left in him, or perhaps the lesson he had carved into himself to fill the space the Oath had taken. *Identity is not what you remember,* Sef had said once, weeping, holding his blank face in both her hands. *It's what you do next.* He held onto that the way other men held onto names.

* * *

They took a room at the inn on the square, and the innkeeper — a broad, flour-dusted woman named Marta, who chattered the way springs run, constantly and without malice — asked their business as she poured ale.

"Pilgrimage," Sef said. "Of a kind."

"Well, you've picked a season for it." Marta set the jug down and leaned on the table, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman about to share news rather than secrets. "Whole valley's changed since the thaw. You'll have heard, maybe, out on the roads? The wards are gone. Just — gone, one morning, like frost off a window. And things come in now. Peddlers from over the pass, first time in living memory. A chaplain of the small gods set up in the old millhouse. And prayers—" she crossed herself, an old gesture from some faith even older than the valley's silence, "—prayers work again. My sister prayed for rain for her beans and got rain that very night. Only rain, mind. Not a flood. That's how you know it's the real gods back and not something wearing their faces."

Sef smiled the careful smile she kept for civilians. "And how do folk feel about it? The change?"

Marta shrugged, wiping the table though it was clean. "Some are frightened. Some are pleased. Most are tired of talking about it." She straightened, and her eyes passed over the nameless man with the brief, unseeing courtesy people always gave him, then caught — just for a heartbeat — and returned. "You know," she said slowly, "you look like somebody. I can't think who. Somebody I've sold bread to, maybe. Or bread I've bought. Isn't that odd?"

"Very odd," he agreed pleasantly, and her attention slid off him again like water, and she went to see to another table, and Sef looked at him across the rim of her cup with her eyebrows raised in silent question. He shook his head slightly. *Let it go.* Some doors were better left closed until he chose to open them himself.

But that night, lying in the narrow bed with the window open to the smell of blossom, he dreamed — not a memory, for he had none, but something adjacent to memory, the way the shape of a missing tooth is adjacent to the tongue. He dreamed of a market stall. He was behind it, and there was a weight in his pocket that clinked, and a woman across the counter was crying, and he was saying something gentle and practiced and false, and the dream had a texture of competence that felt almost like happiness, and underneath it ran a thread of emptiness fine as wire. He woke before dawn with the wire still humming in him, and lay listening to the bees beginning their rounds in the orchard, and could not decide whether the dream had been grief or homesickness, or whether those were, for a man with no past, the same thing.

* * *

In the morning they went looking for the house.

Sef had directions of a sort — fragments recovered from the memories that had come back to the world with the covenant, letters and ledger-entries and the testimony of minor gods who remembered everything and forgave selectively. Their grandmother's house, she said, had stood on the lane above the tannery, third from the corner, with a blue door. He walked beside her through streets that meant nothing to him and everything to his feet, which turned corners before he decided to, hesitated at thresholds, slowed where the cobbles dipped.

The tannery was gone — replaced by a cooper's yard smelling sweetly of fresh-shaved wood — but the lane remained, and the third house from the corner had a door that might once have been blue and was now the gray of wood that has given up arguing with weather. The windows were shuttered. Weeds stood waist-high in the dooryard, and a swallow's nest crusted the lintel.

"No one's lived here," Sef said quietly, "since she died."

"You're sure it's hers?"

"I'm sure." Her voice had gone thin. "I remember the door. I remember being carried through it. I don't remember anything else, and that's its own kind of theft, isn't it." She pressed her palm flat against the door as if taking its pulse. "Three hundred years she kept this valley shut. Kept everyone safe and everyone small. And I keep thinking — I should hate her, and I can't, because I also owe her my life, and because you—" she stopped, started again. "Because whatever she did, she did it trying to keep one particular person out of a god-king's hands. And it worked. For a while."

"For a while," he agreed.

The lock was rusted through. He could have picked it — his fingers told him so, flexing with an expertise he had no story for — but there seemed no point in subtlety with a house that had been empty longer than most kingdoms last, so he put his shoulder to the door instead, and it gave with a groan and a shower of rust, and the smell of the interior rolled out to meet them: dust, mouse, dried lavender gone to hay, and beneath it all something faintly mineral, like the air of a cave.

Inside, the house resisted him.

There was no other word for it. Not the way a locked room resists, with bolts and bars, but the way a body resists a hand pressing on a bruise — a flinching, a drawing-in. The hallway seemed narrower than hallways should be. The stairs creaked warnings rather than complaints. When he reached toward the banister, the wood was cold in a way the morning-warmed house had no right to make it, and Sef, watching him, went pale.

"You feel it too," she said. "The house remembers you."

"The house has better recall than I do, then."

"It isn't funny."

"No," he said. "It isn't. But if I don't laugh at it, Sef, I'll stand in this doorway forever waiting to feel like the man who used to live here, and I never will. So I laugh. Come on. Show me the room."

She knew which room he meant without asking. Everyone who had heard the story knew: the hidden chamber behind the pantry wall, where their grandmother had kept the stolen god-memories in vials labeled in her careful hand, row upon row, each one a god's whole life decanted like cordial. The vials were long gone — returned, burned, or scattered in the war at Ashfall — but the room remained, and the slot remained, the single empty recess cut into the shelf at eye level, and beneath it, scratched into the wood in the same careful hand, a name.

*Kestrel.*

He stood in front of it a long time. Sef hovered at the chamber's mouth, not entering; she had said once that the room felt to her like a throat, and she did not care to be swallowed. Dust hung in the light from the single high window. Somewhere in the walls, mice went about their small economies.

He had expected — he did not know what. Rage, maybe, arriving late. Grief. The hot animal injustice of a man reading his own name on a prison shelf. What he felt instead was quieter and stranger: recognition, the way one recognizes a face in a crowd years after the face's owner has died. *So this is where I was kept.* Not horror. Almost gratitude, and almost resentment, and neither one able to finish becoming itself.

"She wrote your name before she wrote the others'?" Sef asked from the doorway. "Or after?"

"After, I think. The scratch is shallower. Hesitant." He traced the letters without touching them. "She stood here a long time deciding."

"Deciding whether to hide you or use you."

"Deciding whether loving me and saving me were the same act." He heard his own voice say it and marveled mildly at its calm. "Most days they aren't. She chose, and the choosing cost her everything, and it cost me everything, and Veyran cost us both besides. I forgive her. I decided that in a cell in Ashfall, and I haven't found a reason to un-decide it."

"And yourself? Have you forgiven yourself?"

He turned. The light from the high window fell across the empty shelf, across the scratched name, across the dust motes wheeling like tiny slow planets. "For what? I don't remember doing anything."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he admitted. "It's a moat. But it's an honest one." He looked around the little chamber one last time — the shelves, the slot, the name — and felt the house's resistance ease, very slightly, like a fist unclenching by degrees. Whatever the walls had been braced against, it was not him. Perhaps it had never been him. Perhaps houses only ever guard their dead.

On the way out he paused in the pantry, where the wall's seam showed faintly if you knew to look, and his hand rose and found the catch without searching for it — a trick of wood and finger he had no memory of learning — and he laughed aloud, alone in the dusty kitchen, at the sheer stubbornness of a body that refused to forget what the soul had surrendered.

* * *

They stayed in Hollow nine days, because spring in a valley newly opened to the world is a thing that asks to be witnessed slowly.

On the second day the chaplain of the small gods — a round, cheerful woman with ash smudged on her brow from a dozen different hearth-shrines — cornered Sef outside the millhouse and talked at her for an hour about the difficulties of ministering to deities who had spent three centuries starving and had come back with appetites and manners to match. On the fourth day a peddler arrived from over the pass with silk and salt and rumors, and the rumor he carried — that the god-king Veyran had been seen in the mountains, diminished, moving from shrine to shrine like a beggar, gathering worshipers the way other men gathered firewood — made Sef go quiet for the rest of the evening, and made the nameless man feel a cold spot in his chest that had nothing to do with memory and everything to do with instinct. On the sixth day it rained, gently, the first rain he could remember — truly remember, the way you remember your own name — and he stood out in it in the dooryard of the inn with his face turned up, laughing, while Marta watched from the window and decided, visibly, that the tall stranger was either a holy fool or a simpleton, and settled comfortably on holy fool.

And on the ninth day, market day, he walked into the square and stopped in front of a stall that sold nothing but candles.

He did not know why he stopped. The stall was unremarkable — a striped awning, a trestle board, candles in ranks from thumb-length votives to altar pillars thick as his arm. The woman minding it was dozing in the sun. There was no reason at all for his feet to carry him there, except that they had carried him, the way they had carried him down the mountain road and up the stairs of a dead woman's house, following grooves worn into him by a life he no longer owned.

He stood before the candles and felt an echo move through him like a bell struck softly in another room.

A stall. A widow. A soldier's burning city lifted out of one skull and a grieving woman's past lifted out of another, and coin changing hands, and the practiced gentleness, and the wire-fine emptiness underneath. He had done that here. In this square, at some stall much like this one, he had made his living peeling souls, and he had been good at it, and being good at it had hollowed him in advance of the great hollowing, so that perhaps — he wondered at this now, turning it over like a smooth stone — perhaps the Oath had chosen him not despite the thief he'd been but because of him. Because a man who has spent his life handling other people's memories knows exactly what they weigh. Knows what it costs to carry them, and what it costs to give them back.

The candle-seller woke, saw him standing there, and smiled the smile of trade. "Looking for something, sir? Devotional tapers, blessing-candles, we've honey-wax from the south meadows—"

"How much for the smallest one?" he asked.

She named a price. He paid it — his purse held road-coins earned hauling timber on the passes, honest money, money with no history — and she wrapped the little taper in paper and wished him a blessed spring, and he walked away with the candle in his pocket feeling absurd and light, like a man carrying a letter he cannot read to an address he does not know.

Sef found him at the fountain afterward, sitting on the rim with the sun on his shoulders.

"Marta says you bought a candle."

"Marta sees everything."

"What's it for?"

"I don't know yet." He weighed the little paper packet in his hand. "Maybe for the shelf where my name used to be. Maybe for a stranger. Maybe I'm just practicing — buying things for reasons I'll understand later. It seems like that's going to be most of my life."

She sat down beside him. Around the square, market day went on in its ancient rhythm — haggling, laughter, a dog stealing a sausage to universal applause, children weaving between the stalls. Two of the children, chasing each other, skidded to a stop near the fountain and stared at him with the frank appraisal of the young, and one of them, a girl with a scrape healing on her knee, asked the question children ask everywhere and always:

"Who are you?"

Sef tensed. He felt it, the small flinch beside him — she had spent a year learning to answer that question for him, arming herself with explanations, ready to defend her brother's strangeness against the world. But he found he didn't need defending. The question landed on him like sunlight: warm, weightless, requiring nothing.

"I don't know yet," he told the girl, and meant it cheerfully. "I'm still choosing."

The girl considered this with great seriousness, found it acceptable, and demanded to know if the candle was magic. He said all candles were a little bit magic, which delighted her, and then her friend pulled her away toward the sausage-thief drama, and the square swallowed her up.

Sef exhaled. "You're getting better at that."

"At what?"

"At being no one. At letting it be enough." She looked out at the market, at the blossom-heavy trees beyond the roofs, at the mountains where the passes stood open for the first time in three hundred years. "Veyran is still out there. Odile writes that the shrines in the high country are going quiet again, one by one, like lamps going out — and not sleeping this time. Frightened. Whatever he's doing in the mountains, he's doing it to them." She paused. "I have to tell you that. I won't keep the world's weight off you by pretending it isn't there. You taught me that, or — the man you were taught me that. Or you did. Whoever you turn out to be."

He nodded slowly. The cold spot in his chest from the peddler's rumor stirred again, and he let it, because pretending it away would be a kind of theft, and he was done with theft — done receiving it, done committing it, done building lives on other people's missing pieces.

"But not today," Sef added, softer.

"No," he agreed. "Not today. Today there's a candle to light, and a shelf with my name scratched under it, and I think—" he stood, brushing off his trousers, and the sun was warm and the bees were loud in the orchards and the whole valley smelled of wet earth and white flowers, "—I think I'd like to write something new there. Something small. Not a name. More like... a promise. The way you'd mark a seedling so you remember where you planted it."

They walked home together through the market, two strangers among strangers, and behind them the fountain went on speaking its one fluent language, and in his pocket the little candle rode like a held breath — unlit, unnamed, waiting for the dark it was meant for.
