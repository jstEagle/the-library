## 1. The Weight of Entries

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Ines Vane found the discrepancy.

She sat in the counting room of the Salt Guild on Copperlane Street, where the windows were shuttered against the winter dark and the air smelled of tallow, ink, and the faint mineral bite of the guild's namesake — salt, but not the kind you ate. This salt came in stoppered jars and lead-sealed boxes, and it was worth more per ounce than silver, because it was not merely salt. It was time, pressed into crystal. It was grief, distilled until it wept. It was, on three occasions in the guild's long and unlovely history, a name that someone had paid to have taken out of every mouth in Corvane.

Ines kept the ledgers of all of it.

She turned back two pages with a fingertip still damp from her tongue, found the entry again, and set it beside the winter inventory like a magistrate setting two witnesses face to face.

*Received, 14th of Hoarfrost: one (1) borrowed year, unspent, from T. Merren, runner. Condition: fair. Interest: none, per Guildmaster's discretion.*

And below it, in the inventory column where it should have appeared:

*Nothing.*

The year was not in the vault. She had checked twice, then a third time with a lamp held so close to the shelf labels that the wax dripped onto her knuckle and she hadn't noticed until the skin stung. Somewhere between the receiving desk and the deep shelves, Tam Merren's year had gone missing — twelve months of a boy's life, unspent, unaccounted for, gone as cleanly as if it had never been written down at all.

Ines did not sigh. Sighing was for people whose errors were forgivable. She dipped her pen, opened a fresh folio, and began, in her small exact hand, an inquiry docket: date of receipt, name of receiving clerk, seal numbers, chain of custody, every hand the jar should have passed through. The pen scratched. The candle guttered. Outside, Corvane's bells rang the ninth hour, and she counted them out of habit, because counting was what she did instead of praying.

Except that it wasn't. That was the thing no one at the guild quite understood about her — not Ossa, who thought her devotion was a professional affectation, like her straight-backed chair or the way she sharpened her own nibs; not the clerks, who thought she was simply strange. But every night before she closed the ledgers, Ines Vane said a prayer. It was always the same prayer, and it was always silent, because you did not speak to the Silent God aloud. Speaking was how you asked for things, and asking was vulgar. You *wrote* to him. You entered your need in the ledger of yourself, balanced and precise, and you trusted him to read it in his own time.

*You know what I keep,* she prayed now, the way she had prayed every night for ten years, since the morning the god's voice stopped coming and everyone said he had died and Ines alone refused to say the word. *You know what I hold open for you. I am still here. I am still exact.*

The prayer went nowhere, as it always went nowhere. It fell out of her like a stone dropped down a well that had no bottom and no water, and returned nothing, and she wrote it off the way she wrote everything off: neatly, without comment, in the column marked *losses accepted.*

She was sealing the inquiry docket when she heard the knock — three quick raps, low on the door, at the height of someone who had been knocking on this particular door since he was small enough to need a stool to reach the counter.

"Enter," she said, and did not look up, because looking up would show him her face, and her face would show him the docket, and the docket would show him his own name.

Tam Merren came in the way he did everything: too fast, too warm, trailing cold street air and the smell of roasted chestnuts he had almost certainly bought with money he didn't have. He was sixteen, all elbows and restless hands, with his sister's dark curls cut short for running and a scar on his chin from the year he'd fallen off the guild's loading ramp trying to beat his own delivery record. He had been Ines's shadow since he was eleven, since the day she caught him stealing bread from the clerks' kitchen and, instead of reporting him, sat him down and taught him double-entry bookkeeping on the grounds that anyone willing to work that hard at taking something ought to learn what it cost to keep it.

He had never stolen anything since. He had, however, learned exactly what things cost.

"You're here late," Tam said, dropping onto the stool across the desk from her, which was his stool, which everyone knew was his stool.

"The ledgers don't close themselves."

"They could. You'd figure out how." He craned his neck to read upside down, and Ines turned the docket over with the smoothness of ten years' practice. "Anything good?"

"Inventory."

"Anything bad?"

"Inventory," Ines said again, and let her voice flatten into the tone that meant *stop asking*, and Tam grinned and let it go, because he trusted her, and that trust sat in Ines's chest tonight like a swallowed coal.

He had come to tell her, she realized. He'd rehearsed it on the stairs — she could see the rehearsal still stuck to him, the way his fingers kept finding each other and letting go. He was working up to it. And she knew what it was, because Ossa had mentioned it that morning with the careful neutrality of a man handing her a knife handle-first: the girl's debt had come due. Lira Merren owed the physickers forty crowns for the fever-cure they'd sold her in autumn, and the collection house had given the family until midwinter, and there was no version of arithmetic in which a laundress and a runner produced forty crowns by midwinter.

There was one version, though. There was always one version. That was the whole point of the Salt Guild — that despair, properly collateralized, could be made to balance.

"Ines." Tam said her name the way other people cleared their throats. "I need to file a sale."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Ossa told me this morning." She kept her eyes on the ledger. "I've been preparing the paperwork."

"Oh." He deflated slightly, then rallied, because Tam could rally from anything. "Good. That's — good. Saves time. It's just — it's one year. One, off the end, they barely count those, right? Sefa told me she sold three once and you can't even feel it, you just wake up a little later than you meant to someday, and—"

"Sefa also stole her own year back out of our vault," Ines said, "and spent six months in the reeking cells for it. Don't cite Sefa to me as an authority on anything except lockpicking."

That got the grin out of him again, unwilling and crooked. Then it faded, and he looked at his hands, and said, quieter: "It's Lira's wedding in spring. If the collectors come at midwinter, they'll take the loom. They'll take the *room*. She'll start married life in a doorway, Ines. I can fix it. This is the one thing I can actually fix, and it's just a year."

A year. Twelve months off the end of a sixteen-year-old life, and he said *just* the way he said it about everything — just a scrape, just a fever, just a debt. Ines had watched him grow up measuring himself against the world's costs and deciding, every time, that he was worth less than they were. She had never found the entry in any ledger that showed him where to correct the error.

"The interest schedule is punitive under eighteen," she said. "I can get Ossa to waive it. He will, if I ask. But Tam—" and here she finally looked up, and let him see her face after all, because some things needed seeing, "—a year sold is a year *gone*. Not deferred. Gone. You will die twelve months earlier than the man you were going to be, and you will never know which months they were, and neither will I, and I will think about it more than you will, so consider that my interest."

"That's not how it works."

"That is exactly how it works. I write the entries. I know how it works better than anyone alive."

He was quiet for a moment. The candle flame leaned and steadied. Somewhere above them, the old building ticked and settled, the way old buildings do when they are listening.

"Will you be the one who writes it?" he asked. "The entry? I'd rather it was you. I'd rather it was your handwriting in the book. Everything you write stays written. Nothing you touch ever gets lost."

And there it was — the coal, swallowed deeper. Because the truth, which she could not say, was that his year was already lost. Missing from the vault since the fourteenth of Hoarfrost, vanished through a chain of custody she herself had designed, and she had spent the evening building an inquiry docket that would either find it or expose her own system's first failure in a decade, and either way, tomorrow, she would have to stand in front of this boy and account for a piece of his life the way she accounted for everything else: precisely, or not at all.

"I'll write it," she said. "Tomorrow. Come at the eighth bell, not before — I want the vault reconciled first, so your year goes on the shelf the same hour it leaves you. No gaps. Nothing of yours sits loose in the dark."

Tam blinked at her, and then smiled — that full, unguarded smile he saved for maybe three people in the world — and Ines felt it land somewhere beneath the place where her oath lived, in the unledgered country underneath.

"You're the best thing in this building," he said, "and this building has actual magic in it."

"It has *inventory*," Ines said. "Go home. Sleep. Eat something that isn't bought on credit."

He went, taking the cold air with him, and his footsteps ran down the stairs light as a boy's, and Ines Vane sat alone with her candle and her ledgers and the sealed docket, and did not move for a long time.

* * *

At the second bell past midnight, she took the docket down to the vault herself.

The deep shelves of the Salt Guild were not, strictly speaking, entirely in the world. Every guildhouse had its oddness — a stair that gained steps, a room that was warmer in the memory of summer than in fact — and the Salt Guild's oddness was that its cellar was longer inside than outside, shelved corridors receding farther than the street above could possibly contain, lit by lamps that burned without oil because Ossa had bought their burning from someone decades ago and the transaction, whatever it had been, still held. Ines walked the aisles with her key ring chiming softly, checking seals by touch, reading dates by lamplight, and the silence down here was of a different quality than the silence upstairs. Upstairs, silence was just absence of noise. Down here, among the bottled years and the grief-jars and the three name-boxes that no one spoke of, the silence felt *attended*. Like a room with someone standing in the corner of it.

She had always assumed that was the god. Ten years ago, the Silent God had spoken to his faithful one last time — a single word, heard simultaneously by every sworn soul in Corvane, and the word was *wait* — and then nothing. The temples emptied. The priests unfrocked themselves or went mad or took up honest trade. The city moved on, because cities always move on; grief is a luxury market and Corvane had debts. But Ines had been newly sworn that year, nineteen years old and certain as only the nineteen are certain, and she had decided that a god who said *wait* was a god who intended to come back, and that someone ought to keep the books current against his return.

So she stayed. She kept the ledgers. She balanced the impossible trades — the widow's grief sold by the dram to a playwright who needed to weep on cue; the merchant's thirty borrowed years funding his daughter's fleet; the small desperate sales, always the hardest to enter, the years and memories sold by people like Tam who had nothing else in the house to pawn. And every night she prayed her silent prayer into the attended silence, and every night the silence declined to answer, and every night she marked it *losses accepted* and locked the doors behind her.

Tonight, she found the empty slot on Shelf Nine where Tam's year should have been.

She stood in front of it for a long moment, lamp raised, and made herself look at the gap the way she made herself look at everything: completely. Dust unbroken on the shelf lip. Seal-wax residue from the original jar still in place, meaning the jar had been lifted cleanly, by someone with access, sometime in the last eleven days. Her own receiving signature on the original entry, dated the fourteenth, flawless, confirming a deposit she could now prove had never reached the shelf.

Someone had stolen a year out from under her. Or — and this was the thought she had been declining to think all evening, the thought that now arrived with the patience of something that had all night — someone had *collected* it. Because a sold year was not idle stock. A sold year was a debt instrument, and debts, in the oldest sense of the word, were owned by powers older than guilds.

Ines set her palm flat against the empty shelf, on the spot where the dust was not disturbed, and did the thing she almost never did.

She spoke aloud.

"Where is it?" she asked the attended silence. "What took it? Answer me. You owe me that much. I have kept every entry. I have never once rounded against you. *Answer me.*"

Her voice fell into the dark of the aisles and did not echo, which was wrong — the vault echoed; she knew its acoustics the way she knew her own handwriting. The sound simply stopped, absorbed, as though the silence itself had a mouth.

And then, faintly, from far down the corridor where the lamps gave out — from deeper in the cellar than the cellar went — Ines heard a sound she had not heard in ten years.

Not a voice. Not yet. Something smaller and more terrible: the sound of a page turning.

Somewhere in the dark, something was reading.

Ines Vane stood very still, her lamp trembling just enough to make the shadows lean, and understood with the certainty of a woman who has audited ten thousand entries that the ledger was not missing a line at all.

Something had simply begun calling them in.
