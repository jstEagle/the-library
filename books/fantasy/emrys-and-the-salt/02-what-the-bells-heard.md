## 2. What the Bells Heard

The bells of Corvane began lying on the third morning after the page turned.

Ines noticed it first because she noticed everything that touched the guild's accounts, and the bells were, in their way, an account. Every dawn for as long as she had kept the ledgers, the Vigil bell in the Saltspire had rung eleven strokes — one for each god of the old pantheon, ten of them silent, and a final stroke for the one who had fallen quiet last and hardest: hers. The eleventh stroke had always been struck by a mechanism no living hand wound. The priests claimed it rang itself out of habit. Ines had privately suspected it rang itself out of spite.

On the third morning, the Vigil bell rang twelve times.

She stood at her counting table with a pen still wet against the margin and listened to the twelfth stroke fade over the rooftops, and around her the guild hall went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes. Somewhere above, a clerk dropped a tray of ink pots and did not swear. Tam, curled on his usual stool by the delivery door with a heel of bread, looked up at her with his eyebrows raised in a question he was too superstitious to ask aloud.

"Counting error," Ines said, and wrote *twelfth stroke, unpriced* in the margin, because that was what she did with things she could not explain: she gave them a line, and a line made them manageable.

The line did not make it manageable.

By midmorning the first runner came in from the Harborside ward with news that stopped the sorting room cold: old Mother Quill, who had prayed every dusk for nine years for her drowned son's boat to come home, had woken to find the boat sitting in her yard, dry, mended, and full of fish so fresh they were still turning. Not salvage. Not coincidence. The boat had been under forty feet of grey water since the spring floods, and everyone in Harborside knew it, and now everyone in Harborside also knew that Mother Quill's prayer had been answered, and half of them were already on their knees in the street asking for things.

"Price it," Guildmaster Ossa said, when the runner finished. His voice was flat as a scale-pan. "Whatever it is, we price it."

"You can't price a miracle," said Vetch, the senior broker, whose job for thirty years had been pricing things that should not exist.

"You can price anything," Ossa said. "That is the entire business. Get me comparables."

And that was how Ines spent the strangest day of her professional life: building comparables for miracles. She sat in the back office with the winter ledgers open and tried to establish precedent, because the Salt Guild traded in borrowed years and bottled grief and names, but never once — not in the oldest book, not in the founding entries written in a hand like knotted rope — had it traded in answered prayers. Prayer was the currency the gods took. It had never been currency the gods gave back.

She found herself writing categories. *Restoration of lost objects.* *Reversal of death (partial; aquatic).* *Weather intervention, localized.* Each entry got a question mark where the price belonged, and by noon the page looked less like a ledger than like a fever chart.

Tam brought her tea at some point and stood reading over her shoulder, which he was not supposed to do and which she never stopped him from doing.

"That one says a man grew a new hand," he said, tapping an entry a runner had brought in from the Tanner's Row ward. "His old one got caught in a press two years ago. Now there's two of them. Both work. He says the new one is better." He paused. "Is that good or bad?"

"It depends," Ines said, "on what the hand costs him."

"Miracles are free. That's the whole point of them."

Ines looked up at him — sixteen years old, all elbows and stubborn cheer, a boy who had sold a year of his own life three winters ago to clear his sister's debt and had never once spoken of it with anything but practical pride, as if it were a chore well done — and felt something cold move through her chest.

"Nothing is free," she said. "Everything has an entry somewhere. The only question is whether you can see the line item."

Tam grinned at her, unbothered, the way he was unbothered by everything, and took his empty mug back toward the kitchen, and Ines sat looking at the entry about the man with two hands and thought about the sound of a page turning in a cellar deeper than the cellar went.

*Something had begun calling the entries in.*

She had told herself, in the three nights since, that she had imagined it. That grief and lamplight and too much strong tea had conspired. But the city outside her window was filling up with evidence that something had woken, and the guild's whole architecture rested on the certainty that the gods were asleep, or dead, or indifferent — that prayer went into the dark and stayed there, and the dark kept it, and that was the arrangement. The guild did not trade against the gods. The guild traded in the gaps the sleeping gods left behind.

If the gaps were closing, everything in every book she kept was suddenly either worthless or due.

* * *

The drowned child came out of the river at the Hour of Lanterns, and Ines was there because Ossa sent her.

"A valuation," he'd said, handing her the assignment slip himself, which he almost never did. "Harborside. A woman named Corla Brenn swears the river gave her daughter back. Go and tell me what actually happened. You're the only person in this building who writes down what happened instead of what people wish had happened."

So Ines walked down through the evening streets with her ledger satchel and her lamp, past temples that were lit when they should have been dark, past shrines with more candles burning than wax could plausibly account for, past a knot of people kneeling in the middle of Cobbler's Way praying aloud with their eyes shut while traffic broke around them like water around a stone. She heard fragments as she passed. A woman asking for her husband's cough to leave him. A man asking, over and over, with terrible specificity, for his brother to forgive him. A child asking for snow, because the other children had snow to play in and she didn't, and it was nearly winter anyway.

Ines did not pray. She had not truly prayed in ten years — not since the silence settled, not since she had understood that her god heard nothing or chose to hear nothing, and that her oaths and offerings went into the same dark as everyone else's. But she kept the forms, because the forms were part of the oath, and the oath was part of her the way the scar on her palm was part of her. So as she crossed the bridge at Harborside she performed the small nightly observance, thumb to the salt-ring on her finger, the words said inwardly in the old cadence:

*Keep my hands honest. Keep my lines true. Hear me, if hearing is a thing you do.*

Silence. The same silence as always. Ten years of the same silence, familiar as her own heartbeat.

Except that tonight, for the first time, the silence had a texture. It did not feel like absence. It felt like attention withheld — like standing outside a door and knowing, newly and horribly, that someone was on the other side of it, listening, and choosing not to answer.

Ines stopped in the middle of the bridge and gripped the rail.

"No," she said quietly, to the river, to the mountain, to whatever was paying attention without answering. "No, if you're awake, you answer. That's the covenant. That's the whole shape of it. You don't get to be awake and silent. Silence was the *honest* thing. Silence meant you were gone."

The river said nothing. Downstream, lanterns were gathering at the water-stairs below Corla Brenn's house, and Ines made herself let go of the rail and walk toward them, because she had a valuation to perform, and because work was the wall she had built between herself and every unbearable thing for fifteen years, and tonight of all nights she needed the wall.

They had laid the girl on the steps. Small — seven, maybe eight — wrapped in three blankets, hair still dripping, lips blue-grey, and around her a crowd held at bay by a fishwife with a boathook and the righteous fury of a woman guarding a miracle. The mother, Corla Brenn, knelt with the child's head in her lap, saying nothing at all, which frightened Ines more than any amount of weeping would have.

"What happened?" Ines asked. "Exactly. In order."

A dozen voices started at once. The fishwife silenced them with the boathook and nodded at Ines, recognizing the guild badge. "You tell it, Doss. You pulled her out."

Doss was a young eel-fisher with river-mud to his knees and an expression like a man trying to remember a dream. "She went in at midday," he said. "Slipped off the landing stage chasing a gull, maybe. Current took her past the second piling. I dove. I dove twice. Cold season, current like that — nobody comes back from past the second piling, mistress, everybody knows it. I came up empty both times." He wiped his mouth. "Then I went in a third time, because her mother was screaming and I couldn't not. And the river—" He stopped. Started again. "The river wasn't moving. Not frozen. Just — still. Like held water. Like a cupped hand. And she was lying on top of it. On the surface, mistress, like a leaf, arms folded, dry. Dry as you are. And the river set her down at the stairs gentle as a servant laying a coat over a chair, and then it started moving again like nothing ever happened."

"Show me where," Ines said.

He showed her. She stood at the second piling and looked down into black water and made herself think like an auditor instead of a woman whose skin was crawling. *Entry: reversal of death, complete. Witnessed by four. No payment observed. No contract shown. No signature taken.*

Every transaction she had ever recorded had a signature. Souls, years, griefs, names — the guild's whole law was that nothing moved without consent marked down, even desperate consent, even consent sold cheap. Whatever had given this child back had not asked anything. Had not written anything. Had simply *answered*, the way a body answers a blow.

And that was the thing that curdled the wonder in her chest into dread, standing there with her pen poised over a clean line: a gift with no entry was not generosity. It was appetite without manners. Things that gave without accounting were things that had not yet decided what they wanted to be paid.

She wrote the line anyway. *Reversal of death, complete, aquatic. Price: unknown. Terms: none observed.* Her hand was steady. She was proud of that, later, in the way you are proud of small fortitudes.

When she turned back, the crowd had gone strange and quiet, and Corla Brenn was looking at her across the blankets with the child's wet hair spilling over her wrists.

"You're guild," the woman said. Not a question. "You keep the books on what things cost."

"I do."

"Then tell me true, guildswoman, because everyone here is afraid to say it." Her voice did not shake. "My girl prayed. Night after night for a year, she prayed to the Old Ten to let her learn to swim before summer, because she was ashamed of being afraid of the water. That was her prayer. To *learn to swim*. And the river took her instead, and then the river gave her back, and now—" Corla Brenn's composure cracked, just at the edge, just enough to show the abyss underneath. "Now she talks in her sleep. Since they carried her inside. She talks in a voice that isn't hers, and she says the same thing over and over, and I want it written down somewhere official, because if it's written down then it's real, and if it's real then somebody has to deal with it."

Ines crouched, opened the ledger, and readied her pen. "Say it."

Corla Brenn closed her eyes. "'Received,'" she recited. "It says — *she* says — 'Received. Received. All accounts will be called in. Received.'"

The lamp flame leaned though there was no draft. Somewhere upstream, a temple bell began ringing and could not stop ringing, stroke after stroke after stroke, until it was less a sound than a scream in bronze, and dogs started up along the whole waterfront, and the crowd on the stairs pressed together like sheep smelling weather.

Ines wrote it down. Word for word. Her handwriting did not shake until the final letter, and she hated herself for the way it shook, and she finished the letter anyway.

* * *

She walked back to the guild hall fast, past shrines blazing with candlelight, past the kneeling crowds, past a priest of the Twin Sisters standing in his own doorway with his vestments on inside out, laughing and weeping at once, saying to no one: "Twenty years. Twenty years I poured it into the dark. It's *full*. Do you understand? It was never empty, it was *full*, it was holding all of it, and now—"

Ines did not stop. She climbed the guild steps two at a time and went straight past the sorting room, past the counting tables, past Vetch shouting about comparables, straight to Ossa's office, where the Guildmaster sat with the sealed contract from the deep archive open on his desk — the one he'd shown her two nights ago, the one from decades past, the one with the deferred payments and the clause written in a hand like knotted rope — and she put her ledger down in front of him and opened it to the day's entries.

"Read it," she said.

Ossa read. He was a hard, careful man, built like a dock crane, and she watched him go carefully harder as he reached the end.

"All accounts will be called in," he repeated.

"The river gave a child back tonight. Whole. Unasked-for terms. And the child speaks in a voice that isn't hers, and the voice says the accounts are being called in." Ines laid her palm flat on the page. "Ossa. The bells rang twelve. Mother Quill's boat came home full of fish. A tanner grew a hand. Every shrine in this city burned double candles tonight and half of them burned triple. Something woke up. Maybe several somethings. And whatever it is, it doesn't bargain, it doesn't sign, it doesn't price — it just *gives*, and then it says *received*, like a merchant marking stock against a debt."

"The Silent God's contract," Ossa said slowly, "defers payment until waking."

"Yes."

"And the faithful who sold—"

"Sold souls for miracles, payment on waking. Decades ago. My god has been silent for ten years, but the contract predates the silence. If it's awake — if any of them are awake — then every deferred soul-debt in every sealed book in this city just came due." Ines heard her own voice go very quiet, which was what it did instead of shaking. "And I have been keeping those books. I have been keeping them *beautifully*. For years. Every name, every sale, every desperate signature, indexed and preserved and protected, because that was my oath — because I thought I was keeping records safe."

Ossa looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the mad bell finally stopped, and the silence it left was worse.

"You think your god is one of them," he said. "The ones that woke."

"I think the eleventh stroke rang for ten years out of habit," Ines said. "And last night it rang a twelfth. And I think something in the cellar of this guild has been reading our ledgers in the dark, and I think it reads them the way I taught myself to read them, because whoever trained me learned from the same hand." She swallowed. "I think mine didn't die, Ossa. I think mine was *fasting*. And I think it just smelled the rest of the table being served."

For once, the Guildmaster of the Salt Guild had no arithmetic ready. He sat with his big hands flat on the desk, on either side of the sealed contract, and Ines saw — clearly, terribly — that beneath all his hardness he was as frightened as she was, and that his fear wore the face of a man calculating which losses a business can survive.

"First bell tomorrow," he said at last, "we audit the sealed contracts. All of them. Every soul-debt, every deferred payment, every name in every vault. I want to know exactly what we owe and to whom before whatever woke finishes its breakfast." He paused. "And Ines — the boy. Tam. His grandmother signed one of those contracts. You know that. Her debt passed down. If the accounts are being called in—"

Ines was already moving, already through the door, already taking the stairs three at a time, because Tam worked late shifts in the dispatch room on the ground floor, and the dispatch room faced the mountain, and the mountain stood black against the stars with a light in it that had not been there yesterday — a faint, warm, patient glow, deep in the rock, like a lamp lit behind a wall, like a page being turned toward the beginning.

She found the dispatch room empty except for Tam's coat, folded neatly over his stool.

Folded neatly, the way he never folded anything.

Out on the flagstones beyond the loading doors, in the frost, in his shirtsleeves, facing the mountain with his eyes shut and his face peaceful as a sleeper's, stood Tam — walking, one slow step at a time, toward the thing that had begun calling in its accounts.
