## 3. A Contract in Ash

The summons came at the hour when the guild's lamps were trimmed low and the counting-house smelled of tallow and cold iron. A boy I didn't know — new, nervous, his collar too big for him — stood at my desk with a folded slip of paper he held the way runners hold things they've been told are urgent: too tightly, as if squeezing would make them lighter.

"Guildmaster wants you," he said. "The under-vault."

I looked at the paper. It was blank. That was Ossa's way of saying *come now and bring nothing you'd mind losing*, because a blank page could be read as anything, and anything could be read as an order.

"Where's Tam?" I asked.

The boy blinked. "Running the river route. Why?"

"No reason." I capped my ink, squared the ledger I'd been auditing so its edges kissed the desk's edge, and followed him down.

* * *

The under-vault of the Salt Guild was not on any map of Corvane, which was fitting, because most of what the guild kept was not on any ledger either. You reached it by taking the counting-house stairs past the floor where the bottled griefs slept in their straw, past the cold room where borrowed years ticked faintly in their jars like insects dreaming of summer, and then through a door that only opened for people whose names had been written into the guild's master roll in ink mixed with salt from three different seas.

Ossa was waiting inside with two lamps lit, which meant bad news. One lamp meant routine. Three meant ceremony. Two meant she wanted to see my face clearly while she told me something.

She was not a tall woman, Guildmaster Ossa, but she had the kind of presence that made rooms feel smaller and more honest. Her hands were stained to the second knuckle with forty years of ledger-ink, and she held them folded now, which she only did when she didn't trust herself to gesture.

"Ines." She said my name the way she said all difficult words: precisely, without apology. "Close the door."

I closed it. The sound it made was very soft and very final, the way vault doors are built to be.

"You've been auditing the winter ledgers," she said. "The deep ones."

"The deep ones, yes." The deep ledgers were the guild's oldest holdings — contracts so old their clauses referenced laws that no longer existed, debts denominated in currencies that had been dead for centuries. I audited them every winter because no one else could read the old shorthand without weeping, or so Brannoc-the-junior-clerk used to say before he was dismissed for drinking the inventory. "I found nothing irregular. Two entries misfiled by a predecessor with worse handwriting than sense, but nothing that touches current stock."

"I know," Ossa said. "You're good. That's why this is going to be harder than it should be." She turned, and her shadow moved long across the shelves behind her — shelves I had passed a hundred times, catalogued a hundred times, shelves holding sealed boxes marked in the old notation for *dormant instruments*. "There's a contract I've never shown you. Never shown anyone below Guildmaster. It came to the guild sixty-one years ago, and every Guildmaster since has sworn the same oath about it that you swore about your god."

"And what oath is that?"

"*Silence until waking.*"

The words went through me like a splinter finding the grain. I knew that phrasing. Every sworn servant of the Silent God knew it. We said it at novitiation, we said it at funerals, we said it over the guild's books each quarter-close: *silence until waking*. It was supposed to mean that our god kept silence until the world woke to it again. It had never occurred to me — not once in ten years of faithful, unanswered prayer — that the phrase might have been a clause. A term. Something with conditions attached, the way everything in Corvane had conditions attached if you read far enough down the page.

"Show me," I said.

Ossa lifted a box from the shelf. It was small — smaller than a bread loaf — and made of lead, and it was warm. Not lamp-warm. Body-warm, the way a thing is warm when something inside it is breathing slowly, or pretending to.

"The seal broke three nights ago," she said. "On its own. No one touched it. I've had it in my keeping for nineteen years, and three nights ago it cracked along the seam like an egg, and ever since then it's been—" she turned it in her hands, and I saw the fine fracture running around its middle, weeping a thin line of grey light— "*listening.*"

"To what?"

"To prayers, I think. Or to whatever's answering them." She set it on the table between us, and neither of us touched it. "You've seen what's happening in the city. The drowned child. The baker's wife who prayed for rain and got a flood that drowned her own cellar but filled it with fish. The bells ringing themselves at dawn."

"I've seen."

"The gods are awake, Ines. All of them, or enough of them. And this contract was written against exactly this day." She drew a breath that sounded like it cost her. "Open it with me. If we open it together, the reading binds both of us, and I won't have you carrying this alone."

That, more than anything, frightened me. Ossa did not share burdens. Ossa believed burdens were like debts — divisible, yes, but best carried by whoever had signed for them.

We put our hands on the lead box together, and the seam parted like a mouth.

* * *

Inside was a single sheet of vellum, folded eight times, and a lock of hair gone white as salt.

The vellum was covered in writing I recognized before I understood it, the way you recognize a voice through a wall. It was the hand of the Silent God's clergy — the old clergy, the pre-silence clergy, the ones whose training manuals had trained my trainers. The loops were wrong for human fingers, too regular, each letter identical to its siblings as if stamped rather than written. But the *structure* — the way obligations nested inside obligations, the way definitions were deferred to later clauses, the way mercy was always mentioned early and never mentioned again — that structure I knew intimately. I had spent ten years learning to write contracts in that shape. I had thought I was learning a style.

I had been learning a language. And languages are learned from someone.

"Read the preamble," Ossa said quietly.

I read aloud, because some things need to be heard in a human voice to be believed:

*"In the year of the last bell, we the undersigned, being in desperation beyond the reach of ordinary remedy, do sell unto the Quiet One below the mountain that portion of ourselves which endures, in exchange for miracle rendered now and payment collected later. Let the debt sleep as the god sleeps. Let the debt wake as the god wakes. Let it pass to blood as land passes to blood, for a soul sold is a soul owned, and ownership does not die with the owner."*

Below the preamble were the signatures. Forty-one of them. Each one written in the signer's own hand, and each one accompanied by a small pressed mark where the signer had been made to bleed onto the page — a thumbprint in rust-brown, faded now to the color of old brick.

Forty-one souls. Sold in one season of desperation — I checked the date, and felt my stomach drop, because I knew the date. The Famine Winter. The one the histories called the Year of Boiled Leather, when the granaries failed and the temples' charity ran out and half of Corvane's poor did things they never spoke of afterward to keep their children fed.

They hadn't stolen. They hadn't sold their children. They had sold themselves — the part of themselves that endures — to the god I served, in exchange for miracles. Bread appearing in empty cupboards. Fevers breaking. Wells that stayed sweet. Small mercies, forty-one of them, rendered instantly and paid for across sixty years of deferral.

And the payment terms were written in the same beautiful, terrible hand:

*"Collection shall commence upon waking."*

"They knew," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, like a clerk reading figures. "The old clergy wrote this knowing the god would wake someday. They built a harvest into the theology. Ten years of silence wasn't death or sulking or mystery — it was *ripening*."

"Yes," Ossa said.

"All those prayers I said. All those nights I sat in the chapel asking why my god had abandoned us, wondering what I'd done wrong, whether my faith was too small—" I stopped, because my voice had begun to shake, and shaking voices don't get to finish sentences like that. "It wasn't silent. It was *eating*. Slowly. And we kept the plates warm."

Ossa didn't correct me. That was its own kind of confirmation.

"There's more," she said. "Turn to the codicil."

* * *

The codicil was newer — added perhaps thirty years ago, in a human hand, though still in the god's structural grammar. It listed amendments: deaths among the original signatories, transfers of obligation, and one entry that stopped my heart the way a hand stops a clock.

*Debtor: Maren Ashka, signatory the eleventh. Deceased. Obligation passes to issue.*

*Issue: Sela Ashka. Deceased. Obligation passes to issue.*

*Issue: Tam Ashka. Living. Age eleven at inheritance. Obligation dormant until waking.*

I read the name four times. The letters didn't rearrange themselves. The room didn't lurch. The world simply continued, indifferent, while I stood in a lead-lined vault beneath the counting-house holding a page that said the boy who brought me tea when I worked too late, who named the guild's carrier pigeons after saints, who had sold a year of his own life last month to pay his sister's debt because he was too proud to ask for help — that boy had been *owned* since he was eleven years old, and I had held the ledger that recorded it, and I had audited it, and I had certified it accurate.

"I filed this," I said. "Three winters ago. There was a transfer entry — Ashka, dormant instrument, confirmed non-executable pending divine dormancy. I initialed it. I initialed it, Ossa. I checked the chain of title like it was a shipment of salt."

"You did your work," Ossa said. "Perfectly. That's what I'm telling you, Ines. You did nothing wrong. The horror isn't an error in the book. The horror *is* the book. It's been the book since before either of us was born, and every audit we've ever run has kept it tidy, kept it enforceable, kept it *ready*."

I set the vellum down because my hands had begun to tremble and I would not let it see that. Whatever was listening in that lead — whatever had cracked its own shell to hear the city praying — I would not give it the satisfaction.

"Tam's grandmother," I said. "Maren Ashka. What did she buy?"

Ossa consulted a slip of notes she'd prepared — of course she had; Ossa prepared for grief the way quartermasters prepare for siege. "A fever cure. Her daughter Sela was dying of lung-rot, age six. The miracle rendered was complete and permanent. Sela lived to fifty-two, married, bore two children, died in her bed." A pause. "Maren died the following spring. The record doesn't say of what."

"Of what she'd done," I said. "Or of what she'd learned she'd done. Selling your enduring soul buys you a long time to regret it."

"Perhaps." Ossa folded her ink-stained hands again. "Or perhaps she died knowing the debt would pass to a granddaughter not yet born, and decided she'd rather face judgment than watch that child grow up under a cloud she couldn't name. Some knowledge is a weight the heart sets down the only way it can."

"That's generous."

"I'm a guildmaster. Generosity is arithmetic I do on purpose." She looked at me then, directly, and I saw that she had aged three days in the hour since the boy fetched me. "There's a reason I showed you this tonight instead of burying it another nineteen years, Ines, and it isn't sentiment."

"Then say it."

"The seal broke three nights ago. The contract is *active*. Dormant instruments wake with the god — that's in the master clause, you read it. Which means somewhere in this city, a fourteen-year-old runner is walking around with a lien on his soul that just became collectible." She let that sit between us like a coin on a table. "And three nights ago, Ines, is also when the first answered prayer was reported. The drowned child. The river gave her back."

I thought of the girl on the bank, coughing up river-water and river-light, her mother screaming joy into her wet hair. I had thought it was a miracle. I had knelt in the mud and almost prayed my thanks to a god who, it turned out, had merely opened its mouth.

"Collection," I said. "It starts with collection."

"It may start anywhere. But the contract specifies venue." Ossa tapped the final clause, and I read it, and the words seemed to arrive in my head already known, the way a nightmare arrives: *payment shall be rendered at the throne below the mountain, where the debtor shall walk unbidden, drawn by the debt as water is drawn by the sea.*

"Tam will start walking," I said.

"Soon," Ossa agreed. "If he hasn't already begun in his sleep."

* * *

I left the under-vault at a pace I told myself was professional. I crossed the counting-house, nodded to the night clerks, took my coat from its peg with steady hands. I walked out into the Corvane dark, where the bells were ringing again — not dawn-bells, not alarm-bells, just ringing, all of them, every tower at once, a sound like the whole city being gently shaken awake by something enormous and pleased with itself.

Tam lodged three streets from the guildhall, above a chandler's shop, in a room he shared with his sister Wren and whatever pigeons he was nursing back to flight that month. I had walked that route a thousand times. Tonight it stretched.

Because here is what I could not stop calculating, in the cold ledger-arithmetic my training had ground into me: a soul-debt sixty years deferred, coming due. A god waking hungry after a decade of silence — and how long, really, had it been silent? Perhaps the silence itself had been feeding. Perhaps the famine miracles had been an investment, and investments mature. And perhaps the reason my prayers had gone unanswered for ten years was not absence at all, but appetite — a god with its mouth full, patient, letting the faithful ripen.

I had sworn myself to it. I had given it my oaths, my hours, my precision, my love disguised as bookkeeping. And the whole time, the thing I served had been keeping a list of everyone it intended to eat, and I had *alphabetized it*.

I climbed the chandler's stairs two at a time.

The door to Tam's room was ajar. That was wrong — Tam latched his door, always, because Wren sleepwalked and once nearly stepped out a window chasing something only she could see. I pushed it open with a heart already braced.

Wren was asleep in her cot, breathing slow, one arm flung out. Tam's bed was empty. The blanket hung neatly folded over its foot — folded, the way you fold bedding when you expect to come back, or the way a body folds it when the body is no longer the one deciding.

The window stood open to the ringing bells and the cold.

And on the sill, in a thin line, deliberate as a signature: a trail of coarse salt, leading out over the rooftops, down toward the mountain, glittering faintly in the bell-light like a path of tiny stars laid down by someone who wanted to be followed — or by something that wanted to make sure its property knew the way home.

I put my hand on the sill. The salt was still warm.

"TAM!" I shouted it into the dark, into the bells, into the listening night, and somewhere below me, faint and getting fainter, I swear I heard a boy's voice answer — cheerful, breathless, terribly far away already:

"Ines? Is that you? I can't stop walking — I tried, I really tried—"
