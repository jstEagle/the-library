## 4. Tam, Running

Tam woke with his boots on.

That was the first wrong thing. Tam never slept in his boots — Ines had scolded him about it a dozen times, said a runner who slept shod would wake running and never stop, and he'd laughed and promised and done it anyway half the time, but not tonight. Tonight he woke sitting upright on the narrow cot in the guild's attic dormitory, laces tied, soles dusty, and the dust was gray-white and gritty between his toes where it had worked through the stitching, and he knew — the way you know the taste of your own name — that it was mountain dust. Corvane sat in the valley's lap like a child in its mother's, and everything in the city carried a little of the mountain in it, but this was different. This was *from* there. From up. From the warrens under the old mines where nobody went anymore because the shafts had gone soft and the air went strange and the guild marked them CLOSED BY ORDER OF OSSA in letters that everyone could read and no one obeyed.

The second wrong thing was the window. It was open. The latch was a brass hook-and-eye, stiff as an old man's knee, and Tam had never once managed to open it without swearing, and now it stood swung wide to the February dark and the cold had come in and lain across the floor like a sheet pulled over a body.

The third wrong thing was his feet. They ached. Not rested-ache, not grown-too-fast ache, but the deep hot ache of walking, of hours of walking, of stone and slope and the hard crown of the road where it lifted toward the mountain gate before turning back down into the tannery district.

He got up. His legs buckled and caught him on the bedframe, and he stood there swaying, thirteen years old and suddenly old in the calves, and looked at the window, and the window looked back at him with all the patience of a door.

*You walked,* he thought. *In the night. You got up and you laced your boots and you opened the window and you climbed down — how did I climb down? — and you walked.*

He didn't say *I*. That was the thing he noticed fourth, standing in the cold with his heart going like a runner's: some part of him had done the walking, and it hadn't been the part that did the talking. He said *you* to it in his head, the way you'd address a dog that had gotten out of the yard, and the part of him that had walked didn't answer, but it leaned, very slightly, toward the window. Toward the mountain. The way a compass needle leans. The way a hungry man leans toward a lit window where bread is being broken.

Tam Vane — no, Tam wasn't a Vane, that was Ines's name, but she'd written *Tam Vane* in the guild ledger once by accident when she was tired and he'd made her leave it, made her strike it through properly but leave the ghost of it visible beneath the correction, because that was the closest thing to family paper he'd ever had — Tam closed the window. He hooked the latch. He sat on the cot and unlaced his boots with fingers that shook, and he put them under the bed where boots go when they're off duty, and he lay down, and he did not sleep.

He listened instead. To the dormitory breathing around him — Pip and Marlow and the others, runners all, sprawled and snoring in the honest way of children who have sold nothing yet except time they could spare. To the wind working at the shutters across the attic. And underneath all of it, so low he might have invented it, a sound like a bell rung underwater, like a hymn heard through three walls, coming from everywhere and nowhere and most of all from *up*.

He didn't sleep. But somewhere in the small hours, sleep took him anyway, the way the river takes a stick when you stop watching it.

And he ran.

* * *

Morning found him two streets away, standing in the tannery yard with his hand flat against the wall of the rendering shed, breathing hard, boots muddy to the ankle. A tanner's boy stared at him over a hide stretched on its frame.

"You been there long?" the boy asked.

"No," Tam said, which was true, and then, because lying is just bookkeeping with words: "I don't know."

He walked back to the guild house. He went in through the kitchen because the kitchen meant warmth and the kitchen meant Sefa-the-runner (no relation to any other Sefa) who saved him the ends of the bacon, and the kitchen meant normal, and he needed normal the way a drowning man needs a plank he can pretend is a boat.

Ines was at the long table.

She had the winter ledgers open in front of her and her pen behind her ear and her spectacles pushed up into her hair, which meant she'd been working since before dawn, which meant something in the books had frightened her, because Ines only worked frightened. She looked up when he came in and her face did a thing — quick, gone in a blink, a flinch folded inside a smile — and Tam, who had spent nine years learning to read faces the way other people read street signs, read hers perfectly.

*She knows,* he thought. *Or she suspects. Or she's frightened of something else and I'm going to make it worse by telling her, or better by telling her, and either way I have to tell her because if I don't tell Ines then who is there to tell?*

"Sit," she said. "Eat. You look like you slept in a ditch."

"I slept in my boots," Tam said, and told her the rest.

He told it plainly, the way she'd taught him to report a delivery: facts first, feelings after, nothing embellished because embellishment was a kind of theft. The boots. The window. The dust. The ache. The second night — because there had been a second night, last night, and this morning made two — and the direction, always the same direction, always *up*, along the old pilgrim road past the gate shrine where the god's face had been worn smooth by centuries of thumbs, up toward the mines, up toward the mountain that the maps called Mount Corvane and the miners had called, in the old days, the Quiet One.

Ines listened without moving. That was the frightening thing. Ines was a still person the way deep water is still, but she usually moved *some*thing — a pen, a finger, the corner of her mouth. Now she was stone from the collarbones up, and her hands, below the table's edge where he couldn't see them, were doing something he couldn't read.

"When did it start?" she asked.

"Two nights ago." He hesitated. "Maybe three. Maybe I did it before and slept through the walking part. There's mud on the windowsill outside. Under it. Old mud."

"Three nights," Ines said softly, and Tam watched her do arithmetic behind her eyes, and whatever sum she reached, she didn't share it.

"It's the prayers, isn't it," Tam said. "Everyone's saying the gods woke up. Sefa says the fountain on Bellmaker's Row ran wine for an hour. Marlow says a baby in Lowbridge spoke its dead grandmother's name and the grandmother answered through the well. It's the gods, isn't it. One of them wants me."

The kitchen went quiet around them. Somewhere a kettle ticked as it cooled.

"One of them doesn't get to want you," Ines said.

It came out fast and flat and hard as a slammed ledger, and Tam blinked, and Ines seemed to hear herself, and the hardness cracked and something else showed through — something scared, something fierce, something that loved him in the specific unsentimental way of people who have chosen their family out of a crowd.

"Sorry," she said. "That was — sit. Eat. We'll fix it."

"How?" Tam asked. "How do you fix being wanted by a god?"

Ines was quiet a moment. Then she smiled, and it was mostly real, and Tam held onto the mostly like a rail in a storm.

"The same way we fix everything," she said. "We write it down, and then we renegotiate the terms."

* * *

The salt-cord went on his wrist that evening.

Ines made it herself, at the worktable in her room above the counting office, and Tam sat across from her and watched, because watching Ines work was one of his favorite things in the world and also, tonight, a way of not thinking about the window and the dust and the bell-under-water sound. She took cord of plain hemp and she braided into it grains of salt — guild salt, ledger salt, the salt they used to seal contracts and mark thresholds — chanting under her breath numbers, not prayers. Dates. Amounts. Entries. Tam realized after a while that she was reciting ledger lines, binding the salt with the grammar of accounts, making the cord less a charm than a *record*, a thing that remembered.

"Hold out your wrist," she said.

He held it out. She tied the cord on snug, two fingers' width below the palm, and knotted it seven times, and blew on the knots, and sat back and looked at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere.

"What does it do?" he asked.

"Anchors you," Ines said. "Salt marks what belongs where. As long as you wear it, your feet will know which ground is yours."

"That's not what I asked," Tam said. "What is it *for*, Ines? Why am I walking?"

And here is the thing Tam would remember afterward, in the bad nights and the good ones, in the year that followed and all the years after that: she hesitated. Just a breath's worth. Just long enough for a girl who trusted her completely to notice the gap and choose, generously, trustingly, to step over it rather than into it.

"There are debts moving," Ines said. "Old ones. Contracts the guild holds from before you were born. When the gods woke, every debt woke with them, and some of those debts have names attached, and the guild has to know which names, and keep them close until we understand the terms." She met his eyes. "You're not in trouble. You're not being punished. This is me keeping inventory."

"You're saying I'm an entry," Tam said.

"I'm saying you're *mine*," Ines said, "and I don't lose track of what's mine."

And Tam believed her. That was the unbearable part, the part that lived in his chest like a swallowed coal for three more nights while the cord held and his feet stayed in the dormitory and the bell-under-water rang and rang: he believed her *completely*. He believed her the way he'd believed her when she'd paid his sister's debt out of her own purse and called it a loan and never once mentioned interest; the way he'd believed her when she said the guild was a family and the ledgers were honest; the way he'd believed her the first day, aged four, crying in a doorway, when a tall tired woman with ink on her fingers had crouched down and said, *Well. You look like an entry that hasn't been filed yet.* He believed her because believing Ines was the truest thing he knew how to do.

Which meant that when she lied to him — kindly, carefully, with seven knots and a breath blown on each — the lie went all the way down to the bedrock of him and sat there, humming, waiting.

Because Tam was a runner, and runners know routes, and on the third night, when the summons came again and the cord burned cold against his wrist and his legs tried to swing out of bed, he felt the truth of it through the lie: the cord wasn't anchoring him. It was *measuring* him. He could feel it pulling taut, gentle as a fishing line, whenever his body turned toward the mountain — feel it the way you feel a leash when the dog at the far end is stronger than the hand holding it. Ines hadn't tied him to his bed.

She'd tied herself to him. One end on his wrist, and the other end — he understood this with sudden terrible clarity — wrapped around her own arm, where he'd glimpsed, that evening, a red pressure-mark rising in the shape of a knot.

She was going to feel him pull. All night, every night. She was going to lie awake in her room above the counting office with the cord's twin biting her skin, feeling her youngest runner strain toward the thing that wanted him, and she was going to do it silently, and smile at breakfast, and call it keeping inventory.

Tam lay in the dark with his heart hammering and made himself very small and very quiet, and he did not cry, because runners don't, and he did not wake anyone, because this was his weight and not theirs, and he did not take the cord off, because taking it off would tell Ines he knew, and knowing was a debt too, and he didn't know yet what it cost.

But he lay there, and he listened to the bell under the water, and for the first time he let himself hear the words inside it — not words really, more a gravity, a leaning, a patient enormous attention turned toward him from up the valley like a lighthouse beam sweeping slow — and he understood, in the wordless way of children and animals, exactly what wanted him.

Not his body. His body was a cheap thing, all elbows and appetite.

His *name*. The one his grandmother had traded before he was born. The one Ines's ledgers said was owed.

*Come home,* said the mountain, in the voice of a thing that had been silent for ten years and had learned, in its silence, exactly how a family sounds. *Come home, little entry. Your account is due.*

And Tam, thirteen years old, wearing a salt-cord braided from lies, did the bravest thing available to him in the dark of the guild attic:

He stayed.

He stayed, and he kept staying, night after night, while the cord bit and the bell rang and his sleeping feet strained against the knot like a tide against a seawall. He stayed because Ines had asked him to stay, and because he trusted her, and because trusting her was the only ledger he'd ever kept that had never once come up short.

But tides do not negotiate. And on the fifth night, when the moon went dark and the mountain's attention narrowed to a point, the salt-cord snapped clean through — all seven knots popping one after another like knuckles cracking — and Tam Vane rose from his cot with his boots already laced, eyes open and empty as winter windows, and began to walk.

Down the stairs. Through the kitchen. Out the door Ines had warded twice over.

Into the street, and up, toward the mountain, alone.

Behind him, in her room above the counting office, Ines Vane woke with a cry, her wrist bleeding where the cord's twin had burst apart — and the last knot, the seventh, the one braided from her own true name, lay cooling on her floorboards like a dead coal.

She knew before she reached the window. She knew by the silence, by the absence of the pull, by the terrible lightness of an arm suddenly unburdened.

"Tam," she said, to the empty street, to the dark shape already climbing past the gate shrine, small and steady and dreadfully awake. "Oh, Tam. Hold on. I'm coming."

And far above, in the warrens beneath the mountain, something ancient opened eyes it had kept closed for ten years, and smiled with a mouth made of every prayer it had ever swallowed, and said, in a voice like a ledger closing:

*Received.*
