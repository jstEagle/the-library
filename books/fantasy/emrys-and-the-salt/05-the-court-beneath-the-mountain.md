## 5. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The warrens had been closed for sixty years, since the last of the Corvane silver played out and the mining companies pulled their ladders out of the earth like splinters from a wound. Ines knew the official history. She had audited it: the Shaft Closure Act, the compensation ledgers, the names of four hundred miners struck from the guild rolls with a single line each — *deceased, destitute, departed*. Three words to end a life in her trade. She had written a few herself.

What the histories did not record was that the mountain kept breathing.

She felt it at the first landing, where the old tollgate arch still stood with its iron chain rusted into decoration. The air moved — not wind, which has direction and temper, but something slower, tidal, like the exhalation of a sleeper. It smelled of salt and hot stone and, beneath both, faintly of incense. Guild incense. The cheap kind they burned at winter closings, resin and ash.

Ines stood on the landing with her lamp raised and let the breath move over her, and every instinct she owned told her to climb back up into the daylight where prayers went unanswered and everything could be priced.

She went down instead.

* * *

The descent took the better part of two hours, through galleries that narrowed and widened like the chambers of a shell. The miners' marks were still cut into the walls — tally strokes, saints' signs, the crude spiral that meant *gas, turn back* — but newer marks overlaid them, and the newer marks were not crude at all. They were precise. Columns of small characters, inked rather than carved, running along the stone in lines so straight they might have been ruled.

Ines stopped at one stretch and held her lamp close.

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

She knew that hand. Not the hand itself — the *training* behind it. The flat-topped ascenders. The way the numerals leaned exactly three degrees from vertical, because three degrees was the angle at which a column of figures remained legible by lamplight without smearing. Every clerk the Salt Guild produced learned it in their first year, drilled into the wrist until it stopped being handwriting and became posture. Ines had spent eleven years perfecting it. She had corrected Tam's slouching numerals a hundred times with a ruler and a look.

Someone — or something — had been writing down here for decades in the guild's own trained hand.

"No," she said aloud, and the word fell straight down the gallery and did not echo. The walls ate sound the way the ledger ate debts.

She checked the entries anyway, because she was who she was. The nearest column recorded weights. Souls, if the old contract-language held, were weighed in years — and the numbers here ran long. Decades per entry. One column alone totaled more than the entire winter ledger of the Corvane Salt Guild, and the Corvane guild was the largest house in the northern provinces.

The ink was fresh. When she touched it, it came away on her fingertip, wet and black and smelling faintly of the same cheap incense.

Somewhere below, something was still writing.

* * *

The galleries opened without warning into a space that should not have existed.

Mining maps showed solid rock beyond the third deep level; Ines had audited those maps too, had traced their boundaries with her finger and trusted them the way she trusted all clean arithmetic. But the rock ended at a threshold of dressed granite, and beyond it rose a cavern vast enough that her lamp gave out before it found the ceiling. The light simply climbed and thinned and became nothing, as if the dark above were a depth rather than a height.

Stairs descended from the threshold in wide shallow flights, each step bearing an inscription worn nearly smooth. Ines read what she could. Most were contracts. *For rain in the dry year, one daughter's voice. For the passing of the fever, forty days of memory. For a son returned from sea, the remainder of my life, deferred.* Names followed, then signatures — some signed in blood gone brown, one pressed in a child's whole palm.

Payment deferred. That phrase again. Ossa's sealed contract had used it, and Ines had thought it a legal curiosity, an antique formula. Now she saw it carved ten thousand times down a mountainside, and understood it was not a formula. It was a policy. A promise made by something patient enough to wait out generations, accruing interest in the dark.

She descended the stairs because the alternative was to stand at the top forever, and because Tam was somewhere below her, or would be — his sleepwalking feet already finding these same steps in the nights to come. She had tied him to her wrist with a salt-cord and lied to him about why, and the lie sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone. He had believed her. He always believed her. That trust was the only currency she had ever handled that she was afraid to spend.

The stairs ended at the court.

* * *

It convened in silence, which she should have expected, and its silence was worse than noise.

Servitors stood in ranks across the cavern floor — dozens of them, tall and narrow, robed in gray that drank the lamplight. Each wore a mask of white ceramic, featureless except for a single slit where a mouth would be, and none of the slits moved. They did not breathe. She watched for the rise and fall of cloth and found none, and understood that whatever animated them was not interested in the ordinary maintenance of a body. They were less like people than like punctuation — placed at intervals, holding the grammar of the court open, waiting for a sentence to be finished.

Between their ranks lay the ledgers.

They were stacked on stone tables in rows, and they were old in a way that made the guild archive look like a nursery. Some were bound in hide she refused to identify. Some were tablets of fused clay, cracked and re-fused, repaired so many times the repair was the artifact. One, near the aisle, was a slab of slate with entries scratched directly into it and never erased, layer over layer, until the surface had become a palimpsest a finger thick.

Ines walked the aisle because walking was the only thing keeping her legs from deciding otherwise, and she read.

The earliest entries predated the guild by centuries. Pre-dated Corvane's walls. The script shifted — older alphabets, then pictographs, then marks so ancient they were barely language — but the *hand* never changed. The same discipline, the same three-degree lean, the same flat-topped ascenders, sustained across a thousand years of entries. As though the writer had learned its craft once, at the beginning, and simply never stopped practicing.

And the entries were not transactions. That was the thing that turned her awe cold in her chest. She had come expecting bargains — miracle given, soul received, the honest ugliness of commerce. There were bargains here, yes, thousands of them. But between the bargains ran other records. Records of *keeping*.

*Received: the soul of Maren of the Fen Houses, sold 214 years past. Status: stored.*

*Stored.* The word repeated down the columns like a heartbeat. Stored, stored, stored. Not spent. Not consumed, even. Catalogued. Cross-referenced. Each entry carried a location code, and the codes pointed — she followed several, heart hammering — to the walls themselves, to niches cut into the cavern rock in neat grids, thousands of them, each niche holding a stone.

Prayer-stones. She recognized them from the temple stalls, the little river-smoothed rocks the faithful bought for a copper and whispered into before setting them on shrine shelves. Here they filled the walls floor to unseen darkness, tens of thousands of them, and each one, she now understood, was not a prayer at all.

Each one was a person.

"Ah," said Ines, very quietly, to no one. "You've been hoarding."

The word fit with the terrible click of a correct figure entering a balanced column. Her god had not died ten years ago. It had not fallen silent from grief or exhaustion or the slow death of belief. It had gone quiet the way a merchant goes quiet when counting — withdrawn, attentive, lips moving over sums. Ten years of silence while the city above prayed into emptiness, and all the while, down here, the counting continued. The souls it had bought across centuries sat in their niches like coin in a vault, and the god had never spent a single one.

She thought of Tam's grandmother, whose debt had passed down his family like an heirloom nobody wanted. Somewhere in these walls might be the stone that held her. Somewhere in these walls, someday soon, there would be a niche waiting for a boy with quick hands and a crooked grin who sold a year of his life to cover his sister's debt and never knew he was paying down a balance older than he was.

Ines's hands were shaking. She noted this, distantly, the way she noted discrepancies — *irregularity observed, cause under investigation* — and found that the clerk's habit held her together when nothing else would.

* * *

The throne stood at the far end of the court, and she had saved it for last without meaning to. Her eyes had simply kept sliding off it, the way they slid off a figure that wouldn't balance.

It was built of prayer-stones. Fused — melted together into a single mass, thousands of them, their surfaces bubbled and run like candlewax, so that whispers from different centuries bled into one another in the flow-lines. It was roughly chair-shaped. Roughly. The arms were too high, the seat too deep, proportions set for something that had never bothered to learn what bodies wanted. And it was warm. She felt the heat on her face from six paces off, a dry furnace warmth that smelled of incense and hot stone and, underneath, very faintly, of salt.

Of the god itself, seated or standing or otherwise present, she saw nothing at all. Only the sense of attention — vast, unhurried, turned upon her the moment she crossed the threshold and never withdrawn since. It had known she was coming. Perhaps it had written her coming somewhere, in that patient hand, three degrees from vertical.

"You knew I'd audit you eventually," she said. Her voice sounded obscene in the silence, small and human and loud. "That's why the training matches. You taught us. Every clerk, every ledger, every rule of entry — you built the guild as your own filing system. We weren't servants. We were *stationery*."

The warmth pulsed. Nothing answered. Ten years of silence, maintained even now, even here, at the center of its own hoard — and somehow that was the worst violation yet. Not the theft. The refusal even to gloat.

In the nearest table of ledgers, she saw it then: a book newer than the rest, bound in gray leather she knew intimately, because the guild bought its covers from the same tannery. Its pages lay open to a fresh entry, ink still gleaming.

*Tam of Corvane. Age fourteen. Balance outstanding: one year, transferred. Summons issued.*

Below it, in the same hand, a second line, added recently:

*Expected: imminently.*

And below that, a third line, in slightly different ink, as though added after consideration:

*Accompanied.*

The lamp trembled in her grip. She steadied it against the table, took out her own notebook — her working book, the one she kept in her coat, the one with Tam's debts tracked in her own careful hand — and copied the entry line for line. If she could not steal him back tonight, she would at least know the exact size of what she owed and to whom.

When she looked up, the servitors had turned.

All of them. Every white mask in the cavern, oriented toward her with the smooth simultaneity of weathervanes finding a wind. The mouth-slits remained closed. Nothing spoke. But the attention of the room had changed texture — from observation to *assessment*, the way Ossa's gaze changed when a client's collateral came up short.

At the far end of the court, the throne of fused stones brightened. Heat rolled off it in a slow wave, and within the bubbling mass of it, voices surfaced — hundreds of them, thin as threads, prayers rising through the melt like bubbles through pitch. And among them, distinct, clear, horribly familiar, she heard a boy's voice saying her name.

Not Tam's voice. *A recording of* Tam's voice — lifted from some prayer he'd muttered over a shrine-stone years ago, *please keep Ines safe, she works too hard* — preserved in the wax of the throne, replayed now into the warm dark like bait.

The god had heard him pray for her. Had kept it. Had filed it.

Ines closed her notebook with hands that no longer shook, because the shaking had burned off into something colder and more useful.

"I'm coming back down," she told the masks, the throne, the listening dark. "And when I do, I'm bringing an auditor's pen. Everything in this room is about to be marked *void*."

The mouth-slits opened — all of them at once, a cavern-wide intake of breath that was not breath — and from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like a ledger closing, the court answered:

*"Entry accepted."*

Above her, muffled through sixty fathoms of rock, the bells of Corvane began to ring — and did not stop.
