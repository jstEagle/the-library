## 6. Price of Passage

The mountain did not want them.

Ines had known that from the moment she and Tam reached the old mining gate at Vesper's Foot, where the warrens opened like a throat in the hillside. The iron doors stood rusted half-open from some collapse decades past, and the air that breathed out of them was warm and mineral and wrong — the breath of something sleeping with its mouth open. Salt crystals had grown across the threshold in thick white teeth, and where Tam's shadow touched them they darkened, as if the salt remembered being sea.

Tam slept against her shoulder on the cart ride up, his head lolling, his hands twitching in dreams of walking. Even asleep, his feet moved. Small, patient steps against the cart boards, over and over, like a man climbing stairs in his sleep. The salt-cord she'd tied between his wrist and hers two nights ago had worn a red groove into his skin. She'd checked it every hour. She would check it every hour until this was done.

The broker was waiting inside the gate.

She should have expected it. Nothing about the Salt Guild happened without a broker. The woman sat on a folding stool just past the threshold, in the last grey light that reached down from the world above, dressed in guild-grey with her ledger balanced on her knees. She was older than Ossa, or she wore age the way some guild brokers did, borrowed and ill-fitting. Her eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of salt-bleached cloth.

"Vane," she said. Not a greeting. An entry.

"I don't know you."

"You do. You wrote my retirement entry eleven years ago. Marguerite Fell, senior broker, transferred to deep holdings." She smiled without warmth. "Nobody transfers to deep holdings, Vane. They're posted here. To the door. To take payment."

Ines kept Tam's wrist in her left hand and let her right rest near the knife at her hip, though she knew it was useless. Knives were always useless in transactions. "Payment for what? The warrens are abandoned. The guild hasn't mined here since—"

"Since the god went quiet, yes." Marguerite closed her ledger with a soft clap, like a mouth shutting. "And now it's awake. And now everything down there has a price again. The warrens are not abandoned, Vane. They are *occupied*. And passage through occupied territory costs."

"We're guild. I'm sworn—"

"You were sworn." Marguerite's voice didn't change, but something behind it did, some vast patience shifting its weight. "You've read the sealed contracts by now. You know what your oath is worth down there. It's worth nothing. Less than nothing — it's a *claim*, and claims get collected. If you walk in wearing that oath, you walk in as property. Yours will be the easiest soul in the mountain to find."

Tam stirred against her shoulder. She felt him surface toward waking and pressed him gently back down with a hand on his hair, the way she'd done since he was nine and afraid of thunder. He subsided. His feet kept climbing their invisible stairs.

"What does passage cost?" Ines asked.

Marguerite opened her ledger again and turned it around. The page was blank except for one line, written in ink so fresh it shone:

*One impossible thing.*

"The guild's own tariff," Marguerite said. "We taught the mountain how to price things. It learned well. It wants an impossible thing from each traveler. From the boy—" she glanced at Tam with something that might have been pity, in a broker's economy of expressions, "—it has already taken its price. He's paying in installments. But you, Vane. You have to pay at the door."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you wait out here until the summons finishes with him, and in a week you can come collect whatever the throne leaves of him. A husk. A name with no one answering to it." Marguerite tilted her head. "Or you pay, and you walk down there free, unclaimed, and maybe you get him back whole. Your choice. Brokers don't choose for clients. We only make sure the client understands the terms."

Ines looked at the blank page. At the line waiting beneath *one impossible thing*.

"What counts as impossible?"

"Anything the ledger can't hold." Marguerite said it simply, the way you'd explain water to a fish. "Years we can hold. Grief we can hold. Names, debts, sins, first kisses, dying words — all of it fits in books, and anything that fits in a book isn't impossible, it's inventory. The mountain wants something that doesn't fit. Something that leaks."

*

They sat outside the gate while Ines thought, because she refused to negotiate in the dark. The afternoon light came long and amber off the western hills, and Corvane glittered far below in its valley, temple bells ringing again — they hadn't stopped ringing in six days, and the sound carried all the way up here, thin and constant, like insects.

Tam woke properly as the sun touched the ridgeline. He ate the bread and hard cheese she gave him without being asked twice, which frightened her more than anything else had all week. Tam asked twice about food. Tam joked about food. Tam had once eaten an entire wheel of smuggled cheese rather than surrender it to customs, and told the story for a year.

Now he chewed and watched the mountain and said nothing, and his feet tapped their small rhythm against the stone.

"Ines," he said finally. "If I go down there and I'm not me when I come out—"

"You'll be you."

"But if I'm not." He said it patiently, the way you correct a child's arithmetic. "My sister. Lark. She thinks I took work on a river barge. If I'm not me, someone should tell her I wasn't scared. Or — no. Someone should tell her I *was* scared, but I did it anyway. That's better. That's a better story."

Ines found she could not speak. She looked at the salt-teeth on the threshold and thought about ledgers. About entries. About the way a life reduced cleanly to columns: years sold, grief bottled, names pawned. Tam was four lines in her book. Four lines, and none of them held him. None of them could.

"Marguerite wants an impossible thing," she said.

Tam frowned. "From us? What do we even have that's—"

"Me. Not you." She took his chin and turned his face to hers. "Listen. Whatever happens down there, whatever I say, whoever I seem to be — you remember yourself. That's your job. Not the walking, not the fighting. Remembering. Can you do that?"

His eyes were wet and furious and thirteen-going-on-thirty, the eyes of a boy who had been selling pieces of himself since he was ten because the world priced children cheaply. "What are you going to give them?" he whispered.

"Something I won't miss," Ines said, and lied to him with her whole steady voice, and hated herself with a completeness that felt almost holy.

*

Marguerite accepted her at moonrise.

The broker had lit a single candle and set it on the stone beside her stool, and in its light the ledger's blank page seemed to breathe. Ines knelt — not out of reverence, but because kneeling put her head below the level of the salt-teeth, and there was a superstition in that she chose not to examine.

"Name the thing," Marguerite said.

Ines had spent the whole afternoon choosing, turning her life over column by column, looking for the leak. Her mother's face — no, too cruel, and besides, grief fit in bottles; the guild sold it by the dram. Her first kill — no, guilt was common currency, the mountain was drowning in it. Her name — a name was a handle, and she needed her handle.

Then, near dusk, she had understood. There was exactly one thing in her that did not fit anywhere. One thing the guild had never managed to enter, though she'd tried, early on, out of professional thoroughness. One memory that leaked.

"The day I took my oath," she said.

Marguerite's pen stopped moving. For the first time, the old broker looked at her as a person instead of an account. "Vane. That's not a memory. That's the load-bearing wall."

"It's the only thing I own that the books can't hold." Ines heard her own voice from very far away, calm, precise, a broker's voice. "I've tried to write it down three times. Every account comes out wrong. The words change meaning between the remembering and the ink. I swore myself to a silent god in a flooded chapel under the Tanners' Row, and I cannot tell you what I promised or why or what it cost, because every time I reach for it, it runs. Ten years of trying. It's the one impossible thing I have."

"You give it up and you won't know what your oath means. You won't know *why* you serve. You'll be faithful without a reason. Do you understand what that is? That's not a payment, that's an amputation."

"I understand arithmetic," Ines said. "I've done it my whole life. Take the boy's soul out of the equation and tell me what my oath is worth."

Marguerite was quiet a long time. The candle flame leaned and righted itself, leaned and righted, as if listening.

"There's a version of this where I refuse the payment," the broker said at last. "Where I tell you the terms are unconscionable and post you to the deep holdings myself, and you spend the rest of your career stamping receipts in the dark, alive, useless, and the boy goes to the throne alone. That's the prudent entry. That's what Ossa would write."

"Ossa isn't here."

"No," Marguerite agreed. "Ossa is downstairs doing sums, and sums never saved anybody." She turned the ledger around and slid the pen across the stone. "Then pay, Vane. Last chance to walk away."

Ines took the pen.

Here is what she knew, and what she would stop knowing: there had been rain. There had been a chapel under Tanners' Row that flooded every spring, knee-deep in grey water that smelled of tallow and river. There had been other initiates, or there had been no one — even now the memory refused to decide. There had been a moment when she spoke words into the dark and the dark did not answer, and she had understood, in the marrow-deep way you understand cold, that the silence *was* the answer, that she was swearing herself to a god that would never, ever speak to her, and she had knelt in the floodwater anyway and given it everything, gladly, because—

Because—

The reason slipped. It always slipped. It ran through her fingers like the water in that chapel, and she reached for it one last time, greedily, the way you grip a rope over a drop —

"Done," Marguerite said.

The candle guttered. Somewhere far below, in the roots of the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the salt-teeth on the threshold chimed together like struck glass.

Ines knelt on cold stone with a pen drying in her hand and a boy asleep against her shoulder, and she felt — fine. That was the horror of it. She felt entirely fine. There was a hole in her shaped like the most important day of her life, and the hole did not hurt, because you cannot grieve what you cannot miss, and she could not miss it, because she no longer knew there was anything gone. She knew only that she served. The serving remained, load-bearing, with the wall removed. Faith without a floor under it, holding anyway, holding the way a roof holds when you burn the house.

"Why am I crying," she said. It wasn't a question. Her voice came out flat and puzzled, a clerk noting an anomaly.

"Entry fees," Marguerite said softly. "Sometimes they leak." She took the ledger back, and Ines saw, before the page turned, a single new line in her own handwriting — her handwriting, exact and familiar, though she had no memory of writing it:

*Received of Ines Vane: one oath-day, unrecordable. Paid in full. No receipt issued — the item cannot be returned.*

Marguerite stood, folded her stool, and stepped aside from the threshold. Beyond her, the warrens opened downward into the breathing dark, and lamplight — someone had lit lamps down there, recently, many lamps — glimmered along the walls like a procession going deeper.

"Passage is granted," the broker said. "Free transit to the court and back, unclaimed, uncollected. The mountain honors its tariffs." She paused at the edge of the candlelight. "One courtesy, Vane, broker to client. When you stand in front of it — and you will stand in front of it — it will offer you things. It knows what people lack. It's very good at sounding like the answer." Her washed-out eyes were almost kind. "Remember that you paid full price. Don't let it sell you anything else."

*

They walked down through the old workings while Tam slept against her back, tied to her with the salt-cord, his feet still climbing their invisible stairs.

Ines carried a lamp in one hand and her knife in the other and felt nothing where her oath-day used to be, and the nothing was worse than any wound she had ever taken, because wounds announce themselves. This was silence. This was a room in her house she would walk past every day without knowing it was empty.

The tunnels were older than the guild. Older than Corvane, maybe. The pick-marks on the walls were wrong somehow — too regular, too deliberate, as if the miners had been carving letters in a script too large to read from inside. Salt bloomed everywhere, white and glistening, and where the lamplight touched it, the salt threw back reflections that arrived a half-second late.

At the third junction they passed a shrine niche, and Ines stopped, because the niche held a prayer-stone, and the prayer-stone was warm.

She knew it was warm the way you know a hand on the back of your neck. She raised the lamp. The stone was black, fist-sized, shot through with veins of gold like a word written in lightning, and it hummed faintly against her palm when she touched it — a hum she felt in her teeth, in the hollow where her oath-day had been, a hum that recognized her.

*Faithful,* something whispered, from nowhere, from the stone, from the walls, from the deep humming dark below. *Faithful, faithful, come down, come home—*

Ines snatched her hand back and walked faster.

Behind her, in the dark, Tam's sleepwalking feet kept their small patient rhythm against the stone — step, step, step — and ahead of them, far below, the lamps of the court burned in their hundreds, and somewhere among those lights a ledger waited with her name in it, and she was walking toward both, freely, having already paid everything she had that couldn't be counted.

She just wished she knew why her eyes wouldn't stop leaking.

She wiped them on her sleeve, checked the salt-cord, and went down.
