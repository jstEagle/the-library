## 7. Ossa's Arithmetic

Ossa had learned arithmetic from her mother, and her mother had learned it from a famine.

That was the lesson beneath every lesson: numbers were not cold. Numbers were the warmest thing in the world, because they were the only honest way to say *I want you to live*. A merchant who loved her cargo fed it. A guildmaster who loved her people counted them. Everything else — sentiment, loyalty, the sweet noise of the heart — was just arithmetic that hadn't finished yet.

She sat in her office above the counting floor with the sealed contract open between them, and she did the sum out loud, because Ines Vane deserved to hear it done properly. Not softened. Not dressed in mercy. Done properly, the way you'd do it for anyone you respected.

"One soul," Ossa said. "One runner, sixteen years old, already short a year of his life and selling more of it by the season to keep his sister fed. Against that: four hundred souls currently bound in our vaults under contracts the god below has decided to collect. Against *that*: the city above us, where prayers have started answering themselves, and every temple bell in Corvane has been ringing since dawn without anyone pulling the ropes."

Ines said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing. It was one of the things Ossa had hired her for, back when Ines had been a hollow-cheeked girl with an oath-sworn stare and hands that could find an error in a ledger of ten thousand entries by touch alone.

"The god is awake," Ossa continued. "It has woken hungry. It holds Tam's grandmother's contract, and through her, Tam's inheritance of debt. We cannot fight it. We cannot buy it — we tried; Sefa's people got as far as the third warren before the mountain spat them back out with their shadows missing. We cannot run, because Corvane is the only city with a Salt Guild, and the guild is the only thing standing between four hundred desperate families and the open market for souls." She turned the contract page. The old wax seal, stamped with the sigil of silence — a closed mouth over an open hand — caught the lamplight. "So. The arithmetic."

"Don't," Ines said quietly.

"You'll want it anyway, once your temper cools. You always do. You're the best auditor I've ever employed precisely because you can't stand an unfinished sum." Ossa laid her palm flat on the contract. "We honor it. We deliver what was promised. One boy, descended on his own feet, walking to the court beneath the mountain as his grandmother walked before him. In exchange, the god releases its claim on the other four hundred and lets the guild continue its work. That is the trade it has offered. It is, I will note for the record, a *generous* trade, by the standards of gods."

"It's not generous. It's patient." Ines's voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "You've read the older ledgers now. You know what 'honoring' means down there. Tam won't be delivered, Ossa. He'll be *kept*. Fed into that throne stone by stone, the way the others were. His grandmother's soul is still in there — I saw the entry. Forty years, and it hasn't been released or spent. Just held. Hoarded." Her hands were flat on the desk too, mirroring Ossa's, and Ossa thought: *there she is. The girl who matches my posture because I trained her, matching me across a table like a blade matching its whetstone.* "You're not doing arithmetic. You're doing division. You're dividing one boy out of the count because he's small enough to lose."

Ossa let the silence sit. She was better at silence than Ines would ever be; she'd had sixty years of practice, most of them spent burying people.

"Yes," she said finally. "That is exactly what I'm doing. And I will tell you why, and then you will either accept it or you will make me do something I do not want to do."

* * *

The office above the counting floor had one window, and through it Ossa could see the mountain — the old thing, the mined-out thing, the mountain whose insides the guild's founders had hollowed and whose deepest veins the guild had never legally owned but had always, quietly, used. Smoke rose from the city in a hundred thin columns. Somewhere in the middle distance, a church bell rang again, unbidden, and stopped mid-peal as if embarrassed.

"When I was nineteen," Ossa said, "I sold two years of my life to pay my brother's fever-debt. You know this. Everyone knows this; it's in my entry, first page of the founder's roll. What nobody knows is that I also prayed. I knelt in the ash-chapel on Tallow Street and I prayed to the Silent God — the god of the sold and the silent, the god whose whole doctrine was that it heard even the prayers people were too ashamed to speak aloud. Do you know what it did?"

"Nothing," Ines said. "It was already silent by then."

"It did nothing for eleven years," Ossa said. "And then, last week, when the waking started — it answered. Retroactively. A voice in my head, older than any voice, saying: *Your prayer is granted, Guildmaster. Your brother lives.*" She laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all. "My brother has been dead thirty years, Ines. Drowned off the Salt Quay. And this god — this thing we built our ledgers around, whose silence we treated as holiness — reached up out of its hole and *granted* a prayer it had no right to grant, about a man it had no power over, purely because it wanted me to feel *grateful*. Because gratitude is a leash, and it is collecting leashes. Half the city is wearing one now. You've seen the miracles. You've seen what follows them."

"I've seen a drowned child walk out of the river," Ines said. "Alive."

"And her mother now kneels at that riverbank every dawn, praying thanks, and every dawn something in the water prays back, and in a year that woman will be hollowed to a shell with a smiling face, because that is what worship costs when the god doing the eating is starving." Ossa stood. She went to the window so she wouldn't have to watch Ines's face while she said the rest. "The guild survives by being *boring*, Ines. By pricing things fairly and keeping records honestly and never, ever pretending to be holy. The moment we become a temple — the moment we start believing our own incense — we die, and everyone who depends on us dies with us. Four hundred bound souls. Two thousand dependents. Three hundred runners and clerks and salt-hands. That is the number I am responsible for. You are asking me to spend all of it on one boy."

"I'm not asking you to spend anything." Ines rose too. She was smaller than Ossa, always had been, but she filled the room the way frost fills a crack. "I'm asking you to look at the entry properly. Read the clause. The contract says the debt passes down the bloodline — it doesn't say the guild delivers. Delivery isn't our obligation. Collection is the *god's* act, and the god collects through summons, and the summons takes Tam whether we cooperate or not. All the guild has to do to be innocent is nothing. What you're proposing isn't honoring a contract. It's volunteering him. Wrapping him in guild colors and walking him to the door yourself so the god sees how compliant we are. So it likes us."

"So it leaves us alone," Ossa said. "Yes. That is what I am proposing. And you may call it cowardice if it helps you refuse me, but I will call it what it is, which is the only kind of courage I have left at my age: the courage to be hated by the people I protect."

* * *

They had fought before. Over the pricing of grief in the famine years — Ossa had wanted to raise rates to throttle demand, Ines had wanted to cap them and eat the loss. Over Brannoc's expulsion, over the Vellum Street fire, over a dozen smaller wounds. But those fights had been fights inside a shared house, two people arguing about how to keep the same roof standing. This was different. Ossa could feel the difference the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it.

Because Ines wasn't arguing anymore. Ines had gone quiet in the specific way she went quiet when she had finished calculating.

"You've already decided," Ossa said.

"I decided the moment I read Tam's grandmother's name in the sealed ledger." Ines straightened the contract's pages — a small, precise, maddening gesture, tidying the instrument of her own defiance. "You taught me that every entry is a promise, Ossa. Every line in every book we keep is a promise that someone's life, someone's grief, someone's borrowed year was *real*, and will stay real, and won't be erased by whoever holds the pen next. You don't get to teach a girl that for twelve years and then ask her to write a living child into the loss column because the sum comes out neater."

"The sum doesn't come out neater," Ossa said. "The sum comes out *survivable*. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters." She opened the drawer of her desk — the deep drawer, the locked one — and took out the roll. The guild's master roll, the founder's roll, the book of names that predated the charters and would outlast them all. Her name was first. Ines's was forty-seventh, entered in Ossa's own hand on a rainy spring day twelve years ago, beside the notation: *oath-sworn, Silent God, bonded.*

"Ines Vane," Ossa said formally, and her voice did not shake, because she had practiced not shaking for sixty years and she would not waste the practice now. "Before I do this, I am required to offer you the reconsideration. Sit down. Withdraw your objection. Deliver the runner as contracted. Keep your place, your wage, your rooms, your oath, and everything else you have built here. Or refuse — and be struck from the rolls, stripped of guild protection, barred from the vaults and the floors, and rendered, in the eyes of every creditor and collector in Corvane, a woman with no house."

The lamp flickered. Down on the counting floor, faintly, Ossa could hear the night clerks turning pages, the soft industry of honest work, the sound of the world she was about to take away from the best person in it.

"I offered," she said. "For the record, I offered."

"For the record," Ines said, "you did."

Ossa took out her pen. She had struck names from the roll before — dead clerks, absconded thieves, one embezzler she'd personally chased across three rooftops — and each time her hand had been steady, because the roll deserved steadiness. This time her hand was steady too, but for a different reason, and she knew Ines could tell the difference, and hated that she could.

The line went through the name cleanly. Beneath it she wrote the formal notation, because Ines of all people deserved correct procedure even in ruin: *Struck at own insistence. No debt outstanding. No dishonor attached. Guildmaster's note: this entry is false, and I am keeping it anyway.*

"What does that mean?" Ines asked. Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, just enough.

"It means the roll is a ledger," Ossa said, "and I taught you that every entry is a promise. So there's my promise. Whatever you're about to do — and I know exactly what you're about to do, because I would do it too if I were twenty years younger and hadn't already buried everyone I ever loved — whatever you do, no one in this guild will hunt you, price you, or sell what you leave behind. You'll be a ghost to us. Ghosts are cheap, Ines. Cheaper than martyrs." She closed the roll. Her hands, she noticed distantly, wanted very much to tremble, and she denied them. "Now get out of my office before I do something sentimental, like remember that I found you half-starved on the Vellum Street steps and told the recruiters you had good hands."

Ines stood there a moment longer. Ossa kept her eyes on the window, on the mountain, on the smoke columns rising from a city full of answered prayers.

"You could come with me," Ines said.

"No," Ossa said. "Someone has to stay and keep the books honest for the four hundred. That's my post. You have yours." And then, because she was old and the hour was late and the god below was awake and listening, she added, very quietly, in the old ash-chapel cadence they'd both been raised on: "Go well. Come back. Those aren't contradictory instructions, whatever you think."

Footsteps on the stairs. The office door. Gone.

* * *

Ossa worked until the third bell. She always worked until the third bell; sleep was a luxury she had priced out of her own life decades ago and never regretted. She audited the winter ledgers, initialed the grief-leases, corrected a clerk's error in the bottled-sorrow inventory — someone had mislabeled a jar of a widow's mourning as ordinary melancholy, and the difference in price was cruel — and did all of it with perfect precision, because precision was the shape her love took, and there was no one left alive who needed to be told that.

At the third bell she allowed herself one indulgence. She unlocked the deep drawer again and took out not the roll but a smaller book, a private one, kept in her own cipher, updated once a year on the anniversary of her hiring of one Ines Vane.

Today's entry wrote itself almost without her:

*Lost today: one auditor, the finest I ever trained. Cause of loss: honesty, inherited from me. Prognosis: poor. Recommendation: none. Some sums you do knowing the answer will break you, and you do them anyway, and you initial the page, and you go back to work.*

She blotted it. She looked at the mountain through the dark window, where somewhere beneath the stone a throne of fused prayer-stones was growing warmer, and fatter, and more awake.

Then she rang for the night clerk and said, in a voice like closing a ledger: "Send for Sefa. Tell her the guild has a job — off the books, unpaid, deniable. Tell her it involves the guild vaults, and a certain pawned memory, and that the woman who wants it recovered has been struck from the rolls tonight and will therefore need friends."

The clerk hesitated. "Friends, Guildmaster?"

"Friends," Ossa said. "Write it down exactly like that. Every entry is a promise."
