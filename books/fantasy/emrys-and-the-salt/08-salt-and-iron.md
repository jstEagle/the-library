## 8. Salt and Iron

The city had learned to pray loudly again, and I hated it.

I heard it everywhere in the days after Ossa struck my name from the rolls — in the fishmarket, where a woman shouted her dead husband's name at the sky until a gull fell out of the clouds with its feathers turned to salt; in the temple quarter, where the bells no longer rang on schedule because the bell-ringers were too busy weeping to count. Prayer used to be a private thing in Corvane, muttered into collars, folded small as coins. Now it was a bazaar. Everyone shouting their wants upward like the heavens were a shopkeeper who'd finally opened his shutters.

Nobody asked what the gods wanted in exchange. That was the part that kept me awake. Everything in Corvane had a price — I had spent nine years writing those prices down in a fair hand — and now the whole city was spending like the bill would never come.

The bill always came. I had the ledgers to prove it. Or I *had* had them. They were ash now, or close enough, and Tam was asleep in my rented room above the chandler's, tied by a salt-cord to my wrist, breathing slow and even while something under the mountain counted down the distance between him and a throne of fused prayer-stones.

Three nights since the summons began. His sleepwalking took him farther each time. Last night he'd made it to the door before the cord went taut and I woke with my arm nearly wrenched from its socket, and he stood there swaying, eyes open and empty as wells, saying *down, down, it's time to come down* in a voice that was his and wasn't.

So I needed help. Which meant I needed people, which meant I needed the two people in Corvane I trusted least and most: a priest who'd been spat out by his own god, and a thief who'd committed the one crime the Salt Guild never forgave.

* * *

I found Brannoc where I knew he'd be, because disgraced priests are creatures of habit and grief is a kind of liturgy.

The Ashen Chapel sat wedged between a tannery and a shuttered pawnhouse in Lowmoor, and it had been falling down for so long that the falling had become its architecture. Its god was Veyl-of-the-Lamp, keeper of lost things, and for twenty years Brannoc had tended its lamp and swept its floor and prayed in a voice worn smooth as a stair. Then, eleven days ago, Veyl woke up with all the others — and the first thing the god of lost things did upon opening its eyes was look directly at its most faithful servant and forget him.

Not metaphorically. Brannoc told it plainly, the way he told everything, sitting on the chapel steps with his vestments still on because he owned nothing else. "It looked at me," he said. "Twenty years of prayers, and when it finally looked, there was nothing behind its eyes that knew me. Like a man waking and finding a stranger in his bed." He'd been formally unmade — stripped of office by a god who didn't bother with ceremony, just reached into him and took the ordination out the way you'd take a splinter. He said it didn't hurt. He said that was worse.

He was mending the chapel roof when I came up the street, which was the most Brannoc thing imaginable: cast out, forgotten by heaven, and still up a ladder patching shingles for a god that didn't want him.

"You're not supposed to be here," he called down without turning around. He'd always known my footsteps. "Excommunicate grounds. Bad luck."

"I was struck off the guild rolls three days ago. We can be unlucky together."

He came down the ladder slowly. Up close he looked older than his fifty-odd years, gray coming in at the temples like frost claiming a field, but his hands were steady, and I have always trusted steady hands more than steady faces. He studied me the way he used to study penitents — not for guilt but for weight.

"They say the Salt Guild burned its ledgers," he said. "They say a woman walked out of Guildhall with ash in her hair and hasn't been seen since."

"The ledgers deserved burning."

"Everything deserves burning, if you wait long enough. The question is what you build after." He set down his hammer. "You've got the look of someone building something, Ines Vane. Or breaking something. With you it's often both."

"Tam's been sold." Saying it aloud cost more than I expected. My voice came out flat, which is how I pay for things that are expensive. "His grandmother sold her soul to the Silent God forty years ago for rain during the drought. The debt passed down. He's being summoned under the mountain right now, asleep, walking toward it a little more every night, and I need to get there before he does and take him back."

Brannoc was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere below us the tannery vats breathed their sour breath, and the lamp in the chapel window buzzed with moths.

"And you came to me," he said at last, "because you need someone who knows what it's like to be discarded by a god."

"I came to you because you know the old warrens under Grayfall Mountain better than any living soul. You mapped them for Veyl, hunting lost things. And because—" I stopped. Honesty is a currency too, and I've learned to spend it deliberately. "And because you'll understand why I'm doing it. Everyone else thinks I should let him go. Honor the contract. It's arithmetic, they say. But you spent twenty years praying to something that was eating your devotion like bread, and you didn't walk away when it forgot you. You're still fixing its roof. So you know this isn't arithmetic."

"It's not," he agreed softly. "It's love wearing work clothes." He looked back up at the chapel, at the lamp he still kept lit for a god that had erased him from its memory. "You know what the worst part is? I'd forgive it. If it looked at me again — really looked — I'd forgive it in a heartbeat. That's the trap they build us in. They make the hunger mutual."

"That's why I'm not asking you to come for theology," I said. "I'm asking you to come for Tam. He's fifteen. He sells years of his life to cover his sister's debts and calls it helping. He laughs at his own jokes before he finishes them. He once carried a bottled grief across the city in a storm rather than risk cracking it, and cried the whole way, and delivered it dry." My throat tightened. I loosened it by force. "He trusts me completely. Right now I'm lying to him about why there's a cord on his wrist. I intend to stop lying as soon as lying stops being necessary, and until then I need people beside me who won't flinch."

Brannoc picked up his hammer, weighed it, then set it down again — a man setting down an old life.

"Veyl took my ordination," he said, "but it couldn't take what I learned earning it. I know the names of the things in the deep warrens. I know which doors open for prayer and which open for blood and which don't open at all. And I know the rites of severance — the old ones, from before the guilds made contracts of them. If there's a way to cut a soul loose from a throne of prayer-stones without killing the body it belongs to, it runs through me." He met my eyes. "But hear the price, Ines. If Veyl wakes fully — if these answered prayers keep spreading — I will meet my god again down there or up here, and I don't know what I'll do. I might kneel. Twenty years is twenty years."

"Then I'll be standing next to you when you decide," I said. "That's the whole offer."

He almost smiled. It was a ruined thing, mostly scar tissue, but it moved. "Give me an hour to pack. And Ines — bring salt. More than you think you need. Under the mountain, salt isn't a seasoning. It's a language."

* * *

Sefa was harder.

Finding her was easy — she lived, when she lived anywhere, in the rafters of the Gullwing Theatre, above the costume rooms, in a nest of stolen blankets and borrowed candlelight. Getting her to listen was another matter, because Sefa had a policy about me, and the policy was: run.

She saw me coming up the theatre stairs and I watched her calculate the exits with the fluid ease of long practice. There was only one exit. She sighed and stayed put, arranging herself on her windowsill with theatrical boredom, though her dark eyes never stopped moving over my face, reading me like a lock.

"Salt Guild," she said. "Come to arrest me? Bit late. Also bit unemployed, I hear."

"I don't work for the guild anymore."

"Everyone says that right before they arrest you." She swung her legs. She was maybe twenty-five, quick-boned and sharp-faced, with ink stains on her fingers from forging things and grease stains on her knuckles from picking things, and she wore three rings she absolutely hadn't paid for. "What do you want, Vane?"

"Nine years ago you stole a year of your own life back from the guild vault."

Something flickered behind the boredom. "Allegedly."

"There's no allegedly about it. Vault Seven, winter inventory, one sealed year belonging to Sefa Ilvari, aged nineteen, sold to pay her mother's fever-debt. Gone by morning. No forced entry, no broken wax, no trace. The only theft in guild history that left the ledger itself intact and the thief invisible." I stepped closer. "I audited that loss four times. I never found you. Do you know why that mattered to me? Because everyone else who sold a year — and there were hundreds, Sefa, hundreds of desperate people in those pages — everyone else stayed sold. You're the only one who ever got yourself back."

"So you've come to admire me. Lovely. Send word to my fans."

"I've come to ask how."

The theater creaked around us. Downstairs, stagehands dragged scenery across boards, and the sound came up through the floor like distant surf.

"How," she repeated.

"How you did it. Because the Silent God holds Tam's soul in a master ledger older than anything the guild ever built, bound inside a throne of prayer-stones, guarded by servitors, behind a tollgate that eats true names. And I am going to take him back anyway. And the only person alive who has ever unwritten a soul-contract and survived it is currently sitting on a windowsill pretending not to care." I held her gaze. "I need the method, Sefa. Not the theory. The actual hands-on knowledge of how you break a seal that was made in good faith by someone desperate, without destroying what it holds."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she slid off the sill and crossed to a loose board in the floor, and from beneath it she drew out a small tin box, and from the box a strip of pale parchment, and she held it out to me without letting go.

I read it. My stomach turned over slowly, the way it does on stairs in the dark.

It was a contract. Her contract. One year of life, seller's mark pressed in thumb-blood, buyer's sigil the closed eye of the Silent God. Except the seal had been altered — microscopically, beautifully altered. Someone had rewritten the terms in a hand so fine it passed for the original ink, changing *in perpetuity* to *held in trust*, changing *payment rendered* to *payment deferred*. A year sold forever had become a year merely stored. And stored things can be reclaimed.

"You forged the god's handwriting," I said.

"I forged the *clerk's* handwriting," she corrected. "Gods don't write. Clerks write. Gods just collect." She tapped the parchment. "Here's what nobody in your precious guild understood, Vane, because you were all too busy believing in the sanctity of the books: a contract is just words, and words were made by mouths before they were ever made by pens. Change the words, change the truth. The seal doesn't check whether the contract is *moral*. It checks whether the contract is *consistent*. I made it consistent with a different world — one where my mother's debt had been paid some other way, where that year was only ever in the vault on loan. The ledger believed me. Ledgers always believe a well-told story, because that's all they are."

"And the god didn't notice."

"The god was asleep." Her voice dropped, and for the first time the performance fell away entirely, and underneath it she was just a nineteen-year-old who had done an unthinkable thing alone in the dark and never told anyone. "That's the thing nobody says out loud. All those souls in the vault, all those years and griefs and names — the Silent God never once checked on them. It hoarded us like a miser counts coins through a locked door. It never *looked*. So yes, I stole from a god, and it worked, because the god's whole nature is not-looking." She finally released the parchment into my hands. "But your mountain god is awake now. Awake things look. So whatever you're planning, you'll need to make your lie faster than its attention. That's the whole craft, Vane. Theft is just speed plus story."

I looked down at the altered contract, at the fine hand that had out-lied a god, and felt the first thing in three days that wasn't dread. It wasn't quite hope. Hope is a debt you take on against uncertain collateral. This was smaller and sturdier than that. It was the feeling of finding, in a burned ledger, one page that survived.

"Come with me," I said. "Under the mountain. I can't forge a god's hand. Brannoc knows the warrens and the severance rites. Between the three of us there might be a version of this where Tam walks out."

"Why would I go near that place?" Sefa said. "I got out. That's the entire point of me. I'm the one who got *out*."

"Because there's a boy down there who never sold anything in his life except a year to keep his sister fed, and the debt wasn't even his." I put her contract back in her hands and closed her fingers over it. "Because you know better than anyone in this city what it is to be a line item. And because—" I paused, and decided to spend the last of my honesty. "Because I'm going to need people, Sefa. After. Whatever happens down there, I burned my ledgers and my oath and my name in the guild book, and what's left of me is going to need a family that isn't written down anywhere. I'm assembling one out of the discarded. A disgraced priest. A thief. A clerk with no guild. And a runner who laughs at his own jokes. It's not much of a company. But it's the only one recruiting."

Sefa stared at me. Outside, far off toward the temple quarter, a great shout went up — joy or terror, impossible to tell anymore, the two sounds had merged lately — and bells began ringing wildly, unscheduled, unsanctioned, and neither of us looked toward the window because we'd both already heard enough bells to last several lifetimes.

"You're crying," she said, with genuine surprise.

"No," I said, and wiped my face. "It's the salt air."

"Right." She looked at the contract in her fist for a long moment. Then she shoved it in her coat, grabbed a satchel, and started stuffing it with picks and ink and a coil of thin wire and, inexplicably, a birdcage. "Fine. Fine! But I want it noted that I said this was insane, and when we die under a mountain, I get the last word in the story. 'She was right,' you'll write. Those exact words."

"Noted," I said.

We went down the theatre stairs together, and out into streets gone strange with answered prayer, where somewhere a man was levitating three feet above the fountain square while the crowd screamed, and the light was starting to do wrong things at the edges, gold where there should have been dusk, and Brannoc was waiting at the corner with a packed bag and a face like a man walking back into a fire he'd sworn off.

Above Grayfall Mountain, faint as a heartbeat, the ground hummed.

Tam, tied to my wrist in a room above the chandler's, slept on — and tonight, I knew, the cord would pull sooner, and harder, and the voice in his sleeping mouth would say *down* a little louder.

We had perhaps two nights. The gods of Corvane were awake, and hungry, and careless, and the city was beginning to burn with wishes — and we were going to walk into the middle of the oldest hunger of all and steal from it.

"Speed plus story," Sefa said, hopping a puddle that reflected no stars.

"Salt and iron," said Brannoc.

I said nothing. I was counting steps, and saving my breath, and holding in my mind the image of a boy laughing at his own joke, unfinished, waiting for me to finish it for him.
