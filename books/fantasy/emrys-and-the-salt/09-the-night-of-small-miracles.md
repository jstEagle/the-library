## 9. The Night of Small Miracles

Brannoc had prayed for twenty years, and on the night his god finally answered, the answer was a fire.

Not a real fire — not yet. A fire in the way a match is a fire: small, struck, held to something dry. He was standing in the doorway of the Ashen Chapel on Candlemaker's Row, where he had once been rector and was now, by decree of the Synod, nothing at all, watching a woman kneel at the scorched altar rail and ask Saint Vellin — no, not Vellin anymore; the icon above her had shed its saint's face like a mask slipping — ask *the god* to heal her son's lung-rot.

The god healed it.

Brannoc heard it happen. There was a sound like a bell being rung underwater, and the boy, who had come in wheezing with that wet whistle in his chest that Brannoc knew from twenty years of parish work, suddenly breathed. Cleanly. All the way down. The boy laughed, startled by his own lungs, and the mother made a sound Brannoc had never heard a human being make before, some raw syllable that wasn't gratitude or prayer or language at all, just the noise a body makes when its worst fear is amputated.

The congregation went mad.

That was the word for it. Not joy — joy has structure, joy has liturgy. This was madness, forty people surging toward the altar with their hands out, screaming their wishes over each other, a fishmonger demanding his drowned nets be mended, an old man asking for his dead wife back, a girl of maybe ten shouting that she wanted wings, *wings, please, I want to fly*, and the god — whatever wore the chapel now, whatever had crawled up out of the silence where Saint Vellin's patience used to live — began granting them.

The nets mended. Brannoc watched it: rotten hemp knitting itself whole with a smell like lightning and tar. The old man's wish it did not grant, and the old man collapsed against a pillar weeping, and nobody comforted him because everyone else was too busy receiving. The girl with the wings — the god gave her wings. Small ones, grey and membranous, bursting through the seams of her coat with a sound like wet paper tearing, and she screamed, and then she laughed, and then she rose six feet off the flagstones, flapping, graceless as a gull in a gale, while her mother clawed at her ankles trying to pull her down.

*This is what you wanted,* Brannoc told himself. *Twenty years. This is what you begged for.*

He had prayed every day of his priesthood for the gods to wake. He had prayed it into his knees and his knuckles and the backs of his eyes. *Wake up. Look at us. We are drowning down here and you are sleeping.* He had believed — he had built his whole life on the belief — that the silence was grief, or weakness, or some divine wound the faithful were meant to help heal through devotion. That if enough voices called, something would turn over in its sleep and open its eyes and *see us*, and the seeing would be salvation.

The god in the Ashen Chapel did not see anyone. It granted. The distinction took him until midnight to understand, and when he understood it, he understood it the way you understand a knife: all at once, and from the inside.

* * *

By the second hour past midnight, Corvane was burning in small places.

Brannoc walked because he could not stay still. The streets were full of people running — running toward things, mostly, which was worse than running away. Toward temples. Toward shrines. Toward the little household altars in doorways where salt dishes had sat empty for a decade, now piled high with offerings flung there by passersby who didn't even know which god they were feeding. A man knelt in the middle of Tanner's Way with his hands pressed flat to the cobbles, sobbing, *I take it back, I take it back,* over and over, though what he was taking back Brannoc never learned, because the man was surrounded by a crowd laying hands on him, each one whispering a wish into his ear as if he were a door.

At the Saltmarket, a shrine to the Tide-Mother had cracked open along its seam like an egg, and the water inside — holy water, stagnant for years, thick with algae — was moving. Rising. Shaping itself into a figure that stood in the niche and looked out at the crowd with eyes made of current, and the crowd did not run. The crowd *cheered*. They pushed forward with their sick and their debts and their grudges, holding up infants, holding up contracts, holding up locks of hair from people they wanted found or forgotten, and the Tide-Mother — or the thing wearing the Tide-Mother's hunger — reached out with a hand of moving water and touched them, one after another, and each one it touched got something.

A goiter vanished. A lame leg straightened. A drunkard's craving lifted out of him like a splinter drawn clean, and he stared at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, and perhaps they did now.

And one woman — Brannoc saw this clearly, would see it clearly for the rest of his life — one woman asked the water-figure to bring back her husband, lost off a fishing boat three winters past, and the Tide-Mother smiled with a mouth of ripples and said, in a voice like a wave withdrawing over shingle:

*Bring me more.*

The woman didn't understand. Brannoc did. He had spent twenty years learning the grammar of gods, and even silent ones spoke it, and what the thing in the shrine had just said was not a blessing. It was an invoice.

*They're starving,* he thought, standing in the press of bodies with the cold coming off the shrine-water like a grave's breath. *All of them. Every god in this city woke up starving.*

It made a horrible kind of arithmetic. Ten years of silence. Ten years of prayers unanswered, offerings unclaimed, faith curdling into habit and habit into resentment. The temples had emptied by degrees — first the rich, who found better returns elsewhere, then the devout, worn down by futility, until only the desperate remained, and desperation is thin fare. Whatever the gods fed on, they had been fasting for a decade. And now they were awake, and awake things are hungry, and worship —

Worship is meat.

He could see it now that he knew to look. Every miracle he'd witnessed tonight had come with a pull, a drawing-in, the way a leech swells. The healed boy's mother hadn't just received a miracle; she had poured something into the altar as she knelt, some bright thread of herself, and the god had swallowed it. The winged girl was leaking awe from every pore, and the god drank it through the air itself. Even the crowd's terror at the wings — terror is a vintage, Brannoc thought bitterly; the old texts say so, terror and ecstasy are the two great vintages — even that was being sipped.

Granting wishes carelessly wasn't generosity. It was chumming the water.

* * *

He ended up, without deciding to, at the steps of the Cathedral of the Ninefold Chorus, where the riots had already begun.

It was not a riot of anger, at first. That was the terrible thing. It began as a riot of need. Thousands of people packed against the cathedral doors — the doors had been barred from inside hours ago, the clergy overwhelmed, the Ninefold Chorus waking in nine voices at once and each voice granting indiscriminately, and the cathedral nave had become a slaughterhouse of answered prayer. Brannoc heard the stories secondhand, shouted between strangers in the crowd: a man whose wish for wealth had been granted as gold teeth erupting through his gums until he choked; a child asked to be "big" and grown four feet in an hour, skin split at the joints; a widow who wished to sleep and had not woken, breathing slow and deep and smiling, and her family could not rouse her, and the priests said her soul was being *counted*.

The crowd outside didn't disperse. The crowd outside pressed harder. Because half of them had seen miracles tonight, real ones, and the other half had seen those miracles' price, and both halves wanted in, and the doors were barred, and someone threw a rock, and then it was a riot of the older kind after all.

Brannoc did not throw rocks. He climbed instead — he was fifty-one years old and his knees informed him of this at length — up the flank of the pilgrims' stair to the ledge beneath the cathedral's rose window, where he had once stood in vestments on feast days, and he looked down at ten thousand upturned faces lit by torchlight and burning shrines, and he did the only thing left in his repertoire.

He preached.

"You don't know what you're feeding!" His voice cracked on the second word and he pushed through it, twenty years of homiletics carrying him the way a current carries driftwood. "You think these are gifts! You think your gods have come back out of love! Listen to me — I served the Chorus for thirty years, I gave it my whole life, and last night I called it by name and it did not know me!"

The crowd below surged and roared. Some of them were screaming at him to shut up. Some were screaming at him to keep going. A few — a handful, faces upturned, torchlit — had gone very still, and it was to those he leaned.

"I prayed for twenty years," he said, and his voice broke properly this time, and he let it. "Twenty years I knelt on stone floors and asked the gods to wake. And do you know what I learned tonight, standing in a chapel while a god handed out miracles like a drunkard hands out coins? It doesn't know our names. Any of them. It knows our *hunger* — that's all it ever knew about us. That's all any of them know. We are not children to them. We are *crops*."

Someone hurled a bottle. It burst against the stair below him, and he didn't flinch, because he had been stoned before, in the bad years, in the plague summers, when the faithful needed somewhere to put their grief and the priest was the nearest wall.

"The miracles are real," he called down. "Take them if you must — I won't lie to you, they're real, I've seen a boy breathe tonight who should have died by spring. But know what you're paying. Know that every answered prayer is a hook with you on it. Know that when the god says *bring me more*, it does not mean bring me more love."

Below him, at the edge of the torchlight, a figure detached itself from the crowd and stood looking up at him. Small, hooded, arms crossed. Even at that distance and in that light, Brannoc recognized the set of the shoulders — he'd seen those shoulders hunched over guild ledgers two nights ago, in a back room that smelled of salt and ink, when a mercenary named Ines Vane had told him in a flat voice that his god had woken and rejected him, and that hers might be next, and that if he wanted to matter to anything again he should come find her when the city started screaming.

The city was screaming.

Ines Vane raised one hand — not waving, exactly. Beckoning. Then she turned and walked away into the mob, unhurried, certain he would follow.

Brannoc looked back at the crowd, at the rose window glowing above him with a light that had no warmth in it, at the barred doors behind which nine voices were counting souls like coins. Twenty years of prayer, and here was the answer: not a god who knew his name, but a woman who did.

He climbed down, and followed her into the smoke.

* * *

She led him through alleys he half-knew, away from the burning shrines, to a shuttered dye-house near the river where the smell of old mordant covered everything. Inside, by the light of a single shielded lamp, a thief named Sefa was counting knives, and a boy — Tam, the runner, the one Ines had spoken of with a ferocity she wore like armor — lay asleep on a pallet with a cord of braided salt tied from his wrist to hers.

"He walks in his sleep now," Ines said, without preamble. She looked exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "Toward the mountain. Every night a little farther before I can turn him around." She glanced at Brannoc, and her eyes went briefly to the salt-cord, and he understood that she was showing him the truth of her situation so he would trust her with his own. "Your god woke. It rejected you. Tell me what happened. All of it. Leave nothing out — especially the parts that shame you."

So Brannoc sat on a dye-stained bench in a dying god's city and told her. About the chapel. About the wings. About the Tide-Mother saying *bring me more*. About standing on the cathedral stairs shouting harvest metaphors at a mob. About twenty years of kneeling, and the specific texture of being unknown by the thing you had given your life to.

When he finished, Sefa snorted without looking up from her knives. "Gods don't love anybody. I've been telling people that for years. Nobody tips a thief for theology."

"They loved once," Brannoc said. "Or something like it. The old texts—"

"The old texts were written by priests." Sefa flipped a knife, caught it. "No offense."

"Some taken."

"Quiet," Ines said, and they quieted, and she looked at Brannoc for a long moment with those ledger-keeper's eyes that weighed everything and showed nothing. "Here's what I believe," she said finally. "The gods fasted for ten years. Now they're awake and eating everything in reach, and they'll eat carelessly until they're strong enough to eat carefully. Cities have died this way. Not destroyed — *consumed*. Worshipped to death." Her hand moved, unconsciously, to the salt-cord at her wrist. "I'm going under the mountain to take something back from one of them. I can't do it alone. You hate them right now, which means you're clear-eyed. And you know their grammar."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then walk home carefully," Ines said. "Avoid the shrines. Don't wish for anything out loud. And when your god calls your name someday — and it will, when it's grown strong enough to bother learning names — don't answer."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a temple bell began to ring — not the measured toll of liturgy but a wild, arrhythmic clamor, as if the bell itself were praying. Brannoc felt it in his teeth. Twenty years he had begged the heavens for a sign, and now the sky over Corvane was full of them, and every sign was a mouth.

He looked at the sleeping boy, at the salt-cord, at the woman who had crossed a burning city to collect him specifically.

"When do we start?" he said.
