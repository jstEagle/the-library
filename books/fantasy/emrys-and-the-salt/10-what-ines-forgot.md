## 10. What Ines Forgot

Sefa came back at the hour when Corvane's bells had stopped meaning anything — when the city had rung itself hoarse with answered prayers and now rang only to hear its own voice. She came through the workshop window rather than the door, because Sefa had never in her life entered a room the way it asked to be entered, and she was carrying a lead-lined strongbox against her chest like a sleeping child.

"Vault three," she said, setting it on Ines's worktable among the ink pots and the salt. "Under the winter tallow contracts. Ossa's people didn't even look at me. They're all down at the Temple of the Tide watching a statue weep honey." Her grin flickered and went out. "It's heavier than I thought it would be."

Ines did not touch it.

The box sat there, dull as a coffin nail, and she found that her hands had gone still in the way they went still over a ledger line that refused to balance. Ten days since she'd been struck from the rolls. Ten days since Ossa's pen had drawn the line through her name with the same unhurried stroke he used for everything, and she had walked out of the guildhall carrying nothing but Tam's salt-cord, frayed now from his sleepwalking, and the memory of his voice saying *I'll walk awake, Ines. I'd rather.*

"You don't have to open it," Sefa said. She perched on the table's edge, close but not touching, which was Sefa's particular genius — knowing exactly how much of a person could bear to be near another person. "We could burn it sealed. Brannoc's got fire enough these days to spare."

"No." Ines heard her own voice come out flat, ledger-flat, and hated it a little. "No. I need to know what I paid."

*What I paid.* Not what was taken. That was the word the guild used, the word she had written ten thousand times in her own careful hand: *paid*. As if the vault were a shop and memory a commodity like any other. As if somewhere in that box was the day she became herself, folded up small enough to fit under lead.

Sefa slid it across. "I'll stay," she said, "if you want. Or I'll go. Whichever one you'd never ask for."

Ines almost smiled. "Stay."

The lock was guild-work, old pattern, and her fingers knew it without consulting her — three turns left, one right, press where the maker's mark should be. The lid came up on a smell she hadn't expected: cold stone and lamp oil and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a church. Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age, lay a stoppered vial of gray glass no bigger than her thumb, and beneath it, flat as a pressed flower, a single sheet of vellum.

She knew the vellum before she knew anything else about it. Guild stock. The heavy grade, the kind reserved for oaths and death-entries and other things meant to outlast their writers. And the handwriting on it —

The handwriting on it was hers.

Not hers-now. Hers-then, younger, the letters still carrying the stiffness of a hand trained by someone else, each character built rather than grown. She had seen that early script in the oldest ledgers, in the entries copied by apprentices long promoted or long dead. She had admired it once, the discipline of it. Now she looked at her own name at the bottom of the page and felt the floor tilt gently, the way a ship tilts when you realize the water underneath is deeper than the rope is long.

"Ines," Sefa said quietly. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing." She wasn't, quite. She unstoppered the vial instead, because her hands wanted a task, and held it to the lamplight. The memory inside moved like smoke in water, slow and silver-gray, and when she tilted it, it did not pour. It clung to the glass the way grief clings. "This is it? This little thing?"

"That's it. Vault-master's tag said *oath-day, full extraction, donor consented.*" Sefa's mouth twisted around the last word. "Consented. Like anyone consents to having themselves scooped out."

Ines set the vial down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. "Why can't I take it back?"

"Brannoc asked the same about mine, when I got my year home." Sefa picked at a thread on her sleeve. "He says a memory kept outside you too long goes... stale isn't the word. It goes *foreign*. Your body forgets it was ever yours. Take it back all at once and it doesn't knit — it rejects. He watched a man in Vell's parish try it after twenty years pawned. Took the whole day back in one swallow and forgot how to speak for a month. Forgot his wife." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something; Ines saw it land in her shoulders and stay there. "You feed it back in pieces. Drops. Weeks of drops. And even then—" She stopped.

"Even then what?"

"Even then you get it back changed. It sat in the dark next to other people's pawns for years. Memories leak. Yours has been marinating in a vault full of sold souls' worth of *want*." Sefa met her eyes. "You might open that vial and find the day you took your oath wearing somebody else's face."

Outside, a bell rang wrong — three strokes where four belonged, then silence, then a peal like laughter. The city had been doing that all week. Prayers answering themselves in the streets, small miracles stacking up like driftwood before a flood, and underneath it all the mountain humming a note only dogs and debtors could hear. Tam slept in the back room with the salt-cord looped twice around his wrist and Ines's own wrist through the other end, though he hadn't sleepwalked in two days now, and somehow that was worse. A summons that stops pulling usually means it no longer needs to pull.

She turned to the vellum instead. Reading was safer than drinking. Reading was what she was *for*.

---

*In the tenth year of the Silence,* the page began, *before the witness-stone of the Salt Guild of Corvane, the undersigned does swear herself body, breath, and name to the god who keeps no court and speaks no word, whose throne is beneath the mountain, whose hunger is patient—*

Her eyes snagged on *hunger*. She read it again. *Whose hunger is patient.* She had taken an oath at nineteen years old and the oath itself had called her god hungry, and she had signed beneath it anyway, and in eleven years of faithful service she had never once read her own contract back.

Because the guild didn't do that. The guild wrote the entry and shelved the entry and trusted the entry, and trust was the whole architecture, and she had been the architecture's most devoted mason.

*The undersigned offers service until death or dissolution,* she read, *in exchange for the office of Ledger-Keeper, and for the following consideration:*

There was a line ruled across the page there, a deliberate break, the way contracts broke before the ugly part. Her younger self had copied the terms in a fair hand, term after term, and Ines read them the way you read a diagnosis.

*First: the keeping of the guild's books, that no debt be lost, no sale forgotten, no soul unpriced.*

*Second: the silence of the keeper, who shall not speak of the god's nature, the god's terms, or the god's accounts to any soul unsworn.*

*Third:* 

Third was longer. Third had been written smaller, cramped against the margin, as if even her nineteen-year-old hand had tried to make it take up less room.

*Third: before the oath is sworn and sealed, the undersigned shall surrender into the guild's keeping all memory of those persons to whom she stands bound by love, so that her service may be undivided. This surrender is permanent. The names shall not be restored to her. The god requires a keeper with clean hands and an empty heart, that no earthly claim outrank the ledger.*

Below it, in the same cramped script, a list.

Seven names.

She knew them the way you know a song you can't place — a rhythm without words. Seven lines of her own handwriting, seven names she had written out at nineteen and then been made to forget writing, and she stared at them and felt nothing, and the nothing was the most violent thing she had ever felt.

"Sefa." Her voice came out strange. "Read this to me. Out loud. All of it."

Sefa took the page. Ines watched her face while she read — watched the third clause land, watched her lips move over the seven names, watched her look up with an expression Ines had no practice decoding because no one had ever worn it at her before. Pity, maybe. Or its cousin, grief on behalf of someone else.

"These are your people," Sefa said. "Your mother. Two brothers. A girl — there's a word scratched out next to her name, looks like it said *beloved*, and then someone crossed it out and wrote *friend* instead, and then crossed that out too." She turned the page toward the light. "There's a note in the margin. Same young hand. It says: *I don't understand why this hurts yet.*"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below the street, the mountain hummed.

"I don't remember them," Ines said. It was important to say it plainly, to put the fact on the table like a coin. "I don't remember a mother. I don't remember brothers. When I try to reach for my childhood I get the orphanage at Saltrow and then fog, and I always assumed the fog was just — poverty. Bad feeding. Children forget." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "Children forget. Yes. That's what happened here. A child forgot, on command, permanently, and then grew up into a woman who loves people so hard it frightens her, and never once wondered why loving feels like breaking a rule."

Tam. The thought arrived with the force of a door slamming. *Tam.*

"Sefa. When did I meet Tam?"

Sefa blinked. "What?"

"When did I meet Tam. Think. You've known us both three years. Someone must have told you, or you asked, because you ask about everything —"

"You told me he followed you home from the river district during the fever year. You said he was fourteen and stealing bread off your windowsill and you caught him and fed him instead." Sefa paused. "That's the story you tell. Why?"

"Because it's the story I *have*." Ines stood, and the room swayed, and she gripped the table. "Don't you see it? The god demanded I forget everyone I loved. Everyone. So either Tam came after the oath, or —" Her voice failed and restarted lower. "Or he didn't. Or he was on some list, some later list, some amendment, and I don't know it because I don't know what I've already surrendered. How many times, Sefa? How many times did I walk down into that vault and pay? I keep books for a living. I know every entry I've ever written. But I don't know the entries written *about me*."

The strongbox. She wrenched it fully open, dumping the wool out, running her fingers along the seams the way she'd been taught to check false bottoms in confiscated goods. Nothing. But the vault itself — vault three, under the winter tallow — Sefa had said the tag said *oath-day, full extraction*. One tag. One vial. But vaults had indexes, and indexes had cross-references, and if there was one extraction there could be —

"There are more," she said aloud, and heard how it sounded: not a question. A finding. "There are more extractions under my name. There have to be. Nobody surrenders seven people in one payment — it would kill the collateral value. The guild prices in installments. The god prices in installments. I've been paying in installments for eleven years and calling it devotion."

Sefa had gone very still, the stillness she wore when she was casing a room. "Ines. If that's true, then everything you remember —"

"— is what was left," Ines finished. "Everything I am is what was left over after the god took its share. My precision. My patience. The way I can hold a whole ledger in my head and feel nothing about any of it. I thought that was discipline. I thought I *built* that." She looked down at her hands, the ink-stained, steady, capable hands, and for the first time in her life they looked like someone else's hands, tools shaped by a craftsman she'd never met. "I was curated, Sefa. Like a collection. Like a shelf. It reached into me and kept what was useful and shelved the rest, and then it gave me a job that made me proud of the shape it left."

The vertigo came then, properly, the way Sefa had warned it lived in this room. Not dizziness of the body — dizziness of the ground itself. Every memory she owned suddenly required provenance. The night she'd pulled Tam out of the fever — real, or issued? Her first ledger, the one she'd slept with under her pillow like other girls slept with sweethearts — hers, or assigned? The quiet pride she took in a balanced column, the closest thing she had to joy — was that Ines Vane, or was that the god's ideal servant, installed?

And underneath all of it, colder than the rest: *the god knows exactly what it removed. It kept the receipts. It could give them back.*

That was the trap she could feel closing even now, gentle as family. Because she wanted them. She wanted the seven names to mean something, wanted the mother and the brothers and the girl who had been beloved and then friend and then nothing, wanted the fog rolled back, wanted *herself* the way a drowning woman wants air — and the thing beneath the mountain had all of it in a vault, priced and waiting, and gods that woke hungry did not give gifts. They made offers.

"It offered Tam his grandmother's debt forgiven," she said quietly. "Down in the dark, before I cut the audience short. It offered me things too. I didn't listen. I was in a hurry." She picked up the vial, weighed it in her palm. Gray smoke, silver light. The day she became a servant, pickled and portable. "If I drink this back drop by drop, I get the oath-day. I get whatever the vault let leak. And every drop passes through a thing that wants something from me."

"So don't drink it," Sefa said simply.

"I have to read it, though. All of it. Because there's a binding clause somewhere — Brannoc thinks the master ledger below runs on oath-pages, that ours are the roots of it, and if mine is a root then I need to know what's written in the wood." She set the vial down beside the vellum, side by side, the liquid and the letter, the thing she'd paid and the proof of payment. "And I need to know the seven names. Not to take them back. To write them down. Somewhere it can't collect them."

Sefa slid off the table and fetched the ordinary blank book from the shelf — the plain one, the cheap one, the one Ines had started keeping the night the guild struck her out, because a person struck from every roll needed somewhere to exist. Ines opened it to a fresh page. Dipped her pen. Her hand, traitorously, hesitated.

"What if I copy them wrong?" she asked, and heard how young she sounded. "What if I misread my own handwriting and write down a stranger's mother, a stranger's brother, and some dead woman's real name never gets remembered by anyone ever again?"

"Then you write them the best you can," Sefa said, "and you put a mark by them, and you spend the rest of your life finding out. That's all any of us do with the people we've lost. We keep the record and we check our work." She tapped the page. "Start with the girl. Start with *beloved*."

Ines bent over the book. The lamp leaned close. And in her own present hand, older and harder-won than the script above it, she copied the first of the seven names, and then the second, and her eyes burned and she did not know — could never again be sure she would know — whether the tears were grief for women she'd lost or rage at the thief who'd taken them, and understood that this uncertainty was itself the wound, the exact shape of it, the god's signature cut into her.

She was three names down when the salt-cord, hanging on its hook by the door, snapped clean through.

They both heard it. The little sound of braided salt fibers parting, soft as a bone breaking under a blanket. Ines was moving before the cord hit the floor, and behind her she heard Sefa say, very calmly, the way people talk when the arithmetic has already come out bad:

"The mountain stopped waiting. It's taking him back tonight."

Ines grabbed her coat, the vial, the vellum, the ordinary book — all of it, everything she was and everything she'd been robbed of — and ran for the back room where Tam slept with his eyes open, smiling at the ceiling, already walking.
