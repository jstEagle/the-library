## 11. Descent

Tam woke with salt on his lips and his feet already moving.

That was the worst part — not the dream, not the voice like a bell rung underwater, but the waking and finding his body had not waited for him. He was three steps from the bedroll, barefoot on cold stone, and Ines was already there, catching him by the shoulders before his knees could fold.

"You were walking," she said. Not a question. She'd been watching. She'd probably been watching all night, sitting cross-legged by the dead fire with the salt-cord looped twice around her wrist and twice around his, the little knots of it glowing faintly where they'd rubbed against each other in the dark.

"I know," Tam said. His voice came out small. He cleared his throat and tried again, aiming for the cheerful register he used when Sister Maren's debt collectors came knocking. "I know. I'm sorry. Did I say anything? Last time I said something about *stairs*, and Brannoc got that look—"

"You said nothing." Ines's hands were steady on his shoulders, which meant her hands were shaking somewhere he couldn't see. "You just walked."

*Just walked.* Down through the guild cellar, down the old smuggler's stair, into the warrens beneath Corvane that even the Salt Guild only mapped in whispers. Three nights now. Each night a little farther, a little faster, as if whatever called him from under the mountain was losing patience, or gaining appetite.

Brannoc crouched by the fire pit, feeding it splinters of broken crate-wood. He didn't look up. "Third time," he said to no one. "It's pulling harder. You can hear it in the cord."

Tam could hear it too, if he was honest. The salt-cord Ines had tied between them hummed at night, a note so low it lived more in his teeth than his ears, and every night the note came a half-step higher, like a song climbing toward its chorus. He'd told himself it was the mountain settling. Mountains settled. Everyone knew that.

But mountains didn't settle in rhythm. And this one kept time with his heartbeat.

Sefa dropped down through the ceiling hatch feet-first, landing light as a cat with a sack of provisions over one shoulder. "Warrens are shifting," she announced, in the tone other people used for reporting weather. "The stair we took yesterday isn't there anymore. There's a wall where the stair was. A very rude wall." She tossed the sack down and grinned at Tam, and it was a good grin, sharp and warm at once, the grin of someone who'd decided the world was a joke worth laughing at even while it ate people. "Good news is I found another way down. Bad news is it's a chimney."

"A chimney," Brannoc repeated flatly.

"An inclined chimney. With handholds. Mostly."

"Mostly," Tam said, "is my favorite kind of news."

Ines wasn't smiling. She was looking at him the way she looked at ledgers when the columns refused to balance — like the answer was there, hiding, and she would find it or die with her fingers still turning pages. "Tam," she said quietly. "Tell me what you feel. All of it. Don't make it smaller for me."

And that was the thing about Ines. Everyone else in Corvane wanted you to be fine. Fine was cheap; fine kept the queues moving. Ines wanted the true number, entered in full, no rounding.

So Tam sat down on the cold stone and gave her the audit.

"It's like being a door," he said. "Like somebody built a door out of me and they're standing on the other side turning the handle, slow, slow, testing whether the hinges will hold. When I'm awake I can lean against it. When I sleep—" He swallowed. "When I sleep I don't lean. I just open. And there's something on the other side that's so *glad* to see me. That's the part I can't explain. It's not hungry exactly. Or it is, but it's hungry like family is hungry, like it thinks I've been away too long and it wants me home and fed and *kept*." His hands had curled into fists without asking permission. "Grandmother used to sing a song about coming home. I think it learned the tune from her."

The fire crackled. Sefa had stopped grinning. Even Brannoc's hands had gone still over the kindling.

"The contract," Ines said. Her voice was level, ledger-level, each word an entry in a column that had to sum to zero. "Your grandmother sold herself to buy your mother's cure. The debt passed down. It's not a hunger, Tam. It's *collections*."

"Then let me pay it," Tam said, and heard his own voice come out wrong, too steady, door-voice instead of boy-voice. "If it's owed, it's owed. I've balanced books since I was nine. I know how debts work."

"No." Ines said it fast, fast enough that it cracked. Then slower: "No. Debts work because someone chooses to pay them. Nobody gets to choose *for* you. That's the whole point of everything I've ever—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "You don't walk down there asleep, dragged by the wrist like a parcel. If you go, you go awake, on your own feet, because you chose it. That's the only version I'll allow."

"That's the version I want too," Tam said. And found, saying it aloud, that it was true — that underneath the terror there was a stubborn coal of it, banked and burning. He was tired of being a door. Doors don't get dignity. "Teach me to stay awake. Teach me to walk toward it with my eyes open. If it wants me, it can look me in the face when it takes me."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief. "You sound like a priest," he said. "The bad kind. The kind that gets martyred."

"The best kind," Sefa corrected. "The kind they write songs about. Terrible songs, mind, sung badly in taverns, but songs."

* * *

They went down through the chimney at midday, because Sefa argued that if the mountain was going to eat them it should at least have to do it in daylight, on principle.

The shaft was exactly as advertised: inclined, crumbling, mostly handholds. Old mining warrens honeycombed the mountain's roots — Corvane had been dug out of this rock generations before anyone thought to build a city above it, and the deep galleries had been abandoned since before Tam's grandmother's grandmother. Now the abandoned dark had woken up. Every few minutes the stone shuddered, a long rolling groan like a giant turning over in sleep, and grit sifted down through the blackness, and somewhere far below something answered the shudder with a sound that was almost singing.

Tam climbed last, with Ines directly above him and the salt-cord paying out between them hand over hand. He'd asked her to keep it tied even awake. She'd looked at him for a long moment and then tied it fresh, whispering the knot-words her god had taught her — the god nobody knew was listening, the god hoarding souls under the mountain like a miser hoarding coin. Tam didn't understand the theology. He understood the knot held.

Above them, Brannoc prayed. Not loudly. But continuously, a low mutter like water over stones, and Tam had noticed that wherever Brannoc's muttering touched the walls, the shudders came gentler. Whatever god had rejected Brannoc, some residue of the man's twenty years of devotion still worked on the world like grease on a hinge. It was, Tam thought, deeply unfair and deeply beautiful, which described most holy things.

Halfway down the chimney the mountain screamed.

Not the settling-groan. A real scream, high and vast, tearing up through the rock, and the chimney lurched like a ship in weather. Tam's foothold crumbled. For one long heartbeat he hung by his hands on a ledge the width of a prayer-book, dust boiling past him into the dark below, and he felt — clearly, unmistakably, the way you feel a hand on your shoulder — the thing beneath take an interest. Felt it *pull*. Gentle. Enormous. Glad.

*Come home,* said the pull, in Grandmother's singing voice. *You're late. We saved your place.*

"No," Tam said out loud, to it, to the dark, to his own hammering heart. "No. I'm not late. You're early. Learn to wait."

He hauled himself onto the ledge. His arms shook. Above him Ines's face appeared over the rim of stone, white with dust, eyes huge.

"Tam—"

"I'm here." He grinned up at her, and the grin cost him everything he had left, and he paid it anyway, because she needed it and because paying things was what he was good at. "Told you. Mostly-handholds. I'm getting good at mostly."

They reached the bottom gallery an hour before dusk, if dusk still meant anything a quarter-mile underground. Sefa lit lamps. The light showed a corridor of fused stone — walls melted smooth and rippled, like candle wax, like the whole passage had been poured rather than carved — and set into the walls at intervals, fist-sized stones that glowed with a pale inner light and pulsed.

Pulsed in rhythm. Tam put his hand flat against the nearest wall and felt it: the beat matched his own heart, half a step behind, like an echo learning the song.

"Prayer-stones," Brannoc whispered. He'd gone gray. "I've read about these. They said the Silent God's faithful pressed their prayers into stone because their god never answered, and the stones kept the prayers safe. Thousands of years of unanswered prayers, all stored down here like grain in a granary." He looked at the pulsing walls and his voice went thin. "It's not storing them anymore. It's *eating* them. That scream earlier — that was ten thousand years of unanswered prayer going down its throat."

"And it's still hungry," Sefa said. "Lovely. I do love a job with growth potential."

Tam laughed. It came out ragged but real, and Sefa winked at him, and that was the thing about the ragged ones, the discarded ones, the struck-from-the-rolls ones: they knew exactly when a laugh was load-bearing.

They moved. The corridor sloped down, always down, and the pulse in the walls quickened as they went, and Tam's summons quickened with it. By the second hour he could feel the pull even awake — a gentle constant pressure behind his sternum, like a current in deep water. He told Ines. He told her everything now; that was their arrangement, sealed with a knot. She listened with her ledger-face and then did something he didn't expect: she took out a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper and wrote down what he said, word for word, and folded it away.

"What's that for?" he asked.

"So it's remembered," she said. "Whatever happens. What you felt, what you chose, what you said. Somebody keeps the true number." Her jaw tightened. "Even if the gods won't."

* * *

The collapse came at midnight, or what Sefa swore was midnight by her pocket-watch, and it nearly took Tam with it.

One moment the corridor ran smooth ahead of them; the next the floor bucked like a living thing and thirty yards of gallery simply folded, stone pouring downward into a black throat of broken rock, dust exploding outward. Sefa yanked Brannoc back by the collar. Ines grabbed the salt-cord and *held*, both hands, boots braced, and Tam slid toward the edge with the whole mountain's weight dragging at him and the glad voice in his chest rising to a shout —

*Come HOME, come home, come home* —

and he planted his feet and leaned back against it, against the pull, against the door-hinge of himself, and said through his teeth:

"I said *wait*."

And the pull — paused. Actually paused, a held breath, a surprised silence, as if in all its centuries of collections nothing had ever told it no in its own grandmother's voice. In that pause Ines hauled him back from the edge and they stood pressed together in the roaring dust, alive, the broken gap yawning where the floor had been.

Below the gap, far down in the new darkness, something glowed. A faint gold seam, like light under a door.

"The throne room's that way," Sefa said, peering over the edge. "Straight down. Of course. Why would anything ever be easy."

"We could go around," Brannoc started. "There are side galleries, the old survey maps show—"

"The side galleries are collapsing faster than the main shaft," Ines said. She'd gone still, reading the mountain the way she read ledgers. "It's hollowing itself out. Feeding-fires burning through the rock. Everything falls eventually. The question is whether we fall *toward* it or *away* from it." She turned to Tam, and in the lamplight her face was stripped of everything except the question. "This is the choice point. I promised you'd choose. So choose."

Tam looked at the gap. At the gold seam far below, pulsing now in time with his heart, no longer half a step behind but perfectly, horribly synchronized. He was terrified. He catalogued it honestly, the way Ines had taught him: terror in the knees, terror in the gut, terror singing in the teeth where the low note lived. Terror, entered in full, no rounding.

And under the terror, the stubborn coal. Because he was Tam Vane's grandson, and Vanes paid what they owed, but they paid *standing up*. Because Sister Maren was upstairs in Corvane right now believing her brother was safe. Because Ines had tied a cord around his wrist and lied to him about why, and he'd known it was a lie, and he'd loved her anyway, and love like that deserved to be met with the same courage it was made of.

"I walk down awake," Tam said. "On my own feet. And when it opens its door, I want it to see me coming with my eyes open, and I want it to know that I *chose* the road, even if it thinks it chose for me." He took a breath. "Because that's the difference, isn't it? Between a payment and a theft. Between a gift and a grab. It's been stealing all this time. So I'll make myself into the one thing it can't steal."

"What's that?" Brannoc asked softly.

"A giver," Tam said. "On my own terms. Watch me."

They climbed down through the break together — Sefa first, finding holds in the dark like she'd been born in one; Brannoc praying the rocks gentle; Ines and Tam roped close, descending hand over hand into the gold-lit dark while the mountain groaned and sifted and sang. The pulse in the walls rose around them, faster now, eager, a chorus approaching its final verse.

And Tam walked. Awake. Eyes open. Feet his own.

The gold light swelled ahead until it filled the whole world, and out of it, rising to meet them, came the sound of a door beginning to open — and standing in the threshold glow, tall and patient and wrong, the first of the masked servitors lifted its head and spoke with a voice like pages turning:

"*The account holder arrives.*"
