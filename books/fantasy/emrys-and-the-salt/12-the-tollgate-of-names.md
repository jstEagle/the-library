## 12. The Tollgate of Names

Sefa had stolen a great many things in her life, and she had a private taxonomy for all of them. There were the easy thefts — bread, coins, a watchman's boots while he slept off his drink. There were the hard ones: the year of her own life, pried back out of a guild vault with wax picks and a forged signature and three nights of shaking hands. And then there were the strange thefts, the ones that didn't fit anywhere — names, mostly. Names she'd lifted off gravestones, off debt-bills, off drunk men who'd forgotten they ever had them. She kept them the way other thieves kept lockpicks. You never knew when a name would open a door.

She was thinking about that now, standing before a door made of names.

The warren passage ended at a wall that should not have been there. The miners' maps — Brannoc's copies, older than Brannoc — showed a junction, a sloping gallery, another mile of tunnel. Instead there stood an archway of black stone, and set into the archway was a face.

Not carved. *Set.* A servitor, like the ones they'd glimpsed from a distance in the upper galleries, but this one had been built into the rock itself, its body fused to the arch, its arms spread along the stone as though it were holding the mountain up and resented every minute of it. Its mask was porcelain-white, featureless except for a single vertical seam where a mouth might be. When it spoke, the seam did not move. The voice came out of the stone around it, out of the floor, out of Sefa's own teeth.

*PASSAGE IS TOLL.*

"Of course it is," Sefa said. "Everything down here is toll. Toll for air, toll for walking, toll for having a mother." She said it loudly because her knees were doing something she preferred no one notice. "What's the toll?"

*A TRUE NAME. FREELY GIVEN. TRULY HELD.*

Behind her, Tam made a small sound. He was walking again — had been walking since the last collapse, awake now by sheer stubbornness, one hand on Ines's salt-cord as if it were a leash he'd agreed to. His eyes had the flat shine of someone watching their own feet move without quite owning them. The summons pulled him downward, and every step closer to the throne made him shudder like a man walking into cold surf.

"I don't understand," Tam said. "How do I know if I've got a true name? What if mine's wrong? What if I've been using a knockoff my whole life?"

"You haven't," Ines said quietly.

"How would you know?"

"Because I keep the ledgers." Ines stepped forward, and Sefa watched her do the thing she always did — square her shoulders, lift her chin, become exactly the person the situation required. Except that wasn't true anymore, was it. Somewhere below them, in a vault or a court or a throne, sat the memory of the day Ines Vane took her oath, and the woman standing here was missing a piece of herself and pretending very hard that the shape still held. "It doesn't want your name, Tam. It wants leverage. A name truly given is a hook. Whatever passes through that gate carrying your name can be called back."

"So we don't give it anything real," Sefa said. "We give it garbage." She patted her coat, where a dozen folded scraps of paper lived in an inner pocket, each bearing a name in careful ink. *Merrick Dole, drowned age nine. Aldous Wren, hanged. Cathis Vane, died of nothing in particular.* She'd taken them off graves in the Lanternfield cemetery during her second career, back when she'd thought names might be worth money someday. Everyone had told her she was mad. Sefa had been called worse things by better people.

Brannoc was staring at the servitor the way a man stares at a fire he once fell into.

"You know what it is," Ines said to him.

"I know what it is." His voice came out sanded flat. "I spent twenty years learning the theology of gates. Threshold servitors. They're not guards. Guards protect something. These are *tollgates* — they exist so the god never has to look at what comes in. Everything is filtered through intermediaries. That was always its way." His jaw worked. "That was always its way, and I loved it for that. I thought silence was holiness. I built my whole life on the idea that a god who doesn't speak must be listening very hard."

"And now?" Sefa asked.

"Now I think it just didn't want to hear the bill come due." He rolled his shoulders. His hands were steady, which surprised Sefa; she'd seen him drop a cup two days ago when a prayer-stone hummed at him across a market square. "Let me go first. I have something it will take."

"That's what worries me," Ines said.

"It should." Brannoc almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, gallows-built, and Sefa recognized it because she owned several of the same model. "But you need me at the doors below, and this thing won't let me pass carrying what I'm carrying. So." He turned to the archway. "Hear me, threshold-thing. I am Brannoc Vale, formerly priest of the Second Choir of the Waking Deep, defrocked, unfrocked, unmade. I surrender—"

"Don't," Ines said.

"—my god's secret name."

The seam in the mask split open half an inch. Not speaking. *Inhaling.* Sefa felt the air tug past her ears toward the arch, felt the dark lean in like a beggar at a kitchen window.

"The Waking Deep gave me its name in my ordination year," Brannoc went on, over Ines's sharp intake of breath. "Every high priest receives it and is forbidden to use it. I was forbidden for twenty years. Then it woke up, ate a temple full of worshippers' prayers, looked straight through me, and forgot I existed. So the name isn't sacred anymore. It's salvage." He drew a breath. "*Vaelummar. Tide-that-turns-on-itself. That is the name of the god beneath the harbor, and I give it freely, and I hold it truly, and I will never say it again after this breath.*"

He said it. The name went out of him — Sefa actually saw it go, saw something leave his chest like heat-shimmer rising off summer cobbles, saw it stream into the seam in the mask and vanish. Brannoc staggered. Ines caught his arm.

"What did you *do*?" she demanded.

"Bought us passage." He was gray as porridge. "And paid off a debt twenty years old. That name was the last thing my god ever gave me. Now it belongs to a door." He laughed, short and awful. "You know what's funny? That's the most honest transaction I've ever been part of. No hymns. No incense. Just a price and a payment. Twenty years of priesthood and I finally understand how worship works down here."

*THE NAME IS HELD,* the servitor said. *PASSAGE GRANTED FOR THE NAMED.*

The archway's darkness thinned to a shimmer, and through it Sefa could see the gallery continuing on the far side, sloping down, lit by veins of that faint gold light that seemed to seep out of the mountain's bones.

"One of us," Ines said. "It takes one name per passage?"

*ONE NAME. ONE PASSAGE. THE TOLL IS NOT SHARED. NOTHING DOWN HERE IS SHARED.*

"Charming hospitality," Sefa muttered. "Right. My turn."

"Sefa—" Ines started.

"Nope. Don't do the thing where you argue with me and waste time. Tam's pulling downhill harder every hour, and I can feel it from here, like a hook behind my sternum, and I'm not even the one being summoned." She dug into her inner pocket and fanned out her grave-scraps. "Now. Lesson in applied larceny. A name is only worth what someone will pay for it. This one—" she held up Merrick Dole, drowned age nine, "—belongs to a boy who's been dead forty years. Nobody's using it. Nobody's *missing* it. Technically I'm not even stealing it; I'm recycling."

"That's not how names work," Brannoc said.

"How would you know? Your whole profession ran on guessing." She smoothed the scrap flat against her palm. "Merrick Dole. That's who I'll be. Merrick Dole, nine years old, dead of the river, fond of frogs according to his headstone. If the door wants a true name freely given and truly held, fine. I give it freely. And I'll hold it truly — I hold everything truly, it's my one virtue. Ask anyone. Actually don't, they'll lie."

"Sefa." Ines's voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. "If you walk through that gate as Merrick Dole, and something down there decides to call that name—"

"Then a dead boy answers, and I keep walking." She met Ines's eyes. "You're down here to steal a soul out of a god's pantry. I stole a year of my own life back from people scarier than a door. Let me do my job."

For a moment Ines looked at her the way she looked at ledger entries that wouldn't balance — hunting for the error, the hidden cost, the line item that would make the sum come out wrong. Sefa let her look. There wasn't one. That was the whole point of grave-names: they were pre-paid, pre-grieved, already spent by the world. Whoever Merrick Dole had been, his losses were long since tallied.

"Merrick Dole," Sefa said clearly, to the archway. "Freely given. Truly held. Come and get it, you great porcelain filing cabinet."

The shimmer rippled. Something reached out of the dark — not a hand, more the *idea* of a hand, a pressure that pressed against her sternum and rifled through her like fingers through a coin purse. It found the name she was offering. It paused. Sefa had the distinct, skin-crawling sensation of being *read*, of some vast attention turning her over, checking the stitching, testing whether the name fit her shoulders.

Then it took it.

The scrap of paper in her hand went blank. Not faded — *blanked*, the ink drawn out of the fibers entirely, and with it went... something. Not memories. Not her self. But a small hollow opened up somewhere under her ribs, a little room where *Merrick Dole* had briefly been hers, now swept empty and locked. She swayed. Tam caught her elbow.

"Ow," she said. "Okay. Ow. Note for future heists: doors down here bite."

*THE NAME IS HELD,* the servitor intoned. *PASSAGE GRANTED FOR THE NAMED.*

The shimmer widened. Brannoc went through first, moving like a man wading upstream. Sefa followed, and passing through the gate felt like walking through a curtain of cold water that checked her pockets on the way — a brief, total inventory of her soul, conducted with the bored efficiency of a customs officer. On the far side she was herself. Mostly. Nine years old and dead of the river, technically, but herself.

Tam came next, and the trouble started.

He walked up to the arch with the salt-cord trailing from Ines's wrist to his, and the servitor's mask tilted — the first movement it had made besides breathing — and the seam opened wider.

*THE SUMMONED CARRIES A TRUE NAME ALREADY HELD.*

"I'm not giving it," Tam said. His voice shook, but he planted his feet, and Sefa felt a fierce, unexpected pride in him, this skinny runner who'd sold a year of his life for his sister's debt and was now arguing metaphysics with a door. "It's mine. You can't have it. I need it — my sister calls me by it, and Ines writes it in her book, and if you take it then who are they calling?"

*THE DEBT STANDS. THE NAME WILL BE COLLECTED AT THE THRONE.*

"Then collect it there," Tam said, "and not one inch sooner," and he walked through the gate with his chin up and both fists closed, and the shimmer parted around him like it was afraid of him, or maybe like it respected him, and Sefa decided right then that if they all survived this she was going to teach him to pick locks, because boys like that deserved a trade.

Which left Ines.

She stood before the arch alone, the salt-cord hanging slack between her wrist and the empty air where Tam had been. The servitor regarded her. The gold light in the walls dimmed, the way candlelight dims when a cloud crosses the sun, and Sefa understood that something far below had noticed them — had noticed *her* — and was paying attention through this porcelain intermediary the way a miser pays attention through a clerk.

*THE LAST TRAVELER. PASSAGE IS TOLL. A TRUE NAME. FREELY GIVEN. TRULY HELD.*

"I know the terms," Ines said.

"You could give it a grave-name," Sefa said quickly. "I've got more. Take Cathis Vane, died of nothing in particular, lovely name, barely used—"

"It would know," Ines said. "It knows what I am. It's known since the upper galleries." She was quiet a moment. Sefa watched her reach for something — a name, an identity, the oath-shaped piece of herself she'd pawned in the vaults above — and find the space where it used to be, and stand there holding the absence like a tool with no handle.

"I don't have one to give," Ines said at last, and her voice did not break, which Sefa thought was the bravest thing she'd ever witnessed. "Whatever my true name was, I traded it away for passage, days ago, to a guild that sells memories by the ounce. The god I served took the rest when it wrote me. There's nothing left that's truly mine to hold. So I'm giving you nothing."

*NOTHING IS NOT TOLL.*

"Nothing is all I have." Ines stepped forward. "And I am done paying this family's debts. Mine, my god's, this mountain's. I've spent ten years keeping perfect books for a thing that was eating children, and I am *finished*. Open the gate or don't. I'm coming through either way."

*THE TOLL IS NOT WAIVED.*

"Then waive this." She drew the knife — Sefa hadn't even seen her take it out, the woman moved like closing a ledger, all business, no flourish — and she didn't threaten the servitor, didn't posture. She simply reached up and cut the salt-cord off her own wrist, the cord that bound her to Tam, the last thread of the oath-magic she still carried, and she let it fall at the archway's base.

"This is what I have left of binding," she said. "Salt and vow and name. Take it. It's not freely given — it's *seized*, by me, from myself. Hold that truly, if you can."

The servitor went very still. Down in the mountain's bones, something vast shifted its weight, and the gold light guttered, and for three long heartbeats Sefa was certain they were about to learn what a tollgate did to travelers who refused to pay.

Then the seam in the mask curved. Upward. Into something that on a human face would have been a smile, and on this face was infinitely worse.

*THE LEDGER-BREAKER PASSES UNPAID,* the servitor said, and its voice was no longer coming from the stone. It was coming from everywhere, from below, from the throne room they hadn't reached yet, warm and familiar and horribly gentle, a voice like family. *IT ALWAYS DOES. THAT IS WHY WE KEPT HER LEDGERS SO CAREFULLY. COME DOWN, INES. YOUR SEAT HAS BEEN WAITING.*

The shimmer tore open, edge to edge, ragged, unceremonious, like a wound rather than a door.

"Move," Ines said, white-faced, and they moved — all four of them, through the cut gate, into the deepening gold, while behind them the servitor's mask began, softly, to laugh.

* * *

The gallery beyond sloped steeply, and the gold light thickened until it was less like light than like honey they were wading through. Sefa counted turns out of habit — left, left, right, a stair of forty-one steps worn concave by feet long dead — because counting kept the fear in a box with a lid on it. Behind them, faint but persistent, the laughter followed. Not chasing. *Accompanying.* Like a host walking guests to their table.

Tam stopped suddenly at the bottom of the stair. His hand flew to his chest.

"It's close," he whispered. "I can hear it. Not words. Just — appetite. Like standing outside a kitchen when you're starving, except I'm the food." He looked up at Ines, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It's happy I'm coming. Do you understand? All the way down here, it's *glad* to see me."

Ines knelt and gripped his shoulders. "Then we're going to ruin its dinner."

Ahead, the corridor opened into a vastness that swallowed their lantern-light — a cavern where the ceiling was lost in golden haze and the floor was a plain of fused prayer-stones stretching farther than sight, hundreds of thousands of them, millions, each one murmuring, the whole floor whispering like rain on a roof. And at its center, on a raised dais of black glass, stood a table.

Not a throne. A table. Long, low, set as if for a meal, with places laid in ranks receding into the haze — and seated at the far places, motionless, masked figures without number, waiting.

Sefa's mouth went dry. "Tell me that's not what I think it is."

"It's a court," Brannoc breathed. "It's always been a court. We just never knew what was being served."

At the near end of the table, one chair stood empty. As they watched, it slid back from the table by itself, smooth and welcoming, and a place-setting gleamed at it — plate, cup, and a small folded card, written in handwriting Sefa recognized with a lurch, because she'd seen it on guild contracts a hundred times.

The card read: *Welcome home, Ines.*
