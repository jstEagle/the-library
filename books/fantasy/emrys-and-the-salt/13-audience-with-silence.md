## 13. Audience with Silence

The throne room had no ceiling. That was the first thing Ines understood, standing at the edge of the last landing with the salt-cord still knotted around her wrist and Tam's name written along its length in her own hand. Above the throne of fused prayer-stones there was only dark — not the dark of a cavern, which has texture and distance, but the dark of a closed book, of a page turned over, of something deliberately shut.

She had expected rot. She had expected, in some honest corner of herself, that ten years of silence would have left the god's court like an abandoned house: dust on the servitors' masks, moss eating the prayer-stones, the whole architecture sagging under neglect. Instead everything gleamed. The warren walls were veined with pale mineral light. The floor was smooth as poured tallow and warm through the soles of her boots, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and she hated that her body responded to it — shoulders dropping, breath slowing — like a dog recognizing its master's door.

*You were trained here,* she reminded herself. *This warmth is handwriting. You know the hand.*

Brannoc and Sefa waited behind her at the tollgate passage, holding the narrow way against whatever came. She had told them to hold it for as long as they could. Sefa had grinned and said, "Define *long*," and Brannoc had said nothing at all, which from Brannoc was a kind of blessing.

Now Ines walked alone down the long approach, past the masked servitors who stood in ranks like cutlery laid out for a feast no one would eat. Their masks were blank — no mouths, no eyes, only a faint suggestion of features, unfinished, as if the god had begun making faces and lost interest in what faces were for. As she passed, each one turned its head. Not quickly. Not menacingly. With the slow, courteous attention of servants acknowledging the return of someone long expected.

That was the worst of it. Not menace. *Welcome.*

The throne rose ahead of her, and she made herself look at it properly, because looking away felt like the first step of a longer surrender. It was built of prayer-stones — thousands of them, tens of thousands, fused into a single mass the size of a granary, each stone once carried by some faithful hand to some shrine. She recognized the make. The Salt Guild sold prayer-stones by the crate; she had audited the entries herself, year after year, *forty stones to the Ashmarket shrine, sixty to the river temples*, never asking where the old ones went when shrines were decommissioned. They came here. They all came here. Every stone was a prayer somebody had paid to have heard, and every one of them had been eaten instead.

And in the heart of the throne, deep enough that she saw it more than looked at it, something moved. A slow turning, like a vast eye adjusting to light. Like attention.

Ines stopped ten paces out, where the warmth became almost unbearable, and did the thing she had done every day for ten years, the thing that had defined her more thoroughly than her face or her name.

She prayed.

Not words. Words were for guilds and contracts. She prayed the way she'd been taught — a shaping of silence, an offering of attention, the soul held open like a ledger awaiting entry. She did it now partly because it was habit, partly because it was defiance, and mostly because if this thing had taken Tam, she wanted it to know exactly who had come to collect him.

The god answered.

It did not speak in sound. It spoke the way the guild's oldest contracts worked — meaning arriving already written, already witnessed, already binding, so that she understood the words a half-second before she could have said she'd heard them.

***Ines.***

Her knees nearly went. Not from awe — from recognition. The voice was her mother's cadence. No — not her mother; she barely remembered her mother. It was the cadence of everyone who had ever said her name kindly. Her first guildmaster. The cook at the Wayhouse who'd fed her free through a hungry winter. Tam, saying "Ines" the way he said it when he brought her tea without being asked. The god wore their voices the way its servitors wore masks.

"You took him," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small and human and filthy in all that polish. "Tam. Sixteen years old. He runs messages for the Salt Guild. He cheats at cards badly and gives his winnings back. You took his soul."

***He was owed.***

"He was *inherited*. His grandmother's contract. A debt signed before he was born." Ines kept her hands loose at her sides, though every muscle wanted to fist. "I've read the entry. I helped preserve it. Page nine hundred and eleven, winter ledgers, deferred payment clause. I know exactly how you dressed it up."

***You read my writing,*** the god said, and there was something in it that was almost pride, almost fondness, and it went through her like a blade wrapped in wool. ***You always read it well.***

"I learned from the best liars in Corvane."

***No.*** And here the voice changed — softened, gathered close, the way a parent's voice changes when a child comes home bleeding. ***You learned from me. Ossa taught you columns. I taught you why the columns matter. Every entry is a promise kept or a promise broken, and you have spent your whole life keeping promises for people too frightened or too foolish to keep their own. That is mine. That is what I put in you.***

"That's what *I* built." But her voice shook on the second word, and the god heard it, and she knew it heard it, and the warmth of the floor seemed to rise up around her ankles like tide.

***Come and see,*** it said.

The throne shifted. She hadn't thought a thing that size could shift, but the prayer-stones flowed apart like a crowd making way, opening a hollow at the center, and in the hollow —

Tam.

Not his body. His body, she knew with sick certainty, was somewhere above them in the warrens, walking in his sleep down the same road she'd raced, tied by nothing now because the summons had grown too strong for cord and knot. This was the rest of him. He sat cross-legged in the hollow of the throne, and he looked — whole. Better than whole. He looked like Tam on a good morning, well-fed and rested, eyes bright, that crooked grin already loading.

"Ines!" He scrambled up. "You came — of course you came, you absolute — I *knew* you'd come—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her harder than she meant. "Tam. Look at where you're sitting."

He looked. He frowned. And then his face smoothed again, easy, unbothered, and that smoothness terrified her more than screaming would have. "It's fine," he said. "Honestly, Ines, it's — it's actually nice? It's quiet. I'm not tired for the first time in — I don't even remember. And there's all these voices, but they're not mean ones—" He caught himself, winced. "Okay, that sounds bad. But it's not bad. It's like being in the guild hall after closing, when everyone's gone home and the lamps are still lit. You know how you like that? You always stay late. I've seen you."

She had stayed late every night for six years. She had told herself it was diligence.

"It isn't you," she said to the god, and her voice came out flat and cold and she blessed it for holding. "That's a shape wearing his cheer. Give him back."

***I am offering to,*** the god said.

And then it made its offer, and Ines Vane, who had priced impossible things for a decade, who could set a value on a borrowed year or a bottled grief without blinking, discovered that she had no training at all for being offered exactly what she wanted.

***First,*** it said, ***the boy. Forgiven. The grandmother's debt dies with the contract; I will unwrite it myself, in front of you. He walks out of this mountain with his soul, his year restored, and no memory of the fear. He will sleep well for the rest of his short, ordinary life.***

The hollow brightened as it spoke, and Tam laughed at something — at nothing — and the sound was so purely, stupidly *Tam* that Ines's chest seized.

***Second. Your oath. Returned intact. You felt it go missing when you traded your memory for passage — you feel the shape of it even now, don't you, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. I can give it back. The discipline. The certainty. Ten years of knowing exactly who you are and what you are for.***

She did feel it. Gods help her, she felt it — the absence shaped precisely like her purpose, aching at the center of her like weather. Since the tollgate, since the vault, since Sefa had pressed the sealed memory-vial into her hands and she'd been unable to open it, she had been walking around inside a self with a hole in it, and the hole was comfortable in the way old wounds are comfortable, familiar in their ache.

***Third,*** the god said, and now its voice dropped into something so gentle it was almost private, almost ashamed, ***your memories. All of them. The day you swore. The people you loved before you swore. I required you to forget them — I see you've guessed that, or found it written — but I never destroyed anything, Ines. I keep everything. I am, if nothing else, a careful keeper. Say yes, and you will remember your mother's face. You will remember why you chose the oath. You will be whole.***

The floor was warm. The light was gold. Tam was laughing. And the god spoke in the voices of everyone who had ever been kind to her, and it said, in the smallest voice yet:

***You have been so lonely.***

Ines stood very still.

Because that was the true thrust of it, wasn't it. Not the power, not the restoration. The loneliness. Ten years of prayers into silence. Ten years of keeping ledgers for a god that never once, not one single time, acknowledged her existence — and she had called that faith, had built her entire identity on top of that silence like a house on a filled-in well. And now the well had opened its mouth and said *I saw you the whole time.*

"You weren't silent," she said slowly, working it through, testing each word like a bad coin. "You were *eating*. Every prayer that came down the mountain, you swallowed. Every soul the desperate sold, you banked. You didn't die ten years ago. You got *fat*."

***I kept them safe,*** the god said.

"You kept them like I keep inventory."

***Is that so different?*** And there it was — the thing beneath the tenderness, surfacing for just a moment, cold and vast and patient. ***You count other people's griefs for coin. You write children's years into columns. Do not preach to me about ledgers, little auditor. We are the same profession. The difference is only scale.***

For one horrible, vertiginous moment, she couldn't find the flaw in it. That was the genius of the thing — it didn't lie, not exactly. It *amended*. It took the truth of her and entered it in a column she'd never agreed to, and suddenly she owed it her whole self.

But she had spent six years auditing. And auditors learn one lesson above all others: *when the numbers flatter you, check them twice.*

"No," Ines said.

The warmth stilled.

"We're not the same profession," she said. "Here's the difference. When I make an entry, the person knows. They sign. They weep, they bargain, they curse me — but they *know* what they're paying and what it's for. You let a sixteen-year-old inherit a debt he couldn't read. You let a city pray to you for ten years while you used their voices as bedding. And now you stand there offering me my own life back like it's a kindness, when it's ransom. You want souls. More of them. That's the fourth term of your offer, the one you haven't spoken yet. So say it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled through miles of stone, Corvane's bells were ringing — the night of small miracles rolling on, the city drowning in careless answers, and every bell was a prayer being eaten somewhere, and the throne drank the sound like wine.

***Yes,*** the god said at last, and the family-voices fell away all at once, leaving something older underneath, something with the texture of the mountain itself. ***Bring me the faithful. The guild has ten thousand clients. Open your books to me again, Ines. One soul a season — no more, I am reasonable — chosen as carefully as you choose everything. And you will have the boy, the oath, the memories, and a place at my right hand forever. You will never be silent again. You will never be alone.***

"And if I refuse?"

***Then the boy stays where he is,*** the god said, with terrible mildness, ***and I will keep him the way I keep everything. Carefully. Forever.***

In the hollow of the throne, Tam had stopped laughing. He was looking at her now with his own eyes — really his own, frightened, awake — and his lips moved, shaping words she couldn't hear. She read them anyway, because she had spent six years learning to read Tam across a crowded guild hall: *Don't. Don't you dare. Don't take the deal.*

"Ines," he said aloud, and his voice wavered like a boy's should, thank all the drowned gods, like a scared kid and not a well-fed echo. "Whatever it's offering — it's lying about the price. It's always lying about the price. Isn't that what you taught me? Read the whole contract?"

She smiled at him. It hurt her face.

"Yes," she said. "Yes, it is."

She rolled her shoulders. She reached down and untied the salt-cord from her wrist — not to drop it, never to drop it, but to wrap it twice around her knuckles, the way a fighter does, the way a person does when talking is finished. The prayer-stones of the throne began, very slowly, to close around the hollow where Tam sat, patient as a purse-string drawing shut.

"You asked me to bring you the faithful," Ines said, and walked toward the throne. "I've decided to bring you something else."

* * *

Above her, at the tollgate passage, steel rang against servitor-mask, and Brannoc's voice roared down the warrens calling a name no living throat should have known — and the mountain itself flinched, and the god's attention flicked upward for one unguarded instant toward the doors.

One instant was all Ines needed. She ran.
