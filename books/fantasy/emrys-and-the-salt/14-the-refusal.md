## 14. The Refusal

The god's voice was not a voice.

It was the memory of every voice Ines had ever trusted — her mother's lilt on market mornings, Ossa reading terms aloud by lamplight, Tam laughing through a mouthful of stolen bread — layered one atop another until they became something new and terrible: a sound like being known completely.

*Bring me more souls,* it said, in all those voices at once, *and I will give you back everything you sold.*

Ines stood in the court beneath the mountain with her hands empty and her heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door. The throne of prayer-stones rose before her, ten thousand fused pebbles of whispered hope, each one dark except where Tam's soul had begun to glow — a small gold seam splitting the black, like dawn coming up wrong.

"You don't get to use his voice," she said.

*I have used every voice ever given to me.* The god's attention settled over her like snowfall, soft and total. *You gave me yours when you swore. Do you imagine I have forgotten how it sounds?*

"I know exactly how it sounds." Her jaw ached from clenching. "I've been writing in your silence for ten years. Every entry. Every price. You let me believe you were dead, and I kept your books anyway."

*You kept them beautifully.*

And that — that was the knife turned just so. Because some small, starving part of her *had* kept them beautifully, had taken pride in it, had built an identity out of precision and devotion and the belief that somewhere, somehow, her god was listening even if it never answered. Ten years of muttering prayers into a well and calling the echo faith.

She thought of the vault below the guildhall. Of the sealed contract in ash-paper and iron wax. Of Tam's grandmother, who had sold her soul for a miracle and passed the debt down like an heirloom nobody wanted. Of Tam, sixteen years old, selling a year of his life to cover his sister's debt because that was what you did in Corvane — you paid, and you paid, and you called it family.

"You're not offering to forgive him," Ines said slowly. "You're offering to *refinance* him."

The silence stretched. Even a god, it seemed, did not enjoy being understood.

Behind her, at the far end of the court, the great doors groaned. Brannoc's voice carried across the stone, ragged but steady: "They're pushing! Whatever you're going to do, do it *fast!*"

Sefa's laugh followed, bright and unhinged, the laugh of a woman balancing on a ledge over an abyss and finding it funny. "Take your time, Ines! We've got maybe forty masked horrors between us and the door!"

Ines did not turn around. She looked up at the throne, at the gold seam that was Tam, and felt something in her chest finish hardening — the way water becomes ice, the way grief becomes war.

"Here are my terms," she said.

*You are in no position to set terms.*

"I'm the only person in this room who can read your handwriting." She took a step forward, and another, boots ringing on stone worn smooth by centuries of kneeling faithful. "I've seen your master ledger. I know my oath-page is in it. And I know — because I was trained by the best bookkeeper in Corvane, and she learned from *you* — that a contract is only as strong as its binding clause."

The god went very still. Stillness, from a thing made of ten thousand voices, was louder than screaming.

"So here is my counteroffer," Ines said. "Void the contract. Release every soul in your ledger — Tam's, his grandmother's, all of them, every desperate faithful who ever knelt at your altars and paid in the only coin they had left. Do that, and I walk away. I don't burn your book. I don't unmake your throne. I let you keep your silence, which is apparently the only thing you actually love."

*No.*

"No," she agreed. "I didn't think so."

*Then you leave me no choice.* The voices softened, wrapped around her, tender as a blanket drawn over a sleeping child. *I will hold the boy until you understand. Souls keep, Ines Vane. I am very good at keeping things. Ask your memories — oh. Forgive me. You don't have them anymore, do you?*

The cruelty was exquisite. It knew precisely where the wound was, because it had made the wound. It had demanded she forget everyone she loved before swearing her oath; Sefa had recovered the pawned memory from the guild vaults, and Ines had read her own recorded oath by candlelight and learned that the person she'd been for ten years was a room someone else had furnished.

"You think that frightens me," Ines said quietly.

*I think it grieves you. Grief is useful. Grief makes people agreeable.*

"It used to." She rolled her shoulders. Somewhere behind her, metal shrieked — servitors against steel, Brannoc shouting words in a language that made the prayer-stones flinch. "Now it mostly makes me angry. And anger, as any good bookkeeper knows, is just arithmetic with teeth."

She began walking toward the throne.

*Stop.*

"No."

*Ines.* Her mother's voice now, warm and broken. *Daughter. Come home.*

"She's been dead eleven years," Ines said, and did not stop walking. "You don't get her. You don't get him. You don't get *anyone*."

The throne flared.

Light poured out of the fused stones — not gold, not white, but the color of held breath, the color of a decade of unanswered prayers finally exhaling. The heat hit Ines like a wall and she threw her arm across her face and kept moving, because if she stopped now she would never start again.

And out of the light came Tam's voice.

"Ines?"

Her whole body turned traitor. Her feet wanted to run to it. Her hands reached before her mind could stop them.

"Ines, it's cold, why is it so cold, where—" A sob, young and terrified and utterly real, because the god had eaten enough souls to know exactly how a frightened boy sounded. "Ines, please, I can't see you—"

"He's not there!" she shouted at herself, at the god, at the lying light. "Tam is asleep on a mountainside with a salt-cord on his wrist, and you are a *thief* wearing his fear like a coat!"

*Am I?* The god's tone was almost gentle. *Or are you simply unwilling to hear what he would say, if he could speak? He chose to sell a year of his life, Ines. He chooses debts. He chooses sacrifice. He would choose to stay, if it meant sparing you pain. They always do. That is what makes them worth keeping.*

Every word was true. That was the horror of it. Tam *would* choose to stay. He would smile that stubborn cheer of his and say something like *it's fine, Ines, really, I'm heavy anyway, put me down* — and he would mean it, and it would break her heart, and the god knew all of it because the god had been listening to hearts like his since before Corvane had walls.

"That's exactly why I'm going to take him back," Ines said. "Because he'd never ask. So I'll ask for him. Loudly. Permanently. With fire."

She reached the base of the throne.

Up close, the prayer-stones were worse than she'd imagined — each one a compressed whisper, faces sometimes surfacing in their depths like drowning swimmers. She saw a woman mouthing *please* over and over. She saw a child. She saw, near the top where Tam's gold seam burned, the faint outline of an old woman with kind hands — Tam's grandmother, still waiting after sixty years, still paying.

"You kept them *alive* in there," Ines breathed. "All this time. Not consumed. *Kept.*"

*Souls are not meat,* the god said. *They are interest. Compounding.*

Something in Ines went very quiet and very clear, the way it did in the last hour of a brutal audit when the numbers finally reconciled and she could see, all at once, the entire shape of the fraud.

"Then this is a foreclosure," she said.

She pulled the oath-page from inside her coat.

Sefa had stolen it back along with the memory — or rather, Sefa had stolen the memory, and Brannoc had pried the page from the guild's sealed archive with hands that shook, because the page was *hers*, the original, the binding clause, the sheet of vellum on which a younger Ines Vane had written herself into service with ink and blood and a forgetting. It was warm against her chest. It had been warm for three days. Sometimes she thought she could feel it trying to remember for her.

She didn't read it. She didn't need to. She had written it once, whatever self she'd been then, and everything essential about that self was standing right here: a woman who kept books, who loved a boy who ran messages, who had spent ten years praying into a silence that was only ever appetite.

But not yet. Not while the god still held Tam. Tear the clause free while the contract lived, and the ledger would simply rewrite itself around the loss — she'd seen ledgers heal, she'd *made* ledgers heal. First the debt had to be declared void. First the god had to be forced to acknowledge, in front of its own court, in front of its own law, that the contract was broken.

And gods were bound by their contracts. That was the one mercy in all of it. That was the whole reason the guild existed.

"Ines!" Brannoc's voice cracked across the court. "We can't hold much longer — they've got Sefa pinned at the east door — *Ines!*"

"Declare it void yourself," the god said, and for the first time there was something underneath the tenderness, something old and vast and afraid. *Tear the page. Free yourself. Walk away and live. I will even let the boy go — one soul, as a courtesy, between old friends.*

"One soul." Ines laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, half a sob. "There are thousands of you. Thousands of grandmothers and children and farmers who prayed for rain. You think I'd trade a mountain of them for one boy?"

*You would,* the god said simply. *You already have. You traded your memories for passage. You will trade anything for him. It is your nature. I wrote it.*

"Yes," Ines said. "You did. And here's the flaw in your handwriting, you hoarding, starving, silent *thing*—"

She pressed her palm flat against the throne's base, against the stone that screamed faintly at her touch, and looked up into the burning gold seam that held a boy she loved more than her own name.

"—you taught me that every entry must balance. Every debit has a credit. Every contract has two parties. And a contract," she said, "requires *consent*."

The court went silent. True silence, for the first time in a decade — the god holding its breath.

"Tam never signed. His grandmother signed, under duress, in a famine year, and duress voids consent in every legal tradition from here to the coast, and *you know it*, because you wrote half of them. The debt does not pass down. It died with her. And every soul you've collected on inherited claims — every single one — was taken without consent, without signature, without *law*."

She straightened. Her voice dropped into the register Ossa used for closing negotiations, flat and final as a stamp on wax.

"I declare this contract void. All clauses. All inheritances. All interest accrued. In the sight of your own court, by the law you yourself authored: *void*."

The prayer-stones shuddered.

Somewhere deep in the mountain, something enormous turned over in its sleep. The gold seam flickered. And the god made a sound — not in any borrowed voice now, but in its own, the sound of ten years of ignored prayers compressed into a single note of fury and hunger and, beneath it all, unmistakably, *fear*.

*You cannot unwrite me.*

"I can unwrite your book," Ines said. "That's better."

The doors burst.

Servitors flooded in — dozens of them, masks blank as eggs, moving with the horrible smoothness of things that had never once hesitated. Brannoc stood in the breach with his arms spread and blood running from his hairline, roaring something in his dead god's secret tongue, and the air around him *bent*, servitors stumbling as though the floor had opinions. Sefa came over the rubble behind them like a cat made of knives, laughing, throwing something that unfolded midair into a net of guild-salt that caught three servitors mid-stride.

"Ines!" Sefa screamed. "Whatever you're doing — *do it now!*"

Ines tore the oath-page from her coat.

The vellum resisted. Of course it did — it had been written in blood and it did not want to die, and neither, she realized with a strange calm, did the self it described. Her hands shook. The page whispered in her own handwriting, *I, Ines Vane, freely and forever—*

"Freely," she repeated aloud. "That's the word, isn't it. Freely."

She looked at the throne one last time. At the gold seam. At the old woman with kind hands, mouthing something that might have been *thank you* or might have been *run*.

"Hold on, Tam," she said. "Both of you. I'm coming."

And she turned toward the master ledger on its lectern of fused bone, the greatest book in the world, the book that held her name — page open, flame waiting in the throne's own light, war written across her face where heartbreak used to be.

Behind her, the god began, at last, to scream.

* * *

The master ledger was bigger than a door and older than the guild, its pages of skin so thin they moved like water when the mountain breathed. Ines climbed the lectern's steps two at a time, oath-page crushed in her fist, servitors converging from every archway, Brannoc's voice fraying behind her, Sefa's salt-net blazing as it burned through—

The ledger opened itself to her page.

Of course it did. It had been waiting. Her own handwriting marched down the vellum, neat and certain, the girl she'd been declaring herself into bondage one beautiful line at a time — and above it, in the god's hand, identical to hers because hers had been copied from it, a single binding clause:

*Let this oath be the lock, and this signer the key, and all debts herein secured thereby, world without end.*

"There you are," Ines whispered.

The first servitor reached the lectern. She kicked its mask and it split like porcelain and behind the mask there was nothing at all, just hunger shaped like a face, and she drove her shoulder into the next one and felt her ribs creak and did not stop, could not stop, because the flame was right there, the throne's own light pouring off the altar-stone beside the book, hot enough to unmake ink and blood and oaths and selves.

She held the page over the flame.

And the god spoke one last time — not in her mother's voice, not in Tam's, but in her own. Her voice, aged ten years, tired and fond and unbearably human:

*If you burn it, you won't remember why you came.*

Ines smiled through the blood in her mouth.

"Then write it down for me," she said. "Somebody has to."

She let the page fall.
