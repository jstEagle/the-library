## 16. Brannoc's Answer

The doors were singing.

Brannoc had spent twenty years listening to gods, and he knew the sound of one straining against its hinges. The servitors on the far side of the great bronze doors did not pound or batter — they *sang*, a low harmonic drone that made the metal shiver and weep rivulets of condensation, as if the doors themselves were being persuaded to melt. Each note was a prayer. He recognized the technique. He had once taught it.

"Brannoc." Sefa's voice came sharp from across the antechamber, where she crouched behind an overturned brazier with two knives drawn, her stolen year humming faintly around her like heat off a road in summer. "How long?"

He pressed his palm flat to the door. The vibration crawled up his arm and settled in his teeth. "Minutes," he said. "Maybe less. They're not forcing it. They're asking."

"Asking?"

"The door wants to open for them. It's old god-work — courtesy magic. Everything down here is polite until it isn't." He looked back at her, at the corridor beyond where Ines had vanished into the dark toward the master ledger, at the place where Tam's soul had been pulled screaming into the throne of prayer-stones not ten minutes past. "Ines needs time."

"Ines always needs time. It's her whole personality."

"It's her whole *plan*." He smiled, and it hurt his face, because smiling had become a stranger's gesture to him somewhere between his defrocking and this moment, this impossible hour beneath the mountain. "So we give it to her."

Sefa squinted at him. Behind them both, the doors' song climbed a half-tone. Somewhere in the walls, dust sifted down like gray snow. "You have that look," she said. "The look priests get right before they do something stupid and holy."

"I'm not a priest anymore."

"You're wearing the collar."

He touched his throat. He'd forgotten it was there — a strip of black wool, frayed at the edges, that he had worn every day for twenty years even after the temple stripped him of everything else. Even after they burned his vestments and struck his name from the rolls of the Weeping Choir. He had kept the collar because taking it off felt like admitting the silence had been real.

Now the silence was over. Now his god was awake, awake and starving somewhere above them in the rioting city, granting wishes carelessly, consuming worship like meat, and it still did not know his name.

*It does,* said a small treacherous voice in him. *It must.*

*It doesn't,* said the older, wiser voice, the one that had kept him alive through disgrace. *That's the whole wound of it.*

"Brannoc." Sefa again, quieter now. She had a way of going quiet when things mattered; it was the opposite of most people and one of the reasons he liked her. "Whatever you're thinking of doing — think faster, because I hear hinges."

She was right. The song had changed key. The bronze was beginning to glow along its seams, thin lines of amber light like veins in a waking body, and through them he could see shapes moving: the masked servitors, faceless and courteous, carrying their courtesy like weapons.

He thought about the night his god woke.

It had been three weeks ago. He had been sleeping in the flophouse above the tannery because a disgraced priest could afford nothing better, and he had dreamed — for the first time in twenty years, he had *dreamed* — of water. Not the Weeping God's water, not the blessed brine of the choir-fonts, but river water, brown and fast and indifferent. And in the dream a voice had spoken, vast and wet and new, saying words he could not remember on waking except that none of them were his name.

He had run to the temple. Of course he had run to the temple. Twenty years of faithful silence, twenty years of prayers poured into a well with no bottom, and the god was *awake*, and he had stood in the flooded nave among the screaming celebrants and shouted himself hoarse and the god had looked — he would swear this on anything, on the ordinary book Ines carried now, on Tam's crooked grin — the god had looked *through* him. As through glass. As through a man who had never existed.

They had taken him for mad. Then they had taken him for dangerous. Then they had simply taken the collar's authority away and left him the cloth.

And here was the thing he had never told anyone, not Ines, not Sefa, not the dark at three in the morning: he understood why the god didn't know him. He had spent twenty years praying into silence, and silence is a discipline, and discipline builds walls, and he had built his so high and so smooth that when his god finally turned its attention outward, he was just another smooth wall in a city full of them. He had made himself invisible in the shape of devotion. That was the bitter arithmetic of it. His faithfulness had erased him.

But there was one thing in all the world that could make a wall visible again.

A name.

Not his own. He had given up believing his own name mattered — names were currency down here, Sefa had proven that at the Tollgate, trading a stolen true name like a counterfeit coin. No. There was another name. A secret one. The kind of name a god tells no one, the kind that gets whispered once, at the founding, into the ear of the first priest, and passed down mouth to mouth through generations of the ordained like a coal passed hand to hand.

He knew it because he had been first-voice of the Weeping Choir before the fall. He knew it because old High Cantor Vell, dying and drunk and sentimental, had gripped his wrist in the vestry nineteen years ago and breathed it against his skin like a confession:

*Ulmothennarai.*

The name of the Weeping God, which no living tongue was permitted to speak, which Brannoc had carried in his mouth for nineteen years like a stone he could neither swallow nor spit out.

The doors began to open.

Not burst — opened, with terrible gentleness, swinging inward on their own weight as the servitors stepped through in single file, masked faces tilted at angles of perfect politeness, their hands empty and their intentions not. There were nine of them. Brannoc counted automatically, the way you count anything that intends to kill you.

"Sefa," he said. "When I tell you, get to Ines. Don't argue, don't look back, don't be noble. She'll need hands for whatever she's doing to that ledger, and I'm about to be very busy."

"Busy how?" But Sefa was smart; he watched the understanding arrive in her face like weather. "No. Oh, no, priest, whatever you're—"

"*Ulmothennarai.*"

He spoke it quietly. That was the trick of it — Vell had told him that too, in that last drunken hour. *Never shout a god's name. Shouting is begging. Say it like you're owed.*

The word went out of him and the room changed.

It was not loud. It was the opposite of loud — it was a hole in sound, a held breath, and every servitor in the chamber stopped mid-step, their masks tilting further, further, until the angles were no longer polite but *startled*. The amber light in the doorway flickered. Dust hung suspended. Brannoc felt the name leave him and keep going, up through the warrens, through the bones of the mountain, out into the night air of Corvane where a bloated and bewildered god was gorging itself on answered prayers — felt it travel the way a hook travels a fishing line, seeking the thing at the other end.

For three heartbeats, nothing.

Then the world *turned*.

He couldn't see it, but he felt it with the whole surface of his soul: something enormous, somewhere above, ceasing to move. Something that had been devouring the city's joy one miracle at a time going suddenly, absolutely still. Attention is a physical force; any child knows it, any animal knows it, the feeling of being *looked at*. Brannoc stood in a dead god's court beneath a mountain and was looked at by a living one, and it was the worst and best sensation of his life, like sunlight and a blade in the same instant.

*Who speaks my name?*

It was not words. It was pressure, grief-flavored, salt-flavored, the taste of the choir-fonts. It filled the chamber and pressed him down against the floor and he stayed standing because if he knelt now he would kneel forever.

"Me," Brannoc said aloud, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "First-Voice Brannoc of the Weeping Choir. Ordained in the year of the Long Frost. You don't know me. I know. I've made peace with—" No. Lie. He wasn't at peace; he might never be at peace. "I know you don't. But you know your name, and I know it too, and that makes us *bound*, Ulmothennarai, bound the old way, the founding way, and I am calling in the binding."

The servitors had begun to move again, slowly, as if wading through deep water. The nearest one raised a courteous hand.

*What do you want.* Not a question. A tide coming in.

"Look at me."

*I am looking.*

"No." His eyes were burning. When had his eyes started burning? "You're looking *through* me. Everyone does. My god did it for twenty years and then woke up and did it some more, and I have been a window my whole life, Ulmothennarai, a window people pray at, and I am so tired of being transparent. So *look*. Look with the part of you that was a god before you were a hunger. One moment. That's all I'm asking. One moment of being seen, and then you can go back to eating your worshippers alive, and I will never say your name again."

Silence. Real silence, the kind he had prayed into for two decades, and he thought: *there it is, the old refusal, the old indifference, I was a fool to think a name could—*

And the god looked at him.

There was no light. There was no vision. What happened was smaller and vaster than either: Brannoc felt himself become *legible*. Every year of his life read at once, the ordination and the disgrace, the collar and the flophouse, the prayers he'd meant and the ones he'd only recited, the night High Cantor Vell died smelling of communion wine and secrets, the morning he'd found Ines half-frozen outside the guildhall and fed her soup without knowing he was feeding a legend. All of it seen. All of it *counted*. He had spent twenty years pouring himself into a bottomless well, and now, for one moment, the well looked back and said, without words, in the grammar of pure attention:

*Oh. There you are. There you always were.*

Brannoc wept. He did not bother to hide it. There was no one left in the chamber whose opinion mattered — the servitors had frozen entirely, caught in the backwash of their masters' attention, masks bowed like mourners — and Sefa was staring at him with her knives forgotten and her mouth open, and none of it mattered, none of it, because he had been seen, once, truly, by the thing he had loved longest and least wisely, and it was enough. It would have to be enough. It was more than most people ever got.

*One moment,* said the god, and the grief in it was bottomless, and Brannoc understood suddenly that the hunger and the sorrow were the same thing, that gods starve the way widows starve, that it had woken into a world where everyone wanted things from it and no one wanted *it*. *This is my moment. Spend it well, First-Voice.*

"Oh, I intend to," Brannoc said, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and grinned at Sefa with all his teeth. "Sefa. *Run.*"

She ran. He heard her boots take the corridor toward the ledger-vault at a dead sprint, and he turned back to the nine frozen servitors and the glowing doors and the vast grieving attention pressing down on him from above, and he did the only thing left to do with a god's undivided gaze.

He preached.

"Ulmothennarai!" he cried, arms wide, collar black against his throat, voice filling the chamber the way it used to fill the nave in the years when his voice could crack a wineglass at forty paces. "Hear your servant! Your court below is robbing children! Your brother below — the Silent One, the hoarder, the miser of souls — has taken a boy of twelve winters into his throne-stones, a boy who never sold a thing, a boy paying his grandmother's debts! Is that justice? Is that the law of the waking gods? Or is it just the appetite of an old thing that slept through the age of mercy and woke up hungry?"

*Careful,* said the pressure above him, but softly. Curiously. It was *listening*. Gods never listened. That was the whole disease of divinity, and here he was, curing it with blasphemy.

"Careful nothing!" He laughed, wild and free, the freest laugh of his life. "You want careful, hire a priest who still has a temple! I've got nothing left to lose but this moment, and I'm spending every coin of it! Look at your brother's court, Ulmothennarai! Look at what silence buys! Look at what it bought *me*!"

And the god looked.

Brannoc felt its attention swing downward, through stone, into the dead court, and the whole mountain flinched. The prayer-stones in the distant throne room — he could feel it even from here, feel it as a tremor in the floor, a change in the air's texture — the throne *noticed* it was being watched. Somewhere beyond these walls, the Silent God, mid-meal, mid-theft, holding a child's soul in its fused and greedy heart, felt its sibling's gaze land upon it like a searchlight.

The servitors moved.

All nine at once, no longer courteous, masks splitting along seams he hadn't known they had, and Brannoc realized with distant clarity that he had perhaps miscalculated — that a distracted god's servants might panic exactly as any servants do — and the first one's hand closed on his collar and the second took his arm and the third—

He fought. Of course he fought. He was a big man and had once broken a rioter's jaw with a censer on a chain, and he broke the third one's mask with his forehead and felt something ancient shriek. But nine was nine, and he was one, and the moment — his moment, his one precious moment of being seen — was ending, he could feel it ending, the vast attention above beginning to withdraw like a tide, called away by its own hungers, by the riots, by ten thousand prayers screaming for fulfillment—

*First-Voice,* said the god, at the very edge of leaving. And then, in the grammar of attention rather than words, in the language it had used to say *there you are*: *I remember the frost-year. I remember your ordination hymn. You sang it badly.*

"I sang it *honestly*," Brannoc snarled, pinned, bleeding, laughing.

*Yes,* said the god, and was gone.

The servitors dragged him down. He went hard, cheek against cold stone, and the last thing he saw before they bore him away from the corridor — away from Sefa, away from Ines, away from the vault where a woman was even now unmaking herself to save a boy — was the amber light of the doors, flickering, uncertain, as the mountain groaned under the weight of two gods noticing each other.

They put him in a cell of fused prayer-stone, and the stones whispered with every prayer ever ignored, and Brannoc, wrists bound, collar torn, blood in his eye, lay back against the whispering wall and laughed until he cried.

Because it had looked at him. Because he had sung badly and honestly and it remembered. Because somewhere above him, a girl with a knife was running toward a ledger, and somewhere deeper, a boy's soul was waiting in the dark, and somewhere in between, a god had learned his servant's name at last — twenty years late, one moment long, worth every year of the silence.

"Hurry, Ines," he said to the whispering stones. "I've bought you minutes. Make them count."

And far above, through leagues of rock, he felt — faintly, unmistakably, like a hand laid on his head — the Weeping God pause in its feeding, and listen.

* * *

The cell had no door he could find and no darkness he could trust. The prayer-stone walls gave off a dim luminescence, the trapped residue of ten thousand unanswered petitions, and in that gray glow Brannoc took inventory of himself the way he'd once taken inventory of vestments: two ribs cracked, probably, on the left; wrist sprained; scalp split and sticky; collar gone. They had taken the collar. Twenty years he'd worn it and the servitors had peeled it off him like a label, and he found, examining the raw place on his throat, that he didn't mind. The collar had been a wall. Walls were for men who hadn't been seen.

He was still taking inventory when the whispers changed.

At first he thought it was the stones cycling — they murmured constantly, fragments of old prayers, *bless my harvest, cure my mother, let him love me* — but the murmur gathered, organized, and became a voice. Not the Silent God's voice. Smaller. Warmer. Familiar in a way that made the hair rise on his arms.

*Brannoc.* His own name, in his own mother's accent, though his mother had been thirty years dead. *Brannoc, look what you've done. Look what you've cost them. The thief is lost in the dark and the girl is unraveling herself thread by thread and the boy is screaming where no one can hear, and you — you are lying in a box telling yourself stories about being seen.*

"Shut up," Brannoc said comfortably.

*You preached to a god that doesn't care. You spent your one moment and got a cracked skull for it. Was it worth it, First-Voice? Was your little glimpse worth—*

"I said shut up." He tapped the wall beside him, twice, the way you knock on a liar's door. "You're not my mother and you're not the Silent One — it's busy, and besides, it talks in pressure, not in memories. Which makes you a fragment. A loose piece of the throne, come to nibble at the prisoner." He smiled at the gray glow. "My god looked at me. Whatever you are, you weren't there. You didn't see it. So everything you say is just weather."

The whispers stopped.

And then, from far away — up through the warrens, through the vault, through stone and silence and the unraveling of a master ledger — came a sound that Brannoc felt in his teeth before he heard it with his ears, a sound like a book the size of a mountain tearing along its spine, and underneath it, rising, a scream built of every prayer it had ever ignored.

The prayer-stones of his cell went dark, one by one, like windows going out at evening.

"Well," said Brannoc to the sudden blackness, grinning with bloody teeth. "There's my cue."

Somewhere in the dark, a lock that had held for a decade clicked open — because the thing that made the locks was dying — and the door swung inward, and Brannoc walked out of his cell into the shaking dark, a freed man with no god, no collar, and no plan except the oldest one the faithful ever have:

To go where he was needed, and sing.
