## 17. The Unwriting

The master ledger lay open on the altar-stone before the throne, and it was breathing.

Not a figure of speech — Ines had learned, in the last hour, that very little down here was. The book's pages rose and fell in a slow rhythm, the way a sleeping man's chest does, and each exhalation stirred the candleless air with a smell like cold iron and old incense. Its cover was not leather. She had thought at first it was some dark hide, but up close she could see the grain of it was wrong: whorls and ridges like fingerprints, thousands of them, pressed together into something smooth and patient. Skin of prayers. Skin of everyone who had ever knelt.

The throne behind it was worse. Fused prayer-stones, each one a compressed decade of devotion, stacked and melted into a shape that only suggested a seat because a seat was what worship expected. And in the stones, faint as heat-shimmer, faces moved. Mouths opening on words with no sound left in them.

*Every prayer it ever ignored,* Brannoc had said, back in the lamplight of the warrens, when they'd still been planning instead of surviving. *It didn't refuse them. Refusing takes effort. It just let them fall. Ten years of falling, and it caught every one.*

Behind her, at the far end of the court, the great doors shuddered. Sefa's voice came thin through the stone — *"Ines! Whatever you're going to do, do it faster!"* — followed by the sound of something heavy being wedged against something heavier.

Ines looked down at the ledger.

She knew this handwriting. Of course she did. She had been trained in it — the narrow upright letters, the way numbers were written without flourish so they could never be misread, the small hooked crosses over entries marked *paid*. Guild script. Guildmaster Ossa wrote it. Ines wrote it. Every keeper of the Salt Guild for three hundred years had written it, because the guild had learned its hand from the thing that sat on this throne, and had never once wondered why the lesson felt like remembering rather than learning.

Her oath-page was near the front. She had seen it on the descent, when the servitors had made her walk past the open book as a courtesy or a taunt. Her own name in her own hand — no. Not her hand. The hand she'd been taught to write, which she had believed was hers. *Ines Vane, sworn in salt and silence.* Below it, the clause she hadn't known she was signing: *the swearer's word binds the book; the book binds the swearer; what is written here is owed here.*

One page. One line of ink. And every soul in this court — Tam among them, Tam most of all — held fast by the grammar of it.

"Ines!" Sefa again, and now the doors boomed, once, twice, the sound of servitors who did not tire and did not hurry and did not need to.

She put her hands on the page.

It was warm. That was the detail that would stay with her afterward, she thought — not the horror of it but the warmth, the way a page should never be warm, the way a book is a dead thing made of dead trees and this one had a pulse under her fingertips like a bird cupped too hard in two hands.

"Tam," she said quietly, to the air, to the boy somewhere inside the throne's fused light. "Hold on. I'm bad at goodbyes and worse at speeches, so I'm just going to do the thing."

She gripped the edge of the oath-page.

The ledger fought her.

It was not dramatic, at first. The page simply would not tear, the way wet cloth will not tear, the way a held breath will not break. Then the resistance changed character — became intelligent, became personal — and the ink began to move. Letters rearranging themselves under her fingers, the binding clause rewriting even as she pulled, *the swearer's word binds the book* becoming *the swearer's word IS the book*, the sentence eating its own tail to keep itself whole.

And the god spoke.

Not aloud. Not in any voice. It came up through her bones, through the ten years of silence she had carried like a stone in her chest, and it used the only language it had ever truly mastered: absence. She felt the shape of her mother's laugh and could not fill it in. She felt the outline of a day — sunlight, salt water, someone's hand on her shoulder, *kneel, child, and be kept* — and the memory slid away from her like fish through open fingers. It was taking back the collateral. It was calling in everything she had ever pawned.

"No," Ines said, and pulled harder.

The page ripped an inch. A sound went through the whole court — through the walls, through the floor, through the servitors hammering at the doors — a sound like a chord struck on an instrument the size of a mountain. The prayer-stones in the throne flickered. Somewhere inside them, a face turned toward her.

*You will unmake yourself,* the silence said. Not a threat. An accounting. *The page is you. Burn the page, and the girl who swore is ash. What remains will not remember why she came. What remains will not remember him.*

"I know," Ines said.

And she found, to her surprise, that it was true, and that knowing it did not weigh what she had feared it would. She had spent years being the woman who kept the ledgers perfect, who never missed an entry, whose precision was the load-bearing wall of her entire self. But precision had always been in service of something. The ledgers were never the point. The point was that Tam ate dinner. The point was that Sefa laughed with her whole body. The point was that people who had been sold — by debt, by desperation, by grandmothers trying to buy medicine — deserved someone who would write their names down and refuse to let the ink be the last word.

Take the clerk, then. Keep the reason.

The doors cracked. Stone split along the lintel with a report like a cannon, and grey light poured through the gap, and through the gap came the first servitor — tall, faceless, its mask a smooth oval with no features except where features were needed, and right now it needed hands. Sefa's silhouette moved across the breach, quick and low, a knife flashing. Brannoc's voice rose underneath, ragged, shouting words in a language that hurt to hear — his god's secret name, dragged out into the open like a weapon he'd sworn never to draw.

Ines tore the page free.

It came out all at once, the last resistance gone, and the sound it made was not paper. It was a scream — small, human, hers — sucked backward out of the air as the page separated from the book, as if the ledger had been holding her voice all these years and had finally lost its grip on it. She staggered. The court tilted. For one white instant she could not remember her own name, and the terror of that was total and clean, and then — like a hand catching her wrist — the name came back. *Ines.* Just Ines. Not Vane, not keeper, not sworn. A name with nothing attached to it but a girl who had chosen to be here.

The ledger convulsed on the altar. Pages began turning themselves, faster and faster, a blur of entries — names, years, griefs, souls — and from every page, faintly, a whispering, hundreds of voices reading themselves aloud, trying to hold their own lines intact by recitation.

The throne flared. Inside the fused stones, the faces were all looking at her now.

*Stop,* said the silence, and for the first time in ten years, the silence sounded afraid.

"You know what the funny thing is?" Ines said. Her voice shook. She let it. "I audited your books for a decade. I balanced your faith to the copper. And I never once checked whether *you* were good for it."

She turned to the throne. To the flame.

Because that was the last cruelty of the place, the detail Ossa's old contract had hinted at and the descent had confirmed: the throne burned. Cold fire, blue-white, fed on worship, licking up between the prayer-stones without consuming them — a hearth that had been lit since before Corvane had a name, tended by no one, needing nothing except what the faithful brought down to it. The god's own altar-fire. The only flame in the world hot enough to burn a true oath.

A servitor came through the broken doors. Then another. Brannoc's shout broke off into a grunt of impact. Sefa screamed something — Ines's name, or half of it, the half that still belonged to her.

Ines walked to the throne.

The heat was not heat. It was attention. Standing before the cold fire was like standing before an eye the size of a cathedral, and every prayer she had ever muttered — every unanswered *keep them safe, keep him safe, let me be enough* — rose up in her throat at once, wanting, even now, to be answered.

"I take it back," she told the flame. "All of it. Every word I ever gave you. Consider the account closed."

She touched the oath-page to the fire.

It caught like a living thing — flame running up the ink, devouring the letters in order, and as each word burned, Ines felt it leave her. *Sworn in salt* — and she tasted the sea, briefly, and forgot why. *Silence* — and ten years of quiet peeled away from her chest like a bandage off a wound that had healed crooked underneath. *The swearer's word binds the book* — and the master ledger shrieked, every page fluttering, the whispers rising to a wail.

Then the last line burned, and the book came apart.

There was no other word for it. The spine split. The cover — the skin of pressed prayers — sloughed open like a mouth, and from inside it the souls came pouring out. Not ghosts, not wisps: *people*. Light in the shape of lives, decades of them, centuries, the sold and the inherited and the desperate, bursting upward out of the unravelling pages like water from a struck well. They filled the court. They filled the walls. They passed through Ines and each passing was a touch, a thanks, a name spoken once and released — and some of them she almost recognized, faces from the guild's oldest ledgers, debts she had dutifully recorded and never questioned.

The throne went dark stone by stone. And the Silent God screamed.

It screamed in every voice it had ever ignored. That was the horror and the mercy of it — the scream was not one sound but ten thousand, layered, the drowned child's mother begging the river to give her daughter back, the miner praying for his lungs, the bride praying against the fever, Tam's grandmother praying for a debt forgiven, all of it, all the falling prayers of ten silent years striking the air at once now that there was nothing left to catch them. The sound took the roof off the world. Cracks raced up the walls. The servitors stopped mid-stride, masks splitting down the middle, and folded like empty coats.

Ines stood in the middle of it and let it happen to her. Let the noise pour through the place where the silence had lived. It hurt. It hurt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying, which is its own kind of pain — the blood rushing back into a numb limb, the ache of returning feeling.

When it was over, the court was quiet in the ordinary way. Dust settling. Blue fire guttering out along the dead throne. The master ledger reduced to a drift of grey flakes that stirred when she breathed, and settled, and were only ash.

Somewhere behind her, coughing in the dust-choked ruin of the doorway, Sefa said, "Tell me that worked."

Ines opened her mouth to answer and found she didn't know. Didn't know what worked. Couldn't quite remember what *that* had been — there was ash, and a dark throne, and a boy —

A boy.

The name arrived before anything else, pushing up through the rubble of her mind like a green shoot through a collapsed floor. *Tam.* She couldn't say how she knew it. She couldn't say why her eyes were suddenly burning, why her chest had gone tight around something that wasn't there anymore, some load-bearing wall torn out and the whole structure somehow still standing. She only knew that somewhere above them, on the mountainside, there was a boy called Tam, and that she had come down into the dark for him, and that whatever it had cost, she would pay it again.

"Yeah," Ines said, wiping her face with a hand that smelled of smoke and salt. "I think it worked."

Brannoc leaned against the broken doorframe, bleeding from the scalp, grinning like a man who had finally been seen. "Then let's go get him," he said. "Before the mountain figures out it's allowed to fall."

Above them, deep in the stone, something groaned — long and shifting, the sound of a weight changing its mind — and the first crack of daylight fell through the shattered ceiling onto the ash of the master ledger.

Ines started climbing toward it.
