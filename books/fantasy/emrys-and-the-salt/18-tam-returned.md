## 18. Tam, Returned

The first thing Tam knew was the cold.

Not the deep, dragging cold of the mountain's belly — that other cold, the one that had lived in his chest for weeks like a stone swallowed whole — but the ordinary kind. Wind on his face. Frost stiffening the wool at his collar. The particular bite of high air in early spring, when winter is losing but hasn't admitted it yet.

He opened his eyes to a sky gone grey-gold with dawn, and a slope of scree and dead grass, and someone holding his hand so tightly it hurt.

"Ines," he said, or tried to. His voice came out as a rasp, like he'd been shouting. Had he been shouting? He couldn't remember.

She was kneeling over him, hair full of grit, a cut across her cheekbone already going brown with dried blood. Her eyes were red-rimmed and streaming, and she was crying without any expression at all — tears just falling, one after another, as if they'd stopped waiting for permission from the rest of her face.

"Hey," Tam said. "Hey. I'm here."

"I know." Her voice cracked clean down the middle. "I know you are. I don't know why I'm—" She wiped at her cheek with the back of her wrist, smearing ash. "Sorry. Give me a moment. I can't seem to stop."

"You never cry," Tam said. In eleven years of running messages through Corvane's worst streets together, he had seen her take a knife wound without blinking, had seen her read Guildmaster Ossa's audit findings without flinching, had seen her stand in the rain outside the debtors' gate all night without complaint. He had never once seen her cry.

"I know that too," she said, and laughed, wet and strange. "There's rather a lot I apparently don't know anymore. But I know your hand. Isn't that odd? I know your hand."

Tam looked at their joined fingers. Hers were blackened to the second knuckle, burned, the skin split in places — the mark of someone who had held something burning and refused to let go. His own hand looked pale and new, like a candle just lit.

* * *

Memory came back to him in pieces, and not in order.

First: the mountain. The warrens beneath it, the tunnels where the old miners had dug themselves into the dark and the dark had dug back. The throne of fused prayer-stones, each one a compressed scream, glowing faintly like coals banked under ash. The servitors in their blank masks. And the god — the Silent God, which had not been silent at all down there, only saving its voice, hoarding ten years of ignored prayers the way a miser hoards coin.

Then: the summons. That part he remembered with his body more than his mind. The pull behind his sternum, night after night, walking him out of his cot while he slept, his feet finding the mountain roads on their own. His grandmother's contract, signed seventy years ago in desperation, the debt passed down blood to blood like a crooked inheritance. *One soul, freely given by the line of Maren Hale, payable upon waking.*

And then — this was the strangest piece, the one he kept turning over like a stone found on a beach — he remembered choosing. Down in the last corridor, when the summons had come for him again, hard enough to make his knees buckle, Sefa gripping one arm and Brannoc the other, both of them shouting at him to fight it — he remembered standing up. Deliberately. Putting his feet under himself and walking toward the throne awake, because if he had to go, he would go with his eyes open, and no god was going to have the satisfaction of dragging him.

He'd stood in front of the thing that owned him and said, "My name is Tam Hale, and my grandmother would be ashamed of you."

He didn't remember what happened after that. There was a gap there, wide and white, like a page torn out of a book. But around the gap, everything else was intact — and lighter. That was the only word for it. For weeks his chest had felt like a room with someone else living in it; now the room was just his.

"I'm missing something," he said. "Aren't I."

Ines's crying slowed. She looked at him the way she used to look at ledger entries that wouldn't balance — searching for the error, knowing it was there, dreading the size of it.

"A year," she said. "You sold a year of your life. Last winter. To cover your sister's debt." She frowned, and he watched her frown deepen into genuine confusion. "I know that. How do I know that? I shouldn't—" She pressed her palm flat against her forehead. "There are holes in me, Tam. Big ones. Shaped like things I must have loved very much."

"The oath," Tam said quietly. "Your oath to the Silent God. You told us — before we went down — you told us what it cost."

"Did I?" She sounded genuinely curious. "What did it cost?"

"You don't remember telling us?"

"I remember the tunnel. I remember Sefa complaining about the smell. I remember Brannoc praying, which I thought was a waste of breath at the time, though I couldn't have told you why I thought that." She shook her head slowly. "It's like reading a ledger where someone has inked out every third line. You can see the shape of what was written. You just can't read it."

Tam sat up. The world tilted and settled. Below them, Corvane lay in its bowl of morning haze, roofs smoking gently, the river a bright thread through the middle of it. From up here the city looked small enough to hold in two hands, and impossibly far from the throne-room dark they'd crawled out of.

Somewhere below, bells were ringing. Not the alarm pattern, not the mourning peal. Just ringing — all the temples at once, wild and unsynchronized, like children let out of school.

"What happened?" he asked. "After I—"

"You went into the throne," Ines said. "Or the throne took you. Your soul did. Your body stayed standing, which was the most horrible thing I've ever seen, and I've apparently seen things I can't remember seeing." Her jaw tightened. "The god offered me a trade. It spoke. First time in ten years, and it spent its voice on trying to sound like family. It offered me my memories back. It offered to forgive you. All I had to do was bring it more souls."

"And you said—"

"I tore my oath out of its master ledger and burned it." She said it simply, the way she'd report a completed delivery. "The whole book unraveled. Everything it had ever collected poured loose. It screamed with ten years of unanswered prayers, and then it was done." She looked at her burned hands. "My oath-page was the binding clause. Burning it burned the self it wrote. So whatever I was when I swore that oath — whoever I was before it — that person is mostly gone. What's left is..." She gestured vaguely at the mountainside, at the smoke of Corvane, at herself. "This."

Tam took this in. He was fifteen — sixteen now, though the year he'd sold meant the arithmetic had gone soft somewhere — and he had learned early that the world ran on debts, and that most people would rather die than admit how much of them was owed to somebody else.

"So you don't remember why you came for me," he said.

"No."

"You don't remember the guild. Or Ossa. Or the ledgers you kept for six years."

"Not really. Shapes. Habits. My hands still want a pen in them."

"But you came anyway. Before you could remember why."

Ines looked at him then, really looked, and the tears started again, and this time her face did something with them — a smile breaking up through the grief like a fish rising through dark water.

"Yes," she said. "Apparently that part of me wasn't written in any book."

* * *

They sat on the mountainside while the sun climbed, because neither of them could quite manage standing yet.

Sefa appeared first, picking her way down the scree with a thief's economy of motion, though even her walk had a limp in it now. She dropped beside them, produced a flask from somewhere inside her coat, and handed it to Tam without a word. It was watered wine, half-frozen, tasting of the barrel. It was the best thing he had ever drunk.

"You look terrible," she told him.

"You should see yourself."

"Oh, I intend to. As soon as I find a mirror that won't sue me for damages." She tipped her head back against a boulder and closed her eyes. "For the record, I want it noted that I stole back a year of my own life once, and it nearly killed me, and I swore never again, and then I walked into a god's basement for a boy who once paid me in candied chestnuts. Candied chestnuts, Tam. You owe me interest."

"I'll pay you in chestnuts for the rest of my life," Tam said solemnly.

"See that you do." But her voice caught on the last word, and she turned her face away, and pretended to be very interested in the view.

Brannoc came last. He moved like a man carrying something heavy that no one else could see, and when he sat down among them he did it carefully, the way you'd sit down next to a fire you weren't sure was yours. His god had woken too, months ago — woken, looked out over the world, and passed over him without recognition. Twenty years of prayer, and the god didn't know his name. Tam had heard the story in fragments on the road down. He understood maybe a tenth of it. But he understood the look on Brannoc's face, because he'd worn it himself, the winter his father died and the gods stayed quiet.

"They're fading," Brannoc said, by way of greeting. "All of them. The ones that woke. They fed too fast — the riots, the temples overflowing, all those careless miracles — and now the worship's thinning out and they're starving again. By summer, I think, most of them will be quiet again. Properly quiet, this time. Dead quiet, some of them." He looked at Ines. "Yours is certainly done."

"Good," Ines said.

"It screamed when it died. Did you hear it?"

"Everyone in Corvane heard it."

"No." Brannoc shook his head. "Everyone in Corvane heard noise. I heard it scream. There's a difference." He rubbed his face with both hands. "It sounded like being ignored. Ten years of it, all at once. I almost pitied it." A pause. "Almost."

They were quiet for a while. The bells below kept ringing, softer now, finding something like harmony.

"Ossa will reopen the guild," Ines said eventually. "Under new rules. No more soul-trade. I don't know how I know that. I just do."

"How do you feel about that?" Sefa asked. "Going back?"

Ines considered it with the same careful attention she gave everything, the habit of precision surviving the loss of everything it had been precise about.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "Ask me after breakfast."

* * *

They came down off the mountain in the late morning, four ragged figures on a road littered with the debris of miracle and riot — fallen temple banners, scorched cobblestones, a market stall selling bottled prayers at three coppers each, marked down from ten. People stared at them. Word had spread, the way word does, growing stranger with every retelling: the Salt Guild's people had gone under the mountain and killed a god, and souls had rained loose over the city all night like warm snow, and everyone who'd lost someone to despair or debt or the old contracts had woken at dawn feeling oddly full, oddly forgiven, oddly *returned*.

An old woman stopped Ines in the road near the river gate. She gripped Ines's sleeve with both hands and said, "You're the one. You're the ledger-breaker." And Ines, who had no memory of any ledger worth breaking, said, "I might be. Someone probably ought to be," and the old woman kissed her burned hands and wept, and Tam watched Ines endure it with the bewildered patience of someone being thanked for a favor performed by a stranger.

By afternoon they'd reached the guildhouse. Ossa met them in the courtyard — older-looking than Tam remembered, or perhaps Tam was simply seeing clearly for the first time in a year. The Guildmaster's eyes went over each of them in turn, counting, the old auditor's reflex, and settled longest on Ines.

"You don't remember me," Ossa said.

"No," Ines said. "But my hands remember your office. And I'm told I struck myself from the rolls rather than abandon this boy." She put her hand on Tam's shoulder. "Whoever I was, I stand by her decision."

Something moved through Ossa's face — grief and respect tangled together, neither willing to yield to the other. "The offer stands," was all the Guildmaster said. "Whenever you want it. Whatever terms you write yourself."

* * *

That evening, in the small room above the kitchen that had always been Tam's, Ines sat on the edge of his cot while he ate soup — real soup, with actual meat in it, sent up from the kitchen by a cook who kept saying *bless you, bless you* through the door.

"You should sleep," Ines said.

"I've slept enough for a while. Weeks' worth, probably. Possibly a year's worth." He set down the bowl. "Ines. Can I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"When you cried, on the mountain. When you saw me wake up. You said you didn't know why."

"I said that, yes."

"Do you know now?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the city murmured — bells dying down, voices in the street, the ordinary evening sounds of a place deciding to keep existing.

"No," she said finally. "I've been turning it over all day. I know facts about you. Your sister's name is Wren. You're afraid of deep water and embarrassed about it. You once carried a message through a quarantine district with a fever of a hundred and two because you'd promised, and you keep promises past the point of sense. I know all of that the way I know entries in a ledger — clearly, precisely, and from the outside." She looked at her hands. "But the crying wasn't knowledge. It came from somewhere the ledger doesn't reach. Somewhere they didn't burn."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Here's the closest I can get: whatever I lost, I must have chosen to spend it on you. And whatever's left of me — the part that survived the burning — it looks at you and knows the purchase was good." She smiled, tired and true. "Love without its reasons. Apparently that's what I am now. Apparently that's enough."

Tam thought about the torn-out page in his own memory, the white gap where the throne had taken him. He thought about his grandmother, whose name he carried like a debt and now, somehow, like a flag. He thought about a year of his life sitting in some vault or grave or god's cold stomach, unreturnable, unrecoverable — a year he would simply not live, and would never miss, because you cannot miss what you never reach.

"We should write it down," he said suddenly.

Ines blinked. "Write what down?"

"All of it. Everything we almost lost. Names, debts, what happened, what it cost, what it was worth. Not in a guild ledger — in a real book, an honest one. Because the gods kept records and it made them monsters, and the guild kept records and it made them rich, and nobody — nobody — keeps records just so the people we love don't get forgotten." He was talking fast now, the way he talked when a delivery route came clear in his head all at once. "Someone should do that. Someone should keep an ordinary book."

Ines stared at him. Then she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright as a struck match.

"An ordinary book," she repeated. "Yes. Yes, all right. Tomorrow I'll buy one. With my last coin, if it comes to that." She stood, and paused at the door, and looked back at him with an expression he couldn't read — grief and gratitude and the beginning of something new, all layered like pages pressed together.

"Get some sleep, Tam Hale," she said. "You've got a life to get back to. Every hour of it."

The door closed. Tam lay back in his own bed, in his own body, light as a returned letter.

Down in the city, the last of the bells fell silent — and in the silence, from the direction of the mountain, so faint he might have imagined it, came a whisper. Not a voice. Not a prayer. Just the smallest stirring, like a coal refusing to go out.

He pulled the blanket up, and slept, and did not dream of thrones.

But Ines, walking the lamplit streets alone with her burned hands in her pockets, stopped mid-step outside a stationer's shuttered window — where a plain blank book sat displayed behind the glass — and felt, for no reason she could name, a cold finger trace the length of her spine.
