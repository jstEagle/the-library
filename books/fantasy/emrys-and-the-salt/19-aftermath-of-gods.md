## 19. Aftermath of Gods

The guildhall smelled of smoke and wet ash, and Ossa Vane-keeper — no, not Vane, he corrected himself for the hundredth time that morning; the name had been struck from the rolls along with hers — Guildmaster Ossa stood in the great counting room and counted what was left.

Not coin. Coin was the least of it. The vaults were intact; the Salt Guild had survived fires, floods, three succession disputes, and one memorable incident involving a tax assessor and a barrel of pickled eels. What could not be inventoried was everything else.

The ledgers were gone. Not lost, not stolen — *gone*, in the way a thing is gone when it has been unwritten. He had sent runners to the archive on the second day after the mountain screamed, and the runners came back pale and empty-handed. The shelves stood in their rows. The dust lay undisturbed where books should have been. When Ossa went himself and ran his fingers along the spines of empty air, he felt nothing at all, which was somehow worse than feeling grief. It was as if the guild had never kept accounts at all. As if forty years of his life had been spent maintaining a fiction so complete that even its destruction erased the memory of its existence.

Except he remembered. That was the strange mercy of it. Ines had burned her oath-page, and the master ledger below the mountain had unraveled like a rope with its knot cut out, and every soul bound in it had poured loose into the dark and then upward, into daylight, into bodies gasping awake on hillsides and riverbanks across half the city. But the guild's own records — the winter ledgers, the debt rolls, the contracts in ash — those had gone with it, because they had all been written in the same hand, trained by the same discipline, bound by the same clause.

Her clause. Her page. She had been the binding, and she had cut herself out, and the whole book had come apart in her fist.

Ossa had spent thirty years teaching her that hand. He supposed he ought to feel proud.

* * *

Corvane was learning to live without miracles, and it was going badly.

For eleven days after the Night of Small Miracles, the city had been drunk on answered prayer. The temples overflowed until worshipers stood in the streets, arms raised, shouting their wants at ceilings that had been silent for generations. A fisherman's wife prayed for her husband's boat and found it mended, tarred, and riding high at anchor by morning. A widow prayed for rain and got a flood. A boy prayed to be tall and woke with legs that ached for a week but carried him a head above his brothers.

And then the gods began to starve again.

It happened gradually enough that no one wanted to name it. The mended boat sprang its seams. The flood receded and took topsoil with it. The tall boy stopped growing and started forgetting things — small things first, the names of streets, the words to songs. The drowned child who had walked out of the river alive, the one everyone pointed to as proof, caught a fever in the third week and died quietly in her mother's bed, and this time no prayer moved anyone to answer.

The priests said the gods were tired. The priests said the gods were testing them. The priests said a great many things, in fuller and fuller temples, because fear filled pews faster than faith ever had. Ossa read the reports his runners brought him and recognized the pattern immediately, because it was the oldest pattern in his trade: a debtor who cannot pay begins by making excuses and ends by avoiding the creditor entirely. The gods of Corvane had woken hungry, gorged themselves on a fortnight of desperate worship, and were now discovering that a city's devotion is not an annuity. It is a loan. And they had spent it.

By the end of the month, three temples had closed. Not emptied — closed, doors barred from the inside, the priests unwilling to face congregations whose prayers had begun to curdle into demands, and then into accusations, and then, in the case of the temple of the Twin Sisters, into stones through windows. A fourth god, some river-thing whose name even Brannoc's researches had never recovered, simply stopped answering altogether, and its shrine on the Weepstone Bridge became a place where people left flowers for each other instead.

That was the one that made Ossa pause, reading the runner's report by lamplight. Flowers for each other. No god involved at all. He wondered if anyone had told the people leaving them that they had invented something new, or whether they knew, or whether it mattered.

He wrote in the margin of the report: *Note the date. Note the custom.* Some habits outlive their reasons. He had built a career on that principle, though he had never expected to see it demonstrated quite so literally.

* * *

The guild reopened on a grey Tuesday, under new rules.

Ossa had spent four sleepless nights drafting them, and another two cutting them down, because his first instinct had been to legislate against every horror the old ledgers had ever contained, and the resulting document had run to forty pages and read like a confession. The final version fit on a single sheet, which he nailed to the guildhall door beside the old brass plaque, and which read:

*The Salt Guild trades in borrowed years, bottled grief, and names. It does not trade in souls. Any contract touching the soul — one's own or another's — is void ab initio, unenforceable, and will be reported to the magistrates as attempted murder. This rule has no exception, no grandfather clause, and no price at which it bends.*

He had argued with himself about the last line for most of a night. There was always a price. That had been the guild's founding wisdom, older than any charter: everything has a price, and the guild's virtue is knowing it. To write *no price* was to write heresy in his own house.

But he had seen the court beneath the mountain. Well — he had seen Ines afterward, which amounted to the same thing. He had seen her come up out of the warrens carrying Tam Vane like a sack of grain, both of them scorched, both of them weeping, neither of them able to say why. He had watched her stand in the guildhall doorway with ash in her hair and look at the counting room — her counting room, the ledgers she had kept better than anyone he had ever trained — and he had watched her face do something he had no column in any book to record.

She had looked at the ledgers' absence and been *glad*.

No price. He nailed it to the door and let the heresy stand.

The city, predictably, did not thank him. Half of Corvane still believed the guild had engineered the whole catastrophe — that they had bottled the gods' hunger and sold it back at markup, or worse, that they had brokered the original soul-contracts and profited off every desperate grandmother who signed one. The second part was true, in the way that uncomfortable truths are always technically true and entirely useless. The guild had held the paper. The guild had collected the fees. Ossa had personally witnessed signatures on contracts whose terms he had not understood until a girl with ash in her hair explained them to him in three sentences and then walked out of his life.

He did not defend the guild in the broadsheets. He did not defend it in the temple squares. He did what he had always done: he opened the doors, he set fair prices, he honored every legitimate debt in full — and there were more than anyone expected, because the unwritten ledgers had not erased the debts themselves, only the record of them, and Corvane discovered in that season that its people were mostly honest. Debtors came forward unprompted, saying *I owed the guild a year, I want to settle it properly*. Creditors came forward saying *they owed me, prove it*. The guild became, for one strange autumn, less a bank than a court of memory, arbitrating claims that existed nowhere but in people's mouths and hearts.

Ossa found he did not hate it. He found, in fact, that he slept better than he had in twenty years, and he distrusted the finding deeply.

* * *

Sefa came to him in the fourth week, on a day when the fog off the Saltreach sat so thick the harbor bells sounded underwater.

She did not knock. She never knocked. She simply appeared in the doorway of his office with her hands in her coat pockets and the expression of someone about to make a withdrawal she hadn't earned.

"You're rebuilding," she said.

"I'm reopening. Rebuilding implies I know what shape it'll take."

"Same thing, for you." She dropped into the chair across from his desk without invitation, which was also her way. "I need to ask you something, and you're going to want to charge me for it, and I need you not to."

Ossa set down his pen. "Ask."

"The memory." She leaned forward. "Ines's. The day she took her oath. It's still in your vault — I checked before I broke in last time, and I checked again after, and it's still there. Sealed, catalogued, priced. You've got a piece of a woman's soul sitting in a drawer, Ossa, and she can't take it back yet, and every day it sits there it's—" She stopped herself, jaw working. "It's a hostage. That's what it is. However you dress it up."

"It is collateral," Ossa said, "against a debt she incurred voluntarily, under contract, with full disclosure—"

"She didn't have full disclosure. She didn't even have her own *name* by the end of it. You know what the god demanded. You read the terms when you countersigned."

He had. That was the wound of it, the one he kept turning over at night like a stone with a sharp edge: he had countersigned. His seal sat beneath hers on the contract of passage, and beneath both, in handwriting he now recognized as the god's own — because it matched the training, because it matched *her*, because everything he had taught her had been copied from something that ate souls — the clause that required her to forget everyone she loved before swearing. He had put ink to that page. He had thought himself a careful man.

"The debt is real," he said at last. "Whatever else is true, the debt is real. She traded the memory for passage, and passage was given, and the guild honors its side of every bargain. If I simply hand it back, then every contract the guild has ever written becomes a suggestion. Then the new rules on my door become suggestions. Then we are nothing but a charity with a brass plaque, and charities starve, Sefa, I have watched them starve."

"And if you keep it?"

"Then I keep my word."

They looked at each other across the desk, thief and guildmaster, two professions separated by a signature.

"I'm not asking you to give it away," Sefa said finally, and her voice changed, lost its edge, became almost gentle, which from Sefa was more alarming than a knife. "I'm asking you to change the terms. That's what you do, isn't it? That's the whole trade. Nobody pays what the contract says; everybody pays what the contract *means*. So mean something better." She stood. "She's not coming back for it, Ossa. She's not coming back for anything. She bought a blank book with her last coin. A *blank book*. Do you understand what that means? She's writing it all down herself now. Names. Debts forgiven. She forgave *my* debts, and I stole from her twice." At the door she paused. "You could be in that book. If you wanted. I don't think it's too late. But it's getting later."

Then she was gone, and the fog rolled through the doorway after her like it had been listening.

Ossa sat for a long time. Then he unlocked the lower drawer, took out the sealed jar — small, ordinary, a curl of grey light turning slowly inside like smoke in water — and set it on his desk, and looked at it, and did not open it, and did not put it away either.

* * *

He went to find her on the first clear morning of winter, when frost furred the gutters of Tanner's Row and the sky over Corvane was the color of a blade.

Her workshop sat between a chandler's and a shuttered chapel, which Ossa considered either irony or providence depending on his mood. Smoke rose from its chimney. Through the window he could see lamplight, and shadows moving — several of them, Brannoc's broad shoulders bent over something, Sefa's quick hands dealing cards or knives, a smaller figure chasing a cat. Tam. Alive, upright, laughing at something. The year he'd sold still missing from him somewhere, a gap in his stride that only the people who loved him could see.

Ossa stood outside in the cold for longer than was dignified. He had prepared a speech. He had prepared, in fact, three speeches and a formal document offering restoration to the rolls with full seniority, back pay, and a seat at the council table, all of it drafted in his best hand, all of it folded in his breast pocket, all of it suddenly weighing as much as the mountain.

Because here was the truth he had carried up from that doorway weeks ago, the one he had not let himself finish thinking: she would not come back. Not because of anger. Anger he could work with; anger had rates and terms. But she had looked at the absence of the ledgers and been glad, and a man could not compete with gladness. He could offer her everything the guild was. He could not offer her a reason to want it.

He knocked anyway.

Ines opened the door with a pen behind her ear and ink on three fingers, and when she saw him her expression did something complicated — surprise, and under it something older and warmer that she perhaps could not name, because the day she'd taken her oath was still sealed in a jar on his desk, and whatever affection had been forged in those years lived now in her without its reasons, the way Tam's laugh lived in her, the way tears had come to her on the mountainside that she couldn't explain.

"Guildmaster," she said.

"Ines." He cleared his throat. "I've brought you something. Two things. One is a document you'll likely refuse."

"Likely."

"One is a jar you may not be able to use yet." He held it out between them, the grey light turning inside. "Sefa spoke to me. She was right, which I resent. The debt stands — I won't pretend otherwise, and you'd distrust me if I did. But the terms are changed. The memory is yours, to reclaim when you're able, on your schedule, at no further cost. Consider the original contract amended by mutual consent of the holder." A dry pause. "One of us consents retroactively. I've decided not to consult you on which."

Ines took the jar in both hands and looked into the smoke-grey turn of it, and Ossa watched her watch a piece of herself she couldn't remember missing.

"There was a day," she said quietly, "when I swore myself to something. Everyone tells me so. Brannoc says I gave up everyone I loved to do it. I look at all of you and I believe it, the way you believe a ledger entry you didn't write." She glanced up. "I'd like to remember choosing wrong. Someday. When I can afford it."

"That," said Ossa, "is the most guildsmanlike sentence anyone has said to me in a month."

From inside, Tam's voice: "Ines! Is that the moneylender? Tell him dinner's in an hour and refusing isn't an option!"

"He means it," Ines said. "Refusing isn't an option. House rules. They're on the door."

There was, indeed, a sheet of paper nailed crookedly beside the doorframe. Ossa read it. *House rules: 1. Knock if you're a stranger. 2. Don't knock if you're family. 3. Debts between people in this house are forgiven on entry. 4. Dinner is at seven.* Below, in different hands, various amendments and grievances, initialed like signatories to a charter.

Ossa read rule three twice.

"You understand," he said, "that as written, this constitutes an unlicensed clearinghouse for debt forgiveness, operating without bond, without oversight, and without—"

"Without a single brass plaque," Ines agreed. "We're shameless."

He should have gone back to the guildhall. He had a council meeting at noon, and the magistrates wanted a statement on the temple riots, and there was a debtor on Harbor Street who claimed the guild owed *him* three years on the strength of a dream. All of it waited, orderly and demanding, the great machine of Corvane grinding on toward spring.

Instead Ossa Vane-keeper — Guildmaster Ossa, he corrected himself, and then, standing on that threshold, abandoned the correction altogether — stepped inside, hung his coat on the peg marked with a carved little fish that no one could explain, and sat down at a scarred table among thieves and broken priests and runners with gaps in their stride, while a girl who had once kept his ledgers better than God kept sinners ladled out stew.

Before eating, Tam insisted they go around the table and each say one thing worth remembering, because that was the house custom now, invented by nobody, obeyed by everybody.

When it reached Ossa, he thought of the empty archive shelves, and the flowers on the Weepstone Bridge, and the jar of grey light waiting in a workshop drawer for a woman to grow rich enough in other ways to buy back her pain. He thought of forty years of entries, and the one entry he had never learned to write.

"My name," he said, "is Ossa. I was Guildmaster of the Salt Guild. And today I am having dinner with my family, and the accounts," he paused, and found, to his mild astonishment, that his eyes were stinging, "the accounts are finally balanced."

Tam wrote it down in the ordinary book, word for word, and underlined it twice.

Outside, the frost thickened on the glass, and far beneath the mountain, in a dead court where one warm stone whispered to no one, the last silence of the old world kept its counsel — and in a plain book on a plain table, in a hand that had once bound souls and now recorded recipes, the new world began, entry by entry, to write itself down.
