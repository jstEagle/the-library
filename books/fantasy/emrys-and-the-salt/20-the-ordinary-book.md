## 20. The Ordinary Book

The book cost me my last coin.

I want that recorded plainly, because everything else in this account will be suspect — a woman who spent ten years keeping ledgers for the Salt Guild does not lose her head for arithmetic overnight. One copper bit, worn smooth as a river stone, pressed into the palm of a stationer on Tanner's Row who didn't know me from any other hollow-eyed customer. He had a shelf of fine things: calfskin tooled with gilt corners, vellum that gleamed like frost, books whose covers promised importance.

I took the plainest one he had. Unbleached paper, boards wrapped in grey linen, no title, no crest, no lock. When I set the coin in his hand he looked at it, then at me, and said, "That's all you've got?"

"That's all I need," I said, and discovered it was true, and was so startled by the truth of it that I nearly laughed in his shop.

He must have thought me mad. Perhaps I was, a little. A month out from the mountain, and I still caught myself reaching for a ledger that no longer existed, still woke at dawn with my fingers curled around a pen that wasn't there. The guild had given me back nothing when they struck me from the rolls — no severance, no blessing, just the absence of my name where my name used to be. Ossa had offered, later, to restore it. I hadn't answered yet. Some entries you leave blank until you understand what belongs in them.

But the book. The book I understood immediately, the way you understand thirst.

* * *

The workshop on Candlemaker's Lane had been Sefa's find, because of course it was. She'd appeared on my doorstep three days after the mountain came down silent — three days of me sitting in a rented room staring at walls, unable to explain to anyone, least of all myself, why grief and relief were sharing the same chair inside my chest.

"You look terrible," she'd said, shouldering past me into the room. "Also, I found us a place. Also, Brannoc's coming, don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were composing the argument. I could see it behind your eyes. You have very legible eyes for someone who spent a decade being paid not to show anything." She'd dropped onto my bed like she owned it, boots and all. "Two rooms above an old chandler's shop. Smells like tallow and regret, which is honest, at least. Rent's cheap because the last tenant swears the cellar's haunted."

"Is it?"

"No idea. We'll find out together. That's rather the point of us now, isn't it?" And she'd grinned at me, that crooked thief's grin, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the throne of prayer-stones screamed its last scream — loosened, just slightly, just enough to breathe around.

So: Candlemaker's Lane. The shop below, which we were slowly turning into something between a scrivener's office and a repair stall — Sefa mended locks and picked the ones that couldn't be mended honestly; I took in copying work, contracts and wills and letters for people who couldn't write, ordinary ink for ordinary words. Brannoc lived above and pretended he was only passing through. He'd been pretending that for five weeks.

On the first evening in the new rooms, I sat at the workbench with my plain grey book open to its first page, and I could not write anything.

Not because I had nothing to say. Because I had spent so long writing in a language of debt — principal, interest, term, forfeiture — that my hand kept trying to structure memory that way. *Item: one soul, returned. Item: one god, deceased. Item: one oath, broken, value—* Value. Everything had to have a value. That was the grammar the guild had taught me, and beneath the guild, older and quieter, the thing I'd sworn to had taught me the same lesson in a different alphabet: every feeling weighed, every attachment priced, every love entered as a liability against the self.

I put down the pen. I went upstairs and made tea, badly, because Brannoc had bought the wrong kind and Sefa refused to drink anything that tasted like "boiled hay," and we argued about it, and somewhere in the middle of the argument I realized I'd gone a full hour without thinking about ledgers at all.

That was the first entry, when I finally came back down:

*Tam laughed today. He says the scar on his palm looks like a fish. It does not. It looks like a lightning bolt, but I told him the fish story was better, so now it is officially a fish.*

It isn't a record anyone would pay for. It wouldn't balance. There is no column it fits in.

That is precisely why I keep it.

* * *

Tam comes by most evenings, when his shifts at the ropewalk let him. His sister Lira comes with him more often now — the debt that started everything is forgiven, struck through in a ledger that burned, but old habits of worry die hard, and Tam still checks on her like a man checking a wound that has already healed. He brings bread, usually, or gossip, always.

Tonight he sits on my workbench because chairs are for other people, swinging his heels against the drawer-fronts, watching me copy a widow's will in my best fair hand.

"You're doing it again," he says.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you're adding something up." He taps his own temple. "You do it when you think nobody's looking. You did it before, too. Before the mountain."

I set down the pen. This is the strange arithmetic of what happened under Corvane: I remember him. I remember all of them. What I lost was narrower than they fear and stranger than they guess — the day itself, the oath-day, gone like a page cut cleanly from a book, edges smooth, no ragged stub left behind. I remember loving them. I remember choosing them, in the warrens, in the dark, with the god's voice pouring tenderness into my ears like warm oil. What I don't remember is whatever I was before I belonged to them, and some nights that absence itches like a missing tooth.

"Do you ever miss it?" Tam asks suddenly. "Your oath? Everyone keeps asking me if I miss the year I sold, and I don't know how to answer, because how do you miss a year you can't remember living?"

I consider lying to him. I am, apparently, constitutionally incapable of it anymore — the god's parting gift, or curse, or simply the shape left behind when a lie gets burned out of you at the root.

"I miss having a reason," I say. "For being the way I am. Precise, and — careful. People used to assume the oath made me that way. Now I have to wonder if I was always like this, and the oath just gave me permission." I turn the widow's will toward him. "Look. She's leaving her good blanket to the neighbor who sat with her husband at the end, and her ring to a granddaughter she hasn't seen in nine years, and she's written a whole paragraph explaining why the ring matters, and none of it is legally necessary. It's just — true things, written down so they survive her."

Tam reads it. His throat works. "That's what your book is, isn't it. The grey one."

"It's what the book is trying to be. I'm out of practice at true things that aren't debts."

"Read me some."

So I read him the fish entry, and the one about Sefa teaching Brannoc to pick pockets as a joke and Brannoc turning out to be appallingly good at it ("the gods waste their gifts"), and the one about the cellar ghost, which turned out to be a family of ferrets, whom Sefa has named and refuses to evict. Tam laughs until he wheezes, and then goes quiet, and then says, "Put my name in it again. Just — whenever you get to the end of a page, if there's room. Put my name in."

"Why?"

"So it's in a book that doesn't eat people."

I wrote his name four times that night, in the margins, in the corners, small as a signature and as permanent as one.

* * *

Brannoc moved in properly on a Tuesday, though he announced it as though it were a temporary military arrangement.

"The temple sent for me again," he said, standing in our doorway with two bags and a face like a man reporting a defeat he refuses to call one. "They want me to lead the morning devotions. The Dawnfather's congregation has tripled since the waking, and half of them are praying to be noticed, and the other half are praying to be left alone, and the priests can't tell the difference anymore."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the Dawnfather doesn't know my name." He set the bags down inside the door, which was, I noted, the entire negotiation concluded. "Twenty years I served him. Twenty years of prayers, fasts, vigils in the snow. And when he finally opened his eyes — that night on the mountain, when everything was screaming — I called him by his secret name, the name they give you at ordination, the name that's supposed to mean *he sees you*. And he looked at me." Brannoc's hands were steady. His voice almost wasn't. "He looked right through me, Ines. Like I was weather. Like I was one more mouth making noise at him while he ate."

Sefa, from the stairs, said quietly: "You bought us time with that look. Whatever it cost, it bought Tam back."

"I know what it bought." He rubbed his face. "That's not — I'm not ungrateful. I'm just learning what I actually believed in, now that the object of it is standing there being disappointing in person." He looked up, and there was something underneath the bitterness, something almost shy. "The rooms above the shop. Are they —"

"Yours," I said. "As long as you want them."

"And the rent?"

"There isn't any. There's a list on the kitchen wall. Chores. You're on latrine duty until further notice."

Sefa cackled. Brannoc protested. The ferrets, sensing commotion, poured up through the floorboards to supervise, and within the hour the three of us were arguing about whether a priest could be excommunicated by rodents, and I slipped away to the workbench and added an entry:

*Brannoc lives here now. He thinks he's visiting. Nobody corrects him. Some fictions are load-bearing.*

* * *

Autumn came down the valley and settled over Corvane like dust on furniture. The miracles thinned. Whatever the waking gods had fed on during those first frantic weeks — worship, attention, the raw meat of answered prayer — they seemed to be finding less of it, or wanting less, and the city adjusted the way cities do: quickly, cynically, with new festivals built on top of old fears. The temples emptied by degrees. The Salt Guild reopened under rules Ossa himself had written, the first line of which read, in his own cramped hand: *Nothing that cannot be returned shall be traded.* They dealt now in years still freely given, in grief bottled only with consent, in names sold for a season and bought back at a fair rate. Ossa sent me a copy of the new rulebook with a note: *You would have hated drafting this. You would have loved enforcing it. The offer stands.*

I put the note in the grey book. I haven't answered. Not yet. Some doors you leave ajar until you know whether you're the person who walks through them.

On a raw evening in late autumn, rain needling the windows, Sefa found me at the bench with the book open and my pen idle, and dropped into the chair across from me with the particular gravity she reserves for serious thefts.

"I've been thinking about what you lost," she said.

"Sefa —"

"Not the memory. I know you can't miss what you can't remember, I stole enough of my own years to know how that works." She spun a copper bit across her knuckles — my copper bit, actually, lifted off the mantel an hour ago, and I let her keep it because it made her happy. "I mean the rest of it. The shape. You were somebody's instrument for ten years, and then you broke the instrument, and now everybody's waiting to see what you'll build instead. Including you. Especially you." She flipped the coin, caught it. "So here's my professional opinion, free of charge, which should alarm you."

"Go on."

"You keep waiting for someone to assign you. The god assigned you. The guild assigned you. Even breaking the oath — part of you treated that like an assignment, didn't it? Something the situation required." Her eyes were sharp and kind at once, a combination she'd deny under torture. "Nobody's assigning you anything now. That's not a void, Ines. That's a vacancy. Fill it with whatever you damn well choose."

She got up, ruffled my hair in the way guaranteed to make me scowl, and went upstairs to torment Brannoc about dinner.

I sat for a long time. Then I turned to a fresh page, and at the top, in letters larger than my usual hand, I wrote:

*This book has no terms, no interest, no penalties, and no god. Entries herein are made freely, by choice, by the undersigned.*

And beneath it, my name. My own name, chosen, unsworn, belonging to no one — Ines Vane, of Candlemaker's Lane, keeper of an ordinary book.

Then, because identity is also made of smaller things, I added: *Recipe, Sefa's mother's stew, as dictated under duress: she claims the secret is saffron. The secret is butter. Do not tell her I know.*

* * *

Winter's first frost arrived on a morning when the shop smelled of ink and woodsmoke and the ferrets had gotten into the paper stock again. I was alone below, copying a ferryman's contract, when I heard the footsteps stop outside the door — unhurried, deliberate, the tread of someone who had stood there before deciding to knock.

Ossa came in without waiting, which was answer enough to the question of whether the visit was social. Guildmaster's robes, guildmaster's cane, guildmaster's eyes that missed nothing, including the grey book lying open on my bench.

"Ines."

"Guildmaster."

"Still angry with me?"

"No," I said, and found it was true. "Still disagreeing with you. Those aren't the same thing anymore. I'm told that's growth."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; Ossa rationed those like wartime bread. "The rolls are open. Your name can be restored with a word. The new rules need an auditor who understands what the old ones cost." He laid a folded paper on my bench, next to the ferryman's contract, next to the book. "No hurry. But before you decide, you should know why I came in person."

He nodded toward the window, toward the east, where the mountain stood black against the pale sky.

"There's smoke," he said. "Thin, and grey, and coming from a mine shaft that's been sealed for sixty years. And the miners won't go near it. They say the mountain is whispering." He met my eyes. "They say it's whispering names."

My hand, without my permission, had already closed around the grey book.

*End of Chapter 20*
