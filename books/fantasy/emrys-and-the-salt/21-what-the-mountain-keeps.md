## 21. What the Mountain Keeps

The mountain had not forgiven anyone. That was the first thing Ines understood, standing at the mouth of the old adit where the warrens began — the rock itself had a grudge now, a low tectonic sulk she felt through her boot soles, like standing on the chest of something sleeping badly. A year since the burning. A year since the master ledger came apart in her hands like wet bread and ten thousand souls went up through the stone in a column of light that half of Corvane swore they saw and the other half swore was weather.

She had promised herself she would never come back.

She had also promised herself, once, that she would never break an oath, and look how that had gone. Promises, she had learned, were just entries waiting to be amended.

The guild's new surveyors had mapped the upper warrens after the collapse — Ossa's doing, part of his penance-by-bureaucracy, cataloguing every shaft and gallery the dying god had cracked open so no child of Corvane would wander into them the way Tam had sleepwalked into them. The maps stopped at the Tollgate of Names, because the surveyors' nerve stopped there too. One of them, a gray-faced man named Pell, had told her: *Past the gate it's wrong, mistress. Not dangerous-wrong. Wrong like a room where someone died and nobody opened the windows.*

He wasn't wrong. But he hadn't gone far enough down to learn what Ines had learned, which was that the mountain kept things. It kept the grooves worn by cart wheels two hundred years dead. It kept the soot handprints of miners who had signed their names in ash on pillar-stones for luck. And it kept, apparently, one prayer-stone still warm.

Sefa had told her about it thirdhand, the way Sefa told everything — sideways, with a shrug pretending to be indifference. A scavenger crew had gone down past the tollgate in autumn, chasing rumors of silver in the flooded galleries, and come back up fast and pale, refusing their pay. All except one boy, sixteen maybe, who wouldn't stop talking about what he'd heard in the throne hall. *A voice,* he said. *A woman's voice, or near enough. It knew my mother's name. It knew the song she used to sing when I was sick.* He'd had to be dragged out by his collar. He'd been crying without any expression on his face at all, which was somehow worse than sobbing.

Ines had listened to all of this over supper in the workshop, Brannoc frowning into his stew, Sefa watching her sidelong, Tam — Tam mercifully oblivious, laughing at something, alive, whole, one year short and never asking why everyone flinched at arithmetic around him.

*Don't,* Sefa had said afterward, on the stairs, quietly. *Whatever you're thinking. Don't.*

*I'm not thinking anything.*

*You're thinking in ledger columns. I can see it. You're weighing whether it's still owed.*

And Ines had said nothing, because Sefa was right, and because some debts you don't pay, you visit.

* * *

She went alone. She told herself it was because Brannoc's knees were bad and Sefa would make jokes in the dark and both of those were true, but the truer reason was simpler and uglier: whatever waited below was hers. Her god. Her oath. Her handwriting, once, in a book older than empires. You didn't bring family to a thing like that. You brought yourself, and you brought a lamp, and you brought the ordinary book in your coat pocket against your heart, because if the mountain wanted to trade in memories today it could bloody well see what memory looked like when a person chose it.

The descent took most of a day. The warrens were quieter than she remembered — no servitors, of course; the masked things had crumbled to salt and iron filings within hours of the god's death, and the tunnels were littered with their leavings, drifts of white grit that whispered under her boots. No summons either. Whatever thread had once run from the mountain into Tam's sleeping body was cut, cauterized, gone. She checked anyway, three times, pressing her palm flat to the stone and listening with the part of her that used to listen for a silent god. Nothing answered. Nothing even pretended to.

That was its own kind of grief. She'd spent ten years praying into silence and hating the silence, and now the silence was total and honest and she found she missed even the false version of company, the way you miss a draft from a window after you've bricked it up.

At the Tollgate of Names she stopped. The gate still stood — a slab of black basalt carved with a mouth that wasn't a mouth, the servitor that had guarded it reduced to a mound of salt at its base like snow that refused spring. The demand was still cut into the stone, though: SURRENDER A TRUE NAME. She remembered Sefa giving a stolen one, easy as tipping a beggar. She remembered Brannoc giving his god's secret name, the word that had cost him everything twice over. She remembered cutting through instead, because she had never had a name worth surrendering — only a title, *Ledger-Keeper*, and titles belonged to offices, not people.

She pressed her hand to the basalt. Cold. Dead cold, colder than the air, cold in a way that felt deliberate, like a door locked from the inside.

"Nothing here for you," she told it, and walked through the gap she'd cut a year ago, which the mountain had not healed. Some wounds even stone declined to close.

* * *

The throne hall was exactly as she'd left it, and that was the horror of it.

The court beneath the mountain had died mid-gesture. The fused prayer-stones of the throne still rose in their terrible spiral, hundreds of thousands of prayers compressed by hunger into mineral — every one of them a wish somebody had made in desperation, in faith, in love, all of it eaten and compacted and displayed. The dais where the Silent God had manifested was a scar of glassy melt. The soul-ledgers, the ones older than the guild's, lay where they had fallen when the master book unraveled: shelves buckled, spines split, pages drifted across the floor like the world's largest and saddest snowfall. She knelt and picked one up. Blank. Every page blank. When the binding clause burned, everything written in the god's hand had un-happened — debts voided, sales unwritten, souls released. Ten thousand contracts erased retroactively, and the paper left holding nothing, the way a wound leaves a scar holding nothing.

Except one shelf, high on the wall behind the throne's melted root, where a single object remained.

It was small. Fist-sized. A prayer-stone, but unlike any she'd seen in the court — the others had been gray, compressed, mute as river cobbles. This one was dark, almost black, veined with faint lines of amber light that moved. Not pulsed. *Moved*, slow as honey, like something turning over in its sleep.

And it was warm. She knew it before she touched it, knew it the way you know a lit stove in a cold room, the way Tam must have known the mountain calling him through three miles of rock and a year of borrowed time. Warmth has a voice if you're listening right.

She did not touch it. She stood six feet away with her lamp held up and her ordinary book against her ribs and she made herself look at it the way she had once audited ledgers: item by item, line by line, no sentiment permitted until the totals were struck.

"You're a fragment," she said aloud. Her voice fell flat in the dead hall, swallowed by all that blank paper. "A crumb. The last bit of it that didn't burn."

The amber veins shifted. And the stone spoke.

Not in sound, exactly. In memory. That was how the Silent God had always spoken — not words but recollections, delivered whole into the mind like coins dropped into a palm. Ines had forgotten that. Or rather, the forgetting of it had been taken from her along with everything else, and now the mechanism of it came back with the first whisper, and her knees nearly went.

*—the smell of the guild kitchen on the morning of your first winter, tallow and burnt barley, and Tam asleep in the corner with his head on a sack of salt because his sister's fever kept the whole tenement awake and this was the only quiet left in Corvane—*

"No," Ines said.

*—the weight of Ossa's hand on your shoulder when you were promoted, heavier than you expected, warmer than you expected, and how you stood very straight so he wouldn't feel you shaking—*

"I said no." Her voice cracked on the second word. She hated that. She planted her feet. "I know what you are. I've read this contract before. I wrote half of them."

The warmth deepened. The amber brightened, patient, unhurried. It had hoarded patience for centuries; a year of death had not taught it urgency.

*—the day you took your oath. You think it's gone. It isn't gone. It is right here. I kept it safe, little keeper. I keep everything. Come and take it back and you will remember the face of the priest who spoke the words, the color of the rope, the name you surrendered — your true name, the one you've missed every single day without knowing what the missing was—*

And there it was. The hook, baited with the exact thing she could not buy anywhere else in the world.

Because that was the wound under all the other wounds. Everyone assumed the lost memory that hurt was the oath-day itself, the ceremony, the vow. It wasn't. It was what the vow had required: the god had demanded she forget everyone she loved before swearing — and somewhere in that amputated day was the record of *who they had been*. Her mother's laugh. Whoever had taught her numbers. The person she'd been before the guild, before the ledger, before the silence — a whole childhood sealed in a stone like a letter never sent. Sefa had recovered the pawned memory from the vaults a year ago; Ines had read her own recorded oath and learned its price, and had chosen, deliberately, painfully, not yet to reabsorb it, because the memory was tangled with the oath and the oath was tangled with the god and she had not trusted what might come back attached.

But this fragment was offering it clean. Or claiming to. That was the thing about gods — even dead ones, even crumbs of them — they were salesmen to the marrow. *Safekeeping*, it called it. The same word it had used on Tam's grandmother, on every desperate faithful who had signed a soul away for a miracle and called it a bargain because the alternative was watching someone die.

Ines sat down on the floor of the dead court, cross-legged, lamp between her knees, and let it talk.

Not because she was tempted. Well — because she was tempted. Terribly. Honesty was the whole discipline of the new life; she wrote it in the ordinary book every week like a prescription. *Entry: I wanted to open the vault today. Entry: I dreamed about my mother's hands and woke up angry. Entry: temptation is not a failure, feeding it is.* So she sat and she let the stone pour its inventory into her, and she wept, quietly, without drama, the way you bleed from a wound you've already decided not to die of, and she listened to her own stolen life described back to her in fragments like torn letters:

*—a woman singing off-key in a language Ines no longer knew, kneading dough, flour to the elbows—*

*—a boy, maybe a brother? teaching her to count on river stones, cheating, letting her win—*

*—rain on a ferry roof, and someone's arm around her shoulders, and the enormous uncomplicated safety of being young and loved and not yet sold—*

Each one landed like a coin in an empty bowl. Each one rang. Each one was gone again before she could hold it, because the stone gave tastes, not meals. Hoarders didn't give. Hoarders *lent*.

When the flood finally slowed, she wiped her face with her sleeve, the way Tam did, and stood up, and her legs shook and held.

"Here's what I know about you," she said. "I audited you for years without knowing it. Your handwriting trained mine. And I know what a deferred payment looks like. This—" she nodded at the warm stone, "—isn't generosity. It's a lure. You can't restore anything. You're a last ember in a dead hearth, and the only food left in this whole mountain is me, and the only currency I have that you can eat is wanting. So you're going to show me my mother until I reach for you. And the moment I reach — the moment I *need* — that's the meal. That's how it always worked. That's how Tam's grandmother ended up in a throne. That's how a whole city's prayers ended up packed into mineral like coal."

The amber veins stilled. For a long moment the hall was so quiet she could hear the blank pages settling, one soft tick at a time, like the last grains of an hourglass.

Then, smaller, thinner, almost pitiable — and she recognized the register instantly, because she'd heard it in the audience chamber a year ago, wearing the voice of family, wearing the voice of forgiveness, wearing whatever voice opened the door:

*—you'll be alone with this. Forever. Is that what you want? To be a woman with a hole in her, walking around, calling it freedom?*

And Ines laughed. It startled her, coming out of her, rough and real, echoing off the fused prayers of ten thousand dead wishes.

"That's the difference between us," she said. "I have a hole. You *are* one. And I filled mine with people. You tried to fill yours with everyone else, and look at you."

She crouched, brought her face level with the stone, close enough to feel its warmth on her chin like breath.

"My mother sang off-key," she told it. "I don't need to remember it to know it's true. Someone loved me before I was worth anything, and I don't need the receipt. That's the whole secret, you starved old thing. That's why you never understood worship. We don't pray because we remember. We remember because we love. And love doesn't live in stones. It lives—" she tapped her own chest, over the ordinary book, "—here. In the keeping. Which was supposed to be *your* job. You had one job."

She straightened. Took out the ordinary book, more for the ritual than the record, and wrote by lamplight while the stone watched with its warm amber patience:

*Entry: Found one prayer-stone, warm, behind the throne. Contents: stolen memories, offered as bait. Disposition: left unfed. Note — it will still be warm when I'm dust. Note — that is not my problem anymore. Note — some silences deserve to stay broken, and some voices deserve to stay unheard, and the difference is whether they ask or take. This one takes.*

She read it over. Added: *Note — her name was Maren. I don't know how I know that. Maybe I never forgot everything. Maybe some things were too small to steal.*

She closed the book. Put it away. Picked up her lamp.

"Don't wait up," she said, and turned her back on it, and walked.

Behind her, fainter and fainter, the whispers followed — her mother's off-key song, the river stones, the rain on the ferry roof — growing thinner with every step, a seller's cry fading down an empty street, until the dark of the warrens closed over it entirely and there was nothing left in the mountain's keeping that wanted anything from her at all.

* * *

Daylight, when she came out of the adit, was the low gold of late afternoon, and it hit her face like a hand laid gently on a fevered forehead. She stood blinking in it, gritty, stiff, wrung out, smelling of salt and lamp oil and old stone, and she let the sun do its work.

Tam was sitting on the boundary wall.

She should have known. Of course he was. He was nineteen now and ran messages for the rebuilt guild and had a runner's genius for knowing where people would be before they knew themselves. He'd probably worked out her destination the moment she'd lied about visiting the salt-works. He swung his legs, watching her come up the path, and didn't ask a single question, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"You're crying," he said.

"I'm not."

"You are. Kind of." He dug in his pocket and produced, with the gravity of a physician, a slightly linty handkerchief. "Here. Also I brought rolls. They're from Marta's and they're terrible, she burns them on purpose now, she says the char keeps off evil. I think she just likes burning things since the riots."

Ines took the handkerchief. Blew her nose with tremendous dignity. Sat down next to him on the wall, and the mountain lay behind them, gray and cooling in the evening light, keeping what it kept.

"Do you want to talk about it?" Tam asked.

"No," she said honestly.

"Good," he said. "Marta says talking about things is how you catch cold." He handed her a roll. It was, indeed, half charcoal. She ate it anyway, and it tasted like daylight, and like the particular flavor of a boy who had once sold a year of his life and gotten it back and never needed to know the price, because someone else had paid it gladly and would do it again.

They sat while the sun went down. Somewhere below them, in the dark under the mountain, a warm stone whispered to no one, unfed, unvisited, keeping a woman's off-key song for an audience of blank pages.

Ines finished her roll, brushed the char from her fingers, and thought about what tonight's entry would say. Something small. Something true. *Entry: went back. Left it hungry. Came home to bad bread and good company.* And then, because the discipline demanded honesty even when it cost:

*Entry: her name was Maren. I am going to find out who remembers her.*
