## 22. Entries, Continued

The wedding was held in the courtyard behind Ines's workshop, because Tam's sister Wren had declared that she would be married under no roof but the sky's, and because the sky over Corvane in late spring had finally learned to be kind again.

Ines had spent three days preparing for it, and none of that preparation had involved ledgers. She had hung lanterns from the workshop eaves — Sefa stole half of them, then returned them with better wicks. Brannoc had built the arch out of old mine timber he'd carried up from the warrens himself, refusing to explain why, though everyone understood: wood that had already been buried deserved a second life in the light. Ossa came early, in plain clothes rather than guild gray, and stood awkwardly by the well until Tam dragged him into helping set benches.

And on the table beneath the arch, where other weddings might have put gifts or flowers, Ines set her book.

It was an ordinary thing. She had bought it with her last coin two days after the mountain stopped shaking, from a stationer on Candlemaker's Row who had tried to sell her something finer — vellum, gilt corners, a spine stitched in silk. She had run her thumb along it and felt nothing. It was beautiful the way a god is beautiful, and she wanted no part of that anymore. The plain book beside it, bound in undyed leather gone soft at the corners, cost almost nothing and had already survived one spilled kettle, one rainstorm, and one attempt by Sefa to use it as a coaster.

A year of entries filled it now. Not accounts. Not contracts. Just what happened, written down so it wouldn't depend on anyone's memory to keep it — not hers, not a god's, not even the city's.

Wren found her there an hour before the ceremony, adjusting the book's position for the fourth time.

"You're going to read from it," Wren said. Not a question. "During."

"If you'll have it."

"It's your book." Wren was twenty now, tall and quick like her brother, with their grandmother's stubborn jaw. She wore a dress Sefa had "acquired" and then paid for, once Ines raised an eyebrow. "But Ines — you don't have to make it a record. You can just let today be today."

"I intend to do both." Ines straightened the book one last time. "That's rather the point of me now."

Wren laughed and hugged her, quick and fierce, and went off to argue with the florist about lilies.

* * *

They married at midday, when the light came straight down into the courtyard and turned everything gold. The priest was a young woman from one of the new congregations — the kind that had sprung up in the empty temples after the gods faded, congregations with no miracles in them at all, only songs and shared meals and the radical idea that people might be good to each other without divine supervision. Brannoc had vetted her personally. He sat through the whole ceremony with his arms crossed and his face arranged into skepticism, and Ines saw him mouth every word of the blessing along with her, because he still knew them all by heart, and some things a man carries whether or not he believes them.

Tam cried first. He made no effort to hide it. He stood as witness with his borrowed-year face — he was seventeen now in every way but the ledger of his body, which said sixteen, and would always say sixteen, one year short forever — and he wept openly and gloriously while Wren said her vows, and Sefa put an arm around him and pretended her own eyes weren't shining.

Ossa gave Wren away. Nobody had planned this. He simply stepped forward when the moment came, as if the arithmetic of it were obvious — the girl whose grandmother's debt had started everything, given by the man who had once argued it was better to let her brother go. Debts honored by changing the terms. He'd taught her that, in his cold way, and she had never forgotten it, and neither had he.

When the vows were done and the courtyard erupted, Ines rose from her bench, took up her ordinary book, and walked to the arch.

The crowd quieted. They knew. Half of Corvane's survivors knew what the Salt Guild had been, what the mountain had kept, what had burned beneath it a year ago this month. The other half had heard the stories secondhand and disbelieved most of them, which Ines considered healthy.

"Tradition says the eldest speaks at a wedding," she said. "I'm not anyone's elder. I'm just the one who writes things down. So I'm going to read you the record."

She opened the book to the first page.

* * *

"*Entry the first,*" she read. "*Bought this book with my last coin. The stationer asked what it was for. I said: remembering. He said books remember fine on their own. He was wrong. Books are only paper until someone puts the truth in them.*"

She turned pages slowly, letting the year unfold.

"*Entry the ninth. Tam ate eleven honey rolls at the market and blamed Sefa. Recorded here so history judges correctly.*"

Laughter. Tam shouted, "There were twelve!" which proved the entry necessary.

"*Entry the fourteenth. Brannoc fixed the roof without being asked and denied doing it. He has now done this six times. I have stopped recording the denials; they are always the same words.*"

Brannoc's ears went red. He did not deny it, which was itself notable.

"*Entry the thirty-first. Ossa came to dinner. He brought a fish he claimed to have caught himself. Sefa checked his boots for river mud. There was river mud. We let him keep the story anyway.*"

Ossa, seated near the front in his plain clothes, inclined his head with the gravity of a man accepting a formal honor.

Then the entries changed, and the courtyard changed with them, because Ines reached the middle of the book, where the ink got darker and the handwriting tighter, and everyone present knew what lived in those pages.

"*Entry the fifty-second,*" she read, quieter now. "*One year ago. The mountain is quiet. The ledgers are ash. I am sitting in the ruins of a court where a god hoarded souls like coin, and I am trying to understand what I feel, and I cannot, because the feeling has no name I own. So I will write down facts instead. Fact: Tam is alive. Fact: his soul is his own. Fact: I do not know why I am crying. Fact: I think that is allowed.*"

Sefa had stopped pretending about her eyes. Tam had taken Wren's hand.

"*Entry the fifty-third. A man from the Ash Ward asked me today if the gods are dead. I told him the truth: I don't know. I told him something else too, and I want it written down so I can find it again on bad days. I said: maybe it doesn't matter if they're dead. Maybe what matters is that we stopped waiting for permission to save each other.*"

She looked up. The courtyard was very still. Even the children — and there were children now, the neighborhood's, drawn by the food and staying for the story — even they had gone quiet.

"*Entry the sixty-first. Sefa moved into the rooms above the workshop. She claims it's temporary. I have recorded the claim for posterity.*"

"I'm still deciding," Sefa called out.

"Noted," Ines said, and wrote it, right there, standing at the arch, and the laughter that went around was warm enough to cook with.

"*Entry the sixty-fourth. Brannoc preached his first sermon at the congregation on Tanner's Lane. Twelve people came. He prayed afterward, alone, the way he does — not asking for anything, just talking, like a letter to someone far away. His god never answered. But he keeps writing the letters. I asked him why once. He said: because the writing is the prayer. Because saying a name out loud is how you keep it alive. I have thought about that every day since.*"

Brannoc cleared his throat and studied the sky with tremendous interest.

"*Entry the seventy-seventh. Tam's sister announced her betrothal. Tam attempted to give a speech about it and could not finish. Recorded here in full fairness: neither could I.*"

She turned toward the end of the book. The last pages. And here she paused, because she had known for a week what the final entry was, and she had rewritten it eleven times, and the twelfth version was the right one, and she was suddenly, unreasonably nervous — she who had faced a god's throne with steadier hands than these.

"The last entry," she said, "is short."

She found the page.

"*Entry the last. Everything worth keeping, kept:*"

And she read the names.

"Ines Vane. Tam of the Salt Ward. Wren, his sister, bride of this day. Sefa, who steals and returns and stays. Brannoc, who writes letters to a silent heaven and means every one. Ossa Vell, Guildmaster, who learned late and learned true. And Merrin — Tam and Wren's grandmother — who sold what she sold out of love, and whose debt died on a mountain so her grandchildren wouldn't carry it."

She closed the book.

"That's the whole record," she said. "Everything else is detail. The names are the account, and the account balances."

Wren was crying now, properly, and Tam was holding her up, and Ossa had produced a handkerchief with the crisp efficiency of a man who had prepared for exactly this contingency. Somewhere in the back a child asked loudly what a god was, and its mother shushed it, and Sefa said, "Let the kid ask," and the mother said, "At a wedding?" and Sefa said, "Especially at a wedding," and the noise of the courtyard rose up like weather.

* * *

Evening came down soft. Lanterns lit themselves — no, Sefa lit them, quickly, before anyone noticed, and Ines noticed, and said nothing, because some magic should stay.

Ines ended up at the well with her book, adding the day's entry while the light lasted. *Married under the sky, as requested. Honey rolls consumed: disputed. All names present.*

Tam dropped onto the bench beside her. Up close, in the lantern light, you could see it if you knew to look — the faint wrongness of a boy carrying a year that wasn't his anymore, a year spent feeding a hungry thing under a mountain. He never talked about it. Once, drunk on festival wine, he'd said: "Do you think it hurts it? Wherever years go when nobody spends them?" and Ines had said, "I think it's gone, Tam," and he'd said, "Yeah," and that was the whole conversation, and it had been enough.

"Wren wants kids," Tam said. "Like, soon. Which means I'm going to be an uncle, which means I need to start practicing being impressive."

"You're already impressive. You walked into a god's court awake."

"Yeah, but the kid won't know that story till they're older." He grinned. Then, softer: "Hey. Ines. Read me the last page again sometime? Not today. Someday. When I forget the sound of it."

"You won't forget."

"I forgot a whole year once." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, and it landed heavy anyway. "People forget things. That's why you write them down. That's why you're you."

Ines looked at him — this runner she had tied to her wrist with a salt-cord, this soul she had torn out of a throne of prayer-stones, this boy whose name was the first one she'd written in the book and would be the last word of her life if she had any say in it.

"Someday," she promised. "Whenever you ask."

He bumped her shoulder with his and went off to be impressively an uncle-in-waiting at the dance that was starting by the arch.

* * *

Later — much later, when the lanterns burned low and Brannoc had been persuaded, terribly, to sing, and Sefa had fallen asleep sitting up and been left there out of respect — Ossa found Ines at the well.

He sat down without asking. For a while they watched the dancing.

"The guild reopened the soul-trade prohibition formally this morning," he said. "Signed, sealed, entered into the master roll. First law of the new charter." A pause. "I thought you'd want to know it's in writing now. Permanent. Not dependent on anyone's memory."

"Thank you."

"There's a copy for you. If you want it for the book."

"I'll write it out myself," Ines said. "Copies can be lost. My handwriting is worse than any fire."

Ossa almost smiled. Then he was quiet a long moment, and when he spoke again his voice had the careful weight she remembered from the day he struck her from the rolls — the day two people who respected each other chose opposite sides of a fault line and both survived the earthquake.

"Ines. The offer stands. Your place. Your desk. Your ledgers." He looked at the ordinary book in her lap. "Though I begin to suspect you've already founded a rival institution."

"This?" She patted the cover. "No ledgers here. No debts. No prices." She thought about it. "Well. One price. Everyone who enters has to stay for dinner."

"Infinitely more binding than anything I ever drafted." Ossa rose, brushed off his plain clothes, and extended his hand — not guild-fashion, palm flat for a seal, but the way friends do it. She took it. "Goodnight, Ledger-Breaker."

"Just Ines," she said. "Just Ines is plenty."

* * *

The last lantern guttered out near dawn. Ines stayed in the courtyard after everyone had gone — Sefa snoring upstairs, Brannoc asleep in the chair he always claimed he wasn't sleeping in, Tam and Wren long since walked home by their new household.

She sat with her book open on her knees and wrote the day's final entry by the grey beginning of light:

*All debts forgiven or discharged. All names accounted for. Nothing owed to gods, guilds, mountains, or memory.*

*Remaining balance: one wedding, one family, one book.*

*Carried forward: everything.*

She was closing it when she noticed the envelope tucked under the well's rim, where it must have been placed while the dancing was loudest. No seal. No signature. Just her name on the front, in handwriting she almost recognized — round, careful, patient — the handwriting of someone trained the same way she had been trained, a lifetime ago, by the same silent teacher.

Inside was a single sheet. On it, in that familiar hand, one line:

*Some of us kept our oaths. Some of us kept our silence. Come and see which is worse — the temple on Tanner's Lane, when the bells next ring.*

Ines read it twice. Behind her, upstairs, Sefa turned over in her sleep. Down the lane, the bells of Tanner's Lane hung still in the windless morning, waiting.

Ines folded the letter, opened her ordinary book, and wrote down the date, the words, and the fact that she intended to be there — because whatever came next, it would go in the record, and the record would be kept the only way that had ever worked:

By the people who chose each other.

She blew out the candle, and went inside to put the kettle on.
