## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The merchant's name was Odo Fenwick, and he had a memory in his coat pocket that he didn't know about.

Ada knew this the way she knew most things — by watching. He'd come down from the north road with a cart full of dyed wool and a face like a man carrying something heavy that wasn't in his hands. Travelers through the Hollow always had that look. The valley did something to people, though nobody could say what. It was simply a place where things went quiet: no charms held their shine here, no blessings stuck, no curses took root. Priests passing through complained of it like a head cold. Witches refused the road entirely.

And Ada Yarrow, nineteen years old and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with bread, sat on the wall outside the Drowned Lamb inn and waited for him to set his burden down.

He did it the way they all did it, eventually. He bought a bowl of stew, ate half, stared into the middle distance, and sighed the sigh of a man whose heart was too full for one body. Then he reached up, rubbed the ache behind his eyes — everyone touched the same spot, just above the ear, where the heavy things lived — and let a little of it slip loose without knowing.

It drifted off him like steam off soup. A small grey shimmer, no bigger than a moth, that settled into the air above the inn yard and pulsed gently, patiently, the way memories do when nobody's holding them down.

Ada caught it before it could fade.

She'd learned young not to grab. Grabbing bruised them, and a bruised memory came back on you sour, all its edges wrong. Instead she cupped her hand as if she were scooping water from a stream, and let the little light land there on its own terms, and only then pressed it home against her temple, just above her left ear, where the hollow place in her opened like a keyhole.

The world went soft.

* * *

Lanterns. Hundreds of them, strung between apple trees in an orchard gone gold with late summer. Music from someone's fiddle, slightly out of tune because the fiddler was crying too hard to mind his fingering. And a girl in a blue dress — no, a woman, but she'd always be a girl to the man remembering her — walking down a row of trees on her father's arm, laughing at something he'd whispered, which had been, Ada understood with a jolt, a joke about the groom's terrible boots.

Odo Fenwick had terrible boots himself. She'd noticed them an hour ago, cracked at the toe, resoled twice. In the memory he wore new ones for the wedding and had spent the whole ceremony aware of how they pinched, and had not cared even slightly, because his daughter was glowing, and the man she'd chosen was nervous in a way that meant he'd stay, and the lanterns were lit, and for one whole day everything Odo loved was in one orchard at the same time.

Ada stood inside the memory the way you stand inside a warm house after years outdoors. She knew the rules. An hour, maybe two, if she didn't push it. She walked the orchard. She tasted the wedding cake — honey and plum, a recipe from the bride's grandmother. She danced once, badly, feeling Odo's bad knee complain about it. She stood beside him during the toast and felt what he felt, which was a fullness so enormous it was nearly grief, the way joy is when you know it can't be kept.

That was the thing about borrowed memories. You got all of it. Not the shape of a thing but the weight of it. Ada could have described hunger to you in perfect detail — she'd carried a beggar's memory of a famine winter once, and it had taken a week to shake off — but she could also describe this: being loved so steadily, so long, that you stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, until one day your daughter takes your arm under a row of apple trees and you notice everything at once.

When the hour thinned out and the lanterns began to gutter at their edges, she let the memory go gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping child. It rose off her and hung in the dusk above the inn yard, dimmer now, a little worn at the seams. Borrowed things wore out. That was the cost, and she paid it every time, and hated herself a little for paying it anyway.

Then she sat on the wall in the ordinary evening, in the ordinary cold, with her own thin self, and felt the hunger come back in like water into a footprint.

* * *

"You've got that look," said Marta behind her.

Marta kept the Drowned Lamb, or rather the Drowned Lamb kept Marta, the way old furniture keeps a house standing. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a barrel with opinions, and she was the closest thing Ada had to a friend, which is to say she fed her sometimes and insulted her regularly and asked no questions, which in the Hollow was practically a marriage bond.

"What look?" said Ada.

"The look of a cat that's been in the cream." Marta set a bowl on the wall beside her without being asked. Barley stew, more barley than stew. "Fenwick's been sighing at my tables all evening like a man auditioning for a tragedy. I wondered who'd be picking up what he dropped."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." Marta wiped her hands on an apron that had given up pretending to be clean. "You know what your trouble is, girl? You're good at it. Too good. A clumsy thief steals a purse and feels bad for a night. You steal a man's best hour and wear it home like a shawl."

Ada looked at the stew instead of at her. "He doesn't miss it."

"No. That's the worst part, isn't it." Marta said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Nobody misses what you take. So nobody ever catches you. So nothing ever stops you." She tapped the wall twice, a habit of hers, as if knocking on wood for a luck she didn't believe in — nobody in the Hollow believed in anything, that was rather the point of the place. "Eat your supper."

Ada ate her supper. It was good. It was not enough. Nothing was ever enough, and she had long ago stopped expecting it to be, and this — this competent, managed, low-burning emptiness — was simply what her life was, the way the valley floor was what the mountains' lives were.

Above the inn, Odo Fenwick's worn-out wedding shimmered once more and dissolved entirely, spent. Somewhere on the north road tomorrow, a man would feel a strange lightness he couldn't account for, a day with a hole in it shaped exactly like his daughter's laugh, and he'd rub the ache above his ear and never know why.

* * *

The Hollow had three hundred souls, give or take the ones who left and the ones who arrived and pretended they'd always been there. It sat in its valley like a coin at the bottom of a well: green, sheltered, forgotten on purpose. The roads came in over two passes and left again, and the travelers who used them were wool merchants and tinkerers and the occasional pilgrim going somewhere holier, and all of them passed through without lingering, because lingering in the Hollow felt like being wrapped in wet wool. No god heard you here. No charm worked. Old Brammel, who mended fences and minds along the south lane, liked to say the valley was "a room with the door shut," and everyone nodded, and nobody asked what was outside the door that made shutting it seem wise.

Ada had been found at the valley's edge when she was four years old. Wrapped in a grey blanket, alone, with no note and no name anyone claimed. Widow Cetch had raised her alongside six other foundlings in a house that smelled of tallow and boiled cabbage, and Ada had discovered her gift at seven, quite by accident, when she'd caught a stray thought off a drover and spent an afternoon believing she owned a dog named Turnip.

She'd cried when the afternoon ended. She'd been crying, in one way or another, about the same thing ever since.

Because here was the arithmetic of her life, plain as a ledger: she could hold anything except her own past. Other people's memories fit her like tailored gloves. Her own were a locked drawer — the widow said she'd arrived with nothing, but Ada sometimes suspected otherwise, suspected there was a first year, a first face, a first word, some seed of herself that had been taken or lost before she could walk. There was a smooth blank place behind her eye where something should have been, the way your tongue finds the gap of a missing tooth. She'd stopped probing it years ago. Probing hurt, and yielded nothing, and the Hollow rewarded patience with nothing, same as it rewarded everything else.

So she borrowed. Carefully, sparingly, with a craftsman's discipline and a starving woman's shame. A sailor's morning ashore with his family. A weaver's pride at her first finished cloth. A mother's memory of teaching her son to whistle — that one had lasted three days in her before she'd had to give it back, weeping into Marta's sink water, because some weights you cannot carry and some you cannot put down, and that was the whole rotten trick of her gift: it gave her everything except the ability to keep any of it.

"Girl," Marta said, collecting the empty bowl, "you're doing the staring thing again."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"On the middle of nowhere?"

"It's very restful."

Marta snorted and hauled the bowl inside, and Ada stayed on the wall a while longer because the inn was warm and loud and full of people who belonged to their own lives, and the wall was cold and hers.

That was when she saw the child.

A little girl, six or seven, barefoot despite the chill, standing in the market square across the lane where the candle-seller had packed up hours ago. She was holding something — a dead bird, Ada saw, a sparrow, stiff and still — cupped in both hands like an offering. And she was talking. Not to Ada, not to herself, not the way children narrate their games. She was talking *up*. Eyes shut, chin lifted, the particular fierce earnestness of a child who has not yet learned that prayers are a currency and most of us are broke.

"—and please, please, I know you're probably busy, but Nana says you listen to children first, on account of we don't waste words, so. Please. Please make him breathe. I'll give anything. I'll give my tooth, the wobbly one, I don't need it—"

Ada should have looked away. Children prayed in the Hollow the way they whistled — sound going nowhere, filling the silence. Everyone knew the valley kept such things out. Everyone knew—

The candle-stub in the seller's abandoned tray relit.

Not flickered. *Relit*, flame standing up straight and gold in the dead wax, and then the second stub, and the third, a little congregation of flames lighting themselves in order like guests rising when the bride enters.

And in the child's cupped hands, the sparrow shuddered.

Ada felt it go through the valley like a crack through ice — a single, high, ringing note of *attention*, as if something vast and patient that had been holding its breath for a hundred years had finally, quietly, exhaled. The air changed taste. The dark leaned in. Every hair on her arms stood up and paid attention to something she could not see.

The bird breathed. The child laughed — a wet, astonished, glorious laugh — and the candles guttered out one by one, politely, their work done, and the dark came back, and the valley was silent again, silent the way a room is silent after a door has been unlocked somewhere far away.

The girl ran home clutching her sparrow. The square emptied. The night resumed its ordinary business of being night.

Ada sat very still on the cold wall with her hand pressed flat over the blank place behind her ear, and her heart was going like a drum, and she thought, with the strange clarity of a person watching their life change shape from the inside:

*Something just answered.*

And somewhere beyond the passes, she would think later — much later, when it was far too late for innocence — something else began, very softly, to laugh.
