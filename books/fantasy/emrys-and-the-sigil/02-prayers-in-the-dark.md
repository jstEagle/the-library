## 2. Prayers in the Dark

Market day in the Hollow smelled of wet wool, fried onions, and the particular desperation of people trying to sell things nobody needed. Ada Yarrow moved through it like smoke through a keyhole — present enough to be unremarkable, absent enough to be forgotten.

That was the trick of it. You didn't steal memories by being clever. You stole them by being *forgettable*. A woman with a face like a closed door and hands that never fidgeted could stand beside a merchant for ten minutes and he'd swear afterward he'd been talking to himself.

She'd been working the spice stall for most of the morning. Master Odell — broad, red-faced, with the laugh of a man who had never once doubted that the world was fundamentally on his side — was telling anyone who'd listen about his daughter's wedding. He told it to the woman buying saffron. He told it to the boy delivering candles. He told it, at one point, to a goat that had wandered in from somewhere and shown no interest whatsoever.

"Third toast," he was saying, "my Lissbet stands up on the bench, full cup in hand, and she says, *Papa, if you cry again I'm charging you for the wine.*"

The crowd chuckled. Ada, standing at the edge of it with her basket held just so, let her attention settle into him the way a cat settles into a sunbeam.

It wasn't grabbing. That was the mistake novices made — reaching, clutching, tearing. Memories didn't work like purses. They worked like warmth. You had to be near them, and you had to be still, and you had to want nothing, because wanting made ripples, and ripples woke people up.

So she wanted nothing. She simply stood, and breathed, and let his joy pour into her like water into dry ground.

*The wedding.* It came in pieces first — a smell of beeswax and cut grass, a flash of blue ribbon, the sound of a fiddle slightly out of tune. Then all at once, whole: she was standing in Odell's body at the back of a crowded green, watching a girl in yellow walk toward a boy who looked like he might faint from happiness, and every person there had known Lissbet since she was small enough to fit in a soup pot, and the love in the air was so thick you could have spread it on bread—

Ada's breath caught.

There it was. The feeling. The one she came back for, again and again, the way other people went back to a well. For one hour — that was how long borrowed memories lasted before they faded to ash and ache — she would know what it was to be loved by a whole village. To have a daughter who teased her. To have a life with witnesses.

She withdrew gently, leaving Odell's memory intact but lighter, the way a loaf is lighter after one slice. He wouldn't miss it. People never missed what they had too much of.

"Thank you for your custom," she murmured, and he nodded at her with the vague courtesy of a man who couldn't quite place her face, and she drifted away into the crowd feeling rich as a queen.

An hour. She intended to spend it well. There was a bench by the well — ironic, that — where an old woman sold roasted chestnuts, and Ada liked to sit there when she was full of someone else's happiness and watch the market go by and pretend, just for the length of a paper cone, that any of it belonged to her.

She was three chestnuts in and deep inside the memory — Lissbet laughing, the fiddle striking up, Odell's own heart so full it hurt — when the child started praying.

---

You heard prayers in the Hollow the way you heard rain: constant, background, meaningless. Everyone prayed. Nobody expected anything. That was the deal the valley had struck with heaven, though nobody remembered signing it. You could call out to whatever gods you liked; the words just went up and stopped, like birds against glass.

This child prayed loudly.

He was maybe six, sitting cross-legged in the mud between a fishmonger's cart and a shuttered fortune-teller's booth, and he was holding something cupped in his grubby hands and talking to it with the fierce seriousness of a general addressing troops.

"He's not breathing," the boy announced, to the thing in his hands and then, louder, to the market at large. "He's *not breathing* and nobody cares."

Ada glanced over. The thing in his hands was a bird. A sparrow, or what had been a sparrow — it lay stiff and still, its little feet curled up like question marks.

"Marta says dead things stay dead," the boy continued, "but Marta also says the moon follows you around when you're walking at night, which is a lie, because I checked." He lifted the bird up toward the gray sky as though presenting evidence. "So I'm asking anyway. Please. Please make him breathe again. I'll give you anything. I've got a marble. It's got a swirl in it. It's the best one."

A few passersby smiled the tired smile adults reserve for children talking to God. A fishwife said something about boys and mud. The market flowed on around him, indifferent as water around a stone.

And Ada, warm with stolen wedding-joy, almost smiled too.

Almost.

Because the candle stall behind him — old Pemmy's stall, forty tallow candles in a row, half of them guttered down to stubs in the damp morning air — *relit.*

All of them. At once. No wind, no match, no hand. One moment: smoke and dying wicks. The next: forty small flames standing up straight as soldiers, burning clean and gold, throwing light that had no business existing under that flat gray sky.

The market did not notice. That was the first terrible thing. People walked past the candle stall without a glance, haggling, spitting, hauling carts, while forty impossible flames burned behind them like a secret written in a language only Ada could read.

The second terrible thing was the silence.

Not quiet — the Hollow was always quiet, magicless, muffled, a valley wrapped in wool. This was different. This was the silence of a room after a long-held note finally stops. Ada felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the borrowed memory inside her, which flickered like the candles had — Lissbet's laughter stuttering, the fiddle going sour, the whole warm hour of it suddenly uncertain of itself.

The boy lowered the bird. Stared at it.

"You're supposed to be a god," he told the sky, aggrieved. "Marta says gods are big."

Then the sparrow shuddered.

Ada saw it happen. She would see it every night for the rest of her life, she suspected, the way you see a door open in a house you thought was empty. The bird's stiff wings flexed. Its head tipped up. Its beak opened on a sound too small for anyone but Ada to hear — a thin, astonished *cheep*, the sound of something coming back from a place with no name.

The boy screamed. Not in fear. In triumph. "HA!" he shouted at the heavens, brandishing the living bird overhead. "I TOLD Marta! I TOLD everyone! Best marble I ever spent!"

The sparrow, presumably having opinions about all this, flapped free of his hands and vanished into the eaves of the fishmonger's awning.

The market kept flowing. The fishwife laughed at the boy's antics. Old Pemmy came out of her stall, frowned at her candles — all forty burning like it was midsummer festival — muttered something about tallow quality these days, and began snuffing them out one by one, annoyed, as though miracles were a waste of good wax.

Nobody else had seen.

Nobody else had *felt* it — that crack running through the floor of the world, hairline but real, the sound of a long silence breaking.

Except Ada.

---

She sat very still on the chestnut bench for a long time afterward, the paper cone cooling in her hands, the borrowed wedding-memory flickering inside her like a lamp low on oil.

Here was the thing about the Hollow that Ada knew better than anyone, because she had spent twenty-two years testing its walls the way a prisoner tests bars: nothing answered here. Nothing *moved* sideways. Magic didn't fail in the Hollow; it simply wasn't, the way cold isn't fire. Travelers who came down the mountain roads arrived trailing charms and blessings like dust, and by the time they reached the market their amulets were just pretty stones and their prayers were just words. Ada had built her whole life in that deadness. Her gift worked here precisely because everything else didn't — no wards, no hexes, no guardian spirits to catch a thief in the act. The Hollow was a locked room, and Ada was the mouse in the walls.

Locked rooms did not have cracks.

But she had watched forty candles relight themselves, and she had felt the note stop, and now she sat with her chestnuts going cold and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives right before everything changes, that the room had a crack in it. Maybe it had always had a crack, hairline and patient. And something on the other side had just pressed its eye to it.

*A god,* she thought. *A small one. Small enough to fit through.*

It had taken a child's prayer and a dead sparrow and a marble with a swirl in it. It had cost the boy nothing and given him everything, and it had done it *here*, in the one place in the world where such transactions were supposed to be impossible.

Ada finished the chestnuts mechanically. The wedding-memory was nearly gone now, fading the way they always faded — colors first, then sounds, then the feeling, that golden being-loved warmth draining out of her like heat from a stone, until she was left with the usual residue: the shape of the joy without the joy itself, and beneath it, as always, the hunger.

Only today the hunger had company. Today there was something else sitting in her chest alongside the wanting, and it took her a while to name it, because she hadn't felt it since she was small enough to believe in things.

Dread.

Not of the god. Gods, if this one was anything to judge by, were mostly interested in sparrows and marbles. No — the dread was older and more practical than that. It was the dread of a person who has lived quietly in a locked room for twenty-two years and has just heard the lock turn.

Because locks that can turn can be opened by anyone. And Ada had spent her whole life taking things from travelers, and travelers came from *outside*, and outside was currently — judging by the crack in the sky, the relighting candles, the sparrow that had come back — in the process of remembering that it existed.

Something had changed. Something had answered. And in Ada's experience, which was long and specific and mostly learned the hard way, when the rules of the Hollow changed, they changed for everyone — including the people who had built their lives in the space the rules left empty.

She stood, brushed crumbs from her skirt, and told herself she was imagining it. Candles guttered and caught. Birds stunned themselves and recovered. Children prayed into the empty air and the empty air, being empty, said nothing.

The gray sky said nothing still. But it felt, for the first time in her life, less like a ceiling and more like a held breath.

She was halfway home, cutting through the lane behind the tannery where the light always pooled strangely, when she heard the boy again — running along the top of the wall, sparrowless now, shouting to anyone who'd listen:

"It answered! It ANSWERED! His name's Ashen! He told me!"

Ada stopped walking.

The boy ran on, glorying in it, and his voice faded down the lane, and the word stayed behind, hanging in the strange pooled light.

*Ashen.*

Ada had stolen a great many memories in her life. Names of gods were rare among them — people rarely thought about gods vividly enough for the names to stick — but she had one, an old one, taken years ago from a dying pilgrim who had babbled it like a fever: a name from a country that wasn't on any map, a doorkeeper-god, small and gray and faithful, who had gone silent the same year the country did.

The pilgrim had wept saying it. *Ashen. Ashen keeps the door. Ashen keeps the door and the door is—*

And there the memory had torn, the way old things tear, and Ada had never learned what the door was, or where, or why a dying man two hundred miles from anywhere had cried for it.

She stood in the lane behind the tannery with her empty chestnut cone and her fading borrowed wedding and her heart going hard and strange against her ribs, and she thought: *the valley is a seal, and something has just found the seam.*

Behind her, faint across the rooftops, old Pemmy was still cursing at her candles.

They had relit again.
