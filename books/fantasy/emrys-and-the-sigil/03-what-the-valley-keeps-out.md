## 3. What the Valley Keeps Out

Old Brammel was mending his fence when Ada came down the hill, which was a strange thing to see, because Brammel's fence had been falling apart for as long as anyone could remember, and everyone in the Hollow had long ago agreed to pretend it wasn't.

It was the kind of fence that had given up on being a fence and become more of a suggestion. Three rails missing. A gate that hung at an angle best described as *aspirational*. Chickens came and went through it freely, with the air of commuters who no longer bothered checking the timetable. And yet there was Brammel, seventy if he was a day, hammering a new rail into place with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"Brammel," Ada said, "that fence has been dying since before I was born. Let it rest."

"It's not dying," Brammel said, without looking up. "It's holding."

"Holding what? The chickens get out twice a week."

"The chickens," said Brammel, "are permitted."

Ada leaned against the gatepost — carefully; the gatepost had opinions about weight — and watched him work. He drove the nail home, tested the rail with both hands, and nodded once, satisfied, like a physician who'd felt a pulse he liked.

"You're up early," he said. "Or late. With you it's hard to tell."

"I couldn't sleep."

"Bad dreams?"

"Worse. Good ones." She picked at the bark of the post. "I borrowed a memory off a tinker yesterday. A wedding. His daughter's."

Brammel set down his hammer. He did this sometimes — stopped everything, gave her his whole attention, as though she were a fire that needed watching. He was the closest thing the Hollow had to a grandfather, if you didn't count actual grandfathers, and Ada didn't, because hers were either dead or theoretical.

"And how was it?" he asked.

"Lovely. Terrible." She shrugged, aiming for casual and landing somewhere near it. "There was dancing. Her father cried into his hat and pretended it was the smoke from the candles." She smiled despite herself. "For an hour I knew what it felt like to be loved by somebody who'd known me my whole life. Then it wore off and I was just me again, standing in a ditch holding someone else's happiness like a stolen pie."

Brammel grunted. It was a complicated grunt. Ada had spent years learning to read Brammel's grunts the way scholars learned dead languages, and this one contained: sympathy, worry, and a third thing he never let surface.

"You shouldn't do it so often," he said.

"I don't do it often enough to live, and too often to be proud of it. That's the whole arithmetic of my life, Brammel. Don't make me show my working."

That got a laugh out of him, brief and dry as August. He picked the hammer back up, then paused, rail in hand, and looked at her in a way that made her stomach do something unhelpful.

"How long," he said, "have you lived in the Hollow?"

"All my life. You know that."

"And in all that time, have you ever seen a prayer answered here? A blessing take? A charm work true? A single candle lit for any god burn steadier than one lit for none?"

Ada opened her mouth. Closed it. The Hollow was a practical place. People didn't pray much because praying here was like posting letters into a locked drawer — nothing came back, and eventually you stopped writing. She'd always assumed that was just how the world was, out here away from cities and cathedrals.

"No," she said slowly. "But neither has anyone else."

"That," said Brammel, "is exactly my point."

He set the rail against the fence and turned to face her fully, and Ada realized, with a small cold shock, that old Brammel was frightened. Not the way he got frightened by weather or tax collectors or the year the well went sour. This was older. Deeper. A fear kept in the cellar so long it had grown its own roots.

"Come inside," he said. "And mind the step. It's been loose since the Flood, whatever the Flood was."

* * *

His cottage smelled of woodsmoke, dried herbs, and the particular dustiness of a house where a man lives alone and has decided decoration is a war he will not fight. He poured them both nettle tea, which tasted the way an apology sounds, and sat across from her with his hands wrapped around his cup like it might try to escape.

"There are things about this valley," he began, "that I was told when I was younger than you, by a man who was told when he was younger than me, all the way back. Not written down. Never written down. Writing can be found."

"So tell me," Ada said. "I'm famously good at keeping things. It's sort of my trade."

"This isn't a memory you can steal back if it goes wrong, girl. Listen first."

She listened.

"The Hollow isn't a valley," Brammel said. "It's a seal. Or part of one. A long time ago — longer than the stories bother counting — there was a country called Vael. You won't have heard of it. That's rather the point. Vael did something, or had something done to it, or was something — the story frays there, every telling — and the powers of the age decided the only safe thing was to close it away. Lock it behind a door. And around that door they built rings of protection, places where magic simply *doesn't*. Places where gods cannot hear, where blessings fall flat, where a witch's spell dies in the air like a moth in winter."

He gestured, with his cup, at the world outside his window. The green hills. The slow river. The market square where, two days ago, Ada had watched a child pray aloud and — impossibly, wonderfully, terribly — been answered.

"The Hollow is one of those rings," Brammel said. "The innermost ring, some say. We sit on the lock itself. That's why nothing answers prayers here. That's why your gift works the way it does — taking memories is a small magic, a *quiet* magic, quiet enough to slip under the door, but even yours feels thin here, doesn't it? Like breathing through cloth?"

It did. She'd never had words for it before, but she did now: every memory she lifted felt slightly muffled, slightly secondhand, like music heard through a wall. She'd assumed that was the nature of the gift. Apparently it was the nature of the cage.

"The gods were locked out on purpose," Brammel said. "All of them. From everything. Because whoever built the seal couldn't trust them not to pick at it. And for — however many hundreds of years — it held. Quietly. Nobody here even knew what they were living inside of. They just knew their charms fizzled and their prayers echoed and life was simple and safe and *small*."

"But?" Ada said. There was always a but. Buts were load-bearing.

"But two days ago," Brammel said quietly, "a child prayed in your market square, and something answered. Candles relit. A dead bird breathed. Girl, if that happened, then the seal is cracking. And things that have been locked out for centuries do not knock politely when the lock starts turning."

The tea had gone cold in Ada's hands. She hadn't noticed drinking it, or not drinking it.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why now?"

Brammel looked at her for a long moment. And there it was again — that third thing in his grunts, surfacing at last, blinking in the light.

"Because last night," he said, "I dreamed of soldiers. And I haven't dreamed of anything in forty years, because dreams need magic the way fish need water, and we've never had any. Something is reaching *in*, Ada. Whatever cracked the seal cracked it from both sides."

* * *

They came that night.

Ada saw them from her window above the chandler's shop, where she rented a room that smelled permanently of tallow and honesty. Moonlight lay over the Hollow like spilled milk, and down the single street came eleven figures, walking in step, unhurried, the way people walk when they own the road and know it.

Soldiers. She'd seen soldiers pass through before — tax escorts, caravan guards, once a whole company marching to some border squabble, loud and dusty and human. These were not those.

These wore armor the color of deep water, dark enough to drink the moonlight. Their cloaks moved wrong, or rather didn't move at all, hanging as still as painted cloth while the night wind stirred everything else — the banners, the thatch, the hair of the dogs barking themselves hoarse at the edges of the square. And their faces —

Their faces were blank.

Not masked. Masked would have been better. Masks have eyeslits; masks have intention. These faces were smooth as the back of a spoon, featureless from brow to chin, and somehow Ada knew — the way you know things in nightmares, without being told — that underneath the smoothness there *were* features, ordinary noses and mouths and eyes, and the blankness was something worn over them, the way her stolen memories were worn over her loneliness. A garment. A uniform for the face.

They stopped in the middle of the square. Eleven of them, arranged in a shape that was almost a circle and deliberately not quite, and the one at the center — taller, broader, its blank face tilted slightly upward like a man scenting rain — spoke.

Its voice was the worst part. It was a perfectly normal voice. Warm, even. A voice like a favorite uncle's, coming out of a face like an egg.

"Good people of the Hollow," it called, and shutters closed all down the street, one after another, like a wave of small wooden flinches. "We come in service of the Crown, asking after a person of interest. A thief. One who takes what is not given. One who *remembers*."

Ada's blood went from warm to cold to nothing at all.

*The thief who remembers.* She pressed herself flat against the wall beside her window, heart slamming, and made herself think. Because that description fit half the cutpurses in three counties — but *remembers* was specific. *Remembers* was aimed. Someone, somewhere, knew what she could do, and had sent these things to find it.

"We mean no harm," the uncle-voice continued pleasantly. "We ask only questions. Where does she sleep? Whom does she speak to? What does she carry?" A pause, courteous as a headsman's. "Bring her to us, and the Crown will remember your village generously."

Down the street, a door opened. Ada's stomach dropped — until she saw it was old Marta the baker, ancient and round and utterly without fear, standing in her doorway in her nightdress with her arms crossed like a customs official.

"We don't hold with thieves here," Marta announced to the square, "but we hold less with folk who wear their faces indoors. Come back when you've got chins like Christians and we'll talk."

A ripple went through the blank ranks — not laughter, nothing so human, more a shifting, like cards being shuffled. The tall one inclined its smooth head.

"A fair answer," it said. "We will return."

And Ada, flattened against her wall, understood with terrible clarity that this was not a threat deferred. It was a promise with a schedule. They would go door to door. They would ask their pleasant questions. And sooner or later someone — frightened, tired, hoping to be left alone — would say the words *there's a girl who rents above the chandler's, and she talks to travelers on the road.*

She thought of Brammel's fence. *It's not dying. It's holding.* All her life she'd thought the Hollow was dull — a bowl of safe, gray nothing where the most exciting thing that ever happened was a good harvest or a bad marriage. She'd resented it, a little, the way children resent soup. And all along it had been a wall around her, thick and old and loving, and now it was cracking, and through the crack came soldiers with borrowed faces asking after her name.

The safety of home, it turned out, had always been the safety of a cage. And nobody had asked the bird.

Below, the eleven figures turned as one and walked out of the square, unhurried, owning the road. As the last one passed beneath her window, it stopped.

Ada stopped breathing.

The blank face tilted up. For a moment — a moment stretched thin as wire — she felt it *looking* at her, though it had no eyes, felt it listening to the sound of her heart, which was doing her no favors at all.

Then the uncle-voice said, softly, almost kindly:

"Goodnight, thief."

And it walked on, and the milk-moonlight closed behind it, and Ada Yarrow stood in her little room above the chandler's shop, in the valley that had kept the whole world's magic out to keep her safe, and packed her bag.

She took the memories, of course. All of them — the wedding, the sailor's storm, the midwife's lullabies, forty small stolen joys wrapped in oilcloth like a pie of other people's lives. She took her mother's shawl, though it was more holes than wool. She took the knife Brammel had given her at fifteen and the dried apples Marta pressed on her at the door with fierce instructions to eat them before the road did.

At the edge of the village, where the fields gave up and the hills began, she stopped and looked back once. The Hollow lay sleeping in its bowl, unaware it was a lock, unaware it was a cage, unaware that the thing it had been keeping out had already sent its fingers through the crack.

"Sorry," she whispered to it. To the fences, the chickens, the crooked lane, old Brammel snoring behind his holding-fence. "Thank you. Sorry."

Then Ada Yarrow climbed into the dark hills, carrying forty borrowed lives and no idea that the first person waiting for her up there knew her secret already — and had been waiting a very long time.
