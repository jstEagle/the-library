## 4. The Priestess Who Fell

Ada had never run uphill before.

She hadn't known it was a thing you could fail at until the third switchback above the Hollow, when her legs turned to wet rope and her lungs filed a formal complaint. Downhill was easy. Downhill was what fear did naturally. But the hills north of the valley went up and up and up, into pine and fog and a wind that had opinions, and Ada climbed them anyway because behind her — far behind her now, but *behind* — men with blank faces were asking questions in a village that had never needed to answer any.

*The thief who remembers.* That's what they'd called her. Old Brammel's fence post voice still rang in her ears: *the Hollow is a seal, girl, and you are carrying pieces of it in your head.*

She'd laughed at him. She'd actually laughed, that ugly little bark she did when the world said something too large to swallow whole. And then Brammel had looked at her with his milky old eyes and said, very gently, the way you tell someone their house is on fire: "They'll come for you before the week is out."

It was day two.

So: uphill. With a satchel containing half a loaf of black bread, a skin of water, three stolen memories she couldn't give back if she tried, and no plan whatsoever beyond *up* and *away*, which were not plans so much as directions panic points.

The fog thickened as she climbed. The pines closed around her like a congregation bowing heads. And somewhere past midday — or what she guessed was midday, because the sun had become a rumor behind the cloud — she stopped at a stream to drink and saw, reflected in the water beside her own face, a stranger's boot.

She came up swinging. It was, in hindsight, not her best decision. The stranger caught her wrist without apparent effort, and Ada found herself looking up at a tall woman in a traveling cloak gone gray with road dust, hair cropped short and practical, and eyes the color of storm water over slate. Around the woman's neck hung a broken chain. Whatever pendant had once hung from it was gone; only the snapped link remained, pressed flat against her throat like a scar.

"You swing like someone who learned to fight from books," the woman said. "Bad ones."

"Let go of me."

"Are you going to run?"

"Yes."

"Then I'll hold on." But she did let go, and when Ada bolted — three steps, four — the woman simply walked alongside her, unhurried, matching her pace through the trees as though they were strolling to market together. "You're going the wrong way, incidentally. There's a ravine about half a mile on. Very scenic. Very final."

Ada stopped, bent double, breathing like a bellows. "What do you want?"

"What do *you* want? Because from where I'm standing, you're a girl from the Hollow running north with stolen light coming off her like smoke." The woman crouched, bringing those slate eyes level with Ada's. "I've been watching you since the stream bend. You glow, thief. Did you know that? Not to most eyes. To mine."

"I don't glow."

"You absolutely glow. It's quite rude, honestly. Do you know how hard it is to hide when there's a walking lantern stumbling through my hills?" She straightened, and something in her face shifted — softened, then hardened again, like weather deciding itself. "My name is Sera Ilune. I was a priestess of the Vigilant Flame for nineteen years, and then I wasn't, and now I'm a woman who lives alone in a ruined watchtower and knows exactly what's burning inside your skull."

Ada's hand drifted toward her knife. Sera watched the gesture with the weary patience of someone who'd seen a thousand hands drift toward a thousand knives.

"Eat first," Sera said. "Threaten me after. Everything goes better on a full stomach. That's not wisdom, it's just true."

And because Ada's stomach was louder than her suspicion, she followed.

* * *

The watchtower clung to the hillside like a tooth in an old jaw. Half its top had fallen away centuries ago; moss owned the rest. Inside, one room, one hearth, one table, and shelves of books arranged with a precision that contradicted every other detail of the place. A kettle went on the fire. Bread appeared — better bread than Ada's, with actual salt in it. Tea that tasted faintly of pine and something sweeter underneath.

Sera sat across from her, wrapped both hands around her cup, and said nothing for a long moment. Ada had the distinct feeling of being read. Not skimmed — *read*, slowly, the way you'd read a letter that might contain bad news.

"The Hollow," Sera said at last. "You grew up there."

"Everyone grows up somewhere."

"Not everyone grows up in a place built specifically to keep gods out." She said it lightly, testing. Ada kept her face still, which was apparently answer enough, because Sera nodded as if confirming a sum. "Brammel told you. I wondered if he would. He's older than he looks, that man. Older than he should be."

"He said the valley is a seal."

"It is. One of seven, once. Now six, and fraying." Sera turned her cup in her hands. "And you, Ada Yarrow, have been picking pieces off that seal your entire life without knowing it. Every memory you've ever taken from a traveler passing through — some of them weren't memories at all. Or rather, they were, but they were *carrying* something. Fragments. Shards of an old working, hidden inside ordinary recollections and smuggled into the one place in the world where magic can't touch them." Her mouth twisted. "Whoever hid them there was clever. Hiding light inside a blind woman's house."

Ada set down her cup. Her hands were not entirely steady. "You're saying I've been stealing —"

"Junk," Sera said. "Beautiful junk. A merchant's wedding, a soldier's homecoming, a lullaby in a language nobody speaks anymore. You took them for the warmth, yes? For the hour of feeling loved?" Something flickered across her face — pity, quickly banked. "That's not a crime. That's just hunger. But those warm little hours are also the scattered pieces of a sigil that once sealed an entire country away from the world. And now the seal is breaking, the gods are answering prayers again, and every power on this continent wants what's in your head."

Outside, the wind moved through the pines, and for a moment Ada could almost believe she heard voices in it.

"Which country," she asked, though some part of her already knew the shape of the answer, the way you know a word on the tip of your tongue.

Sera looked at her for a long time. Then she rose, crossed to her shelves, and pulled down a book — an atlas, its leather cracked, its pages soft as cloth. She opened it to a map of the northern kingdoms and laid her finger on a stretch of mountains east of the Hollow.

"There," she said.

Ada looked. Mountains. Forest. A river with a name. Nothing else.

"That's empty."

"That," said Sera, "is Vael."

* * *

The fire had burned low by the time Sera began the story properly, and she told it the way people tell things they've carried too long — in pieces, in the wrong order, stopping once to feed the fire and twice to stare into the middle distance as though the story were happening again on the far wall.

"I was nineteen when I took my vows," she said. "Young even for the Vigilant Flame. We were the smallest order — no great cathedrals, no armies. Our whole purpose was remembering. Someone had to, you see. Because Vael had been erased, and erasure is hungry work. It doesn't stop at borders. It eats outward. First the country, then the roads leading to it, then the songs, then the names of anyone who ever traded there. We lost three villages in my grandmother's generation to pure forgetting — people who woke one morning unable to say why their gran's recipes used spices nobody sold anymore."

Ada hugged her knees. "Erased by who?"

"By everyone. That's the part nobody wants to hear." Sera's laugh was dry as old paper. "There was a war, a hundred years ago. Not a war of armies — a war of *gods*. Vael's gods were old and strange and answered anyone, and the young kingdoms' gods were jealous, and the priests on both sides fed the fire until the sky itself was burning. In the end, the surviving powers did the unthinkable. They didn't defeat Vael. They *unwrote* it. Sealed it behind a sigil of seven pieces, locked its gods outside the world, and erased it from every map, every ledger, every mind strong enough to hold it. They called it mercy. Ending the war. Saving the world." She stared into the flames. "They called it mercy, and I believed them, and that belief is why I am sitting in a ruin instead of a temple."

"You helped make the seal." Ada said it quietly. It wasn't a question. The broken chain at Sera's throat seemed suddenly very loud.

"I helped write it." Sera's voice didn't waver, but her hands did, just slightly, around the teacup. "Seven fragments, each hidden inside a living memory, each carried unknowing by ordinary people, all of them funneled over generations into the Hollow — the one place the gods could never reach to take them back. I was twenty-six years old and certain I was saving the world." She finally looked up. "I was wrong. The war ended, yes. And Vael didn't die. That's the thing no one understood. Erasure isn't execution. It's imprisonment. Everyone inside that seal has been alive for a hundred years, Ada. Trapped. Fading. And the seal is failing now because the old gods have started answering prayers again, and a seal is only as strong as the silence it's made of."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse conducted small business.

"So the memories I stole—"

"Were never yours to keep, and never truly theirs either. They're keystone shards wearing borrowed faces." Sera leaned forward. "How many do you carry?"

"Three that feel... different. Heavier. One's a lullaby. One's a wedding. One's—" Ada hesitated. "A door. Just a door in a mountainside, and someone laughing."

Sera closed her eyes briefly, and when she opened them they were wet, which she clearly found inconvenient and ignored. "A door in a mountain," she repeated softly. "Of course. Of course it is." She stood abruptly, went to the window slit, and stood looking out at the dark hills for a long moment.

"You should sleep," she said finally. "Tomorrow we go east. And Ada — eat the rest of the bread. You'll need your strength, and I need you not fainting on me. I'm too old to carry you and too proud to leave you."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," Sera agreed pleasantly. "But you will. Sleep on it."

* * *

Ada did not sleep, or slept badly, which amounts to the same thing. She lay on a pallet of blankets that smelled of cedar and old paper, staring at the broken rafters, turning everything over.

Here was the thing she kept circling back to, the stone in her shoe: *someone laughing at a door in a mountain.* She had carried that memory for four years, taken off a dying peddler woman on the south road, and in all that time she'd thought of it as hers — one of the good ones, one she rationed like sugar. A door, golden evening light, and a woman's laughter, bright and unafraid. When the Hollow got too quiet, Ada would open that memory and stand in it a while, letting the laughter wash over her like sunlight through a window she wasn't allowed to open.

And now she learned it wasn't hers at all. It was a *shard*. A brick in a prison wall, disguised as a gift.

Except — and here was the worse thought, the one that made her turn her face to the wall — except it had felt like a gift. All of them had. The merchant's wedding, the soldier's homecoming, the lullaby. For one hour apiece, Ada Yarrow had been someone loved, someone welcomed, someone sung to sleep. If those moments were prison bricks, then what did that make her hunger? What did it make the only warmth she'd ever had?

Toward dawn she gave up on sleep, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and padded to the window slit. The eastern sky was going gray-gold. And below the tower, on the winding track, she saw them.

Four figures. Moving fast. Too disciplined to be travelers, too quiet to be soldiers on any honest errand. Even at that distance, something about them made her skin crawl — the way they moved like a single creature wearing four coats.

"Sera," she whispered. Then, louder: "*Sera.*"

Sera was already awake — already dressed, already stuffing books into a battered satchel with the brisk economy of a woman who had fled places before. "Memory-eaters," she said, without surprise. "Crown hunters. They've been tracking the fragments since the seal started singing again, and a defrocked priestess who helped write the thing leaves a trail like spilled honey." She slung the satchel across her back and thrust a second, smaller bag into Ada's arms. "Out the back. There's a goat path. Don't argue, don't look back, and whatever happens —"

The tower door exploded inward.

Later, Ada would remember it in flashes: splinters, a figure in the doorway whose face was smooth and blank as an egg, Sera shouting words in a language that made the air taste of iron, the goat path steep and slick underfoot, and — worst of all — a *pulling* sensation, like fingers hooking behind her eyes, like something trying to drink straight out of her skull.

She ran. The blank-faced thing pursued for perhaps fifty yards and then stopped, as though reaching the edge of its leash, and stood watching her go with its featureless face tilted, almost politely, like a listener at a concert.

Ada ran until the tower was gone, until the hills swallowed her, until her legs gave out and she collapsed in a hollow beneath a lightning-split pine, gasping, sobbing, furious.

When she finally lifted her head, wiping her face with a sleeve, she discovered two things.

First: Sera Ilune was crouched beside her, breathing hard, a burn mark seared across her cloak where something had touched her — but alive, grinning fiercely, absurdly, like a woman who'd just won a bet.

Second: standing in the ferns a few feet away, watching them both with enormous solemn eyes, was a boy. Eight years old, maybe nine. Barefoot. Wearing a coat several sizes too big, and holding a dead bird cupped in his small hands.

The bird was breathing.

"Um," said the boy. "I think somebody answered my prayer. And I think —" he looked from Ada to Sera and back, and his voice dropped to a whisper, awed and frightened in equal measure — "I think it sent me to *you*."

Behind them, far down the valley, the Hollow's morning bells rang thin and uncertain, like a heart missing beats.

Sera went very still. "Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, no. It's starting."
