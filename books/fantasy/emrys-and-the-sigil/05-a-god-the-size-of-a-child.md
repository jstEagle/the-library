## 5. A God the Size of a Child

The chapel found them before they found it, which Ada would later decide was the first suspicious thing about it.

They had been walking through the hills for two days, following Sera's stubborn conviction that roads existed somewhere beneath the bracken and the fog. Ada's feet hurt. Her pack hurt. The stolen memories in her head hurt most of all, because the merchant's wedding had finally faded and left behind that particular ache, like the ghost of a warmth you'd leaned into and lost.

"There," Sera said, pointing with her chin. "Roof's half-gone, but the walls are standing. We'll sleep dry."

It was a chapel the way a skeleton is a person. The roof had collapsed inward at one corner, letting the sky in like a rude guest, and moss had claimed the altar steps. But the stones were pale and fine-grained, nothing like the grey fieldstone of the Hollow, and the doorway was carved with a pattern of interlocking lines that made Ada's eyes water if she looked too long.

She looked away quickly. She'd been doing that a lot lately — looking away from things that hummed.

"Old," she said, which was safe.

"Older than old," Sera murmured, running her thumb along the doorframe. Her face did something complicated. "Older than me, and I'm older than I look."

"You look fifty."

"I'm thirty-four."

Ada decided not to touch that. She ducked under the fallen lintel and into the nave, and that was when she heard the humming.

Not the humming of magic — the Hollow had taught her that silence, and the hills had only a faint whisper of it, like a room where someone had recently been crying. This was an actual hum. A tune. Small, off-key, and coming from behind the altar.

Sera's hand closed on Ada's shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Wait."

But Ada had already seen him.

A boy sat cross-legged on the altar steps, maybe eight years old, maybe ten — the kind of child whose age was a guess because he'd stopped growing for a while. He was thin in the way of children who give their bread away, with a shock of dark hair and a hole in his left boot that his toes had made into a window. He was arranging something on the step in front of him: acorns, a chipped teacup, three smooth stones, and a dead sparrow laid out with terrible care, like a king in state.

He was humming to the sparrow.

"Wren," Sera breathed, and the name came out of her like it had been waiting there for years.

The boy's head snapped up. His eyes were the problem. They were grey, the grey of ash, and they were not entirely the eyes of a child — there was something behind them, patient and vast and faintly amused, like someone peering out of a keyhole.

"You're late," the boy said.

Ada looked at Sera. Sera looked at Ada. "I've never seen this child in my life," Ada said.

"You're still late," the boy said. "The chapel was supposed to be found on the third day, and this is the third day, so really you're exactly on time, but you *feel* late, so." He shrugged, a small, sharp gesture. "Late."

"Wren," Sera said again, and knelt, and her voice did something Ada had never heard it do — went soft and cracked at the same time. "Little brother. You're alive."

"Obviously." But he launched himself off the altar steps and into her arms hard enough to knock her backward, and for a long moment the ruined chapel held nothing but a defrocked priestess and a scrap of a boy holding onto each other like the world had been trying to pry them apart and had failed.

Ada stood in the doorway and felt, with uncomfortable precision, like a burglar at a family reunion.

* * *

The boy was Wren, it emerged, and Sera had known him in her old life, before the falling, when she had still worn the grey-and-gold and served at a shrine in the lowlands. Wren had been a shrine orphan, left on the steps at an age too young to remember anything else, and when the order fell and the priests scattered, he had run. And kept running. And, apparently, been followed.

"By what?" Ada asked.

They were sitting around a small fire in the chapel's corner, under the one stretch of surviving roof. Wren was eating bread with the focused intensity of the truly hungry, pausing only to feed a corner of it to the sparrow, which — Ada had checked three times — was definitely, unquestionably dead. It sat up now. It tilted its head. It had eaten the bread, which raised theological questions Ada was not equipped to explore.

"By Ashen," Wren said, with his mouth full.

"The god," Sera said. "The one who answers children."

"The one who answers *my* prayers," Wren corrected. "He's small. He says all the big gods got all the big prayers, and nobody left anything for him, so he's the size of what's left over." He held up his thumb and forefinger, a hand-width apart. "About this big. He lives in my chest mostly, but he can sit on my shoulder if I'm not moving too much."

"Does he," Ada said. "Sit on your shoulder."

"He's doing it now, actually. You're being rude. He wants you to know that."

Ada looked very hard at Wren's shoulder. There was nothing there. But the air above it seemed, for a moment, to have a texture — a faint warmth, a shimmer like heat off a summer road — and she had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that something was looking back at her.

"I can't see him," she said.

"Nobody can. Except—" Wren stopped chewing. The ash-grey eyes went distant, and when he spoke again the voice was different. Not deeper, exactly. Wider. Like a small room with the walls taken out. "Except the thief."

The fire popped. Ada's hand went to her knife on pure reflex, and Sera said, "Ada," in a warning tone, and the boy-god-thing tilted its head at her with a child's face and an ancient patience.

"You carry what is not yours," it said through Wren's mouth. "You carry it well. That is the tragedy of thieves — the good ones forget they are thieves at all."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Ada said, which was a lie, and by the way the boy's head cocked, a lie it could taste.

"You carry a wedding," it said. "You carry a lullaby in a language with no word for *alone*. You carry a door. You carry the shape of a door, rather, which is worse, because a shape of a door is a hunger that thinks it's a memory." Wren's small hands folded in his lap, neat as a priest's. "You stole from a dying country, Ada Yarrow. Piece by piece. You have been carrying its bones in your head and calling them souvenirs."

The fire seemed very loud. "I steal from *travelers*," Ada said. "Merchants. Pilgrims. People with too many memories and no use for all of them. That's what I do. That's all I do."

"You stole from travelers," the god agreed, gentle as snowfall. "But the road brought you what the road was told to bring you. The Hollow kept its thieves close to the seal, and the seal kept leaking, and the leak had to go *somewhere*." The ash eyes closed. Opened. "You are not the wound, child. You are the bandage. But bandages must come off, or the flesh beneath never heals."

"I don't *want* to be a bandage," Ada said, and heard how young her own voice sounded, and hated it.

"Few do," said Ashen.

* * *

Sera built the argument all night, the way she built everything — patiently, in layers, with the maddening calm of a woman who had once talked a bishop out of excommunicating her and considered it her finest hour.

"It answered a dead bird back to life," she said, poking the fire. "Do you understand what that means? The Hollow kept the gods out for — what, centuries? And the seal cracks for a *week*, and already there's a god sitting in a ruined chapel answering the prayers of one specific orphan. It's not just any god, Ada. It's a god of leftovers. Of the unclaimed. Of course it's the one that got through. The seal was built to keep out the *great* ones. Nobody thought to bar the door against something small enough to slip under it."

"So we have a small god," Ada said. "Wonderful. Can it get us out of the hills?"

"It can tell us where the door is."

"It told us to give everything back. That's not a map, that's a mugging."

Sera looked at her across the fire, and there was something in her face that Ada had learned to dread over two days of travel: the expression of a woman about to be right at you. "What did it feel like? When it spoke. Through him."

Ada opened her mouth to say *nothing*, which was her second-favorite lie. But the truth was there, and it was worse. "Warm," she said reluctantly. "Like — like standing in a kitchen doorway when someone's cooking. Like you're not invited in, exactly, but nobody's going to make you leave, either."

"Yes," Sera said softly. "That's what it's like. That's what I spent ten years of my life kneeling to, and I threw it away because I thought the kneeling was the point." She fed the fire a stick. "It's not the point. It was never the point."

* * *

The problem, Ada decided the next morning, was the sparrow.

The sparrow was still alive. It had been alive for a day and a half now, which was a record for a bird that had been dead when they arrived. It followed Wren around the chapel in short, lopsided flights, and it had taken a violent dislike to Ada, dive-bombing her whenever she came within arm's reach of the boy.

"He doesn't like thieves," Wren explained cheerfully.

"I'm not a *thief* thief. It's a profession."

"He says a profession is just a theft you've gotten good at."

"He can't just — *say* things through you whenever he wants?"

"He mostly saves it for when you're being stubborn. So. Constantly."

Ada sat down heavily on a fallen roof-beam and looked at the boy. He was re-arranging his altar — the acorns, the teacup, the stones — with the gravity of a general positioning troops. His boots were falling apart. His ribs showed when he stretched. He had, by Sera's account, walked alone through the hills for a year, praying to a god the size of a leftover, and surviving.

Something in her chest did a thing she didn't authorize. She ignored it. She was good at ignoring things. She'd had practice.

"Ask him," she said finally. "Your god. Ask him why it has to be *me*. The memories. There are memory-eaters coming — real ones, Crown ones, the kind that take everything and leave you a husk. If I give up what I'm carrying, I'm just — Ada Yarrow, from the Hollow, with nothing. No gift. No armor. Nothing."

Wren went still. The ash-grey eyes went distant and wide, and the small room with no walls opened again.

"Ask her," the god said, "what she was before she learned to take."

The silence stretched. Ada's hands had gone cold.

"I was a child," she said. "In a valley where nothing works. I was hungry, and I was alone, and taking was the only thing that — that *worked*. You want me to go back to that. To being nothing."

"I want you to be *something*," said Ashen, "that is not made of other people. It is a smaller thing than you fear and a larger thing than you can imagine. You have spent your whole life holding a door shut with your body, thief, and calling the dark behind it *home*." The boy blinked, and was Wren again, and grinned at her with a gap in his teeth. "He says you argue like his old donkey. That's a compliment. The donkey was his favorite."

"I hate both of you," Ada said, and the sparrow dive-bombed her head, and Wren laughed — a real laugh, a child's laugh, bright and stupid and warm — and she felt the awful, treacherous thing in her chest do the unauthorized thing again, twice.

* * *

They left the chapel on the fifth morning, the three of them and one resurrected sparrow, heading north by whatever passed for Ashen's directions, which involved Wren walking confidently in wrong directions until the god corrected him through fits of giggling.

It was on the second day of walking that Wren stopped in the middle of the road, went rigid, and said, in the wide voice, with no riddle in it at all:

"The hunters have crossed the river. They are coming for the thief, and they are coming for the priestess, and they are coming for me, though they do not know it yet, because they think I am only a boy." A pause. "They are right to come. They are wrong about everything else."

Then he was Wren again, wobbling, and Ada caught his arm before she'd decided to, and he leaned into her side as if it were the most natural thing in the world, as if children had always leaned on her, as if she had always been someone to lean on.

"Walk faster," she said, gruffly, to cover the thing in her chest.

"Walk *fastest*," Wren corrected, "is the grammatically correct—"

"Walk faster or I'm feeding you to the sparrow."

And behind them, far off across the hills, on a road that did not yet know it was being watched, something with a blank face turned its head toward the north, and began, patiently, to walk.
