## 6. Memory-Eaters

The trouble with being followed, Ada thought later, was that you never found out until it was far too late to do anything clever about it.

For three days they climbed north through the hills above the Hollow, and for three days the world behaved itself — roads went where roads should go, streams ran downhill out of stubbornness rather than malice, and the only thing hunting them was Wren's appetite, which was bottomless and indiscriminate and had once, briefly, considered a pinecone.

"Are we lost?" Wren asked, on the third morning, in the tone of someone asking whether it might rain.

"No," said Sera Ilune, without looking up from the map she was not reading, because the map had gone blank two days ago past the river, all its ink faded to a pale ghost of itself, as if the paper were embarrassed to admit anything was out there at all.

"Are we *slightly* lost?"

"We are taking the scenic route," Sera said, "to a chapel that may or may not still exist, guided by a god who communicates exclusively through a nine-year-old."

"Ten," said Wren. "I'm ten."

"You're nine and a half and you know it."

Ada walked behind them with her hood up and her hands in her sleeves and let their bickering wash over her like warm water. It was a strange luxury, bickering. In the Hollow she had traveled alone for years, and alone meant silence, and silence meant listening to your own thoughts, which was a hunger of an entirely different kind.

She was thinking about that — about how she'd almost gotten used to them, which was dangerous, because things you got used to could be taken away — when the birds went quiet.

All of them. At once. The whole hillside dropping into a silence so complete she could hear the blood in her own ears.

Sera stopped mid-sentence. Her hand went to the broken medallion at her throat — an old habit, Ada had noticed, the way other people touched a wound.

"What is it?" Wren whispered.

"Down," Sera breathed. "Off the road. Now."

They slid down the embankment into a stand of birches, white trunks close together like a crowd pretending not to watch. Ada pressed her back against one and looked up through the branches.

On the road above, four figures came around the bend.

They walked like soldiers — in step, unhurried, tireless — but they did not look like any soldiers Ada had seen. Their armor was grey without shine, dull as slate, and their faces —

Their faces were blank.

Not masked. *Blank.* Smooth where features should have been, like eggs, like unfinished clay, and yet somehow they saw, because the lead figure paused exactly opposite their hiding place and tilted its smooth head, slowly, the way a dog tilts its head at a sound no one else can hear.

Wren's hand found Ada's sleeve and gripped it.

The figure stood there for the length of ten heartbeats. Twenty. Ada did not breathe. She thought of the merchant's wedding memory, of candlelight and dancing, and held onto it like a rail in a storm, because if these things took memories then surely a mind full of something bright and borrowed was better than a mind full of fear—

Then the figure moved on. The others followed. The blank faces passed above the birches like moons crossing behind cloud, and the birds, after a long cautious minute, began again, tentatively, as if apologizing.

"They didn't see us," Wren whispered.

"No," Sera said. Her voice was strange. Flat. "They didn't need to. They're not tracking us, child. They're tracking *me*." She sat down abruptly on a root, as if her legs had filed their resignation. "I hoped I had more time."

Ada crouched beside her. "Those were the memory-eaters. The Crown's hunters. Brammel warned—" She stopped. "You've seen them before."

"I helped make them." Sera laughed, and there was nothing funny in it at all. "Or — no. That's not fair. I helped write the seal. The Crown built the hunters to enforce it. Same crime, different hands." She looked up, and her eyes were the eyes of someone reading a sentence she'd written long ago and only now understanding. "They can't find me by my face, Ada. I made sure of that, years ago, when I ran. They find me by my guilt. It has a — a texture. A taste. Like smoke to a hound."

"That's horrible," said Wren, with the simple moral clarity of children.

"Yes," said Sera. "It is."

"And us?" Ada asked. "What do they want with us? With me?"

Sera was quiet for a moment too long.

"Sera. What do they want with *me*?"

"They don't want you dead." Sera's voice dropped, gentle in the way people are gentle when the news is bad. "Dead sigil-bearers are useless. They want you alive, Ada. Whole. They want to open you up and take every stolen memory out of you, piece by piece, carefully, the way you'd harvest fruit before the frost. And then they'll take everything else you have besides. Every memory you've ever made of your own." She reached out and took Ada's wrist, not unkindly. "You have to understand what you're carrying. You're not a thief to them. You're a *larder*."

The word sat in Ada's chest like a swallowed stone.

A larder. A storehouse. A thing kept cool and dark and full, waiting to be emptied.

She thought of every traveler she'd ever brushed fingers with in the market crowds of the Hollow. She had always told herself it was survival. Taking from the full to feed the empty. But she had never once asked what it would feel like from the other side — to be opened, to be *shopped from*.

Above them, far off along the ridge, a crow lifted from a dead tree and flew north, fast, low, purposeful.

Like a messenger, Ada thought.

Like something reporting back.

* * *

Corvane did not like the forest.

This was not sentiment; it was professional assessment. Forests were full of textures — moss, rot, sap, the green noise of growing things — and all of it muddied the trail. Guilt tasted cleanest in open country, under open sky, where a man's regrets rose off him like heat off a stone.

He knelt at the place where the road bent by the birches and pressed his bare palm flat to the earth.

There. Faint, days old, but unmistakable: shame, old and layered, shot through with a newer thread of fear. The priestess. Ilune. She had stood here — right here, close enough that he could almost reconstruct her posture from the residue — and she had been afraid.

And threaded through hers, brighter, stranger: something he had no name for. A sweetness. A wrongness. Memories that did not belong to the body carrying them, stacked like coins in a purse, each one humming faintly with a light he could not see but could, increasingly, *almost* taste.

The sigil-bearer.

His three hunters waited behind him without fidgeting, because he had taken their fidgeting years ago, along with most of their impatience and all of their doubt. Good hunters needed to be mostly empty. That was the whole secret of the trade, though the Crown dressed it up in doctrine and scripture: you could not pour new wine into a full cup. He had given his cup away a long time ago, drop by drop, and told himself each time that what he was pouring out was worthless.

He stood. "They went north. Toward the chapel ruins."

One of the hunters inclined its smooth head. *Why do we not simply take them now,* it asked, in the voiceless way his kind spoke, a pressure against the inside of his skull. *We are four. They are three.*

"Because the girl must come back intact," Corvane said. "Every fragment. Do you understand what's in her? Not just the sigil pieces. Everything she's ever eaten. Decades of harvested lives. The Crown wants the vault delivered *unbroken*, and if we frighten her, she'll burn it. They always threaten to burn it. Some of them mean it."

*And the priestess?*

He was quiet a moment.

"The priestess is authorized for feeding," he said. "On site. Keep her alive — the Crown likes its confessions spoken aloud — but she's owed. She wrote the seal and then she ran from it. There's a debt there, and debts to the Crown are paid in the only coin it accepts."

*Which is?*

"Everything," said Corvane. "It's always everything. Move."

They moved. And as he walked, Corvane did the thing he did every night, the ritual no doctrine required, the small theft he permitted himself: he tried to remember his mother's voice.

He knew he had one. He knew this the way you know there's a room behind a bricked-up door. Somewhere in the first years of his life — in a country that no longer appeared on maps, among people whose names had been struck from every ledger — a woman had sung to him. He had proof. He had a single fact, salvaged like one chair from a burned house: *she sang.*

He could not recall a single note.

That was why he served the Crown. Not loyalty. Not belief. The Crown had promised him, at the end of all this, at the end of the harvesting and the sealing and the tidy erasure of inconvenient things, that it would give him back what Vael's fall had scraped out of him. It would remember him back into existence. It would put his mother's voice in his mouth again like a coin returned to a beggar.

He had been paying toward that promise for eleven years.

He did not let himself think about whether the Crown had ever once honored a debt.

* * *

They reached the ruined chapel at dusk, and it was exactly as welcoming as a ruin ever is, which is to say it had a roof over roughly a third of its floor and opinions about drafts.

Wren claimed the corner beneath the one surviving arch immediately, in the manner of a general seizing high ground. "This is my room," he announced. "The rest of you live outside it."

"It's a ruin, Wren. You live in the ruin. We all live in the ruin."

"My *room* is in the ruin. There's a difference. Ashen says boundaries are important."

"Ashen," said Ada, "is a sparrow who ate a moth last week and talked about it for two days."

"He said it was *sacramental*."

Sera had barely spoken since the birches. She built a small fire — small, shielded, the fire of a woman practiced at not being seen — and sat beside it turning her broken medallion over and over in her fingers, and Ada watched her and felt the stone in her chest shift into something sharper.

"You knew they'd come," Ada said quietly, when Wren had finally gone down into sleep, curled around the sparrow like a comma around its clause. "You knew, and you didn't say how bad it was."

"I said enough."

"You said they were hunting us. You didn't say they'd crack me open like a jar of preserves."

Sera flinched. Good, thought Ada, and hated herself a little for thinking it.

"I'm sorry," Sera said. "Truly. But I've learned something about bad news, Ada, in the years since I fell: people can only carry so much of it at a time. I gave you what you could walk with. Tomorrow I'd have given you the rest."

"And tonight?"

Sera looked into the fire. "Tonight the rest arrived early."

Ada wanted to stay angry. Anger was easier than the other thing, the colder thing underneath it, which was the image she couldn't shake: herself as a larder. Cool. Dark. Full. Waiting.

"What happens to you," she asked instead, "if they catch you? Really. No softening it."

Sera was silent long enough that the fire popped twice.

"They feed," she said finally. "That's what memory-eaters do, Ada. They don't kill you. Killing wastes the crop. They eat your memories while you watch, and they take the oldest ones first, because those hold the most of you, and they leave you—" She turned her hand over, palm up, empty. "Hollow-eyed. Smiling, sometimes. The ones who've been fed on smile a great deal. There's nothing left heavy enough to grieve with."

"You've seen it."

"I've done worse than seen it." Her thumb moved across the broken medallion. "I signed the order that created them. My name is in the founding text, in the old script, in ink mixed with ash. When they catch me, they won't be punishing a traitor. They'll be collecting on an investment."

Ada sat with that. Outside, the wind moved through the birches, and somewhere far off a nightbird called once and got no answer.

"Sera," she said. "If it comes to it — if they corner us — you run. You don't fight, you don't cover our retreat, none of that heroic nonsense. You run, because you're the only one who knows where the door is, and I'm—" her mouth twisted— "apparently a pantry with legs."

Something crossed Sera's face — grief and gratitude fighting over the same ground — and she reached across the fire and squeezed Ada's hand, brief and hard.

"You're not a pantry," she said. "You're the worst thief I've ever met. You steal things and then you can't stop caring about the people you stole them from."

"That's not a compliment."

"It's the highest one I know."

Ada pulled her blanket around her shoulders and lay down with her back to the fire, and did not sleep, and somewhere in the black hours she heard Sera whispering to the broken medallion in a language that predated the valley, and the words sounded, though Ada could not have said why, like an apology being rehearsed.

* * *

They came in the hour before dawn, when the fire had burned to red eyes and the world was at its least defended.

Ada woke not to sound but to absence — the birdsong cutting out, that same terrible switch thrown — and was moving before her mind caught up, grabbing Wren, shaking Sera, and then the doorway filled with grey and the blank faces came in like weather.

There were six of them. More than the road. They had split, or called to each other, or simply *known*.

"Wren, the window, *now*—" Ada shoved the boy toward the gap in the east wall, and he went, clutching the sparrow, scrambling over fallen stone with the desperate agility of the young, and then a hunter stepped between Ada and the window as smoothly as a door closing.

It did not draw a weapon. That was the worst part. It simply stood there, featureless, patient, and Ada understood with total clarity that she was not prey tonight. Prey gets chased. She was *stock*. Stock waits.

"Take the priestess," said a voice from the doorway — a human voice, roughened by disuse, and Ada turned to see a man in the same slate-grey armor, but with a face: hollow-cheeked, grey-eyed, ordinary, and somehow more frightening than the blanks for having features at all. Features meant he had once been someone. "The bearer is not to be touched. Not a hair. The Crown will have her whole or not at all."

Two hunters closed on Sera.

She did not run. Ada screamed at her to run, lunged, was caught — gently, horribly gently, a blank hand closing on her arm with the care of someone handling glass — and held, forced to watch.

Sera stood her ground in the failing firelight with her chin up and her broken medallion raised like a torch, and said, in the old script, in the language of the order she had betrayed: *"I repent. I recant. Take what is owed and no more."*

The nearest hunter reached out and laid its palm against her forehead.

Ada would spend the rest of her life trying to describe what happened next, and would never manage it. There was no light, no scream. There was a *pulling* — she felt it in her own teeth, in the roots of her hair — and Sera's face changed. Not all at once. In stages, like a house going dark room by room. First the fierceness went out of her eyes. Then the grief. Then, horribly, the recognition — she looked straight at Ada and there was nothing behind her gaze, no alliance, no guilt, no name, just a pleasant vague warmth, the face of a woman at a festival who cannot quite place where she knows you from.

Then the hunter withdrew its hand, and Sera smiled.

"There you are," she said kindly, to no one in particular. "Lovely evening, isn't it?"

The stone in Ada's chest cracked clean through.

"Give her back," she heard herself say. "Give her *back*."

"She isn't taken," said the grey-faced man — Corvane, though she didn't know the name yet. He studied Ada with an expression that was not cruelty and was worse than cruelty: appraisal. The look of a buyer. "She's spent. What was in her is in the Crown's keeping now, where it will be catalogued and preserved far more safely than it ever was in her." He stepped closer, and up close his eyes were the emptiest thing in the room, emptier than the blanks'. "You, though. You I'm instructed to deliver untouched. So I'll say this once, thief, and then we'll walk north together, all of us, comfortably: every step you run makes the harvest harder on you. Come willingly, and perhaps — *perhaps* — some few of your little hoard get returned to their owners afterward. Fight, and the Crown takes the lot and burns the husk."

Behind him, through the window gap, Ada saw a small shape slip away over the wall in the grey pre-dawn — Wren, alive, running, the sparrow a dark fleck above him.

She held onto that. She held onto it the way you hold a rope over a drop.

"And if I come with you," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out, "you'll feed on me anyway. Slowly. Piece by piece. Won't you. That's what 'harvest' means."

Corvane said nothing, which was answer enough.

So Ada did the only thing left in her larder: she grabbed the memory closest to hand — the merchant's wedding, candles and dancing and being loved for one golden hour — and she hurled it, all of it, deliberately, catastrophically, into the mind of the hunter holding her arm.

The effect was instant and enormous. The blank face *cracked* — a fine line down the middle, weeping light — and the creature staggered back with its hands pressed to where its eyes should be, and every hunter in the room turned toward it, and in that single heartbeat of chaos Ada wrenched free and went out the window after Wren, into the birches, into the dark, running blind north with a hollow-eyed priestess's hand caught in hers because some part of her refused — *refused* — to leave even the emptied version of Sera Ilune to the Crown.

Behind her, Corvane did not shout. He did not chase.

He stood in the ruined chapel amid his reeling hunters, watching the girl vanish into the trees, and he felt, rising unbidden through eleven years of disciplined emptiness, something he had no category for anymore.

Not anger.

*Recognition.*

She had thrown away her most precious thing to buy one second of freedom. He knew that arithmetic. He had done it every day of his life.

"Let her run," he said quietly, to the hunters. "North. She'll run north. They always run toward the door."

And he turned to follow, patient as winter, while behind him the cracked hunter wept light into the ashes of the fire — weeping, for the first time in its hollow existence, tears that belonged to somebody else's wedding day.
