## 7. The First Piece

The woman was dying the way candles die — not all at once, but guttering, her light pulling inward toward some last stubborn wick.

Her name was Maren Ostwick, and she had been a weaver once, though you'd never know it from the room she lay in now: a rented bed in a hospice in Lowmark, one window, one chair, a basin of water gone gray. Sera stood by the door with her arms folded and her jaw set in the particular way she set it when she thought Ada was about to do something foolish. Wren sat cross-legged on the floor, feeding crumbs to a moth he'd named. The god Ashen — small as a candleflame, dim as an ember — flickered on the windowsill like something trying to remember how to burn.

"She's asking for you," Sera said. "She's been asking since dawn."

"I don't even know her."

"You know her better than anyone alive does." Sera's voice went flat. "You're carrying half her life around in your head, girl. She's been missing it for thirty years. She says it's why she can't die properly. A person needs their whole self to finish."

Ada looked at the old woman in the bed. Maren's hands were the giveaway — broad-palmed, fingers crooked from decades at the loom, even though there was no loom here, no thread, nothing made in this room for years except the slow work of letting go.

"I've given memories back before," Ada said. "Little ones. A first kiss. The smell of somebody's mother's kitchen. It's like handing someone a coat they didn't know they'd lost." She swallowed. "This is different. This is *decades*. If I open myself up to give it back—"

"You'll see everything," Ashen said.

They all turned. The little god rarely spoke unless spoken to, and when it did speak its voice was strange — not deep, exactly, but *wide*, like a word said in a large empty room. It sounded, Ada thought, the way a child sounds when repeating something very old.

"You will see what she saw," Ashen said. "Memory does not travel one way. When you return a thing, you walk it home. And you will feel every step of the road."

"Cheerful," Ada muttered.

"It is meant to be." The ember brightened slightly, which Ada had learned was the god's version of a smile. "Grief is only love with nowhere left to go. You are about to give hers somewhere to go. That is not a small kindness, thief. Do not pretend it is nothing."

Wren looked up from his moth. "I think it's brave," he offered.

"Thank you, Wren."

"I also think you look like you're going to be sick."

"*Thank you*, Wren."

* * *

Maren Ostwick's eyes were open when Ada sat beside the bed. They were pale eyes, washed-out as river glass, and they fixed on Ada with an urgency that had outlived most of the rest of her.

"You're the one," Maren rasped. "The one who has it."

"I have—" Ada began, and stopped, because how do you apologize for that? *I stole your life because I was hungry and lonely and taking other people's joy was the only warmth I could afford.* "Yes. I have it. I'm sorry. I'm going to give it back."

The old woman's cracked lips moved through something that might have been a laugh. "Sorry." She lifted one crooked hand — it took visible effort — and caught Ada's wrist with fingers light as paper. "Child. I sold it. Did Brammel never tell you? Thirty years ago I came through the Hollow with my whole life in my head and grief in my chest so heavy I couldn't carry it over the mountains too. There was a fence-post trader who gave me three silver marks and a hot meal for the years between one spring and another. Best bargain of my life." Her grip tightened, impossibly, for someone so close to gone. "And the worst. Because now I can't remember what I was dying to forget, and it turns out I was dying to remember it after all."

Ada's throat closed. In the Hollow, everyone assumed the memories were stolen. It was easier that way — easier to be a thief than to be a market where desperate people traded away their own hearts for bread. Easier for them, and easier for her.

"Brammel said you'd come," Maren whispered. "He said, someday the valley cracks and the girl who holds the pieces will come looking. He said—" A cough took her, long and rattling. "He said tell her it isn't theft if it's kept safe. Tell her thank you for keeping it safe."

Behind her, Ada heard Sera exhale slowly, and Wren go very quiet, and the little god on the windowsill burn a shade steadier.

"Are you ready?" Ada asked.

"No," said Maren Ostwick, with feeling. "Give it to me anyway."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about giving a memory back: you don't hand it over. You become the road it travels.

Ada let the fragment rise in her — she knew it by its weight, the oldest and heaviest thing she carried, a dense dark knot behind her breastbone that she'd always assumed was just hunger — and the knot opened like a door, and she fell through.

She was standing in a street that smelled of bread and woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, something like honey and cold stone, and the street was *singing*.

Not the people. The street itself. The cobbles under her feet hummed a low chord, and the walls on either side — white stone shot through with veins of blue crystal — picked it up and harmonized, and overhead the sky was the particular bruised gold of late afternoon in high summer, and banners hung from every window in colors Ada had no names for: not red, not orange, but the color of embers; not green, but the color of new leaves seen through wine.

Vael.

She knew it without being told, the way you know your own name in a dream. This was Vael, the country that wasn't, the country erased from every map and ledger and tongue, and it was *alive*, it was loud, it was full —

People everywhere. A festival — of course a festival, the memory would have been a festival, who sells away the worst day of their life and keeps the best? — lanterns strung between towers so tall their tops vanished into cloud, and the towers themselves leaning gently toward each other across the streets like old friends sharing secrets. Children ran past her trailing ribbons. An old man played a stringed instrument with nine strings and somehow made it sound right. Two women argued cheerfully over a stall of fruit that glowed faintly from within, and a dog the size of a pony lay in the middle of the square while toddlers used it as furniture, and nobody minded, because this was Vael and apparently in Vael enormous dogs were public property.

And the door. There — at the end of the street, framed between two towers, cut into the living rock of a mountain that rose up behind the whole city like a parent standing behind a child — a seam of light. Not a door that opened. A door that simply *was*, a shape in the mountain exactly like the sigil that lived scattered in Ada's head, glowing softly, patiently, the way a hearth glows. People walked past it constantly and barely glanced at it, the way you stop seeing your own front door.

*That's it,* Ada thought, and her dream-self pressed a hand to her mouth. *That's the door. That's what we're walking toward. Oh, it's real. It's really real.*

"Maren!" someone shouted, young and joyful, and Ada turned because the memory turned, because she was inside Maren Ostwick now, wearing her eyes, twenty-six years old and laughing—

A young man vaulted off a fountain's edge and caught Maren around the waist and spun her, and she shrieked and laughed, and Ada felt it — felt the spin, the dizziness, the specific joy of being held by someone who loved you and wasn't afraid to show it in the middle of a street. His name rose up through the memory like a fish surfacing: *Tallen*. Her husband of one year. He had flour on his sleeves because he'd been baking since dawn for the Festival of the Open Door, and he hadn't slept, and he didn't care.

"You're going to drop me!"

"I have never dropped you."

"You dropped me in the river!"

"That was a *baptism*."

And Maren laughed so hard she had to hold onto his shoulders, and the memory-Ada-inside-Maren looked past him at the mountain door glowing in the golden light, and thought — and Ada felt the thought arrive whole, warm as a coal:

*We are so lucky. We are so lucky to live here. Someday I'll bring our children to see the Door-light festival, and they'll ask why the door glows, and I'll say because it's waiting, love, doors that matter are always waiting—*

The memory surged forward, drunk on itself. Lanterns lit one by one down the whole length of the street as the sun set, each flame catching with a sound like a struck bell. Music started somewhere. Tallen danced badly and Maren danced worse and neither of them cared. Someone handed her warm spiced wine in a cup she was expected to keep — *keep the cup, it's yours now, that's the tradition, the festival gives everyone one thing to keep* — and Maren held the cup up to the mountain and toasted the door, and hundreds of voices around her did the same, a whole city raising cups to a seam of light, and the sound they made—

The sound they made was the sound of a country that did not yet know it was going to die.

Ada understood then, with a lurch, why the memory had been worth selling. Not because it hurt. Because it was *perfect*, and perfection is the heaviest thing a person can carry once it's gone. Every step away from this street, this night, this spinning laughing man with flour on his sleeves, would be measured against it forever.

The memory began, gently, to turn toward morning.

* * *

It should have ended there. Festivals end; memories end; that was the shape of things. But the fragment shuddered as it turned, and Ada — still inside Maren, still holding the festival cup — felt the edges of the memory tear, felt it snag on something, and the scene lurched sideways into a different day.

Gray sky. Smoke. The same beautiful street, and the singing cobbles silent, and the towers leaning together not like friends now but like mourners. People running. Not screaming — that was the terrible part, nobody screamed, they moved with the grim efficiency of people who had practiced this — carrying children, carrying the glowing fruit, carrying the nine-stringed instrument, carrying whatever could be carried toward the mountain door, which blazed now, blazing like a wound in the rock, wide open and pouring out light.

"The erasure's coming up the valley!" someone shouted. "Half a day! Half a day, get to the Door!"

And Maren — Maren running, Maren with a bundle in her arms that was not a child, couldn't be a child, was a bundle of woven cloth, her own weaving, her masterwork, the tapestry she'd spent four years on — Maren stopping dead in the street because Tallen wasn't behind her anymore, Tallen had turned back for the neighbors' boy who'd frozen in the smoke, and the air at the far end of the street was going *wrong*, going thin, going the color of nothing—

"TALLEN—"

And the memory tore free of that moment like a hand snatched back from a stove, and Ada was falling, falling out of it, out of the gray and the smoke and the name she'd been about to lose, falling up through gold light and lantern-bells and the taste of spiced wine, falling back into a narrow room with one window and a basin of gray water—

* * *

She came back to herself kneeling on the hospice floor, gasping, Wren's small hands gripping her arm, Sera saying her name twice before she heard it.

"I'm fine," Ada managed. "I'm fine. I—"

She looked up at the bed.

Maren Ostwick was crying. Silently, steadily, tears running into the pillow, but her face — her face had come alive. The washed-out eyes were bright. Her crooked hands had risen from the blanket and were moving in the air, weaving, fingers remembering patterns thirty years forgotten, and she was smiling the way people smile at things no one else can see.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, the lanterns. The bells. He had flour on his sleeves, the fool, he baked all night, I told him—" A sob broke loose and she let it. "I got out. I got out and I never knew what I ran from. Now I know both ends of it. Now I'm *whole*." She turned her bright ruined eyes on Ada and caught her hand again, and pressed something into it — small, hard, warm. "Keep the cup," Maren whispered. "That's the tradition. The festival gives everyone one thing to keep."

Ada opened her fingers. A tiny clay cup, glazed ember-orange, no bigger than a thimble — a charm, a keepsake, the kind of thing a woman might have carried out of a burning country and kept for thirty years without knowing why.

"I can't take—"

"You can. You must." Maren's voice was fading now, but it was a peaceful fading, a candle settling into its last comfortable glow. "You're going to the Door. I can see it in you — you're all going to the Door. Take it. Show them. Show them we kept the tradition even at the end." Her eyes drifted shut. "Even at the end... we kept..."

The breath went out of her slowly, like a tide withdrawing, and did not come back.

For a while nobody moved. Wren was crying without noise, which broke Ada's heart more cleanly than sobbing would have. Sera bowed her head and murmured something in an old liturgical cadence, half-swallowed, the words of a priestess who no longer believed she had the right to them.

Ashen flared on the windowsill — brighter than Ada had ever seen it, a true small flame now, steady and gold.

"She died whole," the god said quietly. "Do you understand what you did, thief? The Crown hunts you to harvest what you carry. The Hollow called you a thief. And today you were neither. Today you were a door, and something lost walked home through you." The flame bent, almost like a bow. "That is the work. That has always been the work."

Ada looked down at the tiny cup in her palm, still warm, absurdly warm, warm as festival-wine, warm as a hand catching yours in a crowd.

"One piece down," Sera said at last, hoarse. Her eyes, when she raised them, were wet and fierce. "How many left?"

Ada closed her fist around the cup and felt, deep beneath her ribs, the sigil shift — one fragment settled home, and the rest stirred like sleeping birds.

"Enough to worry about," she said. "Come on. We're going to the mountain."

Outside the hospice window, far to the north, the horizon flickered — just once, just faintly — the color of nothing at all.
