## 8. Roads That Forget Themselves

The road out of the hills had been a road once. Ada could tell by the stones, worn round and patient underfoot, and by the way the hedgerows leaned back from it like old men making room for a story they'd heard a thousand times. But somewhere in the last hour it had stopped being sure of itself.

She noticed it first as a wrongness in her boots — that faint, subterranean disagreement between what her feet expected and what the ground delivered. A rise where there should have been a dip. A dip where she'd have sworn there was a rise. She'd walked enough roads in her life to know their moods, and this one was sulking.

"Are we going in circles?" Wren asked, for the fourth time.

"No," said Sera, without turning around.

"We passed that tree before."

"That's an alder."

"It's the *same* alder."

Ada looked back at the tree. It was a crooked thing with a lightning scar down its middle and a crow's nest wedged in its highest fork, and she had to admit it did look familiar. But then, most crooked trees look familiar when you're tired, the way all strangers start looking like your cousin if you stare long enough.

"The same alder," Wren insisted, "with the same nest. And I think the same crow. It looked at me the same way."

"Crows only have one way of looking at people," Ada said.

"This one was *judging* me."

"Then it's definitely not the same one," Ada said. "The first one judged me."

Sera stopped walking. That alone was alarming; Sera Ilune walked the way water flows downhill, with a kind of grim inevitability, and she did not stop unless something had earned it. She stood in the middle of the uncertain road, turned slowly once, and Ada watched her face do something complicated.

"What?" Ada asked.

"When we left the chapel this morning," Sera said carefully, "the sun was on our left."

"And now?"

"Now it's behind us."

Wren squinted up at the sky as though the sun were a suspect he intended to interrogate personally. "Maybe it moved."

"The sun doesn't move, love."

"*Everything else* has been moving," Wren pointed out, with the unassailable logic of children everywhere. "The trees moved. The hills moved. The village we slept in didn't exist when we woke up, so why can't the sun move too?"

He had a point, which was the worst part. The village — three farms and a shrine with a well — had been there at dusk, warm bread and a woman who'd let them sleep in her barn. At dawn the barn had been a ruin, roofless, the beams black with forty years of rot, and the well choked with brambles. The woman's bread had still been in Ada's pack, real and heavy and smelling of caraway, which somehow made it worse. You could argue with a ghost. It was harder to argue with breakfast.

"The erasure is spreading," Sera said quietly. "I told you it would. Vael isn't just being forgotten inside its borders anymore. The forgetting is leaking."

"Leaking how?"

"Like ink through wet paper." Sera started walking again, faster now. "Roads first. Roads are the oldest memories a country has. Then villages. Then—" she didn't finish it, and Ada didn't ask her to, because the shape of the unfinished sentence was obvious: *then people.*

Ashen had been silent since morning.

That was the other wrongness, the one Ada kept trying not to think about. The little god rode in Wren's arms or perched on his shoulder — a small grey presence like a fledgling made of smoke and cinders, weightless except when it mattered — and usually he muttered constantly. Riddles, complaints, observations about the moral failings of crows. Today he sat in the crook of Wren's elbow like a stone that had given up being a bird, and when Wren poked him, he flickered.

"He's tired," Wren said, catching Ada looking. "Gods get tired. He said so."

"When did he say so?"

"A while ago." Wren's chin went up in the stubborn way that meant he was protecting something. "Before you were listening."

*Before you were listening.* Ada filed that away with the other small wounds Wren dealt out without meaning to, the ones that reminded her she was the newest piece of this strange little family and might always be. She was used to taking things from people. Being kept outside of things was newer, and it stung worse than she'd expected.

The road looped them past the alder twice more before Sera admitted defeat and cut cross-country through the bracken, navigating by a hill with a standing stone on top that she swore she could see from anywhere. They climbed toward it for an hour. It did not get closer. It got *further*, politely, the way a dream rearranges itself around your attention, until Sera stood panting on a ridge with her fists clenched and said something in the old liturgical tongue that Ada was fairly sure was not a blessing.

"All right," Ada said. "New rule. Nobody walks more than ten steps from anybody else. We hold hands like children crossing a market square if we have to."

"I'm not holding *her* hand," Wren said, jerking a thumb at Sera. "She prays in her sleep and it wakes me up."

"You'll hold my hand, then," Ada said, and offered it before she could think better of the offer, and Wren took it with the matter-of-fact gravity of a boy accepting a duty rather than a kindness, and Ada spent the next several minutes fiercely attending to the path because her throat had gone tight over something as small as a child's warm grip. She had stolen a hundred memories of hands held. None of them had prepared her for the actual weight of it.

*

They found the second village by accident, which was the only way anyone was finding anything that day.

It appeared out of the bracken all at once — a cluster of slate roofs and smoking chimneys, a green with a maypole, geese honking their opinions from a pond. Smoke rose from the chimneys. A dog barked at them from behind a gate with genuine professional commitment.

"See?" Wren said. "A whole village. The world's fine."

But Ada had learned to read places the way she read faces, and something about the village was off-key. The smoke rose straight up in a wind she couldn't feel. The geese circled the pond without ever quite landing. And the people — a man splitting wood, two women at a loom under an awning, children chasing a hoop — none of them looked up. Not at three strangers walking into their green. Not at the dog's racket. Not at anything.

"Hello," Sera called out, using the loud clear voice of someone accustomed to being obeyed in large buildings.

The wood-splitter raised his axe and brought it down. Raised it. Brought it down. The same log, Ada realized after a moment. The same swing, the same angle, the chips flying the same arc, again and again, like a memory of work rather than work itself.

"They're not real," she said. "Or they're—"

"Held," Sera finished. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when the truth was heavy and she wanted to set it down without dropping it on anyone. "Caught mid-day. This is what erasure looks like from the inside, before it finishes. The Crown's clerics call it *stasis vulgaris*. The common stillness." She crossed the green slowly, and where she walked, the villagers' eyes tracked her — just barely, just a flicker, like sleepers feeling a draft. "They're not dead. They're being *edited*. Somewhere in a census hall in Corvale, someone is ruling these people out of existence, line by line, and here they are, waiting to see if they survive the arithmetic."

One of the children chasing the hoop stumbled and fell directly in front of Ada, and Ada — reflex older than thought — knelt and caught her. The girl looked up. Her eyes were brown and present and *there*, for one heartbeat, and she whispered:

"Don't let them count me out."

Then the stillness took her again, and she got up, and ran after her hoop, and the hoop rolled through Ada as though she were the ghost.

Ada stayed kneeling on the green for longer than she meant to. Wren put his hand on her shoulder, awkwardly, the way boys do when they've seen a grown-up cry and want to acknowledge it without any of the messiness of conversation.

"She knew us," he said. "Sort of."

"People mid-erasure see true things," Sera said softly. "The veil thins. It's why the priests banned speaking to them." She paused. "I helped write that ban."

"You keep doing that," Ada said, standing, brushing off her knees. "Confessing in advance. Like you're paying tolls."

"Guilt is cheaper than absolution these days. Absolution requires gods, and ours are—" Sera gestured vaguely at Ashen, who flickered like a candle in a doorway.

And that was when Ada noticed Wren was no longer holding her hand.

*

The green was perhaps forty paces across. She had been kneeling in the middle of it. There was nowhere a seven-year-old could go.

"Wren?" she said.

The hoop-child ran past. The axe fell. The geese circled.

"WREN!"

She spun, heart slamming, scanning the lanes between houses, the doorways, the well. Nothing. Sera was already moving, calling his name in a voice that cracked on the second syllable, and Ada felt something open under her ribs like a trapdoor — that specific vertigo of losing a child in a crowd, except there was no crowd, there was nothing, there was a village full of people who could not see him either—

"Ashen," she gasped. "Ashen, find him, please—"

The little god lifted his head from Wren's abandoned cloak — the cloak, dropped on the green, still warm — and for one terrible moment he did nothing at all. Then he shuddered, guttered, and spoke, and his voice came out thin as ash sifting through fingers:

*I cannot. The roads here do not lead anywhere. He has stepped off the map.*

"What does that MEAN?" Ada shouted, at a god, in a dead village, which she supposed was the sort of thing her life had become.

*It means,* said Ashen, and his flickering steadied into something ancient and frightened, *that the erasure has reached the roads themselves. The paths between here and Vael are being unwritten. Your boy is not lost. He is somewhere that does not exist yet — or exists no longer. And I am too small to follow.*

Sera had gone white. "The mountain door," she breathed. "If the roads to it are unwritten—"

"Find my brother," Ada said, "or I will find a way to make you bigger, god or no god, I swear it on every stolen thing I own—"

*Child of the Hollow,* Ashen said, and there was no riddle in it now, only weariness and something terribly like tenderness, *I do not need threats. I need prayers. He is mine. Call him the way he calls me.*

Ada didn't understand. Then she did, and it was the simplest and hardest thing anyone had ever asked of her.

She knelt on the green of a village that wasn't there, beside a cloak that was, clasped her hands the way she'd seen travelers do a thousand times on the road — the gesture she'd always watched from outside, hungry, taking their prayers later like scraps from a table — and prayed.

She didn't know how. She did it anyway, badly, aloud, in the plain voice of a woman who had never asked anyone for anything she couldn't steal:

"Wren. Wren, come back. Come back to us. Come back to *me*. Please. I'm asking. I don't— I don't know how to do this, but I'm asking."

The stillness broke.

Every villager on the green turned their head at once — wood-splitter, weavers, geese-stabbing children — and looked at her, and their eyes were all brown and present and awake, and the hoop-girl smiled at her, and the wind she couldn't feel finally touched her face, and out of the lane between the third house and the well came the sound of small feet running.

Wren burst onto the green clutching something to his chest, tear-streaked, furious, alive. "There was a DOOR," he yelled. "In the side of the hill! And it was opening! And something on the other side was calling my name in a nice voice and I *almost*—"

He stopped. Looked at the villagers. Looked at Ada's face.

"You prayed," he said, wonderingly. "For me. Out loud."

"Don't make it weird," Ada said, and gathered him up anyway, cloak and god and all, and held on hard enough that he complained about it, which was how she knew everything was fine.

It was Ashen who saw it first. He went rigid on Wren's shoulder, staring past them all at the hillside above the village — at the seam in the rock that hadn't been there a moment ago, a vertical crack shaped almost, *almost* like half of a sigil, glowing faintly, breathing.

*That,* Ashen said, very quietly, *is not supposed to be visible yet.*

Behind them, the village began, gently, to forget itself — roofs greying, smoke thinning, the hoop rolling to a stop in grass gone suddenly forty years tall.

Ahead, the door in the hill was already opening.
