## 9. Corvane's Offer

Ada had learned long ago that the worst lies were the ones you told yourself with your eyes open.

She told herself one now, standing in the market square of a village called Tallowmere — a village that had not been on any map Sera owned, and which Sera insisted should not exist at all — telling herself that she was only browsing. That the stalls of smoked fish and dyed wool held no interest for her beyond idle curiosity. That she was not, in fact, scanning every hooded figure and heavy boot for the man with the blank face who had been one market behind them for three days.

"You're doing it again," Wren said, tugging her sleeve. He was holding a honey-bun he had definitely not paid for, and Ada decided, as she often did, that this was Ashen's problem and not hers. The god lived in the boy's shadow these days, small as a candleflame in a drafty church, and gods could surely cover the cost of pastry.

"Doing what?"

"Looking like a rabbit that's heard the dog but hasn't seen it yet."

"Thank you, Wren. You have a poet's gift."

"I do, actually. Ashen says I—" He stopped. His face did something complicated. "Ashen's been quiet since the crossroads."

Ada crouched to his level. This was another thing she'd learned: never loom over a frightened child, even when you were the frightened one. "Quiet how?"

"Quiet-quiet." He shrugged, too casually, the way children shrug when they are carrying something much heavier than they want anyone to see. "He still hums sometimes. But he doesn't answer." He looked up at her, and his eyes were too old for his face, the way they always were when he talked about the god. "Ada, what if he's forgetting? What if the roads ate him too?"

The roads. Since they'd turned toward the mountain, the world had begun misbehaving in ways Ada didn't have words for. Bridges that led back to their own beginnings. A shepherd who swore he'd never seen the hill he was standing on, though his sheep clearly disagreed. Villages that blinked out of Sera's memory between one evening and the next morning, so that she woke insisting they'd slept in the open all along.

And now, apparently, gods.

"He won't forget," Ada said, because Wren needed her to say it, and because she wanted it to be true so badly she could taste it. "Gods don't forget. That's the whole point of them."

"That's not true," said a voice behind her, mild as milk. "Gods forget constantly. It's people who remember. That's why we keep eating your memories — we're tidying up on your behalf."

Ada turned slowly.

Corvane stood by the fishmonger's stall, and he was not wearing his blank face today. That was somehow worse. Without the smooth white mask of the memory-eaters' order, he was just a man — tall, gray at the temples, with tired eyes and a traveler's coat gone soft at the elbows. He might have been anybody's uncle. He might have been somebody's father. He held two cups of cider, and he offered her one as though they were old friends meeting by chance in a market, which was exactly the kind of thing a hunter would do, and exactly the kind of thing that worked, damn him.

"You're alone," Ada said.

"I'm alone," he agreed. "Sera Ilune is half a mile east, arguing with a woman about whether her grandmother's village ever existed. The boy is beside you, eating stolen goods. And I am buying cider." He waggled the cup. "It seemed polite, under the circumstances."

"Under what circumstances?"

"The circumstances of my having followed you across three provinces without harming a hair on any of your heads, which I feel has earned me at least a conversation." He glanced down at Wren, and something in his face shifted — softened, or pretended to. "Hello, Wren. Your god is smaller than I expected."

Wren stepped behind Ada's legs. Children always knew. They knew before adults did, before the smile arrived, some animal part of them clocking the difference between warmth and its imitation. Ada put her hand on his shoulder and felt him tremble once and then go very still, the way small creatures do when they've decided hiding is no longer an option.

"You're not taking him," she said.

"I'm not taking anyone." Corvane set both cups on the fishmonger's barrel, stepped back from them — a gesture of good faith, or theater; with him it was impossible to tell — and folded his hands. "I'm going to talk to you, Ada Yarrow. Just talk. And then I'm going to leave, and you can decide what to do with everything I've said. That's the whole of my offer. Ten minutes and my departure."

"People have died for less than ten minutes of your talking."

"Yes," he said simply. "They have. I've done it myself. Which is why you should find it interesting that I haven't done it to you."

* * *

She took the cider. She hated herself for it, a little. But her hands wanted something to hold, and refusing would have made the conversation a battle, and she needed it to be a conversation, because conversations were where she was strongest. She couldn't fight a memory-eater. She couldn't outrun one — she'd seen Corvane move, back on the ridge above Harrow Fen, when he'd crossed forty yards of open ground in the time it took Sera to finish a prayer. But she could talk. She could read a face the way other people read weather, because she'd spent her whole life lifting things off faces, and she knew where the cracks were.

So they walked. Away from the market, along the river path, Wren glued to her side until she murmured, "Go find Sera. Run, don't walk, and don't stop to buy anything," and he went, after a look at her that promised he'd be back with reinforcements or die trying.

Then it was just her and Corvane and the brown water sliding past, and the willows doing their slow green nodding, and Ada waiting.

"You want to know why I haven't taken you," Corvane said.

"I assume it's a trap."

"It's a question." He walked with his hands clasped behind his back, an old man's walk, though he wasn't old. Not yet. "The Crown wants the sigil pieces. The Order wants them more. Either of us could take you — you know that. We've been gentle so far, and gentleness is expensive, and my superiors have begun asking why I'm spending so much of it." He glanced at her sideways. "Do you know what they call you? In the reports?"

"No."

"*The Thief Who Remembers.* It has a ring to it. There's a wager pool on what you actually are. Some think you're a vessel. Some think you're the seal's last anchor, walking around in a stolen coat. One very imaginative junior eater thinks you're Vael itself, wearing a girl." He smiled faintly. "I know better. I've watched you work. You're not a country or an anchor. You're hungry. There's a difference, and it matters."

Ada kept her eyes on the river. "You didn't walk me out here to flatter me."

"No." He stopped walking. So did she. The willows hushed around them, and somewhere upstream a cart rattled over a bridge, ordinary as bread, and Ada thought: *this is how it always happens. In the middle of an ordinary moment. Nobody ever comes for you during thunder.* "I walked you out here because I know what's missing from you. The oldest piece. The first one they took."

The river went on sliding. Ada discovered she had stopped breathing and made herself start again, one careful breath at a time, because Corvane was watching her the way she watched marks — reading the face, reading the cracks.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Of course you don't. That's rather the point." He turned to face her fully, and there was nothing cruel in his expression, which was the cruelest thing about it. "You think you were born hollow, Ada. You think the hunger came first and the theft came second, and that underneath all the borrowed memories there's just a hole where a person should be. I've read your file. I've read the intake record from the Hollow, twenty-two years ago. A child brought to the valley's edge by a woman who set her down in the grass and walked away and never came back." He paused. "That's what you believe happened. Isn't it?"

"It's what happened."

"It's what you *remember*," Corvane said gently. "There's a difference. And you, of all people, should know better than anyone alive how large that difference can be."

The willows nodded. The cart rattled away over the bridge. Ada stood very still and felt the ground tilt under her in a way that had nothing to do with the earth.

"What are you saying?" Her voice came out flat. She was proud of that. Flat was safe. Flat was a door she could close.

"I'm saying the Order keeps archives. Every memory harvested in two hundred years, catalogued and stored, because a memory eaten is a memory owned, and the Crown does not throw away property." He reached into his coat — she flinched, she couldn't help it, and he slowed his hands deliberately, showing her, drawing out not a weapon but a slim black ledger bound in gray leather, stamped with the sigil of the memory-eaters. "I'm saying that somewhere in that archive is a single vial. Glass, no bigger than my thumb. Inside it, the first hour of Ada Yarrow's life that anyone bothered to keep. And inside *that*—"

"Don't." 

"—is your mother's face," he finished anyway, softly, "and what she was doing when she left you, and why. The truth of it. Not the story the hole in you has grown around like scar tissue. The actual truth. Proof, Ada. One way or the other, proof."

The river made its small endless noise. Ada became aware that her hand had drifted to her chest, to the place below her collarbone where the stolen memories sat in their careful stacks, warm as coals, and she snatched the hand back down as if it had betrayed her. Because it had. Because some traitorous part of her had already opened the vial. Had already drunk.

"You're lying," she said.

"Probably," Corvane agreed. "I might be. I've lied to marks before; it works. But consider the shape of the lie. If I wanted to frighten you, I'd threaten the boy. If I wanted to break you, I'd threaten Sera — she's already cracked, you've seen it. Instead I'm offering you the one thing in the world you cannot steal for yourself." He spread his empty hands. "You can lift a wedding from a merchant. You can lift a first kiss, a funeral, a name-day, a whole childhood if you're greedy. But you cannot steal a memory you don't know is missing. The hole doesn't show itself. It never does. Only someone outside can see the shape of what's gone — and only someone holding it can give it back."

"And the price."

"The pieces." No hesitation. He'd rehearsed this; she could hear the rehearsal in it, the smooth worn places where a sentence had been said to a mirror until it shone. "Every fragment of the sigil you carry. Give them to the Order, and I give you the vial, and you drink, and you know. Whatever the truth is — that she abandoned you, or that she didn't, or something stranger than either — you'll finally stand on solid ground. No more hunting. No more running. The Order lets you go; I'll see to it personally. You could go back to the Hollow. Or anywhere. You could be a person instead of a parcel everyone's chasing."

"And Vael?" Ada asked. "Everyone inside it? The door?"

Corvane was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had changed — lower, and for the first time less certain, as though she'd pressed on a bruise he'd forgotten he had.

"Vael is a wound the country made for itself," he said. "A hundred years of holy wars, sealed behind a door, and the world healed over it like flesh over a splinter. The Crown's position is that the healing should be allowed to finish." He met her eyes. "My position is that wounds don't finish healing while something inside them is still alive and screaming. But my position is not the one that pays my wages."

"So you do know they're alive in there."

"I know," Corvane said, "that the erasure is spreading, and that it spreads faster the closer you get to the mountain, and that whatever you and the fallen priestess and the child and his shrinking god are trying to do — it is racing against something enormous and patient and very, very good at forgetting things." He closed the ledger and slid it back into his coat. "I'm offering you a way to stop being the rope in a tug-of-war. Take it or don't. But decide soon, Ada. The road ahead of you is going to start eating itself, and when it does, offers like mine stop being available."

* * *

She didn't answer him. She watched him bow — actually bow, a small ironic dip of the head, like a dance partner taking his leave — and walk away along the river path with his soft coat and his tired eyes, until the willows took him, and then she stood there for a long time doing absolutely nothing, which was the most dangerous thing she'd done in weeks.

Because here was the thing. Here was the rotten, wormy heart of the thing.

She believed him.

Not entirely — she was no fool, and the offer smelled of hook and line and worm. But some load-bearing beam of it rang true, and she knew true from false better than almost anyone alive; it was her trade. The Order did keep archives; she'd felt the truth of that in the hunters' hunger, the way they savored rather than merely consumed. And the hole — oh, the hole. She had lived with it her whole life without knowing it was a hole. She'd thought the hollowness was her, was Ada, was the natural shape of a girl raised in a valley where love couldn't follow. She'd built herself on top of it the way a house builds itself on a cellar it never goes down into.

And now Corvane had gone down into the cellar with a lamp and come back up saying: *there's a room down there. There's a door down there. And I have the key.*

Her mother's face.

She tried to summon it and got, as always, the smear — the place where a face should be, smooth as the hunters' masks. Twenty-two years of that smear. She had stolen hundreds of mothers out of other people's heads just to know what the word meant. She had worn a baker's memory of his mother's bread and a soldier's memory of his mother's lullaby and a dying woman's memory of her mother's hands, and every time, for an hour or a day, she had known what it was to be loved from the beginning of things, and every time it had drained out of her like water through cupped fingers, and she had told herself that was the price of the gift, that was just how borrowing worked.

But what if it wasn't? What if it drained out because there was nothing in her to hold it — no keystone, no first stone, no mother's face at the bottom of the well for everything else to rest against?

What if she gave Corvane the pieces and drank the vial and found out her mother had set her down in the grass on purpose, had walked away on purpose, had chosen the Hollow the way you choose a grave?

Even that, Ada thought, and was ashamed of the thought even as it bloomed, warm and terrible. Even that would be something. Even abandonment was a shape. Even a bad answer was an answer, and God — or Ashen, or whoever was listening — anything was better than the smear. Anything was better than standing in a market at thirty-one years old not knowing if the woman whose face she'd lost had loved her or used her or simply forgotten her, the way the roads forgot themselves, the way the world was forgetting Vael.

She could give up the sigil. People would die — maybe a country full of people — but countries died all the time, didn't they? History was mostly a list of doors closing. And she was one woman, tired, hunted, out of her depth, holding pieces of something she barely understood, and here was a man offering her the only thing she had ever truly wanted, gift-wrapped in gray leather, and all it cost was everyone else.

"All it costs," she said aloud to the river, "is everyone else."

The river didn't answer. Rivers never did. That was why she liked them.

But something else answered — or almost did. Far off, downstream, past the bridge and the market noise, a sound like a hum. Small. Faint. A candleflame-in-a-draft hum, the sound Wren said Ashen made when he was too diminished to speak. It rose once, hung in the air like a struck bell that couldn't quite finish ringing—

—and stopped.

And in the silence after it, Ada felt it: a coldness moving through the willows, subtle as a change in light. The same wrongness she'd felt on the roads. The erasure, breathing nearby. Somewhere close, something was being forgotten, and the forgetting had a direction, and the direction was toward them.

Ada turned and ran for the market, her decision unmade and burning in her chest like a coal she'd swallowed — and found the square in an uproar, stalls overturned, and Wren standing in the middle of it all, white-faced, pointing at the eastern road.

"Sera," he gasped. "Ada — they took Sera."
