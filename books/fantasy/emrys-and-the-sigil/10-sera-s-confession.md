## 10. Sera's Confession

Sera Ilune had spent thirty years perfecting the art of not thinking about it.

She was very good at it now — better than she had ever been at prayer, better than she had ever been at anything the Order had taught her. Not-thinking was a discipline like any other. You rose before dawn and you did not think about it while you braided your hair. You walked through the hills and you did not think about it while you counted your steps. You sat by a fire with three unlikely companions — a memory-thief, an orphan boy, and a god the size of a teacup — and you did not think about it while you pretended to sleep.

But tonight the fire was low, Wren was actually asleep for once, curled against Ashen's little stone form with his mouth open in a way that would embarrass him terribly if anyone mentioned it, and Ada was awake.

Ada was always awake at this hour. Sera had noticed it on the third night of their traveling together: the girl lay still as a held breath until well past midnight, eyes open, watching the dark the way other people watched doors.

"You're doing it wrong," Ada said quietly, without turning her head.

"Doing what?"

"Sleeping. You're doing it wrong."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're staring at the stars like they owe you money."

Sera huffed a laugh despite herself. "That's a strange way to put it."

"That's what my mother used to say about people who couldn't sleep." Ada rolled onto her side, facing the fire. The light caught the edge of her face, all sharp angles and wariness. "People who can't sleep are usually hiding from something. She said that too. She said a lot of things." A pause. "I only know that because I stole it from someone. I don't remember if she was mine."

And there it was. The opening Sera had been dreading for days, offered up casually, like a hand of cards laid face-up on the table.

The girl didn't know she was asking. That was the worst part. Ada Yarrow asked questions the way other people bled — without meaning to, without noticing, leaving little marks everywhere she'd been.

"Sera," Ada said. "Why do the hunters always find us first? Before we hear them, before Ashen feels them. They come straight for *you*. Every time."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl called once and thought better of it.

"They track me," Sera said.

"Why?"

Because I helped build the cage, she thought. Because I wrote half the words. Because when the seal broke and the gods came back, everything I locked away came looking for me first, the way water finds the crack you made yourself.

What she said was: "Pass me that flask, and I'll tell you."

* * *

It began, as most disasters do, with good intentions and a committee.

"The Order of the Quiet Flame wasn't always disgraced," Sera said. "In fact, we were rather beloved, which should have been the first warning. Nobody loves you properly unless they need something from you. We were the ones who kept the peace between the gods' churches. Do you know what the world was like before the Hollow? Before the seal?"

"Old Brammel said there were wars."

"Wars." Sera laughed, and it came out bitter, and she hated that it came out bitter. "Wars is such a tidy word. What there were, child, were arguments between gods, fought by people who would never meet those gods but would die for them anyway. The Church of the Nine Rivers would burn a village because the god of the Nine Rivers had quarreled with the god of the Hearth, and the villagers on both sides had never done anything worse than bake bread. Whole harvests burned over theological disputes no living person understood. And every priest swore their god wanted it. And here is the terrible thing—" she leaned forward, and the firelight made her shadow enormous against the hillside, "—they were mostly right. The gods *did* want it. Gods are like children, Ada. Enormous, immortal, adored children. They don't understand that other people are real. They feel a slight and they want it answered, and they have armies of the faithful to answer it for them."

Wren stirred in his sleep and mumbled something that sounded like "*don't eat the blue ones*," and settled again.

"So the Order asked a question nobody else dared ask," Sera went on. "Not *which god is right*. But — what if none of them were allowed to answer prayers at all? What if we could make the whole world quiet? No answered prayers, no miracles, no divine grudges. Just people, working things out the slow human way. We thought — I thought — that without gods whispering in their ears, people would finally be kind to each other."

"And were they?"

"No," Sera said simply. "They were just people. Which is to say: cruel in new ways, and inventive about it. But fewer burnings. Fewer holy wars. We told ourselves it was worth it."

Ada's eyes had gone narrow and bright. "You keep saying *we*. And *I thought*. Sera. How old are you?"

"Rude question."

"How old?"

"Old enough," Sera said, "to have been one of the nine hands that wrote the Hollow Seal."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You *wrote* it," Ada whispered. "The valley. My home. The thing that kept magic out, that kept me—" She stopped. Sera watched her do the arithmetic, watched her arrive at the sum. "You built the prison. And then you lived next door to it for thirty years."

"I lived inside its shadow, yes."

"Why?" Ada's voice cracked upward. "Why would you—"

"Because someone had to watch it," Sera said. "And because I couldn't bear to be far from the thing I'd done. There's a word for that. Penitence, the Order called it. Guilt, everyone else calls it. I called it duty for thirty years because duty sounds better at dinner parties."

* * *

Here is what Sera had never told anyone, in thirty years of not-telling:

The seal had required a price. All great workings do; that was the first law of the craft, older than any order or church. The Hollow Seal needed to be anchored — not to a place, but to a person. A living soul, freely given, who would hold the shape of the quiet in their bones for as long as they lived.

Nine hands wrote the seal. One heart anchored it.

Her name had been Maren. Sera's teacher. Sera's friend. The woman who had taught her that mercy was a discipline and not a mood. When the vote came down among the nine — and oh, they had voted, they had been so civilized about it, so full of solemn nods and shared glances — Maren had raised her own hand before anyone could speak.

"It should be me," she'd said. "I'm oldest. I'm strongest. And I trust myself to hold it without resentment, which is more than I can say for the rest of you."

And Sera, twenty-six years old and certain of her own righteousness, had *agreed*. Had bowed her head. Had spoken the binding words alongside her eight colleagues while her teacher knelt in the center of the circle and became, slowly, over the course of one long night, the valley.

Maren became the Hollow. Her patience became the stillness that kept spells from catching. Her love became the fence at the border, gentle and absolute. Her forgiveness became the reason nothing in the valley ever quite burned, even lightning.

And her loneliness — because even saints are lonely — became the quality of the air there. That particular hush. That feeling every traveler described on entering: like being the last person awake in a house where everyone else is sleeping.

"She's why the valley felt safe," Sera said aloud, and her voice surprised her by being steady. "Everyone in the Hollow has been living inside my teacher's kindness for three generations. You grew up in her arms, Ada, and never knew her name."

Ada had both hands pressed over her mouth. Above her fingers, her eyes were wet and furious.

"You killed her," she said through them.

"Yes."

"For peace."

"For peace," Sera agreed. "Which is the same sentence with better grammar."

* * *

The fire had burned down to embers. Sera fed it a stick and watched the sparks climb.

"There's more," she said. "The part that matters to you. To all of us."

"The hunters."

"The hunters." She nodded. "When we wrote the seal, we knew the gods would hate it. We knew some of the faithful would hate it. So we built a safeguard into the working — a way to find the seal-writers if anything ever went wrong. A thread tied from the anchor to each of our hearts. If the seal cracked, the thread would pull, and the Crown's wardens — we created the wardens too, don't look so surprised, the Order and the Crown were very close in those days — the wardens would know exactly where to find whoever was nearest the break."

Ada had gone very still. "The memory-eaters."

"Are the wardens' grandchildren. Corvane and his kind. And the thread—" Sera touched her own sternum, lightly, the way you touch an old scar. "It's still in me. It doesn't hurt. It *sings*, very faintly, whenever they come near. That's how they find us. I'm a bell that rings for them. I've been ringing since the night the seal broke and the candles relit in your market."

"Can it be cut?"

"By the one who tied it. And she's been a valley for eighty years." Sera smiled, and meant it to be wry, and suspected it came out sad. "So no. Where I go, they can follow. Which means every camp we make, every road we take, every child we protect—" she glanced at Wren, sleeping, "—I am the leak in the boat."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lost its anger, gained something sharper and more careful.

"You could leave. Walk away tonight. They'd follow you and we'd get clear."

"Yes."

"You're not going to."

"No."

"Why not?"

And here was the thing Sera had rehearsed for thirty years and found, now, that she didn't need the rehearsal at all. The words were just *there*, worn smooth by decades of handling in the dark.

"Because shame is a debt," she said, "and I have been paying it in installments of cowardice my whole life, and I am tired of small payments. You're going to rebuild the sigil, Ada Yarrow. You're going to open a door that I helped nail shut, and when you do, I intend to be standing there — not hiding behind you, not two hills away, not conveniently dead of old age. Standing *there*. Letting the gods see my face. Letting Maren's ghost see it too, if ghosts work that way, which I sincerely hope they don't because she'll box my ears."

Ada stared at her. Then, slowly, the corner of her mouth moved. "Box your ears."

"She was very strong. Farm family. She could lift a barrel with one arm."

"You're smiling."

"I'm terrified," Sera said, "and smiling. They're not mutually exclusive. That's another thing Maren taught me. Courage isn't the absence of fear, it's fear that's decided to be useful."

* * *

She slept, after that. Actually slept — deep and dreamless for the first time in weeks — and woke near dawn to the smell of something burning that was not the fire.

Ada stood over a small tin pan balanced on stones, wearing an expression of profound concentration and cooking what appeared to be a griddle cake that had recently survived a house fire.

"It's food," Ada announced, defensively, before Sera could say anything.

"It's certainly something."

"Brammel taught me. He said hunger is the best seasoning."

"Brammel," Sera said, accepting a blackened disc and biting into it, "was a liar and a poet. This tastes like a roof."

"Eat your roof."

From the bedroll, Wren sat bolt upright, hair sticking out in six directions, nose working. "Is that *cake*?"

"It's a roof."

"I want roof!" He scrambled over, and Ashen tumbled from his arms, caught itself on a stone, and regarded the proceedings with the weary dignity of a god reduced to breakfast theater.

"*The small one hungers,*" Ashen intoned. "*As does the tall one. As does the thief.*"

"I'm not hungry," Ada said automatically, then, quieter, almost to herself: "I don't actually know if I'm hungry. I never learned to tell."

Something in Sera's chest turned over. She looked at this ragged little company — the girl who didn't know her own body's language, the boy who talked to stones, the god who fit in a pocket, and herself, a bell that rang for hunters — and felt the strangest sensation, unfamiliar and warm, which took her a moment to identify because she hadn't felt it since before the sealing.

Hope. Messy, unearned, entirely inconvenient hope.

"Well," she said briskly, dividing the last of the roof-cake four ways, "eat anyway. Today we cross the Ferrun flats, and I want distance between us and the last warden's trail. The thread's been quiet all night, but it hums when I'm idle, so we'll keep moving and keep me busy. Ada — you'll walk with me. I want to teach you the old signs, the ones the Order used to mark safe roads. If I'm going to be the leak in the boat, the least I can do is also be the map."

Ada took her share of cake, examined it, and ate it in three defiant bites. "Fine. But if you start preaching, I'm feeding the rest to Wren."

"He'd love that," Wren said, mouth full.

Ashen's stone surface flickered — the closest thing the little god had to laughter — and then, abruptly, went still. Utterly still, in a way that made Sera's spine prickle before the god even spoke.

"*The bell,*" Ashen said softly. "*Ring for me, keeper-of-words. Tell me what you hear.*"

Sera closed her eyes. Reached inward, down the old thread, into the faint singing that had been her curse and compass for weeks.

It wasn't humming anymore.

It was tolling. Fast, urgent, close — closer than it had ever been, closer than it should be able to get without her hearing boots on gravel —

And it wasn't coming from one direction.

It was coming from *all* of them.

"Oh," Sera breathed, opening her eyes. "Oh, they've learned. They're not following the thread anymore. They're *herding along it*. They've spread out in a ring, and they're closing."

Ada was already on her feet, pulling Wren up by the collar. "How long?"

"If they're walking? An hour. If they're riding—"

A horn sounded in the eastern hills. Long, low, patient. Then another, west. Then a third, south, where there was supposed to be nothing but flatland and fog.

Ashen flared, a spark of gold in the grey dawn. "*There is a place,*" the god said quickly, urgently, "*a town that is not on any road, that does not wish to be found, that hides the ones the Crown erased. The thief's feet know the way even if her head does not. Run, children. Run toward the forgetting.*"

Ada grabbed Sera's sleeve. "Did you understand any of that?"

"Every word." Sera snatched up her pack, her heart hammering, and found — to her genuine astonishment — that she was grinning. Thirty years of guilt, and it turned out all that shame had only ever been fuel waiting for a match.

"Then run," she said, "and try to keep up with an old woman."
