## 11. The Market of Unclaimed Names

The road forgot them for three days.

That was how Ada had come to think of it — not that they were lost, but that the road itself kept misplacing them, doubling back through the same birch stand twice, offering a bridge that hadn't been there an hour before and taking away one that had. Wren thought it was a game. Sera thought it was a symptom. Ashen, speaking through Wren in that voice like embers settling, thought it was *interesting*, which from a god was roughly equivalent to a scholar saying *hmm*.

On the third evening, the fog parted around a bend in the hills, and there it was: a town that should not have existed, tucked into a fold of land like a coin slipped into a seam.

Lanterns. Dozens of them, strung on wires between crooked houses with mismatched shutters. The smell of frying onions and woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath — spiced wine, maybe, or just the particular sweetness of a place where people were glad to be home. A woman was arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the freshness of a trout that was, by any reasonable measure, still flapping.

"Is it real?" Wren whispered. He'd been quiet all day, favoring the ankle he'd turned on the looping road, and now his eyes were enormous. "Ada. Is it *real* real?"

Ada didn't answer right away. She'd spent her whole life in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and she knew what unreal places felt like — the Hollow's hush, its held breath. This was the opposite. This place hummed. It hummed like a struck bell that no one could hear but her.

"It's real," said Sera quietly. Her hand had gone to the broken sigil at her throat — the defrocked priestess's habit, touching the wound where her faith used to be. "I've heard rumors. I never believed—" She stopped. Started again. "They call it the Market of Unclaimed Names."

"Unclaimed," Ada repeated.

"People the Crown erased. Struck from the census, from the rolls, from every ledger and record. Not killed — that would be wasteful, and besides, dead people leave graves, and graves leave questions. Just... removed. Their names unrecorded, their births unwitnessed, their taxes unowed." Sera's mouth twisted. "Legally speaking, none of these people exist."

A boy selling roasted chestnuts looked up at them from his brazier, took in their mud-caked boots and Wren's bandaged ankle, and jerked his chin toward a narrow lane. "Tallow's expecting you," he said. "She's always expecting somebody. Down past the cooper's, the blue door with the green paint over it."

"How did he know we were coming?" Ada murmured as they followed the lane.

"Nobody told him anything," Sera said. "That's rather the point of this place. Word travels here the way it used to travel everywhere — mouth to mouth, hand to hand, unrecorded." A pause. "The Crown can't erase what it never wrote down."

* * *

Tallow turned out to be a broad-shouldered woman in her sixties with flour to her elbows and laugh lines like river deltas, who greeted them at the blue-and-green door with the air of someone greeting children she'd been expecting since Tuesday.

"You'll be the memory-thief," she said to Ada, not unkindly. "The priestess. And the small god's boy." She looked at Wren. "You'll be wanting supper and a chair with a cushion, in that order."

"How do you know who we are?" Ada asked. Every instinct she owned was bristling. In the Hollow, being known meant being robbed; being known too well meant being caught.

Tallow wiped her hands on her apron and smiled. "Girl, everyone in this town knows exactly who they are. That's the one thing the Crown couldn't take, though gods know it tried. We know each other the old way. By telling." She stepped back from the door. "Come in before the onions burn. Nobody forgives burnt onions here. It's practically law."

Inside was warm and low-ceilinged and full — full of people, is what Ada meant, though it was also full of bread and copper pots and a cat with a torn ear who regarded Wren with immediate, fateful approval. There must have been twenty souls crammed into the long kitchen, eating off mismatched plates, talking over each other, laughing. A man with a miller's forearms was teaching three children a song with a chorus that involved slapping the table. An old woman in the corner was mending a coat and correcting everyone's grammar simultaneously.

Ada stood in the doorway with her back against the frame and felt something in her chest do a thing she didn't have a name for.

It wasn't hunger. She knew hunger intimately; they were old dance partners. This was different. This was the feeling she got for one stolen hour when she wore a merchant's memory of his daughter's wedding — the feeling of being *inside* something, of warmth that included you without your having to steal it.

Except she hadn't stolen this. She'd walked in the door.

"Sit," Tallow commanded, pressing her into a chair. "You look like a fence post that's been asked to hold up a barn. Eat first, talk after."

The stew was lamb and barley and more rosemary than any reasonable cook would sanction, and Ada ate two bowls of it while Wren charmed the entire table with an account of the road that forgot itself ("and then the bridge was GONE, and Ashen said *hmph*, which means he doesn't know either") and Sera sat very straight and ate slowly, like a woman relearning how to eat among people.

It was only afterward, when the bowls were cleared and the wine came out and the children were shooed toward bed with the practiced inefficiency of adults who enjoyed being disobeyed, that Tallow sat down across from Ada and folded her hands.

"Now," she said. "Tell me what's chasing you. And don't say nothing. Nothing doesn't chase people into the hills looking like you look."

Ada told her. Most of it. The Hollow, the sigil pieces, the memory-eaters with their blank faces, Corvane. She left out the keystone — the hole in herself she was only beginning to understand was a hole — because some things weren't ready to be said aloud, even to a woman with flour on her elbows and kindness in her voice.

Tallow listened without interrupting, which Ada suspected was a discipline rather than a temperament. When Ada finished, the old woman was quiet a moment.

"Memory-eaters," she said at last. "Crown-trained. They harvest what isn't written down, because written things can be forged but a memory is proof." She tapped her own temple. "That's why we're unclaimed, dear. You can't eat what a whole town remembers together. They'd have to swallow us all at once, and even the Crown chokes on that much."

"So you hide."

"We *gather*," Tallow corrected, with some spirit. "Hiding is for rabbits. We gather. We keep each other. Every name struck from their ledgers gets spoken here, out loud, at supper, until it's worn smooth as a river stone. Marta Voss. Tam Ellery. Old Fenwick, may he rest, though nobody can prove he was ever born." She gestured around the kitchen, at the milling, laughing, argumentative crowd. "You can't erase a person who is loved out loud every day. Takes too long. Costs too much. The Crown is many things, girl, but it is not patient."

Ada looked around the room again — really looked, this time. At the miller teaching the song. At the grammar-mending grandmother. At the chestnut boy, home now, feet up, stealing plums.

And there it was again, that unnamed thing in her chest, except now it had edges, and the edges hurt, because she understood what it was.

Belonging. She was looking at belonging, served up in bowls, passed around with bread.

"I stole memories my whole life," she heard herself say. "To feel like this. One hour at a time. And you all just—"

"Just live here," Tallow said. "Yes. Terribly unfair, isn't it." But her eyes were kind. "You're welcome to stay the night, thief. Longer, if you like. We don't ask what people were. Only what they're becoming."

* * *

What Ada became, that night, was a dancer.

It happened the way such things happen: someone brought out a fiddle, and someone else objected that the last time there'd been a fiddle, the cooper had danced into his own rain barrel, and then the fiddle started anyway, and the objection dissolved into laughter, and hands pulled her up before she could construct an excuse.

She didn't know the steps. It didn't matter. The steps were less important than the collision-avoidance, and she was, as it happened, excellent at reading people — a lifetime of lifting memories had taught her to see the turn of a shoulder before the turn happened. She spun the grammar-grandmother gently, dodged the cooper with professional respect, and laughed — actually laughed, out loud, the sound surprising her like a sneeze.

Wren danced with the torn-eared cat, which permitted it with the weary dignity of a god's chosen companion. Sera did not dance. Sera stood at the edge with her cup and watched, and once, when the fiddle hit a high sweet note, Ada saw her press her lips together and blink hard and pretend to be interested in the ceiling beams.

Later, near midnight, the fire burned low and the town did what towns of forgotten people apparently did: they spoke names.

Tallow stood, and the room settled, and one by one people rose and said names into the warm dark. Some were their own. Some belonged to others — the lost, the scattered, the ones who hadn't made it to the Market. Each name was spoken plainly, without ceremony, and after each one the room would murmur it back, soft as rain.

"*Marta Voss.*"

"*Marta Voss,*" the room returned.

"*Tam Ellery.*"

"*Tam Ellery.*"

When it seemed finished, Wren tugged Ada's sleeve. His face was solemn in the firelight, and Ashen was close behind his eyes — she could tell by the way the shadows leaned toward him.

"The small god has a name to say," Wren announced, in the carrying voice children use when they have been given permission to be important.

The room went still. Even the cat looked up.

Wren closed his eyes. When he spoke, the words came out doubled, a child's voice braided with something older and quieter, like a bell rung under water.

"*Vael.*"

Just that. One word. The name of a country that was not on any map, erased so thoroughly that most scholars believed it had never been.

And the room — the room of unrecorded, legally nonexistent, officially impossible people — murmured it back.

"*Vael.*"

Ada's throat closed. She looked at Sera and saw the priestess had gone white, her hand white-knuckled on her broken sigil, tears running freely down a face that wasn't bothering to hide them.

Nobody here needed proof. Nobody here needed a ledger. They simply said the name, and it was true, and that was the whole of the law.

Ada found herself standing. She hadn't decided to. Her legs had opinions of their own tonight.

"My mother," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "I don't remember her name. Someone took it, or I gave it away, or — I don't know. But I'm saying it anyway. Whatever it is. Whoever she is." She swallowed. "*My mother.*"

And the whole warm room, every soul in it, murmured back into the firelit dark:

"*Your mother.*"

Ada sat down. Tallow pressed another cup of wine into her hands without comment, and if the old woman's eyes were wet too, well, the fire smoked sometimes. Everyone knew that.

For one night — she would remember this later, hoard it, guard it like the last coal of a fire — for one night, Ada Yarrow belonged somewhere.

* * *

She woke to silence of the wrong kind.

Not the Hollow's held-breath hush. A listening silence, the kind a house makes when everyone in it has stopped moving at once. Ada was on her feet before her mind finished waking, hand already at the knife in her boot.

Downstairs, the kitchen was full of people standing very still. Tallow stood at the window, one shutter eased open a finger's width. Dawn was a gray suggestion beyond it.

"Horses," Tallow said softly. "Six. Coming up the valley road, slow. They stopped at the chestnut boy's corner." Her jaw tightened. "He'll have told them nothing. He tells everyone nothing. It's his best quality."

Sera was already armed — a short blade and a longer one, and a competence in her stance that Ada kept forgetting and kept re-learning. "Memory-eaters?"

"Worse." Tallow stepped back from the window. "Riders wear livery. That's census men — clerks with swords. Where the clerks go, the eaters follow. They've found the road in." She turned, and her face was terrible and calm. "How long until your trail leads them here, priestess? Be honest. Your guilt rings like a bell to them. I've seen it pull hunters across counties."

Sera's face went gray. "Two days. Maybe less."

"Then we have less." Tallow clapped her hands once, softly, and the room moved — not panicked, but purposeful, the way water moves when a dam opens. People began gathering children, bundling ledgers — actual paper records, hidden ones, the town's counter-history — dousing ovens, calming the cat, who declined to be calmed.

Ada grabbed Tallow's arm. "There has to be another way out. If they follow the road—"

"They'll follow *us*," Tallow said. "You especially, girl. You're carrying half a god's seal in your head and the Crown knows it. Which means every soul in this town is safer the moment you're gone — and every soul in this town will fight you on leaving, because that's the kind of fools we are." She patted Ada's hand where it gripped her sleeve. "But there's one thing you could do. One thing that would blind them for good."

"What thing?"

Tallow's eyes glinted, and for a moment she looked less like a baker and more like something older — a woman who had been erased and had decided, on reflection, to find it funny.

"There's a census wagon with those riders," she said. "There always is. The master ledger. Every name the Crown ever wrote down — including, girl, yours. Including the warrant that says *the thief who remembers*. Burn that book, and the hunt doesn't just lose your trail." Her smile showed teeth. "It loses its *reason*."

Outside, the first horse crested the ridge, and a horn sounded — one long note, flat and official, the most frightening sound Ada had ever heard.

"And if I fail?" Ada asked.

Tallow was already turning to rally her people, but she answered over her shoulder, light as passing the salt:

"Then we all get to find out together whether love out loud beats ink. I've got coin on love. Now *run*, dear — the cooper knows the back ways, and he owes me for the rain barrel."
