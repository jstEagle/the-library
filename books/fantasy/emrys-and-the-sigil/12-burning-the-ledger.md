## 12. Burning the Ledger

The ledger was bigger than Ada had imagined.

She had pictured a book — something she could tuck under her arm and run with, the way she'd once run with a merchant's memory of his daughter's wedding tucked behind her eyes. But the Crown's census of the Hollow provinces was not a book. It was a chest. An iron-banded, lock-plated, corner-studded chest that took two grown men to carry, and it sat in the surveyor's wagon like a fat black beetle pretending to be furniture.

"It's all in there," Maren said. She was the oldest resident of the town-with-no-name — the market of unclaimed names, Sera called it, though the people who lived there mostly just called it *here* — and she had a voice like gravel steeped in honey. "Every name they ever wrote down. Every birth, every debt, every tax. You burn that, and as far as the Crown is concerned, none of us were ever born at all."

"Isn't that what they already think?" Wren asked. He was sitting on an overturned crate, swinging his legs, one arm still bound from the fall he'd taken on the ridge road. Ashen sat on his shoulder in the shape of a small grey bird that was very obviously not a bird, preening feathers that occasionally flickered like embers.

"They think we're *unrecorded*," Maren said. "There's a difference. Unrecorded means forgotten. Forgotten means they might remember you someday, if it suits them. But if your name goes into the book and then comes out of the book—" she tapped the chest with one knuckle, and the sound rang oddly, like a bell struck underwater "—then you're not forgotten. You're *erased*. Deliberate. There are laws about erased people. Mostly the law is: don't be one."

Ada looked at the chest for a long time.

She had spent her whole life being nobody official. In the Hollow, nobody kept books. The valley didn't even keep *weather* properly — rain fell upward sometimes, and fog came when it pleased, and the roads forgot where they went overnight. That was the point of the Hollow. It was the place where the world's ink wouldn't dry.

But she had never been erased *on purpose*. There was a difference, and Maren was right, and the difference made her stomach hurt.

"You're thinking too loud," Wren said. "Your face does this thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you've already decided and you're waiting for someone to talk you out of it so you can pretend you tried."

"I do not have a—"

"She does," Sera said quietly, from the doorway of the surveyor's tent. She looked better than she had a week ago, after the memory-eater had chewed through thirty years of her order — hollow-eyed still, but the hollowness had filled in with something new, something with edges. Resolve, Ada supposed, if resolve could look like a woman who had lost everything and decided to be inconvenient about it. "She's been doing it since the hills. I've watched her argue herself into three rescues and a robbery."

"That was *one* rescue and *one* robbery."

"And the time with the ferryman?"

"That was a *negotiation*."

Sera almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a smile anyone in the town had seen from her, and several of the unclaimed names watching from doorways pretended very hard not to notice.

*

The plan, such as it was, had come together the way their plans always did: badly, quickly, and with Wren contributing ideas that were either brilliant or criminal depending on which god you asked.

The hunters had found them. Of course they had. Sera's guilt was a scent trail the Crown's hounds could follow across oceans, and Corvane — patient, terrible Corvane, who wanted Ada harvested rather than dead — had been circling the hidden town for two days now like a hawk that had learned manners. Scouts had seen his blank-faced soldiers at the tree line that morning. By nightfall, maybe tomorrow, they would come down into the valley, and they would find forty families who did not exist, and they would write those families *back* into existence in the worst possible way — as assets. As harvests. As entries in a ledger.

Unless the ledger stopped existing first.

"The wagon stays with the surveyor," Sera had said, laying it out on Maren's table with salt and dried beans for markers. "He'll bring it into the town square at dusk to take the evening count — he does it every settlement, every night, it's ritual for them. The chest comes out of the wagon. Ada opens it."

"With what? It's locked."

"With you." Sera had looked at her then with an expression Ada was learning to hate, the one that meant *you're going to have to be extraordinary again, I'm sorry*. "You open locks the way you open memories. Brammel told me. Anything built to hold a person's name inside it — you're the key, Ada. You've been picking at sealed things since you were six years old."

And now here she was, at dusk, standing in the square of a town that wasn't on any map, watching the surveyor's wagon roll in with its fat black beetle of a chest, and trying very hard not to think about how much her hands were shaking.

"You're thinking too loud again," Wren whispered beside her. He was supposed to be keeping watch at the well. He was instead standing directly next to her, because Ashen had declared — through him, in one of the god's riddling moods — that *the smallest flame guards the greatest fire*, which everyone had agreed probably meant Wren should stay close, or possibly that Wren should go to bed. With Ashen it was genuinely hard to say.

"Go stand at the well."

"I am at the well. This well. The spiritual well. Inside you."

"Wren."

"Fine." He shuffled off three steps, which put him still well within whispering distance, and settled for watching her with the enormous solemn eyes of a child who had already buried more than most adults.

The surveyor climbed down from the wagon. He was a grey man in a grey coat, the kind of Crown functionary whose face seemed designed to be forgotten even by people with no talent for forgetting, and he began the evening count the way they always did: unrolling a portable desk, lighting a lamp, calling names.

"Maren Ostwick."

"Here," said Maren, from the crowd, in a voice like honeyed gravel, giving nothing away.

"Tomas Ilbreth."

"Here."

"Petra, daughter of none."

A pause. A murmur ran through the gathered townsfolk. Petra, daughter of none — that was how the census recorded children born to the unclaimed, and everyone knew what happened to daughters-of-none when the Crown finished counting a town. They went on the special list. The list that Corvane's people read.

"Here," said Petra's mother, answering for her, and her voice cracked exactly one degree, and Ada saw the surveyor's pen pause over the page.

That was the moment. Ada moved.

She had stolen memories from travelers for fifteen years; she knew how to walk like someone who belonged nowhere and therefore everywhere. She crossed the square with a basket on her hip — borrowed, filled with turnips for verisimilitude — and reached the wagon at the exact moment the surveyor bent to relight his guttering lamp, and put her hand flat against the chest's lock.

It was cold. Colder than iron should be, cold like the inside of a locked room, and beneath her palm she felt it — the hum. Every name in that chest had been written with ceremony and stamped with wax and witnessed by clerks, and all that ceremony had soaked into the metal like dye into cloth. The lock wasn't holding a lid shut. It was holding forty thousand lives in place.

*You've been picking at sealed things since you were six years old.*

Ada closed her eyes. She found the seam the way she'd once found the seams in travelers' minds — not forcing, but *asking*. The lock was a held breath. She breathed with it. And then, gently, the way you slip a memory loose from its owner without them feeling the loss —

— the lock sighed open.

The lid came up. Lamplight fell into the chest, and Ada caught her breath, because it wasn't paper in there. Or it was paper, thousands upon thousands of pages, but every page *glowed* faintly, the way her stolen memories glowed when Sera looked at her sideways. Names. Lives. Weddings and debts and births and the small ordinary miracles of forty thousand people, pressed flat and filed away, and the Crown thought of it as *bookkeeping*.

"There she is," said a voice behind her.

Ada turned. The surveyor stood with his lamp raised, and his grey face had gone very carefully blank — the particular blankness she had learned to dread, the trained emptiness of the Crown's servants, men whose own memories had been thinned until only duty remained.

"Thief who remembers," he said. "Hold still. This will only take a moment, and then you'll be comfortable forever."

Behind him, at the edge of the square, torches were kindling. Not the townsfolk's torches. Soldiers. Blank faces, rank on rank, coming down out of the dark like the tide.

They were early.

*

What happened next happened fast, and afterward Ada could never quite get the order straight.

Sera shouted something in a language that made the air taste like incense, and the nearest four soldiers simply sat down, mid-stride, overcome by a grief that wasn't theirs — Sera pouring her guilt outward like oil, weaponizing the very thing that let the hunters track her. Maren's voice rose over the square, honeyed gravel turned to thunder, and the unclaimed names came out of their houses with everything they had, which was not much: pots, hoes, fury, desperation.

And Wren — Wren stood up on the rim of the well, cupped his hands around his mouth, and screamed, in the piercing register that only children and kettle-whistles achieve:

"*ASHEN! NOW!*"

The little grey bird on his shoulder unfolded.

Ada had seen Ashen angry before, or what passed for angry, which was mostly riddles delivered with unusual sharpness. She had not seen Ashen *vast*. For one heartbeat the god remembered being Vael's doorkeeper, and the square filled with a light like dawn deciding to happen early, and every soldier within thirty feet dropped their torches and covered their eyes and *wept* — wept for doors long closed, for homes they couldn't name, for a country that officially did not exist and yet lived somewhere in the marrow of everyone the Crown had ever erased.

It lasted a heartbeat. Then Ashen collapsed back into bird-shape, spent, and Wren caught him before he hit the ground, and the soldiers were scrambling up, and the surveyor was lunging for Ada, and there was no more time for anything except the one thing.

Ada reached into the chest.

She had planned to carry it out. Steal the ledger the way she stole everything, run into the hills, burn it later at leisure. But the soldiers were between her and the hills, and the chest weighed as much as a life, and there was a lamp right there on the surveyor's portable desk, guttering, patient.

Some things you cannot steal fast enough. Some things you have to burn where they lie.

She grabbed the lamp.

"No!" The surveyor's composure cracked at last, and underneath it was pure animal panic — a clerk watching his life's work die. "Do you know what you're — that's *forty thousand* — that's the *order of things*—"

"I know exactly what it is," Ada said. "I've been outside the order of things my whole life. It's drafty out here, but you get used to it."

She poured the lamp dry across the glowing pages.

For one horrible instant nothing happened — the oil spread, the pages drank it in, and Ada thought with distant hysteria that even the fire wouldn't take, that the Crown's ink was too stubborn to burn. Then the first page caught, and the flame found the names, and the names *wanted* to burn. She felt it through her gift like a chorus inhaling. Forty thousand held breaths, released at once.

The chest went up like a signal pyre.

Flames leapt twenty feet, gold and white, and the heat pushed the surveyor back and the soldiers back and everyone back, and in the heart of the fire Ada saw — or dreamed she saw, or remembered seeing, which for her amounted to the same thing — shapes rising with the smoke. Names lifting free. A wedding. A debt forgiven. A girl called Petra, daughter of no one, becoming daughter of everyone. All of it unwritten, unbound, going up into the dark sky over a town that wasn't on any map, and the whole square stood silent in the firelight and watched themselves become impossible.

"Run," Maren said softly. "All of you. While they're staring at the pretty lights. *Run.*"

*

They ran.

Out the far side of the valley, along a track that only existed because enough unclaimed feet had walked it, mothers carrying children, old men carrying older men, Wren carrying Ashen, who had gone boneless and dim and muttering riddles under his breath (*what is left when the page is ash but the hand that wrote it... ask me tomorrow...*). Sera brought up the rear, walking backward half the time, watching for pursuit that — miraculously, impossibly — did not come.

Because there was no pursuit. Ada realized it an hour up the trail, and stopped, and laughed until she cried, and Sera looked at her with alarm until she understood.

They can't follow us, Ada gasped. Don't you see? We're not *in the book anymore*. None of us. The hunters' orders — the warrants, the descriptions, the bounty notices — they were all written *from the ledger*. And the ledger is ash. As far as the Crown is concerned, there is no thief who remembers. There is no defrocked priestess. There is no town. There's nothing here to arrest.

Sera stared at her. Then, slowly, she started to laugh too — a rusty sound, a hinge unused for years — and around them the ragged column of the unclaimed caught it, laughter moving down the line like the fire had moved through the pages, and for a few minutes on a dark mountainside forty families who did not exist laughed at the empire that had tried to file them away.

"We're no one," Wren said happily. "We're *legally no one*. That's the best thing I've ever been."

Ada's laughter faded slowly, the way warmth fades from a stone after sundown. Because it was true, and truth has edges. She had burned the book, and the book had held her name too — the name the Crown had given her when the hunters first marked her, *the thief who remembers*, entry eleven thousand and something, province of the Hollow. Burned with the rest. And whatever else she was now, whoever she became, no clerk would ever write her down again. No record would ever prove she had existed. If she died on this mountain, there would be nothing to show she hadn't simply never been born.

Freedom, it turned out, smelled like woodsmoke and cost exactly everything.

Sera fell into step beside her. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've paid a price and you're deciding whether to tell anyone."

Ada watched the fire-glow fade behind them, below them, the last light of forty thousand names rising into the stars. "My mother's in that book somewhere," she said quietly. "Whoever she was. Whatever she wrote when she left me at the Hollow's edge. It burned tonight too. Whatever proof there ever was that she existed — that she *chose* anything — it's ash now."

Sera was quiet for a while. The mountain wind moved through the column of the unclaimed, and somewhere ahead Wren was asking Maren whether legally-nonexistent people still had to eat their vegetables, and Maren was explaining that vegetables, unlike the Crown, required no paperwork.

"Then there's only one proof left," Sera said at last. "You. You're the record now, Ada. Everything she was, everything she did — it survives exactly as long as you remember it, and not one moment longer." She glanced sideways. "Which is why it matters what you choose to carry."

Ahead, the road bent around a shoulder of rock, and beyond the rock the land changed — Ada felt it before she saw it, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The air grew thick and strange, and the stars overhead flickered like candles in a draft, and Ashen lifted his head from Wren's shoulder and said, clearly, in a voice that was not a bird's:

"*The road forgets itself from here. Hold hands. Hold names. And thief —*"

The god's ember-eyes fixed on her.

"*— the mountain remembers you, even if the Crown doesn't. It has been waiting a very long time to see what you'll give it.*"
