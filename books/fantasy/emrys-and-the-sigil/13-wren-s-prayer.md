## 13. Wren's Prayer

Wren had decided, somewhere around the third hour of hiding in the ditch, that being a hero was mostly mud.

Not the exciting kind of mud either — not the kind you got in stories, where a brave boy squelches dramatically through a swamp to rescue somebody. This was the cold kind. The kind that got into your boots and then into your socks and then into the space between your toes where it lived like a tenant who didn't pay rent.

"Ada," he whispered up at the edge of the ditch, "I don't think this is a very good plan."

"It was *your* plan," Ada hissed back from behind the deadfall above him. She had her arm out, ready to grab him if the riders came around the bend of the road again. Her hair was full of twigs. She looked, Wren thought, like a very annoyed hedge.

"The first half was my plan," he said with dignity. "The part where we got away clean. The second half was Sera's plan."

"Don't drag me into it," came Sera's voice from further back, low and dry as old paper. "My plan involved not being in a ditch."

"Well," said Wren, "nobody asked you to be right about everything."

The road above them was empty for now. An hour ago it hadn't been — an hour ago it had been full of the Crown's hunters, six of them on grey horses with grey banners, and one of them had turned his head toward the treeline the way a man turns toward a smell, and Wren had known, in the small animal place behind his ribs, that they'd been found.

They'd run. That was when things went wrong.

That was when the rock went over.

* * *

It happened fast, the way bad things do. One moment Wren was scrambling down the hillside after Ada, and the next the whole slope shrugged, and a stone the size of a sleeping pig came loose and rolled, and he'd had just enough time to think *oh* before it caught his leg and pinned it against a root, and the world went white and then red and then very quiet.

Ada had gotten the rock off him. He didn't remember much of that part — just her face, close and furious and frightened all at once, saying *don't you dare, don't you dare look at me like that, you're fine, you're going to be fine,* which is what people say when they are not sure it's true.

Sera had wrapped the leg with strips off her own sleeve, her hands steady, her mouth a thin line. "Not broken," she'd said, pressing along the bone while Wren made a noise like a kettle. "Badly bruised. You'll walk. Eventually."

"I can walk now," Wren said.

"You can hop," Sera said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that hopping gets us killed."

So: the ditch. Because the hunters were still out there, quartering the woods, and because a limping boy and two tired women could not outrun horses, and because Ashen had gone quiet.

That was the worst part. Worse than the rock. Worse than the mud.

Ashen was always there. Not loud — Ashen was never loud; the god spoke the way candlelight falls on a wall, soft and sideways — but *there*. A warmth behind Wren's sternum. A sense of being listened to by something enormous and patient. Ever since the ruined chapel, ever since the little god had chosen him — or maybe Wren had chosen Ashen; neither of them was sure how it worked — Wren had never once prayed into silence.

Until today.

He'd tried. In the ditch, with his leg throbbing in time with his heartbeat, he'd closed his eyes and done it properly, the way he did every night: hands cupped, eyes shut, the words spoken inside rather than out, because Ashen had told him once that prayers shouted were requests, but prayers whispered were conversations.

*Ashen. I know you're tired. I know you're small right now. But I'm scared, and Ada's scared even though she won't say it, and I would really, really like it if you were here.*

Silence.

Not empty silence. That was the horrible thing. It was the silence of someone standing in another room, listening through a wall. Present. Unreachable.

"He's spending himself," Sera had murmured, when Wren told them. She'd looked older, suddenly, in the grey light under the trees. "Keeping watch over three people across miles of hunted country, after centuries of being bound and starved. He's a hearthfire trying to heat a whole house. There isn't enough of him left."

Wren hadn't liked hearing that. He'd liked it even less when Ada, without looking up from re-wrapping his bandage, said quietly, "Then we get him more."

"How?" Wren asked.

She'd tied off the bandage and met his eyes, and for a second she looked almost afraid of her own answer. "I don't know yet."

* * *

The riders came back at dusk.

Wren heard them before he saw them — the particular creak of leather and jingle of harness that meant soldiers moving slow, searching, not marching. He put his eye to a gap between the roots above the ditch and watched four shapes come down the road, dismounted, walking their horses. Leading them was a tall man whose face was wrong in a way Wren couldn't name — smooth, attentive, like a mask worn by someone very good at wearing masks.

Corvane. Ada had said the name once, spitting it like a seed.

They were close. Twenty paces. Fifteen. Close enough that Wren could hear the leader breathing, slow and even, and hear one of the others mutter, "Tracks end here. It's like the ground ate them."

And Wren understood, with a lurch, why the ground had eaten their tracks — because Ashen had spent the last of himself doing it, smoothing the mud behind them, bending the hunters' attention away — and that meant the god was pouring out what little remained, right now, to keep them alive, and it wasn't going to be enough, because the tall man had stopped, and tilted his head, and was looking directly at the deadfall.

"There," Corvane said softly. "Under the roots. I can feel it. A child's faith burns brighter than you'd think."

Wren's whole body went cold and hot at once.

Later, he would try to remember deciding what he did next, and he never could. There was no decision. There was only the thing he knew down in his bones, deeper than fear, deeper than sense: *you do not let the people who kept you be taken.* Ada had pulled a rock off him. Sera had given the shirt off her back. Ashen had emptied himself like a cup.

You paid people back. That was the whole of every prayer Wren had ever learned.

He hauled himself up out of the ditch.

"WREN—" Ada's hand closed on air.

He stood in the open, on his one good leg, between the hunters and the deadfall, and he did the only thing left. He knelt in the mud — both knees, bad leg and all, and the pain went through him like a bell being struck — and he pressed his palms together, and he prayed out loud.

Not a whisper. Not a conversation. A shout.

"Ashen! *ASHEN!*"

The word cracked across the clearing. The hunters stopped. Corvane's head tilted, curious, almost gentle.

"Cute," one of them said. "Praying won't—"

"*You always answered!*" Wren screamed at the sky, at the trees, at the warmth behind his ribs that had gone so thin he could barely feel it. "*Every time! Every stupid little prayer, every night, you ANSWERED, and I don't care if you're small, I don't care if you're tired, I am ASKING — you taught me that asking is holy, you TOLD me — so I'm asking! HELP US!"*

His voice broke on the last word. His leg was screaming. Tears were running freely down his face and he didn't care, because that was the other thing nobody told you about praying: you couldn't do it halfway. You had to mean it all the way down, past dignity, past sense, all the way to the floor of yourself.

And then —

The world *inhaled*.

There was no other way Wren could ever describe it. Every leaf in the forest leaned inward at once. The dusk itself seemed to pull tight, like a sheet snapped flat, and the warmth behind Wren's sternum became a *sun*.

"Oh," said Ashen — and his voice was not small anymore. It was not the candle-voice, the riddle-voice, the tired voice of a god worn down to a wick. It filled the clearing the way water fills a bowl. It filled Wren. "*Oh, little one. You shouldn't have had to ask like that.*"

Light came up out of the ground.

It came up through the mud and through the roots and through the ditch where Ada and Sera crouched, and it came up through Wren, and it did not burn. It was gold and green and the color of the moment just before dawn, and where it touched Wren's leg the pain simply — left. Like a debt forgiven. Like a door opened after a long winter.

The hunters staggered back. Their horses screamed and bolted. And Corvane — Corvane stood his ground, and Wren saw, in the blazing light, that the smooth mask of the man's face had cracked, and underneath it was not cruelty.

Underneath it was *longing*. Raw and naked and terrible, the look of a starving man at a lit window.

"You," Corvane breathed. "The Doorkeeper. They told us you were bound. They told us you were *nothing*."

"I was," Ashen said, and his voice was sorrowful and vast. "For a long, long time, I was. Do you know what binds a god, soldier? Not chains. Chains are for bodies." The light gathered, rose, took shape above the clearing — a figure of gold-green fire, small and child-shaped and somehow taller than the trees, ancient and young at once, with eyes like the first stars. "A god is bound by *doors*. By thresholds. By the work he is given to do and forbidden to finish. I was Vael's Doorkeeper. I held the way open. And when they sealed the country, they sealed me with it — folded me small, set me in a chapel, and gave me one task: answer the children. Only the children. Only the smallest voices. Because the ones who wrote the seal believed grown hearts too dangerous to leave a listening ear."

Sera rose slowly out of the ditch. Her face was white. "That was the compromise," she whispered. "In the writing of it. We argued — some of us argued — that the seal should be deaf entirely. And the faction that won said... said mercy required leaving one door unlatched. One voice that could still reach through." She put her hand over her mouth. "We thought it was a kindness. We thought it was a *crumb*."

"It was a crumb," Ashen agreed, gently, terribly. "And crumbs keep a prisoner alive. That is what crumbs are *for*, priestess."

The light pulsed, and Wren felt it pulse with him, heart to heart, and he understood suddenly that the god was drawing strength from the prayer itself — from the meaning of it, all the way down. Faith, it turned out, was not a feeling. It was a *door*, and Wren had just flung his wide.

"I cannot stay like this," Ashen said. The vastness of his voice was already fraying at the edges, like a robe too heavy for the shoulders beneath it. "This shape costs more than I have. Listen, all of you, because I will not be able to say it twice." The burning eyes fixed on Ada, who had climbed out of the ditch and stood frozen, mud to her knees, staring up at her god with an expression Wren had never seen on her face — wonder, naked and unguarded, the wonder of a woman who had spent her life in a valley where nothing answered. "The sigil you carry in pieces, thief-of-memories — I was its keeper. Its living hinge. When Vael was sealed, the sigil was broken and scattered so that no one hand could undo or complete it, and the keystone was hidden where no seeker would think to look. I know where. I have *always* known where. But knowing and telling are different doors, and the second one opens only at the threshold itself. To the mountain. To the seam in the stone."

"The door," Ada said hoarsely. "The real door."

"The real door," Ashen said. "And you must hurry, because the erasure is no longer creeping. It is *running*. I can feel Vael thinning like breath on glass. Days, now. Perhaps less."

"And the hunters?" Sera asked. "Him?"

Corvane had not moved. The light played over his face, and he was crying — Wren saw it plainly, great silent tracks down the cracked mask of him — and when he spoke, his voice was wrecked. "I was born there," he said. "Before. I was three years old. I don't remember it. I don't remember anything except that everyone else has a country and I have a *file number*." He looked up at the burning god, and his voice dropped to ash. "Is it true? Is there really a way back?"

"That depends," said Ashen, and the vastness guttered, dimmed, shrank — the god folding himself down, down, back toward the size of a candle, back toward the warmth behind a boy's ribs — "on whether the woman holding the keystone can bear to give it up. On whether the soldier holding the second piece can bear to surrender it. And on whether any of you reach the mountain before the world finishes forgetting."

The light sank into the earth. The dusk came rushing back, ordinary and cold.

Wren sat down hard in the mud, his leg whole and humming, his eyes streaming, laughing and crying in exactly equal measure.

Ada helped him up. Her hand was shaking.

"Days," she said.

"Days," Sera agreed grimly, already turning toward the dark line of the hills, where far away — impossibly far away, and getting closer with every heartbeat — a mountain waited with a seam in its side shaped like a broken promise.

Wren wiped his nose on his sleeve and grinned at them both, mud-caked and radiant.

"Told you," he said, "praying works."

And high above, faint as a candle in a window, he felt Ashen laugh.
