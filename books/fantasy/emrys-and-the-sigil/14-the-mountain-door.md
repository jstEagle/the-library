## 14. The Mountain Door

The mountain had been watching them for two days before they reached it.

Ada felt it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder — a prickle between her shoulder blades that no amount of hunching could shake off. The peak rose out of the northern mist like a tooth, gray and patient, and every road they took seemed to bend toward it whether she wanted them to or not.

"Roads do that here," Sera said, when Ada mentioned it. She was walking with her staff held crosswise, the way she did when she was nervous, which lately was always. "They forget where they were going and follow whatever's oldest."

"And what's older than this mountain?"

"Nothing," Sera said. "That's rather the point."

Wren, riding on Ashen's shoulders — because the god had taken the shape of a large gray donkey again, and Wren had declared that if a god offered you a ride, refusing was rude — twisted around to look at Ada upside down.

"Doors are older than houses," he said solemnly. "Ashen says so."

"Did Ashen say so, or did you say so and Ashen agreed?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a judge weighing a hanging offense. "Both," he admitted. "Mostly me."

The donkey-god's ears flicked. "Mostly him," Ashen's voice rumbled up from somewhere in the animal's chest, dry as old paper. "But not wrong. Doors come first. You cannot have an inside without deciding what stays outside."

Ada thought of the Hollow, which had decided for twenty years that everything stayed outside — gods, magic, even, some days, hope. She pulled her coat tighter and kept walking.

* * *

They found the door on the third morning, quite by accident, which Ada suspected was how all important things were found.

The trail had climbed through pine and then through scree and then through nothing at all but wind-bent grass, until Sera stopped so suddenly that Wren walked into the back of her legs and sat down hard.

"Oh," Sera said. Just that. *Oh.* In the voice of someone who has spent thirty years apologizing for her life and has just been handed one reason not to.

Ada came around her shoulder and saw it.

A seam in the mountain's face — a crack in the rock, except cracks didn't run in perfect lines, and cracks certainly didn't curve. This one swept down from a ledge forty feet above their heads in a long, unbroken stroke, then hooked, then crossed itself, then spiraled inward like water finding a drain. It was the sigil. Not shaped like it. It *was* it — the whole design, cut into the mountain by something that had understood geometry as a form of prayer.

Every stolen memory Ada carried stirred at once.

She'd gotten used to them over the months of travel: the merchant's wedding, the fisherman's storm, the woman named Maren and her towers and festivals and the door in the mountain. They lived in her now like tenants who paid rent in other people's feelings. But standing before the seam, they all woke together, and she gasped — because each fragment lit faintly under her skin, gold-white, and each one strained toward the rock like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"It knows us," she whispered. "It knows what I'm carrying."

"The pieces always knew where home was," Ashen said. The donkey shape folded away like a coat being taken off, and the god stood among them as he truly was — small, gray-robed, his face the face of a child drawn by another child, all earnest smudges and enormous eyes. He looked, as ever, like something that should be sitting on a windowsill. He looked, as ever, like the oldest thing in the world. "I sealed this door myself, when I still had hands enough for sealing."

"You had hands," Wren said, holding up his own small ones for comparison. "You have hands now."

"I have borrowed hands," Ashen said. "There is a difference, little one, and it is the whole difference."

Sera had already begun to climb the scree toward the seam, moving with a certainty Ada hadn't seen in her before. At its base she pressed both palms flat against the stone and closed her eyes, and her lips moved — not prayer, exactly. More like greeting an old colleague you'd wronged.

"It's warm," she called back, wonder cracking her voice. "After all these years. It's still warm."

Ada climbed up beside her. The rock under her palms wasn't just warm; it had a pulse. Slow, deep, patient — once every ten heartbeats or so, a thrum she felt in her teeth more than her ears. And beneath her fingers, where the seam's lines ran, the stone was smooth as glass, worn by nothing, because nothing natural had ever touched it.

She let one of the memories rise — Maren's, the dying woman's, the one that showed Vael whole. It leapt toward the surface of her mind so eagerly it hurt, and for half a breath she saw the seam overlaid with the sigil as it had been: blazing, complete, every line singing.

Then the vision guttered, and she counted what remained.

"We're missing two," she said. Her voice came out flat. She made herself say it again, louder, so there would be no pretending later. "Two pieces. Maybe three, depending on how you count the keystone." She glanced at Ashen, who had drifted up the slope behind them with his child's bare feet making no sound on the stones. "How many is it missing?"

The god laid one hand against the seam. His eyes closed. For a long moment the only sound was wind and Wren below, narrating his own climb to nobody ("—and *this* bit is very slippery, note that down—").

"One piece is close," Ashen said at last. "South and west. Moving. It travels inside a man who smells of ink and fear." His eyes opened, and they were kind and terrible at once. "Corvane carries it without knowing. The Crown harvested it from a Vaeli archive and gave it to their hunters as a compass. It pulls them toward the door. That is why they have never lost us, Ada. They were never following *us*."

Ada's stomach dropped. All those narrow escapes, those mornings waking with frost in her hair and hunters' sign on the ridge behind them — she'd thought it was skill, theirs or hers. It had been gravity. The piece in Corvane's keeping wanted to come home, and Corvane had simply been falling alongside it.

"And the last one?" Sera asked quietly. She already knew, Ada realized. Or guessed. Her face had gone carefully blank, the way it did whenever the original seal came up — guilt wearing politeness as a mask.

"I do not know," Ashen said.

Wren, arriving breathless at the top of the scree, froze mid-step. "You don't *know*?" He said it the way only children say it — with genuine scandal, as though omniscience were a promise that had been broken personally to him. "You're a *god*."

"I am a small god," Ashen said, "and I was bound for a hundred years, and doors keep many secrets, and some of them I locked even from myself." He turned his smudged face up to the great curving seam. "When the seal was written, the keystone was hidden so well that even its maker forgot where. That was the point. A hiding place that the hider remembers is not a hiding place. It is a shelf."

"So we're missing a piece," Ada said slowly, "that no one alive knows exists, in a place no one alive can remember."

"Yes," said Ashen.

"And the Crown wants the sigil assembled too — or wants it destroyed, we still don't know which—"

"They want it *controlled*," Sera said. Her knuckles had gone white on her staff. "I wrote the specifications, Ada. Trust me. If they get all the pieces, they won't open the door and they won't close it. They'll hold Vael hostage behind it forever. A country in a bottle, and the Crown holding the cork."

Below them, Wren had gone quiet — never a good sign. He was crouched at the base of the seam, head tilted, listening to something. When Ada scrambled down to him, he was pressing his ear flat against the stone the way children listen to seashells.

"There's people in there," he whispered. "Lots of people. They sound far away. Like through a wall." He looked up at her, and his eyes were shining and frightened in equal measure. "Ada, they sound like they're waiting."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the mountain rather than descend — descending meant losing the door's warmth, and none of them could bear it. Sera built a fire with the flint and the small rituals of competence that were, Ada had learned, how Sera prayed now. Wren fell asleep halfway through a sentence about whether gods got cold feet, sprawled against Ashen's side, and the god sat unmoving as furniture, letting himself be leaned on.

Ada couldn't sleep. She sat at the fire's edge and opened her memories one by one, the way you check your coin purse on a bad road.

The merchant's wedding first. Music, wine, a girl in blue laughing with her father's hands on her shoulders. *Someone loved,* the memory said, in its wordless way. *Someone loved and it was ordinary.*

The fisherman's storm. Terror, then the specific golden relief of surviving it.

Maren's Vael. Towers. Festivals. Lanterns released over a lake so still it held two skies. A door in a mountain, glowing, guarded by a small gray figure —

She stopped. Went back. Held the memory up to the firelight and looked harder than she had ever looked at anything.

In Maren's memory, the door in the mountain had the same seam, the same curves. But it had blazed with light along every line, and the light had come from *within* the rock, as though the sigil weren't carved into the mountain but grown through it, roots of brightness. And at the center of the design — the place where all the lines converged, the keystone position — there had been something. A shape. A person-shaped absence, bright-edged, standing in the door.

Not a person. A *memory*. Someone had stood at the door and given themselves to it, and the door remembered them, and the remembering was the lock.

"Ashen," she said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Come look at this."

The god came without hurrying. Gods, she'd noticed, never hurried; it was one of the things that made them unnerving. He bent over the memory as she held it, and his smudged face went very still.

"That is the keystone," he said softly. "That is what stands in the center. A given memory. Freely surrendered, freely placed." He straightened. "I told you I hid it even from myself. But I see now I misremembered my own cleverness. We did not hide the keystone *somewhere*, Ada."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale.

"We hid it in someone," Ashen finished. "As the final act of the sealing, a volunteer gave their oldest memory to the door, and the door keeps it, and the door will not open — truly open, not crack, not leak — until that memory is returned to its owner." He looked at her across the fire, and his enormous child's eyes were full of something that was either pity or apology. "And the owner must give it back willingly. That is the mechanism. That was always the mechanism."

Ada's hands had gone cold despite the fire. "So we find the volunteer's descendant, or their grave, or—"

"No." Ashen's voice was gentle and absolute. "Memories do not pass to graves. The volunteer is either dead beyond return — in which case the door sleeps until the end of the world — or they are alive, and near, because the keystone *pulls*. Like calls to like. Why do you think you found the Hollow, Ada? Why do you think a valley with no magic raised a girl who steals memories? Why do you think the door's pulse quickened when you touched it?"

The mountain's heartbeat thrummed through the stone beneath her, slow and deep, and Ada counted the interval without meaning to.

Once every ten heartbeats.

Hers.

"No," she said.

"You were born the year the seal was written," Ashen said. "Your mother carried you to the Hollow's edge and left you where the valley's silence would keep you safe, because the keystone in her — the memory she volunteered — had begun to pull at her, and through her, at you. She hid you in the one place the pull could not reach." He knelt, so that his strange child's face was level with hers. "The final piece is not *near* you, Ada Yarrow. It is not *in* your keeping. It is the hole in you. The first memory, the mother-memory, the one you have always felt missing and blamed yourself for losing. The door has been calling it home since the day you were born."

The fire burned. Wren slept. Sera stood at the edge of the light with her back to them, and her shoulders were shaking, and Ada understood that Sera had known — or half-known — and had walked beside her for weeks carrying it.

Ada looked at her own hands. Thief's hands. Hands that had spent a lifetime taking other people's memories because her own had a hole in the middle of them, and she had never once wondered why the hole fit the shape of a keystone.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice surprised her by being steady. "Then we need Corvane's piece. Because I'm not giving anyone my mother's face until I absolutely have to — and if I *do* have to, I'd like the door to actually be worth opening."

Above them, the seam caught the moonlight along its whole length, and for one moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — the lines glowed faintly gold, like something breathing in its sleep.

And far to the south, small as a spark, a lamp was climbing the mountain road.

Corvane had arrived.
