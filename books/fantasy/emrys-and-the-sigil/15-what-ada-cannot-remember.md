## 15. What Ada Cannot Remember

The chapel had no roof anymore, which meant it had a ceiling of stars, and Wren insisted this made it the finest church in the world.

"It's drafty," Sera said, feeding their small fire with splinters of pew. "A church should keep the weather out. That's rather the point."

"That's what's *wrong* with other churches," Wren said, lying back on his bedroll with his hands behind his head, supremely satisfied. "They're all walls and rules. This one lets God look in."

"Gods don't need windows," Sera muttered, but she said it softly, the way she said most things that used to be prayers.

Ada sat apart from them, as she usually did, turning her satchel of stolen memories over in her lap. Eleven pieces now. She had counted them so many times the counting had become a kind of prayer itself — not to any god, just to the arithmetic, which had never once lied to her. Eleven fragments of light and weight, each one a sliver of someone else's life: a wedding, a harvest festival, towers against a green sky, a door in a mountain. The sigil, scattered like a dropped plate, and she was the one who'd swept up the pieces without ever knowing what they came from.

She had been a thief for twenty years. It still startled her, some nights, how good she'd gotten at it.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing?"

"The brooding thing. Where you stare at your bag like it owes you money." He rolled onto his stomach, chin in his hands. "Ashen says you do it when you're frightened."

"Ashen says a great many things," Ada said. "Most of them are riddles, and all of them are rude."

"I'm *right* here," said a voice from the fire.

It came from nowhere in particular and everywhere at once — the small god's voice, dry as old paper and about as warm. Ashen did not have a body anymore, or perhaps never had one worth mentioning; he lived in candle flames and dying embers and, once memorably, in a beetle that had immediately regretted the arrangement. Tonight he flickered in the coals, a suggestion of a face, an ember-orange eye.

"Yes," Ada said. "That's generally the problem."

Sera looked up sharply. In three weeks on the road together, Ada had learned to read the priestess's silences the way other people read faces, and this silence said: *here it comes.* Whatever Sera and Ashen had discussed while Ada slept — and they discussed things, she'd caught the tail end of murmured conversations twice, both times going quiet when she stirred — tonight was apparently the night for it.

"Ada," Sera began.

"No," Ada said.

"You don't know what I'm going to—"

"You're going to tell me something I won't like, in the gentle voice you use for bad news, and Wren is going to look at me with those enormous eyes, and Ashen is going to be insufferable about it." She hugged the satchel to her chest. "I've been on this road long enough to know the liturgy."

Wren, enormously-eyed already, said nothing, which was somehow worse.

The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called, and called again, and got no answer, because there were fewer birds every week now. The erasure was spreading. They had all seen the empty villages, the roads that forgot where they went. The world was being tidied away, corner by corner, and somewhere behind them Corvane rode with the twelfth piece of the sigil in his keeping.

"Tell me," Ada said finally, because not knowing had become heavier than knowing could possibly be.

Ashen rose up out of the coals in a column of pale flame, and for once he didn't joke.

"The sigil has twelve pieces," the god said. "Eleven are in your bag. One is held by the man who hunts you." A pause, long enough for the wind to move through the broken windows. "That is what we believed. We were wrong. There is a thirteenth piece. There always was. And it is the keystone — the piece that locks all the others into shape. Without it, the sigil is a beautiful circle with a hole in its heart. With it—" The flame shivered. "With it, the door can be opened. Or closed forever. Or anchored open, if you're mad enough to try."

"Where is it?" Ada asked. Her voice sounded very small under all those stars.

Ashen turned his ember eye upon her, and she felt it like a fingertip pressed gently to her forehead.

"It is in you, child. It has been in you since before you could walk."

* * *

She laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like something escaping.

"In *me?* I'd know. Believe me, I would know. I've carried stolen memories my whole life — I know exactly what's mine and what isn't. Mine are—" She stopped.

Her own memories were a short list. That was the nature of her life; she'd spent it filling herself with other people's days because her own supply ran thin. But she knew them. The Hollow. Brammel's fence. The first traveler she'd ever robbed, a peddler with a laugh like a hinge. The taste of stolen apples. Rain on the caravan roof.

"I remember everything," she said, and heard how young she sounded saying it.

"Do you?" Ashen asked, terribly gently for a god. "Then tell me your mother's name."

"Her—" Ada reached for it and found her hand closing on nothing, the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue and find the whole language gone. "I never knew her name. She left me at the edge of the Hollow when I was a baby. Everyone knows that. Brammel told me. There wasn't anyone to—"

"Tell me her face."

"She left when I was a *baby*, I wouldn't—"

"Tell me the sound of her voice. One word of a lullaby. The color of the blanket you were wrapped in. Tell me anything at all from before the fence, Ada Yarrow, and I will let you go to sleep believing you are whole."

The fire popped. Wren had gone very still. Sera had put her hand over her mouth.

Ada searched. That was the only way to describe it — she went digging through herself the way she'd dug through travelers' heads for twenty years, down past the Hollow and the fence and the peddler's hinge-laugh, down to the bottom of herself, looking for the floor.

There was no floor.

There was a place where something should have been, and it wasn't an absence like an empty room. It was an absence like a missing tooth — smooth-edged, worn comfortable, a hole she had learned to run her tongue around without noticing. Twenty years of not-noticing. She had built her whole self on top of it and called the ground beneath her solid.

"There's nothing there," she whispered. "Why is there nothing there? Babies remember things. Feelings. Warmth. *Something.*"

"Someone took it," Ashen said. "Not stole it — took it. Carefully. Completely. By someone who knew exactly what they were doing, and why, and what it would cost you." The god's flame bent low, almost bowing. "Your first memory, Ada. The first year of your life, entire. Your mother's face. Her voice. The warmth of being held by someone who loved you more than the world. All of it — it is the keystone. The sealers hid the last piece of the sigil inside the one person who could never be made to give it up: a child too young to miss what she'd lost, hidden in a valley where memory-magic cannot follow."

"They put it in a *baby*," Sera said hoarsely. "They sealed the keystone into an infant and left her in the Hollow."

"They left her where no memory-eater could reach her," Ashen corrected. "And where she could not accidentally find it. Until the gods came back. Until the seal cracked. Until she walked out carrying eleven stolen pieces and a hunger she thought was loneliness." The ember eye fixed on her. "You were never a thief by accident, Ada Yarrow. You were a vault that learned to pick pockets."

* * *

Afterward — she didn't know how long after; time had gone strange and syrupy — she found herself outside, sitting on the chapel's broken steps with her knees pulled up to her chin.

The stars were indifferent and beautiful. Below the hill, the road unspooled toward the mountain, toward the door, toward Corvane and the twelfth piece and everything. She should be planning. Sera would want to plan. Wren was probably asleep, dreaming whatever orphans dreamed, and Ashen was probably sulking in a candle somewhere because gods sulked, she'd learned that much.

Instead she sat with her hands flat on the cold stone and tried to feel the hole.

It was such an ordinary-feeling thing, from the inside. She had gone her whole life thinking she was simply a certain kind of person — watchful, hungry, quick to take and slow to trust. A thief by temperament. And all along, some of that hunger hadn't been hunger at all. It had been the ache of a socket missing its tooth. She had robbed dozens of people of their mothers' faces because she couldn't understand why hers was blank, and every wedding memory she'd lifted had been her pressing her tongue against the gap.

*I felt loved once,* she thought. *For an hour. A merchant's daughter's wedding. I remember how it felt.*

But whose love had come *first*? Whose arms? What voice had sung to her before the fence, before the silence, before the long magicless quiet of the Hollow became the only home she knew?

Nothing. Smooth edges. Worn comfortable.

Footsteps on gravel — Sera, wrapped in her cloak, lowering herself onto the step beside her with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. For a while neither of them spoke. That was one of the things Ada had come, grudgingly, to love about the disgraced priestess: she didn't fill silence with comfort. She let it sit there until it told the truth.

"I helped write the seal," Sera said at last. "I've told you that. What I haven't told you is that I argued for the hiding places. The scattered pieces. I designed half of them." She stared out at the dark. "I don't remember proposing a child. I need you to believe that. But I also can't swear no one did. We were desperate, and desperate people write terrible liturgy."

"You think it was deliberate," Ada said. "Me. Someone chose me."

"I think," Sera said slowly, "that a mother brought her baby to the edge of a valley that eats nothing but keeps everything safe, and left her there, and either she was fleeing Vael's fall and saving the only thing she could—" Her voice caught. "—or she was the sealers' hand, delivering the keystone to its vault. And I think you will spend the rest of your life not knowing which, unless you take the memory back."

"Take it back?" Ada turned to look at her. "From whom? Whoever took it is dust by now."

"Not from a person." Sera's eyes were wet in the starlight. "From the sigil. When the door opens — when all twelve outer pieces come together — the keystone reveals itself. You'll see it, Ada. Everything. Her face. Her voice. Whether she was running away from something or delivering you to safety. Every answer you've been starving for since before you could talk." She took Ada's hand, and her grip was fierce. "And here is the cruelty of it, the truly divine cruelty: to set the keystone, you must give it up. Place it in the door. Let it go. You will learn the truth of your whole life in the same moment you surrender it forever."

The nightjar called again. Still no answer.

"And if I refuse?" Ada asked. "If I keep it? My one memory. The only thing that's ever really been mine."

"Then the door stays shut, or worse — it stays *half*. And the erasure keeps spreading, village by village, bird by bird, until there's nothing left to forget us." Sera smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ada had ever seen on her. "Welcome to priesthood, child. It's mostly this."

* * *

She went back inside eventually. The fire had burned to embers, and in the embers Ashen waited, patient as ash himself.

"Well?" the god said.

"I hate you," Ada said. "I want you to know that. Specifically and personally."

"Noted. Frequently expressed." The ember eye glimmered. "For what it's worth — and gods' worth is debatable — I am sorry. I've known since the mountain road. I watched you count your pieces every night like a rosary and I said nothing, because I am a coward the size of a candle flame, and because I hoped I was wrong."

"Why would you hope you were wrong?"

"Because I was Vael's doorkeeper, child. I have watched a thousand people carry keys they never asked for." His flame guttered low. "It never ends well for them. It sometimes ends well for everyone else."

Ada sat down across from the coals and pulled the satchel into her lap. Eleven pieces, faintly glowing, murmuring their borrowed lives. Somewhere ahead, a twelfth, in the hands of a terrified man who only wanted to exist. And underneath it all, threaded through her bones since before she had words for bones: a thirteenth. Her mother's face. Her mother's voice. The single year of being someone's whole world.

She had spent twenty years stealing other people's memories because her own ran out so soon. Now she knew why. Now she knew the theft had a shape, and the shape had a name, and the name was *keystone*.

"Wren prayed for you tonight," Ashen said quietly. "Did you hear him? Before you came outside. He prayed that you'd stop being sad."

"He prays for everyone. It's his worst habit."

"He prayed that you'd find out you were loved." The god's voice softened to almost nothing. "Careful what children pray for, Ada Yarrow. I have to answer them. Even when the answer costs."

Ada looked into the embers, and the embers looked back, and far away in the dark a mountain waited with a seam in its rock shaped like everything she was.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We ride at dawn. And Ashen—"

"Yes?"

"If my mother's face is the last thing I ever give away—" Her throat closed. She finished anyway. "Make sure I get to see it clearly first. However long it takes. Promise me that."

The little god flared, once, bright as a struck match.

"I promise," said Ashen, keeper of doors. "Now sleep, vault-that-picks-pockets. Dawn is coming, and so, I fear, is Corvane — his fires are visible on the southern ridge, and he is riding hard."

Ada's head snapped toward the black line of the hills — and there, faint as a fallen star, a pinpoint of torchlight moved along the ridge. Coming their way. Coming for the thirteenth piece he could never take gently, and the girl who had just learned she was the lock, the key, and the door all at once.
