## 16. Corvane's Truth

The trap had been simple, and that was what shamed him.

Corvane had built complicated traps before. Traps with three layers of misdirection, traps that required forged documents and a bribed ferryman and a week of watching a target's tea-drinking habits. He had once spent eleven days pretending to be a cheese merchant to catch a memory-thief in Marrow's Crossing, and he still couldn't smell gouda without his hands shaking.

But the boy had made it easy. The boy was hungry, and hungry children followed bread.

Corvane sat on an overturned crate in the abandoned mill and watched Wren sleep, curled in the corner where Corvane had put him after binding his wrists with soft cord — not rope, never rope on a child, some old instinct from a life he did not officially remember insisting on it. The boy had fought like a wet cat for exactly four seconds, then gone limp with the particular surrender of someone who had been caught by adults many times before and knew the choreography.

That had bothered Corvane more than the fighting would have.

He checked the boy's pulse again. Steady. He'd fed him half a wheel of hard cheese and a heel of bread, because you didn't interrogate information out of an empty stomach, and also because the boy had looked at the food the way Corvane imagined men looked at miracles. The cord was loose enough not to bruise. He'd checked twice.

*You're getting soft,* said the voice in his head that sounded like his handler, Vess, back in the capital. *You're supposed to be a hunter, not a nursemaid.*

*The boy is bait,* Corvane told the voice. *Bait stays fresh.*

*And when the Yarrow woman comes?*

*Then I do my job.*

The voice laughed at him. It had been laughing at him for years, ever since the day he'd stood in the Hall of Records and watched a clerk strike a line through a name — *his* name, his true one, the one that began with the soft consonant of a language no map admitted to — and felt something tear loose inside him and never quite reattach.

Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages turning. Somewhere up the valley, the Yarrow woman and the fallen priestess would have discovered the boy missing by now. Sera Ilune would be praying, or cursing, or both; she'd always struck Corvane as the type who could do them simultaneously. And Ada Yarrow would come. She had to come. That was the whole architecture of the thing.

He found himself hoping she'd bring the god.

Which was absurd. You did not hope for complications. Complications were how hunters died. But there it was anyway, sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone: a small, traitorous wish that the little doorkeeper-god would come too, so that Corvane could ask it — could finally, after thirty-one years of official nonexistence — ask someone who actually knew:

*Was there really a country? Was I really born in it?*

Because here was the thing they never put in the recruitment pamphlets of the Crown's memory-eaters: the ones who hunted stolen memories best were the ones who were missing some themselves. A hole in you recognized a hole in the world. Corvane had been recruited at nineteen, plucked off a work crew on the northern roads by a woman who'd watched him flinch at the word *Vael* — a word he hadn't known he knew — and had said, gently, "Oh, you poor thing. You're from *there*, aren't you?"

He had said, "Where?"

She had smiled the way you smile at a wound you intend to clean. "Exactly."

They'd told him the erasure was mercy. That Vael had been a wound in the world, a country of wild gods and unlicensed miracles, and that the Crown had amputated it to save the body politic. They'd told him his memories of it were dangerous, contaminated, and that the Order would hold them safely in trust until such time as—

"Until such time as *what*?" nineteen-year-old Corvane had asked.

"Until the Crown decides you've earned them back," the woman said, and wrote his new name in the ledger — *Corvane*, a nothing-name, a placeholder, the bureaucratic equivalent of a blank wall — and he understood, in the marrow-deep way you understand things you'll spend decades refusing to understand, that *until such time* meant *never*, unless he became useful enough to make it mean otherwise.

So he'd become useful. Twenty years useful. Twelve hundred recovered memories useful. He'd eaten other people's pasts and handed them over in glass vials and told himself every night that this one, this next one, would tip the scales, would earn him the restoration, would make the clerks un-strike the line.

And then the sigil had surfaced. Then a thief girl in a magicless valley had started glowing with the pieces of the seal. Then the old gods had woken up and started answering prayers again, and the Crown had gone pale in its marble halls, and Corvane had understood that the game had changed: if Vael came back, everyone erased with it might come back too. Officially. On the record. Remembered.

Or the Crown would burn what remained of it first, to keep the lie intact.

It depended entirely on who reached the mountain door first.

* * *

Wren woke at dusk, the way orphans wake — all at once, silently, eyes open before the body moves. Corvane pretended to be asleep against the far wall and watched the boy take inventory: wrists (bound, softly), exits (two, both visible), strange man (awake, pretending otherwise), cheese rind (gone, mourned).

"You snore," Wren announced.

"I don't."

"You do. Like a cart with a bad axle." The boy sat up, wincing at his bound wrists, and glared with the full force of a child who had been personally blessed by a god and considered this a shield against ordinary consequences. "Ashen's going to turn you into something. A beetle. A boring one."

"Ashen," Corvane said carefully. "The doorkeeper."

Something flickered across the boy's face — surprise, quickly hidden, badly. "You know about Ashen?"

"I know about doors." Corvane sat up fully and decided, in the space of a breath, to stop performing. It was exhausting, the performance. He'd been doing it for twenty years and his face ached from it the way feet ache from boots two sizes wrong. "Boy. Wren. What does your god say about a country called Vael?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the pines turned their pages.

"He says," Wren said slowly, "that names are just doors wearing coats. He says—" and then the boy's voice changed, deepened slightly, took on a cadence that no eight-year-old owned, and Corvane's whole body went cold and hot at once, "*the struck-out line remembers the ink.*"

Corvane closed his eyes. "Tell him—"

"He can hear you," Wren said, in his own voice again, matter-of-fact. "He hears everything anybody says near me. He mostly ignores it. He says most adult talk is weather." A pause. "He's listening now, though. He says you taste like a locked room."

"I feel like one," Corvane said, before he could stop himself.

And then, gods help him, he talked. He hadn't planned to. There was no tactical advantage; the boy was bait, not confessor. But twenty years of carrying a secret shaped like a country, and here at last was a child through whom the actual doorkeeper of that country was peering out, and Corvane's discipline simply — folded. Like a letter you'd read too many times.

"I was born there," he said. "I think. I don't remember it. They took the memories when I was small — or maybe I was born after the sealing, outside, to parents who'd fled, and the memories were taken later, I don't know, I *don't know*, that's the whole — that's the entire problem." His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with detached interest, as though they belonged to a colleague. "There's a hole where my childhood should be. Not empty like nothing happened. Empty like something happened and was *removed*. You can tell the difference. An empty room and a room with a shape of dust where furniture used to be."

Wren was quiet. Then, gently, horribly: "Ashen says he knows the shape of your dust."

Corvane laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Of course he does."

"He says your name isn't Corvane."

"No," Corvane agreed. "It isn't."

"He says it starts soft. Like water going over a stone."

"*Cael,*" Corvane said, and the word left him like a bird leaving a cage it had forgotten it hated — sudden, violent, followed by stunned stillness. His mouth hung around the shape of it. Thirty-one years. The clerks had struck the line, the maps had lost the borders, the language itself had been sanded out of the world, but his mouth had kept the name folded away all this time, waiting.

"Cael," Wren repeated, tasting it. "Ashen says that's better. He says names should fit like shoes, not like handcuffs."

"That's not very reverent."

"He's not a very reverent god. He's the size of a teacup right now. Reverence is for big gods." Wren shifted, and his expression turned older, sharper — the look of a child who had learned to read adults the way sailors read sky. "Why do you serve them? The Crown people. If they're the ones who erased you."

And there it was. The question at the center of the locked room, asked by an eight-year-old in a mill, while a teacup-sized god listened.

"Because they promised to give me back," Corvane said. "Myself. My memories. My name in the ledger instead of a line through it. Every memory I recover for them buys a little credit toward my own restoration. That was the bargain. Twenty years of it." He heard his own voice go flat and bitter and didn't care. "And then the seal started breaking, and I realized — I realized they were never going to restore any of us. Because restoring us means admitting Vael existed, means admitting the erasure happened, means the whole history they wrote is a forgery. We weren't assets. We were witnesses. And you don't restore witnesses. You *manage* them."

"So why are you still doing their job?"

"Because if Vael comes back *their* way — if they control the door, control the story — maybe they'll need loyal erased people to populate the narrative. Reformed witnesses. Proof the amputation worked." He shook his head. "And if it comes back the other way, your way, the thief-girl's way — then maybe nobody needs the Crown's permission to exist anymore. Either way, Cael survives only if Vael does. So I'm not serving the Crown anymore, boy. I'm serving myself, and the Crown just hasn't noticed yet."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Ashen says that's the saddest smart thing he's heard in four hundred years."

"It's honest, at least."

"He says honesty from a hunter is like milk from a stone. Surprising, and you shouldn't build a farm on it." The boy leaned forward, cords creaking. "He says one more thing. He says — he says he remembers you."

The mill went very quiet. The wind stopped turning pages.

"He says there was a boy who lived by the eastern gate of the mountain city, who used to leave honey-bread on the doorstep of the doorhouse because he thought the doorkeeper got lonely. He says the boy grew up and the country got scared and the boy's family ran in the night, and the doorkeeper held the door open as long as he could, and the last thing the boy shouted back was—"

"Stop," Corvane whispered.

"—'keep it lit, I'll come home.'"

"*Stop.*"

But Wren didn't stop, because gods don't stop, and neither does grief once it finds the crack it's been looking for. "He kept it lit, Cael. Four hundred years, dimmer and dimmer, bound smaller and smaller, but he kept it lit. He says you're the last one who promised. He says—" and now the boy's voice wavered, the god's certainty and the child's compassion tangling together, "—he says he's sorry he let the door close behind you."

Corvane put his head in his hands and wept the way men weep when no one has taught them how: silently, furiously, as though crying were a security breach. Twenty years of vials and ledgers and blank-faced orders, twelve hundred stolen pasts, and it took a bound orphan and a teacup god four minutes to find the one memory the Crown had missed — not in his head, but in his chest, where they never thought to look, because the Crown believed everything important was written down.

When he finally looked up, his face was wrecked and his decision was made, and both of these facts terrified him equally.

"The Yarrow woman," he said hoarsely. "Ada. When she comes — and she's coming, she'll have tracked us by now — I have to bring her in. Or hand over the sigil pieces. It's the only leverage I have left, the only thing standing between me and being a line through a name forever." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless there's another way to be remembered." He stared at the boy — at the god behind the boy's eyes, small and patient and lit like a kept promise. "Your god opens doors. What if — what if there was a door that didn't require the Crown's permission? What if existence didn't have to be *granted*?"

Wren tilted his head, listening. Then he grinned, gap-toothed and luminous.

"Ashen says that's the first smart thing a hunter's said in four centuries. He says—" the boy paused, and his grin faltered into something urgent, "—he says she's here. The thief. She's at the tree line, and she's brought the priestess, and Sera has her fire lit already, and Ashen says—" Wren's eyes went wide. "He says whatever you're going to choose, choose it *now*, because Ada Yarrow doesn't knock."

Boots crunched on frost outside the mill door. Through the gaps in the boards, firelight flickered — gold, steady, furious.

Corvane stood up, heart hammering the rhythm of a name beginning with a soft consonant, and went to meet her.
