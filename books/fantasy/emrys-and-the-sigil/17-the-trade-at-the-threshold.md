## 17. The Trade at the Threshold

The door did not look like a door.

It looked like a scar in the mountain — a seam of pale stone running floor to ceiling, thin as a knife cut, and carved across its face was the sigil. Or most of it. Ada stood before it with her hands pressed flat against the cold rock and felt the pieces inside her stir like sleeping birds.

"Almost," Ashen said through Wren's mouth. The boy's voice came out doubled, layered with something older and smaller and sadder. "Almost whole. Two pieces missing."

"One," Sera corrected. She stood a few paces back, arms crossed against the wind that funneled down the pass, her defrocked priestess's braid whipping over one shoulder. "One piece is Corvane's. The other is—" she glanced at Ada, and her jaw tightened, "—the other we know about now."

Ada didn't look at her. Looking at Sera meant thinking about it, and thinking about it meant feeling the shape of the hole inside herself, the one she'd carried all her life without knowing it was a hole. *Your first memory,* Ashen had told her, gentle as snowfall. *Taken when you were small enough not to miss it. It holds the keystone.*

Her mother's face lived somewhere in that hole. Her mother's voice. Her mother's reason for leaving her at the edge of the Hollow with nothing but a blanket and a name.

*Later,* she told herself, the way she'd been telling herself for three days. *Survive first. Grieve after.*

Wren sat on a flat stone nearby, his ankle wrapped in strips of Sera's spare robe, swinging the good foot and pretending he wasn't scared. He was eleven. He was very bad at pretending. Every few minutes his eyes flicked down the pass to where the hunters' torches would eventually appear, and every few minutes Ada pretended not to see him do it.

"So how does this work?" Wren asked. "Do we knock? Is there a bell? There should be a bell."

"There is no bell," Ashen said. "There has never been a bell. I kept this door for four hundred years and no one ever once asked for a bell."

"I'm asking now."

"You are asking for a bell on a mountain door in a country that officially does not exist while being hunted by men who eat memories, and your contribution to this crisis is *a bell.*"

Ada felt her mouth twitch. That was the thing about Ashen — even at the end of everything, the little god made you want to laugh, and laughing made the fear loosen its grip just enough to breathe. She suspected that was deliberate. Gods who answered only children's prayers probably learned early that children can't walk toward terror unless someone makes it small enough to carry.

"The sigil needs to be complete," Sera said, businesslike, because Sera coped with fear by organizing it. "When Corvane arrives — and he will arrive; he's been one step behind us since the market town — we take the piece from him. By force if we must."

"And then what," Ada said quietly. "I give up my own memory too. The keystone. And I spend the rest of my life not knowing why my mother left me. Not knowing her face." She'd said it aloud for the first time, and hearing it made it real in a way that hurt worse than she'd expected. "That's the price. That's always been the price. Everyone gets their memories back except me."

Sera opened her mouth. Closed it. She was a woman who had helped write a seal that locked gods out of the world, who had spent thirty years paying for that choice, and Ada watched her decide — visibly, painfully — not to offer comfort she didn't believe in.

"It might be worth it," Sera said finally. "If Vael lives. If those people live. It might be a fair trade."

"That's what frightens me," Ada said. "I think it might be."

* * *

Corvane came at dusk, alone.

They heard him long before they saw him — boots on scree, unhurried, the walk of a man who knew exactly where he was going and saw no point in hurrying toward it. Ada positioned herself between him and the door, and Sera flanked her, and Wren — despite explicit instructions involving sitting still and touching nothing — crept to a boulder where he could watch everything.

He looked terrible. That was Ada's first thought, and it shamed her. The last time she'd seen Corvane, at the edge of the burning ledger's smoke, he'd been iron and certainty, a hunter in the Crown's grey livery with a blank-faced calm that made her skin crawl. Now the calm was cracked. His coat was torn at the shoulder. There were shadows under his eyes deep enough to lose things in, and his hands — the hands of a man whose trade was reaching into other people's heads — would not stay still.

"You came alone," Sera said.

"I sent the others away." His voice was rough, like a door that hadn't been oiled in years. "Officially, I'm scouting ahead. Unofficially—" he exhaled, "—unofficially, I've stopped caring what's official."

"You could have brought an army," Ada said.

"An army can't open this door. Only you can." He looked at her then, and there it was — the thing underneath everything, the thing she'd glimpsed in chapter and campfire and chase: he wasn't looking at her like prey anymore. He was looking at her like a drowning man looks at a rope. "And only I have the second piece."

"Then let's talk terms," Ada said, and hated how steady her voice sounded, because inside she was already doing arithmetic with her own soul.

* * *

Here is what Corvane offered, standing in the wind with the pale seam of the door glowing faintly behind them:

The Crown's memory-eaters kept vaults. Deep ones, under the capital, warded and catalogued, full of everything they had ever harvested — including, Corvane said, records going back twenty-three years. Including a harvest taken from a young woman at the edge of the Hollow. A harvest never destroyed, because even the Crown didn't destroy keystone material; they hoarded it, the way dragons hoard, the way frightened people hoard anything powerful.

"They have it," he said. "Your first memory. Your mother. I've seen the entry in the ledger. Harvested from one A. Yarrow, age four, by order of the Seal-Wardens. They took it the same night she left you there."

Ada's breath went out of her.

She hadn't known. That was the worst part — she hadn't even known it was gone until Ashen told her, and now here was a stranger confirming it, holding it out like a lamp. Somewhere under the capital, in a drawer or a crystal or a lead-lined box, was the sound of her mother's voice. The exact color of her eyes. Whether she'd cried when she walked away.

"I can get it," Corvane said. "I have access. I'll bring it to you — unharmed, unopened, yours. In exchange, you keep your keystone. You don't feed it to the door. We find another way to anchor the sigil — there are other ways, Sera wrote the seal, Sera can rewrite it—"

"Sera cannot rewrite it," Sera said sharply. "What I built, I built with nine others, half of whom are dead, using materials that no longer exist. There is no other way, Corvane. There is the door, and there is the wave of erasure eating the roads behind us, and there is Ada's memory, and that is the entire list."

"Then we find a third thing."

"There is no third thing!"

"There has to be!" And it tore out of him louder than he meant, cracking down the middle, and for a moment Corvane stood there breathing hard with his composure in pieces around his feet, and Ada understood — really understood, for the first time — what he was.

He was her.

He was a person with a hole in himself, born in a country that had been erased so thoroughly that his own name was a legal fiction, serving the machine that erased him because it was the only machine that acknowledged he existed at all. He wanted to be remembered back into existence. And now he was offering her the one thing she wanted most in the world, because some part of him believed — needed to believe — that bargains like that could be won. That holes could be filled by taking from someone else.

Because his hole had never been filled any other way.

"Don't answer yet," he said, quieter. "Just — think about it. You of all people know what it is to be empty. I'm offering you the piece of yourself you've been missing since you were four years old. No one else in the world can offer you that. No god. No priestess. Just me."

Behind him, the door hummed. Behind her, Wren had gone very still on his boulder. And beside her, Sera reached out and gripped Ada's arm — not gently, not kindly, but desperately, the grip of a woman who had watched a bargain like this one from the inside and knew exactly what it cost.

"Ada." Sera's voice was low and fierce. "Listen to me. I helped build the seal. Do you know what the keystone piece actually is? It isn't fuel. It isn't a brick. It's the *anchor* — the part that holds the sigil in the world instead of letting it drift into metaphor. If you keep it, the door opens halfway. Maybe. For a moment. And then the erasure comes through the gap and takes Vael anyway, and takes everyone in it, and you'll be standing here with your mother's face in your head and ten thousand people gone forever, and you will spend the rest of your life knowing you chose the smaller love."

"I know," Ada whispered.

"Then refuse him."

"I know what it costs, Sera." Her eyes stung. The wind was cold and the door was humming and somewhere under the capital a box held her mother's voice. "But do you know what it costs to say no? To stand in front of the only chance I will ever have, and turn away? I've stolen a thousand memories. I know exactly what I'm asking myself to give up. I know it better than anyone alive."

Sera's grip loosened. Something in her face broke and reformed — recognition, maybe, the look of a woman seeing her own younger self making a catastrophic choice for reasons that were, horribly, good ones.

"What are you going to do?" Corvane asked.

Ada looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," she said.

Sera made a sound like she'd been struck.

"Yes," Ada repeated, and her voice shook and she let it shake, because lying to a memory-eater required truth everywhere else. "You're right. I want it. I want her face more than I want air. Bring me the memory, and I'll keep the keystone, and we'll find your third thing together. Give me your hand."

Corvane hesitated. Then — because he was drowning and she was a rope — he stepped forward and extended his hand.

* * *

His memories were guarded the way a miser guards coins: jealously, expertly, and with a lifetime of practice. But Ada Yarrow had been stealing from practiced guards since she was six years old, and she had one advantage no hunter had ever planned for.

She wasn't reaching for the sigil piece.

Every defense he had — every ward and wall and trained reflex — was built to stop a thief going for the fragment of the seal tucked behind his sternum. She slid past all of it sideways, quiet as water, and found what she was actually looking for: not the piece, but the *map*. The location of the Crown's vaults. The layout of the memory-archives. The ledger entry, word for word, that described a harvest taken from one A. Yarrow, age four.

She took it the way she took everything — cleanly, leaving no wound — and she took one more thing besides: a single memory of Corvane's own, small and bright, of a woman in Vael singing in a kitchen. His mother, presumably. Someone had left it unguarded, right at the surface, the way people leave their truest treasures where they think no one will look.

Then she let go of his hand and stepped back.

For a heartbeat, nothing. Corvane blinked, frowning, patting his coat as if checking for pickpockets — and then his face changed. Slowly. Wonderfully. Like dawn coming up over a country that officially did not exist.

"You didn't take the piece," he said.

"No."

"You took—" he pressed two fingers to his temple, staring at nothing, watching his own knowledge rearrange itself. "You took the vault maps. The ledgers. You took—" his voice caught, "—you took the kitchen. Why would you take the kitchen?"

"Insurance," Ada said. "You're going to walk into that vault yourself, Corvane. You're going to use your access, and these maps, and you're going to get my mother's memory out — because if you don't, I'll give the kitchen back to you in pieces, and then I'll find every other memory of Vael you're hiding, and I'll scatter them where the Crown can never reassemble them. You'll be no one again. Completely, permanently no one." She paused, and gentled her voice, because cruelty was not the point and had never been the point. "Or — you bring me the memory, and I give you back the kitchen. All of it. And more, if there's more. I'm a thief, Corvane. I know exactly how much a stolen thing is worth to the person it belongs to."

He stared at her. And then — she would remember this all her life — he started to laugh. It was a broken, astonished sound, half sob, the laugh of a man realizing he'd been out-hunted by a poacher from a magicless valley.

"You said yes," he managed. "You looked me in the eye and said yes."

"I did say yes." Ada shrugged, and felt Wren, behind the boulder, start to giggle, and felt Sera slowly exhale thirty years of tension. "I said yes to buying time. The deal was real right up until the moment your hand touched mine. After that it was just theft with extra steps." She held up the invisible weight of what she'd taken. "You taught me that, actually. The Crown's whole trade is promising people things and keeping the fine print. I just read the fine print faster than you did."

"The erasure is days behind us," Corvane said. His laugh had died, but something steadier had replaced it — resolve, maybe, or surrender. "Days. Maybe less."

"Then you'd better ride fast," Ada said. "And Corvane? When you get there — check the entry twice. Make sure they didn't damage it. Some things," and here her voice did finally break, just a little, just enough, "some things you only get one of."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he bowed — actually bowed, hunter to thief, enemy to something that wasn't quite either — turned, and went down the mountain at a run.

* * *

"Well," said Wren, emerging from behind his boulder with the shameless grin of a boy who had enjoyed every second, "that was the best thing I've ever seen, and I once saw Ashen turn a tax collector into a frog for a whole afternoon."

"That was a miscommunication," Ashen said stiffly, through his mouth.

"He hopped, Ashen. He definitely hopped."

"Wren, I am four hundred years old and I will put you in a pond."

Ada barely heard them. She was leaning against the door, palms flat on the pale stone, feeling the sigil-pieces stir, feeling the shape of the bargain she'd just made settle over her like a coat that almost fit. She had chosen the many over the self. Sera was right that it was the larger love. Sera was also wrong that it didn't cost anything — it cost everything, constantly, and would keep costing, and there was no version of this where she got to stop paying.

But somewhere under the capital, in a lead-lined box, her mother was waiting. And for the first time in her life, Ada Yarrow had a map to the vault.

"Ada." Sera's hand on her shoulder, light now. "That was either the bravest or the stupidest thing I've ever witnessed, and I once watched a man argue theology with a thunderstorm."

"Both," Ada said. "Definitely both." She looked up at the seam in the mountain, at the incomplete sigil, at the door that waited for its final piece — the one piece she carried in the hollow center of herself, the one she had promised, silently, to give.

*One problem at a time,* she thought. *Get the mother. Grieve later. Survive first.*

Behind them, far down the pass, the wind changed — and with it came a sound none of them expected. Not hoofbeats. Not hunters.

Bells. Faint, cracked, impossibly familiar bells, ringing from the far side of the mountain, from the side of the door.

From Vael.

Someone, on the other side, was ringing a bell — as if they'd known, all along, that a girl would come asking whether the door had one.
