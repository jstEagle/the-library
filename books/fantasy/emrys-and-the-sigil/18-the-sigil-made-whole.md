## 18. The Sigil Made Whole

The door in the mountain was a seam of light shaped like nothing Ada had ever seen and exactly like everything she had ever stolen.

It stood taller than a chapel, thinner than a knife's edge, cut into the grey rock as though the mountain had been holding its breath for two hundred years. And along the seam, in grooves worn smooth by time, the sigil waited — incomplete, hungry, a pattern of interlocking shapes with three gaps where pieces should be.

Ada stood before it with her hands shaking.

Not from cold. The Hollow had taught her cold; she could carry cold like an old coat. This was something else. This was the feeling she got when a traveler slept too near her fire and their memories rose up around her like steam off warm bread — except now the memories were hers to give back, not take.

"You don't have to do it all at once," Sera said quietly. She looked terrible. The memory-eater had left her hollow-eyed and thin as winter reeds, and she leaned on Wren's shoulder more than she would ever admit. But her voice was steady, the way it always went steady right before she said something brave. "One piece at a time."

"There are so many," Ada whispered.

She hadn't known until this moment. She had thought of herself as carrying — what? A dozen memories? Twenty? But standing before the door, feeling it pull at her the way the tide pulls at a shore, she felt every theft of her whole life wake up inside her at once. The merchant's wedding. The shepherd's first snow. The soldier's letter home. The baker who'd sung to his ovens. The woman with the red door. Dozens. Hundreds. A lifetime of borrowed light, hoarded in the dark because she couldn't bear to let any of it go.

"I'm scared," she said, and was surprised how easy it was to say out loud.

Wren took her hand. His was small and grimy and gripped like a vise. "Ashen says," he began, then stopped, frowned, listened to whatever the small god was murmuring in his ear, and started over. "Ashen says being scared just means you know what you're giving away is worth something. He says cowards never feel it."

"That sounds like something a god would say to get out of doing the scary thing himself," Ada said.

Wren grinned. "He says that too."

* * *

She started with the smallest one, because you learn to eat a meal one bite at a time, and Brammel had taught her that grief works the same way.

The memory was old — a fisherman on the coast, decades ago, remembering the morning he taught his son to gut a mackerel. It wasn't precious. It wasn't even kind; there had been blood and swearing and the boy crying. But it was somebody's, and it had lived in Ada's chest for eleven years like a pebble in a boot.

She pressed her palm to the seam of the door and did the thing she had never once done in reverse: she opened herself and let it flow out.

It hurt.

Not the way a wound hurts. The way a splinter coming out hurts — a bright, specific wrongness, the body objecting to losing something that had grown into it. The fisherman's morning poured out of her through her fingers and into the groove of the sigil, and where it landed, the stone lit up. One small shape in the vast pattern filled with soft gold light, and the seam hummed like a struck bell.

And Ada felt lighter. And smaller. As if someone had taken a room out of her house.

"Oh," she said.

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked.

"Yes." She flexed her fingers. "But it also feels like putting down something heavy I forgot I was carrying. Both things. At the same time."

Sera nodded slowly. "That's what giving is supposed to feel like. That's why they made taking illegal in every holy order I've ever heard of." Her mouth twisted. "Well. Most of them."

Ada laughed — a wet, cracked sound — and reached for the next one.

* * *

They came faster after that, though never easily.

The shepherd's first snow. The midwife's hundredth birth. A girl's memory of her grandmother's kitchen, all steam and cardamom. Each one tore loose on its way out, each one left a gap behind, and each one lit another piece of the sigil until the seam blazed half-alive and the mountain itself seemed to lean toward them, listening.

Ada worked with her eyes closed most of the time. It was easier that way. When she opened herself fully, she could feel each memory's shape as it left — the weight of it, the warmth of it, the little hooks it had grown into her over the years. Some came willingly, sliding free like a hand letting go. Some fought. The merchant's wedding — the first memory she had ever stolen, the one that had taught her what being loved felt like — clung to her ribs like a child clinging to a mother's skirts, and she had to sit down afterward and breathe for a long time with Wren's arms around her middle.

"It's not fair," she said into his hair. "I know it isn't mine. I've known since the night I took it. So why does it feel like cutting off my own arm?"

"Because you kept it alive," Sera said. She had settled onto a rock nearby, watching the sigil glow with an expression Ada couldn't read — guilt and awe fighting for the same face. "Memories don't live in the people they happened to, Ada. They live wherever they're loved. You loved them. Badly, selfishly, in the dark, but you did. That counts for something."

"Does it?"

"It counted enough to keep the sigil pieces from fading. Two hundred years, and every other fragment of the seal crumbled to dust — except the ones you were carrying." Sera's voice caught. "You weren't just a thief, Ada. You were an accidental archivist. The gods have worse senses of humor than anyone gives them credit for."

From somewhere near Wren's ear, a voice like dry leaves said: *"Compliment noted. Insufficient. Continue."*

"See?" Wren said. "He liked it."

"He has a strange way of showing it."

*"So do you,"* said Ashen.

* * *

By the time the sun crossed the high ridge and began its long slide down the western sky, Ada had given back everything she could name.

She sat on the cold stone, empty-handed in a way she had never been, and felt the strangeness of it. For twenty-three years she had been a house full of other people's furniture. Now the rooms stood bare, echoing, and she kept turning toward corners expecting to find a memory that wasn't there anymore — the taste of someone else's wedding cake, the smell of someone else's sea.

What remained was just Ada. Small, tired, hungry, frightened Ada, with a hole where her oldest memory should have been and no idea what used to fill it.

And yet.

"We're close," Sera breathed.

The sigil was nearly whole. Nearly every groove in the seam burned with gold light, the pattern blazing brighter than sunset, brighter than the forge-fires of the unclaimed-names town. Only three shapes remained dark — and one of those, Ada knew, lived in Corvane's keeping, wherever Corvane was now. He had walked away from the threshold two days ago carrying his piece and his terror, and none of them knew if he would come back.

The second dark shape was the keystone. The one at the center. The one Ashen had gone very quiet about.

"And the third?" Ada asked.

Wren tilted his head, listening. Then his face did something complicated. "That one's yours," he said softly. "The last one. It's the only thing you ever stole without knowing it."

Ada looked at the keystone-shaped darkness at the heart of the sigil, and felt the hole in herself answer it, the way two magnets find each other through a wall of flesh.

"Not yet," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "If I give that one, I want to give everything else first. All of it. I want to arrive at that door with nothing left in my hands but me."

Sera looked at her for a long moment. "That's either very wise or very foolish."

"With me it's usually both."

* * *

There was one more thing. She had been avoiding it since dawn, and the sigil seemed to know, because the dark grooves pulsed gently whenever she turned away, patient as a dog waiting by a bowl.

The woman with the red door.

Ada had carried her longest of all — fourteen years, back when Ada was barely more than a child herself and still learning what her gift was. The woman had been dying even then, coughing blood into a kerchief in a roadside inn, and she had told Ada, in the strange calm of the nearly-dead, *take it, girl, take it somewhere safe, I can't hold it much longer.* And Ada had taken it, and told herself for fourteen years that she was keeping it safe, and had never once tried to find the woman again because finding her might mean giving it back.

"She's dead," Ada said aloud. "Isn't she. Whoever owned this. They have to be dead by now, or I'd have—"

"Memories given to a sigil aren't lost," Sera said. "They're planted. When the door opens, everything the seal holds goes home — to Vael, or to whatever's left of the people who dreamed it. Even if she's gone, her memory will be waiting somewhere she'd have wanted it." She paused. "That's the theology, anyway. I wrote some of it. I'm reasonably confident."

Ada laughed despite herself. Then she knelt at the base of the seam, put both palms flat against the warm stone, and gave the woman with the red door back her life.

This one didn't tear. This one *bloomed*. Fourteen years of careful keeping came out of her like water finding its level, and the sigil flared so brightly that Wren shouted and covered his eyes, and for one impossible moment Ada saw — not remembered, *saw*, from the outside — a red door opening onto a garden full of white flowers, and a woman laughing, and a country with towers like held breath under a sky that had never heard of erasure.

Then it was gone, and Ada was on her knees on cold stone, weeping without quite knowing why, and the sigil was one piece short of whole.

"One," Sera whispered. "Corvane's piece, and then—"

"No," said Wren.

Ada looked up. The boy was staring past her, down the mountainside, and his face had gone the color of ash.

* * *

She saw it before she understood it.

Far below, where the foothills met the plains, the world was coming apart. Not dramatically — that was the horror of it. No fire, no flood. Just a soft grey wave rolling across the land like fog with intent, and everywhere it passed, things became *less*. A farmhouse lost its second story, then its windows, then its walls, then the idea of walls. A road unwove itself. A flock of birds mid-flight simply stopped having ever been birds, and the sky closed over the space where they'd been without a ripple.

The erasure. The Crown's great forgetting, the tide that had swallowed Vael two centuries ago and had been creeping outward ever since, patient as rust. Coming for the mountain. Coming for the door. Coming for them.

"How long?" Sera demanded.

Wren listened, and his voice was very small. "Ashen says by tomorrow's dawn. Maybe sooner. He says—" the boy swallowed "—he says the door can't open halfway. If the wave reaches us before the sigil is complete, everyone inside Vael goes with it. Everyone. Forever. And us too, probably, because we'll be standing in the doorway."

Ada got to her feet.

Her legs shook. Her hands were empty. Every borrowed thing she had ever hoarded was gone, given back, planted in gold light along the seam — and she had never felt so stripped, or so strangely, terrifyingly *clear*.

"Then we need Corvane's piece," she said. "Tonight."

"And the keystone," Sera said quietly, and her eyes went to Ada, and Ada knew that Sera knew — had perhaps known since the ruined chapel — what the final piece was and whose chest it lived in.

"Not yet," Ada said again, firmer this time. "First we get Corvane. First we finish the job with everything except the part of me that's mine to choose." She looked down the mountain at the grey tide, crawling closer, eating the world one forgotten inch at a time. "After that—"

"After that," Sera finished softly, "you decide what your mother's face is worth."

The words landed like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling, and Ada felt the hole in herself open wide to swallow them.

Down in the valley, the erasure wave rolled over a village and the village became a field and the field became a rumor, and far off — impossibly, absurdly — a single figure was climbing the mountain road toward them, walking straight out of the grey, carrying something that glowed faintly in his cupped hands like a coal he was afraid to drop.

"Wren," Ada said. "Is that—"

"Ashen says it's the man who was born in the country that wasn't," Wren said. "And Ashen says—" the boy's brow furrowed "—he says the man is praying. He's been praying the whole way up. To *him*."

Ada watched Corvane climb, and the sigil behind her pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat waiting for its last two beats, and the grey tide below ate another mile of the world.

"Well," she said. "Let's hear what he wants."
