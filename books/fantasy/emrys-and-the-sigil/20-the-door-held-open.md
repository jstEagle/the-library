## 20. The Door Held Open

The door did not want to close.

That was the first thing Ada noticed, standing in the seam of the mountain with the sigil blazing above her like a wound in the air. All her life she had assumed that doors were things you pushed or pulled, that they had opinions and you overruled them. But this one *leaned*. It leaned toward Vael the way a tired traveler leans toward a lit window, and every instinct in it said: *stay open, stay open, stay open.*

"It's asking," Wren said. He was sitting on a flat stone with his ankle wrapped in Sera's spare cassock-lining, his face still pale from the injury, but his eyes enormous. Ashen perched on his shoulder — no longer the size of a sparrow, not yet the size of anything sensible, a god roughly the dimensions of a well-fed cat, made of ember-light and patience.

"Of course it's asking," Sera said. She stood with her arms crossed, staring at the sigil like it owed her money. "I helped write it. Seals always ask. That's how they get you. You think you're closing a door, and really the door is closing *you*."

"You would know," Ada said.

"I would know," Sera agreed, without heat. There was something almost peaceful about her now, Ada thought. The confession had burned through her like a fever breaking. What was left was thinner, grayer, but steadier — a woman who had set down a sack of stones and discovered her spine still worked.

Ashen hopped from Wren's shoulder to the ground and walked — actually walked, with little deliberate steps, like an old man inspecting a fence line — to the center of the sigil's glow.

"The old way is finished," the god said. Its voice was small and crackling, a hearth-fire voice. "One keeper. One carrier. One person who holds the whole weight and calls it duty until it crushes them." It looked up at Ada, and its ember-eyes were terribly kind. "You were going to offer, weren't you."

Ada opened her mouth. Closed it.

"I saw it in your face when the door opened," Ashen said. "The same face the last doorkeeper made. *I'll carry it. I'm the one who found it. It's mine.*" The god's light flickered, amused and sad at once. "Child, I have watched that sentence kill better people than you."

"I'm not—"

"You are exactly that," Ashen said. "It is one of your best qualities and it nearly ended you twice this month. So we are not doing it."

"Then what?" Sera asked. "If we don't seal it, the erasure spreads. If we seal it, Vael suffocates behind the seal again, and everyone inside forgets why they're breathing. Those are the choices I helped build, and I built them badly."

Ashen sat down in the middle of the sigil. It was such a catlike thing to do that Ada almost laughed, and the laugh caught in her throat because the god's light began to spread — thin threads of it running out along the sigil's lines, following them outward, past the door, down the mountain, further than sight.

"A sigil is not a chain," Ashen said. "A sigil is a *knot*. And a knot held by one hand can be pulled loose by one pull. But a knot tied into many hands—" the light pulsed, warm, "—must be released by all of them together. Or held by any of them alone."

Ada felt it before she understood it: a gentle tug, deep under her ribs, where the last of the stolen memories had been returned and where something new seemed to be growing in the space they'd left. A thread of light, anchored in her, running out through the door.

"Oh no," she said.

"Yes," said Ashen.

"No. Absolutely not. I've spent my whole life taking things from people so I wouldn't have to be *tied* to them—"

"And now you get to spend the rest of it being tied to them anyway," Ashen said serenely, "but honestly, and by choice, and with their consent. Yes. That is what belonging is. I am told it is terrible and also the only thing worth having. I would know. I answered children's prayers for three hundred years and it was the best work of my existence."

Sera had gone very still. "Split the sigil," she said slowly. "Not sealed shut, not flung open unanchored. *Held open.* By many keepers, each carrying a piece, none carrying enough to be crushed by it."

"The door stays open," Wren said, catching on, sitting up straight despite his ankle, "and nobody can ever close it again unless *everybody* lets go at once. Which means—" he counted on his fingers, frowning, "—which means everybody has to agree to abandon everybody else. At the same time. Forever."

"Which has never once happened in the history of people," Ada said.

"Correct," said Ashen, looking deeply satisfied.

* * *

They argued about it for two days.

Not bitterly — or not only bitterly. There was a great deal of tea, brewed over Sera's small fire just inside the threshold, so that the steam drifted through the door into Vael and came back smelling faintly of someone else's bread. There was a great deal of walking to the edge of the light and looking out at the half-empty country: the towers standing clean against the sky, the terraced fields gone wild and then, in the distance, tiny figures — the first returnees, walking in from exile with bundles on their backs, stopping, staring, falling to their knees or laughing or simply standing very still, the way people do when a name they'd been punished for remembering suddenly works again.

And there was arguing.

"We should choose keepers carefully," Sera insisted. "Trained people. People who understand what they're holding."

"You trained yourself," Ada said, "and look how that went."

"That is *unfair* and also completely accurate," Sera admitted, and drank her tea.

Wren wanted everyone. "Every single person in Vael should hold a piece. Then it's not a burden, it's just — like everyone carrying one brick of a house. Nobody's back breaks."

"And if the town of unclaimed names hadn't come back," Ada said, "if half of them had stayed hidden in the hills — you'd have a house with missing bricks."

"So we find more people," Wren said with the magnificent simplicity of a nine-year-old. "There's *loads* of forgotten people. The Crown's been making them for years."

Which, Ada had to admit, was horribly true.

It was Corvane who settled it, in the end. He had been quiet since the threshold — quieter than Ada had ever seen him, which was saying something, since quiet was his native language. He sat apart from the fire, as he had sat apart from everything for the entire journey, a man built like a drawn bow who had finally been allowed to rest at his full length. His blank-faced mask was gone; he'd buried it somewhere on the mountain path, or thrown it in a ravine, or possibly eaten it, and Ada hadn't asked. His face underneath was younger than she expected, and tired in a way that went all the way down.

"You're all wrong," he said, on the second evening. Everyone turned. He looked mildly alarmed at the attention, like a man who had spoken in a library. "Not about the idea. About the shape of it." He turned a smooth river stone over in his hands — he'd taken up fidgeting with stones since the mask came off, and no one commented, because some mercies are silent. "You want to pick worthy keepers. Devoted ones. People with faith, or training, or — whatever Sera had." He glanced at Sera. "Sorry."

"Carry on," Sera said, "I've made peace with being a cautionary tale."

"But the seal failed," Corvane said, "because it was carried by people who believed they deserved to carry it. Myself included. I served the Crown because I thought being useful to the people who erased me might earn me back into existence. That's not devotion. That's begging." He set the stone down. "Pick people who know they're broken. Pick people who've lost things. They won't confuse the door with a ladder."

Silence around the fire.

"Well," said Sera, after a moment. "The man who hunted us for six hundred miles has just delivered the wisest speech of the expedition."

"He's had a lot of time to think while chasing us," Ada said. "We talked *constantly*."

"We really didn't," Corvane said.

"It felt constant."

* * *

The keeping itself took place at dawn, because Ashen insisted that doors prefer mornings, and because, as Wren pointed out, dawn was when prayers were loudest.

They gathered inside the threshold — the five of them, and then the others, arriving over the following hours in a slow trickle that became a stream. The town of unclaimed names came first, the whole improbable population of it, led by Marta the census-clerk-turned-arsonist-accomplice, who took one look at the blazing sigil and said, "Right. Where do I sign?" Then the returnees, dozens of them, then hundreds, coming up the mountain road because word moved ahead of them the way word does among people who have spent years learning to listen for hope.

Ashen explained it simply, in its hearth-crackle voice, amplified somehow so that everyone on the mountainside could hear:

"One thread each. Not the whole knot. You will feel it — a warmth, a weight, like carrying water in cupped hands. Some days heavier than others. On the heavy days, you will be tempted to give it to someone stronger. Don't. Hand it to someone *weary* instead. Weary hands hold tighter than strong ones. That is the whole secret of doors."

Then the god began handing out pieces of the sigil like a grandmother distributing sweets.

Ada watched it happen and could not stop watching. A baker from the unclaimed-names town received a thread and gasped, pressed her hand to her sternum, laughed. An old soldier who had spent forty years pretending to be from a country that never existed took his piece with tears running openly down his face. Two children got theirs and immediately started comparing whose glowed brighter, and Ashen let them, because gods who answer only children understand that competition is a form of prayer.

Sera knelt to receive hers. When the thread settled into her, she made a sound Ada had never heard from her — small, cracked, relieved — and said, to no one, to the mountain, to whoever had once been listening on the other end of her defrocked vows: "Let me do it right this time."

Corvane went last of the named ones. He hesitated a long moment before stepping forward, and Ada realized he was afraid — genuinely afraid — that the sigil would refuse him. That erasure was contagious. That some things, once unwritten, stay unwritten.

Ashen looked up at him for a long time.

"You were born here," the god said.

"Yes," Corvane said. His voice was rough.

"Then the door knows your footsteps." The thread of light sank into his chest, and Corvane swayed, and did not fall, and when he straightened his eyes were wet and he pretended very hard that they weren't, and everyone kindly pretended along with him.

Ada took hers last. She had expected it to hurt — everything in her life that mattered had hurt — but instead it felt like the hour after the merchant's wedding memory, that borrowed feeling of being loved, except this time it wasn't borrowed, and it wasn't an hour, and it wasn't leaving. It was a warmth under her ribs with a hundred fine threads running out of it, through the door, into the world, tying her to bakers and soldiers and children and a disgraced priestess and a former hunter and a boy who prayed like other people breathed.

"Not so bad," Ashen said, appearing at her elbow.

"Shut up," Ada said fondly.

"I am a god. You cannot make me shut up. It is one of my best qualities."

* * *

They built nothing grand. That was the point.

No temple, no garrison, no throne of doorkeepers. Instead, over the following weeks, a small settlement grew at the threshold — because of course it did; put a lit doorway in a mountainside and people will bring soup to it eventually. Houses went up on both sides of the pass, some in Vael and some outside it, until the border ran straight through the middle of a street, and the tavern that opened there had two taps, one on each side of the line, and the landlord claimed the ale tasted different depending on which side you poured from, and nobody believed him, and everybody kept testing.

Sera took a room above the tavern and began, cautiously, to teach again — not doctrine, she was emphatic about that, no doctrine, absolutely no doctrine, she had seen where doctrine got everyone — but practical holiness. How to listen for prayers. How to sit with the grieving. How to brew tea for a god. Her first student was Wren, who was terrible at meditation and magnificent at compassion, and who told her so with brutal eight-year-old honesty until she laughed for the first time in a decade.

Corvane stayed too. He said it was temporary. He said it every week. He fixed roofs, because it turned out that a man trained to track anything across any terrain was extremely good at finding where water got in. Children followed him around in a respectful cloud, and he bore it with the expression of a man being gently digested by something friendly.

And Ada—

Ada walked the street with the border down its middle, on a market morning, and stopped to watch a traveler arrive.

He came up the road dusty and footsore, the way travelers do, and paused at the threshold where the sigil's light lay across the cobbles like spilled honey. He looked at the door. He looked at the country beyond it — the towers, the terraces, the smoke of a thousand chimneys of a nation that officially did not exist and was doing splendidly anyway. He looked at the people: the baker with her thread of light, the old soldier, the children, the priestess, the roof-fixer with his cloud of admirers, the boy on crutches telling anyone who'd listen about the time he met a god the size of a cat.

Then the traveler looked at Ada, and reached slowly toward his own temple — the universal gesture, the one she knew from a lifetime of the Hollow, the gesture that meant *what do you have worth taking.*

Ada smiled.

"Nothing to steal here," she said. "Everything's already given away."

The traveler blinked. Then, uncertainly, hopefully: "I have a story. Would that — would anyone want it?"

From the doorway of the tavern, Wren's voice rang out, bright as a bell: "We want *everything*! Come *in!*"

And the door — leaning, as always, toward the light — held open.
