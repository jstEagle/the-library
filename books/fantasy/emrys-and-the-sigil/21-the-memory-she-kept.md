## 21. The Memory She Kept

The house they gave her in Vael had a crooked chimney and a window that looked out over a valley so green it seemed to be apologizing for something. Ada liked it immediately. It was the kind of house that had been empty long enough to stop pretending it wasn't lonely.

Everyone else had somewhere to be. Sera was down in the town square arguing theology with three returned exiles and winning against all of them simultaneously. Wren was — well, Wren was wherever Ashen was, which these days meant everywhere at once, trailing wonder behind him like a coat too big for his shoulders. Even Corvane had found work, of all things, carrying crates at the harbor of a country that officially did not exist, and looking, by all reports, happier than he had ever looked wearing a blank face.

Ada had nowhere to be. That was the whole point.

She sat on the floor because the chair felt too formal for what she intended, and she set the memory in her lap like something breakable.

It didn't look like much. Memories never did, outside a head. This one was small and pale and old, older than any she'd ever stolen, and it hummed faintly against her palms the way a bird hums when you cup it too gently. Her own first memory. Taken from her before she could walk, before she could know it was gone. She had carried a hole shaped exactly like this for twenty-three years and had never once noticed the shape of it, because how do you notice the absence of something that left before you learned its name?

Ashen had told her where it was. *In you*, the little god had said, in that voice like embers deciding to be kind. *It never left. It only went so deep you forgot the way down.*

*You don't have to open it,* Sera had said, and meant it, and Ada loved her for meaning it.

But Ada Yarrow had spent her whole life taking other people's memories and feeling, for an hour at a time, like someone loved. She had built a survival out of borrowed joy. And now she sat in a green valley in a country that wasn't supposed to exist, holding the one memory that was truly hers, and she understood — with the simple, terrible clarity of someone who has finally stopped running — that she could not teach anyone to give freely until she had received this one thing herself.

"Right," she said aloud, to the crooked chimney, to nobody. "No more stealing. Not even from myself."

She opened it.

* * *

Rain.

That's the first thing. Rain on leaves, rain on stone, rain coming down through a gap in the world the way light comes down through a roof with a hole in it. She is very small. Small enough that the world arrives mostly as textures: wet wool against her cheek, the smell of woodsmoke and wet earth, the enormous sound of an adult heart beating under an ear pressed to a chest.

Her mother's chest.

Ada had expected — she realized later, standing outside the memory and watching it the way you watch weather — she had expected abandonment. That was the story she'd told herself in the dark for two decades, the story the Hollow had let her keep because the Hollow kept out everything except the truths people brought with them. A woman leaving a baby at the edge of a magicless valley because the baby was inconvenient, or cursed, or simply too heavy to carry another mile.

That is not what the memory shows.

The memory shows a woman running.

She is running through rain with a child bundled against her, and she is not walking away from anything. She is running *toward* — toward a line in the land that Ada knows better than her own hands, the soft gray border where the Hollow begins and magic ends. Behind them, the sky is the wrong color. Not storm-wrong. *Erased*-wrong. The hills back there are going pale at their edges like ink lifted off paper, and there is a sound underneath the rain, a sound like a page being turned by something with no hands, and every time it turns, another hillside forgets itself.

Vael is falling. Not to armies. To erasure. The Crown's great working, the unmaking, the thing Sera helped write and spent thirty years regretting — it is happening right now, right here, and Ada's mother is inside it with a baby in her arms.

And she is singing.

That undoes Ada more than anything else. The woman is running for both their lives and she is singing, badly, breathlessly, some lullaby half-swallowed by the rain, because the baby is frightened and a frightened baby must be sung to even if the world is ending, *especially* if the world is ending. Ada stands outside her own first moment and listens to a voice she has no other record of, a voice that exists nowhere else in the world now except in this small pale humming thing in her lap, and the voice is warm, and tired, and absolutely certain of one thing:

The child will live.

The Hollow's edge comes up gray and soft as fog. The woman stops at the line. Ada can feel, through the memory, why she stops — feels it in her own small body, the way the air changes at the border, the way the pursuing wrongness *slides* along the valley's edge like water off wax. The Hollow. The seal. The place magic cannot follow.

Magic cannot follow. Including this. Including the erasure.

The woman looks down at the bundle in her arms, and Ada sees her face.

She has wondered about that face her entire life. She has stolen a hundred memories of mothers — wedding-day mothers, deathbed mothers, mothers scolding, mothers laughing — trying to assemble a stranger out of other women's love. And now here is the original, rain-flattened hair, a scratch on her chin, eyes the same stubborn gray as Ada's own, and she is not beautiful in any way a ballad would care about. She is beautiful the way bread is beautiful when you are starving.

"You're going to hate me," the woman tells the baby, conversationally, the way you tell a friend bad news. "I need you to know that's all right. Hate me all you like. Just stay inside the line."

She kneels. She sets the bundle down in the dry grass just past the border, and her hands shake, and she keeps talking, low and fast, a whole letter compressed into a whisper because there is no time for paper.

"There's a country dying behind me and I won't let it take you with it. There's a seal on this valley, old and strong, and nothing made of magic can cross it — and what's coming is made of nothing *but*. You'll be safe here. Safe and—" her voice cracks, and she laughs at herself, wipes her face with a wrist already soaked, "—safe and magicless, my love, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, you'll grow up in a place where nothing answers prayers. But you'll *grow up*."

The baby makes a sound. The woman makes a sound back, and it is the worst sound in the memory, worse than the turning-page noise behind her: it is the sound of a person folding their grief up small enough to fit in a pocket so their hands stay free.

"I'll come back," she says. Then, honestly, because apparently honesty ran in the family: "I might not be able to. But I will try until trying kills me, and then I'll try from whatever comes after." She touches the baby's cheek once, lightly, like closing a book you intend to reopen. "Your name is Ada. I picked it because it means *belonging*, in the old tongue, the tongue they're erasing. Whatever they take from the world, they can't take it from your name. Remember who you belong to."

She stands. She steps back across the line, into the rain, into the paling world.

And she does not look back — no. That's the lie Ada would have told herself. She looks back. Of course she looks back. She looks back four times, each one costing her a stride, each one carving her face deeper into the memory, and the last time she smiles, a terrible bright broken smile, and mouths something the rain mostly eats.

Ada has watched the memory eleven times now. On the twelfth, she finally reads the lips.

*Belong to yourself.*

Then the woman turns, and runs back into Vael, into the erasure, into the page-turning dark — and the memory, having shown everything it came to show, closes like a hand.

* * *

Ada came back to herself sitting on the floor of the crooked-chimney house with tears running into her collar and no memory of starting.

Grief hit first, and she let it. She had earned her grief; she was not going to rush it out the door like an unwelcome guest. She cried for the woman whose name she would never know — no, she corrected herself, wiping her nose on her sleeve, whose name she *might* yet know, because Vael was alive now and full of returning exiles and somebody, somewhere, might remember a gray-eyed woman who sang badly in the rain. She cried for the twenty-three years she had spent believing herself abandoned, building her whole sharp careful self on top of a foundation of a lie. She cried for the baby in the grass, who had grown up hungry in a valley of silence, stealing love by the hour because no one told her she'd been left inside the single safest place in the world by someone who loved her enough to be hated.

The Hollow hadn't been a cage. It had been a cradle. Brammel's fence, the magiclessness, the long silence where gods couldn't reach — all of it, a wall between one small child and the end of everything. The safety of home revealed as a cage? No. Revealed as what it always was: a mother's arms, stretched wide enough to hold a valley.

"I take it back," Ada said to the chimney, thickly. "All of it. Every curse I ever laid on that fence."

The chimney said nothing, being a chimney, but the fire crackled in a manner she chose to find agreeable.

Forgiveness came slower, and it surprised her by not being for her mother. She had forgiven her mother almost instantly — how could she not, after that smile, after *belong to yourself*? No, the hard forgiveness was for herself. For every traveler she'd robbed. For every hour of borrowed love. For the girl who had called hunger competence and loneliness independence. She sat with that a long while, firelight moving on the walls, and finally she said, out loud, in the empty room:

"She asked me to stay inside the line. I stayed inside the line my whole life. And then I walked out of it and saved a country." A wet, astonished laugh. "I think that counts as belonging to myself."

Peace came last, the way dawn comes — not all at once, but by degrees, the room getting lighter without your noticing the exact moment. It settled into her like rain settling into ground that had been dry too long. The hole in her was not a hole anymore. It was a room. It had a window, and through the window was rain, and a woman running toward danger with her chin up, singing.

Ada fell asleep on the floor with the empty memory in her lap, and for the first time in her life she dreamed of nothing borrowed at all — just green hills, and a voice humming half a lullaby, and the other half waiting for her to learn it.

* * *

She woke to knocking.

Not urgent knocking. Patient knocking, the knock of someone who has knocked on many doors and expects nothing from any of them. Ada got up, stiff-necked, pushed the hair out of her face, and opened the door onto morning.

A traveler stood on the path. Dusty boots, road-worn coat, a pack worn shiny at the straps — and empty hands. Ada's old instincts woke and reached automatically, the thief's reflex, checking a stranger for anything worth taking.

There was nothing. She could feel it. No memories to steal, no secrets worth the price. The traveler had crossed the mountains and the door and the whole impossible distance into a country that did not exist carrying exactly one possession.

"A story," the traveler said, when Ada raised an eyebrow. "I heard there's a woman here who trades in them. Freely, they said. Someone at the market said you taught a memory to be given instead of taken, and I've walked a long way, and I thought—" the traveler faltered, suddenly young-looking, suddenly hopeful. "I thought maybe you'd want to hear mine. If you have time."

Ada leaned against the doorframe. Behind her, the fire popped. Somewhere down the hill, Wren was shouting something joyful at Ashen, and Sera was probably still winning her argument, and the door in the mountain stood open, held open, held by many hands, and would stand open tomorrow.

She had a feeling — sudden, certain, warm as banked coals — that this was what the rest of her life was going to look like.

"Come in," said Ada Yarrow, keeper of nothing, teacher of gifts, daughter of a woman who sang in the rain. "I have all the time in the world. I live in the country that wasn't supposed to have any."
