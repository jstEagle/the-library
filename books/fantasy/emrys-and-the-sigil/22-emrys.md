## 22. Emrys

The naming took place on a morning so bright that even the mountain seemed to squint.

They gathered in the half-ruined square of the old capital — the one that had been called nothing for so long that the stones had forgotten their own street names. Ada stood near the fountain with Wren on her shoulders, because Wren had insisted he could see better from up there and because, honestly, she liked carrying him. It was a strange new feeling, being someone's high ground.

Ashen stood on the fountain's rim. The god had grown since the door opened — not large, never large, but *fuller*, the way a candle flame is fuller than an ember. Where once he had been a whisper in a ruined chapel, now he was a small grey presence like a child made of woodsmoke and patience, and when he spoke, birds stopped to listen.

"You have asked me," Ashen said, "what my oldest word is."

The crowd — perhaps four hundred souls, some returned from exile, some stumbled out of forgetting, some simply curious — went quiet. Sera stood at the front in her patched vestments, hands folded, wearing her shame the way she now wore everything: openly, and with her chin up.

"It is not a grand word," the god continued. "It is not a word of power or thrones. When I was young — and I was very young; doorkeeper gods are born small and grow by what they keep — the first thing I ever learned to say was the word the children used when they came to the door and did not want to leave. It meant *you may stay*. It meant *there is room*. It meant *I see you and you are not a stranger here*."

He paused. Somewhere in the crowd, a baby laughed at absolutely nothing, which babies consider a sacred duty.

"The word," said Ashen, "is *Emrys*."

And the crowd repeated it — first one voice, then ten, then all of them, the way a fire catches — until the name rolled up against the mountain and came back down again like an echo that had been waiting two hundred years for permission to exist.

Wren tugged Ada's hair. "Say it," he demanded.

"Emrys," Ada said, and found, to her considerable surprise, that her eyes were wet. She blamed the wind. There was no wind.

* * *

The country they named that morning was not the country that had been sealed away. That Vael was gone — its towers crumbled, its festivals unremembered, its roads grown over with a silence no map had ever recorded. What remained was emptier and stranger: a land of green valleys and standing doors, where the returned exiles wandered their own hometowns like polite ghosts, touching walls and asking each other, "Was this the bakery? Was this *my* house?"

But it was a beginning. And beginnings, Ada had learned, were just endings that hadn't given up yet.

She stayed. Of course she stayed. She told herself at first it was temporary — that she'd help set things right and then walk out through the open door and see the world that wasn't the Hollow, a world she'd only ever known secondhand, through other people's stolen weddings and first snows and terrible first kisses.

But there was work here that only she could do.

Because the people coming back from forgetting arrived hollow in a way Ada recognized down to her bones. They had been erased from the Crown's records, and erasure ate outward. Some had lost their names. Some had lost whole decades. One old man arrived knowing he had a daughter but unable to remember her face, and he sat by the fountain every day holding a wooden spoon, because something in him knew the spoon mattered, and he wept without knowing why.

Ada could give memories back. That was the shape of her gift, turned right side up at last. Every piece she'd carried — every wedding and harvest-festival and lullaby she'd lifted from travelers to survive — she had already returned to feed the sigil. But the giving itself had taught her something: memory did not have to be *taken* to be *shared*. Given freely, it left the giver whole. Even richer, somehow, like a song sung twice.

So she founded a school. Well — "founded" was generous. It was three benches under a repaired roof, and the curriculum was Ada sitting cross-legged and saying, "Hold out your hand. No, don't grip it. Memories aren't fish."

The Hollow folk who drifted through the open door — a few dozen of them, restless souls who'd never quite fit the valley's magicless quiet — became her first students. Old Brammel came, to everyone's astonishment, including his own. He sat in the back with his arms crossed and declared the whole enterprise "witchcraft with extra steps," and then came back the next day, and the next, until Ada caught him gently returning a memory of a river to a woman who'd been born after the rivers were erased, and he pretended he hadn't, and everyone let him.

Sera taught beside her, on the days her hands didn't shake. Her order's rites were gone, but she was rebuilding them from scratch — simpler ones, kinder ones, liturgy written in pencil because, as she said, "we've seen what happens when it's written in ink." She still woke some nights convinced the hunters had found her. On those nights she walked the perimeter of the town and counted the doorkeepers' marks on the gates, and by the third gate she always remembered where she was: somewhere she was wanted.

And Corvane —

Corvane stayed too, which had surprised exactly no one less than Corvane himself. He kept the records. Not the Crown's ledgers — those were ash, and good riddance — but new ones, written in his small precise hand: who had returned, what they'd lost, what they remembered. He was meticulous about it. He was also, Ada noticed, careful never to write anyone's name in a way that could be *used*, and he burned each day's draft in the evening like a man feeding a guilt he intended to carry for several centuries.

"You know," Ada told him once, watching him feed the fire, "most people would call this redemption."

"I would call it bookkeeping," Corvane said. But he almost smiled, which for Corvane was practically a festival.

* * *

Autumn came to Emrys, the first autumn the country had had in two hundred years, and it turned out the mountains made the leaves go gold and furious about it. Ada taught her classes in the mornings and walked the door-road in the afternoons.

The Door itself stood open in the mountainside — a seam of light shaped like the sigil, wide enough for five people abreast, humming faintly like a held breath that had decided to become a song. It no longer needed Ada. That had been the whole point. The sigil lived now in twenty-two keepers scattered across two countries: Sera carried a piece in her rebuilt rites; Wren carried his in a prayer he said every night before bed ("*thank you for the door, please don't let it get lonely*"); the unclaimed-names town kept theirs in a shared story told at every winter feast; even Corvane bore his fragment, etched inside the cover of his ledger, where only he would ever read it.

No single person would ever again be the lock. Or the key. Or the price.

Ada visited anyway, most days, because the Door liked company, and because standing before it felt like standing before a fire in winter — warmth without demand.

"You're brooding," said Ashen, materializing beside her in his usual manner, which was to say abruptly and without apology.

"I'm thinking."

"Brooding. In my professional opinion as a god."

"Your professional opinion as a god is that socks are a form of prayer," Ada said. "Wren told you that and you believed him."

"Socks *are* a form of prayer. Warm feet are grace." The little god settled onto a rock, woodsmoke-grey and content. "You're wondering if you'll stay."

Ada looked out through the Door, down the long switchback road toward the pass, toward the world beyond — the real world, the wide one, full of countries that existed on maps and gods that answered adults and travelers who might, if she were very hungry and very careless, become tempting again.

"I'm wondering," she admitted, "if I know how to stay anywhere. I spent my whole life taking from people who were passing through. Passing through was the only relationship I understood."

Ashen considered this with the gravity of a much larger deity.

"When children learn my word," he said at last, "*Emrys* — do you know how they use it? Not as a name. As an invitation. A child sees another child alone at the edge of a game and says, *Emrys?* — meaning, do you belong here? Yes. Will you? Also yes." He tilted his smoky head. "Belonging isn't a place you find, Ada Yarrow. It's a place you keep answering yes to."

"That's very wise," Ada said.

"I stole it from a goose."

"*What?*"

"Geese are extremely loyal. Terrifyingly so. You could learn from geese." And he wafted off before she could formulate a response, which was, she suspected, theologically intentional.

* * *

Winter came early and kind, and the school moved indoors, into the old chapel where Wren had once prayed alone to a god the size of a whisper. They'd rebuilt it together — Sera directing, Brammel grumbling, Wren hammering his thumb at least nine times and treating each injury as a badge of rank. Now it smelled of pine resin and bread, because Sera had decided that worship and baking shared a sacramental quality, and no one argued with a woman who could make cinnamon rolls feel like absolution.

It was on a grey afternoon in that chapel, mid-lesson, that the traveler arrived.

Ada saw him come up the road through the frost-laced windows: a thin man in a coat that had been mended more times than it had been washed, leading a donkey loaded with nothing but a bedroll and a battered fiddle case. He walked like someone who had been walking for a very long time — not lost, exactly, but unhurried in the way of people who had nowhere they were expected.

Her old instincts woke instantly, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar whistle. She catalogued him without meaning to: tired, hungry, a limp favoring the left knee, a grief-shaped hollowness behind the eyes. *Rich pickings,* said the girl she used to be. *One touch of your hand and you could have his best day. His mother's kitchen. Whatever warm thing he's carrying.*

Then she looked at her students — six of them today, including a former exile practicing giving a memory of rain to a boy who had never seen any — and she smiled at her own reflexes the way you smile at an old coat that no longer fits.

The traveler stopped in the doorway, stamping snow off his boots, and blinked at the scene: benches, children, a priestess with flour on her sleeves, a scarred woman at the front who watched him with unsettling directness.

"Forgive me," he said. His accent was from far away — southern, maybe, past the Salt Marches. "I heard there was a town here that isn't on any map. I've been looking for it for — well. A while."

"You found it," Ada said. "Most people do the other way around. They live here and can't find themselves. You're ahead of schedule."

That earned a tired almost-laugh. "I heard—" He hesitated, shifted his weight. "There's a story going down the roads. About a thief who stopped stealing. About a door that opens instead of shuts. About a country that came back from being erased." He looked around the chapel, at the sigil-marks on the lintel, at Wren solemnly supervising from atop a bench. "I didn't believe it. But I've got nothing left to lose by believing, so I walked."

"And what are you hoping to find?" Sera asked gently, setting down her rolling pin.

The traveler's hand went to his chest — an unconscious gesture, protective, over the pocket where a person keeps the thing they can't afford to lose. "Nothing to take," he said quickly. "I know what this place is — what she does." A nod at Ada, and something like awe warred with fear in his face. "I haven't got anything worth stealing. My memories are mostly hard roads and bad winters. But I heard—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I heard memories could be *given*. Freely. That there was a place where you didn't have to lose something for someone else to have it."

The chapel had gone very still. Even the boy receiving the rain-memory had paused, droplets of imagined weather hanging in the air between his palms.

"Yes," Ada said. "That's true. That's the whole point of the place."

The traveler swallowed. Then, with the care of a man handling something breakable, he drew from his coat pocket a small cloth bundle and unwrapped it on the nearest bench.

Inside lay a memory. Ada could see it the way she'd always been able to see such things — a soft glow, silver-grey, worn smooth at the edges from years of being taken out and looked at. An old one. A treasured one.

"My grandmother," the traveler said. "This is her. Her kitchen, her voice, the song she sang when the bread came out. I've carried it eleven years. Through two wars and one flood and a border that moved twice." His voice cracked and steadied. "I always thought I'd die holding it. And then I heard about this place, and I thought — maybe that's not the only thing you can do with love. Maybe you can put it down somewhere it'll grow."

He looked up at Ada, terrified and certain at once.

"I want to give it to your school. So people who've been erased can learn what a grandmother's kitchen sounds like. I don't want anything back. I just want it to *matter* somewhere."

Ada crossed the room slowly. She knelt by the bundle, and the memory glowed up at her, and for a moment — just a moment — she was back in the Hollow, crouched over a sleeping merchant, hungry enough to eat someone's joy and call it living.

That girl was still in her. She suspected she'd always carry her, the way the traveler carried his grandmother. But the girl was quiet now. Fed. Forgiven, mostly, by the only judge whose opinion counted.

"This is a gift," Ada said, "and gifts are the only currency we honor here. Are you sure?"

"I've never been sure of anything in my life," the traveler said. "That's how I know."

So Ada taught him — right there, in front of the class, turning the lesson into the lesson — how to open his hands without opening his veins, how to give so the giving leaves the giver richer. The memory rose from the cloth like steam from bread, and settled into the chapel's air, available now to anyone who needed to remember that kitchens existed, that songs existed, that someone, somewhere, had loved a child enough to sing to her over loaves.

When it was done, the traveler swayed — and then laughed, actually laughed, wiping his eyes. "It's still there," he marveled, hand on his chest. "She's still there. I gave it away and she's *still there*."

"That's the trick nobody tells you," Ada said. "Love multiplies when you stop guarding it."

The traveler stayed for supper, obviously. Sera's rule was that no one witnessed a gift-giving and then left unfed; she considered it canon law now, defrocked or not. Over cinnamon rolls he took out his fiddle, and it turned out he knew a bread-song — a variant, distant, but a cousin of the grandmother's tune — and the chapel filled with music and children and the smell of cinnamon, and outside the snow came down soft as forgiveness on a country that had finally, officially, begun.

Later, when the benches were cleared and Wren had fallen asleep mid-argument about whether geese could be ordained, Ada stepped out into the blue dark. The Door was visible from the chapel steps, a seam of gold light in the black shoulder of the mountain, steady as a heartbeat.

Ashen was already there, of course. Gods and cats share a genius for appearing where warmth is.

"One traveler," the god said. "One gift. Word will spread, you know. That's how it started last time, too — one story walking down one road. Before you know it, they'll all come. The hollow ones. The erased. The ones with nothing left to steal and everything to give."

"Good," Ada said.

"They'll need a teacher."

"They have one."

"They'll need a keeper of the door."

"They have twenty-two."

Ashen was quiet a moment. Snowflakes passed straight through him and came out the other side, undisturbed, which struck Ada as a fairly accurate portrait of most divine intervention.

"There's a rider on the south road," the god said at last, and his voice had changed — gone careful, the way it did when he was choosing words the way Sera chose ink. "Traveling fast. Flying the colors of the Crown."

Ada went cold. "Hunters?"

"No. One rider. Alone. And Ada — the horse is Vael-bred. From *before*." He turned his smoky eyes up to hers. "Whoever it is has ridden a very long way to reach a country that doesn't exist. And they're carrying something wrapped in oilcloth that hums like a piece of the sigil."

Ada looked down the dark road, where far off — impossibly far off, or maybe not — she could just make out the sound of hooves.

"Well," she said, pulling her coat tighter, "I suppose we'd better put the kettle on."
