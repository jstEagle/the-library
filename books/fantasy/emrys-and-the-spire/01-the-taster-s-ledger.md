## 1. The Taster's Ledger

There are two ways to survive a poisoned court, and I have mastered both. The first is to be indispensable. The second is to be forgettable.

I am the king's taster, which sounds like an honor until you understand what the work actually is: I eat, and if I don't die, the king eats too. Twenty-two years old and I have already survived hemlock in the honeyed pears, ground glass in the wedding feast soup — that one was sloppy; nobody grinds glass fine enough anymore — and something in the winter wine that turned my tongue black for three days and taught me the taste of my own blood by heart. Each dish leaves its mark. Not on my body, mostly. My body has been built into a fortress, dish by dish, dose by dose, a pharmacy of small poisons metabolized into armor. No, the marks are elsewhere. In the ledger.

But we'll come to the ledger.

The morning of the night everything changed began, as mornings do in the palace of Vael Corren, with porridge. I tasted it. Almond, cream, a whisper of nutmeg, and beneath it all the faint metallic ghost of arsenic so diluted it was less a poison than an apology. Someone in the kitchens had gotten careless or hopeful. I swallowed anyway, held up my hand for the steward, and said, "It's fine," because it was, for me, and because saying otherwise would mean a dead cook and a day of interrogations, and I had ledgers to balance.

That's the other thing about me. By day I am Lena Halloran, taster, a small woman with a small appetite and a remarkable constitution. By night I am the Keeper of Accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, which is exactly as grand and exactly as shabby as it sounds. We meet in the back room of a chandler's shop that smells perpetually of tallow and secrets, and we keep books that no honest kingdom would recognize. Our entries are not coin. They are years. Names. Borrowed luck. A miller's daughter traded seven winters of her life for her father's recovery from fever. A soldier sold his mother's name to forget why he deserted. A whole village once pooled their memories of a single summer to buy themselves through a famine, and none of them remember the lake now, or the swimming, or the boy who drowned in it, though they still leave flowers on the shore every midsummer and cannot say why.

I record all of it. Debits and credits, realm against realm, mortal against Spire. Because there is a Spire — a frozen tower at the roof of the world where memory is minted like currency — and there is a covenant, older than any guild, older than any crown, that binds the mortal realm to it. And the covenant, whatever the elders say at their meetings full of coughing and candle smoke, is not free. Nothing is ever free. I know this better than anyone alive, because I am the only person who has read every line of every book.

* * *

That evening, the chandler's shop, the back room, the lamp turned low. I had the third ledger open — the one covering debts of names — and I was reconciling a column when I noticed the prophecy book sitting where it should not have been.

Understand: the Book of the Covenant, the old prophecy text, predates the guild itself. It lives in the elders' vault behind three locks and a man named Ostrec whose entire personality is disapproval. It does not sit on my worktable between the ink pot and a half-eaten bun. Yet there it was, bound in grey leather gone soft as moth wing, its pages the color of old teeth.

I should have called for the elders. Instead — because curiosity has always been my most expensive habit — I opened it.

The prophecies were written in a hand that shifted across centuries, ink fading and sharpening like weather. Most of it was the usual fog: *a tower shall stand, a debt shall stand, a bearer shall come.* Prophecy is just accounting done badly, I've always thought. But then I found the page with dates, actual dates, laid out in a column like a merchant's calendar, and my eye went down it the way your eye goes down a list looking for your own name —

And stopped.

Tonight's date. Inked a hundred years ago, give or take. And beside it, in letters that seemed darker than the rest, as if the page had been saving up its blackness for this one line:

*The Halloran girl balances the books. The Covenant-Bearer pays.*

My first thought, absurdly, was: *someone has forged this.* My second thought was colder: *no forger would choose me.* A forged prophecy wants a hero, someone with a sword arm and a tragic jawline. It does not want a woman who tastes soup for a living and keeps double-entry accounts for a guild of memory-thieves.

My third thought was the truest one: *the elders put this here tonight, on purpose, for me to find.* Which meant they wanted me to know. Which meant they wanted something from me. And the guild wanting something from you is never the beginning of good news.

I closed the book. I hid my ledgers — all three, wrapped in oilcloth and shoved behind the loose brick where I keep the things that keep me alive — and I had just pressed the brick home when the temperature of the room dropped.

Not cooled. *Dropped*. The way a well drops. The lamp flame stood still and went blue at its heart, and frost feathered up the inside of the window glass in patterns like script, like handwriting, like an entry being made in some vast cold book. The pantry door — the chandler kept a pantry off the back room, stocked with tallow and dried apples — swung open without a hand touching it, and out of its dark came air that smelled of nothing at all, which is the smell of deep winter, of places where even scent has frozen.

He stepped through. I want to say he stepped out of the pantry, but that isn't honest. He stepped out of the *cold*, wearing the pantry like a coat he'd put on for the occasion. He was tall in the way that architecture is tall, robed in grey-white, and his face was the kind you can't hold onto — not blurred, just unmemorable, the way the village couldn't hold onto its summer. Frost bloomed and died along his shoulders with each breath he didn't take.

"Lena Halloran," he said. His voice was quiet and absolute, like ice settling on a lake. "Keeper of Accounts."

"Guild business hours ended at sundown," I said, because terror makes me flippant, which is a flaw I've decided to reframe as a feature. "If you have a complaint about an entry, you'll need to file it in writing."

Something moved across the unface — I will call it amusement, because the alternative was worse. "You found the book."

"I found a book. Property of the guild. You're trespassing."

"I am the Warden of the Spire." He said it the way you'd say *I am the tide*. "And the covenant is broken, taster. Has been breaking for a century while your elders fed it scraps — sold memories, bought winters, postponed the reckoning. The final frost is coming. Weeks, perhaps days. Unless the Covenant-Bearer pays what the prophecy demands."

"And what does it demand?"

"You already read it."

*I balance the books,* I thought. *The Bearer pays.* Every survival instinct I owned — and I owned many; I'd eaten poison for a living since I was fourteen — told me to run, to bargain, to grovel. Instead I heard myself say, "Then you'll want my ledgers. All of them. Because if anyone knows what both realms actually owe each other — every year taken, every name sold, every drop of borrowed luck — it isn't your prophecy. It isn't your Spire. It's me."

The frost on the window stilled, as if the whole room were listening.

"The elders sent you to collect a sacrifice," I said, and my voice didn't shake, which surprised us both. "You're going to get an audit instead."

The Warden regarded me for a long moment. Then he extended one grey hand toward the pantry dark, and the cold beyond it opened like a door onto the roof of the world.

"After you, Keeper," he said. "Bring your books."

I took the oilcloth bundle from behind the brick. I blew out the lamp. And I walked into the winter, telling myself the whole way that dread was just resolve that hadn't found its footing yet.

Behind me, faintly, I could have sworn the prophecy book turned its own page.
