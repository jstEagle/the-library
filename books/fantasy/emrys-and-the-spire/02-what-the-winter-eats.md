## 2. What the Winter Eats

The Spire did not have stairs so much as it had opinions about elevation. The warden led me up a spiral of black stone that creaked like an old ship deciding whether to sink, and I followed, because the alternative was staying in the pantry with the cold that had learned to walk.

"You're taking this remarkably well," the warden said. He had a voice like a ledger being closed — dry, final, faintly satisfied. Up close, in the light of the frost-lamps he carried, I could see he was not entirely a man. His edges had a certain arithmetic to them, as if someone had calculated a person and gotten most of the way there before running out of patience.

"I'm a food-taster," I said. "I've swallowed worse than this evening has offered."

"Have you?" He glanced back at me, and his eyes were the color of deep ice over deep water. "You've swallowed thirty-one years of poison in homeopathic doses. You've swallowed the king's secrets every time you nodded at his table. And you've swallowed—" he tapped the prophecy book under my arm, which I had grabbed on pure instinct and now clutched like a stolen loaf, "—the truth of what your guild does with its nights."

I said nothing. In my experience, silence is the cheapest dish on any menu, and often the most nourishing.

The spiral ended at a door made of something that was neither wood nor metal but memory given weight — I recognized the grain of it, somehow, the way you recognize a face from a dream you've decided not to think about. The warden pressed his palm flat against it, and the door sighed open onto cold.

Not pantry-cold. Not winter-cold. This was the cold of *before*, the cold the world had been keeping at bay since anyone bothered to keep anything.

"This," the warden said, "is what the winter eats."

* * *

The chamber beyond was vast and white and full of shelves.

I want to say they were shelves of books, because that would have been tidy, and I have a professional weakness for tidy. But they were shelves of *jars*. Thousands upon thousands of them, each one frosted glass, each one holding a curl of pale light that moved when I looked at it — turned toward me, almost, the way a sleeper turns toward a candle.

"Memories," the warden said. "Harvested. Voluntary, mostly. The covenant requires payment, and for nine hundred years the payment has been remembering. A village surrenders its founding day. A family gives up the name of its firstborn daughter. A king forgets why he started a war, which everyone agrees was a bargain." He walked between the shelves, trailing one calculated finger along the glass. "Each jar feeds the frost a little longer. Each forgetting buys a season. That is the covenant, Mistress Halloran: the realm pays its past to postpone its ending."

"And my guild," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended, "collects the payments."

"Your guild *brokers* them. There's a difference, though I grant it's the difference between the knife and the hand that holds it." He stopped before a shelf near the chamber's heart, where the jars were older, the light inside them dimmer, browned like old paper. "But lately the elders have grown... enterprising. They've begun selling."

"Selling memories." I looked at him. "To whom? There's no one to sell to. We're the only realm that—"

"There are other realms. Frozen ones. Hungry ones. Realms that traded away their own pasts centuries ago and have been starving ever since, and would pay *anything* — years, luck, names, bloodlines — for a jar of someone else's yesterday." His finger rested on one of the old jars, and the light inside shrank from him. "Your guild has been selling the realm's history by the pound to keep the final frost off another decade. Every harvest festival forgotten. Every hero unnamed. Every grandmother's lullaby liquefied and decanted and shipped north to powers you will never meet."

I stood very still, the way you do when the floor turns out to be thinner than advertised.

Because here is the thing about being a ledger-keeper: you know what numbers look like when they're lying. For eleven years I had balanced the Guild of Impossible Things' books, and I had noticed the entries that didn't parse — the recurring debits marked only as *north*, the credits arriving in currencies we weren't supposed to deal in. I'd assumed clerical laziness. Elders are lazy the way glaciers are lazy: slowly, and then all at once, and everything under them is gone.

"The ledgers," I said slowly. "My ledgers. They record every transaction. Both sides. What the realm paid out, and what came back in trade."

"They do." The warden turned to face me fully, and for the first time he looked less like a calculation and more like a man who had been waiting a very long time for a delivery. "Nine hundred years of covenant, and no complete record of it exists anywhere but in your hand. The guild kept the sales secret even from itself. But you, Mistress Halloran — you wrote down everything. Every debt. Every credit. Every entry marked *north*."

"Why?" I asked, and it wasn't really a question, more a door I was opening to see what fell out. "Why show me this? Why not just take the prophecy book and be done with me?"

"Because the covenant is broken," the warden said, "and broken covenants require rebalancing, and rebalancing requires an auditor." Something moved behind his frozen eyes — I would have called it hope, if hope were a thing that survived at this altitude. "And because the book under your arm says you're the one who carries it."

* * *

I heard the footsteps before I saw him, and I knew them the way you know a creak in your own staircase.

Corvin Ash. Eldest scrivener of the Guild of Impossible Things. The man who taught me double-entry, who told me once, warming his hands over the guildhall fire, that a ledger was the only honest prayer a person could offer. Seventy years old, ink-stained to the elbow, kind as bread.

He stood in the doorway of the memory-vault with a stiletto held the way a man holds a pen — comfortably, familiarly, like something he'd been using all his life.

"Lena," he said gently. "You shouldn't have read the book."

"Corvin." My voice did not shake. Eleven years of tasting for a paranoid king teaches you that much. "How long?"

"How long have the elders known what you are?" He stepped into the vault, and the jar-light bent away from him too, the same as it bent from the warden. "Since the night you were hired. Why do you think a gutter orphan with quick hands got a position touching the king's plate? You were never staff, child. You were *collateral*. The prophecy needed a Covenant-Bearer raised inside the guild, loyal enough to stay, honest enough to keep perfect books." He smiled, and it was the smile he'd worn over every lesson, every supper, every kindness, and it broke something in me that I would later need to sweep up and carry anyway. "We grew you like a crop, dear. And crops don't get to audit the farm."

Behind me, the warden went very still, in the manner of a man watching weather approach.

"The elders sent you," I said.

"The elders sent me to retrieve the prophecy book and close the account." Corvin's tone stayed gentle, grandfatherly, terrible. "A closed account can't be audited, Lena. You understand. It's nothing personal. It's *bookkeeping*."

And that — that word, that soft, filthy, familiar word — did what fear could not. Fear had been sitting in my chest since the pantry, a swallowed dose I knew the antidote for. But betrayal is a different poison. Betrayal has no immunity you can build, dose by dose, over thirty-one years.

I felt it curdle, hot and fast, into something with teeth.

"You taught me the first rule," I said, backing toward the far archway, the one the warden had not come through, the one breathing colder air from somewhere deeper in the tower. "*Never trust a number you didn't watch being made.*"

"Lena—"

"So tell me, Corvin." I lifted the prophecy book, and the frost-lamps caught its ancient cover, and somewhere above us the whole Spire groaned like a ship changing its mind. "Who watched these numbers being made? Because I did. Eleven years. Every entry. Every debt both ways. And if the elders burn me and my ledgers tonight, then nobody alive knows what this realm actually *owes* — and a covenant without its accounts isn't a covenant. It's just a debt collection with delusions."

The stiletto did not waver. But Corvin Ash hesitated, one heartbeat, two — the hesitation of a scrivener confronting an unbalanced column — and I have built a life on other people's half-heartbeats.

I ran.

Deeper into the Spire, past the vaults, past shelves of other people's yesterdays, down through cold that bit like a debt collector, with the prophecy book crushed against my ribs and Corvin's footsteps falling in behind me, patient as interest accruing.

The archway ahead opened onto a chamber where the air itself seemed to wait — where, I would swear even now, I heard the sound of a great scale settling, deciding, weighing.

Whatever was at the heart of the covenant, I was about to meet it.

And I intended to arrive holding the only complete set of books in either realm.
