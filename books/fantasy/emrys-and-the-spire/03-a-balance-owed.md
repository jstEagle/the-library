## 3. A Balance Owed

The heart of the Spire was a room that had been broken on purpose.

That was my first thought, standing on the threshold while the warden's lantern threw our shadows long across the floor. Someone had shattered the covenant-stone at the center — a great disc of ice-glass, big as a millwheel, cracked into nine uneven pieces like a pie nobody wanted — and then built the whole tower around the wreckage, the way you'd build a shrine around a grave. Or a prison around a debt.

"You're staring," the warden said.

"I'm auditing," I said. "It's what I do."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in some other century. He was older than I'd first taken him for, up close — or perhaps age worked differently here, where memory was coin and he'd spent so much of his own that his face had gone smooth as an unpaid bill. His eyes were the color of the frost on the pantry window the morning I'd found my name in the prophecy book: white, patient, and very cold.

"Sit, Covenant-Bearer."

"Don't call me that."

"It's what the book calls you." He gestured, and the prophecy book rose from under my arm — I hadn't let go of it since the guildsman tried to kill me two floors down, and I wasn't proud of how tightly I'd been clutching it, like a baker's daughter hugging the last loaf before a siege — and drifted to the center of the broken stone. It settled into one of the cracks as if it had always lived there.

I sat. The floor was ice that didn't chill me, which somehow frightened me more than if it had.

"Now," the warden said, folding himself down opposite me with the unhurried grace of something that had watched empires forget themselves, "let us discuss your death."

* * *

Here is what they don't put in the ballads about chosen ones: the choice is never actually offered to you. It's just described, slowly, with great ceremony, until you agree to it out of sheer exhaustion.

"The covenant was forged in blood," the warden said, one pale finger tracing the cracks in the stone. "Nine signatories. Nine debts. One winter, held at bay by what the realms surrendered willingly. But willing surrenders run dry, Mistress Halloran. People stop volunteering their memories when the memories are all they have left. So your guild found a more efficient arrangement." His smile was thin as ledger paper. "They stopped asking."

"I know," I said. "I balance their books. I've known for three years."

"Then you know why the book names you. A century of spring costs a life freely given at the covenant's heart. The last bearer gave hers four generations ago. The elders have stretched her payment ever since — selling scraps of the realm's past to buy weeks of thaw. But the account is overdrawn, Lena Halloran. Tonight the final frost begins. Unless—"

"Unless I die," I said. "Freely. At the heart. On the date already written down, because apparently prophecy can't be bothered to leave room for scheduling conflicts."

"Unless you die," he agreed, gently, as though offering me tea.

And here is the thing I want recorded somewhere, even if no one remembers it after tonight: I was afraid. Properly, stomach-wrenchingly afraid. My hands shook so badly I had to press them flat against my knees. Every survival instinct I'd spent ten years sharpening — every immunity I'd built dish by dish, poison by patient poison — screamed at me to run, to bargain, to grovel, to do anything but sit still on the ice while a dead-eyed warden told me the date of my funeral like it was a dinner reservation.

But I'd spent those same ten years reading ledgers. And when you read enough ledgers, you learn one truth above all others:

Nobody writes down the whole number. There is always a hidden column. Always.

"Before I decide anything," I said, "I want to see the covenant's books."

The warden went very still. "The covenant does not keep books."

"Everything keeps books," I said. "Most things just don't know it. Debts don't vanish because no one wrote them down; they just accrue interest in the dark. You've been feeding this winter for a thousand years. That's not charity, warden. That's credit. And credit has a shape." I pulled my own ledger from inside my coat — the real one, the one I'd hidden from the guildsman in the pantry, because a taster learns early that the second cup is always poisoned and the second ledger is always the honest one. "Three years of entries. Every year the guild sold, every name they took, every borrowed luck and traded season. Cross-referenced against the prophecy book's own record of what the realms paid willingly, back when they were still asked."

"And what will that prove?"

"That's the interesting part," I said, and opened the ledger across my knees. "Let's find out."

* * *

I won't pretend it was elegant work. My hands were still shaking. The light came from frost-lanterns that flickered whenever the tower shuddered, which it did more and more often, deep rumbles like a giant turning over in a sleep that was ending. Somewhere above us, snow was falling inward.

But numbers are numbers, even at the end of the world. Especially at the end of the world.

"Here." My finger stopped on a line. "Four hundred years ago, the kingdom of Vael surrendered the memory of its founding king — willingly, sealed in blood, per the original terms. In exchange, the covenant owed Vael a hundred years of guaranteed spring. The guild never paid it. They let Vael forget there was a debt at all. That's entry one: covenant owes the mortal realm, one century of spring, plus four hundred years' accrued interest."

The warden's stillness had changed quality. It was no longer the stillness of patience. It was the stillness of a man watching someone open a door he'd nailed shut.

"Entry two," I went on, louder, because the tower was rumbling again and I could hear — faintly, horribly — the creak of frost spreading through walls that had stood since before my grandmother's grandmother. "Two hundred years ago, the Spire took the name of every midwife in the Marches to hold back the famine-winter. Names are collateral, not payment. Collateral returns when the debt is settled. Nobody settled. Entry three: the realm owes the Spire nine hundred harvested memories, but the Spire owes the realm nine hundred returned names. Those cancel."

On and on. Line against line. Debt against debt. The warden watched, and did not speak, and the frost crept down the walls in fingers.

"There," I whispered at last, and my voice cracked on it. "There it is."

I turned the ledger toward him. Two columns, tallied and retallied three times because I did not trust my hands and I did not trust my eyes and I did not trust hope, which is the least auditable substance in any realm.

"They nearly cancel," I said. "Nearly. The realms owe each other almost exactly what they've paid. A thousand years of theft and grief and forgetting, and on paper — on *paper*, warden — it balances to within a rounding error." I laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "All this. All this horror. And the difference between eternal winter and eternal spring is a remainder small enough to fit in a teacup."

"Then the covenant could be reforged," the warden said slowly, "without a death."

"No." I closed the ledger. "Not without a death. Look at the remainder. It isn't zero. Something has to pay the difference — that's how covenants work, that's how *everything* works. Blood was never the requirement. Balance was. The old bearers died because the elders were too lazy or too frightened to audit, so they kept paying in the only currency they understood." I looked up at him. "Someone pays the difference. That's the price. That's the only price."

The tower screamed.

Not a rumble this time — a scream, ice shearing off stone somewhere far above, and through the crack in the wall behind the broken covenant-stone I saw it: the long winter itself, pressing its face against the Spire like a beggar at a window. White. Patient. Cold. Falling *inward* now, snow drifting down through the wound in the tower, settling on the nine shards of the covenant-stone, and where it landed, the shards dimmed.

"They're coming," the warden said, rising. "Your guild. The elders. They will not permit an audit."

"Then we'd best finish before they arrive." I stood too, legs unsteady, heart hammering, and heard — distantly, below — boots on stairs, many boots, and a voice I knew giving orders in the clipped tones of a man who had once taught me to tell arsenic from almond by smell alone. Master Corvane. Three years of suppers. Three years of "trust the ledger, girl, trust nothing else."

Trust the ledger. Fine. I would.

"Warden," I said, as the frost reached the edge of the stone and the boots reached the landing below, "what happens to whoever pays the difference?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Snow gathered in his hair and did not melt.

"They are written into the covenant," he said. "As the balancing entry. Their debts are forgiven. Their deaths are deferred. And everything they have ever survived—" his voice dropped, almost gentle, "—is surrendered to the tower. Every scar explained. Every flinch accounted for. Paid in full, and forgotten by the one who paid."

I thought of my ledger. Every entry in my hand. Every survival I'd recorded, dish by dish, night by night, because recording them was the only way I'd ever felt real.

"Then here is my price," Lena Halloran told the breaking heart of the world, as the elders' boots hit the final stair and the winter poured through the walls like a tide coming home. "I pay the difference. I sign myself in. But *my* terms, warden — not the book's. Not theirs. Mine. And my first term is this:"

I put my hand flat on the frozen covenant-stone, and the frost crawled up my wrist like ink recognizing its page.

"—the audit stands. Forever. No elder, no warden, no god sells another memory without a signed, balanced entry in a ledger anyone may read. The age of secret columns ends *tonight*."

Below, the door burst open. Corvane's voice, sharp with fury: "*Girl, step away from the stone!*"

The warden extended his hand, and in it, unsheathed from the air itself, was a pen.

"Terms accepted," he said, "pending signature."

I took the pen.
