## 4. Spring, Unrecorded

The quill was heavier than any I'd ever held, and I have held quills that weighed a man's sins.

"Sign here," the warden said, and there was something almost gentle in the frost of his voice. "And here. And here." He turned the covenant's shattered heart toward me — a great wheel of ice and iron, every spoke a century of the realm's surrendered past — and indicated the empty space at its center where the balancing entry belonged.

I had spent my whole life reading other people's hands. Kings who signed away villages with a flourish. Guild elders who signed away centuries without looking up from their wine. My own hand, signing receipts for poison that never killed me because I'd built my immunity dish by dish, year by year, until my blood was a fortress and my memory was the only ledger no one had thought to audit.

Now I read my own name in the prophecy book one last time. *Lena Halloran, Covenant-Bearer.* The ink was still wet after a hundred years, which should have told me everything about how much the Spire respected finality.

"You understand what you're surrendering," the warden said.

"Every survival," I said. "Every dish I didn't die of. Every night I balanced the books while the elders sold the realm's childhood to keep the snow polite." I looked at the wheel. "You know what's strange? I'm not afraid of losing the memories. I'm afraid of becoming someone who doesn't know why she flinches."

"That is the price of all accounting," the warden said. "To balance a column, you must erase it."

"Well." I dipped the quill. "I've always been better at closing books than keeping them."

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about signing yourself into a covenant: the ink goes in deep.

I wrote my name at the center of the wheel, and then — because I am, whatever else I am, a thorough bookkeeper — I wrote the entry itself. Not *sacrifice*, which is what the prophecy wanted, and not *savior*, which is what the elders feared. I wrote:

*Received of Lena Halloran: one immunity, complete. One hundred and forty-one survivals, itemized. One ledger, closed. In exchange: one spring, perpetual. Debts between realms marked PAID IN FULL.*

The guildsman who had come to silence me stood at the edge of the chamber with murder in his face and frost on his boots, and he watched me write, and for the first time in his long, cold career, he had nothing to say. You cannot argue with a balanced column. It's the one argument in the world that ends itself.

The elders arrived too late, as elders do. They came down the stair with their rings and their seals and their faces full of the particular outrage of men whose theft has been itemized, and they found me already signing, and the winter already breaking.

Because here is the thing about the long winter: it was never weather. It was an unpaid balance. Every flake that fell on the mortal realm was a memory the Spire had bought and not yet digested, falling back to earth as frost because nothing — nothing — stays bought forever. And when I signed, when the columns met and cancelled, the winter simply ran out of reasons.

I felt it go. Not like warmth. Like arithmetic. Somewhere above us, mid-fall, a thousand snowflakes stopped being owed and started being free, and hung in the air like a held breath, and then did not fall at all.

The Spire began to thaw around me, and this is the part I will try to keep even if I keep nothing else: the walls wept names. A hundred years of purchased past came home, and the ice gave them up gently, one by one — a fisherman's daughter's laugh, the smell of a bakery that burned before I was born, somebody's mother singing off-key in a language nobody speaks anymore. The tower filled with returned things the way a granary fills with grain, and the warden stood in the middle of it all with his frost melting off his shoulders like a man shrugging off a coat he'd worn too long.

"The garden will need a gardener," he said.

"You're looking at the last entry," I told him. "I don't survive this one. That's rather the point."

"No," the warden said, and for the first time in a century, he smiled, and it was terrible and kind. "That is rather the *balance*. You'll find the difference."

* * *

I woke in the palace kitchens with flour on my hands and no idea how it got there.

This is unremarkable, if you are Lena Halloran, royal food-taster. Flour happens. Kitchens happen. What was remarkable was the taste in my mouth — copper and ink and something green, like the first day of a season that has been postponed for a hundred years — and the way the under-cook was staring out the window with his mouth open.

"It stopped," he kept saying. "The snow. Mid-air. It just — it stopped, and now it's *raining light*—"

I went to the window. Outside, the palace gardens were drowning in blossom. Fruit trees that hadn't fruited in living memory were bent double with it. And everywhere, everywhere, people were standing in the streets saying names out loud — old names, dead names, names they'd lost and were finding again like coins behind cushions — and weeping, and laughing, and mostly both.

I felt nothing. That's not quite true. I felt peace, enormous and unearned-seeming, the way you feel when a fever breaks. But underneath it, small and sharp as a pin:

The taster's tray came, and I did my work. First dish: almond custard, the king's favorite. I took my spoonful and my body *flinched* — a full-body recoil, spine to fingertips, before my mind caught up and found no reason for it. No reason. Just a flinch, and a ghost of a thought shaped like a number: *one hundred and forty-one.*

Second dish: honeyed quail. Flinch. Third: a soup of white beans and rosemary that made my eyes sting for no reason I could name, though some part of me, far away, seemed to be counting.

I am a taster. I know poisons by their signatures. These dishes were clean. Whatever I was tasting, it wasn't arsenic. It was absence. It was a ledger I couldn't read, written in a hand I almost recognized, and my body remembered every line of it even though I did not.

There was no ledger under my pillow that night. There was no ledger anywhere. I checked, the way you check for a tooth that's been pulled. Nothing. Just smooth sheets and a window full of impossible spring and a question I carried to sleep like a stone:

*If the debts are paid, why does my heart still keep accounts?*

* * *

Far away — farther than far, in a tower that was no longer frozen but was still very good at keeping secrets — the warden walked the thawing halls with a blank book under his arm.

It had appeared at midnight on the covenant's heart, where the wheel of ice and iron was now a wheel of flowering thorn. It was bound in green leather that had never been any animal, and its pages were empty, and it was warm.

He opened it to the first page and watched the ink rise through the paper like water through roots, letter by letter, in a hand he knew intimately — a careful, wry, unkillable hand, a bookkeeper's hand, though the woman who owned it would swear she'd never held a pen in her life that mattered.

The first line wrote itself:

*Being a true and complete record of the debts still outstanding between the realms, begun this first day of spring, by the hand of L.H., who remembers nothing and counts everything.*

The warden read it twice. Then he laughed — a sound the Spire had not heard since before the frost — and sat down on a bench beneath a thorn tree in full, impossible bloom, to see what she would find next.

Because the winter was over. But the audit, the audit had only just begun.
