## 1. The Ledger of Unpaid Debts

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in a cellar beneath the Saltmarket, where the air tasted of tallow and old rain, and the ledgers were bound in leather so worn it had gone the color of forgotten things. Odile Delacroix worked by lamplight, as he always did, with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his quill moving in small, precise strokes. He was good at this work. He was, in fact, the only one who could do it, because the work required a man who did not exist.

He had spent twenty years making himself into an absence. No birth record survived him; he had eaten that one himself, folding the memory of his mother's face into a paper boat and burning it on the canal. No debt bore his name, no portrait hung in any hall, no lover kept a lock of his hair. When clerks looked at him their eyes slid off like rain off oiled cloth. It was the finest theft he had ever committed, and he had committed it against himself, entry by entry, until what remained was a ledger-keeper with no line of provenance—a sum with nothing to sum from.

That night the ledgers were wrong.

He noticed it the way a musician notices a false note: not by looking for it but by flinching. Column forty-one, the inventory of held memories—each entry a small capsule of glass and wax, catalogued by donor, by weight of feeling, by asking price. The guild did not sell happiness, whatever the pamphlets said. It sold *specificity*: a soldier's last morning with his brother, a baker's memory of her mother's hands kneading dough, the exact blue of a sky over a wedding in Vell. People sold their worst memories mostly, and their best ones when they were desperate enough, and the guild kept them safe in the dark like a bank keeps gold, because a memory removed is not destroyed. It is only waiting.

Column forty-one should have held nine hundred and six entries.

It held eight hundred and ninety-one.

Odile set down his quill. He checked the seals on the cabinet doors—intact. He checked the warding chalk beneath the shelves—unbroken. He ran his finger down the column again, slower, and found the gaps: fifteen entries, gone not torn out but *unwritten*, the way a word leaves a page when you breathe on wet ink. And where each vanished entry had been, there remained a payment notation in ink the color of dried blood:

*Received: one answered prayer.*

He knew every hand in the guild's books. This was not one of them. The script was old-fashioned, ceremonial, the letters drawn as if by someone who had learned to write before writing became common—each stroke a small act of devotion. Payments arriving as answered prayers. Fifteen of them. Someone was buying memories out of the guild's stockpile without entering the building, without breaking a single seal, and paying in miracles.

Odile sat very still for a long time. In the streets above, the evening bells rang for Vespers, and somewhere a child was praying for rain, or a grandmother for her son's safe return—and Odile thought, with a coldness that started in his fingertips, about what the old gods had been doing these past three years, now that they had begun answering again.

*Each miracle costs someone something.* That was the oldest arithmetic in the world. He had simply never imagined the gods keeping their accounts in *his* cellar.

* * *

The client came at midnight, which was the hour for clients who could not afford to be seen wanting things.

She was carried up from the street in a chair because she could no longer manage the stairs alone, and Odile met her in the receiving room with two candles lit, because the dying preferred candlelight—it made the shadows honest. Her name was Maren Ostrevant, though she would forget it soon; the physicians had given her a season, and the wasting sickness had already begun its polite burglary, taking rooms in her mind one at a time.

"I want to buy something back," she said. Her voice was dry paper. "You have it. I know you have it. Entry four-forty."

Odile did not consult the ledger. He remembered it: *Maren Ostrevant, aged nineteen, her wedding day entire—the bells, the rain on the awnings, her husband's hand shaking as he pinned the rose.* She had sold it eleven years ago to pay for her daughter's fever-cure, and she had never once regretted it until the sickness came to take everything else.

"Restitution of a sold memory carries a fee," Odile said gently. "And restoration to a failing mind can be—" He searched for the word that wasn't *cruel*. "—difficult."

"My husband died in the spring." She said it the way people say things they have rehearsed against weeping. "I sat with him at the end and I could not remember our wedding. Everyone else remembered it for me. They told me about the rain, about the rose, about how I laughed when the priest dropped the ring." Her hands twisted in the blanket. "I want to die owning my own life. Name your price. I'll pay anything that isn't memory. I haven't any left worth the selling."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then he went down to the cellar, took capsule four-forty from its cradle of velvet and ash, and carried it up without entering the fee in the book. Some debts, he had decided twenty years ago, were better left unrecorded.

Maren held the capsule in both palms like a bird. "How does it work?"

"You press it to your brow and think of the door you locked it behind. The rest finds its own way home."

She pressed it to her brow. She closed her eyes. And she began, softly, to pray—not to any god Odile knew, just the shapeless muttering of the frightened, *please, please, let me have it back, let me remember him, please*—

The capsule cracked.

Not broke—*cracked*, the way ice cracks on a pond when something beneath it moves, and the light inside it did not flow into Maren's brow but *drained upward*, through the ceiling, through the roof, gone, and Maren's eyes flew open and they were not her eyes anymore, they were windows with nothing behind the glass.

"No," Odile said. "No—"

But he had seen this before, three nights ago, in the ledger. *Received: one answered prayer.* The prayer had been answered. The memory had been collected. Not returned to Maren—*taken*, paid upward to whatever power had heard her, and the price of the miracle was the memory itself, and the memory had belonged, all along, to the guild's stockpile, to the vault, to the fifteen entries unwritten in column forty-one.

Maren blinked. She looked at Odile with perfect courtesy, the courtesy of a stranger.

"I'm sorry," she said pleasantly. "Do I know you? There was—I was going to ask for something. Wasn't I? It seems so important that I—" Her brow furrowed, then smoothed. Already the sickness had more room. Already the empty places were joining hands. "What a nice room," she said. "Is it late? I feel as if I've forgotten something terribly important."

Her daughter arrived within the hour to fetch her home, and Odile stood in the doorway watching the chair go down the street, carrying a woman who had begged to die owning her own life and would instead die owning almost none of it.

* * *

He did not sleep. He went back down to the cellar and pulled the payment notations out of the ledger and held them to the lamp, and that was when he saw the seal.

It was pressed faintly into the margin of each entry, so light it might have been breath: a tower split by a sword, crowned with thorns. Odile knew it the way everyone in the free cities had come to know it, from proclamations nailed to church doors and coins stamped with a new face. The mark of Aurel Vance, the Usurper, who had marched out of the east with an army that grew faster than it could be fed, who called himself the gods' own steward, who had shattered the covenant-stone at Corvane two winters past and told the world it was liberation.

The usurper was buying from the guild's vault. Without entering. Without sealing. Paying in answered prayers, feeding the stolen memories of the free cities upward to the old gods like grain into a mill, and the gods ground it fine and gave back armies.

Odile set the ledger down. His hands were steady, because he had trained them to be, but behind the steadiness something was screaming—the part of him that had spent twenty years learning exactly how records worked, exactly how theft worked, exactly how a thing could be taken from a sealed room without a single broken seal. He knew this crime. He had invented half its techniques. And there was exactly one person alive who could read the trail, because there was exactly one person alive who understood what it meant to be erased from every book.

They think no one watches the watchmen. They think absence cannot audit itself.

He blew out the lamps. Above him, faintly, through the floorboards, he could hear the city praying—hundreds of voices, thousands, rising into the dark like smoke—and somewhere in the arithmetic of heaven, each prayer was finding its price, and the price was being drawn, entry by entry, from the cellar beneath his feet.

Fifteen entries tonight. Tomorrow there would be more. And when the vault ran dry, the gods would look elsewhere for their bread—to the living, to the unwritten, to anyone whose memories were not yet claimed.

Odile took his coat from its hook, and the ledger, and a blank book to keep the new account in. There was a ferryman who worked the night crossings of the river Sere, a woman the god-touched called Sefa, who remembered every soul who ever rode her boat and could not remember her own. If anyone knew what the river had seen these past three years—what had been carried downstream toward Corvane, and what had been carried back—it would be her.

He locked the cellar behind him and stepped into the rain, a man with no name going to find out who had been spending his life's work like small coin.

Behind him, in the dark of the vault, another capsule cracked—softly, like ice, like a knuckle, like something patient beginning to count.
