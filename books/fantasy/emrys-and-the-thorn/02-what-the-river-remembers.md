## 2. What the River Remembers

The river called Vell had a smell Odile trusted: mud and rust and old fish, the honest stink of things that did not pretend to be anything else. He stood on the ferry dock at Grayhollow with his ledger strapped to his back like a coffin he'd decided to carry himself, watching the water move. Water was good. Water kept no records.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

The voice came from behind him, low and amused. The ferrywoman leaned against her pole, a broad-shouldered woman in a coat patched with river-clay, her hair bound in grey cord. Her eyes were the problem. They were the color of shallow water over stone, and they looked at him the way no one had looked at him in twenty years—like they saw him. All of him. Every erased entry.

"Odile Delacroix," she said, and his skin crawled at the sound of his own name spoken by a stranger. "Memory-thief. Ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. You paid passage once before, eleven years ago, going the other direction. You tipped badly."

"I don't know you," he said carefully.

"No one does." She shrugged, unbothered, and pushed off from the dock. "I'm Sefa. I remember everyone. It's my curse, or my job—the gods never told me which. Come aboard if you're going downriver. Everyone hollow-eyed is going downriver lately."

He should have taken the road instead. Roads were anonymous; rivers remembered. But roads were slow, and the ledger under his arm was bleeding entries faster every hour. Last night three more pages had gone blank mid-sentence, the ink lifting off the vellum like breath off glass, and where the memories went, prayers were being answered. He'd traced the payments to the Vell. Downriver, then. Toward the free cities. Toward everything the usurper wanted.

He stepped aboard.

* * *

The first hollowed village announced itself by its silence.

Bellwether had been a market town once—you could see it in the bones of the place, the wide square, the empty stalls built for noise. Now the noise was gone. People moved through the streets doing small necessary things, drawing water, stacking wood, but they did them the way sleepwalkers do, faces smooth as unwritten paper. In the square an old man sat on a fountain's edge, smiling faintly at nothing, and when Sefa crouched before him and asked his name, he opened his mouth and produced nothing. Not confusion. Nothing. A page torn out cleanly.

"His wife died last winter," said a woman in a doorway, and she was one of the few whose eyes still had anyone home behind them. "He prayed. Just once, just—*let me not grieve*. And the gods answered." She laughed, a terrible small sound. "Everyone here prayed for something. Rain for the crops. A child's fever broken. An end to loneliness. And we got all of it, and we lost—" She gestured vaguely at her own head, at the whole town. "I can't remember my son's face. I remember that I loved him. I don't remember him."

Odile took out the ledger and made a note, because making notes was how he kept the world from dissolving. *Bellwether. Population drained: near-total. Payment received: answered prayers, aggregate value incalculable.* His hand shook on the last line. He knew this arithmetic. This was guild arithmetic. Someone was spending the stockpile—the vault of stolen memories he had spent his life curating—and spending it lavishly, wastefully, the way only someone who had never paid for anything spends.

"The usurper," Sefa said quietly, reading over his shoulder without shame. "They say his armies answer to him absolutely. That his soldiers love him. That his cities never starve." She spat into the river. "Now I know what the love costs."

"It isn't his to spend," Odile said, and heard his own voice go cold and flat, the ledger-keeper's voice. "Every memory in that vault was stolen. By us. By me. And now he's stolen the theft, and he's paying gods with it, and the gods are—" He stopped. The thought arrived whole and awful: the old gods had gone silent centuries ago because the covenant broke. Now they answered every prayer. Now they were fat again. *Someone fed them.*

Sefa watched him work through it, and something in her expression softened—not pity; he would have thrown himself in the river before accepting pity. Recognition, maybe. "You keep accounts of other people's losses," she said. "Who keeps yours?"

"No one. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

It was two days later, moored at night in the reeds below a shrine-town called Candlemere, that the ferryman's ghost told them about the stone.

Ghosts were common on the Vell—drowned boatmen, mostly, harmless, repetitive—but this one had been a priest before the river took him, and priests remembered doctrine even after death. He stood at the bow dripping spectral water and spoke in the cadence of a man reciting scripture he no longer believed.

"The Covenant-Stone," he said. "Set at the Ford of Oaths, upriver past the falls. Gods on one side, mortals on the other, and the stone between, so that neither could take from the other without giving. Prayer flowed one way. Blessing flowed back. Balanced. *Balanced*," he repeated, wistful. "Then the man with the honest price came, and he broke the stone, and he said devotion was just another transaction, and the gods—who had waited so long to be paid anything at all—believed him."

Odile felt the ledger grow heavy against his spine. "Where are the pieces?"

"The shards hold what passed through the stone. Every prayer ever answered. Every blessing ever given." The ghost smiled with drowned teeth. "And your guild's vault, thief. Your stolen stockpile, poured into the cracks like mortar. He didn't hide the memories. He *built with them*."

The reeds whispered. Somewhere across the black water, a bell rang in Candlemere—a prayer-bell, answered instantly, and Odile watched a fisherman on the far bank stand up straighter, lighter, emptier, and walk into his hut whistling a tune he'd no longer know tomorrow.

"He's mustering an army," Odile said. "Paid in advance. If I don't take the shards back before the muster ends—"

"You'll what?" Sefa was leaning against the mast, arms crossed, watching him with those unbearable clear eyes. "Walk into the usurper's vault alone? You're a ledger-keeper. You file things. You erase yourself so thoroughly you flinch when I say your name."

"I flinch because it works," he snapped. "Do you know what it cost to become no one? Twenty years. Every record, every witness, every memory of my own family—I paid it all to be safe. And it worked. No one found me. Until you."

"Yes," she said simply. "Until me."

"I don't want company. Company is a record. Company is a debt."

"Then call it passage," she said, and picked up her pole. "You've paid for the whole river. I don't put passengers off mid-current. It's bad for trade." She pushed them out into the dark water, and added, not looking at him, "Besides. I remember everyone and nothing remembers me. You're the first person in forty years who might understand what that costs. So no, Odile Delacroix. I am not leaving you. Get used to it."

He turned to the rail and watched the water, because the alternative was letting her see his face.

A family he hadn't chosen. That was how the thought arrived, uninvited, in his mother's accent—except he couldn't remember his mother's accent anymore, only that the thought wore one. He gripped the rail. Downriver, torchlight was gathering along both banks, more of it every mile: campfires, watchfires, the muster-fires of an army bought and paid for.

At dawn they rounded a bend and saw the falls ahead, white and thunderous, and above them, on the bluff, a fortress flying a banner Odile recognized from a hundred sealed ledgers.

The usurper's seal. The buyer's mark. The honest price.

The vault was directly ahead, and between Odile and it lay a waterfall, a garrison, and a ferrywoman who refused, with infuriating gentleness, to forget him.
