## 4. A Name Spoken Aloud

The covenant-site had been a temple once. Now it was a wound in the world—a ring of shattered white stone on a hill above the river, each fragment taller than a man, and between the fragments the air hung wrong, like a held breath that had been held for years. Odile could feel it pulling at him even before he climbed the last of the broken steps: that old familiar hunger, the sense of being *unwritten*, except here the unwriting was not his own work.

The shards of the covenant-stone clinked against his ribs inside the courier's satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as sins. Behind him, far downriver and closing, the usurper's vanguard moved along the trade road—a smear of torchlight in the blue hour before dawn, close enough now that he could hear the drums. Not war drums. Something older. The rhythm gods answered to.

Sefa stood at the ring's center, her ferryman's coat dark with river spray, her hands open at her sides as though she were waiting for passengers. She always looked like that, he'd realized. Like she was waiting for someone to arrive.

"They're two hours out," she said. "Maybe less."

"Then we work quickly."

He knelt at the ring's heart, where the stone's largest fragment lay half-buried, its broken face carved with the old script—*what is given is owed; what is owed is remembered*. He had spent twenty years making sure nothing about him was remembered. He had thought it freedom. Kneeling here, with the stolen memories of ten thousand strangers pressing against his back like a debt collector's hand, he understood at last that it had only been a smaller kind of vault.

"Tell me again what you're going to do," Sefa said. "Slowly. I want to hear you say it."

"I'm going to give them back." He unwound the oilcloth. The shards caught the first grey light and threw it back wrong—each one veined with moving colour, with glimpses: a wedding dance, a mother's voice, a boy running through wheat, a hundred thousand small bright moments the usurper had bought and the gods had drunk. "Every memory. All of them. Into the stone."

"And yours?" Her voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "You said the covenant takes payment. Restitution flows both ways, you told me. To reforge it, something has to be *given*."

"The ledger balances," Odile said, "or it doesn't."

"That's not an answer, Odile."

"It's the only answer I have."

He set the shards into the hollow at the fragment's heart, one by one, and each one sank as if the stone were water. And with each one, he felt the pull strengthen—not on the satchel, but on him. On the sealed rooms inside his skull. The family he had erased to survive the purges: his mother's kitchen, his sister's laugh, his own name spoken by voices that loved it. He had locked those doors so long ago he'd forgotten where the keys were buried.

The gods knew where they were buried. The gods had been drinking from vaults like his for months.

"You could keep them," Sefa said quietly. "Your own. Put them in last, take everyone else's out first. No one would ever know. You're rather good at no one knowing."

Odile's hands stilled over the final shard.

That was the truth of it, laid bare in the cold morning air. He could restore the covenant and keep his erasure intact—walk away still invisible, still safe, still nothing. The ritual did not require his memories specifically. It required *a price*. Any price would do.

Except that wasn't quite true either, and he was, above all things, an honest accountant.

"The covenant binds realms together," he said. "It runs on memory because memory is what makes a promise worth keeping. If I put in stolen goods—if I put in anything less than everything I owe—" He shook his head. "The bargain would be rotten from the first day. The gods would find the flaw. They always find the flaw. I would have built them a door."

"So you give them *you*."

"I give them what I stole." He looked up at her. "Twenty years ago I took myself out of every record in the free cities. Every register, every census, every mind that held me—including my own. That was theft too, Sefa. From my mother. From my sister. From whoever I might have been if I'd stayed to be known." His throat tightened. "Restitution means putting it all back. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* the parts that hurt."

The drums were louder now. Below the hill, torchlight spilled onto the river road like molten brass.

Sefa crossed the broken stones and knelt beside him. She didn't touch him. She just stayed there, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, the way he imagined a hearth must feel to a man who had lived twenty years in cellars.

"Then I'll stay while you do it," she said. "Someone should witness. That's what I'm for. I remember everyone but myself—so let me remember this. Let me remember *you*, once, properly, before the stone takes it."

"Sefa—"

"Hush. You've balanced your ledgers. Let me balance mine."

* * *

He began.

There was no grand incantation. The covenant-stone had never needed words; it needed only honesty, which was rarer and more terrible. He opened the satchel wide and pressed both palms to the hollow, and the shards drank from him instead—from his craft, his guild-knowledge, the shape of every lock he'd ever picked—and reached outward through the broken ring, through the wounded air, into the vaults of the free cities.

He felt them go home.

A wedding day returned to a dying woman three cities downriver—he felt her gasp across the distance, felt her fill like an empty cup under a tap. A fisherman's drowned son, restored. A baker's first loaf. A soldier's reason to desert. Ten thousand small resurrections, pouring out through the stone, and with each one the ring grew brighter, the wrongness in the air easing like a fist unclenching.

And then the stone turned to him.

It did not ask. It simply opened him the way he had opened a thousand locks—patiently, professionally, without malice—and Odile stopped resisting, because resisting was just another way of keeping.

His mother's kitchen. The smell of cardamom and woodsmoke. His sister, seven years old, missing a tooth, laughing at something he'd said—something funny enough that he'd been proud of it for a week. His name, spoken softly across a table, spoken in anger down a hallway, spoken in prayer over a sickbed. *Odile Delacroix. Odile Delacroix. Odile.* Twenty years of deliberate darkness, and beneath it all, the unbearable ordinary gold of having been loved.

He wept without shame, giving it up. It went out of him like a tide going out, and where it had been was not emptiness but *room*—clean, swept, ready.

On the hill below, the drums faltered.

* * *

The gods withdrew the way a creditor withdraws when the debt is paid in full: instantly, completely, without gratitude. Odile felt their attention slide off the world like a hand lifted from a shoulder. Along the river road, the vanguard's rhythm broke. He heard it clearly from the heights—the drums stumbling, stopping, and then the sound he had prayed for without letting himself pray: an army unravelling. Mercenaries who had marched on promises of god-given victory suddenly marching on nothing. Captains shouting. Banners turning. The usurper's honest price coming due, and finding no buyer.

By full light, the road below was empty except for the abandoned cookfires and one discarded standard, its cloth dragging in the mud.

Sefa helped him stand. His legs shook. His head was quiet in a way it had never been—no sealed rooms, no hidden corridors, no ledger of himself kept in cipher. Just a man on a hill, empty-handed, owing nothing.

"Well," she said. "You did it. Justice outside any law. The Guild will hate it."

"The Guild can bill me."

She laughed—then stopped. She was looking at him strangely, the way she had looked at every passenger she'd ever ferried, but never at him. As if she were reading a page she'd finally been allowed to open.

"Odile Delacroix," she said.

The name went through him like a bell struck in an empty house.

"I remember you," she said. "Not the thief. Not the ledger-keeper. *You*. The boy with the sister and the cardamom kitchen. The man who gave away everything he had to make the world whole again." Her eyes were wet. "I don't know how—I gave nothing to the stone, took nothing from it. But it's there. All of it. You're *there*."

"The covenant keeps its own records," Odile managed. "I suppose I'm in them now."

"No." She stepped closer and took his hand, and her grip was warm and certain and utterly unearned. "I'm in them. The stone remembers. But *I* remember too. First person in twenty years, Odile. And I'm not going to stop."

Below them the river ran silver toward the free cities, carrying the news faster than any army. Somewhere in those cities, a woman was waking with her wedding day restored, and a guild of impossible things was about to discover that its finest thief had committed the most impossible act of all: he had made himself known.

Sefa squeezed his hand. "Come on," she said. "I'll ferry you home. You have a family to find."
