## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison arrived with the oyster course.

Tamsin Okafor knew it before the serving girl set the plate down—knew it in the way she knew most things, through a small cold attention at the back of her tongue that had nothing to do with taste and everything to do with survival. The oysters themselves were fine. Brine-bright, cold from the river ice, arranged on their shells like a row of wet grey eyes. It was the mignonette that was wrong. Vinegar, shallot, cracked pepper—and beneath it, so faintly that a lesser nose would have called it merely the smell of the docks clinging to someone's gloves, a sweetness like overripe pear.

Hemlock water-dropwort did not smell of pears. But hemlock cut with honey-wine and left to steep in a warm cellar for three days did, and Tamsin had spent eleven years learning exactly what murder smelled like when it tried to disguise itself as dinner.

She did not flinch. Flinching was for people whose deaths would matter to anyone but themselves.

"Your Majesty," she said, stepping forward from her place along the wall, voice pitched to carry without urgency—the way one might remark on weather. "If it please you, I'll take the first plate tonight."

King Aldous the Second, who was old and gouty and had survived four assassination attempts and one genuinely disastrous marriage, waved a jeweled hand at her with the weary gratitude of a man who had long ago stopped thinking of Tamsin as a person and had come to think of her as a lock. A good lock. One he never had to look at unless something went wrong.

She took the smallest oyster, tipped it from its shell, and let the mignonette pool against her tongue.

Pear-sweetness. Bitterness underneath like a struck match. And beneath *that*—and this was the part she would write up later, in the report no one read carefully—the particular metallic hum of nightshade leaf used as a binder, which meant whoever had done this had bought their poison from more than one source, or had been taught by someone who liked to complicate things.

Tamsin held the taste in her mouth and counted.

One hundred heartbeats. Her pulse stayed level; she made sure of it, because there was always someone watching the taster's throat for a swallow that came too hard. Two hundred. The sweetness bloomed, spread, began its slow familiar crawl toward the back of her jaw. Three hundred. Her fingertips went numb in the way they always did, pins and needles marching up from the nail beds, and sweat gathered at her hairline, and somewhere behind her sternum her heart stumbled once, twice, like a dancer missing steps in a dance she'd memorized years ago.

At four hundred heartbeats, she swallowed, straightened, and said: "Water-dropwort, cut with foxglove and bound in nightshade. Dosed for one person of His Majesty's weight. I'd guess the mignonette was prepared off-site and brought in—no one in the palace kitchens handles foxglove; I checked the herb stores myself on Tuesday." She paused, letting the hall absorb it. "I'll be ill for two days. Your Majesty may wish to have the steward's cousin questioned. He's new. He smells of the Sallow Quarter."

Then she bowed, exactly as deep as protocol required and not one inch deeper, and walked out of the great hall on legs that wanted very much to fold, past the heralds and the candlelight and the courtiers already whispering behind their hands, into the corridor where the noise fell away and she could finally lean against cold stone and be quietly, professionally miserable while her body fought a war it had fought a hundred times before.

*This*, she thought, sliding down the wall until she sat on the flagstones with her knees drawn up, waiting for the vomiting to start, *is what I am for.*

It was not, strictly speaking, all she was for. But the other thing could wait until dark.

* * *

The vomiting, mercifully, was brief. Eleven years of measured doses had built in her a tolerance that her guild physician—a sharp-eyed woman named Pell who kept her accounts in the same hand she kept her surgical notes—described as "frankly unnatural, and don't let me catch you being proud of it." By the time the palace bells rang vespers, Tamsin had taken the antidote draught, drunk two cups of salted broth, and been dismissed by the steward with a purse of coin and the observation that "the King notes your service." He noted it the way he noted the roof not leaking. She had stopped expecting anything warmer around year six.

Her rooms above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane were small and clean and deliberately impersonal: a bed, a desk, a shelf of ledgers she was not supposed to keep at home and did anyway, a single window that looked out on the lane where the lamplighters worked their rounds. She owned good boots, a good coat, and nothing else worth stealing. When she died—which, given her profession, was a question of *when* rather than *if*, whatever Pell said about tolerances—there would be perhaps an hour of paperwork and then someone else in her rooms by week's end.

She dressed for the night in clothes that looked like nothing: grey wool, soft-soled shoes, her hair braided flat against her skull. Then she took the third ledger from the shelf—the one bound in green leather gone soft with handling, its brass corners worn to dull gold—and wrapped it in oilcloth, and went out into the city as the last light drained from the sky.

The Veilwrights' Guildhall had no sign above its door. That was rather the point. To the casual eye, the building in Cooper's Yard was a counting-house for a shipping concern that owned no ships, insured no cargo, and employed no clerks anyone had ever met. Its windows were dusty. Its door was heavy oak with a knocker shaped like a knot of intertwined branches, and if you knocked on it expecting shipping business, a bored man named Fitch would tell you the office was closed and mean it.

But if you pressed the second knuckle of your right hand to the center of the knocker and spoke the word *owed*—just the word, nothing more—the door would open onto stairs going down farther than the building had depth, and the air would change, and you would smell rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Tamsin pressed her knuckle to the branch-knot. "*Owed*," she said, and the door swung inward, and the stairs breathed their cold green breath up at her, and some small tight thing in her chest loosened the way it only ever loosened here, or almost here, or in the general neighborhood of belonging, which was not quite the same thing.

Down. Past the lamp niches where the flames burned silver instead of gold. Past the doorframes marked with sigils in a script that predated the city, predated the kingdom, predated, if the Guildmaster was to be believed, the agreement that had put a border between the world of men and the world that had been there first. At the bottom, the underhall opened up around her: a vaulted space of black stone shot through with veins of pale quartz, lit by lanterns that gave light but no heat, lined with shelves, desks, cabinets, and—along the far wall, behind a railing of wrought iron twisted into thorn-patterns—the Ledger Archive, where the guild kept the true records of every bargain ever struck across the Veil.

Not money. Money was the least of it. The Veilwrights' Guild traded in impossible things: seven years of a man's luck sold to save his daughter's life; a border re-drawn by mutual consent between a village and the river-folk who lived in its ford; a widow's grief traded, at her own desperate request, for the memory of her husband's face, because she could not bear to keep both. Bargains. Boundaries. Borrowed fortune. The covenant between realms had been signed centuries ago by seven hands, human and fae alike, and the guild existed to maintain it—to record its terms, broker its amendments, collect its dues, and keep the Veil between worlds whole and quiet and unremarked upon.

Most people in the capital had never heard of the Veilwrights. This was considered the guild's greatest achievement.

"Tamsin." Sorrel's voice found her before Sorrel did, the way it always did—out of the lamplight-shadow, dry as autumn paper. The Guildmaster was a small woman gone grey early, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of deep water, and she moved through the underhall like a current moves through a pond: everywhere at once, touching everything, visible only when she chose to be. Tonight she carried a ledger under one arm and a look on her face that Tamsin had learned to read as *not now*. "You're late."

"The King was poisoned at supper. Water-dropwort, compounded." Tamsin set the green ledger on her own desk—the narrow one near the archive rail that she had claimed in her first month and defended since with the quiet ferocity of a cat defending a sunlit step. "I'm fine. I filed the palace report already. What's tonight?"

"Audits." Sorrel set her own ledger down on the Master's table and did not meet her eye. "Quarter-end. You know the work."

Tamsin did know the work. She loved the work, if she was honest, in the way that lonely people love the things that fill their hours: line by line, entry by entry, verifying that every bargain in the archive balanced against its collateral. A luck-sale entered must show the luck returned or the debt transferred. A border-treaty amended must show both parties' marks. Every impossible thing the guild had ever done was written down somewhere in this room, in ink mixed with a drop of rain-from-the-other-side, and Tamsin's job—her real job, the one that mattered more than any king's supper—was to make sure the writing told the truth.

She worked for two hours in the comfortable silence of the underhall, quill scratching, quartz-veins glowing faintly overhead like frozen lightning. Around her, the night-shift clerks murmured over their desks: Wren, who handled luck-contracts and chewed licorice root constantly; old Hesper, half-deaf, who verified boundary-stones and shouted even when whispering would do. Familiar sounds. Safe sounds. Tamsin felt herself settle into them the way a key settles into a lock it was cut for.

Which was why it took her until the third hour to notice that something was wrong.

It started small, the way wrongness usually did. An entry in the spring quarter-book that referenced a payment schedule she couldn't find in the master index. Then another. Then a whole run of entries—neat, professional, in a clerk's hand she almost recognized—recording payments made *in starlight*.

She turned back to the index. Checked the cross-reference. Checked it again.

Starlight was legal tender in exactly one kind of transaction, and everyone in the underhall knew it: payments to the Otherworld, dues owed across the Veil to the fae signatories of the original covenant. Starlight paid *to* the far side. Never from it. And certainly never—

She turned the page. The entries continued. Payments received in starlight. Received by the guild. Paid by a buyer whose name was recorded nowhere, whose mark was a blank space in the signature column, a little rectangle of empty paper that made her eyes slide off it like rain off glass.

"Guildmaster." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Sorrel. Come look at this."

For a moment, the underhall's silence changed texture. Wren's quill stopped. Hesper looked up. And Sorrel crossed the black stone floor with a pace that was too quick to be casual and too controlled to be hurrying, and stood at Tamsin's shoulder, and looked down at the page.

Tamsin watched her mentor's face. She had spent eleven years reading faces for the flicker that preceded poison, and she had gotten very good at it, and what she saw now—in the space of half a heartbeat, before Sorrel's expression closed like a door—was fear.

"These aren't mine to discuss," Sorrel said. "Nor yours to audit."

"I audit everything. That's the work. That's—" Tamsin stopped. Started again, quieter. "You taught me that. *Every line tells the truth or the whole book is worthless.* Whose entries are these?"

"They're accounted for." Sorrel reached out and, gently, with two ink-stained fingers, closed the quarter-book. The little rectangle of blankness vanished beneath her palm. "Some bargains are struck above my station, child, and well above yours. The buyer's identity is sealed at the highest level. Take the palace ledgers and reconcile the ward-dues for the north border, and we'll speak no more of it."

"*Child*." Tamsin heard the word leave her mouth and didn't care. "I've kept books here for nine years. I've reconciled the funeral-debts of two signatories. I've held the covenant-terms in my hands. Don't—"

"Don't what?" Sorrel's voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went soft. "Don't treat you as what you are? Tamsin. Sweet girl. You are the finest auditor this guild has produced in a century, and you are a food-taster by trade, and neither of those things makes you party to bargains struck before you were born. Some doors are locked for reasons. Your job is to know they're locked. Not to pick them."

The words landed like a dose she hadn't been given warning to expect. Tamsin sat very still, hands flat on the desk, and felt the shape of the evening tilt beneath her. Above her head, faintly, the quartz-veins pulsed once—twice—as though the stone itself had drawn a breath.

"Of course, Guildmaster," she said.

Sorrel held her gaze a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she nodded, once, and walked back toward the Master's table, and the underhall resumed its murmuring, and Tamsin opened the north-border ward-dues and worked for another hour with perfect mechanical correctness while the closed quarter-book sat on her desk like a stone in her shoe.

*Locked doors*, she thought. *Sealed buyers. Payments in starlight coming the wrong way across the Veil.*

She finished the reconciliation. She blotted the pages. She packed her satchel, said her goodnights to Wren and Hesper, and climbed the long stairs into the ordinary night, where the stars were doing what stars always did—burning, indifferent, each one accounted for in no ledger at all.

At the top of the stairs, just before the door closed behind her, she heard Sorrel below, speaking to someone in a low voice she couldn't make out. Two voices. Only one of them belonged to the Guildmaster.

The other was saying something about *moonrise*.

Tamsin stood a long moment in Cooper's Yard, in the cold, with her collar up and her breath fogging, and told herself it was nothing. Sealed bargains. Locked doors. The guild had kept the world's secrets for six hundred years; it could keep a few from her.

But her tongue—the taster's tongue, the tongue that had caught pear-sweetness under vinegar and death under dinner—kept turning over that second voice like a taste she couldn't place.

And somewhere overhead, though she did not yet know to look, the stars were burning a little less brightly than they had the night before.
