## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The guildhall beneath the city kept its own hours. Above ground, the capital ran on bells and sunlight and the slow arithmetic of taxes; below, in the vaulted dark under the Cordwainers' Row, time was measured in candle-lengths and the scratch of quills. Tamsin Okafor had learned its rhythms the way she'd learned the taste of arsenic—gradually, thoroughly, and with a respect that bordered on affection.

She descended the spiral stair at half past midnight, still wearing the plain grey livery of the palace taster's office, her tongue faintly numb from the evening's work. The king's chef had attempted a new sauce tonight—river eel in a cream of green garlic—and Tamsin had held it on her tongue for the prescribed ten heartbeats while forty courtiers watched her with that particular hunger they reserved for other people's deaths. Nothing. A little too much nutmeg. She had nodded, and the hall had exhaled, and she had eaten a full portion herself because a taster who didn't eat was a taster nobody trusted.

Now, three levels beneath the street, she lit the audit lamp with a taper and let the guildhall assemble itself around her out of the shadows.

It was not a grand room. Visitors always expected grandeur—the Veilwrights' Guild traded in impossible things, after all—but the truth was shelves. Hundreds of them, honeycombing the limestone in every direction, each cubby holding ledgers bound in leather, in bark, in things that were neither. Some of the books hummed. One, on the third shelf of the western wall, wept quietly during the month of Thaw, and no one had ever determined why, and eventually everyone had simply agreed to leave it a saucer of rainwater.

Tamsin took down the night's ledger—the *Covenant Register, Continuance Volume IX*—and settled at the long oak table worn smooth by two centuries of forearms.

Her job, formally, was reconciliation. Every bargain the guild brokered between the human realm and the Otherworld left an entry: what was given, what was received, what was owed, and by whom. Most were small. A miller trading seven years of his shadow's warmth for a harvest that wouldn't rot. A midwife purchasing a fae grandmother's patience for the price of one true name, freely spoken and never used again. A child's lost tooth exchanged for the memory of a song. Tamsin checked the entries against the covenant's terms, initialed the margins, and flagged anything irregular for Guildmaster Sorrel.

Most nights, nothing was irregular. Most nights, the work felt like prayer performed in arithmetic—order imposed on a border that desperately needed it.

Tonight, the third entry stopped her cold.

***

The entry read, in the elegant copperplate of Under-Clerk Fenwick:

*Bought and delivered: one (1) segment of the Border Covenant, being the third clause of the Second Accord, concerning the passage of seasons across the Veil. Sold to: [entry blank]. Payment received: starlight, unrefined, four measures. Witnessed under seal.*

Tamsin read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way she tasted an unfamiliar compound—first the shape of it, then the texture, then the danger underneath.

There were three problems, and she catalogued them the way Sorrel had taught her, back when she was nine years old and newly arrived, all elbows and silence, in this same chair.

First: the buyer's field was blank. Not redacted—redactions were done properly, with a stamped seal over the ink and a marginal note citing the sealing authority. This was simply empty, as if whoever entered it had lifted their pen and never set it down again.

Second: starlight was not currency. It appeared in old ledgers, certainly—in the founding volumes, from the era when the Veil itself was young and the two realms still negotiated like neighbors over a fence. But starlight hadn't been legal tender within the guild for a hundred and sixty years, ever since the Starlight Reforms, because starlight could not be stored, could not be audited, and—this was the part that mattered—could not be taxed by either realm. An entry paid in starlight was an entry designed to be forgotten.

Third, and worst: you could not sell a segment of the Border Covenant. The covenant wasn't property. It was the border. Selling a clause of it was rather like selling a load-bearing wall.

Tamsin reached for the blotter, then stopped herself. Her hands were steady—they were always steady; that was the first thing the guild had trained into her, before letters, before poisons, before anything: *your hands do not shake, because your hands are instruments.* But something behind her sternum had begun to move in a slow, cold way, like water finding a crack in a cellar floor.

She pulled Volume VIII. Then VII. She worked backward through the night, and the pattern assembled itself with the terrible neatness of a well-kept poison garden.

Four entries in Volume VIII. Nine in Volume VII. In Volume VI, the earliest she dared pull without waking the archivist wraiths that nested in the deep stacks, she found fourteen—all in the same hand, all with the buyer's field blank, all paid in starlight, and the segments sold growing larger as the volumes grew more recent. Seasons. Then tides. Then, in the final entry of Volume VI, in ink so fresh it still smelled faintly of iron:

*Sold: the Anchor Clause of the First Accord, being the covenant's root-fastening to the mortal sky. Payment: starlight, unrefined, forty measures. Buyer: [blank].*

Tamsin sat back. The audit lamp hissed softly, its flame burning a color that wasn't quite yellow—guild lamps burned a little sideways, this deep under the city, where the Veil ran close enough to touch.

Forty measures of starlight. Someone was buying the sky's anchor bolts and paying in light that couldn't be kept.

She did what she always did with an anomaly. She wrote it down. Three pages of notes, cross-referenced, dated, signed with her keeper's mark—a small circle bisected by a vertical line, the sign of the taster's office, which the guild had adopted for her personally because, as Sorrel had once said with dry humor, *you are the only person alive whose signature certifies that a thing is safe.* Then she closed the volumes, returned them to their shelves—Volume VI growled at her, and she patted its spine absently, the way one quiets a dog—and went to find Sorrel.

***

Guildmaster Sorrel kept the upper study, the one room in the guildhall with a window, though the window looked onto solid earth and had for as long as anyone could remember. He claimed it was for the principle of the thing. Sorrel was a great believer in principles, the way some men were great believers in ships they would never sail.

He was awake, of course. Sorrel was always awake at this hour, a narrow, silvering man bent over correspondence, his reading spectacles pushed up into hair the color of wet ash. A pot of chicory steamed at his elbow. He did not look surprised to see her, which was, Tamsin would think later, the first wrong note.

"Finished the Continuance already?" he said. "Fenwick will be crushed. He likes to believe his copperplate slows people down."

"The register doesn't reconcile." She laid her notes on his desk, aligned precisely with its edge, because her hands were instruments even when her chest was tight. "Sixty-one entries across six volumes, Guildmaster. All sales of covenant-segments. All to a blank buyer. All paid in starlight."

Sorrel put down his pen. He did it carefully, setting it in the groove of the desk's writing slope, and Tamsin knew that gesture too—it was what he did when he was buying himself three seconds.

"Show me."

He read her notes the way she tasted poison: completely, unhurriedly, giving each line its ten heartbeats. When he finished, he removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and said, "You understand what these entries are."

"I understand what they claim to be. I don't understand how they exist. Segments of the covenant can't be alienated. The Accord of Root and Branch is explicit—"

"The Accord of Root and Branch," Sorrel said mildly, "is four hundred years old and written by people who assumed the Veil would hold forever. Assumptions age badly, Tamsin. Even ours." He rose and went to the window that looked at dirt, and stood looking at it, hands clasped behind his back. "The Veil is thinning. You've seen it yourself, if you've any eyes. The lamps burn sideways down here. The wraiths in the stacks have doubled in number since spring. The fae side of the border is starving—their magic runs on the crossing of our seasons and theirs, and the crossings come later and weaker every year. The covenant needs maintenance. Maintenance requires materials. Materials require payment."

"So the guild is selling pieces of the covenant to repair the covenant." She heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used at the king's table when a courtier tried to fluster her. "That's bleeding a patient to cure anemia."

"That's bookkeeping," Sorrel said. "The alternative is watching the border fail outright. Would you prefer that? Because I promise you, whatever unease those entries give you, it is a gentler sensation than the sky coming apart."

"Then why is the buyer's name blank?"

Silence. The chicory pot ticked as it cooled.

"Sorrel." She almost never used his Christian name. It landed now like a coin dropped in a quiet room. "I keep these ledgers. That is my office. If there is a reason a buyer cannot be named, there is a form for it—a sealed-entry form, countersigned by two masters. There is no form here. There is no seal. There is a blank space in a book I am responsible for, and every instinct I have—which you trained, which you sharpened, which you have spent twenty years making trustworthy—is telling me that a blank space in a ledger is not discretion. It is a wound."

He turned from the window then, and looked at her, and for a moment—one long, suspended moment—Tamsin saw something move behind his face that frightened her more than any entry could have. Not guilt. Guilt she could have managed. It was *calculation*. He was weighing her, the way she weighed a dish, deciding whether she was safe.

"You're tired," he said finally, gently. "You tasted for the king tonight, and then you reconciled six volumes in one sitting, which no under-keeper should attempt. Go home. Sleep. Tomorrow I'll walk you through the sealed-entry protocols myself, and you'll see there's nothing here but an old man's caution and a dying border's bills."

"The entries aren't old," Tamsin said. "The last one's ink is days old. And the amounts are increasing, and the segments being sold are getting closer to the covenant's roots, and if my arithmetic is right, at the current rate of sale there will be nothing left to sell within—" she stopped.

Within what? She had done the calculation in her notes. She had not let herself finish reading her own conclusion.

"Within a year," she said quietly. "Maybe less."

Sorrel sighed, and it was such a human, weary, fond sigh that she nearly believed him. "You were the best student I ever had, and the most stubborn. Go home, Tamsin. I mean it kindly. Some ledgers are kept so that others needn't read them. That is also a form of protection, and it is the oldest one we have."

She gathered her notes. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and asked the question she'd been carrying since the third entry, the one underneath all the others:

"Who buys starlight, Sorrel? What does anyone *do* with it?"

He was already back at his correspondence, pen moving, and he answered without looking up, in the tone of a man discussing the weather:

"Nothing good. Nothing good has ever been built out of borrowed light."

***

She did not go home. Or rather, she went home, to the narrow room above the chandler's shop that the guild kept for her, and she sat on the edge of her bed in the dark with her boots on, and she did not sleep.

Instead she thought about belonging, which was a dangerous thing to think about at three in the morning.

The guild had taken her from the foundling house at six years old, along with eleven other children, and of the twelve, she alone had been kept. She had never asked why—not really, not in the way that leaves a mark in a ledger. The others had been released to trades, to families, to the ordinary world. Tamsin had been kept, fed, taught letters and then languages and then the hundred subtle flavors of death, and she had understood herself to be chosen. Special. The word she had used, privately, for years, was *belonged*.

She belonged everywhere and nowhere. At the palace she was the grey girl with the grey tongue, necessary and untouchable and utterly friendless. In the guildhall she was the keeper of the books, trusted with everything and told almost nothing. She moved between worlds the way the Veil moved between realms—present at both edges, native to neither.

And tonight, for the first time in twenty years, she had watched her mentor weigh her like a dish and decide not to swallow.

*Some ledgers are kept so that others needn't read them.*

She got up, lit no lamp, and knelt to look beneath her bed, where she kept the one volume she was permitted to take home: her own working copy, the keeper's abstract, a condensed record of everything that crossed her desk. She flipped to the last page she'd written and added a single line in her smallest, most private script—a cipher of her own devising, unbreakable except by its author:

*If the buyer is blank because the buyer is Vey, then the sky is already sold.*

Lord Cassian Vey. The name had been in her head all night without permission, surfacing between entries like a body rising in a pond. She had no evidence connecting him to the starlight payments—no crest, no seal, no witness. Only this: Vey was the one man in the capital buying up everything the Veil touched. Fae relics. Ward-stones. Old treaties from dead houses. He bought the way a man buys rope, patiently, in lengths, and everyone assumed he merely collected.

Everyone assumed. Tamsin made her living on the difference between what was in a dish and what people assumed was in it.

She closed the abstract. Through her one small window, high above the chandler's yard, she could see a sliver of sky between the rooftops, and the stars hung in it, distant and orderly and mortgaged.

They were still burning when she finally slept. She would remember that, afterward—that the stars were still there, and she had looked at them and felt, for the first time in her life, afraid of the dark above instead of the dark below.

Three nights later, at moonrise, they went out.
