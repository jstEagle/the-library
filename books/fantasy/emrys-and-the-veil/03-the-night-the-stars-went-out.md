## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The moon rose wrong.

Tamsin noticed it from the guildhall's upper gallery, where she had gone to cool her ink-stained fingers against the stone. She had spent the evening reconciling a column of entries that refused to balance—a shipment of borrowed luck out of the Mire of Small Debts, three years overdue—and the numbers had been swimming before her eyes when something in the quality of the light made her lift her head.

The moon was rising in the east, as it always did, but it came up the color of an old bruise, yellow-green shading into violet at its rim, and it climbed too fast. Not unnaturally fast—nothing so obvious as that—but with the smooth haste of something being *pulled*, like a curtain drawn by a hand impatient to see what lay behind it.

She stood very still and watched it clear the rooftops of the capital, and her taster's tongue, that treacherous, exquisitely calibrated instrument, registered a taste on the air that had no business existing: copper and cold iron and underneath it something sweeter and far worse, like fruit rotting in a sealed room.

*Ward-rot,* she thought, and did not know how she knew the word, only that she knew it the way she knew the difference between arsenic and belladonna on the back of her tongue—instantly, totally, without any possibility of being mistaken.

Below her, the guildhall went on with its evening business. Clerks scratched at their desks. The great ledger-room glowed with lamp-light through its open doors, and somewhere Sorrel's voice rose and fell in negotiation with a hedge-witch who wanted rain for her fields and could pay only in memories. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary night.

Except for the moon, climbing like something fleeing.

And except for the wards.

Tamsin felt them go the way one feels a tooth die—not all at once, but as a sudden absence where pressure had been. The border wards were not visible; they were not supposed to be. They ran along the old covenant-lines, stitched between the realms at seven anchor-points, humming below the threshold of ordinary sense. But Tamsin had kept the ledgers for six years, and the ledgers were not merely records of the wards—they were, in a way the guildmasters never explained to clerks, part of their maintenance. Every entry she balanced was a stitch held true. She had felt the wards the way a seamstress feels thread running through her fingers: constantly, faintly, without thinking about it.

Now the thread had gone slack.

Now the thread had gone *out*.

"Master Okafor." One of the junior clerks appeared at the gallery stair, a boy called Fenwick with ink to his elbows and the anxious face he wore whenever the books disagreed with him. "There's—forgive me, there's something odd in the eastern columns. The starlight payments. They've all gone—" He hesitated, hunting for the word. "They've all gone *due*. All at once. Every entry marked in starlight, going back forty years, has flipped to due-in-full tonight."

Tamsin's stomach dropped through the floor.

"When?" she said. "When did they flip?"

"Just now. As the moon cleared the wall. I checked twice—I thought it must be an error, some cascade fault in the copying, but—"

"It's not an error." Her voice came out flat and strange, and Fenwick flinched from it. "Nothing is an error tonight. Go home, Fenwick. Take the west stairs and don't stop for anything."

"But the ledgers—"

"*Go.*"

He went. She heard his boots clatter down and away, and then she stood alone in the gallery with the bruised moon climbing and the taste of ward-rot thickening on her tongue, and she made herself do the arithmetic she did not want to do.

Every entry marked in starlight. Forty years of them. Flipped to due-in-full.

Which meant every debt the guild had ever paid in starlight had been called in simultaneously—which meant someone had seized the account they were drawn against—and there was only one account in either realm that paid in starlight, because there was only one thing in either realm that *was* starlight.

The Veil itself. The shimmering boundary between the human world and the Otherworld, that thin bright membrane hung with stars on its far side—the stars that were not stars at all, but the lights of the fae courts burning beyond the border, seen through gauze.

Someone had called in the Veil's debts.

Someone had begun foreclosing on the sky.

* * *

She found Sorrel in the ledger-room, standing over the master book with both hands flat upon it, and Tamsin stopped dead in the doorway because Guildmaster Sorrel Ashgrave did not stand like that. Sorrel stood like a man who owned gravity, like a man the floor was pleased to hold up. Sixty years old and straight as a stylus, silver-bearded, voice like a closing door—he was the fixed point around which Tamsin's entire adult life had been arranged. He had pulled her out of the foundling-house at nine years old because she could tell hemlock from fennel by smell across a room. He had taught her ledgers, languages, the seventy-one poisons and their antidotes, the grammar of contracts older than kingdoms. He had given her belonging, or the shape of it, which for a girl raised on charity bread was nearly the same thing.

He was leaning on the master book now like a man leaning on a coffin lid.

"You feel it," he said, without turning. Not a question.

"The wards are slack," Tamsin said. "The moon is wrong. The starlight accounts have all been called due. And there's a taste on the air I can't name, which frightens me more than the rest, because I can name everything." She crossed the room, her heels loud on the stone. "Sorrel. What have you done?"

That made him turn. And what she saw in his face took the anger out of her and left something colder in its place, because Sorrel Ashgrave looked *old*. He looked like a man who had been carrying a weight so long he had forgotten it was heavy, and had just now remembered, all at once, the full measure of it.

"I have done," he said quietly, "what the guild has always done. I have kept the accounts balanced. It is not my fault, Tamsin, that the accounts themselves were rotten from the beginning—or rather, it is entirely my fault, and my predecessors', and we knew it, and we balanced anyway, because the alternative was this." He lifted one hand toward the high windows, toward the climbing moon. "This, arriving early."

"Explain it to me plainly. Tonight. No deflections."

"There's no time for plain."

"Then make time, because in about four minutes I suspect plainness will be the only language anyone speaks."

Something almost like pride moved through his exhaustion. "You always did audit better than you obeyed." He drew a breath. "The covenant, Tamsin. The original covenant, the one that set the border and hung the Veil and keeps the fae courts on their side of the sky. Seven signatories made it, ages ago—three human, three fae, and one who was neither, one who stood between and held the pen. The Veil is not a wall. It is a *contract*, living and ongoing, renewed in perpetuity by payment. Starlight flows out; stability flows back. That is the whole architecture of the world, written in ledger-form, and the guild exists to keep the books of it."

"And someone has been buying pieces of it."

It was not a question, and she watched him absorb the fact that she already knew—that she had known since the night of the unnamed buyer, since the column of impossible entries, since every small wrongness she had swallowed and recorded and told herself was clerical noise.

"Yes," Sorrel said.

"How much?"

"Enough." He said it like a man spitting out a poison. "Segments of the covenant. Clause by clause, right by right, anchor-point by anchor-point. Sold in installments over thirty years to a buyer whose name does not appear in any book I control. And tonight the buyer called everything due at once, and the Veil—" His hand trembled, actually trembled, and Tamsin stared at it as if the world had tilted. "The Veil is being repossessed."

Outside, the first scream began.

* * *

It started as a single voice from the direction of the Temple Quarter, thin and distant, and then it became ten voices, and then it became the whole city, a tide of sound rolling up from the streets below the guildhall like floodwater finding every channel at once. Tamsin went to the window and looked up, because everyone in the capital was looking up, because the sky was doing the unthinkable.

The stars were going out.

Not fading—failing. One moment the vault of heaven held its familiar scatter of lights, and the next, whole constellations simply ceased, snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, and the darkness left behind was not the soft dark of clouded nights but an absolute black, a hole in the world. And in the holes, Tamsin saw—with her eyes and with something older than her eyes—movement. Shapes pressing against the underside of the sky. The gauze of the Veil wearing thin enough, here and there, to show what burned behind it: not friendly court-lights now but vast slow forms, antlered and winged and many-eyed, turning restlessly against a membrane grown suddenly, catastrophically fragile.

"The wards are down," she whispered. "All of them. At once."

"At moonrise," Sorrel said behind her. "As specified. The buyer is precise. Precision is what you purchase, when you purchase the end of the world." He moved past her, and she heard him working at the master book—not reading it, but doing something to it, wrenching free the great iron clasp and drawing out from beneath the main volume a slimmer book bound in grey leather, wrapped in a chain of cold iron, sealed with wax stamped by a sigil she had never seen on any guild document: a bridge with one side of it broken.

"Sorrel—"

"Listen to me." He took her shoulders in his hands, and his grip was iron, and his eyes were wet, and Tamsin understood in one vertiginous lurch that she had never once seen Guildmaster Sorrel Ashgrave afraid until this exact moment, and that the fear was for her. "Everything I should have told you years ago, I will tell you if we both live through the hour. But the hour comes first. This ledger holds the covenant's true state—every clause sold, every signature, every payment, including the ones I signed and will burn in whatever hell is allotted to me for signing. It is the only complete copy in existence. Vey's people will be here within the quarter-hour; they have been in the city for a week, and tonight they stop auditing and start killing. You are the fastest reader of contract-script in the guild, you are unknown outside these walls, and you are—" his voice caught, "—you are the only person I trust with it. Take it. Run. Do not go to your rooms. Do not go to any address associated with your name. Go downriver, past the toll-bridges, and keep walking until you reach Harrowgate. There are people there. Old signatories' people. The ledger will know how to find them—it's keyed to covenant-blood, and yours counts, whatever else is true or untrue about you."

"Whatever else is—" Tamsin grabbed his wrist. "*What does that mean? What's true or untrue about me?*"

The window exploded inward.

Glass burst across the ledger-room in a glittering fan, and through it came the cold of the open sky, and with the cold came a sound Tamsin would hear for the rest of her life however long that proved to be: a long, low, grinding note, like a harp string the size of the horizon snapping one fiber at a time. Above the capital, directly overhead, the Veil tore.

It was not dramatic, at first. That was the horror of it. A seam opened, perhaps a spear-length long, thin as a scratch, and through it poured light that was not light—colors with no names, a radiance that made the stones of the guildhall look transient, provisional, like things sketched in preparation for something real. Through the torn seam drifted motes of that light, falling slowly as ash, and where each mote touched the rooftop opposite, the slate aged fifty years in a breath, moss erupting, tiles sagging, the chimney crumbling softly into ruin.

The screaming below changed key. Fear became panic became something less articulate and more terrible.

"They're fraying through," Sorrel said, and shoved the grey ledger hard against her chest. "Take it. *Run.*"

"The guild—your clerks—Fenwick—"

"Are adults who chose this work, as you did, as I did. The ledger outranks all of us. GO."

She went—two steps. Then she turned and did the last unprofessional thing of her old life: she threw her arms around him. For one heartbeat he held her back, fierce and brief, a father's grip, and then his hands were pushing her away again and his voice was pure guildmaster, clipped and certain:

"West stairs. River gate. Trust no crest, no uniform, no friendly face that asks to see what you carry. And Tamsin—" she was already at the door, the ledger hugged to her ribs, the broken-bridge sigil biting cold even through her coat, "—when you read it, read the marginalia. Everything I couldn't say, I wrote down."

The stairwell was chaos. Clerks with armloads of books, the hedge-witch weeping on the landing, old Master Corvane shouting for the archive-seals. Tamsin shouldered through it all, down and down, past the scriptorium where forty years of her careful handwriting sat abandoned mid-entry, out through the river gate into a night gone utterly strange.

The streets were rivers of people. Families with candle-flames guttering green in the tainted air. A man beating at his own front door, which had begun to grow bark. Somewhere a temple bell rang the alarm and then stopped mid-peal, as if the ringer had simply ceased to believe in bells. Overhead the torn seam in the sky had widened to the length of a street, and other seams were opening now, north and east, little wounds of unbearable color, and through them the shapes pressed harder, patient and vast, testing.

Tamsin ran with the crowd along the river road, hood up, ledger crushed against her heart, and the taste of ward-rot gave way at last to something she could finally name, and the naming was the worst moment of the night: it tasted like *foreclosure*. Like a home repossessed. Like the whole world served notice.

At the toll-bridge a figure stepped out of the crowd directly into her path—tall, cloaked, moving with a stillness no frightened refugee possessed—and for one frozen instant Tamsin saw the crest worked into the cloak's clasp in silver thread: a tower above a closed fist.

Lord Cassian Vey's mark.

The figure smiled and extended an open hand, courteous as a customs officer, and said words that cut through the roaring panic of the street as if the street had gone silent for them alone:

"Tamsin Okafor. You'll forgive the intrusion—but the guildmaster was seen handing you something, and my lord requires all outstanding accounts to be settled tonight."
