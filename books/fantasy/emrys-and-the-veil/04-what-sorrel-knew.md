## 4. What Sorrel Knew

The ledger was heavier than a child.

That was Tamsin's first coherent thought as she ran—absurd, useless, the kind of thought that surfaced in her mind the way a cork surfaces in floodwater, bobbing above the terror. The ledger pressed against her ribs where she'd bound it beneath her coat with a strip of her own shirt, and it was heavy the way a sleeping animal is heavy, the way a secret is heavy, and she could not put it down because Guildmaster Sorrel had put it into her hands with his own two trembling ones and said *run*.

So she ran.

The capital came apart around her as she went. She had seen cities burn in the way one sees anything at second hand—in reports, in ledgers, in the flat accounting of losses—but she had never imagined that a city could come apart *upward*. That was the wrongness of it, the thing that made her stomach lurch every time she glanced up without meaning to: the sky itself was failing. Where stars had been there was nothing now, not cloud-cover nothing but true nothing, a black so complete it seemed less like absence than like an eye pressed shut. And through that black, thin as cracks in old glaze, ran lines of pale light—the Veil, fraying visibly overhead like a garment coming apart at its seams, shedding threads of silver that drifted down over the rooftops and dissolved before they touched the earth.

People were screaming. People were praying. People were looting the chandlers' shops on Tanner's Row because if the sky was dying then by all the dead gods they would have candles, and Tamsin threaded between them with her head down and her hand pressed flat against the ledger's hidden weight, and she thought: *Sorrel knew.*

She had known it even as he shoved the book at her, even as he turned back to bar the guildhall door behind her with his own body. She had known it in the way his hands shook—not the shaking of an old man afraid to die, but the shaking of an old man who has done arithmetic he cannot un-do. She had spent eleven years reading faces across royal tables, watching for the micro-flinch of a courtier realizing the wine was wrong, and Sorrel's face in that last lamplit moment had worn the look of a man who had finally tasted his own cooking.

*Run east,* he'd said. *Not to the palace. Never to the palace. East, past the river, to Harrowgate. Find the signatories. The names are in the front matter—you'll know them when you see them.* And then, when she'd opened her mouth to ask the question that was already splitting her open—*what did you do?*—he had said, quietly, in the voice he used for entries too shameful to speak aloud:

"I did what I thought was keeping us safe. Go."

The door had closed. She had heard the bar drop. And then, three streets later, she had heard something else—the sound of the guildhall door being *un*-dropped, violently, from outside—and she had not looked back, because looking back was a luxury for people whose hands were empty.

* * *

She cut down through the tannery district, where the reek of lime pits covered her scent and the alleys were too narrow for more than two abreast. Her feet knew these streets the way her tongue knew poisons: by long, deliberate, unglamorous study. Eleven years in the capital had taught her which gutters flooded, which courtyards had dogs, which doors belonged to guild-families who owed the Veilwrights favors and would open for a knock-code and a whispered name.

She tried the first door. No answer. The second. A shutter cracked, saw her face, and shut again—faster than it had opened.

By the third refusal she understood. They weren't hiding from the falling sky. They were hiding from whatever was coming *behind* her. Word traveled fast in the under-city, faster than feet, and somewhere in the last hour the word had gone out: the Veilwrights' Guild is finished; anyone who shelters its people shares its ending.

Her fourth try was Mother Casseline's boarding house, a crooked building near the tanners' gate where Tamsin had slept her first winter in the city, back when Sorrel had found her—a half-starved foundling with a taster's cast-iron stomach and nowhere else to be—and asked her, crouching to her level, "Would you like to belong somewhere, girl?" She hammered on the door until her fist ached. Nothing. Then, through the wood, low and ragged: "Tamsin? Child, get away from that door. There are men asking after you. Men with a crest I don't know, and I know every crest in this city." A pause, and then, worse: "They showed me your likeness, love. Drawn fine, like off a seal or a signet. Who are you, that lords draw your face?"

Tamsin put her forehead against the door. "I'm nobody," she said, and heard how it broke in half coming out. "I'm sorry. Thank you. Don't open it."

She went on alone.

* * *

The men with the crest found her at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where the street opened onto a square and the square offered no cover worth the name.

There were four of them, and they did not run the way city watchmen run, huffing and clattering. They moved like water finding a downhill grade—unhurried, inevitable, spreading across the lane's width without appearing to decide to. Their armor was dark, close-fitted, practical, and on each breastplate sat the same device: a tower, crowned, rendered in white enamel against black. Tamsin didn't know the crest. But she knew what it meant to wear a crest during a collapse, when every honest soldier in the capital was busy holding panic-lines: it meant their master's authority stood above the crisis. It meant someone, somewhere, believed the world could end and his orders would still matter.

"Ledger-keeper," said the one in front. His voice was mild, almost courteous, the voice of a man discussing a delivery. "You have property of Lord Vey's. We only want the book. You can keep your fingers."

Every instinct she owned screamed at her to freeze, to calculate, to find the angle out—and underneath the screaming, absurdly, her professional mind was working anyway, cataloguing: *they've been told I'm a ledger-keeper specifically, not a taster, which means someone briefed them from inside the guild; they want the book intact, which means it matters more than I do; four men, open ground, no weapon, no route—*

Then the sky helped her.

A thread of the Veil came down over the square. It fell slow as ash, a ribbon of pale light ten yards long, and where it touched the cobbles the stones *sang*—a high, glassy note that made everyone flinch—and the thread unraveled into motes that hung in the air like snow refusing to land. One of the soldiers reached out, gloved, curious, and touched a mote.

He stopped moving. Not died—stopped, mid-gesture, his hand still raised, his eyes still tracking, everything about him simply *paused*, like a clock with a finger laid against its escapement. The other three shouted his name once and then, sensibly, decided the book was worth more than their comrade's curiosity, and came on.

Tamsin went over the tanner's fence instead of through the gate—her palms took splinters she wouldn't feel till morning—and dropped into the lime-yard beyond, and ran the maze of drying-racks and curing-sheds with the ledger hammering at her ribs like a second heart. Behind her, boots. Ahead, the river-wall, and beyond the river-wall, the east road, and beyond the east road—

Harrowgate. Three days' ride. Four days' walk. Five, if the roads were already choked with refugees, and the glow on the eastern horizon suggested they were.

She found a drainage culvert where the tannery waste met the river, a pipe wide enough to crawl, and she crawled, because pride was another luxury for people whose hands were empty. In the dark of the pipe, with the smell of lime burning her throat and the distant singing of the fraying Veil coming down through the stone, she allowed herself, for exactly thirty heartbeats, to stop running and start thinking.

And thinking meant the ledger.

She unwound the shirt-strip and dragged the book into the gray light that leaked through the culvert's mouth. Sorrel's seal was still intact—black wax, the guild's mark, a balance-beam crossed with a quill. He had told her not to open it until she was beyond the river. She was beyond the river. She was also, she realized, breathing hard, furious, grieving, and done with being managed by men who trembled while handing her their sins.

She broke the seal.

* * *

The first pages were ordinary—inventory, dues, the small commerce of impossible things. Borrowed luck, drawn down and repaid with interest in memory. Border-tolls paid in dreams. A standing account with the palace kitchens (hers, she realized; her salary entered here, in Sorrel's neat hand, marked *taster's retainer — hazard premium*). Page after page of the quiet machinery that kept two realms from grinding against each other like millstones.

Then the front matter. *Find the signatories. You'll know them when you see them*, Sorrel had said, and there they were, inscribed on the covenant-page in ink that still shimmered faintly, seven names in two columns, human and fae intermingled. Three struck through with a single clean line each—dead, the strike-throughs said, in dates going back years. Two marked with locations she didn't recognize. One name circled, annotated in Sorrel's writing, the ink fresh enough to smear: *Vey. Cassian Vey. Signatory the Seventh — how did we never see it —*

And beside the circle, a second annotation, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*I sold him six segments. Six of seven. God forgive me, I thought I was selling him rope to hang the old courts with. I didn't understand what the segments WERE until the wards began to sing. If you are reading this, Tamsin, then I am dead or gone, and I cannot tell you which is truer. The payments are all through this book. Look at the countersignatures. Look at what he made me write.*

She turned pages with hands that had gone strangely calm—the calm she wore at the king's table, the professional calm, the worst calm she owned. The entries were there, just as she'd glimpsed them weeks ago on audit night, the payments in starlight to the unnamed buyer. But now, knowing the buyer's name, she read them properly. Segment the First: the northern wards. Segment the Second: the moon-tithe. Third, fourth, fifth, sixth—each a load-bearing piece of the covenant, each sold, each initialed *S.S.* in Sorrel's hand.

And below each initial, the countersignature. The verification mark of the guild's second keeper. The little hooked cipher that Tamsin herself had been taught to make, that appeared on every legitimate entry she had ever audited, that was supposed to mean *I have checked this, I attest this, I am accountable for this.*

It was hers. Or rather—it was a forgery of hers, and a good one, good enough that she had audited her own forged consent a dozen times without ever seeing it. Sorrel had signed away the world's skeleton in Lord Vey's name and had written *her* name beneath it, over and over, month after month, while she sat across the desk from him drinking tea he poured, trusting him with the only belonging she had ever been given.

She sat in the culvert's dark and did not cry, because crying was loud and the boots might still be above her, and also because what she felt was too strange for tears. It was grief wearing rage wearing grief, braided tight as rope. The guild had fed her. The guild had taught her letters and ledgers and the thousand small competencies that made her *her*. The guild had answered a starving child's question—"would you like to belong somewhere?"—with yes, and meant it, and built her a place at its table.

And the guild had sold the sky out from under that table, and signed her name to the sale, and when the bill came due had pressed the evidence into her arms and said *run*.

"You knew," she said aloud, to a man who was dead or gone, and could not tell which was truer. "You knew what you'd done, and you couldn't carry it, so you gave it to me. Because I'm strong. Because I'm trained to swallow things that should kill me."

The words echoed in the pipe and came back sounding, horribly, like an answer.

Above her, muffled by earth and stone, she heard the singing note again—another Veil-thread coming down somewhere near the river—and beneath it, fainter, rhythmic: boots on the culvert grate. More than one pair. Patient. Spreading, like water finding a downhill grade.

They had followed her into the dark after all.

Tamsin wrapped the ledger back against her ribs, tied it tight, and looked east down the pipe, toward the gray far-off suggestion of open country and the road to Harrowgate. Behind her, the grate rattled. Ahead, the dark went on for a hundred yards and then, impossibly, warmly, there was a light where no light should be—a soft green-gold glow, steady, alive, like foxfire, like a lantern carried by something that was not carrying a lantern at all.

A voice drifted down the pipe, unhurried, amused, accented in a way she couldn't place—half river-smuggler, half something older:

"Well," it said. "You're a long way from any table, little keeper. And you're dripping guild-magic all over my culvert. Come along, then—before the tall gentlemen in black finish arguing with the lock."

Tamsin looked back at the grate one last time. Then she got on her hands and knees, and crawled toward the light.
