## 5. The Free City of Harrowgate

Tamsin smelled Harrowgate before she saw it: woodsmoke and wet wool, tallow and tar, the sour-sweet reek of too many bodies pressed into too little space. It was a city's smell, but wrong—concentrated, feverish, like a kettle left to boil without water. She had spent her life learning what things tasted like when they were about to go bad, and cities were no different from soup in that respect.

She came down out of the hills on the ninth day of walking, following a road that grew steadily more crowded as it went. Refugees moved along it in a slow, shuffling river—farmers with their chickens in wicker baskets slung over their shoulders, a widow pulling a handcart stacked with everything she owned wrapped in oilcloth, children asleep in the crooks of elbows because there was no room left for them to walk. Nobody spoke much. The silence was the worst part. On the road out of the capital, Tamsin had heard screaming, had heard prayers, had heard a man weeping so hard he vomited. Here, three hundred miles north, people had passed through all of that and come out the other side into something quieter and more terrible: the flat-eyed patience of those who no longer expected the sky to make sense again.

Because the sky was still wrong. The stars had not returned. Nine days now, and the night was nothing but a smooth black lid pressed down over the world, and every evening when the sun went under, Tamsin felt the whole road flinch at once.

The ledger rode against her chest, strapped beneath her coat in a sling Sorrel had sewn with his own hands years ago—for carrying poison vials, originally, though he'd repurposed it one drunken night and never taken it back. It was warm against her sternum. Or she imagined it was. She had stopped trying to tell the difference somewhere around day four.

Harrowgate announced itself first by its walls—old ones, dwarf-cut granite gone black with centuries of rain—and then by the smell, and then by the sound: a low continuous murmur like a hive that had been kicked. The gates stood open. That surprised her until she saw why. There was no point closing them. Half the realm was trying to get in, and the other half was already inside.

A woman in the livery of the Harrowgate constabulary—what remained of it—stood on a barrel at the gate arch, calling out assignments in a voice sanded raw. "Coppersmiths' Row is full! Coppersmiths' Row is FULL. If you've a trade, go to the Guild Annex and they'll find you a floor. If you haven't, the temple courtyard, the granary undercroft, or the east terraces. If you're fae—" she paused, and Tamsin watched the pause travel through the crowd like a stone through water, "—the Undermarket. Don't make trouble. We haven't got the hands for trouble."

The crowd compressed around the gate and Tamsin let it carry her through, small and unremarkable, a tired woman in a road-dusted coat. That was the gift of her life, she supposed. Poison-tasters learned early how to be furniture. You sat at the edge of kings' sight and you did not draw the eye, because the eye that lingered on a taster started wondering what else could be done with one.

Inside, Harrowgate was drowning. The free cities had always been strange hybrids—human law above ground, fae custom below, the two meeting in marketplaces where you could buy iron nails and bottled moonlight from adjacent stalls—but Harrowgate had been strangest of them all, built as it was directly atop one of the old covenant-stones, a node in the Veil's architecture. Tamsin had audited the maintenance payments routed here for six years. She knew the numbers. What she hadn't known, until now, was what the numbers *were*.

The streets glowed. Faintly, unevenly, like coals under ash—the cobbles themselves carried a dim luminescence, rising in veins toward the city's heart, and everywhere the glow was strongest, refugees had clustered, sitting on their bundles, holding their hands to the light as if it were a fire. Some of them were praying. Some were just staring.

"The Veil," said a voice beside her—an old man, a tinker, watching her look. "It runs under the city. Always has. My grandmother used to say you could hear it humming on midsummer nights." He spat. "It's not humming anymore. It's *bleeding*."

Tamsin thanked him and moved on, and the ledger seemed to grow heavier with every step.

* * *

She found the guild annex by asking after work, because work was the one language every refugee still spoke. The annex occupied a former chandlery near the river, and it was chaos organized only barely: clerks with ink-stained fingers shouting trades at one another, a wall of wooden tallies, queues of people holding out hands to show calluses as proof of usefulness.

"I keep ledgers," Tamsin told the third clerk who asked.

"Ledgers." The clerk—a harried boy of maybe seventeen—looked up with the expression of someone who had heard the word four hundred times today. "Everyone can count."

"I keep *Veilwrights'* ledgers," she said, and lowered her voice. "Double-entry. Covenant-script. I can read the old notation."

The boy's face changed. Not to warmth—to caution, which in a collapsing world amounted to the same thing. He jerked his chin toward a door at the back. "Talk to the smith. Big fellow. He's been organizing the ward-work since the constabulary gave up."

The back room smelled of coal and scorched metal. Brannic Ashvale—if that was still his name; she'd later learn he'd shed the surname like a coat—was exactly as described: a head taller than anyone else in the building, broad as a door, with forearms crosshatched by old burn scars and a beard going grey at the chin. He was bent over a table covered in ward-plates, hammering a fine silver line into one with a precision that seemed impossible in hands that size.

He didn't look up. "If you're here about the plates, I need setters, not talkers. Can you set a ward-line?"

"No."

"Can you carry?"

"Yes."

"Then carry." He nodded at a stack of etched copper sheets. "Third terrace. The old woman with the goats says the light's failing on her street and she won't leave the goats."

Tamsin picked up the stack. It weighed as much as a child. She carried it.

It took three trips before Brannic spoke to her again, and when he did, it wasn't kindness—it was attention. He caught her wrist as she set down the last plate, turned her hand over, and frowned at the ink stains on her fingers. "You write more than you carry."

"I'm better at it."

"What kind of writing?"

"The kind your ward-plates are trying to imitate," she said, before she could stop herself. His frown deepened. She sighed, set down the plate, and unslung the ledger from beneath her coat.

Brannic went very still.

She watched him look at it—at the Veilwrights' seal pressed in green wax, at the covenant-script stamped along the spine—and she watched something move across his face that she would spend a long time learning to name. It wasn't greed. It was recognition, and grief, and underneath both, a bitter satisfaction like a man seeing proof of a crime he'd been called mad for alleging.

"Guildmaster's seal," he said quietly. "I haven't seen one of those in eleven years. Not since they struck my name off the rolls and burned my masterwork in the yard of the Hall of Smiths." He looked up at her, and his eyes were pale grey and very tired. "You're Okafor. The taster. Word came down the refugee lines about a guild-woman running north with a sealed book and Vey's hounds behind her."

"Word travels fast."

"Word travels fast when half the people carrying it are spies." He didn't reach for the ledger. That, more than anything, made her trust him—not the absence of wanting it, but the visible discipline of the absence. "Who gave it to you?"

"My guildmaster. Sorrel. Right before—" She stopped. Right before the wards failed. Right before the stars went out. Right before Sorrel vanished into the panic of the capital and she had run without looking back, because looking back meant admitting she might never learn whether he'd been murdered, arrested, or simply gone to ground like the institution-man he'd always been.

"Right before," Brannic repeated. He wiped his hands on his apron, leaving silver smears, and for the first time really looked at her—not at the ledger, at *her*, at the nine days of road and the hollows under her eyes. "You've eaten nothing today. Sit down. There's stew. It's bad stew. In my experience the bad stew is the honest kind."

She sat. The stew was bad. She ate two bowls, and somewhere between them, without quite deciding to, she began to talk—about the ledgers, about the starlight payments, about the unnamed buyer whose entries she'd found weeks ago and couldn't reconcile. Brannic listened the way he hammered: slowly, completely, missing nothing.

When she finished, he was quiet a long moment. Then: "Vey's been buying pieces of the covenant for years. I told the Hall of Smiths. I told the Wardens' Conclave. I told anyone who'd sit still long enough." His jaw worked. "They struck me off for slander. Said a disgraced apprentice had no business accusing a lord of the realm." He laughed, short and without humor. "Well. The stars went out. So perhaps I'll have my apology delivered posthumously, along with everyone else."

"You think the ledger matters."

"I think," Brannic said, "that if Vey's riders are willing to burn villages chasing a book, then the book is worth more than the villages. And I think a woman who carries it under her coat for nine days through open country, alone, is either a fool or the most valuable person in this city." He looked at her steadily. "Which are you?"

"Both, probably."

Something that was almost a smile touched the corner of his beard. "Good. Fools survive. The clever ones die first—they can't resist explaining themselves." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered a fresh stack of ward-plates. "There's someone you need to meet. Smuggler. Half-fae. Works the Undermarket. If anyone in Harrowgate knows what a sealed guildmaster's ledger is actually *for*, it's him. And if anyone knows how to get it where it needs to go—" he shrugged his enormous shoulders, "—also him."

"Why help me?" Tamsin asked. It came out blunter than she intended, exhaustion stripping her manners. "You don't know me. Your guild struck you off. Mine sold the world. Why does any of this matter to you?"

Brannic considered the question with the seriousness he seemed to bring to everything.

"Because I spent eleven years being right and useless," he said. "And I find I'd rather be useful." He hefted the plates. "Come on. The Undermarket opens at dusk. Which, these days, means whenever the dark falls—and the dark falls early now."

* * *

The Undermarket lived in a cavern system beneath Harrowgate's foundations, in the hollowed bones of the covenant-stone itself, and it was the only place Tamsin had seen since the Night of Dark Stars where anything resembled joy. Not happiness—joy was too strong—but a kind of defiant festivity, lantern-lit and loud, stalls crammed into alcoves, fae and human traders shouting each other down in three languages. A woman sold memories by the spoonful. A thing that might have been a large fox in a waistcoat offered odds on the war. Somewhere a fiddle played a tune that kept changing key to avoid being taxed.

Here, finally, Tamsin saw the exiles clearly. The fae who had crossed before the Veil frayed—or been pushed across, or fled across—moved through the crowd wearing glamour like worn clothing, patched and thin. She could see the seams now. Everyone could, since the sky went dark. Their true shapes showed through at the edges: a woman whose shadow had antlers; a man whose reflection lagged half a second behind him; a child whose eyes were the exact amber of lamplight and who never blinked.

They looked at her, some of them. Looked at her and away. She remembered Maerin's name from the ledgers—one of the seven signatories, an exile, payments routed through Harrowgate for decades—and wondered how many of these thin-glamoured strangers had signed away pieces of themselves to hold the world together, and how it felt to watch the world fail to hold.

"There," Brannic rumbled, and steered her through the crowd with the gentle inevitability of a barge.

The smuggler had claimed a stall at the cavern's far end, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging charms—iron horseshoes next to fae-knots next to saint medallions, a theology of pure commerce. He was slight and quick-moving, with skin the brown of river stones and hair braided close to his skull, and when he turned to greet a customer Tamsin saw his eyes: one dark, one the pale gold of the lamplight-child outside. Heterochromia, or heritage. With the fae, she was learning, the distinction mattered less every day.

He took one look at Brannic and grinned. "Ashvale. Still alive. The Wardens will be devastated."

"They'll live."

"And you've brought me a customer with a book-shaped bulge under her coat and guildmaster's ink on her fingers." The grin sharpened into something hungrier and more careful. "Let's skip the part where you pretend you're here for charms."

Tamsin glanced at Brannic, who gave a single nod. She drew out the ledger and set it on the stall's counter.

The change in the smuggler was immediate and total. The marketplace swagger dropped away like a mask slipping, and what was underneath was older, quicker, and considerably more frightened. He didn't touch the book. He leaned over it, nostrils flaring slightly—as if he could smell it—and his mismatched eyes tracked the covenant-script on the spine with the focus of a man reading his own death warrant.

"Where did you get this?" he asked. Quietly. All the performance gone.

"My guildmaster. Before he disappeared."

"Sorrel." The smuggler straightened. "Sorrel Dunmore. Green wax, double-entry, the notation with the little hooks on the sevens." He laughed once, a strange, unhappy sound. "I used to run contraband *for* that man. Starlight shipments. Never knew what they were for. Never asked. Asking questions in my trade gets you dead, so I made a religion of not knowing." He looked up at her, and his voice dropped further. "Do you know what you're carrying, taster?"

"A ledger."

"It's a *covenant*. Or the closest thing left to one. Seven signatures holding up the sky, and three of them cold in the ground already, and the rest scattered like dropped coins." He tapped the spine once, lightly, reverently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you hoped wouldn't wake. "Every ward in every free city is running on borrowed time because the thing this book *is* is coming apart. And Lord Cassian Vey has been buying it apart, piece by piece, for years." He glanced at Brannic. "Your slander, smith. Turns out it was audit findings."

"I want it back," Brannic said dryly.

The smuggler ignored him. He was looking at Tamsin now with an intensity that made her want to step backward, except that some instinct—the same one that had kept her alive at a hundred poisoned tables—told her to stand still.

"You ran nine days with this against your heart," he said. "Through country crawling with Vey's riders. Alone. Untrained, unarmed, and—" he sniffed again, delicately, "—unpoisoned, which given your profession is either luck or a story I'll want to hear someday. Why? Sorrel's dead or bought, your guild is a crime scene, and nobody's paying you. Why carry it?"

Tamsin thought about the question. She thought about Sorrel pressing the book into her hands with terror in his eyes, about the entries she couldn't reconcile, about the black lid of sky and the old man at the gate saying *it's bleeding*. She thought about belonging everywhere and nowhere, about six years of keeping accounts for a world she'd believed in, and about how the only honest thing left in her possession was a debt she hadn't incurred but intended, somehow, to pay.

"Because somebody kept the books," she said finally. "Somebody always has to. And whoever kept them last did it badly enough to break the sky. So the next keeper had better do better."

The smuggler stared at her. Then he threw back his head and laughed—really laughed, bright and startling in the lantern-light, drawing glances from three stalls away.

"Oh, I like you," he said. "Yew. Formerly of nowhere, currently of everywhere, professionally of the space between. And before you ask—yes, I know where the signatories are hiding. Some of them. And yes, I can get you to them. And yes—" his grin faded into something graver, "—it's going to cost, and cost, and cost, because nothing in this world moves anymore without something else being paid for it. That's what a covenant *is*, taster. Debts, held in balance." He extended a slim hand across the counter. "So. Do we have a bargain?"

Tamsin looked at the hand. Then at Brannic, who shrugged his mountain shrug. Then at the ledger, warm against the counter, warm against her memory of her own sternum, patient as a loaded thing.

She shook Yew's hand.

His grip was cool and dry, and for just a moment—half a heartbeat, less—she felt something pass between them, faint as static, like the hum the tinker's grandmother used to hear on midsummer nights. Yew felt it too. His smile flickered, and for the first time since she'd met him, he looked genuinely unsettled.

"Huh," he said softly. "That's new."

Behind them, out in the market, a bell began to ring—the dusk bell, except it wasn't dusk yet, and the ringing wasn't the measured toll of hours but a rapid, urgent clanging, and voices were rising through the cavern tunnels, feet hammering down stone stairs.

Yew's head came up like a hound's. "Gate alarm," he said. "Someone's coming down the north road. Armed column. Flying no colors at all."

Brannic was already moving. "No colors," he growled, "means Vey's. They only fly banners when they want credit."

Tamsin slid the ledger back beneath her coat, against her heart, and followed the sound of the bells into the dark.
