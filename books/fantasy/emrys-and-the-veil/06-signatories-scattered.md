## 6. Signatories, Scattered

Yew had smuggled a great many things across the border in his years: salt-cured eels out of the Mirelands, dreamwine sealed in hollowed prayer-beads, a bride once—whole and weeping and wrapped in oilcloth like a bolt of silk—and never once, not in all that time, had he carried anything that felt like this.

The ledger rode against his ribs inside his coat, wrapped in three layers of wardcloth that Brannic had hammered flat with a smith's mallet, as if the metal itself could be persuaded to hold a shape around the book. It was warm. Books were not supposed to be warm. Yew kept his elbow pressed to it the way a man keeps pressure on a wound, and he did not look at Tamsin Okafor when she looked at him, because he knew what she wanted, and he knew what it would cost him to give it.

They sat in the back room of the Saltcellar, a dockside tavern in Harrowgate where the ale was bad and the landlord asked no questions because the landlord was dead and had been for two years, though nobody had told the customers. The room smelled of tar and tallow and the green, cold smell that came off the harbor now—not fish-smell, not weed-smell, but something older, the scent of a sea that had forgotten what shore it belonged to. Through the shutters, where the stars should have been, there was nothing. A sky like a held breath.

Brannic sat by the door with his arms crossed over his chest, a mountain pretending to be furniture. His hands, even at rest, kept making small motions—the ghost-motions of hammerwork, thumb rolling over forefinger, the smith's habit of shaping air. He had been doing it since they'd left the Mire of Small Debts behind them, or rather since the Mire had let them go, taking his oath as toll. Yew had watched him pay it. You didn't watch a man give up the thing that made him *him* without carrying some of the weight of it after.

Tamsin sat across from him, ledger between them on the table, her fingers resting on its cover the way you'd rest fingers on a sleeping animal. Careful. Ready to withdraw.

"Well," Yew said, and found his voice had gone dry as old rope. "You want to know what's in it."

"I've read what's in it," Tamsin said. "Names. Dates. Payments. Seven names, and under each one, clauses I can't read—guild-script, but older than any guild-script I know. What I want to know is what it *means*."

"And there it is." Yew leaned back until the chair creaked. He took out his knife, not because he needed it but because his hands wanted occupation, and began cleaning his nails with the point, an old tell of his that meant he was thinking about running. "There's always a moment, in my trade, when someone asks you what's in the package. And you have a choice. You can lie, and keep your fee. Or you can tell the truth, and lose everything except your life."

"Which do you usually pick?"

"Lie." He smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "I'm told I'm very good at it."

* * *

He had recognized the ledger the moment Brannic had unwrapped it in the safehouse above the chandlery, three nights ago. That was the truth of it, and it sat in him like a swallowed hook.

He'd been eleven years old, maybe twelve—half-fae children aged strangely, and his mother had never been precise about such things—sitting on the floor of his mother's counting-room while she and her partners argued over a manifest. There had been a visitor that night. Tall, gray-cloaked, face like weather. And the visitor had set a book on the table, and Yew's mother—who feared nothing that walked, swam, or flew—had gone white to the lips and said, *"That is not cargo. That is not cargo, and if you ever bring it into my house again I will burn this business down around your ears."*

He remembered the cover. Green leather gone dark with age, corners capped in silver worked into shapes like roots. He remembered thinking, even then, even at eleven: *that book is listening.*

Now it sat on a tavern table between him and a poison-taster, warm as a living thing, and Yew understood why his mother had reached for the word *cargo* first. In the smuggling trade you learned early that some things were not goods. They were not property. They were burdens that permitted themselves to be carried, for reasons of their own, and the only question was whether you'd be carrying them when they woke.

"You're stalling," Brannic said from the door. Not unkindly. The big man had a way of speaking that was like a hammer-tap on a wheel rim—just checking for cracks.

"Aye, I'm stalling," Yew admitted. "Because once I say this out loud, none of us get to unhear it."

Tamsin said nothing. She just waited, and there was something in her waiting that undid him more than impatience would have—a stillness, a patience, the particular quality of attention of someone who spent her days watching other people eat and die by degrees. He thought, absurdly, of his mother again. Then he stopped thinking about her, because that road led nowhere good.

"The covenant," Yew said. "Seven signatories. Three realms' worth of law bound up in one document. You've read the names."

"Six legible ones," Tamsin said. "The seventh is smudged, or struck through, or both."

"That'll be Vey." He watched her face do something complicated. "Lord Cassian Vey, before he was Lord Cassian Vey, before he was anything but a minor noble with more ambition than land—he signed. He was the human realm's youngest signatory. Chosen, they say, because he was clever and because he had no heirs and no ties, so nothing could be held over him. They thought that made him safe." Yew turned the knife over. "It made him hungry instead. Nothing to hold over a man means nothing anchoring him either."

"Vey signed the covenant," Tamsin repeated slowly. "And then he spent—" Her hand went to the ledger, almost involuntarily. "The payments. The starlight entries. Sorrel wasn't selling *to* him. Sorrel was selling him back pieces of his own signature."

"Now you're seeing it." Yew set the knife down flat, which for him was roughly equivalent to another man kneeling. "Here's what nobody tells you about the Veil, lady. Everyone thinks it's a wall. A curtain. Something that hangs between here and the Otherworld and keeps the fae on their side and us on ours. Priests talk about it like a door God locked. Scholars talk about it like a border on a map." He shook his head. "It's none of those things. The Veil isn't a thing that exists *between* the worlds. The Veil is the thing that lets both worlds exist *at all*. It's not the line between the page and the page next to it. It's the spine."

* * *

His mother had explained it to him exactly once, the night before she died, and she had used the only metaphor that had ever made sense to her: ships.

*"Every world is a hull, love. And the Veil is the keel. You don't see a keel. You don't sail to it. But take it out, and the hull doesn't spring a leak—it stops being a ship. The water comes in everywhere at once, and there's no inside anymore, and no outside, and everyone drowns in the same drowning."*

She had laughed after saying it, that bitter half-fae laugh, and drunk herself to sleep, and in the morning Yew had decided it was just one more of her drunken cosmologies, right up there with her theory that the moon was a debt-collector and the tides were its ledger.

He was not laughing now. Nobody was laughing now, with the keel visibly rotting overhead, the whole black sky gone smooth as the bottom of a barrel.

"The covenant is the skeleton," he said aloud, and heard how his voice came out hushed, as if the room itself might object. "Seven signatories, seven anchors. Each one holds a piece of the Veil's structure in place—not by magic they cast, but by *agreement*. By consent, written down and witnessed by both realms. That's what the guild was for, originally. Not brokering bargains between merchants and hedge-witches. Brokering the one bargain that keeps reality load-bearing. Every clause in that book is a rib. Every signatory is a joint. And every payment Sorrel made to Vey—" he tapped the ledger's cover, gently, the way you'd tap a horse you didn't want spooking, "—was a bone being sold off."

"Then why hasn't everything ended already?" Brannic rumbled. "If each sale breaks a piece—"

"Because the Veil is dying slow, not fast. Like a man bleeding inward." Yew spread his hands. "You've seen the wards fail. You've seen the stars go out—the stars are the oldest part of the structure, the roof-beams, the parts anchored deepest in the Otherworld. When those went, it wasn't the end. It was the *diagnosis*. The covenant can lose pieces and limp along, the way a body can lose teeth. But there's a point past which—" He made a small gesture, open-handed, letting the sentence die the way the sky had died.

Tamsin had gone very still. Yew had seen stillness before—in soldiers, in cornered animals, in people reading a letter that changes everything—but hers was different. Hers was the stillness of a professional absorbing information. Cataloguing. He realized, with a small cold shock, that she was doing to this revelation what she must do to poisoned food: taking it in, holding it, testing it against everything she knew, waiting to see what damage it did.

"How many are left?" she asked finally. Quiet. Level.

Yew counted on his fingers, and hated how theatrical it looked, and did it anyway because the number needed to be *seen*, not just spoken.

"Seven signatories. Three dead—true dead, ash-and-grave dead, and their anchor-pieces fell loose into the Veil like dropped stitches. One of them was Maerin's brother, if the rumors hold, which is why Maerin hates your guild with the heat of a forge-fire, but that's a story for when we're desperate enough to need her, which we will be." He folded one finger down. "Two hiding. Signatory Ede Vasska, who was the Otherworld's keeper of thresholds and has not answered a knock in forty years; and Signatory Corvin Hale, a human hedge-lawyer who wrote himself out of his own name to escape notice, and is somewhere in the free cities wearing a face that isn't his. Finding either of them is a task for a saint or a smuggler, and I'm no saint." Two more fingers folded. "One is Vey himself, sitting in his war camp with his stolen pieces, feeding the Veil's own flesh into his cage-lamp to make himself strong." A third finger. "And one—" the last finger, the smallest, "—is whoever the seventh entry belongs to. The smudged name. The one your Sorrel struck through, or tried to."

Silence. The harbor breathed against the pilings below, slow and wrong, like a sleeper whose dreams had turned.

"So the arithmetic is this," Yew went on, softer now. "Three dead. Two hidden. One enemy. One unknown. And a covenant that needs *all seven*—or something that can stand in for what's missing—to be reforged before what's left of the Veil finishes coming apart. This isn't politics, lady. Politics is men fighting over a house. This is the house finding out it was built on a living thing, and the living thing has started to leave."

* * *

Tamsin looked down at the ledger for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of someone handling something fragile in a room full of sharp edges.

"My whole life, the guild taught me that the covenant was a treaty. A contract. Important, yes—reverent, even—but *legal*. Something you audit. Something with line items." Her thumb moved across the silver root-work on the cover, back and forth, a small unconscious motion. "Nobody ever told me it was load-bearing."

"They wouldn't," Yew said. "That's not malice. That's architecture. You don't tell the stones they're holding up the cathedral—it makes them nervous, and nervous stones crack. The guildkeepers who built this system figured that out a long time ago. Keep the signatories ordinary. Keep them *small*. A hedge-lawyer. A threshold-keeper. A minor lord with no ties. People who'd never think to ask what they were actually holding up, because the answer would terrify them into letting go."

"And me?" Tamsin asked. "Where does a food-taster fit into the architecture?"

And there it was. The question he'd been dreading since Harrowgate's broken quays, since the night the stars went out and he'd seen her standing in the crowd with a sealed ledger pressed to her chest like a shield, looking less like a fugitive than like a woman who had just been handed a bill for something she hadn't ordered.

Yew could have lied. He was good at lying; it was practically a family trade, second-generation, half-fae on his mother's side, and the fae half had given him a tongue that could talk a customs officer out of his own boots. He could have told her she was a coincidence, a courier, a pair of quick legs and a quick mind in the right place at the wrong time.

But Brannic was watching him from the door with those patient smith's eyes, and the ledger was warm against the table between them, and Yew was tired—tired in the marrow, tired of carrying things alone, tired of the specific loneliness of knowing what things weigh.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I know this much: the guild doesn't waste training. A taster learns to survive every poison in both realms—that's not a job skill, lady, that's a *curriculum*. Somebody designed that curriculum. Somebody had a use in mind for a person who could drink what would kill anyone else." He paused. "And I know that when the covenant was first written, seven hundred years ago, the signing required witnesses who could survive the presence of both realms' law in one room. Poison-tasters. Immunity-bearers. The old texts call them *vessels*."

Tamsin's face did not change. That frightened Yew more than any expression would have.

"Vessels," she repeated.

"For the covenant's weight. For whatever the reforging requires." He shrugged, helpless. "I'm a smuggler, not a scholar. I know what things cost and how to move them. I don't know what the old texts mean by *vessel* beyond the obvious, and the obvious is: a container made to hold something no one else can carry."

The fire in the little stove ticked and settled. Somewhere above them, in the dead landlord's tavern, a refugee was singing a song about home in a language that had no country anymore, and the melody drifted down through the floorboards like smoke.

Brannic shifted by the door. "So we find the two who are hiding," he said. Practical as an anvil. "Before Vey finds them first."

"We find the one who hates her first," Yew corrected, nodding at Tamsin. "Maerin. Because Maerin knew Sorrel, and Maerin knew the covenant's inner clauses, and if anyone can read what's been struck through in that seventh entry, it's her. Also because she'll try to kill Tamsin on sight, and better we're there for that than not."

"Wonderful," Tamsin said dryly, and something in the dryness steadied Yew more than any speech could have. Here was a woman who had just learned the sky was a ceiling and the ceiling was falling, and her response was *wonderful*, in the tone of a woman noting that the wine cellar had flooded.

"She lives beyond the Grayfen, three days east, in a tower that walks," Yew went on. "Don't ask. Fae nobility. The tower has opinions. We'll need provisions, false papers, and a story good enough to get us through the checkpoint at Fenmarch, and I happen to have all three, or the raw materials for all three."

"And the ledger stays with me," Tamsin said. Not a question. Her hand had closed over the book's cover, and Yew noticed—with the peculiar clarity of a man watching something decide itself—that the warmth of it seemed to lean toward her palm, the way a cat leans toward the one hand that feeds it.

"It was given to you," Yew said. "By Sorrel, with his dying breath, or his running one—I've stopped trying to guess which. Things given like that don't get repossessed. Believe me, I've tried."

He stood, joints cracking, and gathered his knife and his coat, and felt the ledger's absence against his ribs immediately, like a tooth pulled. Good, he thought. Let it be hers. Carried things should belong to someone, even when—*especially* when—they're the kind of thing that carries you back.

At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at the two of them: the disgraced smith standing guard over nothing but a promise, and the poison-taster bent over a book full of bones, reading by tallow-light the architecture of a falling sky.

"One more thing," Yew said. "About the seventh entry. The smudged name." He hesitated, and the hesitation cost him, and he paid it anyway. "My mother kept a copy of the old manifests. Guild manifests, from before the schism. I read them as a boy, bored, hiding from her partners. And I remember—one entry, one name, struck through in ink that glittered. Starlight-ink. The same ink your Sorrel used for Vey's payments."

Tamsin looked up sharply. "What name?"

Yew smiled, and this time it reached his eyes, and what it reached there was fear.

"Not a name," he said. "A title. *The Vessel Who Signs.* And beneath it, in smaller script, the words every smuggler dreads more than any customs seal: *Expected. Unarrived. Still expected.*"

He pulled the door open onto the starless street, where the fog was coming in off a sea that had forgotten its shores, and the last thing he saw before stepping out was Tamsin's hand tightening on the ledger—as if the book had shivered—or as if she had.
