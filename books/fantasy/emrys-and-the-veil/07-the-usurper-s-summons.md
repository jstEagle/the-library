## 7. The Usurper's Summons

The herald came at noon, when the light over Harrowgate was thin and gray as old pewter.

Tamsin saw him first from the wall above the Salt Gate, where she had gone to breathe air that did not smell of the ledger's ink and Yew's pipe-smoke. The road from the south was empty of armies—she would have felt that in her teeth by now, the way she felt poison before it ever touched her tongue—but it was not empty of *him*. A single rider on a white horse too fine for the dust he raised, wearing colors that hurt to look at: a livery of ash-gray shot through with veins of gold, like metal cooling after a fire. Vey's crest rode on his breast—a tower split down the middle, its halves leaning apart—and beneath it, absurdly, a scroll case sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"He's early," said Brannic beside her. He had come up without her hearing him, which for a man his size was either a courtesy or an omen. "Heralds are supposed to give you time to dread them."

"Maybe he thinks we've had enough dread already." Tamsin watched the rider slow at the gate, dismount with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once been told *no* by a locked door. "Or maybe he wants us to know he isn't afraid of what's left of the sky."

She looked up as she said it, because everyone in Harrowgate looked up now, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Where stars should have been there was only a smooth black ceiling, faintly wrong, like skin grown over a wound. The Veil's fraying had a texture to it that her eyes kept trying to resolve and could not—shimmer without light, depth without distance. Three days since moonrise had failed to come. Three days since Sorrel had pressed the ledger into her hands and told her to run, and she was still not sure whether the word *run* had meant *save yourself* or *carry this*.

Below, the gate-warden was arguing with the herald, and losing. Tamsin could tell by the set of the man's shoulders—the particular slump of a soldier who has been given an order he cannot follow and an order he must not refuse, both from the same mouth. She was moving toward the stairs before she had decided to move.

"You don't have to be the one," Brannic said behind her.

"Yes, I do." She took the steps two at a time. "He knows my name. I'd rather hear how from his own mouth than spend a week wondering."

* * *

They received the herald in the moot-hall of the Salt Ward, which was the largest room Harrowgate's refugees could spare and the smallest room that could hold their pride. It had been a fish-market once; the smell of brine lived in the stones, and the long trestle tables still bore knife-scars from a hundred years of gutting seasons. Now they bore instead the marks of a city filling up with the displaced: a fae exile family sleeping under a sailcloth awning in the corner, their skin gone translucent with grief for a homeland that was being unmade behind them; human dockworkers playing dice with borrowed luck and losing it honestly; children of both kinds chasing each other between the table legs, not yet old enough to know which side of the Veil they were supposed to fear.

Yew lounged against a pillar near the door, arms crossed, watching the herald the way a smuggler watches a customs man—cataloguing exits, weighing pockets. Maerin stood apart, as she always did, wrapped in a gray traveling cloak that did nothing to hide the fact that she moved like water deciding to be a woman. Her eyes, when they flicked to Tamsin, still carried the memory of a blade at her throat three nights past. But they also carried something newer. They had read the ledger together since then. They had seen the forged countersignature, Tamsin's name written in a hand almost—but not quite—her own, and something had shifted between them that neither had words for yet. Not trust. Recognition, maybe. Two people who had both been robbed of the same thing.

The herald stood in the center of the hall as though it were a throne room, and perhaps that was the point. He was young, beautiful in the way courtiers are bred to be, and utterly composed—which meant, Tamsin thought, that he had been chosen precisely because nothing in Harrowgate could rattle him. He let the silence stretch until it creaked. Then he bowed, exactly deep enough to be correct and shallow enough to be insulting, and produced the scroll.

"In the name of Lord Cassian Vey, rightful regent of the unified realm, sovereign over the waking world and warden of its borders—" here his voice did something subtle, a half-tone drop, acknowledging without naming the black sky overhead, "—I bring terms."

"Terms," Maerin repeated. Her voice was soft and terrible. "For what? We have committed no crime but existing."

"For your safety," the herald said, and smiled with the lower half of his face only. "Which is, I am instructed to observe, currently stored in a ledger."

The hall went very quiet. A die stopped rolling somewhere. Tamsin felt every eye in the room swing to her—not accusingly, not yet, but with the hungry arithmetic of people calculating what one stranger's secrets might cost them all.

She stepped forward. Her heart was doing something cold and quick behind her ribs, but her hands were steady, because hands were the one part of herself she had trained past the reach of fear. Ten years at the king's table taught you that. Your face is a dish you serve to others; keep it clean.

"I'm Tamsin Okafor," she said. "You seem to know that already. So let's skip the part where you pretend this scroll is addressed to anyone else."

Something flickered in the herald's composure—surprise, quickly filed away. Courtiers, she knew, were trained against tantrums and treachery, but not often against bookkeepers.

"Lady Okafor," he said, unrolling the scroll with a snap. "Formerly of His Majesty's household, appointed food-taster at nineteen years of age. Trained under Master Corvane's regimen—cantharides, henbane, arsenic in graduated doses, the bitter roots of the Otherworld's own nightshade. A remarkable constitution. The Guildmaster Sorrel spoke highly of it."

The name landed like a hook behind her sternum. She did not let it show. *Sorrel spoke highly of it.* As if her body were a vintage. As if her childhood of swallowing fire a sip at a time had been a *recommendation*.

"And what does Lord Vey want with my constitution?" she asked.

"The same thing he wants from everyone of value: honest employment." The herald's tone warmed into something almost reasonable, which was the most frightening register it had held yet. "Lord Vey is not the monster your fugitive friends have painted. He is a realist. The Veil is dying—that is not his doing, whatever the fae exiles whisper; the covenant was rotting for a century before he ever touched it. He means to do cleanly what the guild did corruptly. Sever the realms. End the slow bleed of magic out of your world and theirs. One world, whole, governed, *lit by its own sun*. No more border wards failing. No more bargains trading luck for years. No more guilds selling pieces of the sky to whoever pays in starlight."

He paused, delicately, letting the last phrase find its target. Tamsin kept her face clean. Inside, something that had been Sorrel's voice in her memory—the voice that had taught her the ledgers, that had called her *my girl* in the dry way he called everything *my girl*—curled up tighter around its wound.

"The amnesty is simple," the herald continued. "Deliver the ledger of the Veilwrights' Guild to Lord Vey's steward within six days. In exchange: full pardon for yourself and any who shelter you. A position, if you wish it—Lord Vey has great need of someone with your particular talents, and he pays better than a dead guild ever did. Refuse—" and here the warmth drained out of his voice so completely that the temperature of the hall seemed to drop with it, "—and Lord Vey will be forced to conclude that Harrowgate harbors assets of strategic value and declines to surrender them. You have seen the maps. You know where his legions are. Harrowgate is first. Not because it is guilty. Because it is *convenient*."

Silence. Somewhere in the corner, one of the translucent fae children began to cry, quietly, the way children cry when they have learned that loud crying brings soldiers.

Tamsin found that she was not looking at the herald at all. She was looking at the sailcloth awning, at the small huddled family beneath it, at the mother's hand resting on the child's back with the automatic tenderness of someone who has nothing left to give but that. And she was thinking, with a strange detached clarity, about the first day of her training, when she was nine years old and Master Corvane had put a thimble of something bitter in front of her and said, *This is how you learn what the world is made of. You taste it. All of it. Even the parts that are trying to kill you.*

She had spent her whole life believing that competence was a kind of belonging. That if she was useful enough, careful enough, steady enough, the guild's stone halls would be hers, the king's table would be hers, some place would finally say *yes, you, this is where you sit*. And every institution that had ever said those words to her had been lying, or worse, had been telling the truth while meaning nothing by it—a place to stand, not a place to be.

The herald was waiting for her answer with the patience of a man holding a lit fuse.

"You said six days," she said.

"Five now. I arrived at noon."

"Convenient," she echoed, and heard Maerin make a sound that might have been approval.

Then Tamsin stepped closer to the herald, close enough to see the gold veins in his livery were actual thread-of-gold, expensive, torn from some loom in a capital that still believed itself safe. She kept her voice level, pitched for him alone, though the hall was silent enough that every word would carry anyway.

"Here is my counter-offer, and you may carry it back verbatim. Tell Lord Cassian Vey that I know what he bought from the Veilwrights' Guild, because I have audited the payments. I know what segment of the covenant purchased the collapse of the northern wards. I know what he paid for the silence of the western signatories. And I know"—she let this one land slowly, watching his composure test itself and hold, barely—"that every one of those transactions required a countersignature. Guild law. No sale of covenant-matter is valid without two names. His purchases went through. Which means somewhere in his possession, or in his memory, is a forged version of my hand. Ask him, next time he looks at his trophies, whose name he stole to buy the end of the sky."

The herald's mask did not crack. But behind it, Tamsin saw, something recalculated—the way a duelist revises his estimate of an opponent's reach. Good. Let him report that she was not a courier to be threatened but a witness to be handled carefully. Handled carefully meant delayed. Delayed meant days.

"As for amnesty," she went on, "I decline. Not bravely—I want to be clear about that. I'm afraid. I've been afraid since the stars went out, and I expect to stay afraid until this is finished or I am. But justice doesn't come from thrones. I learned that from a ledger full of everything thrones have ever bought. So no. Harrowgate doesn't burn for me, and I won't be bought to prevent it, and if your lord sends his legions here he'll find the city empty and the ledger gone and every mile between here and the Veil's heart costing him more than he budgeted for."

She stopped. Realized she had said more than she intended—the Veil's heart, aloud, in front of a Vey man—and cursed herself even as she watched the herald file that away too. Well. He would report it, and Vey would move his lines, and the clock would run faster. There was no help for it now except speed.

The herald rolled the scroll closed with a crisp motion. "That is your answer, then."

"That's my answer."

"A pity." He bowed again, correct and shallow. "Lord Vey hoped you'd prove sensible. He finds waste distasteful." At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and glanced back—not at Tamsin, but at Yew, at Maerin, at the whole ragged assembly of them, cataloguing faces the way a tax-man catalogues heads. "Six days ago there were seven signatories of the original covenant," he said mildly. "Three are dead. One is my lord. That leaves three. My lord bids me mention, in closing, that he knows where two of them are hiding. He wonders aloud—one does wonder—whether they know where *you* are."

And he was gone, boots ringing on the salt-stone, leaving the hall full of the shape of what he'd said.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then the dice started again, deliberately, the sound of ordinary people insisting on ordinary life, and the spell broke.

Maerin came to stand beside Tamsin at the trestle table where the herald had left nothing but the ghost of his presence. "He was lying," she said. "Partly."

"About which part?"

"The signatories. He wanted to watch your face when he named the number. If Vey truly knew where the two hidden ones were, he would not send a herald—he would send knives, as he sent them for Sorrel." Her jaw tightened; Maerin had known Sorrel longer, and hated him more thoroughly, and grieved him in a way she refused to examine. "No. The threat is a fishing line. He threw it to see what we'd scramble to protect."

"Then we let it dangle," Tamsin said. "We change nothing we were going to do, and we do it faster." She turned to the pillar where Yew had gone very still, his smuggler's face working through calculations she could nearly read. "You know what he was really offering, don't you?"

Yew exhaled smoke he hadn't been holding. "A way to divide us. Amnesty for the ledger is amnesty for whoever carries it. He figures one of us gets greedy, or one of us gets scared, and delivers the rest." He tapped the pipe out against his boot. "It's a good play. I'd have run it myself, back when I was the kind of person who ran plays like that."

"It won't work," Brannic rumbled, from the doorway, where he had planted himself like a piece of the architecture. "None of us is selling anyone."

"No," Tamsin agreed quietly. "But that's not what frightened me in there." She looked down at her own hands—steady, trained, worth a salary to a usurper. "What frightened me is that he wasn't lying about knowing my name. Or my training. Or Sorrel's regimen, dose by dose. Someone gave him a complete accounting of me, Brannic. Someone who had access to guild records. Someone who knew what I was *for*."

The word was out before she could stop it, and she didn't fully understand why she'd chosen it—*for*, as if she were an instrument with a purpose recorded somewhere in a column she'd never been permitted to audit. But Maerin's head came up sharply, fae-sharp, catching the note in it, and Yew's eyes narrowed, and in the silence that followed Tamsin understood that the question underneath her whole life had just changed shape.

Not *who forged my signature.*

But *why did the guild raise me this way at all—and what, exactly, was I being prepared to survive?*

Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a tide waiting for permission. Five days. Somewhere south, legions were counting the same number down.

"We leave for the Mire tomorrow," Tamsin said. "Before he learns he doesn't need to burn Harrowgate at all—only follow us."
