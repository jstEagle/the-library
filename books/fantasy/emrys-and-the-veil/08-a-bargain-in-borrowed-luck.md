## 8. A Bargain in Borrowed Luck

Brannic had forged his first nail at seven years old, under the eye of a master who believed that iron taught honesty better than any priest. *The metal doesn't lie,* old Hessa used to say, rapping her knuckles against an anvil. *You give it heat and hammer and time, and it becomes exactly what you made it. No more. No less.* He had built his whole life on that creed—oaths taken in front of the forge-fire, contracts sealed in cooled steel, a reputation in Harrowgate's smiths' quarter as solid as a well-made hinge.

Which was why it galled him so thoroughly to be standing at the edge of a bog that ran on lies of a gentler kind: the small, soft debts that people owed each other and never paid.

The Mire of Small Debts spread out before them like a wound in the world, gray-green water stitched with hummocks of black peat and pale, bone-colored reeds. There was no sun overhead—there hadn't been since the stars went out—but a diffuse, sourceless light clung to the mire's surface, the way light clings to the inside of a closed eyelid. Mist moved across it in slow, deliberate curls, and every so often something beneath the water exhaled, a long bubbling sigh that smelled of old promises left out in the rain.

"It doesn't look so bad," Yew said, which was how Brannic knew it was very bad indeed. The half-fae smuggler stood with his arms crossed, weight on his heels, studying the mire the way he studied locked doors: looking for what it wanted from him. "Bad bogs kill you quick. This one wants to talk first."

Tamsin said nothing. She had been quiet for two days—not the sharp, ledger-keeper silence of someone calculating, but the hollowed-out quiet of someone who had learned her whole life might have been a forgery. Brannic kept catching himself watching her the way he watched metal in the quench: waiting to see if it would crack or hold. So far it held. But held metal could still be brittle.

"Tell me again why we can't go around," he said.

"Because around is nine days through Vey's patrol lines," Tamsin said, without looking up from the ledger she'd wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. "Through is one day, if we pay."

"And what does a bog charge?"

Yew smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of a man who had grown up half in this world and half in the other, and knew exactly how much neither side liked him. "Everything you owe. That's the trick of it. The Mire of Small Debts isn't a place, it's an account. Every favor you ever accepted and never returned. Every kindness you pocketed because returning it would have cost more than keeping it. Every *I'll pay you back* you meant when you said it and forgot by morning." He nodded at the mist. "All of it comes due at once, in there. And if your debts weigh more than you do—" He drew a finger across his throat, then, after a moment's thought, pointed down at the black water instead. "Down. Not dead, exactly. The mire keeps debtors. I've seen them. Faces in the reeds, still mouthing apologies."

Brannic looked at the bog for a long time. Somewhere in its depths, something sighed again, patient as interest accruing.

"I don't owe anyone anything," he said. "I've kept my accounts clean my whole life. Ask anyone in the quarter."

"That," said Yew gently, "is exactly what everyone says right before they go in."

* * *

They camped that night at the mire's edge, in the lee of a leaning waystone so old its markings had worn to suggestions. Brannic built no fire—the lightless sky made flame too visible, and besides, the reeds seemed to lean toward warmth the way beggars lean toward bakery windows. They ate cold travel-bread and smoked fish Yew produced from somewhere in his coat with the unbothered sleight of a man who had never once told anyone where his things came from.

It was Tamsin who finally broke the silence, in the flat voice she'd been using since the drowned archive.

"The signatory we're going to see. Maerin." She turned the fish over in her fingers without eating it. "Yew said she hates guildkeepers. Hates humans who touched the covenant at all. If she reads that ledger and sees Sorrel's hand selling pieces of the Veil to Vey—"

"She'll want to kill you," Yew agreed comfortably. "Probably will try. Maerin's always been more sword than diplomat."

"You're very calm about walking me into that."

"I'm very calm about most things that aren't my problem yet." He tore his bread in half and offered her the bigger piece, which Brannic noticed, and filed away, and said nothing about. "Besides. You wanted the truth about your guild. Truth's rarely polite."

Brannic chewed slowly. He had known these two for less than a week—a disgraced ward-smith and a smuggler and a runaway ledger-keeper, three mismatched rivets holding together a boat that shouldn't float—and already they were planning to walk into a fae noble's grudge together. It occurred to him, not for the first time, that trust was a strange kind of metal. You couldn't test it cold. You only found out what it was made of when you put it in the fire.

And here, he thought, looking at the mist-wrapped mire, was fire enough.

"There's a thing you should both know," he said, "before tomorrow."

They looked at him. In the sourceless gray light their faces were pale as unfinished work, features sketched rather than cast.

"I'm going to be the one who pays the toll," Brannic said. "If it comes to paying."

"You don't know that," Tamsin said.

"I know how tolls work. I've sat across the counter from enough customs-men." He set down his bread. "Here's the thing about me. I came up through the Harrowgate forge-guild. Took my oath at fifteen, same as every smith: hands to honest work, iron to honest purpose, no blade forged for a hand I haven't looked in the eye. And I kept it. Ten years, I kept it. Until the night the wards failed and the stars went out, and the guildmaster called an emergency session, and do you know what the great forge-guild of Harrowgate decided?" His laugh came out with slag in it. "They decided to wait. To see which way the wind blew. Half of them were already taking commissions from Vey's quartermasters—ward-anchors for his siege engines, binding-rings for those cages of his. *Honest work,* they called it. Work is work. Iron doesn't care whose hand holds it."

"But yours does," Tamsin said quietly.

"My oath did. So I broke it. Walked out mid-session, in front of everyone, and let them strike my name from the rolls." He turned his hands over, palms up, callused and scarred and suddenly feeling like evidence. "I've told myself since that it was principle. Clean break, clean conscience. But the Mire won't care about my story, will it? It'll care about the truth underneath. And the truth is I took favors my whole life and told myself I'd earned them. Hessa's teaching, unpaid. My master's patience, unpaid. Every meal at a neighbor's table when the forge ran thin, every *we're even, Brannic* that I nodded along to when we weren't, not quite, not yet. A life of small debts. Everyone's got one. Mine's just been sitting in me, compounding, and tomorrow that bog is going to read it aloud."

Silence, except for the mire breathing.

"So you'll pay," Tamsin said. "With what? What does a bog take?"

"Whatever weighs the most." Yew's voice had lost its flippancy; he was watching Brannic with something new in his face, something that might have been respect or might have been pity, and Brannic honestly couldn't tell which was worse. "For most folk it's memory. Love. Years off their life. For a smith—" He hesitated. "For a man sworn to a craft, there's really only one coin that outweighs everything else."

Brannic had known it since the waystone. Had probably known it since Yew first described the place. He said it anyway, out loud, because a debt spoken is a debt shaped, and shaped things can be worked.

"My oath," he said. "My craft-oath. The part of me that's a smith."

* * *

Morning—or what passed for morning under the starless sky, a slow brightening of nothing—came gray and thick as unworked clay. They stood at the mire's edge while Yew laid out the terms of passage the way he'd once laid out smuggling routes: precisely, economically, with the grim fluency of a man reciting rules he'd learned by nearly dying to them.

"You walk the causeway stones. Don't leave them, don't hurry, don't look down at the water longer than you have to—it looks back. The Mire will stop each of us in turn and present our account. You settle it or you bargain it down. Bargaining's allowed; it's a fae place, everything's negotiable, that's the horror of it. But whatever you promise in there, promise it *exactly*. No rounding, no generosity, no fine print. The Mire keeps ledgers too." He glanced sideways at Tamsin. "Good ones."

"And if someone can't pay?" Tamsin asked.

"Then the rest of us can cover him. Debts are transferable between companions—that's the other rule nobody tells you. Which is why," and here Yew's voice dropped into something almost gentle, "you two are going to stay behind me and say nothing, no matter what it shows you. Let it read me first. I've got decades of smuggling sins; I'll keep it busy."

"No," Brannic said.

Yew raised an eyebrow.

"You'll keep it busy, aye, but you're not paying for us. I heard the rule same as you did—debts transfer. Which means if I stand quiet and let you absorb mine, I've just added ingratitude to the pile, and the pile grows." He stepped forward, boots finding the first causeway stone, wet stone dark as a forge-floor. "I go first. I told you last night. This is my debt to pay and I'd rather pay it standing up than hiding behind a smuggler's coat."

"Brannic—" Tamsin started.

"Tamsin." He turned and looked at her, really looked, the way he looked at a piece before committing hammer to it. "Two days ago you learned your whole life might have been somebody else's design. Yesterday you didn't flinch when Yew said Maerin might gut you. You're carrying more than either of us and you haven't once asked to put it down. Let me carry this. It's the only thing I've got that's heavy enough to matter."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the small precise nod of a keeper closing a ledger line, and something in Brannic's chest settled the way a joint settles when it's fitted true.

They crossed.

* * *

The Mire began softly, the way all swindles begin. The first hundred paces were merely unpleasant—cold seeping through boot leather, reeds whispering, the causeway stones slick and uneven. Brannic counted his steps because counting was a form of prayer for men who worked with their hands. Behind him, Tamsin's breathing stayed level. Behind her, Yew murmured a running commentary of route-lore, low as a litany: *left foot on the flat stone, mind the hollow one, don't touch the white reed.*

At two hundred paces, the mist thickened, and the voices started.

At first Brannic thought it was the reeds. Then he understood it was the reeds—each pale stalk a throat, each seed-head a mouth—and they were all speaking at once, in a hundred familiar cadences, saying a hundred familiar things.

*"Thanks, Brannic, you're a lifesaver."*

*"We're square, right? We're square."*

*"I'll make it up to you someday, big man. Someday."*

His neighbor Odo's voice, from the winter the forge ran cold and Odo's family had fed him for a month. His master Corvin's voice, from the day Corvin had covered his mistake with a customer and never mentioned it again. Hessa's voice, oldest of all, from the last afternoon of her life: *Mind my tools for me, lad, just till I'm back on my feet.* Tools he still had. Feet that had never come back. A debt thirty years old, gathering interest in the dark of him.

"Don't answer them," Yew said tightly, at his shoulder. "Don't apologize, don't explain, don't promise. Just walk."

The causeway narrowed. The water rose on either side until it walked with them, hip-high and black and patient, and in its surface Brannic saw reflections that weren't his own: faces, dozens of them, floating upturned like drowned lanterns. Some were strangers. Some were not. One had Hessa's squint and it was mouthing something, over and over, and he wrenched his eyes forward because Yew had warned him about looking down and because he could not, *could not*, find out what she was saying.

Then the mist gathered itself, and the Mire spoke directly.

It did not use a voice so much as it used *his* voice, assembled from all the words he'd ever said, and it said:

**BRANNIC OF HARROWGATE. ACCOUNT PRESENTED.**

The world stopped. Not paused—stopped, the way a wheel stops when the axle seizes. The reeds quit whispering. The water quit breathing. Even Tamsin's footsteps behind him hung silent, though he could feel her there, a warm stubborn presence at his back.

And the account opened in him like a book.

He felt every entry at once. Odo's month of bread. Corvin's covered mistake. Hessa's tools. The widow Annet's gate-hinge, mended free, and the pies that followed, and the way he'd told himself the pies squared it when they hadn't, not quite, because Annet had needed the company more than the hinge and he'd given her the hinge and kept his evening. The apprentice he'd trained and then outshone and never thought twice about. The commission he'd taken from a Vey man two weeks before the stars died—one anchor-bracket, honest work, iron doesn't care—and the coin still in his coat, spent now, spent on this journey, on *them*, and somehow that made it worse, that his single treason had purchased his passage to righteousness.

The weight of it came down on him like a press. His knees found the causeway stone. Water lapped his thighs, cold as a verdict, and the faces in it turned toward him with terrible attention, waiting to hear what he was worth.

**SETTLEMENT REQUIRED,** said the Mire, in his own voice. **WHAT DO YOU OFFER?**

And here was the thing—the thing he would think about for the rest of his life, in whatever life the Mire left him. In that seized-still moment, with the whole gray account of himself open and weighing, Brannic searched himself for something to offer and found, to his astonishment, that the heaviest thing in him wasn't any of the debts.

It was the two people behind him.

It was Tamsin's flat brave voice saying *let me carry this* and meaning the opposite. It was Yew giving away the bigger half of his bread like it was nothing, like generosity was a habit he'd never examined. It was the strange, unearned, terrifying discovery that in six days of running from the end of the world, he had come to love two near-strangers more than he loved the craft that had made him—and that this, this and nothing else, was the truest metal he had ever poured.

So he gave them the next-heaviest thing instead.

"My oath," Brannic said aloud, and his voice cracked the stillness like a hammer on a bell. "My craft-oath. Sworn at fifteen before the forge-fire of the Harrowgate guild: hands to honest work, iron to honest purpose. Thirty years I've carried it. It's the biggest thing I own. Take it."

The stillness considered him.

**THE OATH IS NOT FREELY YOURS TO GIVE,** said the Mire. **IT BELONGS TO THE GUILD THAT HOLDS IT.**

"Then take my *keeping* of it." He forced the words up through the pressure on his chest. "The guild struck my name. They don't hold me anymore—I hold it. I've held it alone since the night I walked out. My keeping, my carrying, my living of it, every hour since—that's mine, and it's heavy, and it's yours. Take the smith out of me. Take the part that knows what iron wants. Leave me the hands if you must, but take the knowing."

The water rose. It rose past his waist, past his ribs, gentle as a tide coming for something it had always owned, and Brannic felt it begin—the unmaking, slow and thorough, like frost working into a joint. He felt the forge-heat in his palms dimming. He felt the deep wordless grammar of metal, the language he'd spoken since childhood, going quiet in him syllable by syllable, and he grieved it, gods, he grieved it, the way you grieve a limb, the way you grieve a father—

But underneath the grief, steady as a core of good iron, was this: *they get across.* Tamsin gets to Maerin. Yew gets to stop running. The Veil gets reforged. Somebody's hands, even empty ones, even these.

**ACCOUNT SETTLED,** said the Mire, in a voice that was no longer his at all.

The water released him all at once, like a fist opening, and Brannic came up gasping on the far side of the causeway's midpoint, soaked, shaking, and lighter in a way that frightened him more than any drowning. He knelt on the stone and pressed his palms flat against it and felt—nothing. No answering hum of ore below. No kinship with the earth's buried metal. Just stone. Just cold. Just a man's hands, empty as unfired clay.

Tamsin reached him first. She hauled at his arm with her small strong ledger-keeper's grip, and when he looked up her face was wet, though whether from mist or otherwise she'd have denied to her grave.

"Why," she said. "You barely knew us. Six days. Why would you—"

"Because you'd have done worse," Brannic said. His voice came out rusty, like a gate long unswung. "Both of you. Don't argue. I've watched you. You'd have paid double and called it fair." He managed something that was mostly a smile. "Someone has to be the selfish one. Keeps the average honest."

Behind them, Yew stood very still on the causeway, and his expression was one Brannic had never seen on the smuggler's mobile face: stripped bare, young, almost frightened. As if Brannic had done something Yew had spent a lifetime making sure no one ever did for him—as if a debt freely paid was a door he didn't know how to walk through.

"We move," Yew said finally, roughly. "Before it thinks of anything else to bill us."

They moved. The second half of the crossing passed in a gray blur, the Mire oddly subdued now, its voices reduced to mutters, its faces sinking back into the dark like sated creditors. Once, only once, a reed near Tamsin's ear whispered something in a voice like Guildmaster Sorrel's, and Brannic saw her go white and then set her jaw and walk on, and he understood that she was paying her own tolls in a currency he couldn't see, and that she would never show him the ledger of it.

They came out of the Mire at dusk-that-wasn't, onto hard ground and rising hills, and behind them the bog lay silent and smooth as a closed book.

That night, in the shelter of a ruined shepherd's cot, Tamsin sat beside him and said, "Show me your hands."

He showed her. Empty, callused, ordinary.

"They'll learn again," she said. "Not iron. But hands learn. That's the whole point of hands." And then, awkwardly, fiercely, the way people embrace when they've never practiced: she hugged him, quick and hard, and was embarrassed by it immediately, and pretended she hadn't.

Brannic fell asleep lighter than he'd been in thirty years and poorer than he'd been at seven, and woke before dawn to Yew shaking his shoulder, the smuggler's face taut as wire.

"Smoke," Yew said. "West ridge. A lot of it, moving fast." He paused, and the next words came out flat and cold. "And crows. Vey's crows, flying ahead of the burn lines. Whatever's out there—" He swallowed. "It's Maerin's valley. We're a day late, or we're a day early, and either way, someone's already lighting the fires."
