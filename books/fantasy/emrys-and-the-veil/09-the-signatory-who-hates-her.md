## 9. The Signatory Who Hates Her

The house at the edge of the Thornwood did not look like the home of a signatory. It looked like a wound that had learned to keep its own shape.

Tamsin saw it first through the trees, half a mile out, where the road gave up pretending to be a road and became a suggestion of packed earth between brambles. The cottage stood alone in a clearing gone wild with late autumn—blackberry canes strangled the fence posts, and the garden had surrendered to something with too many petals and a faint, wrong sweetness. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin gray thread, which meant someone lived there, and living things could be reasoned with.

"Charming," Yew said behind her. He had his hands in his pockets and his weight on his heels, the posture of a man who wanted very much to be somewhere else but refused to admit it. "I want you to know that when she kills us, I intend to haunt you specifically."

"She won't kill us," Brannic rumbled. The big smith carried his pack like it weighed nothing and his fear like it weighed everything; Tamsin could see both in the set of his shoulders. "Maerin's exiled, not mad. There's a difference."

"There is," Yew agreed, "and the difference is usually about forty seconds. That's how long it takes an exiled fae noble to decide whether a stranger is a guest or an insult."

Tamsin said nothing. She was watching the windows.

She had spent three days learning what she could about Maerin of the Autumn Court from Yew's scattered knowledge and Brannic's older, grudging memories—the fae noble who had signed the covenant two hundred years ago as her court's youngest voice, who had argued for binding the realms together rather than merely fencing them, and who had been cast out when the Veil began to fail and her people needed someone to blame. Exile for a fae was not imprisonment. It was amputation. Maerin had been cut away from everything that fed her—her court's music, its seasons, its slow green patience—and left to root herself in mortal soil that never quite took her.

Which meant, Tamsin thought, climbing the crooked gate, that they were not walking into the home of a woman. They were walking into the home of a scar.

She knocked.

Nothing. Then, from directly behind her—a voice like frost forming on glass: "You walk loudly for thieves."

Tamsin turned. No one was there. Then the air beside the door folded, the way heat folds above a summer road, and a woman stepped out of nothing.

Maerin was not beautiful in the way mortals were beautiful. She was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful—all clean lines and implied violence, her skin the pale gold of birch bark, her hair a fall of copper going silver at the temples in a way that suggested time touched her differently than it touched others. Her eyes were the color of deep water over dark stones, and they went from Yew to Brannic to Tamsin with the unhurried assessment of someone counting coins she intended to take back.

"You brought a ward-smith," she said to no one in particular. "A smuggler. And—" her gaze settled on Tamsin and stopped there, and Tamsin felt it like a hand pressing on a bruise, "—a guildkeeper."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Tamsin said. "And a taster."

"A taster." Maerin smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Yes. I know what your guild makes of tasters. Little foundlings raised on drops of nightshade until their blood forgets how to die. You people have always understood that bodies can be *spent* toward a purpose. It's the only language the Veilwrights ever spoke fluently."

"We've come about the covenant," Tamsin said, keeping her voice level the way she kept her face level across a king's dinner table while her tongue burned. "It's failing. The wards are down. The stars are out. And we think it can be reforged—but we need the signatories."

"The signatories." Maerin laughed then, and the sound was worse than the smile—bright and brittle as ice cracking on a pond. "Three of them are dead. Did your ledgers tell you that? Did your careful little entries record *how* they died? Slowly. Unraveling. Because the covenant they signed was being sold off piece by piece by the very guild sworn to keep it, and when a signatory's oath is broken without their consent, the oath breaks *them*."

"I know," Tamsin said quietly. "I've seen the entries."

Something flickered across Maerin's face—grief, quickly buried under fury, the way a hand covers a wound before company arrives. "Then you know, and you came anyway. To my door. Wearing your guild's manners and your guild's silence, asking me to trust the institution that murdered my friends and left me—" her voice caught, just once, a hairline fracture in stone, "—left me here. Rootless. Useless. Watching the sky go dark over a world I helped build and being unable to lift one finger to stop it because my own court stripped my name from me."

"We didn't come asking you to trust the guild," Brannic said. His voice was low and even, the voice he used with frightened apprentices. "We came asking you to trust *us*."

"And why would I?"

"Because," Yew said, and for once there was no mockery in him at all, "we're the only ones left who are trying to fix it. Hate us if you like. But hate us *usefully*."

For a long moment the clearing held its breath. Wind moved through the wrong-sweet flowers. Somewhere in the Thornwood, a bird made a sound and then thought better of it.

Then Maerin moved.

Tamsin never saw the decision arrive—only the result. One instant the fae woman stood six feet away; the next her hand was around Tamsin's throat, lifting her onto her toes, and there was a knife in her other hand, a long curved thing that drank the light rather than reflecting it. Tamsin's back hit the door of the cottage hard enough to knock the air from her lungs.

"You want to know what a broken covenant does?" Maerin whispered, and her breath smelled of cold iron and autumn leaves. "Let me show you. Let me show you what two hundred years of human greed feels like from the inside."

Tamsin's vision sparked at the edges. Her body did what three thousand dinners had trained it to do: it went calm. Her pulse didn't race—it *slowed*, deliberate, conserving. Her mind, that cold clear room where she catalogued flavors and symptoms, opened a new entry. *Pressure on the carotid. Four seconds. She hasn't cut yet. The knife is for threat, not use—or she'd have used it. She wants me to be afraid more than she wants me dead.*

"Fear," Tamsin rasped, "is a flavor. I taste it every day. Yours is underneath the rage. Bitter. Old. You've been swallowing it so long you've forgotten it isn't food."

The knife trembled. Just slightly. Just enough.

"*Don't*," Maerin breathed, "presume to read me, guildchild. You don't know what I—"

"The ledger," Tamsin managed. Black flowers were blooming at the edge of her sight now, pretty and terrible. "In Brannic's pack. Page forty-one. Read page forty-one before you decide what I am."

Maerin's eyes narrowed. For a heartbeat the knife pressed closer—Tamsin felt the cold kiss of it against her jaw, precise as a signature—and then the fae woman made a sound low in her throat, dropped her like a discarded coat, and stalked past Yew to tear the pack from Brannic's shoulder.

Tamsin crumpled against the door, coughing, dragging air. Brannic started forward; she shook her head. *Wait.* This was the moment. She knew it in her bones the way she knew a dish was poisoned before the first swallow—not from evidence but from the shape of the silence around it.

Maerin opened the ledger.

They watched her read. It was, Tamsin thought distantly, still rubbing her throat, like watching someone stand in a river as the current changes. Maerin's face went through winter: disbelief, denial, a fury so complete it passed through heat and came out the other side as something glacial and still. Her finger traced down the page. Stopped. Traced back. Stopped again.

Page forty-one. Tamsin had found it on her second night in Harrowgate, unable to sleep, working through Sorrel's sealed book by candlelight the way she worked through everything—methodically, thoroughly, tasting each entry for rot. Most of the ledger recorded Vey's payments in Sorrel's cramped, confident hand: segments of covenant, borders of the Otherworld, purchased and signed away. And beneath each of those entries, in the countersignature line where every transaction required a second mark of witness—

Her own name. Her own hand. Or something wearing her hand like a coat.

*Tamsin Okafor, witnessed and countersigned,* repeated down the years in flawless imitation of her script—the particular way her letters leaned, the small hook she made on the descender of the f, even the ink-blots where her pen had hesitated. Except Tamsin had never seen this ledger before Sorrel pressed it into her arms and told her to run. She had never countersigned anything beyond the guildhall's internal books. Someone had studied her writing for years. Someone close. Someone patient.

Someone who had built her into the crime without ever telling her she was in it.

"They forged you," Maerin said. Her voice had changed entirely. The frost was still there, but it had melted into something rawer. She looked up from the page, and for the first time she seemed to actually *see* Tamsin—the bruise rising on her throat, the steadiness of her gaze, the particular exhausted courage of a person who has been lied to by everyone who was supposed to protect her. "These signatures. They're yours."

"Not mine," Tamsin said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "But yes. They're mine. Every sale Vey made, every piece of the covenant he bought—I'm written into all of it. Whoever did this wanted the crime to end with me. When the Veil fell, the guild's records would name a single traitor. A taster. An orphan nobody would defend."

"Sorrel," Brannic said heavily. "You think it was Sorrel?"

"I think Sorrel knew," Tamsin said. "I think knowing and doing are different sins, and I think she spent years carrying both." She swallowed, and her throat protested. "I think she pressed this ledger into my hands because it was the only way to clear my name—bring it to the signatories, let them read the truth. And I think she knew what it would cost her."

Yew had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when a smuggling route turned out to be guarded. "So Vey didn't just buy the covenant," he said slowly. "He bought a scapegoat. Packaged. Delivered. With a forged provenance."

"He bought a story," Tamsin corrected. "Stories are what he trades in. Sever the realms, rule a world with no magic but his own, and when anyone asks how the old covenant died—here's the answer, written in a traitor's hand. Neat. Complete. Blame placed."

Maerin closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way one closes a coffin.

For a long moment she said nothing. She walked to the fence post at the edge of her ruined garden and stood with her hand flat against the wood, and Tamsin watched two hundred years move through her—the young noble who had signed her name to a bridge between worlds, the exile who had watched it burn, and now this: the discovery that the fire had been set deliberately, and that a stranger stood in her garden wearing the forged handwriting of her enemies' design.

When she turned around, her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"I nearly killed you," she said.

"Yes," Tamsin said.

"You tasted my intent and stood there and *let* me, because you needed me to read that page more than you needed your throat intact."

"I needed you to read it *angry*," Tamsin said. "Angry people read carefully. Calm people skim."

Something happened to Maerin's mouth then that might, in better light, among kinder histories, have been the beginning of a smile. It vanished immediately, but Tamsin had seen it. Filed it away. First entry in a new ledger.

"I hated your guild before tonight," Maerin said. "Two hundred years of hating. It kept me warm, some winters. But hatred aimed at an institution is a lazy hatred—it lets you pretend the people inside it are all the same creature." She crossed the clearing and stood before Tamsin, and up close Tamsin could see the age in her, centuries layered under the gold of her skin like rings in ancient wood. "You are not the same creature. You are something the guild grew in its cellar without understanding what it planted."

"That's the second time someone's said that to me," Tamsin said quietly. "I'm starting to worry it's true."

"It's true." Maerin said it without softness, as fact, as a blade laid on a table. "And here is what matters now: the covenant cannot be reforged by the dead, and it cannot be reforged by Vey, and it cannot be reforged by a guild that sold itself. It can only be reforged by its signatories—and by whatever vessel the original text demands. I signed my name to that bridge once. I will sign it again. Not for your guild." Her chin lifted, and for a moment Tamsin glimpsed what she must have been in the Autumn Court—young and blazing and certain that worlds could be mended. "For mine. For the courts that forgot me. For every star that went out while I sat in this garden hating safely."

She extended her hand—not the knife hand, Tamsin noted, and appreciated the courtesy—and after a moment Tamsin took it. Maerin's grip was cool and strong and utterly without warmth, and it was, nevertheless, the first handshake Tamsin had ever received that felt like being *counted*.

"Your throat will bruise," Maerin said. "Good. Wear it. Let the next signatory see what the truth costs to carry."

"There are two of them left," Yew said, perking up with the cautious optimism of a man who has survived many bad situations by treating each new development as potentially lucrative. "One's the Drowned Archive, if the stories hold. The other..."

"The other is worse," Maerin said, releasing Tamsin's hand and turning toward her cottage. "Come inside, all of you. If we're doing this, we do it properly—with maps, and wine, and the full ugly history of what your guild did to my friends. And then, tomorrow, we ride for the Archive before Vey's people realize the scapegoat they forged has grown teeth."

She paused at her door and looked back at Tamsin, and the frost in her eyes had shifted into something harder and more durable than hatred.

"One thing more, taster. The hand that forged your signature—whoever they were—they knew you. Knew your pen, your habits, your hesitations. Which means they watched you for years. Which means they may not be finished watching."

Tamsin felt the words settle over her like a cold cloak. She thought of Sorrel's face in the last lamplight, the sealed ledger, the weight of a book full of her own false confessions.

"I know," she said. "That's why we keep moving."

Maerin nodded once, sharply, satisfied—as if Tamsin had passed some final examination she hadn't known she was sitting—and pushed open her door onto firelight and the smell of old wine.

Behind them, far to the north, the starless sky hung like a held breath. And somewhere inside it, Tamsin could not help feeling, something that wore her handwriting was still writing.
