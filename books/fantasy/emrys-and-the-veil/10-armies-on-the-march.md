## 10. Armies on the March

Yew had spent eleven years learning the exact weight of desperation, and he could feel it coming up the road before he saw it.

It was in the way the ground trembled—not with hooves or engines of war but with footsteps, thousands upon thousands of them, arrhythmic and weary. It was in the smell that drifted ahead of the crowd like a tide-mark: woodsmoke, wet wool, sour milk, fear-sweat. And it was in the sound beneath the sound—the low murmur of people who had stopped asking questions because they already knew the answers were bad.

He stood at the crest of the Harrowgate road where it bent down through the ash-birches toward the river crossing, and he watched the refugee column come.

They choked the road for as far as he could see. A gray river of them, flowing west, away from the borderlands Vey's legions were chewing through farm by farm. Old men pulling carts stacked with everything they'd decided a life was worth carrying. Women with children strapped to their backs and hens under their arms. A fae tinker whose antlers had been filed down to stumps—Yew recognized the shame in how he kept his hood up even in this heat—and beside him a human family who gave him a wide berth without seeming to know why they did.

Eleven years ago, Yew would have seen this column and thought: *cargo*.

That was the honest word for it, and he made himself use it now, made himself stand in the truth of it. Cargo. People who needed to move fast and couldn't pay much, moving along routes he knew better than anyone alive, through checkpoints he could bribe, past ward-lines he could slip. He'd built his whole trade on the arithmetic of flight. A family fleeing with nothing pays what it has. A merchant fleeing with goods pays half his stock to keep the other half. The desperate don't haggle; haggling is a luxury of people with tomorrows.

He'd told himself for years that he was a mercy. That without him they'd be dead on the roads, robbed by worse men than him. He'd believed it right up until the night Tamsin Okafor had walked into his shop with a sealed ledger under her arm and a look in her eyes that said she knew exactly what he was, and instead of turning him in she'd asked for his help.

Nobody had ever asked Yew for help before. They'd asked for passage, for silence, for discretion. Help was a different currency entirely, and he'd found, to his considerable discomfort, that he wanted to be rich in it.

So here he stood, on a hillside above a river crossing, running smuggling routes into evacuation lines—for free. The word still sat wrong in his mouth. *Free.* Like a coin clipped so many times it barely passed.

"Staring at it won't make it smaller," said Brannic behind him.

The big smith came up the slope with his pack over one shoulder and a mule's lead in his fist, moving with the deliberate economy of a man who had learned to carry weight and stop apologizing for it. His forge-callused hands looked wrong holding a mule's rope. Everything about Brannic looked slightly wrong since Harrowgate's guild of ward-smiths had struck his name from their rolls—a man built for belonging somewhere, belonging nowhere.

"It doesn't get smaller," Yew said. "That's the problem. Three days ago the column ended at the mill. Now I can't see its tail."

"They'll keep coming until Vey stops pushing." Brannic shaded his eyes against the flat white sky—that awful starless sky, the one that still made Yew's half-fae blood itch like a wool shirt worn too long. "Where's the bottleneck?"

"The ford. One ferry, two barges, and a toll-warden who's charging three coppers a head and pretending it's official." Yew's mouth twisted. "I used to know that warden. He used to pay *me* for route advice. Now he's taxing grandmothers."

"And?"

"And I'm going to go have a conversation with him." Yew pulled his hood up. Half-fae features drew less notice covered—too-sharp ears, eyes the color of pond ice, that faint luminosity at the cheekbones that marked him as Otherworld-touched no matter how human his mother's blood had tried to make him. "You take the mule down to the east bank and start organizing. Families first, then the old, then anyone carrying children that aren't theirs. There are more unaccompanied little ones in that column than there should be. Count them."

Brannic's jaw tightened. He'd been counting them too. "And if the warden doesn't want a conversation?"

"He will," Yew said. "Everyone wants a conversation when you offer them the thing they're most afraid to ask for."

* * *

The thing Warden Doss was most afraid to ask for, it turned out, was absolution.

Yew found him in his toll-shed by the ford, counting coppers into a lockbox with hands that shook just enough to betray him. The shed smelled of lamp oil and old fish. Through the slatted window, the ferry groaned across the current, loaded to the gunwales with people and their bundled lives.

"Doss."

The warden flinched hard enough to scatter coins. When he turned and saw who stood in his doorway, his face went through three expressions in quick succession—recognition, calculation, and finally the sagging relief of a man caught by someone he'd already decided was going to catch him eventually.

"Yew." He wiped his palms on his apron. "If you've come about the tolls—"

"I've come about the tolls."

"They're sanctioned. Magistrate's writ. Costs of administration, defense of the crossing, I can show you the paper—"

"You can show me whatever paper you bought it with." Yew stepped inside and closed the door behind him, soft as a confession. Outside, the column shuffled forward, and somewhere in it a child was crying with the flat, exhausted persistence of a child too tired even to sob properly. "Doss. I ran contraband through four provinces. I moved stolen covenant-goods past wardens twice your grade. You want to know how I always knew which officials to bribe and which to avoid?"

Doss said nothing.

"The ones who shook while they counted." Yew nodded at the lockbox. "The steady-handed ones had made peace with it. You haven't. So let's skip to the part where you tell me why."

For a long moment the only sound was the ferry's winch and the river. Then Doss sat down heavily on his stool, and the story came out of him the way stories come out of men who've been carrying them alone too long—fast, and ugly, and relieved.

His garrison had been reassigned to the southern wall two weeks ago. He was alone at the crossing except for two river-hands. Three nights ago, riders had come wearing no colors at all, and their leader had asked him, very politely, whether he'd noticed any signatories of the old covenant passing through, any guildkeepers, any fae traveling under human glamours. And then he'd shown Doss what happened to crossings that obstructed Lord Vey's inquiries. There was a village upstream called Marrow's Bend that no longer appeared on any map, and Doss had kin there.

"So I take the tolls," Doss finished, staring at his shaking hands, "because if I'm a corrupt little thief skimming coppers off refugees, I'm not worth their attention. Being small is armor. Do you understand? I'm being *small*, Yew."

Yew understood. Gods help him, he understood completely. He had spent eleven years being exactly that kind of small—small enough that no army would bother crushing him, small enough that his conscience could fit in the space he occupied. He'd watched columns like this one from the far side of a ledger, calculating margins, and told himself that survival was neutral.

Tamsin's voice came back to him, from the night in Harrowgate when she'd laid the sealed ledger open on his counter and shown him the entries written in starlight. *Someone kept these books,* she'd said. *Every entry has a keeper. The question is which side of the ledger you're standing on.*

"Here's what happens now," Yew said. "The toll is over. Your two river-hands work the barges dawn to dark, no charge, families first. I'll put coin in your lockbox myself—enough that when Vey's people come asking, you can show them a fat box and a stupid face, and they'll believe you're exactly the small corrupt man you're pretending to be. You keep being small, Doss. But tonight, you're small and useful."

"And when they find out?"

"They won't. Hiding things is my trade." Yew paused at the door. "One more thing. Marrow's Bend. How many got out?"

"Some. Scattered. A few dozen, maybe, heading west."

"Then when this is over—if it ends well—you'll take me to them, and you'll say what you need to say." He held Doss's gaze until the warden looked away. "Redemption isn't a feeling, Doss. It's an appointment you keep."

* * *

By dusk the crossing had become something almost orderly, which on the Harrowgate road counted as miraculous. Both barges ran the river in shifts. Brannic stood hip-deep at the shallows directing traffic with a voice like a landslide, and the mule hauled carts out of the mud two at a time while refugee children—inevitably, irresistibly—climbed onto its back until Brannic gave up shooing them and simply became a slow-moving playground with a beard.

Yew worked the line itself, moving up and down the column with a scribe's slate, sorting. Families first. Unaccompanied children logged by name and description so they could be matched later—or so he told himself the list was for. He suspected, watching Brannic quietly feed six of them from his own rations, that the matching might take longer than anyone wanted to admit, and that neither of them minded.

It was near full dark—he still couldn't call it night, not with that blank ceiling overhead where stars should have been—when he reached the tail of the column and found the fae tinker with the filed-down antlers sitting alone by a cold fire ring, cradling a broken clockwork bird in both hands.

Yew knew him. That was the knife-edge of it. Knew him from the old days, from the border runs—Coriel, a tinker who'd once repaired the music-box Yew used to smuggle hollowed-out soul-charms in. Coriel had never asked what rode inside the boxes. Yew had never offered to tell him.

"Coriel."

The tinker didn't look up. "I know your step, half-blood. Everyone on this road knows your step. The smuggler who sold us all our exits." He turned the broken bird over. Its brass wing hung by one pin. "Half the people in this column paid you something, sometime. Did you know that? Ask around. Ask any of them. They'll tell you what you cost."

"I know what I charged."

"Do you?" Coriel finally raised his head, and his eyes held the flat silver light of the Otherworld, dimmed nearly to nothing out here under the starless sky. A fae away from the Veil faded like a flower pressed between pages. Yew felt it happening to himself some nights, a thinning at the edges, and hated that he understood this creature's exhaustion from the inside. "Do you know what you *cost*, though? Not the price. The cost. There's a difference. My wife paid you our winter wood to get us past the Thornmark wards, do you remember? We froze that winter in a barn outside Gildenhollow. Our daughter coughed until spring." He looked back down at the bird. "She's not in this column. She stayed. Someone has to bury what's left of the orchards."

Yew stood there and took it, because there was nothing else to do with it. Every argument he owned—the arguments he'd polished over eleven years, *I moved them faster than anyone else could, I was cheaper than the alternatives, the roads would have killed them anyway*—every one of them rose in his throat and died there. Coriel was right. Price and cost were different ledgers, and Yew had spent his whole career keeping only one of them.

"I remember the boxes," Yew said quietly. "The music-boxes. What I carried in them."

"I know what you carried in them." Coriel's silver eyes came up again. "Soul-charms. Stolen years, bottled. Fae essence stripped from exiles and sold to humans who wanted a taste of the Otherworld's luck." His voice didn't rise at all. That was the worst part. "My sister's years were in one of those boxes once. I traced it. Two provinces, four owners. It was empty by the time I found it. Drunk down by strangers who never knew whose life they were drinking."

The river murmured. Upstream, the barges crossed and recrossed, patient as priests.

"I can't give her back her years," Yew said. "Nothing I do gives anything back. I understand that. All I can do is—" He stopped. Started again, because the true sentence was harder than the false ones. "All I can do is stop selling. Start giving. It's not enough. It's not *close* to enough. But it's what's in my ledger to spend."

Coriel studied him for a long time. Then he held out the broken clockwork bird.

"Fix it," the tinker said. "You owe me a repair anyway. From Gildenhollow. You never paid for the music-box work." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but the space where forgiveness might someday build. "And when we reach Harrowgate, half-blood, you find my sister's name in whatever books your guild kept, and you tell me what happened to her charm. All of it. Every owner. I want the whole account."

"I'll give you the whole account."

"See that you do." Coriel tucked his hands back around the bird-less space where the bird had been, and for the first time, he looked at Yew the way you look at a person rather than a price. "They say the Okafor woman carries a ledger that names the seven. Say it's true, and say she means to reforge the covenant. If she does—" his dimmed eyes flickered, "—the Otherworld breathes again. And if it breathes, maybe what was stolen can flow home."

"That's the plan."

"Plans are cheap." Coriel almost smiled. "But it's a better one than staying small."

* * *

Yew was halfway back down the column, the clockwork bird riding in his pack next to the slate, when he heard the horns.

Not Vey's horns—those he'd heard described often enough, a low iron bellow like a door swinging open on a furnace. These were higher, thinner, doubled in a way that made his half-fae blood sing and ache at once. Ward-horns. Harrowgate's eastern watchtowers, five miles off, calling the pattern that meant *something is coming up the river valley.*

He climbed the bank for a better view, and the sight took the breath straight out of him.

Downriver, where the valley narrowed to a throat, torchlight had bloomed. Not scattered campfires—an ordered constellation of them, ranks and files, a city's worth of light arranged with military precision. Even at five miles he could see the banners, black fields bearing a white sigil he knew from the forged countersignature in Tamsin's ledger: the closed eye of Cassian Vey.

The vanguard of the legion that had eaten Marrow's Bend. Marching on the crossing. On the column. On every unaccompanied child on his slate and every grandmother on the barges and Coriel with his broken bird and Doss in his little shed being small.

Five miles. At a legion's pace, they'd reach the ford by midmorning.

Yew slid down the bank and ran, and as he ran he was already doing the arithmetic he'd sworn off, the old cold math—but pointed the other way now, all of it, every trick and margin and hidden route he'd ever sold suddenly conscripted into a new accounting. The salt-traders' path along the gorge. The drowned weir where the barges could slip the ford entirely. The checkpoint bribes he could spend to buy confusion rather than passage.

There weren't enough hours in the dark. But there were routes. There were always routes.

He found Brannic at the shallows, still hauling, and grabbed the big man's arm. "Vey's vanguard. Down-valley. Five miles, maybe less—they'll be at the ford by morning."

Brannic went very still. Water sheeted off his forearms. "The column can't clear the crossing by then. Not half of it."

"No. But it doesn't have to." Yew was already unrolling the rough map in his mind, tracing lines he'd memorized in another life, for other purposes. "Get Tamsin. Get Maerin. Wake everyone. Because I know a way to move eight hundred people that the army thinks are trapped on the wrong side of a river—and it runs through country I used to sell, and tomorrow I'm giving it away."

Above them, the blank sky pressed down, starless and waiting. Somewhere south, Tamsin was gathering the scattered names of a broken covenant. And somewhere down that black valley, an army marched on a world it meant to amputate.

Yew spat on his palms and picked up the slate.

Time to balance the books.
