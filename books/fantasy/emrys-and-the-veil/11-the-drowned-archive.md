## 11. The Drowned Archive

The lake had swallowed the library three hundred years ago, and the library had not minded.

That was what Yew kept saying as their boat slid across water so still it looked like poured glass: *the library didn't mind*. He said it in the tone of a man who had made this crossing before and needed to believe his own story. "The archivists of Corvenna were a strange lot. When the dam broke upstream and the valley flooded, they didn't flee. They sealed the doors, warded the shelves against rot, and let the water come in like a slow guest. Said knowledge keeps better underwater. No fire. No moths. No thieves."

"No air," Brannic said from the bow, where he sat with his arms wrapped around his knees like a man twice his size trying to make himself smaller.

"That's the price of no thieves," Yew said.

Tamsin watched the drowned towers rise out of the lake ahead of them—first the tips of spires breaking the surface like the knuckles of a submerged hand, then whole rooftops furred with pale weed, then, as they rowed into the lee of the great dome, the arched windows of the upper floors, dark and patient beneath six feet of clear green water. Fish moved through the reading rooms. Light came down in long slanted columns and lay on the flooded floors like fallen shelving.

She should have been afraid. Three days ago she would have been. But she had crossed the Mire of Small Debts, watched Brannic pay a smith's oath to a thing that wore fog for skin, survived Maerin's fury and Maerin's grief, and learned that her own name had been forged onto payments that unmade the sky. Fear required a world with stable rules. Hers did not have those anymore.

What she felt instead was a kind of grim forward lean. The ledger rode against her ribs inside its oilcloth wrapping, and it seemed heavier every day—not in weight but in insistence, the way a debt grows heavier the longer it goes unpaid.

"The entrance is under the bell tower," Yew said, shipping oars. "There's an air pocket in the archive proper—the archivists built it that way on purpose. A dry heart inside the drowned body." He glanced at her. "You'll have to go down through the flooded stacks. It's maybe forty feet. Can you hold your breath?"

"I can hold my breath for four minutes," Tamsin said.

Brannic turned to stare at her. "Four *minutes*?"

"Taster's training." She began unbuckling her boots. "We practice drowning. Among other things."

"Among other—" Brannic stopped himself, shook his head. In the three days since the Mire he had developed a habit of stopping himself mid-question when the question concerned her past, as if he'd decided some doors were better left shut until she chose to open them. She appreciated it more than she could say. "Right. Four minutes. Of course. Why not."

Yew laughed, but it was a thin sound. He was looking at the windows under the water, and his half-fae eyes had gone the flat silver they went when he was seeing something Otherside. "There's a ward on this place," he said quietly. "Old one. Older than the covenant, maybe. It's been waiting for something."

"Everything old is waiting for something," Tamsin said. "That's what old means."

* * *

The descent was cold in a way that bypassed her skin and went straight to the marrow, cold like a hand closing around her spine. She went first, because she was the best swimmer and because she was tired of being protected; Maerin had appointed herself Tamsin's shadow after the ledger incident, trailing her with an expression caught between apology and suspicion, and Tamsin had decided that if the fae noble wanted to watch her drown, she could watch her swim too.

Down through the window arch, into the flooded reading room. Kelp swayed where students had once walked. A desk drifted past, its inkwell still upright, impossibly, the ink within it black as the space between stars. Books lined the walls behind glass, their pages moving gently, like sleeping things breathing. Tamsin kicked down the central stairwell, following the rope Yew had described, counting strokes, feeling the pressure build in her ears, in her chest, in the small hot place behind her eyes where panic lived if you let it.

*Four minutes,* she told herself. *You've done worse. You drank wyrmroot extract at the king's coronation feast and smiled at the ambassador from Vell while your blood tried to turn to sand.*

At the bottom of the stairwell, a door. Iron-banded, carved with script she recognized from the ledger's margins—the covenant-script, the language of bargains—and set into the door at eye height, a round window of thick glass.

Beyond it: air. A vast dry chamber lit by lamps that burned without flame, blue-white and steady, and shelves rising out of sight, and dust motes hanging motionless in the light like a held breath.

And standing before the door, waiting, a figure.

Tamsin's lungs were burning by the time she got the door open—it opened outward, into the water, which meant she had to haul it against the flood while her body screamed—and she spilled through into the air pocket gasping, soaked, alive. Brannic came through behind her like a breaching whale, coughing. Yew surfaced sleek as an otter. Maerin simply stepped through the doorway without wetting so much as a hem, because fae-fire did not trouble itself with physics.

The figure resolved into an old woman. Small, straight-backed, dressed in the grey robes of a Corvenna archivist gone soft with age and damp, her white hair braided in a crown around a face seamed like old vellum. Her eyes were the problem. They were not old at all. They were young and fierce and utterly awake, and they fixed on Tamsin with an attention that felt like being read aloud.

"You're late," the woman said. "I expected the ledger-keeper years ago."

"I'm not—" Tamsin started.

"You keep ledgers. You carry one against your ribs right now, wrapped in oilcloth, and you've been touching it through the cloth every hour since Harrowgate, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth." The old woman's mouth twitched. "I am Signatory Second. My name was Ivetta Corvane, when names mattered. Come in. Dry off. We have very little time and rather a lot to discuss."

* * *

The Drowned Archive was warm. That was the first impossible thing. The air pocket held heat the way a cupped hand holds a moth, and the lamps burned without consuming anything, and somewhere below the floor Tamsin could hear—or feel—a deep slow pulse, like a heartbeat under stone.

"The reservoir," Ivetta said, catching her look. "The covenant-stone's echo. Every reforging leaves a resonance in the world, and this place was built over one of them. It's why the archivists didn't mind drowning. They knew the water would carry the pulse better than air." She led them between the shelves, and Tamsin saw that these books were different from the drowned ones above—these were bound in grey leather, chained to their shelves, each spine stamped with a seal she now knew intimately: the seven-pointed star of the original covenant. "This is the contract collection. Every bargain ever struck between the realms, copies of copies going back to the First Accord. And at the center—"

The central chamber was circular, domed, floored in mosaic: seven figures standing in a ring around a single stone. On a pedestal of black glass in the middle lay a book the size of a paving slab, bound in metal that was neither iron nor silver but something that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts.

"The original text," Ivetta said. "Not a copy. The copy. Everything else derives from this." She laid her hand on the cover, and Tamsin saw the old woman's fingers tremble—not with age, she realized, but with something closer to reverence, or grief. "Six of us signed it. The seventh signature was added later, in blood that wasn't freely given, and that is a story for another night, or perhaps never. What matters tonight is the final clause."

"There's a final clause?" Brannic rumbled. "Maerin read us the terms. The Veil's skeleton, the signatories as joints, the reforging rite—"

"Maerin read you the *public* terms, the ones every signatory knows, because knowing them is part of holding them up." Ivetta turned to face them, and the lamp-light made her shadow enormous on the dome above. "But a contract of this magnitude requires contingencies. What happens if the Veil frays? What happens if signatories die? What happens if the reforging must be done in haste, under threat, by someone who was never meant to do it?" She tapped the black glass pedestal once. "Clause Nine. The Emergency Reforging. And it has a requirement that has kept me awake for three hundred years."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. She was aware, distantly, of how absurd that was—dry-mouthed in a room full of drowned books—but her body was an instrument and it was telling her something. It was telling her the way it told her when the soup at the duke's table smelled faintly of bitter almond. It was telling her: *here it comes.*

"Read it," she said. Her voice came out steady. Training again. Everything was training.

Ivetta opened the book.

The pages were not paper. They were something like pressed light, and the covenant-script moved across them slowly, rearranging itself, alive. Ivetta found the page she wanted and turned the book so all of them could see, though Tamsin suspected none of them but her could read it—she had spent ten years with the guild's ledgers, and the guild's ledgers were written in this script's plain-spoken descendant.

*Clause Nine. Should the Veil fail and the signatories be unable to perform the Reforging, the covenant may be remade by a vessel—one who stands outside both realms and belongs to both. Such a vessel must have survived every poison of the human realm and every poison of the Otherworld, for the death-curse written into the covenant's heart will seek to unmake the reforgers, and only one who has already died a thousand small deaths can hold it.*

The chamber was silent except for the pulse beneath the floor.

Tamsin read it twice. Three times. The words did not change. She had spent her life among contracts; she knew the difference between words that shifted and words that waited, and these words were waiting, patient as a trap sprung generations ago.

"Every poison," she said. "Of both realms."

"Yes," Ivetta said.

"Every poison of the human realm." Tamsin heard her own voice as if from a distance, calm, precise, the voice she used when reciting findings to a nervous kitchen master. "I have drunk two hundred and eleven distinct toxins in controlled doses over nine years. Metals, alkaloids, fungal compounds, venom fractions, cursed substances—there are poisons in this world that are not chemistry, Guildmaster Sorrel called them moral poisons, distilled from acts, and yes, I've taken those too, in homeopathic sips, because the guild insisted a taster's immunity be *complete*." She stopped. Started again. "Every poison of the Otherworld, I have not. There are perhaps a dozen documented. Most are fatal to humans in any dose."

"They are," Ivetta agreed. "In any ordinary dose, to any ordinary human."

The silence stretched. Brannic had gone very still. Yew was looking at Tamsin with an expression she couldn't parse—pity? awe? the particular horror of a smuggler realizing the cargo he'd been carrying was worth more than he'd imagined?

"How," Tamsin said, and now her voice did shake, just slightly, one hairline crack in the glaze. "How does someone survive the poisons of the Otherworld?"

Ivetta closed the book gently, as one closes a coffin. "One builds tolerance," she said. "As your guild built yours. Drop by drop. Year by year. Beginning, ideally, in childhood, when the body adapts most readily." Her young-old eyes did not blink. "Beginning, ideally, with a foundling. An orphan with no family to ask questions. Raised inside an institution. Trained daily. Tested constantly. Loved, even—love makes the subject compliant and the training durable." She paused. "You see the shape of it, I think. You have been seeing the shape of it since you read Clause Nine. Perhaps longer. Perhaps since the night the stars went out, and you asked yourself why *you*, of all people, ended up holding the ledger."

Tamsin did not answer. She was doing arithmetic. It was the cruelest kind of arithmetic, the kind where every sum comes out the same no matter how you reorder it.

Nine years of deliberate dosing. Two hundred and eleven toxins, each one logged, each one justified—*a taster must know the enemy; a taster's tongue must be a fortress.* The guild's infirmary, where she'd spent fevers that should have killed her, tended by healers who never seemed surprised. Sorrel's particular attention, Sorrel's pride in her, Sorrel's hand on her shoulder saying *you're the finest I've ever trained, Tam, you're exactly what we hoped for.*

*Exactly what we hoped for.*

Not hoped-for. *Designed-for.*

And underneath that, older, deeper: the orphanage records she'd never been allowed to see. The fact that she'd been brought to the guildhall at age five with no history and no surname anyone would explain. The way other foundling tasters had washed out—Brenna, who died of a fever at fourteen; Osk, who "ran away"; little Maren, who was sent to a coastal chapterhouse and never wrote. Seven foundlings, she suddenly counted, seven, in her cohort's generation alone. Seven. The number landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Seven signatories. Seven foundlings. She refused to finish the thought, and finished it anyway.

"They weren't training me to protect kings," she said. The words came out flat and quiet. "They were growing a vessel. All those years. The loneliness, the discipline, the belonging that never quite fit—they weren't failures of the system. They *were* the system. I was livestock that could read."

"Tamsin." Brannic's voice, low and careful, the voice he used near frightened animals.

"Don't." She held up a hand. Not unkindly. "I need to finish the sentence before I can hear anything else." She turned back to Ivetta. "Sorrel knew. Sorrel was raising me toward Clause Nine. And when the wards failed, when the stars went out, Sorrel gave me the ledger and said *run*—and I have been asking myself ever since whether that was rescue or delivery. Whether I was fleeing to safety or being couriered to my purpose like a package."

"And what did you decide?" Ivetta asked.

Tamsin realized she had decided. Somewhere between the flooded stairwell and this moment, without noticing, she had decided. "It doesn't matter," she said. "That's the terrible thing. If Sorrel saved me or used me, the result is identical: I'm here, I'm the only candidate, and Clause Nine doesn't care whether I consented to my own life." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Do you know what the worst part is? For nine years I thought my competence was mine. My immunity, my discipline, my ability to walk into any room in either world and be useful—I thought I had *built* that. And now I learn it was installed. Like a lock fitted to a door that hadn't been built yet."

"It was installed," Ivetta said, without flinching. "I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But listen to me, ledger-keeper, because I am three hundred years old and I have watched vessels break and I know where the breaking starts." She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone. "A designed thing can still choose. The clause says *a vessel who has survived every poison.* It does not say *a vessel who agrees with how she was made.* The guild may have grown your immunity, but they could not grow what you did with it. They could not grow the fact that when Maerin put a blade to your throat, you stood your ground and offered her the truth instead of begging. They could not grow the fact that you crossed the Mire carrying a ledger you could have sold to Vey for a life of comfort, and never once considered selling it. Those things are yours. Whatever else was engineered, the *use* you put it to—that was never in anyone's design."

Tamsin wanted to believe her. She examined the want carefully, the way she examined food, testing it for bitterness, for almond, for the sweetish reek of hemlock. And she found—to her surprise—that the want was clean. It rang true against her teeth.

It didn't dissolve the grief. Nothing would dissolve the grief; she suspected she would carry it for the rest of her life, whatever length that turned out to be, the way she carried trace amounts of two hundred and eleven poisons in her blood. But the grief could sit beside the resolve. She was, she realized, very good at holding contradictory things. It was practically her profession.

"Then teach me the rest," she said. "The Otherworld poisons. If I'm to drink the covenant's death-curse, I need to know what's coming."

Ivetta studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and something in her ancient shoulders eased, as if she had been holding a weight for three centuries and had finally been allowed to set down a corner of it.

"There are twelve," she said. "Grief-of-the-Alders, which kills through memory. The Pale Debt, which kills through obligation. Star-Sickness, which is what your sky is suffering now. And others I will name when you're ready, because naming them is half the exposure." She turned toward the shelves, then stopped. "But understand what you're asking. Even with your tolerance, the full course will take weeks we don't have. We'll have to compress it. Compressed doses of Otherworld poison don't make you sick, ledger-keeper. They make you *strange*. You will see both realms at once. You will hear contracts being whispered in your sleep. By the end, you will not be entirely human anymore, and there is no undoing it."

"Good," Tamsin said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Good," she repeated, and this time she smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile. "Because I am done being exactly what other people hoped for in ways I didn't choose. If I'm going to be remade, I intend to be the one holding the pen." She looked around the drowned library—at Brannic, solid as a forge; at Yew, sharp-eyed and uneasy; at Maerin, whose fae-fire form flickered at the edge of the lamplight like a candle deciding whether to stay lit. "We have the text. We have the clause. We have me. What we don't have is time, and we don't have the remaining signatories, and we don't have a route to the Veil's heart that isn't crawling with Vey's legions."

"We have something else," Yew said slowly. He had gone pale beneath his tan, and his silver eyes were fixed on the doorway they'd entered through—on the water beyond the glass, where the drowned reading room lay quiet and green. "Or we had it. I felt it on the way down and I told myself I was imagining it." His voice dropped. "The ward on this place. The old one. It's not just *waiting* anymore. Something woke it. Something's coming down through the flooded stacks right now, and it's moving like it knows the way."

Tamsin's hand went to the ledger at her ribs. Beneath the floor, the covenant-stone's echo pulsed—once, twice—and then, for the first time in three hundred years, stumbled.

Ivetta's face went grey. "No," she breathed. "It cannot have found this place. The location died with the archivists—"

"Vey bought the guild's secrets," Tamsin said quietly. "Piece by piece. Payment in starlight. Signed in Sorrel's hand." She drew the ledger from its oilcloth, and the covenant-script on its cover had begun, very faintly, to glow. "He didn't just buy segments of the covenant. I think he bought the map to everything Sorrel ever loved."

From the flooded stairwell, far above, came a sound that did not belong underwater and did not belong in any sane world: footsteps. Descending. Unhurried. Patient as a trap sprung generations ago.

"Twelve poisons later," Tamsin said, backing away from the glass, "we might have wished we'd started with the one that kills through fear. Because I think it just walked in."
