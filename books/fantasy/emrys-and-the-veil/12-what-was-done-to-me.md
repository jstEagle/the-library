## 12. What Was Done to Me

Maerin had watched humans die of grief a thousand times, and it always looked the same: like weather. A clouding over, a slow rain behind the eyes, and then—eventually—the sun came back and they went on being human, which was to say forgetful, which was to say forgiven.

She had assumed Tamsin Okafor would grieve the same way.

She had not expected the stillness.

They made camp in the drowned archive's upper gallery, where the water had receded into black pools between the shelves and the air tasted of old ink and river-stone. Brannic built a fire small enough to hide from the world above. Yew counted their supplies twice and then a third time, because counting was how he prayed. And Tamsin sat with the original covenant open across her knees, reading it again—not the clause about vessels this time, but everything before it, everything after it, as though she could find some other sentence hiding among the legal script that would undo the one she'd already read.

*The Veil can be reforged only by a vessel prepared: one raised upon every poison of both realms, that the death-curse may find no purchase.*

Maerin knew that phrasing. She had helped write it.

That was the thing she had not told them yet. That was the thing sitting in her chest like swallowed glass.

She had been young when the covenant was drafted—young even by fae reckoning, which meant she had been old enough to be clever and not old enough to be kind. The seven signatories had gathered at the Veil's heart with the first guildkeepers, and there had been a problem even then, a flaw they all saw and none wanted to name: covenants died. Not peacefully, not cleanly. When a covenant of that weight failed or was broken, it did not simply end—it *punished*. It reached out through every signature bound into it and took back what it had given, and what it took was everything. The drafters called it the death-curse and told themselves it would never come due, the way humans tell themselves the winter will be mild.

And so a smaller circle had met afterward, in a room that smelled of tallow and ambition, to solve the problem improperly. Maerin had been there. Sorrel had been there—Sorrel, already guildmaster's hands, already writing figures in a hand so neat it looked printed. And the solution they drafted was not a solution at all. It was a *breeding program*, though they never used that word. They used words like *continuity* and *preparedness* and *the vessel clause*. They used words like *foundling*.

The guilds took in orphans anyway; that was old custom, older than the covenant. But after that night in the tallow room, the taking-in changed character. The foundlings were chosen for constitution. Their food was dosed, grain by grain, drop by drop, over years—a curriculum written in arsenic and nightshade and stranger things, fae toxins that had names like *widow's mercy* and *the long goodbye*. The strongest survived. The strongest were promoted. The strongest became tasters, and the tasters who survived *everything* became candidates, though no candidate was ever told she was a candidate. That word too had been struck from the record. What remained in the ledgers was a column of innocuous entries: *dietary conditioning, routine; see appendix.*

Maerin had signed off on the appendix.

She had told herself, in the centuries since, that she had been young, that the alternative was a curse that would unmake seven realms' worth of signatories, that someone had to be prepared and it might as well be someone who would otherwise have starved in a gutter. She had told herself the story so many times it had worn smooth as river glass. And then Tamsin Okafor had walked into her exile carrying a ledger, and Maerin had nearly killed her, and then Maerin had read the ledger and seen her own signature in its history—and now the smooth-worn story had cracked down the middle, and inside it was not absolution.

Inside it was a girl.

* * *

Tamsin looked up from the covenant. "You're staring."

"I am considering," Maerin said.

"Consider more quietly."

Brannic snorted from the fire. Yew pretended very hard to be counting.

Maerin rose and crossed the gallery, her mortal shell moving with the careful economy she maintained these days, each motion rationed like the last oil in a lamp. She sat across from Tamsin, on a shelf that had once held the collected jurisprudence of a kingdom now underwater. "You have read the clause."

"I've read the clause."

"Then you know what you are. Or what you were *made* to be." Maerin let the words sit in the damp air. "I want you to hear it from me, and not from the page, because the page will tell you only half. I was there when the vessel clause was drafted. I helped write it. The guild did not stumble into raising you, taster. It was deliberate. Generations deliberate. Foundlings screened, dosed, culled, trained. You were not hired for your immunity. Your immunity was *installed*."

She had rehearsed cruelty before—in courts, in negotiations, in the long slow wars of the fae nobility, where a well-placed truth was worth a legion. She had expected this truth to land like a blade.

Instead Tamsin closed the covenant, set it aside, and said, "I know."

Maerin blinked. Fae did not blink often; it was a human affectation she had adopted for disguise, and it betrayed her now. "You know."

"I figured it out in the archive. Before we surfaced." Tamsin turned her hands over, palms up, studying them the way she studied ledger columns—as if the answer were arithmetic and she merely needed to carry the remainder correctly. "Think about what my life actually looked like, Maerin. An orphan taken in by a guild that trades in bargains. Fed a special diet nobody explained. Trained from childhood to survive things that kill grown soldiers. Promoted every time I didn't die. Do you know what I thought, growing up? I thought I was *lucky*. I thought the guild had seen something in me. Some spark. Some worth." Her voice stayed level, terribly level, the voice she used at royal tables while her mouth burned. "I built myself out of that belief. *They chose me because I was special.*"

"And now?"

"Now I know the diet wasn't a gift. It was a syllabus." She flexed her fingers, closed them. "Now I know 'special' meant 'survivable.' They weren't choosing a child. They were *tempering a tool.*"

Yew had stopped counting. Brannic had stopped pretending to tend the fire. The pool-water reflected the flames in broken pieces, and in those pieces Tamsin's face was calm as a frozen lake.

"You should rage," Maerin said quietly. It was not kindness; it was diagnostic. She wanted to see what the girl was made of now that the tempering was finished. "It is what I would do. It is what any creature with self-respect—"

"Rage at whom?" Tamsin asked. "Sorrel's gone. The guildmasters who signed the appendix are dust or traitors. Rage needs an address, Maerin. Mine is a ruin under the capital." She paused, and something moved beneath the calm—not grief exactly, grief's *shadow*, the shape grief leaves pressed into a bed. "Do you want to know the worst part? It isn't that they used me. It's that they were *good* at it. Every kindness I remember from my childhood—the extra bread, the teacher who taught me letters, Sorrel letting me fall asleep in the ledger room—they're all real memories. And now I have to hold every one of them up to the light and ask: was that love, or was that *husbandry?* Was I a daughter or a crop?" Her jaw tightened. "I don't get to know. That's what they took. Not my childhood—my certainty about it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, water dripped from shelf to shelf, patient as a metronome.

"I grieved it already," Tamsin said at last. "In the archive, standing in front of the original text. I gave it the time it deserved—which was less than you'd think, because I've been grieving it in installments my whole life without knowing why. Every night I couldn't sleep. Every table where I smiled and tasted and belonged to nothing." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and very clear. "So here is what's left, and here is what I'm doing with it."

"Tell me," Maerin said.

"They spent twenty years making me able to survive every poison of both realms. Fine. *Fine.* Then that ability is mine now, not theirs. They built a vessel; I'll decide what it holds. They wrote a clause; I'll write the ending. Sorrel forged my countersignature to sell the world to Vey—" her voice hardened, flint on steel, "—so I will countersign its rescue in my own hand, and every entry will balance. That's not forgiveness. That's *auditing.*"

Maerin sat back. In three hundred years of courts she had watched humans break under lesser truths, and she had watched them rebuild themselves out of spite and stubbornness and call it healing, and she had always found it slightly pathetic, the way one finds a dog walking on its hind legs—impressive, undignified, faintly heartbreaking.

This was not that.

This was a woman taking the poison that had been fed to her and *decanting it into a weapon*, and doing it with the terrible calm of someone balancing a ledger whose columns refuse to sum, and refusing—absolutely refusing—to let the books lie.

"You understand," Maerin said slowly, "what the reforging will cost. The clause does not say the vessel survives. It says the vessel *holds*. The death-curse must go somewhere, and it will go into you, and it will not be one poison, taster. It will be *all* of them. Every toxin of both realms, every debt the covenant ever carried, arriving at once. Your training bought you tolerance. It did not buy you immortality."

"I know."

"The guild spent twenty years preparing your body. They spent *nothing* preparing your soul, because souls cannot be dosed. Whatever you are afterward—if you are anything—"

"Maerin." Tamsin's voice softened by a degree, which from her was a great deal. "You're stalling. Why do you actually care whether I survive?"

And there it was—the swallowed glass turning in Maerin's chest. She looked at the fire rather than the girl. "Because I signed the appendix," she said. "Because I sat in the tallow room three hundred years ago and argued for the *screening criteria*, and I was eloquent, and they listened, and children died of my eloquence in rooms I never saw. Because when the covenant began to decay I told myself it was the humans' fault, the guildkeepers' fault, everyone's fault but the seven of us who built the machine that required keeping." She finally looked up. "You asked once why I hate your kind. The truth is I hate mine more, and yours by inheritance. I exiled myself before anyone could exile me. So no, taster—I am not stalling because I fear for the plan. I am stalling because if you die holding that curse, I will have authored your death twice. Once in ink. Once in silence."

For a long moment Tamsin said nothing. Then she reached across the space between them and did an astonishing thing: she took Maerin's hand—her mortal shell's hand, cold as the pools around them—and held it the way one holds a ledger steady to read.

"Then help me make the second authorship count," she said. "You know the clause better than anyone alive. You know where it's weak, where it can be widened, where the wording can be *renegotiated*. Covenants are contracts, and contracts have loopholes, and you taught the guild everything it knows about finding them." A ghost of a smile, the first Maerin had seen from her since the archive. "Help me loophole my own death sentence."

Maerin stared at her. Around the fire, Brannic had gone very still, and Yew was grinning like a man watching a card sharp turn a losing hand into a house.

"You are either the sanest person I have ever met," Maerin said, "or the most poisoned."

"In this line of work," Tamsin said, "those are the same thing."

* * *

They worked through the dark hours, the four of them bent over the covenant by firelight, and it was Maerin who found it—because of course it was; she had left it there herself, three hundred years ago, a needle sewn into the seam of the clause by a young fae noble who had already begun to doubt.

"*Amendment permitted,*" she read aloud, tracing the archaic script, "*should the vessel consent freely and be witnessed by a signatory of the founding generation, to redistribute the burden of the curse across all bound parties, in proportion to their shares.*"

"In proportion to their shares," Brannic repeated. "Meaning—"

"Meaning the signatories take part of it back," Yew said. "The people who built the bomb carry some of the blast." He whistled low. "Vey's one of the seven. He'd get a share too."

"He'd get a share," Tamsin agreed, "if he's still a signatory when the Veil is reforged. Which gives us nine days to reach the heart of the Veil before his army reaches us—or before the dissolution takes the rest of you." She looked around the fire, at the ward-smith with his burned oath, at the smuggler with his guilty ledgers, at the exiled noble who had just handed her the knife to cut her own chains. "So we march at dawn."

But Maerin was watching Tamsin, not the map. Watching the terrible calm settle back over her like a garment she had tailored herself. And Maerin understood, with the clarity of the very old, that the girl had not rejected what was done to her—she had *appropriated* it. The guild had built a vessel. Very well. The vessel would carry what *she* chose, to the place *she* chose, for reasons *she* chose, and no entry in any ledger, however ancient, however forged, would ever again be written in her name without her consent.

It was, Maerin thought, the most fae thing she had ever seen a human do.

Far above them, through the drowned archive's broken roof, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. And somewhere beyond it, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the fraying world, Maerin felt the first tremor of Vey's legions on the march—felt it in her fading shell the way one feels thunder in a bad tooth.

Nine days.

"Dawn," Tamsin said again, closing the covenant. "And Maerin—thank you. For the loophole. And for telling me the truth, even the ugly parts."

"The ugly parts," Maerin said, rising, "are the only parts I have left."

But as she lay down to rest her failing shell, she kept seeing the girl's face in the firelight—dry-eyed, clear, *decided*—and she thought: *they made her for this, and she knows it, and she goes anyway, and that choice is the one thing in three hundred years that might finally balance my books.*

Outside, the darkness waited, and the armies moved in it, and dawn was coming whether the stars returned or not.
