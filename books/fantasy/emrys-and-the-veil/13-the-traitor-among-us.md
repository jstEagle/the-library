## 13. The Traitor Among Us

Brannic had spent thirty years listening to metal, and metal never lied. A blade under strain sang a certain note before it snapped; a forge gone cold had a silence all its own; a seam badly welded ticked and whispered in the night as it worked itself apart. He had built his life on the certainty that things told you when they were breaking, if only you knew how to listen.

Which was why the map troubled him.

He sat in the lean-to they'd raised at the edge of the Mire's hard ground, the fire banked low because Yew insisted open flame drew things out of the dark that Brannic preferred not to name, and he turned the route-map over in his broad hands for perhaps the hundredth time. It was a good map. Yew had drawn it—Yew drew everything, quick and clever, ink-stained fingers flying—and it showed the smuggler's road east from Harrowgate, through the Mire of Small Debts, past the drowned archive, toward the high country where Maerin said the second signatory waited.

It was a good map, and it was wrong.

Not obviously wrong. Brannic wasn't a cartographer. But he'd walked the first leg of this road twice now, hauling their gear, breaking trail through the reeds, and he had begun to notice a pattern he didn't like: every place they stopped to rest, every ford, every hollow sheltered enough to sleep in, was marked on Yew's map. Every one. As though the man had known exactly where they would choose to lay their heads before they'd chosen it.

A smith knew what a prepared surface looked like. You saw it in iron all the time—a joint cut too cleanly, a hole drilled where a hole would be needed later. Preparation left fingerprints. And someone had prepared this road for them, or prepared them for this road, and Brannic's gut, which had kept him alive through two guild-expulsions and one war, told him the fingerprints were not Yew's.

He looked up across the fire. Tamsin sat with the sealed ledger on her knees, reading by the grey light of the Veil-fray—the sky overhead had settled into a permanent bruised twilight since the stars went out, and it gave just enough glow to work by, like the last coals of a dying forge. Her lips moved slightly as she read, tallying columns no ordinary eye could parse. Maerin stood at the perimeter, fae-fire banked to a dull ember-glow beneath her skin, watching the reeds. And Yew—

Yew was gone. Again.

"Third time tonight," Brannic said quietly.

Tamsin didn't look up. "He's scouting."

"He's scouting," Brannic agreed. "Alone. In the dark. On the exact stretch of road we're taking tomorrow." He set the map down between them, smoothing it flat with one scarred hand. "Tamsin. Look at the map."

She looked. He watched her eyes move the way they moved over ledger entries—left to right, then back again, hunting the discrepancy. He watched her find it. Her face did something complicated: nothing collapsed, nothing broke, but everything tightened, like a weld cooling too fast.

"That's the sleeping-hollow," she said. "And the ford. And—" She stopped. "Every campsite we've used."

"Every one we're going to use." Brannic kept his voice level, gentle even, the way you spoke to an apprentice about to hammer their own thumb. "I've been telling myself it's habit. Smugglers know their roads. But I've known smugglers, girl, and they don't mark campsites they haven't scouted. They mark water and danger and tolls. This is something else. This is a route drawn *for* someone."

The fire popped. Out in the reeds, something small and wet made a sound like a swallowed word.

"You think it's Yew," Tamsin said. Not a question. Her voice had gone very still, the stillness she got when she was tasting something and waiting to see if it killed her.

"I think the evidence points at Yew," Brannic said. "I think evidence is a tool, and tools can be held by any hand. And I think we have to say it out loud before it eats us, because a crew that suspects in silence is a crew already broken." He met her eyes. "Say your piece first. Then I'll say mine."

* * *

They woke Yew when he came back—or rather, they were all awake and arranged around the fire when his silhouette slid out of the reed-shadows, and there was no way to pretend otherwise. Brannic watched the half-fae read the scene in an instant: Tamsin with the ledger closed on her lap, Maerin standing with her arms crossed and her ember-light rising, himself holding the map. Yew's hand drifted toward the knife at his hip and then, deliberately, away.

"Well," Yew said lightly. "This looks like an intervention."

"Sit down, Yew." Tamsin's voice was steady. Brannic admired that steadiness even as he feared what it cost her.

Yew sat. He folded his long legs under him and cocked his head, and his amber eyes caught the firelight the way a fox's do, sideways and bright. "Let me guess. The map."

"The map," Brannic confirmed.

"And the night-walks. And the fact that I knew the ford at Serpent's Bend was passable before we reached it, and the fact that I steered us around the toll-warden's watchpost at Harrowgate without being told it was there, and—" Yew exhaled, and some of the flippancy went out of him like air from a punctured bellows. "And the fact that Vey's outriders found the drowned archive three days after we left it, when it had been lost for four hundred years."

Silence. Even the reeds seemed to hold their breath.

"I've been counting too," Yew said quietly. "I'm a smuggler. Counting is my trade. And every piece of it points at me, and I can't explain any of it, and that frightens me more than you can know, because I *always* have an explanation. It's my other trade." He spread his hands. "So. Ask me. All of it. I'd rather be flayed clean than die of suspicion by inches."

Maerin's voice came out of the dark like a blade drawn slowly. "Where do you go at night?"

"To check the trail behind us. To make sure we're not followed. That's true, and it's also useless, because you'll say a traitor would say exactly that." Yew rubbed his face. "Ask the next one."

"Who taught you the roads?" Brannic asked.

"My mother. Who else?" A ghost of his usual grin, quickly gone. "Half-fae child of a smuggler woman who ran the Veil-paths before the wards fell. She taught me every crossing from Harrowgate to the salt marshes. Which means every route I know, Vey's people could have bought off her, tortured out of her, or learned from her ledgers after she died. My knowledge isn't mine alone. It never has been. That's the honest answer, and it's the worst one."

Tamsin had not spoken. Brannic watched her, and saw that she was doing the thing she did at the king's table: tasting. Weighing. Her thumb moved along the ledger's spine, back and forth, back and forth.

"Yew," she said at last. "When we crossed the Mire, and the debts came due—you paid with Brannic's oath, but the Mire asked *you* a question first. What did it ask?"

Yew blinked. "How do you—"

"It's a ledger-place. It reads accounts. So did it ask you anything?"

A long pause. When Yew answered, his voice had changed, stripped down to something young and raw. "It asked me what I owed the dead on the refugee roads. All the people I'd moved goods around, priced passage for, profited from while they ran. It named names, Tamsin. Forty-one names. It wanted payment in memory—I'd have given up knowing them entirely, every face, every debt. And I refused, and Brannic offered his oath instead, and I have hated myself a little every day since for letting him." He looked up, eyes wet-bright. "Does that sound like a man selling routes to the army burning those refugees' cities? Does that arithmetic balance for you?"

Brannic felt something in his chest loosen—not certainty, never certainty, but the particular relief of hearing a man answer a question he couldn't have rehearsed. Metal sang before it broke. Yew's voice had sung true.

But Tamsin was frowning. "No," she said slowly. "It doesn't balance. And that's what's wrong. None of it balances. If you were the leak, Yew, the leaks would be small and deniable—a campsite here, a delay there. Instead Vey's people keep arriving *just* behind us, *just* accurate enough to terrify us, and never quite in time to catch us. Three days late to the archive. An ambush at the ford that we stumbled into backward and survived. It's not pursuit. It's *herding*." She opened the ledger across her knees. "Someone wants us frightened. Someone wants us looking at each other. Because a company that trusts its own members will walk anywhere together—but a company that suspects them will scatter, and scattered covenant-signatories are easy picking."

Maerin's ember-light flared. "Then who?"

"I don't—" Tamsin began.

And that was when Brannic heard the note.

It was faint, below hearing almost, the way a bad weld whispers in the night. It came from Maerin—from the Maerin standing at the perimeter, arms crossed, ember-light pulsing. Except the pulse was wrong. Fae-fire breathed, he'd learned that much in their weeks together; it rose and fell like a sleeper's chest. This light pulsed in perfect, mechanical intervals, like a bellows worked by a machine, like a heart that had been copied by someone who had only seen hearts from a distance.

Thirty years of listening to metal. Thirty years of trusting the note before the break.

"Maerin," Brannic said, very calmly, "come away from the treeline."

"Why?" The fae noble turned her head, and the motion was a half-beat too smooth, a dancer's turn performed by something remembering dance. "What do you—"

"Tamsin. Your mirror. Now."

Tamsin understood faster than he dared hope—she'd carried a sliver of polished silver since Harrowgate, a taster's habit, checking her own face for the flush of poison. She pulled it out and angled it toward the perimeter, catching the firelight, throwing it at the figure by the reeds.

In the mirror's reflection, Maerin was not there.

Where the exile-noble stood, tall and proud and dimming, the silver showed only a shape like smoke poured into a Maerin-shaped mold, edges fraying, a suggestion of too many joints, a face that in reflection was a smooth blank oval with darkness where eyes should be. It wore her light the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

The thing by the reeds stopped pretending.

It happened fast. The Maerin-shape came apart at the seams—literally, its outline splitting like a garment cut off a body—and the shadow inside poured forward across the ground, fast as spilled ink, fast as fear. Brannic was moving before thought caught up, decades of forge-work translating into one instinctive act: he ripped the burning log from the fire and hurled it, not at the shadow but *past* it, into the dry reeds behind.

Light exploded along the whole treeline. And in that sudden blaze, shadows everywhere flinched—three, four, five shapes peeling away from the darkness between the reeds, each one mid-transformation, each one wearing the beginning of a face. One had Yew's fox-eyes. One had Tamsin's jaw. One had Brannic's own heavy brow, and seeing it nearly stopped his heart.

"They're not spies," Tamsin shouted, on her feet, ledger clutched to her chest. "They're *doubt*. They're made of it—they grow stronger every hour we suspect each other—that's why the leaks were never quite leaks—"

The false-Maerin lunged. Brannic got his smith's hammer up—iron, always iron, iron and a hot fire—and swung with everything his shoulders had ever earned. The hammer connected with a sound like a bell struck underwater, and the shadow tore, shrieking in a frequency he felt in his teeth rather than heard, unraveling into ribbons of cold smoke that streamed away toward the Veil-frayed sky.

The others fled. The burning reeds crackled. Silence rushed back in like water filling a hull.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Yew laughed—one short, shaky, disbelieving bark—and sat down hard on the ground. "I was ready to confess," he said. "I had a whole speech. I'd decided which crime to admit to, the one that sounded least hangable." His hands were trembling. "You were going to let me dig my own grave, Maerin."

"I was going to let you *start* digging," the real Maerin said, striding back from the far perimeter where she'd been walking her round, oblivious, her ember-light breathing properly now, alive and uneven. She looked at the smoking reeds, at the fading ribbons of shadow, at the hammer still in Brannic's fist, and her expression shifted through contempt into something rarer and more costly. "And then I was going to stop you. Probably. Eventually." She paused. "Your smith listens better than most fae. Remember that, human. It may save your life again."

Brannic lowered the hammer. His arms were shaking now that it was over, the great delayed tremor of spent adrenaline, and he let them shake. Found family, Sorrel's ledgers had called such companies once—he'd read the phrase in Tamsin's margin-notes and thought it sentimental nonsense. But families broke and mended; that was what made them families and not crews. Crews that fractured stayed fractured.

They spent the rest of the black hours awake, backs to the fire, iron in every hand. Tamsin paged through the ledger until near dawn, hunting references, and near dawn she found one—an entry in a dead guildmaster's hand, decades old, describing *veilwraiths*, shadow-things that fed on suspicion, deployed historically by "parties seeking to dissolve covenants prior to seizure." Prior to seizure. Vey wasn't hunting them anymore.

He was softening them. Loosening the mortar before the ram came.

"We move at first light," Tamsin said, closing the book. "No more night-walks, no more solo rounds—we stay in sight of each other, all of us, even when it's tactically stupid. Especially then. Trust isn't just how we survive each other anymore. It's armor." She looked around the fire at each of them—Yew, Maerin, Brannic—and Brannic saw that her hands, too, were not entirely steady, and that she was letting them be seen. "Vey knows our route because he's been walking beside us in borrowed faces. Fine. Then we give him a route worth following."

"How?" Yew asked.

Tamsin smiled, and it was the smile Brannic imagined she'd worn at the king's table, the one that made poisoners nervous. "We feed the shadows a lie," she said. "A false campsite. A false road. Let the doubters carry doubt that leads nowhere." She tapped the ledger. "Because according to this, there's a third signatory alive in the high country—and if we're right about what's hunting us, we have maybe six days to reach her before Vey's herders close the pen."

Above them the starless sky pressed down like a lid. Brannic checked the head of his hammer, found it true, and began, quietly, to pack.
