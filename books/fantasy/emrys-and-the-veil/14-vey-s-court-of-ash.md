## 14. Vey's Court of Ash

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first insult, Tamsin thought as the wagon jolted along the starless road—worse than the hood, worse than the manacles of cold iron at her wrists that made her teeth ache with every rut in the earth. They had not bound her hands because they knew what she was. A taster's hands were instruments, calibrated and precious, like a surgeon's or a harpist's. You did not tie a taster's hands any more than you would blindfold a hawk.

The hood smelled of lamp oil and old fear. Through its weave she could see nothing but a brown murk, but she could hear everything: the creak of harness, the wet slap of hooves in mud, and around her the low, ceaseless murmur of an army on the march. Not the ragged noise of refugees—that she had learned in Harrowgate, the shuffling grief of people carrying their lives in bundles—but the disciplined rhythm of ten thousand boots that expected to arrive somewhere.

She counted to keep the terror at bay. It was an old habit, older than the guild. When Sorrel had first put a vial of foxglove extract before her, eleven years old and shaking, he had told her: *Count your breaths, Tamsin. Fear is just a poison too, and you were built to survive poisons.* She had not understood then what he meant by *built*. She was beginning to understand now, and she wished she didn't.

The wagon stopped. Hands lifted her down—not rough, which frightened her more than roughness would have—and someone removed the hood.

Tamsin blinked against torchlight and found herself standing in the heart of Lord Cassian Vey's war camp, and it was wrong in a way she could not immediately name. The tents were fine, black silk shot through with silver thread, the standards bearing his crest of a severed chain. The soldiers were fed, armed, drilled. And yet the whole camp sat under a silence so complete it pressed against her eardrums. No singing. No dice games. No dogs. Armies were always noisy; she had read enough ledgers of provisioning contracts to know that soldiers drank and gambled and quarreled the way rivers ran downhill. This army did none of those things. It simply waited, ten thousand strong, like a held breath.

And above it all, where the stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover blackness, not the soft dark of a moonless night, but an absolute and hungry absence, as if the sky had been scraped clean down to its bones.

"This way," said her escort—a woman in Vey's livery with a face like a closed ledger. "He's been told you're coming. He doesn't like to wait."

*Neither do I,* Tamsin almost said, and swallowed it. Brannic would have laughed at that. Yew would have turned it into three jokes and a smuggling route out. Maerin would have burned something. The thought of them landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well—she heard it fall and fall and never heard it strike bottom. They would come for her. That was the trouble. Yew knew these roads better than anyone alive, and Maerin still owed her a debt from the Mire, and Brannic—Brannic had paid his smith's oath to walk beside her, and men who paid such prices did not abandon what they'd bought. They would come. And Vey would be waiting for exactly that.

The tent at the center of the camp was larger than the guildhall she'd grown up in, and when its flaps opened, warmth rolled out to meet her—the first warmth she had felt since her capture, and therefore the first thing she truly distrusted.

Inside, the Court of Ash.

She understood the name at once, though no one had spoken it to her. The tent's floor was strewn with grey powder—not ash exactly, though close enough to make her stomach turn, because she knew what the Veil left behind when it was ground small and spent. Candelabra burned with flames that gave light but no heat. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, human and otherwise, and the *otherwise* made her breath catch: fae captives, collared in iron, standing among Vey's officers like trophies arranged among their hunters. One of them, a slender figure with bark-brown skin and leaves still growing from her hair—still *growing*, impossibly, defiantly—turned her head as Tamsin passed. Her eyes were the color of deep water, and in them Tamsin saw something she recognized from the mirror on her worst nights: a person calculating whether there was anything left worth saving.

At the center of it all stood the cage.

It was wrought of black metal and hung from the tent's central pole by chains thick as her arm, and inside it burned a shard of the Veil itself. She had seen the Veil only twice—once as a child, when Sorrel had taken her up through the guildhall's deepest door and shown her the border-wards humming like a hive of glass bees; once the night the stars went out, when the sky tore and she had seen the raw stuff beneath, that luminous woven darkness that was neither matter nor magic but the seam between them. The shard in the cage was that same substance, fist-sized, turning slowly, throwing off colors that had no names in any language she spoke. And it was *dimming*. Even as she watched, its slow rotation stuttered, its light guttered like a candle in a draft, and somewhere in the ranks of courtiers a woman sighed with something horribly like satisfaction.

Because the shard was being fed.

Tamsin saw the mechanism of it then, and her trained mind—the part of her that audited entries that shouldn't exist, that reconciled payments made in starlight—laid it out in ledger-lines before she could stop it:

*One unit: captive fae, living.*
*One unit: iron collar, binding essence.*
*One transfer: essence to shard.*
*One withdrawal: power to Lord Vey.*

Beside the cage, on a table of black wood, lay a shallow basin. A fae prisoner knelt beside it—she could tell by the ears, by the too-perfect bone structure worn thin with starvation—and two soldiers held him while a third drew a long needle of grey metal across the inside of his wrist. What fell into the basin was not entirely blood. It was blood threaded with light, with little drifting motes of that nameless color, and when the basin was full a servant carried it, reverently, to the base of the cage and poured it into a channel cut into the floor. The channel glowed. The shard brightened. And across the tent, a man who had been standing with his back to her turned around, and Tamsin watched the stolen light settle into him like water into dry ground.

Lord Cassian Vey was taller than the stories said, and younger. That was the obscenity of him. He should have been ancient, should have been bent under the weight of what he'd done, and instead he looked forty and hale, with silver threading his temples like frost on a healthy crop. He wore black and grey and no crown at all, which was its own kind of message: crowns were for kings who needed symbols. He wore only the faint luminescence of the Veil itself, sunk into his skin, glowing softly at his throat and wrists like banked coals.

"Tamsin Okafor," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like an entry already written. "Ledger-keeper of the Veilwrights' Guild. Trained taster. Immune to every poison of the human realm." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and that was the worst thing she had seen all night. "And, I suspect, rather more than that."

"You've read my file," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Good. Sorrel had taught her that too: *the tongue is the first organ of defense, child. Train it before you train the stomach.*

"I've read everything." He gestured, and a chair appeared—someone brought it, actually, a silent officer, but the effect was of a man for whom furniture simply arrived. "Sit. Please. You've been in a hood for six hours; the least I can offer is a chair."

"I can see what you offer everyone." She didn't sit. She nodded toward the kneeling fae, whose wrist was being bound with linen even now, whose eyes had gone somewhere far away. "How many a day?"

Vey followed her gaze without embarrassment. "Three. Four if the campaign demands it." He said it the way a quartermaster reports ration expenditure. "Before you judge me—and you will, you're clearly composing the judgment right now—consider what the alternative is. The Veil is dying, Mistress Okafor. Has been dying for centuries; your own guild's ledgers say so, in entries your Guildmaster thought you too young to audit. Every year less magic crosses the border. Every year the fae realms grow thinner, colder, more desperate. I am not killing the Otherworld. I am performing triage. I am taking the last light of a dying thing and concentrating it where it can do some good—in hands that can act decisively."

"In your hands."

"There are no other candidates." He spread his own hands, and the glow at his wrists pulsed gently, obscenely, like a heartbeat. "Your covenant-scatterers want to reforge the old bargain. Do you know what the old bargain was? Mutual dependence. Two realms bleeding into each other forever, humans born half-deaf to wonder, fae born half-starved for substance, both sides limping along on a treaty written by cowards who couldn't commit to either union or divorce. I am offering the world something braver: a clean severance. Let each realm stand alone. Let magic die with dignity in one and flourish, unshared, uncorrupted, in the other. Mine."

"And the fae who live here? In the human realm? Harrowgate is full of them. Children born on this side of the border."

"Repatriation," he said smoothly, "or integration without magic. They'll live. Shorter lives. Simpler ones." He tilted his head. "You're angry. Good. Anger means you're engaged. But look at me, Tamsin Okafor, and ask yourself the question your guild never let you ask: why does a food-taster need immunity to *every* poison? Not tolerance. Not resistance. Immunity. Who pays, for decades, to raise a foundling girl on graduated doses of hemlock and nightshade and wyrm-spit, unless they are building something?"

The tent seemed to tilt. She kept her feet through sheer professional discipline, counting breaths—one, two, three—as the floor of her life shifted beneath her. *Built.* Sorrel's word. She had heard it in memory a hundred times since the Drowned Archive, since the original text had named its final clause, since she had understood that her whole childhood had been a preparation she'd never consented to. She had wept about it once, privately, on Maerin's shoulder, in the ugly snotty way she despised in herself. She would not give this man her tears.

"The guild," she said. "Sorrel. You bought them. Segment by segment. Payments in starlight—I audited the entries myself. His hand signed them. My countersignature was forged alongside."

"Forged?" Vey raised an eyebrow, and for a moment the warmth dropped away and she saw the thing underneath, the cold engine, and it was almost a relief. "Is that what he told you? Sorrel was careful, I'll grant him. Careful right up until the wards began to fail and he realized the covenant couldn't hold regardless. Then he came to me, Mistress Okafor, and he sold me the segments willingly, and your countersignature was on every bill of sale. Ask yourself why a man trains an immune vessel and then sells the covenant's pieces to the one buyer who wants it dead. Ask yourself what he was preserving you *for*."

"He was preserving me from *you*."

"Was he? Or was he keeping the last asset liquid?" Vey stepped closer, and the light in his skin brightened, and she felt it press against her like heat from a forge—not painful, not quite, but insistent, persuasive, the way wine persuades. "Here is what I know about you, Tamsin. You belong everywhere and nowhere. The guild raised you but never told you what you were. Your mentor lied to you for twenty years. Your new companions—a disgraced smith, a smuggler, an exile who tried to kill you—are kind, I'm sure, but they need you for the clause. For the vessel. For the death-curse you're meant to drink at the covenant-stone. Have they told you that part plainly? Have any of them said, *Tamsin, the reforging will pour the curse of every signatory into your body, and you will likely die screaming*? Or did they frame it as destiny?"

She said nothing, because the answer was yes—they had told her, Maerin most bluntly, over a map in the drowned archive's antechamber, with terrible honesty—and because she suspected whatever she said, he had a counter prepared.

But he read her silence anyway, and smiled again, softer now. Sincere. That was the horror of Cassian Vey: he was not lying to her. He believed every word.

"They haven't lied to you," he conceded. "I'll grant them that. But they are asking you to die for a world that used you as a laboratory experiment and called it belonging. I am offering you something else." He gestured, and the courtiers parted, and a second chair was brought—not a prisoner's stool but a proper chair, high-backed, set at the level of his own. An equal's seat. "A place. Keeper of my ledgers. You've seen what I'm building—severance is the largest contract ever drafted, and it needs a keeper who can read both realms' script, who cannot be poisoned, bribed, or bewitched. Do you know how rare you are? Do you know what you're *worth*? Your guild knew, and hid it from you. I am telling you to your face, in front of my entire court: you are the single most valuable person in either realm, and I will treat you accordingly. No one will ever again decide what you're for without asking you first."

The words went into her like a blade finding a gap in armor, because they were aimed at the true wound, the one she'd carried since she was eleven years old and counting breaths over a vial of foxglove. *No one will decide what you're for.* She wanted it so badly her mouth watered, and she recognized the sensation for exactly what it was, because she was a taster, and tasting was knowing, and she knew poison when it touched her tongue—even poison this eloquent.

She looked past him, at the cage. The shard had dimmed again. Below it, the fae with leaves in her hair was watching Tamsin with those deep-water eyes, and her lips moved, soundless, shaping a single word. Tamsin read it easily. Fae-mouths were expressive; Yew had taught her that much.

*Remember.*

Remember what? Remember Harrowgate. Remember the Mire of Small Debts, where Brannic had laid his oath on the table like a man laying down his sword. Remember Maerin's rage transmuting into resolve, Yew giving away for free what he'd built his life selling. Remember the drowned archive, and the clause, and the truth: her immunity was never a job qualification. It was preparation. Not theirs—hers. Whatever the guild intended, whatever Sorrel intended, the meaning of a thing was decided by what you finally chose to do with it.

Vey was offering her a seat at a table where the meal was other people.

"I have a counteroffer," Tamsin said.

His eyebrows rose, genuinely amused. "You're in no position to negotiate."

"I'm always in a position to negotiate. It's the one thing I'm better at than you." She stepped forward, into the light of the cage, and let him see her face clearly, let him see the taster's calm that Sorrel had drilled into her across twenty years of small deaths survived. "You're right about one thing. My guild used me. My mentor lied to me. My companions are asking me to carry something terrible. All true. Here's what you have wrong: they asked. Every one of them, at every step, told me the cost and let me choose. Even Maerin, who hates my kind, handed me the ledger and said *read it and decide*. That's the difference between us, Lord Vey. You collect people. They feed them. And I—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it would have been a lie, and she was done being managed by liars, whoever they served— "I have eaten at both tables now. Yours smells like ash. Theirs smells like bread."

Silence took the court. Somewhere in the cage, the shard flickered.

"So you refuse," Vey said quietly. No anger. Worse than anger: assessment. Recalculation. She could see the ledger behind his eyes rebalancing her column from *asset* toward *obstacle*, and she knew what happened to obstacles in the Court of Ash.

"I refuse," she said. "And I'll tell you something for free, since you value accurate books. Your severance won't hold. You can't build a wall out of a dying thing's last light and call it permanence. The Veil isn't property. It's a promise. And promises don't die when the signatories do—they wait."

"For whom?" he asked. "For you? You're one woman in irons, ten days' march from a covenant-stone surrounded by my legions."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "For me."

Something moved behind his eyes—doubt, maybe, or its cousin, caution—and he covered it with a smile and a small gesture, and the guards took her arms. As they turned her toward the tent's rear flap, toward whatever cage they had prepared for a person too valuable to kill and too dangerous to free, she passed close to the fae with the leaves in her hair. Close enough to whisper.

"Harrowgate," Tamsin breathed. "Tell them I'm at the black pavilion. Tell them the ninth day."

The fae's deep-water eyes widened—and then, faintly, impossibly, the leaves in her hair rustled, though there was no wind in the Court of Ash, and Tamsin felt something pass between them, light as pollen, gone before the guards noticed: a message given to the green things, and green things remember everything.

They marched her out into the starless dark, toward the black pavilion, toward the ninth day.

Behind her, muffled by silk and distance, she heard Cassian Vey give the order that meant he had finished recalculating.

"Double the watch," he said. "Something is coming for her."
