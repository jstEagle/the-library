## 15. Escape Written in Ledger-Script

Yew had spent eleven years learning the exact weight of a lie, and so he knew one when he felt it settle over a place. Vey's war camp lay along the river bend like a held breath—ten thousand soldiers, siege engines lashed under oilcloth, cookfires banked low as if the whole encampment were trying not to be noticed by something larger than itself. And at its center, in a pavilion of grey silk that drank the starless dark rather than reflecting it, sat the thing they had come to steal: a caged shard of the Veil, no bigger than a lantern, burning with a light that hurt to remember.

He crouched in the reeds on the far bank with Brannic's shoulder against his and watched the camp breathe.

"Four towers," Brannic murmured. "Two manned properly. The other two are for show."

"Everything here is for show," Yew said. "That's how he holds ten thousand men together without pay enough or food enough. Show them something worth serving." He nodded toward the grey pavilion. "Show them that."

The shard pulsed once, slow, like a heart deciding whether to bother beating again. Even across the water Yew could feel it—the way you feel a door standing open onto a warm room in winter. It was a piece of the border between realms, torn loose and put in a cage, and Vey fed it captured fae the way a miser feeds a fire coin by coin, drawing off what it gave until his own magic swelled and everything else thinned.

Maerin's fire, dimming somewhere behind them in the dark, flickered as if she too could feel it. She had insisted on coming this close. She would not come closer; her mortal shell was gone, burned away on the march, and what remained of her was too bright, too strange, to pass among human soldiers even hooded. So she waited in the reeds like a lamp wrapped in cloth, and her waiting was a kind of prayer none of them knew how to answer.

"She's fading faster," Brannic said quietly.

"I know."

"We should have gone last night."

"We weren't ready last night." Yew settled deeper into the cold mud. "We're barely ready now."

Behind them, half-buried in the reeds where the ground dipped into an old drainage cut, Tamsin Okafor was writing in the dirt with a stick.

Yew glanced back at her every few breaths because watching her work had become, over the past two days, the only thing that made him believe this plan was anything other than a beautiful way to die. She knelt in the muck with her sleeves pushed up, hair tied back with a strip of leather, and she was drawing—not sigils, not wards, nothing a hedge-witch would recognize—but columns. Rows of small precise marks, brackets and bars and little hooked terminals, arranged with the fussy neatness of a merchant who could not abide a crooked line.

Ledger-script. The guild's private hand, the one they used for entries that shouldn't exist. He'd seen it once before, years ago, in a smuggler's strongbox: a contract written in it that had bound a storm to a ship's mast for nine years. He hadn't known then what he was looking at. He was beginning to understand now.

"How long?" he asked.

Tamsin didn't look up. "However long it takes me to get it right. If I get it wrong, we're all dead, so I'd rather you stopped asking."

Brannic huffed something that might have been a laugh. "She's been like this since the Mire," he said. "You threaten her and she just gets more accurate."

"I wasn't threatening," Yew said. "I was scheduling."

* * *

They had been prisoners for six days.

Yew turned the word over in his mind and found it still didn't fit. Prisoners were taken in battle or dragged from their beds. They had walked in—or most of them had. It had been Maerin's idea, in the end, or rather Maerin's fury wearing the shape of an idea: *you cannot steal a shard from the heart of an army, but perhaps you can be brought to it.* They had let themselves be taken on the road, three refugees and a dimming fae-fire hidden inside a hollowed lantern-stone, and Vey's men had searched them, catalogued them, and marched them into the camp as exactly what they appeared to be—nobody worth more than a question.

It had nearly worked. It had worked, in fact, all the way to the fourth day, when a herald with a voice like oiled hinges had summoned Tamsin by name.

*Tamsin Okafor, keeper of ledgers, taster of poisons. Lord Vey extends his courtesy.*

Yew had watched her go between two guards with her chin level and her hands loose at her sides, and he had thought, with the cold clarity of a man watching cargo go overboard: *she won't come back the same.* Everyone came back from Vey's pavilion changed. The ones who took his offer came back hollowed and grateful. The ones who refused came back in pieces, if at all.

But Tamsin had come back that evening walking slowly, deliberately, like a woman carrying water across ice—and that night, in the prisoners' pen among forty strangers and two guards who couldn't hear past their own boredom, she had pressed her mouth close to Yew's ear and whispered four words:

"He showed me the cage."

And then, after a silence in which Yew's heart did something unprofessional: "And he showed me the contract that holds it. He wanted me to admire it. He wanted me to know there was no way out, so I'd stop looking for one." A pause. Her breath had been very steady. "He made a mistake. He let me read it twice."

* * *

Now, in the reeds, she finished the last column and sat back on her heels, mud to the elbows, and looked at what she'd drawn the way a surgeon looks at a wound she's about to open.

"Say it again," Brannic said. "Slowly. I want to hear whether it sounds as mad out loud as it does in my head."

Tamsin wiped her face with her forearm and left a smear of mud across one cheekbone, which somehow made her look older and younger at once. "Contract-wards aren't spells," she said. "Everyone thinks they're spells—wards, barriers, magic you break with bigger magic. They're not. They're agreements. Every ward around that pavilion is a clause in a contract Vey signed, binding the shard to him, binding the cage to the shard, binding the guards' obedience to the cage. It's all one document. He wrote it himself, which is why it's elegant, and he wrote it alone, which is why it has seams."

"And you can see the seams."

"I can see the seams because I've spent twelve years reading entries that were written to be unreadable." She picked up her stick again and tapped it against the largest of her mud-drawn columns. "This is the master clause. *All claims descend from the holder.* Everything Vey owns flows down from that line—his army's oath-wards, his tower seals, the cage. You can't break any of it from below. But a claim is only valid while the holder holds. So—" she drew a small, vicious-looking hook at the top of the column, "—if the descent were interrupted, even briefly, every subordinate claim goes into abeyance. Not broken. Suspended. Like a ledger balanced to zero at year's end. For as long as the interruption lasts, nothing in that camp belongs to anyone. The guards' obedience clauses hang empty. The cage forgets whose it is."

"For how long?" Yew asked.

"That depends on how good my forgery is." She said it flatly, without false modesty in either direction. "Vey countersigned the original in his own hand. I can't forge Vey. Nobody can forge Vey; the man signs his name like he's killing something. But I don't need to forge him. I need to insert myself into the chain of descent—a counter-claim, lodged between his signature and the shard. Guild form. It's called a *contested entry*. When two claims conflict, the older registry holds priority until the dispute is resolved." Her mouth curved, thin and joyless. "The Veilwrights' Guild registry is older than Vey's usurpation. Older than his family. I checked, before we walked in. Sorrel kept the founding dates in the front of every ledger. I used to think it was vanity."

"So you file a dispute," Brannic said slowly, "against the Lord Usurper of the human realm. From inside a hole in the mud."

"I file a dispute against the Lord Usurper of the human realm," Tamsin agreed, "in a script only guild-trained eyes can read, using a registry seal I memorized when I was nine years old because Sorrel made me copy the front matter two hundred times as punishment for talking during audits." She looked up at Yew, and in the light of Maerin's veiled fire her eyes were very clear. "He thought he was teaching me obedience. He was teaching me the only weapon in this world that doesn't care how big your army is. Precedence."

Yew felt something turn over in his chest—an old smuggler's recognition, the click of a lock hearing the right key. All those years he'd moved goods through borders, he'd assumed the borders were walls. They had never been walls. They had been paperwork. And here was a woman who had grown up inside the paperwork, invisible, unthanked, keeping accounts of impossible things in a basement while kings and lords postured above her—and the whole architecture of Vey's power was about to discover what that kind of invisibility was worth.

"It'll hold minutes," she went on. "Perhaps a quarter hour, if his clerks are slow. When Vey finds the contested entry—and he will find it, he reads his own contracts the way I read mine—he'll void it, and everything snaps back, and he will know exactly who did it. There's no version of this where we stay hidden afterward."

"There was never a version where we stayed hidden," Yew said. "There was only ever a version where we got the fragment first." He looked at the grey pavilion, at the pulse of caged light. "So. How do we get you to the cage?"

* * *

The answer, in the end, was inventory.

Every sixth night the camp took stock—grain, arrowheads, salt, and captives. The captives were counted into the compound behind the pavilion because Vey's quartermasters were meticulous men and Vey's appetite required meticulous feeding. Yew had watched the count twice from the reeds, and both times he had watched a clerk with a slate walk the prisoner line and mark each name against a manifest, and both times the clerk had moved down the line without once looking up, because the manifest was the truth and the bodies were merely evidence of it.

"You can't sneak past a man who isn't looking," Yew told them. "So we don't sneak. We get counted."

They went back over the water an hour before the count, wading the cold shallows with their clothes bound on their heads, and presented themselves at the edge of the refugee pen like the exhausted stragglers they had been pretending to be for six days. The pen's occupants barely glanced at them. That was the cruelest arithmetic of Vey's camp: there were always more captives, and grief, given enough company, learns to conserve itself.

The clerk reached them near midnight. Yew stood with his eyes down and his shoulders rounded into the shape of a man who had stopped expecting anything, and heard the stylus scratch: four marks. Four names read aloud from their own lips, flat and rehearsed. The clerk's gaze slid across Tamsin's face without a flicker—because to him she was grain, she was salt, she was a unit—and Yew thought, with a savage little joy: *if you only knew what was being entered into your master's books tonight.*

The compound behind the pavilion was smaller than it looked from outside, which meant the cage was closer than it looked, which meant that when the patrol rotated at the turn of the watch and the torches guttered in the river wind, the grey silk wall was thirty paces away and the light leaking beneath it was bright enough to read by.

Tamsin knelt in the trampled grass and began to write.

Not in mud this time. She had spent the day before the infiltration grinding charcoal from a burnt tent-pole and mixing it with tallow rendered from their ration fat, and she carried it in a clay pot against her skin to keep it soft. Now she painted the contested entry onto the flagstones in a long unbroken line, working backward from the pavilion's threshold, her hand steady, her lips moving silently as she recited the registry dates she had copied two hundred times as a child. The script caught the shard's light and seemed to drink it, each character going faintly luminous, like iron remembering heat.

Yew kept watch and Brannic kept Tamsin, one huge hand hovering near her shoulder as though he could catch a mistake mid-air. The line grew. Ten paces. Twenty. At the twenty-eighth pace she paused, pot raised, and Yew saw her hesitate for the first time.

"The seal," she said softly. "It has to touch the cage's base. The entry isn't filed until it reaches the thing it contests."

Thirty paces of open ground between the last flagstone and the pavilion's edge. Two sentries at the silk wall, bored, cold, alive. Yew weighed the distance the way he weighed any route—by what it cost to fail.

"I'll give you the sentries," he said.

Brannic turned. "With what?"

"With the truth." Yew pulled his collar open and drew out the thing he had sworn he'd never use again: a smuggler's token, lead stamped with the sigil of the river-guilds, the mark of a man licensed to move anything through anywhere. He'd surrendered that life. He'd meant it. But a token wasn't a loyalty; it was a key, and keys didn't care whose hand warmed them. "These two are river-men seconded to Vey's guard. I can smell it—the way they stand, feet apart, weight on the balls. River-men respect the guilds. Show them this and ask to lodge a formal complaint against their quartermaster, and they'll spend the next hundred breaths arguing jurisdiction instead of watching the silk."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we die quickly and someone else finishes this badly." He smiled, and found to his surprise that it wasn't entirely a mask anymore. Eleven years of selling passage to desperate people, and here, finally, was freight worth the risk of the crossing. "Keep her writing."

He crossed the thirty paces the way he'd crossed a hundred borders: openly, unhurried, a man with a grievance. The sentries turned. He held up the token, let the shard's light catch its stamp, and said, in the bored cadence of officialdom, "Guild business. Complaint of improper weighing at the salt stores. Which of you is senior? This needs to go in your log properly or it's worthless to both of us."

The taller sentry's face did exactly what Yew had bet it would: it tightened with the particular misery of a soldier confronted with paperwork. "That's a quartermaster matter—"

"It's a guild matter, which makes it your matter until you log it. Do you want it logged wrong? Do you want to explain to Lord Vey why his salt weights are disputed?" He was already steering them, gently, terribly, toward the far corner of the pavilion, toward the log-book hanging by the mess-tent, his voice dropping into the soothing drone of procedure. Behind him, he did not look, but he listened—with a smuggler's ears—for the small wet sound of tallow-charcoal meeting stone.

Ninety breaths. A hundred and ten. Then Tamsin's voice, low and fierce, from the darkness: "*Filed.*"

The world hiccupped.

Yew felt it before he understood it—a sensation like a floor tilting a half-degree, like a rope going slack. Across the camp, a dozen things happened at once and none of them loudly: the sentries beside him straightened out of their argument with expressions of sudden, sourceless confusion, as if they'd forgotten what they were guarding and why; a patrol by the horse-lines stopped mid-stride and stood blinking; somewhere a sergeant began shouting a question and got no answer, because the answer had been a clause, and the clause was in abeyance.

The grey silk wall hung there, but the authority that made it a wall had stepped out for a moment, leaving only cloth.

"Now," Tamsin breathed. "Minutes. Maybe less. Go."

They went. Brannic went through the silk wall first, tearing it like paper, and the sight inside stopped even Yew's trained heart for a beat: the cage of black iron, tall as a man, and within it the shard turning slowly, shedding light like a wound sheds blood. Around the cage's base, chains of contract-script glowed sullen red—Vey's claims, descending, holding. Except the lowest link had gone dark. Interrupted. Hanging.

Tamsin was already there, on her knees at the base, charcoal pot open. "Help me lift the cage—I have to write underneath it, the seal has to contact the anchor-stone—"

Brannic lifted. The man was fifty years old and built like a bridge pier, and he lifted a cage full of the border between worlds as if it were a plough-blade, teeth gritted, arms shaking, and Yew threw his shoulder under the opposite side and added whatever his smuggler's frame was worth. The cage rose an inch. Two. Underneath, in the space of a heartbeat, Tamsin wrote the guild's registry seal in strokes quick and sure as a signature on a confession.

"*Contested,*" she whispered. "By prior registry. By right of the original covenant. *Entered.*"

The red chains flickered. Dimmed. And in the dimming, the shard screamed.

Not with sound—with light, a single furious pulse that bleached the night white and knocked everyone flat, and in that pulse Yew saw the cage's iron glow cherry-red and split along one seam with a crack like the sky remembering stars. A fragment broke free, no bigger than a knife-blade, spinning, and Tamsin caught it bare-handed.

Yew would carry the image to his grave: her kneeling in the wreckage of the greatest theft in history, cupping a sliver of the border between realms in her palms like water, blood welling between her fingers where the edges cut her—and her face not afraid. *Interested.* Auditing.

"It's still singing," she said. "Can you hear it? It's still part of the covenant. It wants to go home."

"Home is nine days behind enemy lines," Brannic wheezed, hauling himself up. "Move!"

They moved. The quarter hour died around them as they ran—Yew felt the moment the contested entry collapsed, felt the camp snap back into Vey's grip like a released bowstring, heard the first horns begin to scream. Alarm-fires bloomed. Horses shrieked. And behind them, rising over the grey pavilion, a voice that was not a voice, that was pressure and cold and the sound of a contract being read aloud by something that owned the reader:

"*Find the keeper.*"

They found Maerin at the reeds, and her fire flared up to meet the fragment in Tamsin's bleeding hands like a traveler seeing home across a valley, and for one moment the whole riverbank burned gold and shadowless. Then they were running north, four shadows and a splinter of dawn, while behind them Vey's army woke to discover that its master's perfect cage had a crack in it—and that the crack had been made not with armies, not with magic, but with a forged entry in a dead woman's handwriting, filed by a girl who had once been punished for talking during audits.

Yew ran and laughed, breathless and disbelieving, and Tamsin, clutching the fragment, laughed too—a short, wild, liberated sound.

Then the laughter died in all their throats at once. Because ahead of them, on the north road, where no torches had been an hour ago, fires were lighting. Many fires. In a line. Waiting.

Vey had not survived a usurpation by being slow to learn. And somewhere in the last six days, someone had taught him exactly where they would run.
