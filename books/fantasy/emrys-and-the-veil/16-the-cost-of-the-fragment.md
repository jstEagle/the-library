## 16. The Cost of the Fragment

The curse announced itself the way a slow poison always did: not with a scream, but with a smallness.

Tamsin noticed it first in Yew's hands. They had made camp in the ruins of an old waystation three days out from Vey's territory, and he was turning a copper coin over his knuckles—the idle trick he did when he thought no one was watching—and his fingers left faint trails in the air behind them. Not smoke. Not light. Just a thinning, as though the world had forgotten to finish drawing him.

"Yew," she said quietly.

He looked up. Smiled the crooked smuggler's smile that had gotten him through a hundred checkpoints and at least two marriages. "What?"

"Show me your hands."

The smile flickered. He held them out anyway, palms up, and in the grey pre-dawn light she could see it clearly now: the edges of him had gone soft, like ink touched by water. The whorls of his fingerprints were fading. Not gone—fading. As if someone were erasing him from the world one line at a time.

* * *

Brannic found them like that, kneeling in the frost with Yew's hands cupped between Tamsin's, and behind him Maerin drifted through the doorway trailing her dim fae-fire, and for a long moment nobody said anything at all. Then Brannic rolled up his sleeve and showed them his forearm, where the old ward-smith's tattoos—the oath-marks burned into his skin when he was nineteen and certain of everything—were lifting away like ash from a page.

"It started last night," he said. His voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "I thought it was the cold."

"It's not the cold." Tamsin stood. Her legs felt distant, borrowed. She knew this feeling; she had catalogued it across a thousand poisoned dinners. This was what a toxin looked like when it worked slowly enough that the body mistook it for time passing. "It's the shard. When we broke it, the backlash didn't dissipate—it went looking. It went into everyone bound to the covenant."

Maerin's fire guttered. When she spoke, her voice came from somewhere further away than the room. "Every signatory," she said. "And every vessel sworn to carry the reforging." Her burning eyes fixed on Tamsin, and there was something in them Tamsin had never seen before—not blame, not the old contempt. Pity. "You included."

Tamsin looked down at her own hands. They looked fine. Of course they looked fine. But when she pressed her thumbnail hard into the meat of her palm, the white mark it left stayed too long, and faded wrong—faded outward instead of inward, like a stain spreading through cloth.

"Well," she said, because someone had to say something and she had spent her whole life being the person who said something while other people panicked. "At least we know the deadline."

* * *

They laid the ledger open on the waystation's broken table, weighting its corners with stones, and Tamsin read until the light came up properly. Guild training helped here. Panic blurred words; discipline sharpened them. She had spent ten years reading contracts written by entities who considered human language a form of prey, and she read the backlash-clause the same way she read everything: twice, slowly, aloud only when she was sure.

"The dissolution is uniform," she told them. "Roughly a tenth of cohesion per day. That's not me guessing—that's the arithmetic in the clause itself. Nine days, give or take how much of yourself you've already spent. After that—" She stopped. Started again. "After that, you don't die. You just stop having ever been. The covenant forgets you, and then the world does."

Yew laughed, one short bark with nothing in it. "So I get to find out whether my mother remembers me. Convenient. I've wondered for years."

"This isn't funny."

"No," he agreed, and the laugh fell off his face entirely, leaving something younger underneath. "No, it isn't. But Tamsin—if we're dissolving anyway, then the choice is simple, isn't it? We go to the Veil's heart or we evaporate in a ditch. I've done ditches. I'd rather do the heart."

"The Veil's heart is nine days' march through territory Vey has already taken," Brannic said. He was flexing his marked arm as if he could shake the fading loose. "His siege lines are around the free cities. His outriders are on every road worth naming. And we're not exactly traveling light—we've got two recovered signatories, a fragment of shard, the ledger, and whatever this company becomes by the time we get there."

"Which is why," Tamsin said, "we don't go as travelers. We go as a covenant."

She turned the ledger toward them, to the page she'd been staring at since before dawn. The original text, the drowned archive's copy, listed not just seven names but seven functions. Oath-bearer. Ward-wright. Wayfinder. Debt-keeper. Flame-that-remembers. Door-that-closes. And at the bottom, in script older than the others, half-eaten by water damage but legible if you knew how guildhands folded meaning into their letters:

*Vessel.*

"That's me," she said. "That's been me since I was a foundling drinking measured doses of nightshade. So the question isn't whether we can make it. The question is whether we can make it *as what we are*. A covenant moves differently than a fugitive band. A covenant has rights of passage under the old law—rights even Vey's contract-wards have to honor, because his own stolen segments are built on that law. If we walk in wearing our functions openly, declared, witnessed—"

"He'll know exactly where we are," Brannic said flatly.

"Yes. From the moment we cross his outer perimeter, he'll know. And he'll come for us." She met his eyes. "But he was always going to come for us, Brannic. We're carrying the thing that unmakes him. Hiding bought us three days of sleep. Declaration buys us a chance to reach the stone alive."

Silence, except for the wind working at the waystation's broken roof.

It was Maerin who broke it. She drifted closer to the table, and her fire bent low over the ledger like a reader leaning in. "The flame-that-remembers agrees," she said. "I have spent two centuries hating your guild, taster, and I will spend the rest of however long I have hating it still. But I did not survive exile by dying stupidly in ditches either." Her gaze lifted to Tamsin. "Declare us. Write us down. If I am going to be erased, let it be from a page that says exactly who I chose to stand beside."

Tamsin took out her pen.

Her hands were almost steady. She dipped the nib in the ink Yew produced from some smuggler's fold of his coat—good ink, iron-gall, the kind that bit into paper and stayed—and she wrote their names in guildhand, each one paired with its function, each function sealed with a small true sentence. *Brannic Ashfall, ward-wright, who paid his oath for strangers.* *Yew of Harrowgate, wayfinder, who gives away now what he used to sell.* *Maerin of the Autumn Court, flame-that-remembers, who hates well and chooses better.* And beneath them, her own name, and the word *vessel*, and she did not write a true sentence after it because she did not yet know what hers would be.

When she finished, the ledger drank the ink. The page warmed under her palm, faintly, like a sleeping animal.

"So that's it," Yew said softly. "Nine days."

"Nine days," Tamsin said. "Minus the ones we've already lost."

* * *

They marched.

The first day taught them the shape of the curse. It was not pain, exactly—or rather, it was pain the way hunger was pain, a slow subtraction dressed up as normalcy. Brannic stopped feeling his left little finger around midday and did not tell anyone until evening, and when Tamsin examined it the finger was still there, still warm, but when she pricked it with her knife-tip he didn't flinch. The nerves were forgetting their work. Or the world was forgetting the nerves. She genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Yew fared worse in a different way. Half-fae, he was anchored to both realms at once, and the curse pulled from both directions. By the second dawn he cast two shadows—one human-shaped, one longer and stranger, all wrong angles—and neither of them quite matched where he stood. He joked about it. He joked about everything, faster and more brittle, and Tamsin recognized the pattern from the poison tables: the courtiers who laughed loudest at dinner were the ones most afraid of the next course.

On the third night she caught him sitting alone on a ridge above the camp, looking at his hands in the starless dark, turning them over and over.

"You should sleep," she said.

"So should you."

"I'm the vessel. Apparently I'm allowed to be tired."

He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. Then, without preamble: "My mother was fae. Court-born, the real thing, before the courts started crumbling. She left the Otherworld for my father, and when he died she took me back across, and the Otherworld looked at me—half of me—and decided I was a rounding error." He flexed his fingers. The wrong-angled shadow flexed a half-beat late. "I've spent forty years making sure I was useful enough that nobody checked the arithmetic. Smuggling, running messages, moving things that needed moving. You don't get erased if you're load-bearing." He looked at her sideways. "Funny. Now I'm literally being erased, and the only thing holding me together is a book and a woman who drinks poison for a living."

"We're going to make it," Tamsin said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But I've watched a hundred people eat a hundred poisons, Yew, and I'll tell you what I learned. The dose matters less than the will. People who decide they're dead start dying. People who decide otherwise sometimes do the impossible, and I have personally seen it happen often enough that I've stopped calling it impossible and started calling it Tuesday." She sat down beside him on the cold rock. "We have six days left, a road behind Vey's lines, and a covenant that finally knows what it is. That's not nothing. That's actually more than we had a week ago."

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "Tuesday."

"Tuesdays are terrible," Tamsin said. "That's why we survive them."

* * *

The fourth day brought the first scouts.

Tamsin saw them from a kopje at midmorning: four riders in ash-grey livery, moving along the river road two miles south, unhurried, methodical, sweeping the countryside the way a man sweeps a floor he intends to own. Vey's outriders. Which meant the outer perimeter was closer than she'd estimated, which meant the declaration-window was closing, which meant—

"Decision point," Brannic murmured beside her. He'd seen them too. Everyone had. Even the two recovered signatories they'd gathered at Harrowgate—old Corvin, the debt-keeper, gaunt as a winter branch, and young Sefa, the door-that-closes, barely twenty and terrified of her own title—had gone still and watchful.

Tamsin weighed it the way she weighed everything: doses, timing, tolerance. Declare now, and Vey would know their position within hours—but the old law's protections would bind his wards, slow his soldiers, force him to negotiate with them as a covenant rather than hunt them as vermin. Wait, and they kept anonymity another day or two, but crossed his lines as smugglers, subject to every checkpoint and every gallows.

"Tonight," she said. "At dusk. We declare at the boundary-stone on the ridge—there's a marker there, I felt it yesterday, old covenant-work still humming under the moss. Declarations need witnesses, and boundary-stones are the oldest witnesses there are." She looked around the circle of faces: Brannic grim and steady, Yew pale and grinning anyway, Maerin banked low and burning, Corvin already counting costs in his head, Sefa clutching her own elbows. "One day of hiding left. Let's spend it sleeping and eating. Tomorrow we walk in the front door."

"And if the front door is a trap?" Sefa asked, very quietly.

"Then it's a trap we chose," Tamsin said, "with our eyes open, on our feet, together." She heard her own voice and was briefly startled by it—not by the steadiness, she'd had that trained into her along with the immunity, but by the warmth underneath it. Somewhere between the guildhall and the ridge, between the ledger and the dying light, these people had stopped being assets and become the thing she'd never had a word for. Family, maybe. Found, not fed to her. Chosen, all of them, including her.

* * *

Dusk came early that time of year, and the boundary-stone stood up out of the bracken like a broken tooth.

Tamsin set the ledger on its flat crown, and the old covenant-work woke under it with a sound she felt in her teeth—a deep harmonic, like a bell struck underwater. She opened to the page bearing their names. The ink had changed overnight; guildhand always settled as it dried, but this had settled into something more than ink, each letter faintly luminous, each true sentence holding its shape like a held breath.

She read them aloud. All of them. Functions and names and the small true sentences, her voice carrying across the darkening hills, and with each name the stone hummed deeper, and with each sentence something invisible swung into place around the company like the ribs of a ship taking shape.

When she spoke her own name—*Tamsin Okafor, vessel*—the stone went silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The kind of silence that listens.

And then, from far to the north, past the siege lines, past the blackened horizon where Vey's war-fires smoldered like a second, evil dusk, Tamsin felt it answer. Not the stone. Something vaster. The Veil itself, frayed and starving, turning its attention toward her the way a drowning creature turns toward a rope.

Along with it, unmistakable, came a colder attention. Sharp. Deliberate. Amused.

*There you are,* it seemed to say. *Nine days. How generous of you to count them down for me.*

Tamsin closed the ledger. Her hand, she noticed distantly, had begun to fade at the edges—just slightly, just the ghost of translucence along her fingertips, the curse keeping its promise.

"Six days," she said aloud, to the north, to the usurper, to the dark. "Watch us spend them well."

She turned back to her people, and they turned to her, and together they walked down out of the hills toward the Veil's heart, declared and diminishing, stubborn as ink.
