## 17. Mustering the Broken

**Tamsin**

The bells of Harrowgate rang wrong.

Tamsin had noticed it her first night in the city, when she'd still been flinching at every shadow and counting exits out of habit. The great bell in the Saltspire tower had a crack running through it like a healed bone, and so instead of a clean bronze note it gave a broken, doubled sound—two tones fighting inside one voice. The harrowgate folk said it had been that way for sixty years and no founder's smith could mend it without unmaking the tower. So they let it ring crooked, twice-sounded, half-human and half-otherwise, and called it the city's own voice.

Now that cracked bell was calling everyone home.

She stood on the guildhall steps—the smugglers' hall Yew had commandeered, its old ledgers of contraband stacked and burned to make room—and watched them come. Down the Salt Road from the hills: refugees with carts, with children on their shoulders, with nothing but names. Along the river road: deserters from Vey's legions, their grey-and-crimson livery torn off and left in ditches, walking in loose knots of men who kept glancing over their shoulders as if expecting the road itself to report them. And from the marshes and the drowned places, from the hedges and the hollow hills: the fae, coming in ones and twos, wearing borrowed shapes or no shapes at all, their outlines shivering like heat-haze where the fraying Veil touched them.

Not an army. She had seen armies now, from a distance, from hiding—Vey's columns moving like a machine with a thousand iron teeth. This was not that. This was a crowd arguing about where to put the latrines.

And yet her chest ached looking at it.

"Stop smiling," Brannic rumbled beside her. He was at the anvil he'd dragged up from the harbor district, hammering out ward-nails by the hundred, each one singing faintly as it cooled. "You look like someone about to sell them something."

"I am," Tamsin said. "I'm selling them the world. It's the only stock we have left."

She went down among them, because that was what ledger-keepers did—you didn't keep accounts from a distance; you walked the rows, you counted faces, you learned which name belonged to which debt of hope. A woman with flour to her elbows pressed a heel of bread into her hands and refused payment. A deserter with a shattered hand asked, in a voice like a boy's, whether the fae would eat him. Tamsin told him the truth: that Maerin had opinions about humans, most of them scathing, and none of them culinary. He laughed like it hurt.

Everywhere she went, they asked her the same question, in a hundred different ways. *What do you need from me?*

And she began to understand, standing in the press of them, that this was the answer Sorrel had never given her, the thing the guild had always gotten wrong even before it got wicked. You didn't muster the broken by telling them what they owed. You mustered them by asking what they had.

* * *

**Yew**

Yew had spent his whole life learning what things were worth, and the hardest lesson of his thirty-odd years was discovering he'd learned it wrong.

He worked the gates now—not commanding, exactly, though people kept deferring to him anyway, which he found both useful and mortifying—but cataloguing. That was his magic, such as it was: the half-fae knack for knowing what a thing was *for*. He could look at a frightened cooper from the hill villages and see not a refugee but a man who could seal water-tight barrels for a march through the Mire. He could look at a hedge-witch whose gifts had gone sour when the stars died and see a woman who still knew ninety ways to stretch a sack of barley.

So he stood at the gate with a slate and chalk, and he did not ask anyone what they were fleeing. He asked them what they could do.

"Smith?" he said to a broad woman with burn-scars up both forearms.

"Farrier."

"Brannic will want you. Second forge, past the fish market. Tell him Yew sent you and that I owe him a punch."

"And do you?"

"Almost certainly."

By dusk the slate was full, and full was the wrong word for what it held. It wasn't a roster. It was a covenant being written in chalk, one skill, one name, one stubborn refusal to lie down at a time. Yew looked at it and thought of all the manifests he'd ever falsified, all the desperate people he'd once charged double to move one town ahead of the fire, and he took the slate and added his own name at the bottom, in plain view, where anyone could read it.

*Yew of nowhere, smuggler. Owes everyone. Paying up.*

* * *

**Maerin**

Maerin burned low these days, and knew it.

Her mortal shell had been lost on the march—she carried the memory of it like a dress she'd outgrown—and what remained of her was fae-fire wearing intention, a shape of pale gold light that guttered when she grew angry and steadied when she worked. She had expected, after the shell, to be dismissed. Fading things were not wanted at musters. Fading things were pitied, and pity was a poison she had trained two centuries to be immune to.

Instead, the human girl had handed her the census.

"Your handwriting is four hundred years old," Tamsin had said, entirely without ceremony, pressing a stylus into the light that served Maerin for hands. "You're the only one here who can read the First Concordance script. We need the signatory lines transcribed before the reforging, and I need them done right."

So Maerin sat in the hall among the noise of humanity—the smell of it, the sweat and woodsmoke and boiling cabbage, all the gross warm animal life she had once disdained—and she wrote. And as she wrote the old names, line by line, she felt something she had not felt since the courts of her youth, before the exile, before the bitterness calcified:

Useful. Not used. *Useful.*

A human child wandered too close to her flame, reaching for the pretty light, and Maerin braced herself for the mother's scream. Instead the mother bowed—awkwardly, sincerely—and said, "Thank you for coming," as if Maerin were a guest and not a haunting.

Maerin wrote the next name more carefully than the last.

* * *

**Brannic**

Brannic had sworn oaths before. The smith's oath, paid away in the Mire of Small Debts. The garrison oath, broken the day his commanders started welding shackles instead of hinges. He had thought himself finished with swearing—that a man who'd lost two oaths had no business making a third.

But oaths, he was learning, were like nails. What mattered wasn't the metal. It was what you fastened it to.

He stood at the second forge past the fish market, teaching farriers and tinkerers and one very determined grandmother to strike ward-nails, and when the day's work cooled he called them together, awkward as a man proposing marriage to a crowd.

"You've all heard the girl talk," he said. "The Veil. Nine days. The stone at the heart of it. Some of you came here because you believe it. Some because you've got nowhere else. Both are fine reasons." He wiped his hands on his apron. "I can't promise you'll live. Anyone who promises you that is selling something. But I can promise you this: nobody marches alone, nobody dies unmourned, and every nail we strike goes into a door that stays shut behind us. That's the whole oath. Take it or don't."

They took it. All of them, even the grandmother, especially the grandmother, who spat in her palm first because that was how her village sealed a bargain and Brannic found, to his enormous surprise, that his eyes stung.

That night he hung the first finished nail above the hall's door, and the ward it anchored hummed like a struck bell—one clean note, holding.

* * *

**Tamsin**

On the seventh night, with two days remaining, Tamsin climbed to the hall's roof with the sealed ledger Sorrel had died—had *disappeared*, she still couldn't make her hands write the other word—carrying, and she opened it under the starless sky.

Below her, Harrowgate burned with cookfires and forge-light and lanterns strung between buildings by children who had never seen stars and were determined to invent some. She could hear singing somewhere near the fish market, badly, joyfully. Two recovered signatories sat by the eastern wall—an old ferryman named Corvin who had hidden forty years as a bridge-keeper, and a moth-winged archivist called Sef who spoke mostly in quotations—both of them quiet, both of them present, both of them, like her, carrying pieces of a thing larger than themselves.

Seven signatories. Three dead. One a tyrant. Three gathered, plus herself, plus whatever the covenant-stone would demand.

She turned to the last blank page, and found her hand moving before her mind caught up—ledger-script, the flowing interlocked characters of the Veilwrights, writing not numbers but *belongings*. Not what people owned. What people *were to each other*.

*One farrier, sworn. One grandmother, spitting palms. One child who has never seen stars. One exiled flame learning to be thanked. One smuggler paying his debts. One keeper, formerly a taster, formerly a foundling, formerly alone.*

She looked at the page and understood, finally, what Sorrel had been trying to tell her in the last hour of the old world, pressing the ledger into her arms: that a covenant was never made of clauses. It was made of names, entered honestly, kept faithfully.

The cracked bell of the Saltspire began to ring—wrong, doubled, half-human and half-otherwise, the city's own crooked voice calling across the rooftops. But beneath it, faintly, from the east, from the direction of Vey's advancing lines, came another sound threading through the dark.

Drums.

Two days. Perhaps less.

Tamsin closed the ledger, held it against her heart, and went down the stairs to tell them it was time to march.
