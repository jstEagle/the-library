## 18. The March Through the Dark

The sky had been empty for nine days now, and Brannic had learned to hate the dark in a new way. It was not the dark of a smithy at night, or of a cellar where he'd once hidden from his master's temper. It was a dark with a shape to it—a ceiling, vast and low and pressing down like the inside of a closed fist. No stars. No moon. Only the Veil's wound overhead, a bruise-colored nothing where light used to live.

He walked at the head of the column because Maerin had told him to.

"You have a soldier's shoulders and a coward's habit of apologizing," she'd said, back when they mustered at Harrowgate, when there were still stars enough to argue under. "Put both to use. Walk in front. If you die first, we'll know the road is bad."

It was the closest thing to affection she ever offered, and he had carried it like a coal in his chest ever since.

Behind him, three hundred souls moved through the night without torches. That had been Tamsin's idea—of course it had; everything clever was Tamsin's idea—and Yew had made it possible, threading them along smugglers' paths that existed on no map but lived in his memory like scars. They moved by touch and whisper and the faint phosphorescence of the Mire's last gifts: lichen crushed into paste and smeared on the heels of the person ahead, so each walker followed a ghost-glow through the bracken. Brannic could hear them breathing. Three hundred people trying to be quiet sounds like rain on a roof, if rain were afraid.

They were two nights from the Veil's heart, and one night from Vey's siege lines.

* * *

They stopped to rest in the hollow beneath a fallen watchtower, an old ward-station from the first covenant's day. Brannic felt it as soon as they entered—the stones still hummed with dead purpose, like a bell that remembered ringing. He put his palm flat against the foundation and let himself grieve for a moment, privately, the way smiths do: not for the tower, but for the hands that had raised it. Someone had warded this place once, carefully, believing it would matter forever. Nothing mattered forever. He knew that better than most.

"Your face does a thing," said Yew, settling beside him in the dark. "When you touch old work. Like you're listening to it apologize."

"It *is* apologizing." Brannic kept his voice low. "Wards are oaths, Yew. Oaths outlive the oath-keepers, and then they just... ache."

"That's grim even for you."

"I'm a disgraced ward-smith marching an army of refugees past a usurper's siege lines under a sky with no stars in it. Grim is the honest register."

Yew laughed—soft, quick, gone. Brannic had noticed that about him. The half-fae rationed his joy like a man crossing a desert with one waterskin. Somewhere behind them in the column, Tamsin slept sitting up against a supply cart, her ledger strapped across her chest like armor, her breath slow and even. She could sleep anywhere. She called it a taster's habit—"you rest when the food is safe, because you never know when it will be safe again"—and Brannic had come to think of her sleeping face as a kind of proof. Proof that rest was still possible. That the world, broken as it was, still permitted it.

Maerin stood apart, as always, at the hollow's edge, facing the direction of the enemy. Her mortal shell—that borrowed body of flesh and bone she wore so the covenant would accept her signature—looked small against the black. Fae-fire moved under her skin like lamp-oil under paper. She was dimming. Everyone could see it. Nobody said it.

Brannic said it.

"You should sleep," he told her, coming to stand beside her. "Even fae need—"

"I need nothing." Her voice was winter over stone. "I am spending what I am. Sleep is for those who intend to keep themselves."

"You intend to keep yourself."

She turned her head. In the lichen-light her eyes were banked coals, and for a moment—just a moment—the contempt in them flickered into something older and wearier. "I intend to reach the stone, smith. What arrives there is between me and the Veil."

He wanted to argue. He was good at arguing; he'd argued his way out of his guild, out of his marriage, out of every life he'd ever ruined. But Maerin did not argue. She simply stopped existing in the conversation, the way nobility does, and so he left her at her post and went to check the column's flanks, and hated himself a little for how easily he'd been dismissed, and hated the dark more.

* * *

The siege lines announced themselves by smell before anything else.

Woodsmoke and horse and latrine-ditch and iron—Vey's army cooked meat, real meat, wasteful quantities of it, because an army that eats well believes in its own future. Brannic smelled it an hour before Yew's forward scouts signaled halt, and his stomach clenched around the memory of every siege he'd ever stood inside of. He'd been on the losing side of two and the winning side of one, and the winning had felt exactly like losing with better food.

Yew materialized out of the bracken the way he always did, all elbows and grin, though tonight the grin was stretched thin over something serious. "Two camps," he breathed. "Vanguard here—" he pressed a finger into the dirt, drawing lines only he could see, "—and the main host beyond the ridge. Between them, a kill-lane maybe four hundred paces wide. They've left it open on purpose. It's bait. Anyone crossing gets funneled toward the river ford, and the ford is mined with contract-wards. Step wrong and the ground itself owns you."

"Tamsin can void contract-wards," Brannic said.

"Tamsin can't void four hundred paces of them while three hundred people walk single file and quiet." Yew's grin turned rueful. "But I know this ford. Smuggled silk through it for six years. There's a path—the wards were laid by a Harrowgate guildman named Ossel, lazy as sin, and he anchored half of them to a boundary-stone that's been moved twice since. There's a seam. Narrow. One walker at a time, hand on the shoulder of the one ahead."

"So we go blind, in silence, one at a time, through an army's front door."

"Now you're understanding smuggling."

Brannic looked back down the column. In the lichen-glow he could see faces: the Harrowgate refugees with their bundled children; the two recovered signatories, old Aldous and the twins, moving with the careful dignity of people carrying something breakable—which they were; the deserters from Vey's own ranks, who had chosen this march knowing what it meant if they were caught; the fae exiles, luminous and fading, walking among humans for the first time in an age without a wall or a bargain between them. Not an army. A covenant. Tamsin kept saying it like a spell, and slowly, horribly, it was becoming true.

"We'll need to carry the children," Brannic said. "And the old ones. And anyone who shakes."

"Then we'll be carrying half the column." Yew shrugged. "Good thing you've got soldier's shoulders."

* * *

They crossed in the hour before false dawn, which no longer came—the stars being dead, dawn arrived now like a rumor, a gray suggestion rather than a promise. Brannic went first, as always, one hand trailing the guide-rope Yew had strung, the other held back flat against the darkness whenever the line needed to stop. Behind him, hand on his shoulder-belt, came a Harrowgate grandmother named Sefa who had lost two sons to Vey's conscription and walked now with the terrible calm of someone who had already given the war everything it could take.

The seam through the wards was exactly as narrow as Yew had promised. Brannic felt it with his smith-sense: a thread of unclaimed ground winding between the contract-wards like a gap in a brick wall, and on either side of it, the wards themselves, humming. He could read them as he passed—not their words, but their weight. Vey's contracts. Every one of them a promise extracted from someone, somewhere, at someone's expense. He thought of his own smith's oath, spent in the Mire of Small Debts, buying passage for these same people, and wondered idly if some ward-smith on the other side had felt *him* pass—a debt paid, an oath spent, a stranger's sacrifice registered in the great ledger neither of them could see.

Halfway across the kill-lane, the baby cried.

It was nobody's fault. Babies cry; it is their whole profession. But the sound went up thin and sharp into the starless dark, and Brannic felt the entire column flinch like a single animal, and far off to his left a lantern bloomed, then another, then a horn blew—one long note, uncertain, questioning.

"Move," Brannic breathed, and broke the rule of silence because silence was over. "Move *now*. All of you. Run for the ridge."

The seam dissolved behind them as they abandoned it—there was no time to find it anymore, only the open ground and the wards and the growing noise of an army waking up. Brannic grabbed Sefa's arm and ran, hauling her, feeling the column surge behind him like a river jumping its banks. A ward snapped shut somewhere to his right with a sound like a giant closing a book, and a deserter screamed—not wounded, just *caught*, the contract seizing his legs, claiming him—and Brannic heard Tamsin's voice cut through the chaos, clear as a blade:

"Hold! Everyone hold where you are!"

And then she was running *toward* the screaming, ledger already open, and Yew was swearing in two languages, and Brannic made the choice that defined him, the choice he'd been making since Harrowgate: he ran after her.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Brannic's memory of it stayed fast forever after, unsorted, like coins poured on a table.

Tamsin kneeling over the caught deserter, ledger flat on the ground, her pen moving faster than he'd ever seen it move—rewriting the man's claim mid-stride, transferring the contract's anchor, doing in thirty seconds the paperwork that should have taken a guild-office a week. The ward releasing him with a sound like a sigh. The lanterns multiplying on the flank. Arrows beginning to fall, lazy and arcing, more warning than aimed. The column pouring up the ridge in a ragged flood. And Maerin—

Maerin stepping out of the column's tail, alone, onto the open ground, facing the lanterns.

"No," Brannic heard himself say. "No, no—"

She raised one hand, and her mortal shell began, gently, to burn.

Not flesh burning. *Shell* burning—the way a letter burns when its message has been delivered, the way a chrysalis burns when the thing inside has outgrown it. Fae-fire poured up out of her skin in ribbons, silver-green and blinding, and the arrows that had been falling toward the column bent away from her as if ashamed. She stood in the open ground between her people and Vey's vanguard, and she was the brightest thing any of them had seen in nine days of dead sky, and the soldiers in the lantern-light faltered, because you cannot easily shoot at a star when the sky has none.

Her voice carried across the kill-lane without effort, the way it carries in dreams:

"This shell was always a courtesy. The covenant does not require my body. It requires my *name*, and my name is written nowhere you can burn."

Then the vanguard charged anyway, because soldiers are soldiers, and the first crossbow bolt took her through the chest, and Brannic learned in that moment that a fae noble's mortal shell dies exactly as messily as any grandmother's, exactly as any deserter's, blood and shock and the awful human weight of a body falling.

He reached her first. Of course he did. His knees hit the mud beside her and his hands hovered, useless, over the ruin of her, over the fire still guttering in the wounds.

"Maerin—"

"Smith." Her voice was smaller now, coming from farther away, though her lips barely moved. "Stop hovering. You'll scorch your sleeves."

"You're dying. Your shell is dying."

"Yes." Something moved across her face that on a human would have been called a smile. "I told you what arrives at the stone is between me and the Veil. My shell was always going to be spent. Better spent *here*—buying three hundred lives a hundred paces of ground—than hoarded for pride." Her eyes found his, and the coals in them were very bright and very low. "You apologized to me once. In the Mire. When you paid your oath. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"Don't apologize now. Apologies are for debts unpaid." Her hand rose—it weighed nothing, it weighed less than nothing—and touched his wrist, where a smith's burn-scar crossed the pulse. "You paid yours. I am paying mine. We are *paid*, you stubborn man. Now get up. Get them up the ridge. Mourning is permitted at the stone, not before it."

The light in her eyes went out like a lamp pinched by careful fingers.

What rose from her body was not her body. It was fire—her true fire, the fae-fire that had dimmed under her skin for weeks, now freed of the flesh that had been dimming it. It stood over her shell for one long moment, wavering, no taller than a candle-flame, and Brannic understood with a grief that had no floor beneath it that this was what remained: a name without a hand to write it, a signatory without a signature, a person reduced to their purest and smallest self.

The flame drifted to him. Settled, briefly, on his shoulder—warm as a forge, light as ash.

Then it rose, and took its place above the column, and led them up the ridge.

* * *

They gained the high ground as the gray non-dawn thickened, and Vey's vanguard, having lost the dark and found only stubbornness at the top of the hill, did not press. Armies are conservative animals. They had been sent to catch smugglers, not to assault a hill held by a burning spirit and three hundred people who had nothing left to lose, and so they settled into watching, and sending riders, and waiting for orders from a lord who did not yet know his quarry had slipped the trap's teeth.

Brannic dug Maerin's grave himself. It was what he knew how to do—work with his hands, make a thing straight and true. He laid her mortal shell in the earth with the care he'd give a finished piece, and Sefa the grandmother stood beside him and spoke the Harrowgate burial words, and the two recovered signatories added the covenant's older words, older than either realm, and Tamsin stood at the foot of the grave with her ledger open and wrote, in the space where Maerin's living countersignature used to sit, a single line:

*Paid in full.*

Above them, the fae-fire burned on, steady, patient, no larger than a lantern. Dimming. Everyone could see it. Nobody said it.

Yew came and stood at Brannic's shoulder as the company formed up again. "We lost eleven," he said quietly. "Ten to the wards and arrows. And her shell." He paused. "The road from here runs through the Ashvale—the burned forest, Vey's own scorched ground. Two days. After that, the heart."

"Then we walk two more days," Brannic said.

"You're not going to say anything about her? About what it cost?"

Brannic looked up at the small flame riding above the column, at the three hundred faces below it turning toward the road, at Tamsin already walking, ledger against her chest, because grief that stops moving is just a slower way of dying and she had never once in her life allowed herself the luxury.

"She told me not to apologize," Brannic said. "So I won't. I'll just carry what she told me to carry." He hefted his pack and set his soldier's shoulders to the road. "Forward. That's the whole eulogy. Forward."

They marched into the Ashvale as the false dawn failed to arrive, and behind them, far to the south, Brannic saw a new light kindle along the horizon—too orderly for fire, too numerous for lamps.

Vey's main host had broken camp.

Whatever waited at the Veil's heart, they would not be alone when they reached it.
