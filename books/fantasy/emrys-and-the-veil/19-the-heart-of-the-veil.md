## 19. The Heart of the Veil

The valley had no name on any map Tamsin had ever audited, and she suspected that was the point. Maps were contracts too—claims written in ink instead of blood—and the Veil's heart had never consented to be claimed. It sat in a bowl of hills where the grass grew silver at the roots and the air tasted faintly of iron and honey, like a blade dipped in mead. At its center stood the covenant-stone.

She had imagined it larger. In her mind's eye, nine days of marching through the dark had built it into a mountain, a spire, something to justify everything it had cost: Maerin's mortal shell burned away to a thread of dimming fire; Brannic's oath paid out to the Mire until his hands shook with the debt of it; Yew's routes given away route by route until he arrived with nothing but the clothes he'd smuggled himself into years ago. Two recovered signatories besides—old Wren with her river-voice, and Halvard, who had not spoken since they pulled him from a Veyan prison wagon and whose silence had the weight of a held breath.

But the stone was only waist-high. A squat, weathered thing, half-swallowed by moss, unremarkable as a boundary marker between two farmers' fields. Anyone might walk past it. Anyone might lean against it, unknowing, and feel nothing but cold stone through their coat.

That was the horror of it, Tamsin thought. The most important object in two realms looked like nothing at all. She knew how that felt.

"It's smaller than I expected," Brannic said beside her, echoing her thought so exactly that she almost laughed. His smith's hands were wrapped in fresh linen—the Mire still collected from him, even now, small tolls of heat and ache—and he flexed them as if testing whether they were still his.

"Most load-bearing things are," she said. "You should see the foundation stones under the guildhall. Men have built cathedrals on less."

Wren drifted past them toward the stone, her bare feet leaving prints that filled with silver light instead of shadow. She was the eldest signatory left alive, older than the free cities, older perhaps than the idea of cities, and she moved like water finding its level. When she laid her palm flat against the moss, the whole valley exhaled.

Tamsin felt it in her teeth. In her tongue, which had been trained since childhood to read what others could not taste. The air here was *full*—not poisoned, never poisoned, but dense with every bargain ever sealed, every promise kept or broken across the border of the worlds. She could taste the original covenant in it, seven signatures braided into one long chord, three of them snapped like lute strings. She could taste the rot where Vey's purchased segments had been cut away. And beneath all of it, faint as a dying fire, she could taste the curse.

It was already there. Waiting in the stone like venom in a fang, patient, addressed to all seven names and none other. The moment the covenant was reforged—sealed, made whole again—it would wake and flow outward along the old channels, into every signatory living and dead, and unmake them slowly, thoroughly, in the way that curses are thorough. Dissolution. The same slow unraveling that had begun in them when the shard broke, accelerated to its intended purpose. The covenant protected itself the way anything ancient protects itself: by killing those who touch it.

Unless someone drank first.

*Someone who has survived every poison of both realms.* The drowned archive's final clause, read by lamplight while water dripped from the ceiling of a sunken library onto pages no human eye was meant to see. She had spent nine days not saying it aloud. Nine days letting the company believe the reforging was a matter of ritual and will, of oaths spoken in the right order at the right hour. They had enough grief. She would give them this last lie for as long as it would hold.

"Tamsin." Yew's voice, low, at her shoulder. He always came at her shoulder from the blind side, smuggler's habit, though these days he mostly used it to hand her things—water, dried plums, the small kindnesses he was learning to give away instead of sell. "Scouts are back. He's coming."

"I know." She could taste it on the wind from the east: ash and caged starlight, the particular flavor of Lord Cassian Vey's magic, which was the flavor of something eaten alive. "How far?"

"He'll crest the eastern ridge by full dark. Full strength. Ten thousand if it's one." Yew turned his hat in his hands—a nervous habit she'd never seen him indulge before Harrowgate, before the roads filled with people he couldn't charge. "We have forty-one. Forty-two counting Halvard, though I'm not sure Halvard counts."

"We're not an army," Tamsin said. "We're a covenant. Armies win battles. Covenants hold."

Yew looked at her for a long moment, and in the silver half-light of the starless sky his half-fae eyes caught what little illumination remained and held it, twin green coals. "You've started talking like Sorrel," he said quietly. "You know that?"

The name landed in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Sorrel,* who had raised her. Who had taught her ledgers and immunity and the difference between a bargain and a trap. Who had sold pieces of the world to a usurper with her forged countersignature drying on the page. Who had pressed a sealed ledger into her hands and told her to run, and whom she had never learned to mourn properly because she could never decide whether to mourn him or hate him.

"Yes," she said. "I know."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the work was liturgy.

Brannic knelt at the stone's base and did what smiths do: he prepared the surface. Not with hammer and tongs but with oath-work, murmuring the old formulas of tempering and quench, laying his scarred palms against the moss so that the metal-memory in his bones could speak to the stone's deep structure. Where he touched, the silver in the grass brightened. "It remembers being made," he reported, wonder cracking his gravel voice. "It remembers seven hands."

Wren sang. There was no other word for it, though what she produced was less melody than architecture—long sustained tones that seemed to excavate the valley, pulling up from the soil the ghost-shapes of the original sealing. Tamsin watched translucent figures flicker at the edge of sight: seven forms around the stone, hands joined, voices joined, one of them wearing a face she recognized from portraits in the guildhall's oldest alcove. Sorrel's predecessor. Sorrel's predecessor's predecessor. A lineage of keepers going back to the beginning, and somewhere down that line, a decision had been made to raise foundling children with poison in their cups and ledgers in their laps, just in case the world ever needed a vessel again.

*Just in case.* Her whole life, a contingency plan. She set the thought aside the way she'd learned to set aside pain at the king's table—one measured breath, one swallow, one more step without showing it on her face.

Halvard spoke once, near dusk. He stood apart, facing east, and said a single word—"coming"—in a voice like a door opening after years, and then went silent again. But the word was enough. Everyone stopped. Even the wind seemed to pause to listen.

Tamsin climbed the western rise to look.

They came out of the dark like a wound opening. Torches first, hundreds of them, a creeping constellation of orange false stars beneath the true stars' absence. Then the banners—Vey's grey and black, the severed-hand sigil snapping in a wind that smelled of cinders. Then the mass of them, rank on rank, pikes and crossbows and the pale shapes of bound fae driven forward as shock troops, their wings pinned with iron. And at the column's heart, carried on a litter of black wood, a cage.

Even at this distance, she could taste it. The last shard of the Veil, torn from the sky when the wards fell, and it *screamed*—not in sound but in flavor, a high thin note of agony that only her trained tongue could parse. Vey fed on it. Had been feeding on it for months. Every spell he cast, every ward he broke, every soldier he hardened against fear was bought with bites of a living piece of heaven.

"He brought all of it," Brannic said, coming up beside her. "He didn't leave a reserve."

"No," Tamsin said. "Because he doesn't think he needs one. Because to him this is a mop-up operation." She watched the torch-river bend around the valley's eastern rim and begin to descend. "Forty-two against ten thousand, and we're not even trying to fight. We're trying to finish a ceremony."

"Then we'd best be quick about the ceremony."

"That's the trouble with curses, Brannic. They're never quick."

Below, Wren had begun the opening invocations, and the stone had begun to glow.

* * *

Battle, when it came, was not a battle. It was a siege conducted in reverse—a handful of defenders around a stone, holding a circle the size of a threshing floor against a tide that wanted only to reach the center and stop them.

Vey's first wave came fast, expecting terror. What they got was Maerin.

She had been pure fae-fire since the pass, a slender ribbon of blue-white flame that spoke in a voice like frost forming, and when the first soldiers crossed the circle's boundary she rose up out of the grass in front of them and simply *was*, in all the terrible glory her exile had contained. Men dropped their pikes and fell to their knees. Some wept. One tore off his helmet and stared at her with the naked homesickness of a man seeing the sky of his childhood, and Maerin—Maerin, who hated humans, who had nearly killed Tamsin over a ledger, who had lost her body to buy these hours—curved herself around him almost gently and said, in her freezing voice, "*Not tonight. Tonight you may live. Go home.*"

Half the first wave went home.

The rest came on, and then it was ugly and close and real. Brannic fought the way he forged: economically, with terrible patience, each stroke placed like a rivet. Yew was everywhere and nowhere, using smuggling tricks—false trails, sudden darkness, the simple criminal genius of knowing exactly which ground looks solid and isn't—to fold Vey's formations in on themselves. Wren sang and the singing became a wall. Halvard finally spoke again, a stream of words in a language older than either realm, and where the words landed, soldiers forgot why they had come.

And Tamsin stood at the stone with her hands on the moss and read.

That was her part. That was what all of it had been for—not the fighting, never the fighting. The reforging was a contract, and contracts were her native country. The old text rose through the stone into her fingertips, clause by clause, signature by signature, and she recited it aloud in ledger-script, the guild's secret notation, restoring each broken line the way she'd once restored corrupted accounts: find the error, trace it home, correct the entry, initial the change. Three dead signatories' names she spoke from memory of their traces in the archive. Maerin's name she spoke and felt the fae-fire flare in answer. Wren. Halvard. Herself, added in the margin, an amendment no one had authorized but everyone had tacitly accepted.

Six names. Six channels open. The seventh—

The seventh channel ended at Lord Cassian Vey, and it was rotten through, a pipe of ash, and through it the curse was already seeping backward into the working like bilge water into a hold. She could taste it growing. Sour. Patient. Addressed to six names and gathering itself to strike the moment the seal closed.

*Tonight,* she thought, with a clarity so terrible it was almost peaceful. *Tonight, at the end, when the last clause is spoken, it comes for all of us. Unless it comes for me first.*

She kept reading. Her voice never wavered. That, at least, the king's table had taught her: whatever is happening inside you, the outside stays steady.

* * *

Vey came at moonrise—or at the hour that would have been moonrise, in a world that still had a moon worth the name. He came alone past his own lines, walking through the battle as through a garden he owned, and the fighting bent around him the way water bends around a keel.

Up close he was smaller than the stories. A narrow man, grey-eyed, precise, with the neatness of someone who has never once had to clean up his own messes. The cage floated behind his shoulder like a lantern, and inside it the shard of the Veil beat like a wounded heart, dimming visibly even as Tamsin watched, spending itself to power his next sentence.

"Lady Okafor." His voice was mild. Reasonable. The voice of a man auditing accounts. "Or should I say Keeper? You've done remarkable work with my property. Those ledger corrections are quite elegant. I confess I checked them twice for errors."

"They're not your property," Tamsin said. She did not stop reciting. The words came steadily while her eyes measured him—distance, angle, the cage's drift, the exact spot where his attention pooled. "Nothing was ever yours. You bought stolen goods from a frightened old man and called it conquest."

"Sorrel wasn't frightened. Sorrel was *realistic*." Vey tilted his head, and for a moment something almost human moved behind the grey eyes—something tired. "Do you know what I saw, all those years ago, when I first understood what the Veil costs? Fae taking human luck like rent. Humans burning fae like fuel. Two realms bleeding each other through a fence neither can close and neither can open. I don't want to destroy magic, Keeper. I want to end the *transaction*. No more bargains. No more borders. One world, whole, honest, governed." He smiled thinly. "You of all people should appreciate clean books."

"The cleanest book I ever audited," Tamsin said, "had one entry per page, in a dead man's hand, and every entry was a murder. Clean isn't the same as good." She lifted her chin toward the cage. "And you're not ending the transaction. You're the transaction. Look at what you eat."

Something flickered in his face—the first crack—and behind it, in the cage, the shard flared as if it had heard its own suffering named. Vey's hand rose. Grey light gathered.

"You could have had a place," he said, and meant it, and that was the worst thing about him. "Immunity like yours. Do you know how rare—"

Brannic hit him from the blind side with a smith's hammer.

It shouldn't have worked. It was absurd—a mortal man with wrapped hands striking a mage who had unmade ward-stones with a word—but Vey was mid-sentence, mid-offer, mid-*human*, and for one instant he was only a narrow man in fine clothes, and the hammer took him across the shoulder and spun him into the grass. The cage lurched free of his control, drifting, keening.

"*Now!*" Tamsin shouted, and the valley answered.

Yew came out of nowhere with a smuggler's grapnel and hooked the cage's frame. Wren's song spiked into something that cracked pikes at fifty paces. Maerin poured herself into a spear of blue fire and struck the cage's lock—not to free the shard, Tamsin realized, but to *shatter the lock's claim*, to void the ownership written into the iron, and the lock burst like a frost-flower and the cage doors swung wide.

The shard rose. For one heartbeat the whole valley saw it: a fragment of the night sky as it used to be, holding actual stars inside it, tiny and burning and free. Soldiers on both sides stopped fighting. Some screamed. Some prayed. Halvard wept openly, great silent gulps, the first tears Tamsin had seen from him.

And Vey got up.

Blood on his mouth, shoulder wrong, grey eyes gone fully luminous now—he stopped pretending to be a man. Power rolled off him in waves that flattened the grass and snuffed torches, and he reached for the shard with fingers that had become something else, talons of solidified hunger, and the shard—wounded, wild, unclaimed—fled.

It fled *to the stone.*

It fled to Tamsin.

It struck her chest like a falling star and sank in, and the world went white, and in the white she heard her own voice complete the final clause of the covenant, steady as a taster's hand, unwavering as a keeper's pen:

"—sealed, witnessed, and *bound.*"

The covenant closed. All seven channels opened at once.

And the curse woke, and came home.

* * *

It entered her the way all poisons enter: sweetly, at first.

She had time to notice that. Time to stand in the blazing heart of the reforged Veil with six names ringing in the stone behind her and feel the death-curse arrive at the border of her body and *knock*, politely, as if invited. It was vast. It was patient. It carried the dissolution of worlds in it, the unwinding of every signatory across both realms, and it had been waiting centuries for delivery.

Every other poison she had ever drunk had been a letter. This was an ocean.

But she had trained her whole life for oceans, sip by sip.

Tamsin Okafor opened herself and began to drink.

Across the valley, Vey screamed—not in defeat, not yet, but in *denial*, because the reforged Veil was turning its attention on him now, the new-made seal reading its own ledger and finding one entry outstanding: a segment purchased in stolen starlight, signed in a dead man's hand, never returned. The grey light around him guttered. The borrowed magic he had fed on for months tried to leave him all at once, and he clawed it back, and stood, and began—horribly—to laugh.

"You'll die holding the door," he called to her across the burning grass, advancing through Wren's song and Brannic's hammer-line and Maerin's fire as though they were weather. "You'll die a vessel, a thing, a *seal*—is that the belonging you wanted, Keeper? To be furniture?"

Tamsin, drinking the ocean one sip at a time, tasting starlight and sorrow and the slow unraveling of everything, found that she could still smile.

"No," she said. "Furniture doesn't choose."

The stars inside her chest began, very softly, to reignite.
