## 22. A Table Set for Everyone

The guildhall smelled of bread.

That was the first miracle of the evening, and Tamsin noticed it the way she noticed everything now—twice, once with her human senses and once through the thin bright membrane of the Veil that ran beneath the world like a second bloodstream. Bread, roasting meat, honeyed pears, woodsmoke, tallow candles, the green bite of fresh-cut rushes on the floor. Through the Veil's edge, all of it shimmered: every dish carried its own small signature, a warmth or a coolness, a flavor before flavor. She had spent twenty years learning to read poison in the space between a smell and its meaning. Now she read the whole hall at a glance, and there was nothing in it but food, and people, and the long slow work of becoming a family.

She stood in the doorway of the rebuilt guildhall for a moment longer than she needed to, because she had learned—slowly, stubbornly, over three harvests—that moments like this were meant to be stood in.

The hall had been Sorrel's once. Not this room exactly; the old guildhall had burned the night the stars went out, along with half the ledger-vaults and everything Sorrel had ever hidden in them. But when the free cities voted to dissolve the old Veilwrights' Guild and raise something honest in its place, they had chosen these foundations. Brannic himself had laid the new hearthstone, muttering ward-songs under his breath the way he did when he was pretending not to care. Yew had "acquired" the great oak tables from a merchant who had profited handsomely from refugee tolls during the war—a redistribution, he called it, with a straight face and a smuggler's shrug. Maerin's fire had lit the first candle, back when she still had a mortal shell to carry it in, and even now, three seasons gone to flame, a taper in the eastern window burned fae-green and never guttered. The others had stopped finding it strange. Tamsin never would.

The tables were full.

At the near end sat the signatories—or what remained of them, which was more than anyone had dared hope. Old Wren of the Drowned Archive, who had guarded the covenant's original text for two hundred years and still spoke as though the library's water had gotten into his voice, was telling a story with his hands, and the story was making the children laugh. There were children here now. That still caught in Tamsin's chest like a fishbone. Children born after the Veil was reforged, who had never seen a starless sky, who thought the faint auroral shimmer over the northern hills—the scar tissue of the reforging, visible only at dusk—was simply how the world had always looked.

Two seats down, Halvern and Isbet, the recovered signatories, argued amiably about whether the covenant-stone's new inscription should be in the old script or the common tongue. They had been arguing about it since spring. It was, Tamsin suspected, less an argument than a ritual, a way of proving to each other week after week that they were both still here, still solid, still themselves—two people who had felt the curse of dissolution crawling up their veins and lived anyway, because she had drunk it instead.

She touched her own throat without thinking. The habit of a lifetime. Checking.

Nothing there but pulse.

* * *

The kitchen was hot and loud and hers, in the loose way that nothing was ever really hers anymore, thank the stars. Three cooks worked the ovens: a Harrowgate woman named Dell who had fed evacuation lines off Yew's smuggling routes until the routes became relief lines and Yew stopped charging; a fae exile called Sennet whose people had tended orchards on the other side of the Veil since before the covenant, and who wept the first time she bit into an Otherworld pear grown on this side of it; and a boy of perhaps fourteen, Pip, who had come out of Vey's war camp with the last group of freed captives and who flinched at raised voices and watched Tamsin taste everything with wide, hungry eyes.

"You're doing it again," Pip said, as she lifted the spoon to the stew.

"I'm checking it."

"You checked it when you made it. You checked it when it simmered. You checked it when Sennet added the thyme." He folded his arms, and there was something in his face—impatience braided with something softer—that reminded her painfully of herself at that age, standing in Sorrel's shadow, wanting desperately to be told the rules of a world that had never explained itself. "You always check."

"Old habits keep you alive," Tamsin said.

"New ones keep you happy," said Sennet, not looking up from her pears. "My grandmother used to say it. She also said a cook who fears her own pot has no business owning one."

Tamsin laughed—she laughed more easily these days, another thing she was still getting used to—and dipped the spoon anyway, because Pip was watching and some lessons deserved to be taught properly. She let the broth sit on her tongue. Through the Veil's edge she read it the way she'd once read the king's soup: bitter base, sweet finish, salt balanced against smoke, and threaded through all of it the clean signature of nothing harmful at all.

"Clear," she pronounced, and Pip beamed as if she'd knighted him.

Dell snorted. "You say 'clear' like a physician delivering a verdict. One day you'll taste something and just say 'good,' like a person."

"One day," Tamsin agreed, and did not believe either of them, and was fine with that.

Because here was the truth she had come to, slowly, the way sediment settles in wine: the tasting was no longer fear. It hadn't been for a long time. Somewhere between the king's table and the covenant-stone, between drinking poison by profession and drinking a god's malice by choice, the act had changed shape entirely. When she tasted now, she wasn't guarding against death. She was greeting the food. Every spoonful was a small conversation: *What are you? What do you carry? Are you safe for the people I love?* It was the same question she had asked the ledgers, back when ledgers were all she had. It was, she understood now, the only question she had ever known how to ask.

*Are you safe for the people I love.*

The hall beyond the kitchen door was filling with the answer.

* * *

She carried the first tureen out herself, because she was still the First Keeper and First Keepers carried things, and because the sight of her carrying food did something to people that speeches never could. The room quieted in that particular way—not silence, but a gathering of attention, the way a hearth gathers heat.

They came, that season, from everywhere. Refugees from the river towns who had lost homes to Vey's levies and found rooms above the rebuilt market. Deserters from his legions, men and women who had walked away from the war camps with their shields on their backs and their heads down, and who had been fed first and questioned later, because that was the rule Tamsin had written into the new guild's founding ledger, in her own hand, on the very first page: *No one is asked their history before they are asked if they are hungry.* Fae exiles whose courts had crumbled when the Veil frayed, who had crossed over expecting to die in a foreign world and had instead been handed bread. A delegation from the capital, stiff and uncomfortable, bearing the king's apology for two centuries of treating the guild as a convenience rather than a covenant—an apology Tamsin had accepted on behalf of the dead, and then filed, literally, in a drawer marked PENDING, where she looked at it sometimes and smiled.

And at the high table, her impossible, improbable family.

Brannic sat where he always sat, close to the door, a smith's habit even now that he was more keeper than smith. His forearms were bare despite the autumn chill—he ran hot, he claimed, though everyone knew it was the Mire of Small Debts that had taken his smith's oath and left him running warm ever since, a man paid out of his own account forever. He was carving a wooden horse for one of the refugee children, knife moving in small sure strokes, and he glanced up when she entered with the tureen and gave her the nod. Just the nod. It contained, as it always did, approximately nine paragraphs.

Yew sprawled beside him, boots on the bench, looking like a man who had never obeyed a rule in his life, wearing the First Keeper's badge of office upside down because he thought it was funny. He had given away his entire final cargo run—every bolt of silk, every cask of brandy—to the winter shelters in Harrowgate, and had complained about the financial ruin so loudly and so often that everyone understood it was a love song. These days he ran the guild's supply lines, honestly, at cost, and grumbled about honesty the way other men grumbled about weather. His eyes found hers across the room, and he raised an imaginary glass, and mouthed something she was fairly sure was *your stew is better than my conscience,* which was the nicest thing he knew how to say.

Wren. Halvern. Isbet. The ghost-green candle in the east window that was all that remained of Maerin, burning steady, and beside it the small clay pot of ash-and-rosemary that Brannic had placed there the first harvest and never moved, because grief, he said, deserved a seat at the table too.

Tamsin set down the tureen, and the hall began to eat.

* * *

She tasted everything. Of course she did.

It was the ritual of the evening now, half joke, half sacrament, and the newer members of the household had learned to wait for it with the solemnity of children waiting for a bell. She went table to table with a clean spoon, and at each dish she took a single measured sip or bite, held it, and read it.

The roast venison, hunted by deserters turned foresters: clear. The honeyed pears, Sennet's triumph, grown from cuttings smuggled across the reforged border in Yew's last dishonest act: clear, and impossibly sweet, carrying the faint cold-gold signature of the Otherworld's soil, proof that the two realms could feed each other now instead of bleeding each other. The dark bread Dell baked in the shape of the old guild sigil, seven points for seven signatories: clear. The stew, the soup, the spiced wine, the cider pressed from the orchard the freed captives had planted behind the hall: clear, clear, clear, clear.

Table by table. Dish by dish. One sip at a time.

It was, she realized somewhere around the third course, the same motion she had performed at the covenant-stone, kneeling before the death-curse with Vey's army howling at the wards and Maerin's fire guttering low, when she had lifted the black liquid of the curse in a cupped hand and drunk it down the only way she knew how—one sip at a time, trained immunity against cosmic malice, a lifetime of small poisons preparing her for the largest one ever brewed. The hall remembered that night differently. They told it as thunder and light, as the sky splitting open and relighting, as Vey unmade in a single white instant when the reforged Veil rejected him like a body rejecting spoiled meat.

But Tamsin remembered it as tasting. As reading a flavor and pronouncing verdict. *This is safe. This will not harm them. Take it into yourself and let it end with you.*

She reached the last table, the one nearest the hearth, where the newest arrivals sat—three families off the river barges, hollow-eyed still, eating carefully the way people eat when they have recently known hunger. A girl of six watched Tamsin approach with enormous gravity, holding her bowl protectively against her chest.

"This is the tasting," Tamsin told her gravely. "I check everything, so no one is ever poisoned at this table. Not ever again."

"Even the pudding?"

"*Especially* the pudding. Pudding is historically dangerous."

The girl considered this, decided it was true, and surrendered a spoonful. Tamsin tasted it—honey, cream, a whisper of burnt sugar where Pip had gotten ambitious—and through the Veil's edge it glowed like a lantern, harmless and warm and utterly ordinary, which was the most extraordinary flavor in the world.

"Clear," she said.

And then—because the girl was watching, because Sennet was watching from the kitchen door with her arms folded and her grandmother's proverb in her eyes, because Brannic and Yew and Wren and Halvern and Isbet and forty refugees and exiles and deserters and children were all watching, because three harvests of peace had built up inside her like pressure behind a dam—

Tamsin Okafor laughed.

Not the controlled, professional laugh she had deployed at the king's table, not the sharp bark of a ledger-keeper catching an error, but a real one, helpless and loud, that bent her double and made her wipe her eyes, that startled the barge-families until they started laughing too, uncertain at first and then genuinely, contagiously, the sound rolling down the long tables like wind through wheat. Yew was pounding the table. Brannic had gone red trying not to smile and failed completely. Even the green candle in the east window seemed to flicker brighter, one brief flare, like a friend laughing from far away.

"What?" gasped Pip, arriving with the next course. "What's funny?"

"Nothing," Tamsin managed. "Everything. It's—" She straightened, still shaking, and looked down the length of the hall she had inherited from a traitor and rebuilt into something true. "It's clear. All of it. For the first time I didn't have to check."

A lie, technically. She had checked. She would always check. But it was also, somehow, the truest thing she had ever said, and the hall seemed to understand it that way, raising cups and spoons and one wooden horse in salute.

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and the youngest children slept in a heap of cloaks by the hearth, the core of them stayed behind as they always did—Brannic, Yew, Wren, Halvern, Isbet, and Tamsin, with the green candle keeping Maerin's place. Someone poured the last of the cider. Outside, the first true frost of the season silvered the windows, and overhead, through the glass, the stars burned in their courses, steady and numerous and utterly unremarkable, which was the finest thing about them.

"The winter ledger opens tomorrow," Tamsin said, when the talk wound down. "First entry of the new year. I'll want witnesses."

"Same as every year," Brannic rumbled.

"Same as every year." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "Except. There's a line item I've been sitting on. Filed under pending." She tapped the drawer where the king's apology slept. "An envoy arrives from the capital within the fortnight. The crown wants to renegotiate the covenant's terms—formally, publicly, with the signatories present. And there's a second matter." She paused, and let the old taster's stillness settle over her face, the one that made kings lean forward. "The envoy's letter was sealed with wax I've tasted before. Vey's personal blend. Someone in the palace is still using a dead usurper's seal—which means either they're sentimental, or they're continuing his work."

The fire popped. Yew's grin spread slow and sharp as a smuggler's knife. Brannic cracked his knuckles. Wren's eyes went distant and archival.

Tamsin picked up her spoon, tasted the last of the cider—clear, warm, safe for the people she loved—and smiled.

"Well," she said. "Let's find out which."
