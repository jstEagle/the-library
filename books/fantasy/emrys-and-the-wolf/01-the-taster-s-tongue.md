## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The pheasant was clean. Sable knew it before her tongue touched the meat — knew it the way a fisherman knows weather, in the small animal part of her that had been trained, over three years, to taste death in anything. But she took the spoon anyway, lifted the gilded bite from the king's serving dish, and let it sit on her tongue while the whole kitchen held its breath.

Nothing. Bitter from the juniper glaze, rich with butter gone slightly brown, and nothing else. No copper edge, no numbing bloom at the back of the throat, no sweetness that shouldn't be there. She let the silence stretch a moment longer than necessary — a habit the cooks hated and the steward paid for — then swallowed and nodded once.

"Send it up."

The kitchen exhaled. Cooks moved again, the clatter of copper and the hiss of the roasting fire resumed, and Sable Okafor stepped back from the serving table into her corner, the corner that was hers the way a shadow belongs to a wall. Royal food-taster. Third year in the post. The pay was obscene, the hours were worse, and the work was simple: put the king's death in your mouth before he could, and survive it.

She had survived it eleven times. Not that anyone counted but her.

* * *

The trick of it — the real trick, the one no one in Vellamere would ever believe — was that she'd only nearly died once.

That had been her first week, a bowl of plum soup laced with something the poisoner's guild called widow's grace, and she'd felt it take hold like a hand closing around her heart. She remembered the floor rushing up, the steward shouting, the strange detached calm of a woman watching her own death arrive on schedule. And then she remembered waking on the kitchen bench an hour later, weak as a wet cat, with the cook's apprentice staring at her like she'd come back from somewhere with no road home.

The palace physician called it a robust constitution. The steward called it the gods' mercy, and then crossed himself twice, because in Vellamere the gods were a legal matter, not a spiritual one. Sable called it what it was: she was built wrong. Born with a body that treated poison as a rude guest rather than a murderer. Her mother had been the same — a hedge-witch in the southern fens who could drink bog-water and brew tinctures from flowers that killed everyone else's children. Her mother had also burned for it, in the square at Marsh Hollow, when Sable was nine, and Sable had learned the second family trade that day, the one her mother never got to teach her properly:

*Be quiet about what you are.*

So she was quiet. Three years quiet. She tasted, she nodded, she lived in her narrow room above the scullery and took her supper alone, and once a week she walked to the shrine of Saint Vessa — the only saint the Inquisition had never gotten around to burning, mostly because Vessa had been a taster too — and she lit a candle and she kept her head down.

And once a week, on her way back, she did the thing that would get her killed.

* * *

It started small, the way all her mother's magic had. A knot tied a certain way in a scullery boy's apron string, to keep his hands from scalding. A pinch of salt and ash rubbed into the seam of the laundress's shawl, against the fevers that came up from the river in autumn. A word — not spoken, never spoken, just *shaped* behind her teeth and pressed into a copper farthing before she slipped it into a pocket — a word against bad luck, against the small daily deaths of people who worked in a palace where people were poisoned as a matter of course.

Small charms. Hedge magic. The kind of thing that hadn't *worked* in a hundred years, not since the old gods went silent and the Inquisition burned the last of the cunning folk and the whole country agreed, out of terror, that magic was dead. That was the joke Sable told herself in the dark, the only joke she owned: she tied knots and whispered to copper and nothing happened, and that was the safest thing in her life. Her charms were prayers to nobody. They were how she grieved her mother without a grave to visit.

Except.

Except that last month, the knots had held. The scullery boy — Tam, eleven years old, all elbows and burns — had grabbed a pot handle fresh off the fire last Tuesday and felt nothing, and laughed about it, and Sable had stood in the doorway with her heart going like a trapped bird. The laundress, Marta, who'd had the river-fever three autumns running, hadn't coughed once since the ash-and-salt. And the copper farthings — Sable had started checking, in the way you check a wound you're afraid of — and they were *warm*. All of them. Every coin she'd pressed a word into sat in someone's pocket warm as a held hand, and no one noticed, because no one in Vellamere had touched a working charm in a hundred years and wouldn't know one if it bit them.

Sable noticed. Sable lay awake at night doing arithmetic she didn't like. Either she was getting stronger — and she wasn't, she was the same hedge-witch's daughter she'd always been, small magics and a poisoner's tongue — or something on the other side of the silence had started listening.

The gods had been silent for a century. The Inquisition said they were dead, and burned anyone who said otherwise. But Sable's mother had told her once, in the fen-dark, with the reeds whispering: *Gods don't die, little wren. They just stop answering. And the day they start again, you run.*

Tonight, Sable was going to run. Just a little. Just the usual route, just the usual pockets.

* * *

The evening round began after the king's table was served and the kitchen settled into its post-service lull, when the undercooks went out to smoke in the yard and the scullery sank into steam and quiet. Sable moved through it like water through cracks, unhurried, unremarkable. She'd learned long ago that the secret to doing forbidden things in a palace was to look like you were doing tedious things. No one watched a woman checking a storeroom ledger. No one watched her at all, really. Tasters were furniture that could die.

Tam first. He was scrubbing the great cauldron, sleeves pushed up, forearms pink from the water, humming something tuneless. Sable drifted past the shelf above him, straightened a stack of bowls she had no actual interest in, and let a copper farthing drop — a soft, bright *tick* — into the pocket of his apron where it hung on its hook. The coin had been in her fist all evening, warm, the word pressed into it like a seal into wax: *against burns, against burns, against burns.* Tam didn't look up. The cauldron steamed. The coin sat in the cloth like a seed in soil.

Marta next, in the laundry passage, folding the king's table linens with the quick economy of a woman who'd been doing it for thirty years. Sable paused to complain about the cold — a real complaint; the passage was always cold — and while Marta laughed and agreed, Sable's hand brushed the shawl over the drying line and left the salt-and-ash where it belonged, worked deep into the weave with a thumb that looked like it was smoothing wrinkles.

Then the others, the quick ones. A knot in the door-boy's lace. A breath against the night-guard's cold tea, a word for steady hands and a clear head, because the night guard drank his tea on the walls where a man could fall. Small things. A hundred years of silence, and Sable Okafor moved through the palace kitchen like a woman scattering seeds on ground she'd been told was dead, and every seed she'd planted that month had *sprouted*, and she could not stop planting, because the alternative was to let these people be unprotected, and she had watched her mother burn rather than stop.

She was at the scullery door, pulling her cloak on for the walk home, when it happened.

* * *

Later — for the rest of her life, probably — she would try to remember exactly which charm it was. She never managed it. There were four coins in her cloak pocket, all pressed with words that evening: one for Tam's mother, who swept the hall and had trouble breathing; one for the turnspit girl with the bad tooth; one for the old porter whose joints seized in the damp; and one she'd pressed that afternoon without quite knowing who it was for, a word for *keeping*, for *safe passage*, for whoever needed it next. She'd meant to find it an owner on the walk home. She always found them owners. That was the discipline of it — nothing left loose, nothing left glowing, nothing left to find.

She stepped out of the scullery door into the blue cold of the evening, and the coin in her pocket went off like a struck match.

Not warm. Not the gentle hearth-warmth she'd been telling herself was imagination. It *flared* — light through wool, through her cloak, through her fingers where they'd gone instinctively to smother it — a gold-white bloom that lit the scullery doorway like a lantern held up to cloth. Sable made a sound she'd deny later, a small choked thing, and shoved her hand into the pocket and closed her fist around the coin, and the light poured out between her fingers anyway, steady, patient, *glad*, like something on the other end had been waiting a hundred years to be answered and was not going to be quiet about it.

*Put it out,* she thought, with the particular clarity of terror. *Put it out, put it out, put it out.*

She couldn't. She'd never been able to put them out; she'd only ever been able to make them, and they'd only ever been embers. This was not an ember. This was a coin with a sun inside it, and it was shining through her fingers in the open doorway of the royal kitchens, and behind her the scullery was full of people, and beyond the yard wall the night watch walked their rounds, and somewhere in the white tower above the city the Inquisition kept its cells and its irons and its list of names.

Sable turned her back to the yard, hunched over her fist like a woman praying, and waited for the light to die.

It took eleven breaths. She counted. Eleven breaths of gold leaking through her fingers, eleven breaths in which anyone could have looked, anyone could have seen, and then — gently, almost apologetically, the way a guest takes leave — the light folded itself down and out, and the coin in her fist was just a coin, warm as a held hand, and the evening was dark again, and Sable Okafor stood in the doorway of the royal kitchens shaking like a leaf in a gale.

*They're listening,* she thought. Her mother's voice, or her own; by now she couldn't tell them apart. *They're listening, and they're answering, and you just lit a beacon in the one city in the world where that gets you burned.*

She made herself walk. Not run — running drew eyes — but walk, fast, through the yard, past the turnspit girl who said something she didn't hear, out the postern gate into the streets of Vellamere, where the lamps were being lit and the smell of woodsmoke and river-mud lay over everything like a blanket. Her hand stayed in her pocket, wrapped around four warm coins, and she did not take it out until she reached her room above the scullery and barred the door and sat down on the narrow bed in the dark.

*Run,* her mother had said. *The day they start answering, you run.*

Sable looked at the barred door. At the window with its view of the palace wall, and above the wall, the white tower of the Inquisition, its windows dark, its list of names growing by the season. She had a post here. She had a wage. She had Tam and Marta and a hundred small protected lives, and nowhere else to be, and no way to run that wouldn't look like a confession.

And besides — a colder, cleverer voice, the voice that had kept her alive three years — *it was one flare. In a doorway. For eleven breaths. No one saw. No one ever sees.*

She was almost — almost — beginning to believe it when the knocking started.

Three blows on her door, measured and unhurried, from a hand that had never once been kept waiting. Sable went cold from the soles up. Nobody knocked on her door. Nobody knew where her door was, except the steward, who was asleep by now, and the scullery, which never came up the stairs.

She rose without a sound. Through the gap under the door: lamplight, and the shadow of boots, and the long shadow of something else — a staff, or a sword, or a censer on a chain, swaying gently, patiently, like a pendulum counting down.

"Sable Okafor." A man's voice, smooth as poured wax, and underneath the smoothness something that made her skin crawl, the same animal part of her that tasted poison, tasting *this*. "Royal Inquisition. We'd like a word about your evening walk."

* * *

She stood in the dark of her room with four warm coins in her pocket and a barred door between her and the fire, and did the only arithmetic that had ever mattered: *how long until they break it down, and what do I do with the seconds?*

The coins were warm. Warmer than they should be. Warmer than they'd ever been — and as she stood there, palm pressed flat against them through the wool, she could have sworn she felt something on the far side of all that silence turn over in its sleep, and *pay attention*.

The knocking came again. Three blows. Patient as winter.

Sable Okafor, royal food-taster, hedge-witch's daughter, keeper of small and impossible magics, reached for the window latch — and stopped, because far out over the rooftops of Vellamere, past the palace wall, past the white tower, something was *moving*. Something low and fast and wrong for a man, wrong for a dog, wrong for anything the city had a name for, flowing along the ridgelines like smoke given purpose.

Coming this way.

The knocking came a third time, and splinters began to give.
