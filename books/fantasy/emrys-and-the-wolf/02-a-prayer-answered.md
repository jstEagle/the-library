## 2. A Prayer Answered

The prayer woke him like a knife between the ribs.

Emrys had been sleeping in the bell tower of a drowned church — one of the old ones, sunk to its shoulders when the river was dammed and the gods were outlawed, its bells green with a century of silence. He slept badly in those years, the way a dog sleeps whose master has gone away and not come back. He dreamed of running. He always dreamed of running.

Then: *O Nethis of the slow water, keeper of the crossing, hear me.*

Not shouted. Whispered. The kind of prayer that doesn't believe in itself, the kind people say the way they touch iron out of habit — because their grandmothers did, because there is nothing else left to do. Emrys had heard ten thousand of them over a hundred years and felt nothing stir in the bond behind his breastbone but cold ash. The gods did not answer. The gods could not answer. Whatever the Inquisition's fires had done to them, whatever the crown on the king's head drank from them night after night, it had left only husks, and husks do not speak.

But this time the ash *moved*.

He came off the stone floor with his heart slamming, human hands braced against the wall, and felt it again — a thread of warmth drawn through him, thin as spider silk, going somewhere. Going *down*. Toward the river quarter, where the water ran slow and brown under the tannery bridges.

*O Nethis.* A woman's voice, cracked with sleeplessness. *They say you're dead. They say you never were. But my daughter is burning up and I have no coin for the physiker and no one left to ask, so I'm asking anyway. Forgive me. Forgive me for asking.*

And beneath her whisper, so faint he almost missed it, something answered. Not words. Not yet. A god waking is not like a man waking; it is like ice going out on a river in spring — a groan, a shift, a long-held breath released all at once somewhere far upstream. Emrys felt his master turn over in a sleep a hundred years deep, and felt the bond between them pull taut for the first time since he'd been a boy kneeling in the reeds, swearing himself to service.

Nethis was awake.

Gods below. Nethis was *awake*.

* * *

He went over the rooftops because the streets belonged to the Inquisition after dark, and because the wolf in him had never learned to walk slowly anywhere. Vellamere at night was a city of slate and chimney smoke, of watch-lamps burning white at every corner — white fire, clean fire, the fire they said kept witchcraft out. It didn't. It just made the shadows honest about where they stood. Emrys flowed along the ridgelines half-shifted, longer than a man, faster than any dog, his bare feet finding purchase where no feet should, and the whole time the thread of answered prayer pulled at him like a fisherman's line.

It led down past the tanneries, past the bridge where beggars slept under oilcloth, to a room above a chandler's shop where a woman knelt beside a small bed and a small girl burned with fever.

Emrys crouched on the roof beam outside the window and watched, and what he saw made the hair rise along his spine.

The mother had no magic. He could smell that much — she was plain as bread, worn to the bone, a chandler's widow by the wax burns on her fingers. But she had dipped her hand in the water jug and laid it on her daughter's brow, and she had whispered the old words her grandmother must have taught her, the crossing-words, the asking-words, and the fever *broke*. Not eased. Broke. Sweat came up on the child's skin like rain on hot stone, and the girl sighed and turned over and slept, truly slept, and the mother put both hands over her mouth and shook and did not dare to weep loudly because hope, in Vellamere, was also contraband.

And the water in the jug — Emrys saw it clearly, even in the dark, even from above — the water had moved. One slow spiral, turning against everything water does, the way a river eddies around a hidden stone.

*Thank you,* the woman whispered. *Oh — thank you. I won't tell anyone. I swear I won't—*

She stopped. Because the candle flame had leaned toward the window. All the candles in the room had leaned toward the window, the way flames lean toward a draft, except there was no draft, and Emrys understood with a lurch that the leaning was not toward the window at all. It was toward *him*. Toward the bond. Toward the wolf-shaped vessel through which a hundred-year-drowned god was looking at the first answered prayer of his long silence.

The mother looked up too. Mortals always feel it, even the ones who can't name what they feel — the pressure of being seen by something older than seeing. Her eyes went around the room, found nothing, and settled on the window, and Emrys should have gone. Should have melted back into the dark and let her keep her miracle unexamined, because examined miracles get people burned.

Instead he pressed one palm flat against the glass, inside the shadow of the eave, and let a little of Nethis's attention flow through him into the room. Blessing, or warning. Even he wasn't sure which.

The child would live. That much he knew. And the mother would keep her secret for exactly as long as it took someone else to notice a fever breaking like that, in this city, in this year.

Which meant Emrys had work to do, and very little time to do it.

* * *

The problem — and he turned it over and over as he ran the rooftops back toward the heart of the city — was that Nethis hadn't answered the prayer himself. Couldn't have. A god newly woken from a century of drowning has strength for perhaps one gesture, one eddy in a water jug, and even that had cost something Emrys could feel as an ache along the bond, like a muscle torn. No. The prayer had been *carried*. Something had taken the woman's whisper and delivered it whole to a god who could not otherwise have heard it, the way a herald carries a message to a king too ill to leave his bed.

Something with a talent for carrying.

Small magics, then. Charms. Hedge-work, kitchen-witchery, the little domestic craft that the Inquisition called superstition when it suited them and sorcery when it didn't. Someone in Vellamere was still making charms that worked — really worked, working well enough to relay prayers across a hundred years of silence — and had been doing it quietly enough that Emrys, who was supposed to be the gods' eyes in this city, had missed them entirely.

That shamed him more than he wanted to admit. He had spent three years telling himself that quiet was survival, that the bond's dimness was mercy, that a sleeping god needed no enforcer. He had let himself become a relic haunting a drowned church. And all the while some hedge-witch had been keeping the last embers alive without knowing what they fed.

The thread of the answered prayer still hung in the air for him, faint now, fraying. He followed it back the way it had come — not from the chandler's shop, but from elsewhere. From uphill. From the palace hill, where the watch-lamps burned brightest and the walls stood forty feet and the Inquisition kept its white tower full of white fire.

His stomach dropped.

The thread ended at the palace kitchens.

Of course it did. Of course. Where else would a charm-maker hide but among the hundred servants who moved unseen through the greatest house in the city? Kitchens were the old country of magic — herbs and salt and fire and the threshold-lore of doors. Every hedge-witch's granddaughter in history had ended up stirring somebody's pot. And who would think to look for a worker of forbidden craft among the food-tasters and scullions of the king himself?

Someone would. Someone always would. The Inquisition paid informants in silver and absolution, and a palace kitchen held a hundred pairs of eyes and a hundred grudges. If there was a charm-worker in Vellamere — if there was the person who had woken Nethis, the person the gods themselves might need — then that person was living on borrowed time measured in days, maybe hours.

Emrys paused on the ridge of the mint-master's roof, the palace looming black against the stars, and felt the bond shift inside him. Nethis was awake, barely, and awake was not the same as safe. A god surfacing after a hundred years was a whale rising through ice — and the thing that had bound the gods in the first place still sat on its throne up there, wearing a dead man's face, drinking whatever it drank from the deep places of the world. Emrys didn't know what the crown-fed thing on the throne would do when it felt its prisoners stirring. He suspected he didn't want to know.

But he knew what the Inquisition would do. The Inquisition was simple. The Inquisition would burn whoever it found, and ask the ashes no questions.

Change was coming to Vellamere. He could taste it on the wind along with the coal smoke and the river mud — change like weather before a storm, pressure behind the ears. The gods stirring meant hunters moving. Hunters moving meant blood, soon, and choices, sooner.

He dropped from the roofline into the shadow of the palace wall, and went looking for a servant who smelled of river-water and small impossible magics.

He found the scent within the hour — faint, threaded through the kitchen yard, caught in the straw by the delivery gate. Herb-smoke and beeswax and underneath it something bright and strange he had no name for, the smell of a charm freshly made, still warm.

And under that, sharper, closer, wrong: white fire. Watch-fire. The Inquisition was already at the wall.

* * *

By the time Emrys reached the servants' wing, moving low along the garden shadows, the knock had already begun.

He heard it from thirty yards out — three blows, patient as winter, the kind of knocking that men make when they are certain the door will open eventually whether anyone wishes it or not. White lamplight pooled on the flagstones below the kitchen windows. Two figures stood at a door at the end of the wing, and one of them wore the grey coat and iron collar of the Holy Office of Inquiry, and the other carried a lantern that burned with that clean, hungry, watching flame.

Inside, a single candle moved — Emrys saw its light shift behind the shuttered window, someone rising, someone crossing the room. Someone deciding, with whatever seconds remained, whether to run, lie, or open the door.

The wolf in him wanted to go through the wall and be done with it. The enforcer in him knew better: kill an inquisitor tonight and the whole city would be alight with hunts by dawn, and whatever charm-maker lay behind that door would burn twice as certain. There were other ways. There were always other ways, slower and uglier.

The knocking came a third time. Wood splintered.

Emrys bared his teeth in the dark, coiled, and waited to see which way the soul behind that door would jump.
