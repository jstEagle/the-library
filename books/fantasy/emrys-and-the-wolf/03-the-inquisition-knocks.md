## 3. The Inquisition Knocks

They came at the hour of the morning bread, when the ovens were roaring and every soul in the royal kitchens was flour-dusted and half-deaf from the clatter of pans. That was smart, I'd give them that. You can't run in a kitchen. Every exit funnels through one door, and that door was suddenly blocked by six men in grey and a woman in black with a face like a closed ledger.

Inquisitor Hale.

I knew her by reputation before I ever saw her up close. Three years in Vellamere, and everyone who kept secrets knew Hale's name the way sailors know a storm's. She ran the palace interrogations personally when the reports were interesting enough, and apparently *glowing charms in the scullery* had been interesting enough.

"Line up," she said. Not loudly. She didn't need to be loud. The ovens seemed to quiet themselves for her, which should have been my first warning about what she really was, but I was too busy counting exits I already knew didn't exist.

Sixteen of us stood against the wall while her men went through our things. They found Marla's lucky rabbit bone. They found Cook's second knife — technically contraband, nothing more. And they found, in the pocket of Tam's spare apron, the little woven knot of thread I'd given him three days ago. A fever-charm. His sister had been sick; I'd made it without thinking, the way you hand someone bread.

Tam's face went white as the flour on his hands.

"That's nothing," he said. "My gran made it. It's just thread."

Hale took it between two fingers like something dead. "Just thread," she repeated. "And does just thread glow?"

"It doesn't glow."

"It glowed." A scullery maid named Bess had reported it — I learned that later, pieced it together from the way Hale's eyes flicked to her, standing apart from us, hugging herself and not meeting anyone's gaze. "Tuesday night. Bright enough to wake a sleeping girl. Bright enough to be seen through a doorway." Hale turned the charm over. "Bright enough that someone in this room made it, or gave it, and is lying to me right now."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind me, water dripped into a basin. I did the math I always did: sixteen people, one liar, and if she worked down the line methodically she'd reach me last, because food-tasters stand apart. We're furniture with tongues. Nobody suspects the furniture.

She started at the left end of the line anyway.

I watched her work. That's the thing about Hale they don't put in the stories — she wasn't cruel, not exactly. Cruelty is a passion, and there was no passion in her. She asked each person the same questions in the same flat voice: Where were you Tuesday night? Have you handled charms? Do you know anyone who keeps the old ways? And each answer she weighed like a coin bitten for gold, and most of them came back false metal, and she moved on.

Then she reached me.

"Sable Okafor." She said my name like she was reading an inventory list. "Royal taster. Three years' service. No family in the city. No church attendance." Her eyes came up from the ledger she carried. Grey eyes. The color of ash after the fire's out. "That's unusual. Most people attend *something*, even if it's only to be seen."

"I attend my duties."

"Everyone believes in something, taster. What do you believe in?"

"The king's supper," I said. "If it kills me, I'll reconsider."

Someone down the line snorted before they could stop themselves. Hale didn't smile, but something at the corner of her mouth acknowledged the shape of a joke, the way a banker acknowledges a forged note — recognizing the form without honoring it.

"Where were you Tuesday night?"

"Asleep. In the taster's quarters."

"Were you alone?"

"Always."

"Have you ever handled a charm, amulet, talisman, or any object associated with the forbidden practices?"

"No."

It came out smooth. Three years of tasting other people's poison will teach you what your own lies taste like, and mine tasted of nothing at all. That's the trick nobody teaches you: don't make the lie interesting. Make it boring. Make it the kind of answer she'd heard forty times already today. *No. Asleep. Alone.* Let her hunger for something worth writing down.

Hale studied me for a long moment. Long enough that I felt sweat start at my hairline despite the ovens' heat. Then she wrote something in her ledger — I couldn't see what — and closed it.

"You have a steady tongue, taster. Steady tongues are useful. They're also suspicious." She handed Tam's fever-charm to one of her men. "Bag it. Everything gets tested." And then, to all of us: "You'll be questioned again. Individually. Some of you will lie better than others. One of you won't need to lie at all, because one of you is telling the truth, and truth has a smell I never forget."

She turned to go. Then stopped. Turned back.

"One more thing." From her purse she took a copper coin and held it up so we could all see it. Ordinary. Worn. The king's face rubbed nearly smooth. "A gift for the kitchens. For luck." She set it on the shelf by the door, face-up, and looked at each of us in turn. "Anyone may take it. Anyone who takes it will be blessed."

Nobody moved. Sixteen people stared at that coin like it was a snake.

"Take it," Hale said pleasantly, "or don't. But the coin stays until someone does."

She left. Her men left. The ovens roared back up to full voice, and the kitchen exhaled, and everyone went back to work the way people do when terror passes through and leaves the routine standing — scrubbing harder, chopping faster, not looking at each other.

But I looked at the coin.

I'm a taster. Reading objects is my trade. And even across the room, even before I got close, I could feel it the way you feel a draft from a hidden door: wrongness, deliberate and patient. It wasn't a trap in the sense of springs and needles. It was worse. It was a *question*. Take me, it said, and answer honestly whether your hands have ever touched magic, and the coin will taste your answer the way I taste the king's soup. Lie to it and it will know. Tell it the truth and—

Well. Telling the Inquisition the truth was its own death, slower but just as certain.

All day the coin sat on the shelf. All day the kitchen arranged itself around it, the way water arranges itself around a stone. Cook sent the newest boy to fetch salt rather than let anyone walk past it. By evening shift-change, it hadn't moved, and I knew it wouldn't, and I knew what that meant: Hale would come back, find it untouched, and conclude either that her trap had failed or that the guilty party was clever enough to leave it. Clever enough meant *worth watching*. Worth watching meant my name was already in her ledger, written twice.

And there was a third possibility, the one that sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone: that the coin wasn't only a test of honesty. That it was a beacon. That somewhere in the palace, a grey-eyed woman was watching it through some art of her own, waiting to see whose footsteps slowed near it, whose eyes lingered, whose breathing changed.

I couldn't stay. That much was suddenly, brutally clear. If they tested everything they'd bagged — Tam's charm, Marla's bone, whatever else they'd shaken loose — the thread would lead back to me eventually. Charms have makers. Makers have hands. Hands have names in ledgers.

So I did what I'd done once before, three years ago, on the worst night of my life: I waited for the deep hours, packed nothing that could be traced to me, and walked out of the only home I had left with my heart hammering and my face perfectly calm.

* * *

The palace at night is a different animal. Daytime Vellamere runs on rules — guards at intervals, bells marking the hours, everyone in their place. Nighttime Vellamere runs on luck, and I've never trusted luck, which is why I'd spent three years learning its streets the way a pickpocket learns pockets.

I made it further than I expected. Through the servants' gate, past the sleeping guardhouse, down into the warren of lanes behind the fishmongers' row where the smell covers everything and even the Inquisition's informants don't linger. I was two streets from the river gate. I was going to make it. I remember thinking that, actually thinking *I'm going to make it*, which is precisely when the night ended.

"There's a watcher on the bridge," said a voice above me. "You'd have walked straight into him."

I didn't scream. Screaming brings watchers. I did what any sensible poisoner does: I went for the knife in my sleeve and turned to find where the voice was coming from.

He dropped from the roofline without a sound. That was the first impossible thing. Men don't fall four stories silently; men don't land crouched and rise unhurried. He was tall, dark-haired, dressed in rough travel clothes that had been chosen to be forgotten, and his eyes caught the lamplight and threw it back amber, the way a wolf's do.

The second impossible thing was that I recognized him. Not his face — his *stillness*. The same stillness I'd felt in the temple ruins as a child, the feeling of being watched by something old and patient that had decided, for now, not to eat you.

"You've been following me," I said. "Since Tuesday."

"Since the charm flared." He tilted his head. "You wove a fever-knot for a boy named Tam. River-thread, ash-braid, spoken over with words nobody uses anymore. And it *worked*. Do you understand what that means? Nothing works in this city. Nothing has worked for a hundred years. And then a kitchen girl's stitchwork lights up like a signal fire—"

"I'm not a kitchen girl. I'm the royal taster."

"Even better." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "You handle the king's food. You're inside the walls. And you make charms that work, in a city where making charms gets you burned." He spread his hands, palms out — showing me they were empty, though I noticed he did it slowly, deliberately, the way you calm a frightened animal. Or the way you show a fellow predator you're not hunting. "I'm not here to drag you anywhere. I'm here because the gods woke up on Tuesday night, and they woke up pointing at *you*."

"The gods are dead," I said. "Everyone knows the gods are dead. That's the whole point of this city."

"The gods are *bound*." The word came out of him with weight, with heat, like something he'd been carrying a long way. "There's a difference, and the difference is why I exist. My name is Emrys. I serve what sleeps under the temples. And three nights ago, for the first time in a century, it dreamed — and the dream was a small brown woman in a palace kitchen, weaving protection into a sick child's thread, praying to no one, asking no one, and being answered anyway."

The knife was still in my hand. I realized I hadn't lowered it. I also realized my hand wasn't shaking, and that surprised me more than anything else he'd said.

"If I go with you," I said, "and this is a trap — if you're hers, if those amber eyes are just another kind of grey —"

"They're not."

"— then I die in a cellar somewhere. And if I stay, the coin on the shelf tastes my name out of the air by week's end, and I die in a square, publicly, with a crowd." I finally let the knife drop to my side. "Those are my options. Wonderful. I survived three years by being careful, and in one week I've become the most interesting person in Vellamere."

"Yes," Emrys said simply. "You have."

"And you're offering me what, exactly? Protection?"

"I'm offering you what I have." He said it without flourish, without romance, and somehow that was what made me believe him. "Ruins under the city that the Inquisition forgot. Fading power from gods too weak to do more than keep me alive. And company. Which, if you've been doing what I think you've been doing — small kindnesses, small magics, alone, for years —" his voice gentled, and I hated how much I wanted it to be gentle, "— you haven't had in a very long time."

The lane was silent. The fish smell hung thick. Somewhere far off, a bell marked the hour, and I thought about the coin sitting on its shelf in the empty kitchen, patient as a spider, and about Hale's ash-grey eyes writing my name in her ledger a second time.

Every instinct I owned said *run*. Not with him — away from both of them, away from the whole burning mess, find a cart heading south and be nobody again.

But I'd spent three years being nobody, and I knew exactly what it cost.

"Show me these ruins," I said.

Emrys smiled then — fully, this time, and his teeth caught the light, and I would remember later that I noticed.

We went down.
