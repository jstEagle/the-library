## 4. Wolf at the Threshold

The dark under Vellamere was older than the city above it.

Sable had known that in the abstract, the way she knew the palace cellars held wine older than the king's grandfather. Knowing it with her boots was different. The temple ruins beneath the Fountain District had been buried whole — columns still standing, a floor of cracked mosaic under her feet, and somewhere ahead, water moving through channels cut by hands three centuries dead. The air tasted of river-stone and old incense and, faintly, of wolf.

She kept one hand on the wall and let Emrys lead. He moved through the black like it was a room he'd grown up in, which, she supposed, he had.

"You can see," she said. Not a question.

"Down here? Always." His voice came back to her low, unhurried. "It isn't sight, exactly. It's more that the dark knows me."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." A pause, and then something that might have been a smile in it. "You're walking well for someone who fled her own kitchen an hour ago."

"I didn't flee. I left strategically."

"Through a window."

"Strategically."

He laughed — a short, rough sound, like it had been out of use for years — and Sable found herself absurdly pleased at having caused it. She filed that away as a problem for later.

They descended another flight of worn steps, and then the space opened around them, and even blind, Sable felt it: vastness. Cold air rising off unseen water. When Emrys struck flint to a lamp hanging from a rusted bracket, light bloomed across a ruined nave — fallen pillars, an altar split down the middle, and walls covered floor to ceiling in carved figures.

Gods. Dozens of them. Faces worn smooth by time or by deliberate chisels, but their shapes remained: a woman pouring from an endless jug, a man with a river coiling from his mouth, a hound the size of a house, a crowned figure whose face alone had been scraped utterly blank.

Sable stopped walking.

"I know this one," she said quietly, touching the river-god's cheek. Her charm-sense prickled against the stone — faint, faded, like warmth lingering in a cup hours after the tea was gone. "This is Ondru. My grandmother prayed to him. I thought—" She stopped. "I thought he was a story."

"He is a story," Emrys said. "Stories are what gods wear when nobody's looking."

* * *

He built a fire in a hearth that had clearly been built for much larger fires, and they ate hard bread and smoked fish from a cache he kept beneath a loose flagstone. Sable realized, watching him portion the food with practiced economy, that he had lived here a long time. Alone.

"How long?" she asked.

He didn't pretend to misunderstand. "Eleven years."

"Alone?"

"The rats are poor company." He fed a scrap of fish into the fire and watched it hiss. "There were others once. Enforcers, like me. Bound to the other gods. When the gods went quiet, we..." He turned his hand over, studying the palm. "We didn't die. We just stopped mattering. Some walked into the sea. Some took service with the Inquisition, hunting what they used to serve. I stayed."

"Why?"

"Because someone has to be here when they wake up." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that made it worse. "Someone has to open the door."

Sable pulled her knees up to her chest. The firelight made the carved gods flicker on the walls, shadows swimming across their worn faces. "Tell me what you are. All of it. If I'm hiding in your hole in the world, I want to know what dug it."

So he told her.

Not a werewolf — the word made him grimace — not a man cursed, not a beast wearing skin. A mortal man, born ordinary in a village that no longer existed, chosen at nineteen by a god who needed hands in the world while the god slept. Bound by oath and by something older than oath. The wolf-shape was the binding made visible, the god's power running through mortal flesh and reshaping it when called upon.

"And the price?" Sable asked, because there was always a price. She was a poisoner's daughter; she knew every gift came with its antidote.

Emrys was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"The binding runs on their power. Theirs is fading. Every year there's less of them in me, and more of just... me. Aging faster. Healing slower." He flexed his fingers. "When they're gone entirely, so am I. Not dead. Just — unwritten. Like I never happened at all."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the deep channels, water moved with patient indifference.

"That's why you tracked my charm," she said. "A flare of real magic after a hundred years. You thought—"

"I hoped." He looked at her across the fire, and his eyes did something strange in the light — caught it and gave it back gold. "One answered prayer means the sleep is ending. It means maybe I get to finish what I was bound for instead of fading out mid-sentence." A pause. "It also means the Inquisition will feel it too. Which brings us to you."

"To me?"

"Hale marked you tonight. I watched him do it."

Sable's stomach dropped. "The coin."

"The coin. It's a tether-spell. Cheap, ugly work, but effective. By tomorrow noon he'll know exactly where you are." Emrys rose, crossed to her, and crouched — close enough that she could see the scar through his eyebrow, the exhaustion under the gold eyes. "Give me your hand."

Every instinct she owned screamed about strangers and hands and trust. She gave him her hand anyway.

He turned it over. On her palm, faint as a watermark, sat the shape of Hale's sigil — a flame inside a circle. Emrys pressed his thumb to it and spoke four words in a language that made the fire lean toward him. The mark flared white-hot, then cold, then simply — gone.

"He'll think the tether broke," Sable said slowly. "He'll think I'm dead."

"He'll think something ate you. Which, conveniently, is what I am." He released her hand — carefully, the way you set down something breakable — and returned to his side of the fire. "Which raises the obvious question. Your charms shouldn't work, Sable Okafor. Small magics died with the gods. So either you've been lying to the Inquisition for three years about being nothing..."

He let it hang.

Sable stared into the fire. Three years of careful silence. Three years of being small and useful and invisible. And now she was sitting in a buried temple with a dying wolf-man, already marked, already hunted, already burned.

"My grandmother taught me," she said finally. "Before the ban reached our village. Knots and words and pocket-charms. She said the gods weren't gone, just sleeping, and that small magics were like breathing on embers — enough to keep them alive till morning." Her throat tightened. "I've been breathing on embers for fifteen years. Tonight one of them caught."

"Because they're waking." Emrys leaned forward, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "And when they wake fully, they'll need people like you. People who kept the faith alive in pockets and kitchens while the priests were burning." His voice dropped. "The question is whether you'll answer when they call."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I put you on a cart south at dawn and you disappear forever, and I never speak your name." No threat in it. Just fact. "I don't make soldiers. I open doors. Walking through is yours to choose."

Sable looked at him for a long moment — this man who had waited eleven years in the dark for gods to wake, aging out of his own story, asking nothing except that she choose freely.

"You've been alone down here a long time," she said.

"Eleven years."

"That wasn't a question either." She unfolded, moved around the fire, and sat beside him — close, not touching. "I've been alone for longer, just in a crowd. At least yours has wolves in it."

Something shifted in his expression — surprise, then wariness, then a careful, guarded warmth, like a man watching weather change over open water. "You should sleep," he said gruffly. "There's a pallet behind the altar. It's clean."

"Is that where you sleep?"

"Wolves don't need much sleep."

"But you're not only a wolf."

"No," he agreed quietly. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

She woke once in the night, in the dark behind the split altar, to the sound of movement. For one heart-stopping moment she thought it was Hale's men come down through the earth itself — and then she saw him, at the far end of the nave, standing before the blank-faced carved king.

He wasn't praying. He was talking. Low, urgent, the way you talk to someone who hasn't answered in eleven years, and the cadence of it was so nakedly lonely that Sable lay very still and pretended to sleep, and hated herself a little for listening.

"—if she's the one, then tell me," he was saying. "You owe me that much. Eleven years, and you owe me that much—"

The water in the channels surged, sudden and loud, and went still again.

Emrys stood motionless for a long moment. Then, softly, in the tone of a man who has just been answered for the first time in a century:

"...Oh. Oh, no."

Sable closed her eyes and breathed slow, and did not ask in the morning, because some doors only open once — and because far above them, in the palace, bells began to ring.
