## 5. What Wears the Crown

The gods woke Emrys before dawn, and they did not wake gently.

He came out of sleep like a man dragged from deep water — heart slamming, breath ragged, the taste of ash in his mouth. The temple ruins beneath Vellamere were dark and cold around him, the broken columns furred with moss, and for one disoriented moment he thought he was dreaming still. Then he felt it: the pull behind his sternum, that old leash of fading power, going taut as a bowstring.

*Come,* said the river-god, whose name had been Ossa when men still spoke it. *See.*

Emrys had served the sleeping gods for eleven years. He knew their voices the way a man knows the creak of his own house — the low groan of Ossa, the dry whisper of the hearth-goddess Brannic, the wordless pressure of the others, half-dissolved into their own forgetting. He had never heard them *urgent*. Urgency required strength, and strength was the one thing they no longer had.

He rose, dressed in the dark, and knelt at the cracked altar where the last of their essence pooled like candle wax gone cold.

"Show me," he said aloud, because the gods liked words, even now. Even starving. Words were what they lived on, once. "Show me what woke you."

The vision took him the way drowning takes a man.

* * *

He stood in a throne room he knew — the Hall of Ashen Kings, all white marble and banners gone grey with dust — but wrong. The light was wrong. It came from the crown on the throne's occupant, a sick amber glow that moved like something breathing.

King Aldric sat upon the Ironwood Throne, and Emrys's first thought, absurdly, was that the king looked *better* than he had three winters ago, when Emrys had seen him last from a rooftop during the solstice procession. Healthier. Fuller in the face. A man restored.

Then Aldric turned his head, and Emrys saw that the eyes were lamps.

Not lamp-lit. Not reflecting light. *Lamps* — twin points of amber flame burning behind the pupils, steady and patient and utterly without anything human behind them. And the skin — Emrys looked closer, the way a wolf looks closer, hunting the flaw in the herd — the skin was too smooth. Stretched over the skull like wet vellum over a drum. No pores. No lines. The mouth smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near the eyes because there was nothing in there to reach.

"He is dead," Emrys said, and his voice came out small in the vast hall. "Aldric is dead."

*Three winters past,* said Ossa, and the god's voice shook him like water shaking a reed. *Fever. Quiet. The healers were paid to say otherwise, and the ones who could not be paid were buried. What you see is what climbed into the vacancy.*

"What *is* it?"

*A godling. Nameless. Younger than your grandmother's grandmother and twice as hungry. When the old gods slept, when we let ourselves fade rather than feed on the faith of burnings and chains — it did not fade. It found another meal.* The vision shifted, tilted, and Emrys was suddenly looking down at the city from a height no man could stand at, seeing through the walls, seeing the veins of the palace lit faint gold beneath the stone. *Dragon-fire. The last true magic in the world. It drinks nightly from the creature in the menagerie, and with that fire it holds us bound — chains woven of our own stolen substance — and keeps its stolen shape from rotting off its bones.*

Emrys watched, helpless as a ghost, as the thing that wore Aldric descended from the throne and walked — glided, almost; the feet touched the marble a half-beat late — down into the palace depths. It passed guards who bowed and saw nothing wrong. It passed servants who curtsied and felt, perhaps, a small cold draft, nothing more. Down, down, past the wine cellars, past the old dungeons, into a vaulted chamber where the air itself shimmered with heat-haze.

And there, chained in silver and starved to a husk of her former glory, lay the dragon.

She was grey as old ash. Her scales, which should have burned copper and bronze in the torchlight, had dulled to the color of grave-dirt. Tubes of pale glass ran from the hollow of her throat to a basin set into the floor, and in the basin fire gathered — real fire, living fire, the oldest fire — and drained away through channels cut in the stone, upward, toward the crown.

Every night. For three years. While the city above prayed to a king who no longer existed, and every prayer — every whispered blessing, every desperate plea for rain or health or mercy — slid down those same channels and fed the thing that sat wearing a dead man's face.

*That is why we woke,* Ossa said. *Not because we grew strong. Because she grew careless. Three nights ago, a taster's charm flared bright enough to crack the seal for a heartbeat. One heartbeat. Enough for a mother's prayer to reach me through it.* A pause, and in the pause Emrys felt the god's terrible, fragile hope. *One heartbeat, wolf. That is how thin the wall has grown.*

* * *

The vision released him and Emrys came back to himself on the temple floor, on his hands and knees, sweat-soaked despite the cellar cold.

Sable was awake. Of course she was — she'd heard him hit the ground, or felt the surge of power the way she seemed to feel everything now, her charms running hot and restless since the night they'd flared. She crouched at the edge of the altar chamber, wrapped in a blanket, watching him with those level brown eyes that missed nothing.

"You saw something," she said. Not a question.

"I saw the king." He pushed himself upright. His hands were still trembling. "He's been dead three years."

Her face didn't change — she was a woman who'd survived Vellamere by not letting her face change — but he saw her go very still, the stillness of prey scenting smoke. "Dead," she repeated. "Then who—"

"A godling. Wearing him." He told her the rest of it quickly, flatly, the way you'd deliver a field report: the dragon, the siphons, the prayers funneling downward like grain into a millstone. "Every prayer anyone's said since Aldric died has fed it. Every candle lit 'for the king's health.' Every blessing. It's been eating our faith and drinking the last dragon-fire, and using both to keep the old gods chained and its own face from sliding off."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. In the silence, somewhere far above them, the city bells rang the hour, and Emrys thought of thousands of people waking to a ordinary day, praying ordinary prayers to a corpse.

"The charms," Sable said finally. "Mine started working. Hers—" she stopped, corrected herself. "The mother's prayer reached your god. Through my charm?"

"Through the crack it made. You kicked a hole in a wall that's held for a hundred years, taster." He meant it lightly. It didn't come out lightly. "You have no idea what you are."

"I have *some* idea," she said, and there was an edge under it now, the edge he'd glimpsed the night he caught her fleeing the palace. "I've spent three years being nothing, Emrys. Being careful. Being small. If I'm the reason the wall cracked, then I want to know what else I can break."

Something in his chest turned over. He ignored it. There would be time for that particular treachery later, if there was a later.

"There's more," he said. "The gods showed me where the dragon is. Under the palace. She's dying, Sable. They're draining her dry, and when she's empty—" he heard Ossa's voice again, the terrible arithmetic of it, "—the godling will need a new source. And there isn't one. There's only her. When she gutters out, it starts feeding on us directly. On the gods. On anyone who carries a spark."

"And everyone who prays."

"Everyone who prays."

Sable looked up at the ceiling, at the weight of stone and city and enthroned hunger above them, and Emrys watched her do the same arithmetic he had done, and watched her arrive at the same answer.

"The menagerie," she said. "People talk in kitchens. There are rumors about the royal beasts — something old kept apart, something the king visits alone at night." Her jaw tightened. "*The king* visits. It goes to her. It feeds on her personally."

"It does."

"Then I need to get into that menagerie."

"You need to get *out* of this city," Emrys said, and heard his own voice roughen. "You kicked a hole in the wall. Hale marked you. The Inquisition is circling, and now you're proposing to walk into the one place in Vellamere where the godling keeps its heart."

"And you're proposing I run." She stood, dropping the blanket, and in the altar's faint light she looked — not fierce, exactly. Settled. Like a scale finding its balance. "Emrys. I spent three years running by staying still. It worked until it didn't. If that dragon dies, everything dies with her — the gods, the magic, whatever I am. Whatever *you* are." She stepped closer. "You didn't drag me out of the palace to save me. You dragged me out because your gods told you I mattered. So tell me I'm wrong."

He couldn't. That was the rotten truth of it. Eleven years of service, and the gods had never once sent him after someone the way they'd sent him after her — drawn down the rooftops like a hound on a line, straight to the kitchen door where she'd slipped out into the dark. They hadn't said *save her*. They'd said *follow*, and he was beginning to understand that the distinction was going to matter enormously.

"You're not wrong," he said. "You're worse than wrong. You're right in a way that's going to get us both killed."

"Good." Almost a smile. "Then we understand each other."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, because Sable insisted — *a taster plans like a poisoner, everything measured, nothing wasted* — and by dusk Emrys stood on the rooftops above the Palace Quarter, watching the amber glow of the crown-light flicker in the high windows of the king's private chapel, where the thing that wore Aldric said evening prayers to itself.

Below, in the walled gardens, the royal menagerie sprawled dark and silent. And beneath it, he knew, the last dragon lay chained in the dark, giving her fire away drop by drop to keep a dead man smiling.

Ossa stirred in him, faint as a guttering wick. *Wolf. When you go down there — and you will go down there, I know you — remember what she is. Remember what fire does to liars and thieves and things that wear borrowed shapes.*

"And what does it do to me?" Emrys asked silently.

The god's answer was long in coming, and when it came it was soft as snowfall:

*It does what it does. But you were never borrowed, little wolf. You were given.*

Emrys crouched at the roof's edge, the city smoking below him, and made his peace with the shape of what was coming. Tomorrow night the palace kitchens received their winter stores — carts, crowds, chaos. A taster could slip inside a delivery. A wolf could slip inside anything.

He just had to keep her alive long enough to reach the dragon.

And pray — to gods who were listening again, for the first time in a hundred years, and who might yet be too weak to do anything about it — that what waited beneath the menagerie wasn't already expecting them.

Because the godling had worn a king's face for three years without a single soul catching the lie.

It would not forgive the ones who finally did.
