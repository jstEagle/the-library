## 6. The Last Dragon-Fire

The rumor came to me the way all good rumors come to a food-taster: through the mouth of someone who didn't know they were telling me anything at all.

I was skimming cream off the morning's delivery — a taster's privilege, since everything had to pass my tongue anyway — when two scullery boys started whispering by the coal scuttle. I have ears tuned for whispers. Three years in Vellamere teaches you that the dangerous words are never the loud ones.

"—saw it, I swear on my mother. Out past the aviary, where they keep the old pens. A wing, Mart. Big as a sail."

"Dragons are dead."

"This one's dying, then. That's what Cook's cousin said. They carry fire down to it every night and it won't eat anymore."

They scattered when the under-steward barked at them, but the words stayed with me like grease on the fingers. *Fire carried down to it every night.* Every night. To a dragon that wouldn't eat.

I thought of the crown. Of the way King Aldric's eyes had caught the torchlight at the winter feast — not reflecting it, but *drinking* it, the flame bending toward his face like water finding a drain. I'd told myself it was a trick of the light. Tasters are very good at telling themselves things are tricks of the light.

That night I did something I hadn't done since I came to Vellamere. I broke into a part of the palace I wasn't supposed to know existed.

* * *

The old menagerie sat below the aviary like a bruise beneath painted skin — a ring of crumbling pens from the days when kings kept living wonders instead of tax ledgers. The new menagerie, all gilt cages and peacocks, was three terraces up. Nobody came down here. The guards' route bent around it, their lanterns swinging past without a glance, which meant either the place was empty or it was *very* full of something nobody wanted to look at.

I went over the wall in the dark between patrol-lamps, charms humming warm against my skin — too warm, still, ever since the gods had started answering. My little protections burned like coals now instead of embers. I'd stopped lighting them at all except when I needed them, because needing them was becoming a habit, and habits get people hanged.

The last pen was at the bottom of the ring, sunk half into the hillside, its iron door scarred with claw-marks gone brown with age. Heat breathed out of the gaps in the stone. Not forge-heat. Something older, slower, like standing near a banked hearth that had been burning since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to fear it.

I should have walked away. Every instinct I'd honed in three years of survival said walk away, taste nothing you can't spit out, touch nothing that glows.

I put my hand on the door.

It swung open at my touch. Not forced — *opened*, the hinges groaning like something waking from long sleep, and I understood that whatever waited inside had decided, just then, to let me in. That should have terrified me more than it did. Instead I felt the strangest thing: recognized. Like a lock meeting its key.

She was smaller than the rumors promised and vaster than the pen could hold, folded into herself like a letter no one would read. Grey scales gone dull as old pewter, wings tucked tight against ribs that rose and fell in slow, labored bellows. Her eyes were closed. Around her neck, sunk into the flesh beneath her jaw, was a collar of black metal that drank the light — and from the collar, a channel of pale gold ran along the floor and up through the wall, thin as a vein, pulsing faintly.

Fire. Siphoned fire, flowing out of her night after night, up through the bones of the palace, to wherever kings keep their hunger.

Her eye opened. Amber, banked, ancient. It fixed on me and I felt myself weighed — truly weighed, down to the marrow, the way I'd once seen Inquisitor Hale weigh a suspected poisoner before deciding whether he was worth the rack.

"You're the one," she said. Her voice was ash settling in a cold grate. "The one who tastes."

"And you're the reason the king doesn't eat," I said, because apparently I'd lost my survival instincts entirely.

Something moved in her throat that might have been laughter. "I don't feed him. He feeds *on* me. There is a difference, little taster, and it is the whole difference between hospitality and theft." The amber eye narrowed. "You knew that already. You tasted it, didn't you? At his table. The wrongness."

Every meal I'd ever taken in that hall rearranged itself in my memory. The faint sweetness under the roast. The way the wine left a residue like burnt sugar. The way I always, always felt slightly hollow afterward, as if something had skimmed a coin off the top of me along with the poison-risk. I'd blamed the kitchens. I'd blamed my own nerves.

"He's been tasting *us*," I said. "All of us."

"Every soul in Vellamere eats at his table now, whether they sit there or not." Her breath rattled. "But you — you he cannot drink. Your blood is wrong for him. Poisoned against him, you might say." The eye glittered. "Or blessed. Depends who's telling the story."

I crouched down slowly, the way you approach any wounded thing, and this close I could see the collar's work: the scales around her jaw worn thin as parchment, the gold vein pulsing in time with her heartbeat. Slowing. Guttering.

"How long?" I asked.

"Since the gods went quiet, I have paid him every night. Before that, I paid him rarely — coronations, oaths, the great bindings. Fire for vows. That was the old bargain, freely given." Her lip lifted from teeth the length of my forearm. "Then Aldric died, and the thing that wears him decided the bargain should be nightly. And permanent. And mine alone, until there is nothing left of me but the collar and a smell of smoke."

"The gods went quiet when—"

"When the binding tightened. Yes. You're quick. That's why I let you in." She shifted, and the whole pen groaned, and I saw how much the movement cost her. "Now. You've come down here following rumors, which means you've already decided to be foolish. So be foolish properly. Give me your hand."

"Everything I know about dragons says that's a terrible idea."

"Everything you know about dragons comes from songs written by men who were afraid of us. Give me your hand, taster. I'm not going to burn you. I couldn't if I wanted to — he's taken nearly all of it." A pause, and something almost gentle moved through the ash of her voice. "But what's left will know you. Fire knows its own."

I gave her my hand.

* * *

What came off her tongue was barely a spark — a bead of heat no bigger than a pearl, pressed into my palm like a secret passed between thieves.

It didn't hurt.

I want you to understand what that means. I have spent my life immune to poisons — belladonna, arsenic, widow's-wine, the whole apothecary's litany — and I have spent that same life hiding it, because an immunity that strange is only one step from sorcery, and sorcery in Vellamere is one step from the pyre. But fire is not poison. Fire kills everyone. Fire is the great democracy of death.

The spark sank into my palm and lit me up like a lamp behind the skin, and I stood there in the dark of the pen, glowing faintly gold at the seams, and laughed — actually laughed — because it felt like coming home to a house I'd never known I owned.

"What am I?" I asked her.

"Mine," she said simply. "A fire-ward. The old families used to breed them — women whose blood could hold flame the way a cup holds water. Kings kept them close. Kings trusted them with oaths, because an oath spoken over fire can't lie, and a fire-ward standing witness makes certain of it." Her eye half-closed. "There haven't been many of you since the ban. Most burned in the first purges. The rest hid so well they forgot themselves. Not you, though. You hid so well you became a *taster*. Do you see the joke? The city burns witches, and its own king has been drinking from a witch every day for three years without knowing it."

My hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the specific vertigo of a locked door in yourself swinging open after years of pretending the room behind it wasn't there. All those small charms that shouldn't have worked in a godless city. The immunity. The way animals calmed around me and flames leaned toward me in hearths. I'd called it luck. I'd called it constitution.

It was inheritance.

"What do you need from me?" I asked.

"Company, mostly. And visits. Steal down here when you can — every hour you spend near me, the spark roots deeper, and rooted fire can't be siphoned back. He'll feel it eventually. The crown notices everything that resists it." She settled her head onto her forepaws, wearily, like an old dog. "And when the solstice comes, and he renews his oath over my fire — remember what I told you about oaths. An oath spoken over fire can't lie. Which means it also can't survive being fed a lie."

"I don't understand yet."

"You will. You have a poisoner's mind, taster. One night you'll stand in that hall with the flame in front of you, and you'll understand exactly which ingredient kills." Her eye closed. "Now go. And come back tomorrow. Bring honey-cakes if you can manage it. A hundred years of palace cooking and I miss honey-cakes *desperately*."

* * *

I went back the next night. And the next.

I learned her name — Ysolde, older than the city, older than the crown, though she claimed she'd been young and stupid once and preferred not to discuss it. I brought honey-cakes wrapped in napkins, and cut hay for bedding, and once, recklessly, a bottle of the good brandy from the steward's cabinet, which she pronounced "adequate" and finished in one swallow.

I learned the rhythm of the siphoning — midnight, when the palace slept, the gold vein brightening as the crown drew its due, Ysolde's breathing going shallow and her eyes dimming till dawn. I learned to sit with her through it, my hand flat against her flank, feeding what warmth I could back down the bond the spark had made between us. Small rebellions. A taster's magic: tiny doses, administered daily.

And I learned what I was. Not in words — in the body's own plain language. By the fourth visit I could hold a handful of her ember-sparks without flinching. By the seventh, I could breathe in her exhaled smoke and feel it settle in my chest like a second heart. The hiding was over. Not because anyone knew — but because I knew, and there is a particular exhaustion in pretending you don't exist that lifts all at once, like fever breaking.

On the ninth night, Emrys found me.

I heard him on the rooftops first — I always heard him now, some new sense waking in me along with everything else — and by the time he dropped silently into the pen's shadows, I wasn't even startled. Just tired, and oddly relieved.

"You followed me," I said.

"The gods sent me." He crouched at the edge of the firelight, wolf-eyed, wary. "They said their sister was waking. I assumed they meant one of theirs." His gaze went from Ysolde to me, to the faint gold shimmer along my forearms where the sparks had rooted, and I watched him arrive at the truth the way you watch a man step off a ledge. "Sable. What are you?"

I looked at my hands, lit from within like paper held before a flame. Three years of silence, of smallness, of tasting other people's deaths so I could keep my own. I was done with it.

"I'm the king's worst ingredient," I said. "And I think I finally know how to use me."

Behind us, deep in the palace above, something stirred — a vast, hungry attention turning over in its sleep, feeling along the gold vein for the leak in its hoard.

Ysolde opened one amber eye and rumbled, low, like a landslide deciding:

"They've noticed us, children. Whatever you're going to do — do it soon."
