## 7. Betrayal in the Kitchens

The kitchens were wrong.

Sable felt it before she saw it — the particular hush of a room that had been searched. The night cooks worked in silence, and Cook Marta wouldn't meet her eyes, and someone had moved the copper pots. They hung in perfect descending order now, gleaming, the way soldiers' boots gleamed after inspection.

Hale had been here.

She kept her face smooth and her hands busy, lifting the tasting spoon, sipping the king's evening broth with the practiced boredom of three years' survival. Salt. Leek. A whisper of saffron that hadn't been there yesterday — a new cook showing off, nothing more. No poison. No magic. She set the spoon down and signed the ledger with a steady hand while her heart counted the exits.

"Okafor."

His voice came from the doorway to the scullery, and every hair on her arms stood up beneath her sleeves. Inquisitor Hale stepped into the lamplight with his coat brushed and his boots clean, as if he'd never once knelt in blood. Behind him, two of his gray-cloaked wardens filled the door like furniture that happened to breathe.

"Inquisitor." She inclined her head exactly as far as courtesy required. "You've caught me at my work."

"So I see." He picked up the ledger, read her signature upside down, and smiled without warmth. "You have a remarkable constitution, Mistress Okafor. Three years tasting for this household and not so much as a stomach complaint. Do you know what I say about remarkable constitutions?"

"That they're worth their weight in gold?"

"I say they're built on something." He set the ledger down and produced, from his breast pocket, a small object wrapped in linen. He unwrapped it with the care of a man handling a relic: a coin, dull silver, scored across its face with a sigil she knew too well — the trap-coin he'd left behind after his last visit, the one meant to catch whatever touched it. It was scorched black along one edge.

"It was in the pocket of a washerwoman's apron," Hale said. "A charm against chilblains, if you can believe it. Cheap thread, cheap knotwork. And when our trap-coin touched it—" he turned the coin so the light caught the burn, "—it flared like a festival lantern. Enough light to wake half the laundry."

Sable tasted the broth again in her memory. Salt. Leek. Saffron. *Steady.*

"The washerwoman swears she found it in her pocket one morning," Hale went on. "A gift from no one. But gifts come from somewhere, don't they? And pockets in this palace are washed by the same dozen hands." He let the coin lie on the table between them, blackened sigil up, like a dead thing on a plate. "I've been very patient, Okafor. I've watched you sign ledgers and sip soup and smile at serving girls. And every week, another little miracle turns up in this building. A fever broken overnight. A rope that frays just before a fall. Bread that doesn't mold. Small things. Kind things. *Witch's* things."

"They're called good luck," Sable said. "Kitchens run on it."

"Kitchens run on fear and salt," Hale said. "Luck is what people call the residue of sin." He leaned closer, and up close his eyes were pale and patient as a fish's. "Here is what happens now. You will come with me tonight, quietly, or you will come with me loudly. Loudly involves fire. I'm told fire is interesting for people like you."

*People like you.* As if she were a species. As if she hadn't spent three years being nobody, invisible, safe.

Her eyes flicked — she couldn't help it — toward the scullery door, the yard beyond, the wall she'd climbed a hundred nights ago on her way back from the menagerie. Hale saw the flick. Of course he did. He was built of flicks.

"You've been leaving the palace at night," he said softly. "Through the drain-grate by the ash midden. Where do you go, Sable Okafor?"

"Sleepless," she said. "I walk."

"Alone?"

"The city's full of people."

"Not where you go." He straightened, buttoned his coat with two precise movements, and delivered the rest like a man laying out cutlery. "Here is my bargain, since you're fond of bargains. There is a wolf-thing moving through my city. My wardens have found its tracks on the temple roofs. Whatever woke the old charms — whatever made your little trinkets flare — it walks with that creature, or it *is* that creature. Give me its den and I will give you a quiet cell instead of a pyre. A year's penance. Maybe less, if you're useful. You'll taste soup until your hands shake with age, and no one will ever call you remarkable again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow at dawn we burn a witch in Coronation Square," Hale said. "She'll be a laundress. Middle-aged. Bad teeth. We'll say she confessed to the charms, and she'll die screaming your sins, and you'll stand in the crowd and watch, and then I'll come back here and ask again. I can do this every dawn for a month, Okafor. I have enough laundresses."

The lamp flame bent in a draft. Somewhere below, the kitchen bells marked the hour, and Sable heard her own pulse under them, fast and traitorous.

He would do it. That was the worst of it — he wasn't bluffing, wasn't cruel for pleasure; he was cruel the way a scale is balanced, weighing souls against order and finding the arithmetic satisfying. She could give him nothing and watch an innocent woman burn for her knots and charms. Or she could give him something false and pray Emrys stayed away from it.

"There's a place," she heard herself say. Her voice came out flat, dead, a taster's voice reading off ingredients. "Under the old granary on Candlewick Row. The cellar. There's a door in the floor behind the grain bins. He keeps things there. Charms. Bones. If you go at moonset, you'll find him."

Hale studied her for a long moment. Then he wrapped the scorched coin back in its linen, tucked it away, and smiled his fish-smile.

"Moonset," he said. "Thank you, Mistress Okafor. Enjoy your soup."

The wardens peeled away from the door and followed him out, and the kitchens breathed again, and Sable stood over the king's untouched broth with her hands perfectly steady and everything underneath them shaking.

* * *

Emrys smelled the lie on her before she spoke a word of it.

He'd been waiting on the menagerie roof, folded into the shadow of the gargoyles, when she came down the drain-wall with her shoulders wrong — too square, too controlled, the posture of a woman carrying something heavy and pretending it was empty. He dropped to the cobbles beside her and she didn't even startle, which told him how far inside herself she'd gone.

"Hale was in the kitchens," she said.

"I know. I watched him leave."

"He gave me a choice." She stared straight ahead at the dark bulk of the palace. "Betray you or burn a laundress. Every dawn, a new one, until I talk."

Emrys said nothing. The gods were quiet in him tonight — not silent, but watchful, the way wolves watch a treeline. He waited.

"I gave him the granary on Candlewick Row," she said. "It's nothing. An empty cellar. Moonset. By the time he digs through it, we'll be gone from Vellamere entirely, both of us, and the dragon—"

"No."

The word came out of him low and final, and she finally turned to look at him, and her eyes were wet and furious all at once.

"No?" she repeated. "You don't get to say no. I just sold my soul to buy us a night, and you don't get to—"

"You gave him a place," Emrys said, "and he'll bring twenty wardens to an empty cellar, and when it's empty he'll know you lied, and then he won't bargain anymore. He'll take the laundress anyway. He'll take ten laundresses. Men like Hale don't accept lies; they metabolize them." He stepped closer. "And worse — he'll know the wolf cares what happens to you. He'll stop hunting the den and start hunting *you*, because you're the leash that pulls me."

"Then what would you have me do?" Her voice cracked on it. "Let her burn?"

"No," Emrys said. "But not with a lie. Lies spend fast. We spend truth." He held her gaze. "You tell me where the exchange truly is, and I go there myself, and I give Hale his wolf. While he's busy with me, the dragon gets free, the binding cracks, and none of it matters anymore. One night. That's all I need you to buy."

"No." Now it was her turn to say it, and she said it like a door slamming. "Absolutely not. You are not walking into that cellar to be taken apart by gray cloaks so I can—"

"To save the last dragon-fire in the world," he said. "Yes. That is exactly what I'm going to do. It's what I'm *for*, Sable. I was bound to serve the gods, and the gods need the fire free, and you—" His voice caught, roughened. "You are the only person in this city who can carry it out. Don't make my death useless by wasting the night grieving."

"Don't you dare plan your death around my grief," she whispered, "as if I'd allow it."

For a moment neither of them moved. The palace loomed above them, windows dark except one — the high tower where the crown slept on its hollow king, drinking its nightly fire. Emrys looked at her mouth, at the fury in it, and wanted things he had no right to want, and put them away where he put everything.

"Moonset," he said quietly. "Candlewick Row. Go home, Sable. Pack nothing. Be ready to run."

"You're not listening—"

"I'm always listening." And he was gone between the shadows before she could build another argument, because if he'd stayed one breath longer he might have promised her something he couldn't keep.

* * *

The granary cellar was cold and smelled of rat droppings and old grain dust, and Emrys stood in the middle of it with his arms loose at his sides and let them come.

They came at moonset exactly, twenty wardens and Hale, flooding down the stairs with lanterns and iron chains wound in prayer-script. Emrys shifted as the first of them reached the bottom — let the wolf rise through him, huge and silver-gray, eyes catching the lantern-light like coins at the bottom of a well. He heard the wardens' courage stutter. Good. Fear bought seconds.

He bought them thirty.

He went through them like weather — not to kill, though he broke what got close, but to hold the doorway, to keep every chain and every warden fixed on *him*. Hale stood at the top of the stairs shouting orders, and Emrys saw the moment the inquisitor understood: the wolf had come to a lie on purpose. The wolf was spending itself.

"Forget the beast!" Hale's voice cracked across the cellar. "It's a diversion! Move — move to the palace — the menagerie — *now!*"

Half the wardens peeled away, boots hammering up the stairs, and Emrys lunged after them and took a prayer-chain across the muzzle for it, iron biting through fur into thought, white fire of script searing his mind. He went down under six wardens and chains and the weight of his own failing strength, and the last thing he saw was Hale's face above him, pale and triumphant and *disappointed*, like a man who has caught the thief but missed the treasure.

"Where is she?" Hale demanded, kneeling in the dust, gripping the wolf's jaw. "Where is the dragon, beast?"

Emrys showed him all his teeth.

* * *

Sable felt it happen from half a mile away.

She was in the menagerie tunnel, hand pressed to the warm stone, whispering to the old dragon in the dark — telling her, again, that tonight was the night, that the chains were ready, that the crown would starve — when the bond between them shuddered. Not the dragon's fear. Something else. A coldness, sudden and total, like a door closing in a house she hadn't known she lived in.

The dragon raised her great graying head, nostrils flaring at the distant wind, and made a sound Sable had never heard from her: a low, mourning rumble, aimed south, toward Candlewick Row.

"No," Sable said. "No, no — he wouldn't—"

But she knew. She knew the way she knew poison on her tongue: instantly, totally, past argument. He had gone to the cellar. He had given himself to buy her the night, and the night was already spent, because Hale had seen through it in the first five minutes and sent his wardens *here*.

Above her, faint through the menagerie's iron grate, came the ring of boots on gravel. Many boots. Coming fast.

The dragon's fire lit the tunnel red.

Sable pressed her palm flat against ancient scales, heart slamming, guilt and fury rising in her throat like bile — *he lied to me, I lied to him, and we've both paid in the only coin that mattered* — and made the only choice left that was hers.

"Then we do it now," she said to the dragon, to the dark, to the wolf bleeding somewhere in a cellar across the city. "Tonight. Everything. Hold nothing back."

The grate above them began to bend.
