## 8. The Godling's Bargain

They took her crown-ward through corridors she had walked a thousand times, and every one of them was wrong.

Sable had spent three years learning the palace the way a taster learns a dish — by its undertones. The way the east wing smelled of beeswax and old smoke. The way the guards on the night stair never looked at your face, only your hands. She had catalogued it all, filed it away in the part of her mind that kept her alive.

Now the corridors were too clean. No dust in the sconce-corners. No cook's grease ghosting the air from the kitchens below. And the guards — there were more of them than there should be, standing at intervals so perfect they might have been placed by a ruler with a straightedge for a soul. None of them blinked. She counted seven before they reached the antechamber and stopped counting, because counting was a way of being afraid, and she could not afford to be afraid yet.

*Later,* she told herself. *Be afraid later, when it's useful.*

The antechamber doors opened without a hand touching them.

She had seen King Aldric perhaps forty times in three years. Feast days, executions, the annual tasting of the coronation anniversary wine — always at a distance, always flanked, always smiling that broad, easy smile that Vellamere loved. The man on the throne at the end of the hall wore that same face. It was a good face. It was, she thought with a lurch of professional admiration, an excellent forgery.

Because it wasn't moving right.

A human face has micro-hesitations. A blink a half-beat late. A smile that arrives in pieces — mouth first, then eyes. This face smiled all at once, like a curtain drawn back, and held, and held, and the eyes behind it were lit from somewhere underneath, a faint amber glow like coals seen through a grate.

Dragon-fire. Of course. He was wearing it even now.

"Sable Okafor." His voice filled the hall without effort, the way water fills a cup. "Royal food-taster. Three years of service. Not one poisoning survived your tongue." A pause, precisely calibrated to seem human. "You have been very useful."

"I live to serve, Majesty." She sank into the curtsy she'd performed ten thousand times, head bowed, and used the cover of it to breathe. To think. Emrys was in a cell beneath the Inquisition tower — she'd heard the chains when they dragged him past her door, heard him *let* them take him, because if he'd fought he'd have shown them what he was, and what he was would have burned half the escort and confirmed every witch-story Hale had ever told. He'd bought her time with his body. She would not waste it by dying stupidly in this room.

"Look at me," the king-thing said.

She looked.

Up close, the amber glow in his eyes resolved into something worse: movement. Slow turning shapes, like embers stirred by a bellows. Like a fire being *fed*.

"You are wondering why you are here." He rose from the throne — smoothly, too smoothly, joints that didn't quite negotiate the motion — and descended the three steps. "You are wondering about the wolf. You are wondering whether you will burn."

"The thought crossed my mind, Majesty."

"It should not." He stopped an arm's length away. This close, she could smell him, and the smell was the worst thing yet: hot stone and scorched feathers and under it, faintly, sweetly, the smell of a feast left too long in a locked room. "I do not destroy useful things, Sable Okafor. I collect them."

He extended one hand. On the palm lay a ring — heavy gold, set with a stone the color of banked coals. She knew it. Every servant knew it. The king's signet, worn at every public appearance, kissed by ambassadors.

"Wear this," he said, "and everything you have lost is returned. Your position. Your rooms above the scullery. Your name, cleared of every whisper Inquisitor Hale has written about you. The wolf lives — comfortably, indefinitely, as long as you remain useful. And in exchange—"

"In exchange I give you whatever it is you think I have."

"Not think." The smile again, curtain-smooth. "Know. You knelt in a dragon's pen eleven times this season. You put your hand into fire that should have taken it to the wrist. You are immune to flame, taster. Do you understand how rare that is? How *precious*?" His voice softened into something almost tender, which was somehow worse than the hunger. "There is exactly one other creature in Vellamere with that gift, and she is old, and tired, and nearly empty. When she gutters out, I need a new wick."

*A new wick.* The words went through her colder than any blade. Eleven visits. Eleven stolen evenings scratching the old dragon's jaw-scales while she poured out every secret she'd sworn to keep buried — and the thing on the throne had watched the whole time. Had probably watched since the first charm flared in the kitchens.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take it." He said it without malice, the way a man says *the tide comes in*. "Flame-immunity is not a talent, taster. It is a substance. It can be rendered. The rendering is unpleasant and wasteful — most of the gift burns off in the extraction — but I am patient, and I am hungry, and you are not the only vessel in this city. Merely the best one." He tilted his head. "And the wolf would still die. Slower, perhaps. I would want you to watch."

*There it is.* Not a bargain at all. A leash with two collars. She let her breath shake — let it, deliberately, because a frightened woman was what he expected, and what he expected was a weapon she could aim.

"I need time," she whispered.

"You have until solstice." Four days. "Wear the ring tonight. Sleep on it. If it is gone from your hand by morning, I will know your answer, and I will begin with the wolf."

He slid the ring onto her finger himself. His touch was warm — no. *Hot.* Fever-hot, kiln-hot, and beneath the heat she felt something probing, delicate and greedy, like a tongue testing a wound. Testing her blood. Learning its flavor.

*Taster,* she thought, *meet taster.*

* * *

They gave her back her old room above the scullery, which told her how completely she'd been catalogued. Two guards outside the door who did not blink. A tray of food she did not touch, though her stomach screamed — three years of habit died hard, and tonight the habit might be the only thing keeping her alive.

She sat on the narrow bed with the ring on her finger and made herself study it.

Not the gold. The gold was just a setting. She studied the stone — coal-colored, banked-fire colored — and the way it sat against her skin. Warm. Always warm. And when she turned it in the candlelight, she saw what she should have seen the moment it touched her hand: fine lines inside the stone, hair-thin, branching like frost or like veins. Like the pattern of a burn scar.

Like the pattern, she realized, of the oath-circle floor in the coronation hall. She'd mopped around it once, years ago, a punishment detail. A great ring of inlaid stone, runes worn nearly smooth, surrounding the dais where the crown was renewed each solstice.

*Spoken over dragon-fire.* That was what the old texts said, the ones the Inquisition hadn't burned. Every king of Vellamere swore the coronation oath kneeling in that circle, over a flame kindled from the royal dragon's fire. A century ago, when the gods went silent, everyone said the oath was just tradition. Just words.

Just words spoken over the last true magic in the world, year after year after year, each renewal feeding the circle, and the circle feeding—

Him. Whatever he was. Bound into the crown by an oath that had curdled from covenant into cage, and the cage needed stoking, and dragon-fire was the only fuel that burned long enough and pure enough. The old dragon wasn't a pet in a menagerie. She was a lamp. And Sable was being offered a position as the replacement oil.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that with distant surprise. Somewhere between the throne room and this bed, the fear had finished burning down to coals, and what was left was the thing that had kept her alive through three years of poisonings: the cold, clear, almost joyful certainty of a taster who has finally identified the ingredient.

Because here was what three years at the tasting table taught you, the lesson no one survives to learn twice: *anything can be poisoned.* Wine. Soup. A king's favorite dish. The trick is never in the poison. The trick is knowing the dish — knowing what it must contain, what it cannot tolerate, what single drop turns nourishment into ruin.

The godling had told her everything. He couldn't help it; he was proud of his recipe. The binding lived in the oath. The oath lived in the circle. The circle was fed at solstice, publicly, ritually, with the whole court watching — with *dragon-fire*, the one substance in creation her body refused to burn.

They wanted her as a wick.

Fine. A wick stood in the middle of the flame.

She looked down at the ring, at the vein-fine lines glowing faintly against her skin, and smiled the way she smiled at a dish she already knew how to kill.

On solstice night, they would kneel her in that circle. They would speak the oath over the sacred flame. They would expect a frightened girl in a borrowed gown, wearing their ring, ready to pour herself into the crown.

Instead they would get a taster with four days to work, a wolf to break out of an Inquisition cell, and an old dragon owed eleven evenings of kindness — and a very specific understanding, gained at the source, of exactly which ingredient kills a god.

Sable drew the blanket over her shoulders, closed her eyes, and began, silently and methodically, to plan the menu.

Outside the door, the unblinking guards did not hear her whisper:

"Let's see how you like the taste."
