## 9. Unbinding

**Sable**

They dressed her in white.

Of course they did. The godling had a taste for theater — she'd learned that much in four days of watching it through the eyes of a frightened girl in a borrowed gown. White for purity, white for surrender, white so the blood would show, if there was blood. There would be. She'd made sure of that herself, in the small hours, with a lancet stolen from the infirmary and a vial stoppered with wax from the chapel candles.

The gown was silk, and beneath its hem, stitched into the lining where no inquisitor's pat-down would think to look, sat eleven small charms. Eleven evenings of kindness, returned. The dragon's fire lived in them now, banked and patient as coals under ash.

"You'll kneel here," Inquisitor Hale said, walking her across the oath-circle like a bride down an aisle. The great hall blazed with solstice light — a thousand candles, courtiers in their finery, and at the far end, on the throne, the thing wearing Aldric's face smiling its patient smile. "When the flame is brought to you, you will speak the renewal. You will mean it."

"I always mean what I say," Sable said. "It's a professional habit."

Hale's eyes narrowed. He hadn't searched her properly — she'd counted on that, on the godling's hunger making it careless, wanting her whole and willing rather than broken and suspecting. A poisoned meal served to a guest who knows it's poisoned does not nourish.

She knelt. The stone was cold through the silk. Above her, the crown caught the candlelight and gave back something darker than light, a slow pulse like a heartbeat seen through skin.

*Four days,* she thought. *A wolf in a cell. A dragon owed a debt. And one very specific ingredient.*

The doors opened. Two acolytes entered bearing the sacred flame in a bronze brazier, and Sable felt it before she saw it — the pull, the tug behind her ribs, the crown reaching for the last dragon-fire in Vellamere the way a starving man reaches for bread.

She let it reach.

And while it drank, she opened the wax-sealed vial against her palm, and began, silently, to season the flame.

* * *

**Emrys**

The cell had been built to hold men. It was not built to hold what came up through the floor.

He'd felt the dragon before he heard it — a tremor in the stone, old and vast and furious, eleven nights of kindness remembered in every beat of its wings. Then the wall simply stopped being a wall. Dust, torchlight, and a claw the size of a plowshare, and beyond it the night sky of solstice eve, full of falling sparks.

"Eleven evenings," the dragon said, its voice like a landslide learning language. "You are the wolf who kept her company in the dark. Get on."

He got on.

They flew low over Vellamere, over rooftops where people were stepping out of their doors to stare upward, over the temple district where Emrys could feel his masters stirring like sleepers turning toward a window. The gods were waking. He could feel them all along his bones, the fading power that had sustained him for a century suddenly running hot and strange, and beneath it something else — the crown's song, the binding, fraying at one single point.

The oath-circle. The hall. Her.

"Hold," he told the dragon, as the palace rose up ahead, candles blazing in every window like a beast showing its teeth. "I go in alone. You wait for my signal — or for the roof to come off. Whichever comes first."

The dragon laughed, a sound like rockslides. "Wolves. Always alone until the moment they aren't."

* * *

**Sable**

The acolytes set the brazier before her. The flame leaned toward her hungrily, and she let it lean, let the crown drink deep, and into that current of drinking she fed her own blood — drop by drop, from the cupped vial, into the heart of the fire.

Blood-magic was the first magic. Every child knew the rhyme. What no one had known — what only a taster, kneeling at the source of the godling's meals for four days, could have learned — was this: the binding lived in the coronation oath, spoken over dragon-fire, and an oath is a promise, and a promise can be *poisoned*. Not destroyed. Poisoned. Made to turn on the one who swallows it.

The godling had eaten three years of oaths. It had eaten a century of prayers. It had never once tasted anything it did not ask for.

Until now.

Sable spoke the first words of the renewal, clear and carrying, and the hall went silent. The king-thing leaned forward on its throne. Hale's hand drifted toward his blade.

"I, Sable Okafor," she said, "taster of the royal table, keeper of small charms, do offer myself—"

The roof came off.

Not all of it. Just the eastern span, stone and slate and centuries peeling away like the lid of a box, and through the gap dropped a shape out of nightmare and story both: a wolf the size of a horse, silver-eyed, landing in a crouch between Sable and the throne with the oath-circle's chalk lines shattering under his claws.

"Emrys," she breathed.

"The signal," he said, "was going to be subtler."

The hall erupted. Guards, screaming courtiers, Hale shouting orders — and above it all, wings. The dragon settled onto the ruined roofline like a mountain deciding to sit down, and its shadow fell across the entire oath-circle, and every candle in the hall guttered at once.

The godling stood. It wore Aldric's face, but the face was coming apart now, the way a mask comes apart when the wearer forgets to hold it — edges fraying, eyes flickering between blue and a hungry, bottomless dark.

"What have you done?" it asked, and its voice was a hundred voices, every prayer it had ever swallowed.

"Seasoned the meal," Sable said, rising, silk and blood and eleven charms burning bright against her skin. "You should have checked with the kitchen."

* * *

**Emrys**

He felt the oath break before anyone spoke again.

It went through him like a bell struck inside his chest — the coronation binding, the chain his masters had shown him in visions, the thing that had held the old gods sleeping for a century. The poisoned promise turned inward, exactly as designed, and the godling screamed, and the scream was made of every prayer it had ever eaten, and the crown lifted from the throne of its own accord, spinning slowly, drinking nothing, feeding on nothing, *starving*.

The dead king came free like a man surfacing from deep water.

For one moment — one only — Aldric stood beside his own body, translucent, astonished, weeping. He looked at Sable. He mouthed something. *Thank you,* perhaps. Or *forgive me.* Then the light took him, gently, the way night takes a candle, and he was gone, and the thing that had worn him unraveled after — thread by golden thread, a hundred years of stolen prayer streaming off it like smoke, pouring upward through the torn roof into a sky suddenly full of listening.

The old gods woke.

Emrys dropped to his knees. Not by choice. A century of servitude reversed itself in his bones — the leash he'd carried since boyhood snapping taut and then, impossibly, *slack*, and through that slackness poured his masters, awake, aware, enormous, filling the hall the way dawn fills a valley.

Every candle relit. The dragon bowed its head. Even Hale fell silent, blade half-drawn, face gray.

And then the presence turned, all of it, like an eye the size of the sky, and looked at Sable.

"We require a vessel," the gods said, in a voice that was wind and river and hearth-smoke and grave-dirt all at once. "To walk the world. To speak with mortal tongues. To carry justice outside the law."

The voice was not unkind. That was the terrible part. It was the voice of something that had never once considered saying no.

Sable stood in the ruins of the oath-circle, in her white gown, with her blood still wet on her palm, and looked up into the light.

Emrys struggled upright, throat tight. *Choose your duty,* his masters' old law whispered in him. *The vessel must be willing.* But her face — he knew that face now, knew every version of it, the kitchen girl and the liar and the woman who seasoned a god's last meal — and it was not afraid.

It was thinking.

"Sable," he said, and didn't know what he was asking.

She smiled. It was the smile she wore when she knew exactly which ingredient kills.

"Yes," Sable Okafor said to the gods of Vellamere. "I'll carry you. On my terms."

The light rushed toward her like a tide going out — or coming in — and the last thing Emrys saw before it swallowed her whole was her hand, closing around the falling crown.
