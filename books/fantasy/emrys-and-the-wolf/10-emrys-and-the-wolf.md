## 10. Emrys and the Wolf

The gods did not speak in words. Sable had expected thunder, a voice like the mountain breaking, some pronouncement carved across the sky. Instead they arrived as weather — pressure behind her eyes, warmth in her blood, a vast attention settling over Vellamere like snowfall, soft and total.

*You have unbound us,* they said, in the way that water says it is cold. *The vessel must be chosen.*

Around her, the oath-circle lay in ruins. The solstice hall was half-collapsed, dragon-fire still guttering in the broken brazier where she'd poisoned it with three drops of her own blood and every charm she'd ever been afraid to use. The godling was gone — unraveled into ash and old smoke, and beneath it, laid out on the shattered flagstones, the body of King Aldric, three years dead and finally at rest. His face was peaceful. That was somehow the worst part.

Emrys stood at the edge of the circle, wolf-shaped no longer, human again, blood on his knuckles from shattering the binding-stones. He was looking at her the way men look at things they are about to lose.

*Choose,* said the gods.

And Sable Okafor, who had spent three years making herself small, who had tasted poison for a hollow king and hidden her gifts in servants' pockets because a city that burned witches had left her nowhere else to put them — Sable stood up straight.

"I'll do it," she said. "On my terms."

The attention of ten thousand years of divinity turned on her like a lens focusing sunlight. She felt it try to pour itself into her, to fill her the way it had filled its vessels before — mouthpieces, puppets, women who spoke only when spoken through. She felt the shape of what it wanted: a voice with nothing of its own behind it.

"No," she said again, and this time she pushed back, the way she'd pushed back against Hale's questions, against the king-thing's bargain, against every hand that had ever tried to decide what she was. "I'm not your mouth. I'm not your vessel. I'm your *neighbor*. You want to walk the world again? Then you walk it as I walk it — answering prayers one at a time, doing the work, taking the consequences. Justice outside the law. Mercy nobody has to petition a palace for. You don't get a throne. You get me."

Silence. Even Emrys had stopped breathing.

Then, slowly, like dawn deciding whether to come up, the gods *laughed*. It moved through her chest, warm and enormous, and she understood that in all their long history, no vessel had ever negotiated.

*Agreed,* they said. *Small god. Street-level. Yours.*

The change came gently. Not fire, not transformation — just a settling, like a coat that finally fit. Her charms, the little ones she'd sewn into hems and slipped into pockets, lit up along her skin like a constellation finding its name. She could feel every prayer in Vellamere, faint as flies against glass — a baker praying his bread would rise, a widow praying for sleep, a child praying for a dog. Hundreds of small hungers. Hers now, to answer or refuse, one at a time.

She exhaled. "Well," she said. "That's that."

"Sable." Emrys's voice was rough. "What did you just do?"

"What I should've done three years ago." She looked down at her hands. Ordinary hands. Taster's hands. God's hands, now, apparently. "I stopped hiding."

* * *

Emrys felt the bond break at moonrise.

He'd carried it since he was nineteen — the leash of the sleeping gods, the compulsion that walked him across rooftops when they stirred, that starved him when they slept, that made him less a man than an instrument with a heartbeat. For eleven years he had served without being asked, obeyed without being consulted, loved nothing because love was a liability the leash could be used against.

Now it was simply *gone*, and he staggered in the temple ruins beneath the city like a man whose pack-weight had vanished mid-march.

He waited for the emptiness to follow. It always had before — every time the gods went quiet between errands, the hollow opened in him, wide enough to fall into. He braced for it.

It didn't come.

Because the gods weren't quiet anymore. They were *her*. He could feel Sable the way he'd once felt his masters — but warm, close, human-scaled, a presence like a hearth rather than a storm. And threaded through it, unmistakable, was something that wasn't divine at all. Something that had started in a rooftop confrontation and grown in candlelit ruins over stolen bread and shared exile, that both of them had refused to name because naming it would make it real and real things could be taken away.

Nothing could take it away now. He was unbound. He could walk out of Vellamere tomorrow and no power in heaven or under it could stop him.

He found her at dawn, sitting on the temple steps, watching the first light come off the river. She didn't look surprised to see him.

"You're free," she said.

"I am."

"You could go anywhere. The wilds are open. No more errands, no more leash."

"Yes," Emrys said, and sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Here's the thing about freedom, though. I spent eleven years thinking duty and wanting were enemies. That if I ever got to choose, choosing anything would mean abandoning everything." He turned and looked at her — really looked, the way he hadn't let himself before, because looking was a kind of promise. "But you didn't take godhood as a cage. You took it as a job. So I'm asking to be hired."

Sable laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the first he'd heard from her that wasn't armor. "Hired."

"Your enforcer. Your wolf. Unpaid, mostly voluntary, extremely loyal." He paused. "Also, if you'll have me — the other thing too. The unnamed thing. I'd like to name it."

She reached over and laced her fingers through his. Her hand was steady. "It's called staying," she said. "It's called building something instead of inheriting one. I looked it up while I was becoming a god; we have excellent resources now."

Above them, the sun cleared the rooftops, and somewhere in the waking city, a prayer rose — small, frightened, hopeful — and Sable answered it before her coffee even finished steeping, because that was the terms now, and she had written them herself.

* * *

The magic ban didn't crumble all at once. Laws are slow corpses; they take time to notice they're dead.

Inquisitor Hale came to the temple steps himself, three days later, alone and unarmed, which told Sable more than any report could. He looked older. The marked coin from his pocket — the trap she'd never sprung — he set down on the step between them like a man laying down a sword.

"The Inquisition exists," he said, "to police magic in service of the crown. The crown is ash. The magic is—" he gestured vaguely at her, at the temple, at the fact that flowers had begun blooming out of season along the whole street, "—everywhere. Half my agents woke up yesterday able to light candles with a thought. Two of them are crying about it. One quit to become a gardener."

"And the other half?"

"Wants to know who's in charge now." Hale met her eyes. He was not a brave man, but he was an honest one, which was rarer and more useful. "So. Who is?"

"Nobody," Sable said. "Everybody. The gods don't rule, they answer. If someone prays for justice, they get justice — mine, personally, delivered at street level. If someone prays for power over their neighbor, they get a conversation with me about their life choices." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had tasted poison for three years and knew exactly which ingredient kills. "You want a job, Hale? Be my first honest lawman. Not law above the people — law beside them."

Hale stared at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he picked up the marked coin and handed it to her, hilt-first, like surrendering a blade.

"I'm going to regret this," he said.

"Probably," Sable agreed, and shook his hand.

By summer, the witch-burnings were a generation's bad memory. Children in Vellamere learned charms alongside their letters. The royal kitchens — Sable kept them running, because someone had to feed the city and she'd never trusted anyone else's palate — employed four hedge-witches openly. And in the rebuilt temple district, there was no grand cathedral, no throne, no altar demanding sacrifice. Just a long table, always full, where anyone could sit and speak aloud to the air and be heard by a woman with a taster's tongue and a god's patience, while a wolf dozed under the table with his head on her feet.

Bittersweet, Emrys thought sometimes, watching her work. The dead king was buried properly, mourned by a city that finally knew whom it had lost. The last dragon — freed, ancient, guttering no longer — slept coiled in the mountains, its fire its own again, and visited twice a year to let children touch its scales. Everything mended. Almost everything.

Because the gods were awake now, all of them, and awake meant *hungry* in the old way — hungry for worship, for story, for shape. Sable held them to her terms, but terms require enforcement, and enforcement requires presence, and there were other cities beyond Vellamere, other lands where the old gods had woken to find no vessel waiting, no negotiator, no one who had learned the trick of saying *no*.

One evening in autumn, a traveler arrived at the long table, road-dusted and desperate, and prayed aloud for help — not for herself, but for her city, far to the south, where something wearing a dead queen's crown had begun to answer prayers of its own.

Sable set down her cup. Under the table, Emrys lifted his head.

"Well," she said, standing, and the light along her skin came up like dawn. "Shall we?"
