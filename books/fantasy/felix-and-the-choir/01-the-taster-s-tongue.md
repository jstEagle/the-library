## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The pheasant was trying to kill her.

Tamsin Thackeray knew this the way she knew rain coming — not by seeing it, but by the small animal part of her that had been trained since childhood to notice what everyone else missed. The bird sat on its silver platter like any other pheasant, browned and glistening, its wings folded in the decorative pose the kitchen boys spent an hour arranging. The sauce beneath it smelled of elderberry and rosemary and the particular sweetness of a good reduction, long-simmered, finished with a splash of the king's own wine.

And underneath all of it, faint as a whisper through a wall: bitter almonds.

She stood at the tasting table in the royal kitchens with her hands folded behind her back, the way she always did when she wanted them to think she was merely waiting. Around her, the kitchen roared on — forty cooks and scullions and spit-boys shouting over the clatter of copper, the hiss of fat, the bellows wheezing at the great hearth. Nobody looked at her. That was the point of her. A taster you noticed was a taster you worked around.

Master Cottrell, the under-chef, hovered at the edge of her vision, wiping hands that were already clean. He'd been doing it for two minutes. Guilt has a smell too, she'd learned. Sweat and fear-sweat are different animals, and fear-sweat has a sourness underneath, like milk just turning.

"Thackeray." His voice came out higher than he meant it to. "His Majesty is waiting."

"The king can wait another minute," she said pleasantly. "He's waited thirty years for dinner. He can wait for me to do my work."

A few of the nearer cooks went quiet. In the palace kitchens, where the hierarchy was older and more vicious than the court's own, nobody spoke to Master Cottrell that way except the head chef, and Tamsin wasn't the head chef. She was twenty-six years old, small, plain-dressed, and officially ranked somewhere below the man who scrubbed the grease traps. She was also the only reason three kings' worth of enemies had gone hungry and frustrated for a generation.

She picked up the silver spoon.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about being a food-taster: it isn't about dying well. Anyone can die well. It's about knowing your body so completely that you can read it like a ledger, line by line, before the totals come due.

Tamsin had been apprenticed to the trade at six years old, which was late, actually — most tasters were taken younger — but she'd shown an unusual gift. Her tongue could find a grain of arsenic in a barrel of flour. Her nose could separate eleven spices from one breath of steam. And her body — her body was the strange thing, though she hadn't known how strange until much later. Things that should have laid her out for a week only made her sleepy. Things that should have killed her made her sick for an afternoon.

The old taster who trained her, a wiry woman named Maren with burn scars up both forearms, used to say: "You're lucky, girl. You've got a strong cellar." She never explained what she meant, and Tamsin had been too young to ask properly, and then Maren died of something ordinary, a fever, which felt like a joke the world was playing.

So Tamsin grew up learning poisons the way other children learned prayers. Bitter almonds — cyanide, quick, merciful if you had to choose. Garlic-and-copper — antimony, slower, crueler. The sweetish rot-note of hemlock. The green taste of foxglove that crept into sauces when careless herbalists confused their leaves. She kept a mental ledger of every one, cross-referenced against symptoms, onset times, antidotes, and — because she was thorough in a way that unsettled people — the political affiliations of everyone known to use them.

She lifted the spoonful of pheasant and sauce to her mouth and let it sit on her tongue.

Almonds. Definitely almonds. Faint, but there, woven into the elderberry like a single wrong thread in tapestry. Someone had been clever about it — the bitterness masked by fruit, the dose probably calibrated to look like nothing more than a bad batch of berries. Clever, but not clever enough. The dose was the tell. If you were going to poison a king's dish, you committed fully or not at all; half-measures suggested either an amateur or someone who wanted deniability.

She swallowed.

Then she took a second spoonful, because the first rule of the trade was that you never reacted to the first taste. Reaction confirmed suspicion. Patience bought time. She counted her heartbeats — sixty, seventy, eighty — while the kitchen noise washed over her and Master Cottrell's clean hands kept wiping themselves.

At ninety beats, the warmth started.

It began low, in her belly, a spreading heat like brandy taken neat. Then her fingertips went numb, then her lips, then a fine prickling across her scalp, the sensation of a thousand tiny feet walking on her skin. Her pulse kicked up. Not racing — climbing, steady as a stair.

Cyanide, then. Real cyanide, not almond extract, not a trick of the berries. Enough to drop a grown man inside four minutes. Enough that the second spoonful had been, objectively, a stupid thing to swallow.

But her body did what her body always did. The heat crested, held, and began — slowly, grudgingly, like a tide changing its mind — to recede. By heartbeat one-forty, her lips had feeling again. By two hundred, the prickling had faded to a memory of itching. She stood very still at the tasting table, hands still folded behind her back, and let the poison finish losing.

"Well?" Master Cottrell's voice cracked on the word.

Tamsin turned and looked at him. Really looked. Forty-one years old, twenty years in these kitchens, a widower with a daughter in the country. Fear-sweat. Clean hands. Eyes that wouldn't hold hers.

"Cottrell," she said gently, "who gave you the elderberries?"

* * *

They found the berries in the third sack from the wall, the one delivered that morning by a carter no one recognized, paid in new-minted silver by a broker whose name dissolved the moment anyone tried to trace it. The kitchen guard confiscated the sack. Cottrell wept into a towel and swore on his dead wife's grave that he hadn't known, and Tamsin believed him, mostly, because guilt that pure doesn't usually lie.

By the time the sun cleared the inner wall, the story had traveled the palace the way stories do — sideways, through servants' corridors, faster than any rider. The king's food-taster had caught poison in the pheasant. The king's food-taster had tasted twice. The king's food-taster had not died.

That last part was the problem.

* * *

Master Aldous found her at dusk, in the narrow room off the spice pantry that served as her office, if you could call a desk, two chairs, and a shelf of ledgers an office. He didn't knock. Aldous never knocked; knocking, he liked to say, was a courtesy you extended to people whose thoughts you couldn't already read.

He was not a large man. He had the kind of face that was forgettable on purpose, gray eyes, and the patient stillness of someone who had spent forty years watching other people move. He had recruited her, trained her beyond what Maren had given her, promoted her, protected her. He was the closest thing to a father the crown's institutions had ever allowed her, and she loved him the way you love a wall that keeps the weather off — gratefully, and without ever quite forgetting that walls don't love back.

"Tasted twice," he said, settling into the chair opposite her desk without being asked. "On cyanide. And walked away."

"I walked away from worse," Tamsin said. "The halibut incident, year before last—"

"The halibut was arsenic and you vomited for an hour and reported it yourself within the day." He laced his fingers over his knee. "This is different. This is the fourth time in two years, Tamsin. Four exposures that should have put you in the ground. Four recoveries that shouldn't be possible. I've been keeping count."

"You keep count of everything."

"Yes," he said simply. "It's my job. Just as surviving is yours. But mine includes asking why."

She kept her face pleasant and empty, the expression she wore for nobles and cooks and anyone whose opinion might matter later. "Strong cellar," she said. "Maren used to say it. Good bones, good blood, luck."

"Maren said that." Aldous's gray eyes didn't blink often enough. "Maren also filed reports on you for nineteen years. Did you know that? Every exposure, every recovery, every symptom, timed to the minute. I have them all in my archive. You are the best-documented body in this kingdom, Tamsin Thackeray, and the documentation says you shouldn't exist."

The lamp between them hissed softly. Somewhere above, the evening bells rang the hour, and neither of them moved.

"What are you asking me, Master Aldous?"

"I'm asking what you know." He leaned forward slightly. "Because here's what I know. Someone tried to kill the king today with a dose sized for a king. You drank it down like watered wine. And whoever sent those berries chose cyanide — fast, untraceable after an hour, undetectable in a corpse unless you know exactly where to cut. Which means whoever sent them expected success, and expects that if it failed, it would fail quietly." He paused. "Unless, of course, they weren't aiming at the king at all."

The words settled into the room like cold air.

"They were testing the taster," Tamsin said slowly. "Not the dish."

"A poisoned dish is a message. A poisoned taster is a question." Aldous rose, smoothing his coat. "Someone in this palace wants to know whether you can be killed, and how hard you are to kill, and how much it would take. Today they got their answer: very hard, and more than they have." He stopped at the door, hand on the frame, and looked back at her with something that in a warmer man might have been regret. "Go home, Thackeray. Lock your door. Eat nothing you didn't prepare yourself. And think carefully about who benefits from a food-taster who cannot die."

He left. The lamp flame steadied. Tamsin sat alone in the little room with the ledgers of her careful life stacked around her, and realized that in nineteen years of service, through every poisoning and every recovery and every midnight report, no one — not Maren, not Aldous, not the crown itself — had ever once asked her whether she was afraid.

She locked the ledgers away. She banked the lamp. And she walked home through the darkening palace corridors alone, past guards who nodded to her and forgot her in the same breath, carrying the one thought she'd never permitted herself before:

If my whole life is a test, then who wrote the questions?

* * *

Her garret was three rooms at the top of a house in the Tanners' Row, chosen for its four exits, its sightlines, and the fact that the landlady asked no questions. Tamsin climbed the stairs, checked the thread she'd strung across the doorframe — intact — and lit a single candle.

She ate bread and hard cheese she'd prepared herself, from a loaf she'd watched baked, and drank water boiled twice. Supper of the paranoid. Supper of the lonely. She ate it standing at the window, watching the city go gold and then blue below her, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the last of the cyanide working out.

It wasn't. It was the other thing, the thing she never named, the thing that lived in her like a splinter too deep to dig out: the knowledge that she had survived everything the world had thrown at her, and none of it — not one hour of it — had ever been witnessed by anyone who cared whether she lived.

The candle guttered. Down in the street, a drunk was singing, badly, some dockside ballad about a sailor and a mermaid. Tamsin listened to the cracked voice rise and fall and thought, absurdly, that it was the first music she'd heard in weeks.

She was still listening when the bells rang midnight — and when the screaming started at the salt gate.
