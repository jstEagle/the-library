## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Gate

The salt gate smelled the way it always did in late autumn: brine and wet wool and the sour breath of a hundred mules waiting to be counted. Tamsin Thackeray stood in the shadow of the archway with her hood up and her hands in her sleeves, doing nothing that could be called suspicious and everything that could be called careful.

She had come to the gate for salt. That was the truth, and she had learned long ago that the best lies were built on truths like beams on foundations. The kitchens needed coarse grey salt for the pickling vats, and Master Quill had grumbled that his usual man was down with fever, and Tamsin had volunteered before anyone else could open their mouth. Volunteering was what she did. It kept her useful, and useful people were left alone.

It also got her out of the palace, which after last night's supper was worth more than her wages.

She turned the dish over in her mind again as she waited for the salt merchant's boy to finish weighing. The quail. She had tasted it — the proper ritual, silver spoon, bare tongue, the little pause while the king watched her not die — and she had caught it. Not poison exactly. Something *near* poison. A cousin of nightshade that grew only on the southern cliffs, ground fine enough to hide from any tongue less trained than hers. She had named it, they had hauled out the under-cook who'd accepted a bribe from someone nobody would name, and the king had eaten his supper safe and never once looked at her.

But the spymaster had looked at her.

Master Aldous had been standing by the wine cupboard, as he often was, wearing his grey coat and his grey expression, and when she said the word *cliff-nightshade* his eyes had done something small and terrible. They had brightened. Like a man hearing a coin drop in another room.

*How did you know,* he hadn't asked, because he didn't need to ask. He already knew how. He had taught her himself, ten years ago, a scrawny orphan girl pulled out of the taster's barracks and sharpened into an instrument. He knew exactly how good her tongue was. What he had been recalculating, standing there among the wine bottles, was something else: whether her tongue was now too good to control.

Tamsin pushed the thought away and counted mules instead. Counting was calming. Fourteen, fifteen — no, that one was two mules and a very optimistic pony —

The stranger came through the gate at a dead stagger.

She saw him first as a problem: a big man, road-dirty, weaving against the flow of cart traffic, drawing the kind of attention that made gate guards reach for their cudgels. Then she saw the blood. It wasn't the dramatic arterial spray of a tavern fight. It was slow, dark, seeping — the blood of a man who had been hurt hours ago and had simply refused, out of some enormous stubbornness, to stop moving. It soaked his left side from ribs to hip, and it left a trail on the cobbles behind him that even a child could follow.

He was looking for someone. His head swung, wild-eyed, past the guards, past the merchants, past the salt carts — and stopped on her.

Later, Tamsin would try to understand why. She was standing half in shadow, hood up, deliberately unremarkable; there was nothing about her that said *trust me*. But maybe that was it. Maybe a dying man looks past everyone who wants to be seen and finds the one person trying not to be. Or maybe it was simpler than that. Maybe he saw the palace livery pin at her collar — the little pewter spoon-and-cup of the royal household — and decided that anyone who tasted poison for a living would know what to do with what he carried.

He crossed the last ten feet like a man wading upstream, grabbed her arm with fingers gone cold as gate-iron, and folded.

She caught most of his weight, which was to say she went down to one knee under it and stayed there, because dropping him would make noise and noise made questions. Up close he smelled of horse sweat, old smoke, and something faintly sweet and wrong underneath — she knew that smell. Blood going bad. Wound-fever. He had been carrying this hurt for a day at least, maybe two.

"Listen." His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Listen to me."

"I'm listening," Tamsin said, which was true, and also what you said to bleeding men so they wouldn't spend their strength on shouting. "You need a physic. There's one two streets—"

"No physics." His grip tightened hard enough to bruise. "No guards. No—" A cough took him, wet and deep, and when it passed there was red on his lips and his eyes had gone glassy with pain. "No one. You understand? No one can have it."

"Have what?"

His hand moved. Under his cloak, under the blood-soaked shirt, his fingers worked at something tucked against his skin, and Tamsin's trained mind noted, distantly, that he was protecting a wound on his right side too, or hiding something flat and rectangular beneath the bandages there. He brought it out into the grey light.

A map. Folded into eighths, soft with handling, stained dark along one crease. It looked ordinary enough — until you noticed the paper. It wasn't paper. It was too smooth, too pale, and it gave off, when it moved, a smell she couldn't place: cold stone and green things and something older than either.

"Take it." He pressed it into her hands and closed her fingers over it with both of his, and his hands were shaking so badly the map trembled between them like a trapped moth. "Not to the palace. Not to Aldous. Not to *anyone* in a grey coat. Swear it."

"I don't swear things to strangers," Tamsin said automatically, and then hated herself, because the man's eyes were fixing and unfixing and he did not have time for her caution.

"Then don't swear. Just *keep* it." A ghost of a smile crossed his ruined face. "You've got a taster's hands. I watched you count the mules. Nobody counts mules like that unless they're counting everything." He coughed again, longer this time, and sagged, and she braced him against the wall of the archway where the shadow was deepest. "Good. Good girl. Listen now. The words matter."

"The words," Tamsin repeated. Around them the gate traffic flowed on, oblivious; a guard had glanced over once and she had met his eyes with the flat bored stare of a palace servant dealing with a drunk, and he had looked away. Ten years of being invisible, it turned out, had uses.

"The Choir remembers." Each word came out separately, placed like stones across a river. "Tell them — tell whoever opens it — the Choir remembers. Even broken. Even silent. It remembers every voice that ever sang it, and it will know the voice that comes back. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you," Tamsin said. "I don't understand you."

"You will." He laughed, or tried to; it came out as a rattle. "Nobody understands at first. Felix didn't. Felix—" and then his face changed, grief and urgency fighting for the same mouth, and he gripped her wrist one more time. "Felix trusted the wrong courier. That's all it takes. One wrong pair of hands. So find better ones than mine. Find the singer. There's always a singer. The notation is the song, the song is the key, and the key is—"

He stopped.

Not because he chose to stop. Because whatever had been holding him upright finally let go. Tamsin felt it happen under her hands — the specific loosening, the way a body stops being a person and becomes weight. She had felt it twice before, once with a cook who'd seized at the hearth and once with an old gate sergeant whose heart quit between one joke and the next, and she knew there was no coming back from it.

"Felix," she said. "Is that your name?"

But the man was gone. His eyes were open and aimed at the middle distance, at something far beyond the salt gate, beyond the city, beyond anything she had a name for. His mouth was still slightly bent, as if the last thing he'd managed was the beginning of a word he never finished.

Tamsin knelt in the archway shadow, holding a dead stranger's map, and did the arithmetic of her situation with the speed of a woman who had spent her life calculating doses.

One: a man had died touching her, in public, at the main gate, leaving a trail of blood a blindfolded child could follow.

Two: his last words had included the spymaster's name, spoken like a curse.

Three: she was holding the thing he had died rather than surrender, and it smelled of cold stone and green growing things, and every instinct she owned — the same instincts that had kept her alive through eleven years of deliberate poisoning — was screaming at her that this object was the most dangerous thing she had ever touched.

Four: the guards would notice the body soon. Very soon. Any second.

She put the map inside her shirt, against her skin, under the livery collar, and stood up.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and afterward Tamsin could never quite decide whether she had chosen any of it.

The shout went up — *man down, man at the gate* — and the guards came running, and Tamsin did the single most incriminating thing available to her: she walked away. Not ran. Walked, at the pace of a woman with an errand, toward the salt merchant's stall, and picked up her order of coarse grey salt as if the world contained nothing but salt, and paid the boy, and turned back toward the palace with sixty pounds of salt on her shoulder and a dead man's secret against her breastbone.

Behind her she heard the guards' voices, the crowd gathering, someone saying *he was talking to somebody, somebody in servant's clothes* — and then, cutting through everything, a voice she knew.

"Everyone stand where you are."

Grey coat. Grey expression. Master Aldous came through the gate crowd the way water comes through a crack, without appearing to hurry and without being stoppable, and Tamsin's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the autumn air.

He reached the body. He knelt. He did the things spymasters do — checked the pockets, lifted the cloak, examined the face — and Tamsin, twenty feet away and receding, watched him go very still over the dead man's right side. Where the bandages were. Where the map had been.

Aldous stood. He turned, slowly, scanning the gate crowd the way a hawk scans a hedgerow, and Tamsin felt his gaze pass over her — over the salt sack, the servant's hood, the bored face — and move on.

Then it came back.

It rested on her for three heartbeats. She counted them. She was good at counting things when terror demanded precision. Three heartbeats, during which Master Aldous, who had taught her to read a room, to read a face, to read the space between what a person says and what they mean, looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him before.

It wasn't suspicion. Suspicion she could have handled; suspicion was weather, it blew through. This was something quieter and much worse. It was the look of a man confirming a sum he had already done in his head.

Then he raised two fingers, very slightly, off his thigh. *Come here.*

And Tamsin Thackeray, who had spent eleven years being obedient the way other people were breathing, heard herself say, in a clear carrying voice pitched for the whole gate to hear:

"Sorry, master, I can't linger — the king's table won't wait and Master Quill will have my hide if the pickling salt's late." And she shifted the salt sack higher on her shoulder and walked through the gate arch, into the palace ward, at a steady unhurried pace, leaving her spymaster standing over a corpse with forty witnesses and no grounds to seize a kitchen maid carrying condiments.

Her heart was going like a drum in a parade. Her hands, the famous steady hands, were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the salt sack to hide it.

She had just lied to Master Aldous. Openly. In public. Using the oldest trick he had ever taught her — *hide the truth inside a bigger, boring truth* — against him.

She did not let herself think about what that meant. Thinking could wait. Walking could not.

* * *

The kitchens swallowed her the way they always did, in noise and steam and the bellowing of Master Quill, who complained about the salt's coarseness and her lateness in the same breath and then forgot she existed, which was the entire point of Master Quill. Tamsin delivered the salt. She washed her hands. She stood at her station in the tasting chamber and tasted four dishes for the evening meal — a pottage, a roast, a sauce of plums, a honey cake — and found them all clean, and reported them clean, and did not die, and no one remarked on the fact that her voice had gone flat as slate.

Through all of it, the map lay against her skin, warming slowly to her body heat, smelling its cold-stone, green-growing smell. She could feel it the way you feel a splinter: a small wrong presence, insisting on itself. Once, turning to reach the salt — *her* salt, the thought arrived with a hysterical edge she strangled immediately — she was certain she felt it shift, very slightly, as if settling closer.

*Nonsense,* she told herself. *Maps don't settle.* But she had survived poisons that should have killed her, and she had learned never to dismiss a thing merely because it shouldn't be possible.

Supper ended. The king lived. The kitchens wound down toward the banked fires and the scullery boys' exhausted silence, and Tamsin took her leave, and walked to her garret in the north tower stairwell where the palace kept the servants nobody wanted to think about, and bolted her door, and sat down on her narrow bed with her back against the stone.

Only then did she take the map out.

In the light of her one candle, it was worse and stranger than it had been in the gate's grey daylight. The material wasn't parchment and wasn't vellum and wasn't silk. When she tilted it, the surface moved — not visibly, nothing so obvious, but there was a depth to it, a sense that she was looking *into* the page rather than at it. The stains along the fold weren't the stranger's blood. Or weren't only his blood. In candlelight they had a faint iridescence, like oil on water, like the sheen on a raven's neck.

And the markings. She had expected roads. Every map she had ever stolen a glance at — and she had stolen plenty, riding shotgun on supply runs, memorizing passes for men who paid in silver and asked no questions — every map was roads and rivers and the little pictures of mountains that cartographers drew to flatter their patrons.

This had none of that. Instead, marching across the surface in lines that curved like flight paths and clustered like star charts, was writing she almost recognized and entirely could not read. Small marks. Stems and flags and hollow heads and filled ones, grouped in fives upon lines that ran not horizontally but in great sweeping arcs across the whole of the sheet, arcs that rose and fell like—

Like a melody.

"It's music," Tamsin said aloud, to her empty room, and her voice sounded strange in her own ears. "It's a map made of music."

She knew nothing of musical notation beyond what any palace servant absorbed by osmosis — she could tell a sharp from a flat on the choir page, nothing more. But she knew maps, and she knew that whatever these arcs traced, they traced it through mountains. The shapes beneath the notation, faint as watermarks, were unmistakable to her: the Thornfells. The massif she had grown up in, smuggled through, nearly died in twice. And threaded through the peaks, where no pass existed on any map she'd ever memorized, the notation-lines ran straight through solid stone — through the mountain itself, *underneath* it, marking passages that could only exist somewhere no human cartographer had ever been.

There was a mark near the center. Larger than the rest, circled, with a symbol beside it like a cup, or a well, drawn in a single unbroken line.

Tamsin stared at it until the candle guttered.

Somewhere below her, the palace clock struck the hour, and the sound seemed to travel down through the floor and away into the earth, and she thought — absurdly, irresistibly — that it sounded like the first note of something.

She folded the map along its existing creases, tucked it back against her skin, and lay down in the dark without undressing.

Sleep did not come. Sleep was chased off by a procession of images: the stranger's cold fingers closing hers over the paper; Aldous's two fingers lifting off his thigh; the words *not to Aldous* spoken by a dying man who had known the spymaster well enough to fear him by first name. And under all of it, quieter, repeating, the phrase she couldn't shake:

*The Choir remembers.*

Remembered what? Remembered whom? Remembered — a voice?

Near dawn, boots on the tower stairs. Measured, unhurried, familiar. Tamsin was sitting up with her hands folded in her lap before the knock came, the map already hidden in the one place no search would look — sewn years ago into the lining of her mattress, a pocket she'd made for emergency coin and never once used, because she had never had anything worth hiding.

The door opened without waiting for her answer. Of course it did. It was his palace; the locks answered to him.

Master Aldous stood in her doorway with a candle, grey coat, grey face, and eyes that held the particular gentleness he reserved for moments when he was about to take something from someone.

"Tamsin," he said kindly. "We need to talk about the man at the gate."

She met his gaze with eleven years of practiced blankness and said, "What man at the gate?" — and heard, as the words left her, that they were the first fully false words of her life, and that there was no road back from them, and that some traitorous corner of her, deep beneath the fear, was not afraid at all.
